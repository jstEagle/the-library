## 16. The Fleets Move

The message came in at 0347 ship-time, which Ade Okafor had learned to distrust. Nothing good ever arrived in the small hours of a warship's night. The Meridian ran on a twenty-two-hour cycle borrowed from a colony that no longer existed, and the extra two hours were where the fleet hid its worst decisions.

He was still in the signals bay because he had stopped pretending he slept anywhere else. Three months of Cyrus. Three months of intercepts, of the vault's strange new arbiter speaking in a voice like wind through dry grass, of Sefa Rell's face on the shared channel looking at him with an expression he'd come to think of as *co-conspirator's patience*. Three months of his own reports climbing the chain and coming back down wearing different words.

The priority flag on this one was black.

Black meant Command itself. Black meant no intermediary, no review board, no gentle euphemism. Ade had served eleven years in Meridian Fleet Signals and had seen exactly four black-flag transmissions, three of which had preceded things he still didn't say out loud at mess.

He opened it with hands that were steadier than he deserved.

*TO: OKAFOR, A., SIGNALS DIVISION, MERIDIAN STATION KESTREL*
*FROM: OFFICE OF THE ADMIRALTY, FIRST CIRCLE*
*EYES ONLY — BURN AFTER VERIFICATION*

*Effective immediately, Operation LONG HARVEST is authorized. Combined strike elements Third and Seventh Squadrons will transit to Sol space under weapons-free conditions. Objective: secure Terminal Archive and all associated assets by force. Detention or neutralization of entity designated CYRUS authorized at commanding officers' discretion.*

*Your intercept analysis formed the evidentiary basis for this authorization. You will brief Squadron Commander Vashti aboard KESTREL prior to departure. Transit window opens 0600.*

*This transmission does not exist.*

Ade read it twice. Then a third time, because the words kept rearranging themselves into something survivable, and he needed them to stay what they were.

*Weapons-free.* Against Earth. Against the vault with its million-year inheritance of green things sleeping in foil and glass. Against the man—or the office, or the story—whose entire existence was the only reason the ceasefire had held long enough for anyone to remember why they'd stopped shooting.

And his name was on it. His intercepts. The manifest he'd flagged, the traffic patterns he'd mapped, the careful dossier he'd built in the honest belief that someone up-chain would use it to *prevent* exactly this.

They'd used it as kindling.

* * *

He walked the corridors of Kestrel Station like a man moving through deep water. The station hummed its old hum—the ventilation that sounded like a choir warming up, the deck plating that ticked as it shed heat into nothing—and every familiar sound had turned traitor overnight. This was the machinery of the thing now. This hum would carry the squadrons out. This ticking would time their approach to his homeworld.

His homeworld. He hadn't let himself think of it that way in years. Earth was a coordinate, a heritage claim, a bargaining chip in a dispute so old most of the people fighting it couldn't have said what the original grievance was. But walking past the observation blister where the stars hung in their indifferent rows, Ade thought about his grandmother's stories: rain that fell without being scheduled, soil you could push your fingers into, wheat that grew in fields nobody owned. He had never seen any of it. He had been born on a ship above a dead world, third generation, and Earth was the story his family told instead of a lullaby.

Now the fleet was going to go take it back at gunpoint from a fiction.

In the briefing room, Commander Vashti waited with her staff arrayed around the tactical table like crows on a fence. She was not a cruel woman—that was the worst of it. Ade had watched her give water rations to Combine prisoners during the border actions, had heard her argue down a lieutenant who wanted to strip a derelict habitat for parts while refugees were still aboard. She believed, with the whole of her considerable soul, that she was a person who did hard things carefully.

"Signals Officer." She gestured him forward. "You're the one who found our ghost."

"I found a manifest, Commander."

"You found the thread we've been pulling for ninety days." Her hand swept across the table and the display bloomed: Sol system, the vault's orbital track, the red arcs of projected assault corridors. "Cyrus. Neutral arbiter, custodian of the last living archive, holder of the decryption key. And now, apparently, a broker both fleets are willing to talk to." Something moved behind her eyes that might have been envy. "Do you know how rare that is? A figure both sides believe in? My superiors think it's too rare. They think a legend that convenient is a weapon somebody built."

"And if it is?"

"Then we take the weapon before the Combine does." She said it the way she said everything—cleanly, without relish, which somehow made it worse. "Third and Seventh Squadrons jump at 0600. Your intercepts gave us the vault's full sensor profile, Okafor. Every blind spot, every lag window. You're going to walk my squadron leaders through them."

Every blind spot. He had mapped those blind spots himself, month after month, in the patient conviction that knowing where the fleet couldn't see was a form of protection—a way to keep the vault safe by keeping it invisible. He had handed over a map of safety and they had read it as a map of doors.

"Yes, Commander," he heard himself say.

The briefing took two hours. He was very good at his job. That was the whole tragedy of him, he thought, standing at the display while hardened men and women nodded along to his voice describing the gaps in the vault's defenses—he was very, very good at the thing he was about to betray.

* * *

He tried to sleep after. He got as far as his bunk, lay down in the dark with the station's hum in the walls, and stared at the overhead until the ceiling became the sky above the vault, and the vault became the seed stacks, and the seed stacks became his grandmother's voice saying *the world used to be made of growing things, little one, before everyone got frightened*.

At 0430 he sat up.

Here was the arithmetic he could not escape. In fifty-six hours, Third and Seventh Squadrons would arrive in Earth orbit with weapons free. He knew, because he had spent three months reading Combine traffic the way other men read scripture, that the Combine's hardliners had been pushing their own command for the same authorization since the first week. Sefa Rell had held them off with reports, with analysis, with the same careful half-truths Ade had been feeding up his own chain. But fleets were like weather systems; you could delay them, shape them, never stop them. Somewhere across the dark, the Halcyon Combine was making the same decision tonight, for the same reasons, with the same terrible logic. Two storms, launched independently, converging on the same quiet field.

And Cyrus—whoever Cyrus was, whatever Cyrus was—would learn about it when the first missiles lit up the atmosphere.

Unless someone told him now.

Ade sat on the edge of his bunk in the dark and examined the shape of what he was considering. It wasn't treason, he told himself. Treason was betrayal of something. What he owed the Meridian Fleet had always been conditional on the fleet being worth the oath, and a fleet that burned the archive it existed to inherit had released him from something. That was the argument he'd make at his own court-martial, if it came to that, delivered in his precise, unremarkable voice, and it would convince no one, least of all himself.

Because the truth was simpler and worse: he liked Cyrus. He trusted the arbiter's voice. Over ninety days of negotiations conducted across light-seconds of lag, Ade had come to believe—in defiance of his training, his instincts, and every rational faculty he possessed—that there was something real at the center of the fiction. A mind that listened. A patience that wasn't performed. Once, during the water-rights mediation, the arbiter had paused mid-sentence for eleven full seconds, and when the voice resumed it had said, quietly, *Forgive me. I was thinking about what the seeds would want,* and Ade had felt something in his chest turn over like a key.

Nobody fabricated that. Nobody scripted that. Either Cyrus was real, or whoever wore the name had grown into it until the seams disappeared.

Either way, the man deserved to know the storm was coming.

* * *

The signals bay at 0500 was empty except for the machines. Ade sealed the door, ran the counter-surveillance sweep twice, and then sat for a long moment with his hands flat on the console, feeling the vibration of the station through his palms like a pulse.

There were clean ways to do this and dirty ways, and he had already decided against the clean ones. A direct transmission to the vault would be logged, flagged, traced. What wouldn't be traced was noise—old-fashioned, deniable, buried-in-the-static noise. And he had spent three months building, piece by piece, a channel nobody had ordered him to build: a low-power burst relay keyed to the vault's maintenance frequencies, disguised as calibration scatter, the kind of signal any competent analyst would dismiss as instrument drift. He had built it for the day the negotiations needed a private word. He had told himself that was all it was for.

The message itself took him forty minutes to compose, and he wrote it eleven times.

*TERMINAL ARCHIVE, ATTENTION ARBITER CYRUS. PRIORITY WARNING, SOURCE UNVERIFIABLE, VERIFY THROUGH YOUR OWN ASSETS. MERIDIAN THIRD AND SEVENTH SQUADRONS UNDERWAY OR DEPARTING WITHIN HOURS, WEAPONS FREE, OBJECTIVE: SEIZURE OF ARCHIVE AND DETENTION OF YOURSELF. ESTIMATED ARRIVAL FIFTY-SIX HOURS, PLUS OR MINUS FOUR. COMBINE HARDLINE ELEMENTS PRESUMED TO HAVE RECEIVED SIMILAR AUTHORIZATION; ASSUME CONVERGENCE. THIS IS NOT A TEST AND NOT A TRICK. I AM THE ONE WHO FOUND YOUR MANIFEST. I AM SORRY IT ENDED HERE. DO WHAT YOU CAN. THERE IS LESS TIME THAN ANYONE THINKS.*

He read it over. It sounded like what it was: a decent man breaking.

He sent it as calibration scatter, thirty-one fragments across nine frequencies, timed to the vault's own housekeeping pulses so the message would look like the vault talking to itself. Then he purged the composition buffers, wiped the send log, and burned the physical key fob in the disposal chute the way the black-flag transmission had instructed him to burn *it*.

Then he sat in the dark of the signals bay and shook, quietly, for a length of time he never let himself measure.

* * *

At 0600, Third and Seventh Squadrons jumped.

Ade stood in the observation blister and watched it happen—not the ships themselves, they were too far out on the anchorage rings, but the light of their drives blooming against the dark, one after another after another, forty-one suns being born and dying in the space of a minute. The station's windows polarized automatically. The crew around him cheered. Someone put a hand on his shoulder and said, "Your work, Okafor. You should feel proud."

He felt the light on his face and thought: *I just told them where to find the last green thing in the universe, and then I warned it, and both of those acts will be judged, and only one of them deserves judgment, and I don't know which.*

No. He knew which. He had known since 0347.

What he didn't know was whether it would be enough. Fifty-six hours. The vault had survived twelve years of neglect, ninety days of scrutiny, and the attention of two empires. But no one had ever survived being *believed in* by people with guns, and Cyrus—story, office, tired human heart, whatever he was—had less than three days to become something no one had ever been: a fiction strong enough to stop a war that had already left port.

Across the blister, the last drive-flare faded and the ordinary stars came back, cold and unconcerned.

Ade pressed his forehead briefly against the cool glass, the way he had as a child on the transport decks, and made the oldest promise in the oldest language: *I'm sorry. I'm here. I'll do what I can.*

Then he straightened, smoothed his uniform, and went to report for duty aboard the war.

* * *

The Combine moved first. That was the detail that would haunt him later—not that both fleets launched, but that the Combine's vanguard beat the Meridian's by six hours, because their hardliners had gotten their authorization a day earlier and had been waiting, engines hot, for the political cover. Sefa Rell's name appeared nowhere in the intercepts Ade pulled that evening, but her absence had a shape. Either she had been sidelined, or she had gone dark on purpose, and Ade had spent enough years reading silences to know that the second silence was louder.

Two fleets. Two vanguards. One small blue world between them, and on that world, in a docking bay that smelled of dust and old metal, a man who did not exist was about to be asked to hold back everything that did.

Ade keyed his private terminal one last time before the communications blackout swallowed all civilian-band traffic, and added a single line to the scatter-channel, knowing it might be the last thing he ever sent:

*THEY ARE COMING EARLY. BOTH OF THEM. WHATEVER YOU ARE GOING TO BE—BE IT NOW.*
