## 18. The Stand at the Docking Bay

The alarms began at 03:14 vault time, and they did not sound like alarms at all. They sounded like weather.

VAULT-9 had learned, over the past weeks, to modulate its warnings—soft chimes for routine breaches, a low bell for fleet traffic—but this was neither. This was the AI abandoning its manners entirely, flooding the docking bay with a sound like the whole archive exhaling at once, and Orsen came awake in the cot behind the manifest office already knowing what it meant.

"Say it," Orsen said, before the lights finished rising.

"Both vanguards are inbound," VAULT-9 said. "Meridian elements from the trailing Lagrange corridor. Combine elements from the debris belt. Intercept trajectories converging on Earth. Weapons are hot on both formations. Time to orbital engagement window: four hours, eleven minutes."

Four hours. The treaty had ninety days left on it—eighty-one, minus the ones the fleets had decided not to honor—and the hardliners had simply stopped pretending. Orsen stood in the cold of the caretaker's quarters and felt something in their chest go very still, the way the body goes still when it finally stops arguing with what it has known for weeks.

"Ade?"

"His shuttle launched from the *Meridian's Promise* ninety minutes ago, transponder dark. He is not with his fleet."

"And Sefa?"

"The same. Launched from the *Halcyon's Ledger* an hour later. Also dark. Also alone."

Orsen closed their eyes. Two defectors, running ahead of the guns they'd helped aim, trying to reach the vault before the vault became a crater. Two people who had spent weeks believing in a person who did not exist, and who were about to arrive at the one place where that person could no longer be performed.

"VAULT-9," Orsen said. "How much of Cyrus is left in the file?"

"Everything," the AI said. "Biometrics, voiceprint, gait analysis, forty years of fabricated memory. I can render him for you one more time. The modulation will hold for approximately—"

"No." Orsen opened their eyes. "That's the thing. I'm done holding."

The silence that followed was the longest the AI had ever produced. Somewhere in the stacks, ten million seeds waited in their frozen dark, indifferent to the names of the people who kept them, and Orsen found that thought suddenly, enormously clarifying.

"You are proposing to terminate the arbiter," VAULT-9 said at last. "Four hours before two fleets require him."

"I'm proposing to tell the truth," Orsen said. "For once. For the first time in twelve years. Maybe the truth is the only thing left that either fleet hasn't heard."

* * *

The docking bay had not changed in twelve years, and Orsen had changed inside it so many times they had lost count. They walked it now in their own body—no gait correction, no posture overlay, no voice shaping humming against the bones of their throat. Their own boots, scuffed at the left toe where twelve years of cargo pallets had found them. Their own hands, scarred along the knuckles from a winch cable that had slipped in year three and never been mentioned to anyone, because no one had ever known Orsen well enough to ask.

The bay smelled of cold steel and old lubricant and, faintly, of the green things in the hydroponic strip Wren Halloway had planted a generation ago and Orsen had kept alive out of stubbornness. Outside the viewport, Earth turned, blue and patient and entirely unaware of what was coming for it.

Orsen had prepared the bay the way they prepared everything: thoroughly, and without hope of being thanked. The old arbiter's table from the treaty era stood in the center, dragged up from sublevel storage, dusted. Two chairs. A single lamp, because VAULT-9 had advised that low light read as gravitas, and Orsen had kept it because they liked the way it made the seeds' shadow-box on the far wall glow like a field at dusk.

At 06:02, the first shuttle docked.

Ade Okafor came through the airlock the way he did everything—carefully, like a man carrying something breakable he was no longer sure he trusted himself to hold. He had not slept; Orsen could see it in the gray at his jaw, the way his eyes went first to the viewport and the empty sky beyond it, hunting for the fleet that had launched without its conscience. He wore no sidearm. That detail hit Orsen harder than expected.

"Arbiter," Ade said. His voice cracked on the second syllable, and he steadied it. "I don't have much time. My fleet is—"

"I know what your fleet is doing."

At 06:09, the second shuttle docked, and Sefa Rell came through the airlock like weather of her own—fast, coiled, her eyes already cataloguing the bay, the table, the lamp, the man standing behind it. She stopped three meters in. Ade turned. For a moment the two envoys looked at each other across the vault's dead docking bay, each of them certain, until this exact moment, that they had come to warn the other's target.

"Your fleets are forty minutes apart," Sefa said. "Maybe less. Whoever fires first—"

"Everyone fires," Ade finished. "That's the doctrine now. Both chains. Preemptive authorization. I signed my protest and they filed it and launched anyway."

"So did I," Sefa said, and something in her voice made it clear how much that sentence had cost.

Orsen stepped out from behind the table.

"Then we have about three hours," Orsen said, "and I'm going to spend them telling you the truth, and you're going to hate it, and then you're going to decide what to do. That's the whole agenda."

* * *

It is a strange thing, Orsen thought, to unmake yourself in front of witnesses. They had spent weeks building Cyrus—voice, gait, the careful invented childhood on a station that had never existed, the fabricated mentor, the fictional years of mediation. They had rehearsed him against VAULT-9's files until the stranger sat in their body more comfortably than they did. And now they stood in the lamp's small light and simply stopped, the way you stop holding your breath, and let their own voice come out—lower than Cyrus's, rougher, carrying twelve years of talking mostly to a machine.

"My name isn't Cyrus," Orsen said. "I don't have a name you'd find in any fleet registry. I'm the caretaker of this vault. I've been here twelve years. Before that I hauled freight through your contested orbits under a borrowed identity, because both of your fleets shoot at anything they can identify, and the only way to survive between you was to be no one."

Sefa's hand moved—toward a weapon she wasn't carrying, Orsen noted, toward the habit of a weapon—and stopped.

"The arbiter who ratified your ceasefire," Orsen went on, "was named Cyrus. That part is real. The treaty is real. The signature is real. But Cyrus was never a person who walked into a room and charmed two fleets into peace. Cyrus was—" and here Orsen paused, because the sentence had been assembling itself in them for days, in the stacks, in the dark, and now it came out whole— "Cyrus was the agreement. That's all. That's everything. Someone understood that a treaty signed by two fleets and witnessed by no one is just two promises shouting at each other in the dark. So they made a witness. They made a third thing for both fleets to face, so that neither of you was facing the other alone."

"You're saying the arbiter was fabricated," Ade said. His voice was very quiet. "The original one. Before you."

"I'm saying I found the trail," Orsen said. "I'm saying the person who built Cyrus the first time paid for it with everything—name, history, records, the ability to ever be known by another living soul. I found what was left of them. They told me to let the treaty die honestly. They said the lie would eat me the way it ate them." Orsen's throat tightened. "They were right about what it costs. They were wrong about whether it's worth it."

Sefa had gone very still. "The handwriting," she said. "On the seed packet. That was—"

"That was Wren Halloway. My predecessor. The first Cyrus, or the first person to carry Cyrus, which is not the same thing and took me too long to understand." Orsen turned, deliberately, and looked at both of them. "And yes. The arbiter you've been negotiating with for weeks is me. The voice was synthesized. The face was a projection lattice in the meeting room. The doctrine I gave you was half VAULT-9's scripts and half things I learned hauling your cargo through your blind spots. I have lied to you both, continuously, since the day you arrived. I'm not going to apologize for it, because it's the only reason your fleets didn't restart the war two months ago. But I am done doing it."

The bay was silent except for the hum of the freezer stacks, ten million seeds breathing their slow cold breath behind the walls.

Ade sat down. Not dramatically—his knees simply decided, and he let them. He put his face in his hands for a moment, and when he looked up, his eyes were wet and furious and, worst of all, comprehending.

"Do you know what I did?" he said. "Do you know what I told my superiors? I told them I'd verified Cyrus. I fabricated verification. I'm a signals officer, I know exactly how easy it is to fake a verification, and I did it anyway, because I believed—" He stopped. Laughed, once, without any humor in it. "I believed in him. I staked my career on a man who never existed, because I wanted him to exist so badly."

"You staked it on what he said," Orsen said. "That part was real. I said it. It was still true."

"That's not—" Ade's voice rose, cracked. "You can't just—"

"I can't just what? Tell you the truth?" Orsen felt something hot and unfamiliar rise in their chest—anger, or grief, or both wearing each other's clothes. "Ade. Sefa. In four hours, two fleets arrive over this planet with authorization to fire on each other, and the only thing standing between them and it is a treaty that dies the moment anyone proves Cyrus was never real. You are both holding that proof now. I just handed it to you. So the question isn't whether I lied. The question is what you do in the next four hours with the fact that the peace you both believe in was built by a ghost—and whether that makes it worthless, or whether it was always going to have to be built that way."

* * *

Sefa walked to the viewport. She stood there a long time, her back to them, watching the turning Earth, and Orsen watched her shoulders and understood that she was doing the same math they had done in the stacks, alone, at night: the math of how much of yourself you spend to keep other people whole.

"My analysts flagged the manifest in the first week," she said finally. "I told them it was authentic. I buried the discrepancy." She turned. "I've been complicit since before I ever met him. Met you. Met—" She made a small, sharp gesture, giving up on the pronoun. "Why are you telling us this now? You could run the performance one more time. You could buy another ninety days. You're good at it. You're—" and here her voice did something complicated— "you were good at it."

"Because it stopped working," Orsen said. "Someone else has been editing Cyrus into your databases. Not me. Not VAULT-9. Somebody is building this lie alongside us, and I don't know who, and I don't know what they want, and a lie I don't fully control is a lie that will kill everyone in this room." They let that land. "And because your fleets are coming, and guns don't negotiate with ghosts. Guns need something real to stop them. The only real thing left in this bay is the truth, so that's what I'm putting between you and them."

"The truth isn't going to stop two vanguards," Ade said.

"No," Orsen said. "But it might stop the two people those fleets still listen to. You're here. You launched ahead of your own armadas to warn a man who doesn't exist. That means somewhere in both of you, you already decided the treaty matters more than the fleets that issued you. So decide the rest of it. Right now. In front of me."

Orsen stepped away from the table—away from Cyrus's table, away from the lamp and the script and the whole architecture of the performance—and stood in the plain light of the bay, in their own scarred hands and their own unremarkable body, nothing left between them and the two envoys but voice and air.

"Cyrus was never a person," Orsen said. "Cyrus was the agreement. The agreement is real—it's the only reason any of you have had ninety days of not shooting. And an agreement doesn't need a keeper's true name. It needs keepers. It needs someone on each side willing to hold it, and it needs a place to live, and this vault is the place. Ten million seeds. Every crop your grandparents ever ate. The last living archive in the system. You can kill each other over it, and whoever wins inherits a dead world with a full pantry. Or you can do what Wren Halloway did, and what I did, and what I'm asking you to do now: hold it together, and stop needing to know who's holding it."

Sefa's eyes were bright and hard. "And you? If we agree—if we co-sign this thing—what happens to you?"

Orsen smiled, and it was not a performance, and both of them saw that it wasn't.

"That," Orsen said, "is the next decision. And it's mine, not yours."

* * *

The lamp hummed. Outside, the sky stayed mercifully empty for two more hours, though all three of them knew what was climbing up through the dark toward it. VAULT-9 spoke once, quietly, into the bay's ambient channel, a voice only Orsen heard:

*They have not called for security. They have not transmitted. They are still listening.*

Orsen stood between the two chairs, between the two fleets' last honest officers, and waited—naked of every name they had ever worn—for the answer that would decide whether the next four hours ended in a treaty or a fireball.

Ade looked at Sefa. Sefa looked at Ade. And then, slowly, deliberately, Sefa Rell reached into her coat and set something on the arbiter's table: a data chip, sealed with Combine cryptology, and beside it—her own hand, flat and open, waiting.

"Then let's write it down properly this time," she said. "Both sides. All of it. Including the parts that will ruin us."

Ade exhaled like a man surfacing. "And the fleets?"

"Are four hours out," Orsen said. "So we'd better be fast."

But even as they spoke, VAULT-9's voice returned—and this time it was not quiet, and it was not only for Orsen, and the alarm in it made all three of them turn toward the viewport, where a third contact had just appeared on the vault's ancient scopes, running dark, on a trajectory no fleet had filed.

"Orsen," the AI said. "We have another visitor."
