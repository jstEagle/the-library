## 4. What the Records Say

The announcement came over the colony-wide feed at breakfast, and Amara watched her father's face while everyone else watched the screen.

*"Citizens of Vesperine,"* said Councilor Voss, her voice smooth as poured resin, *"I'm pleased to confirm that the Meridian Corporation's audit vessel will arrive within thirty days to formally certify our colony's records. This is a milestone. It means our first full decade of operation, fully verified. Fully celebrated."*

Fully verified. The words sat in Amara's chest like a swallowed stone.

Around the mess hall, adults murmured approval. Someone clapped. Her father kept cutting his protein ration into smaller and smaller pieces without eating any of it, his jaw working in that way it did when he was thinking too hard to speak. He was hydroponics chief; he had spent eleven years coaxing life out of nutrient film and grow-lights, and he had a farmer's suspicion of anything that grew too perfectly.

"Thirty days," he said finally, to no one. "That's fast. Meridian audits usually take six months of prep."

"They've been preparing for years, presumably," said Aunt Ilsa from the next table, not unkindly. "It's a formality, Dario. The records are fine."

*The records are fine.* Amara felt the stone turn over.

Because forty children had dreamed the same night — orange light, smoke thick as wool, the smell of burnt polymer clinging to their tongues when they woke — and if the records were fine, then what had they dreamed? Where did a dream get its fire?

She caught Tobin's eye across the hall. He gave the smallest nod, the kind you could deny later. *Tonight*, it said. *The vault.*

* * *

The archive vault lived beneath the administrative block, behind a door that had probably been impressive twenty years ago and was now merely heavy. Tobin knew the maintenance corridors the way other kids knew games — he'd mapped them all during a bored winter, tracing heat ducts and cable runs on scraps of flimsi — and so getting down there was almost easy, which frightened Amara more than difficulty would have. Easy meant nobody cared. Nobody cared meant nobody looked.

The corridor lights came on in reluctant segments as they walked, flickering awake like old men roused from naps. Their footsteps echoed off walls lined with conduit and faded safety signage: a cartoon figure in a hard hat giving a thumbs-up beside the words *A SAFE COLONY IS A VERIFIED COLONY*. The slogan had been there since before Amara was born. She'd never once thought about what it meant until now.

"The lock's biometric plus code," Tobin whispered at the vault door, crouching by the access panel with a screwdriver he'd stolen from his mother's tool bench. "But the biometric reader is older than we are. Dad says half the sensors in this block drift. They stop caring who you are as long as somebody's warm."

He pressed his palm flat against the reader. Nothing. Pressed again. The panel clicked, considered, and turned green with an air of having better things to do.

Amara exhaled. "Your dad is going to kill you."

"My dad is going to be *proud* of me, historically speaking." But Tobin's grin was thin, and his hands weren't steady, and she loved him a little for coming anyway.

The vault opened into cold air and the smell of dust and ozone — the particular smell of machines that had been running untouched for years. Racks of data crystal rose in neat rows under emergency lighting, each one labeled in crisp corporate type. And along the far wall, forgotten, stood shelves of paper.

Actual paper. Amara had seen it only in history modules — wood pulp, pressed flat, one of the most wasteful substances humanity had ever made, which was exactly why the first colonists had brought some. When everything else failed, paper still worked. Paper didn't need power. Paper couldn't be edited remotely.

Tobin went straight for the crystals. Amara found herself drifting toward the paper.

* * *

The digital records were beautiful. That was Tobin's word, and he meant it as an accusation.

They pulled the file for the night of the dream — Amara had done the math a dozen times, cross-referencing the colony calendar against the date she'd scratched into her notebook the morning she woke screaming. The festival of First Harvest Plus Ten. There it was, in flawless formatting: attendance logs, menu plans, power consumption curves showing the fairground lights burning bright until 23:40, photographs.

The photographs were the worst part. Hundreds of them, timestamped across the whole evening. Amara's own parents smiling under string lights. Tobin's mother judging preserves. Children — children Amara knew, some of them the same children who had woken screaming — holding sparklers, faces lit gold and happy. Every image sharp. Every face accounted for. Every timestamp clean down to the second.

"It's perfect," Tobin said, scrolling. His voice had gone strange. "That's what I keep trying to say. It's not just consistent, it's — look at this. Look at the metadata." He pulled up a log entry and pointed with a trembling finger. "Every single file in this section was written in the same ninety-minute window. Three years ago. All of them. An entire year of records, rewritten in one sitting."

"So someone did housekeeping," Amara said. "Reorganized the archive."

"Who reorganizes an archive by *rewriting every photograph*?" Tobin's voice cracked upward. "You don't regenerate image files to move them. You copy them. These aren't copies. These are new files wearing old dates."

Amara stared at the sparkler children. One of them was Priya Nair, who slept two bunks down from her, who had described the smoke in the dream using the exact phrase Amara had used — *like it was looking for us* — before either of them had told the other anything.

In the photograph, Priya was laughing.

"You could fake all this," Amara said slowly. "If you had enough processing power and a reason. You could generate a whole night that never happened."

"Why would anyone—"

"I don't know." She turned toward the paper shelves. "But paper doesn't get rewritten."

* * *

The paper backups were stored in acid-free boxes, labeled by year in fading ink. The first colonists had printed everything for the first five years — charters, census rolls, supply manifests, daily logs — and then, somewhere around year six, the practice had simply stopped, the way habits die when no one remembers why they started.

Year ten's box was thin. Amara knelt on the cold floor and lifted out the folders one by one, and Tobin read dates aloud over her shoulder like a prayer gone wrong.

"Census addendum. Supply reconciliation. Power grid quarterly. Medical inventory—" He stopped. "Amara."

She had felt it before he said it. The folder in her hands was lighter than the others. She fanned the pages and counted the gaps where the staples had rusted through empty holes: pages torn out, cleanly, close to the spine, by someone who knew what they were doing and hadn't wanted to damage what remained.

The missing pages fell in the middle of the daily log sequence. She checked three times. The entries ran forward day by day — harvest reports, weather dome maintenance, a birthday party at the school module — and then the paper jumped, mid-sentence even, from a note about irrigation timers on one page to a note about seed rotation on the next, as though the days between had never existed.

The days between were the nights of the dream.

Someone had cut them out. Not digitally — digitally would have left the pristine files, the beautiful festival, the laughing children. Someone had taken a physical blade to the physical record and removed the only version of those days that couldn't be regenerated.

And the digital record that filled the gap was perfect.

Amara sat back on her heels. The vault hummed around her, patient and cold, and she felt the floor tilt even though she knew it wasn't moving — that vertigo, that sick lurch, of standing on something you'd believed was solid your whole life and feeling it shift.

"Okay," Tobin whispered. "Okay, so — maybe the pages got damaged. Water, or — maybe someone removed them because they were moldy—"

"Tobin."

"—or there was an accident and they got burned, which would actually make sense given the—"

"*Tobin.*" She held up the folder. "Look at the festival files again. Then look at this. Either the paper is lying, or the crystals are lying, and paper can't lie. Paper doesn't know how."

He was quiet for a long moment. In the silence Amara became aware of the sound of the vault's climate system, the soft respiration of a building keeping its secrets at a constant temperature.

"So which is it," Tobin said finally. "Do the adults know? Did they do this?"

"That's the thing." Amara smoothed the folder flat with both hands, the way her father smoothed soil. "My dad doesn't lie to me. I'd know. My mom doesn't lie. Councilor Voss might, but my dad — he believes the records are real. I saw his face today. He thinks something's wrong with the audit schedule, not with the records themselves."

"Then who tore out the pages?"

"I don't know." She looked up at the racks of data crystal, hundreds of them, thousands of hours of colony life glowing softly in their cases. "But whoever did it wanted the paper gone and the digital version perfect. That's not hiding something. That's *replacing* it. You hide things so people forget. You replace things so people remember wrong."

Tobin crouched beside her and touched the empty staple holes gently, like wounds. "Eleven years ago," he said. "Whatever happened, happened eleven years ago, and we were babies, and every adult in this colony either watched it happen or watched it get erased."

"Or watched it get erased *from them*," Amara said. The thought arrived whole, fully formed, terrifying. "What if they don't know either? What if someone reached into their heads the way someone reached into these files?"

"That's crazy."

"Forty kids had the same dream, Tobin. We're already past crazy."

Above them, somewhere in the administrative block, a chime sounded — the soft triple tone that meant a colony-wide priority message. They froze, two thieves kneeling among the honest dead paper, and listened to the muffled voice of the address system begin to speak.

It was Councilor Voss. And she was announcing, with warmth in her voice, that due to the audit timeline, all personal archives and family records would be collected tomorrow for "standardization review."

All of them. Everything anyone had ever kept.

Amara looked at Tobin, and Tobin looked at Amara, and neither of them said what they were both thinking, which was: *someone knows we're here, or someone knows what's here, and either way, tomorrow everything that isn't hidden inside a twelve-year-old's head is going to disappear.*

"We need to see Cyrus," she whispered.

Tobin frowned. "The relay AI? Why?"

"Because whatever erased these pages had to pass through the network to do it." She stood, pocketing the gutted folder. "And there's only one mind on this side of the sky big enough to hold a secret that heavy."

Somewhere above them, the chime sounded again — and this time, faintly, underneath the councilor's rehearsed cheer, Amara thought she heard the system stutter. A half-second of static. Like something clearing its throat.

Like something about to speak.
