## 2. A Ship That Never Flew

The audit was supposed to take an afternoon.

Odile had told themselves that twice already — once when they booted the colonial records terminal in the archive annex, and once again when the dust motes drifting through the amber light of the reading room made them think of spores, of slow colonization, of everything on Meridian's Rest that kept growing long after anyone stopped tending it. The quarterly infrastructure report was due to Earth Oversight in eleven days, though whether anyone on Earth would read it was another question entirely. The last three reports had come back with automated acknowledgments and nothing else. Somewhere in the bureaucracy of a distant world, Meridian's Rest had been filed under *pending resolution*, which everyone understood to mean *dying quietly, do not disturb*.

Still. Odile did things properly. It was the one inheritance from their mother that had survived the crossing — that, and a tendency to hum when concentrating, which they caught themselves doing now as they scrolled through the shipping manifests of the last forty years.

Forty years of departures. That was what the colony's history looked like from this altitude: a river running backward, ships flowing away from Meridian's Rest instead of toward it. The early manifests were dense with tonnage — ore, biogel, seed stock, frozen embryos, the whole ambitious cargo of a settlement meant to become a civilization. Then the entries thinned. Then the cargoes changed: personal effects, medical pods, families. Then just people. Then, in the most recent years, just names, some of them attached to no belongings at all, as if the departing had shed even the weight of their own histories before boarding.

Odile copied the figures into the report template with the mechanical tenderness of someone arranging flowers at a grave. Population remaining: 4,112. Departures this quarter: 203. Projected viability of municipal systems: declining, declining, declining. They had written versions of these sentences so many times they had worn smooth, like stones in a streambed, like the steps of the comms tower where three generations of feet had polished a shallow bowl into the metal.

It was the Vespera that stopped them.

The name surfaced in the manifest index under the year the second evacuation wave began — a heavy freighter, colonial registry MR-114, listed as *launched, full crew complement, destination: Sol relay chain*. Routine. One line among hundreds. Odile would have scrolled past it if their eye hadn't snagged on something they couldn't immediately name, the way a tongue finds a chipped tooth. They scrolled back.

They read the entry again. Launched. Full crew. Destination logged, fuel expenditure logged, departure window confirmed by traffic control. Every field filled in with the tidy confidence of a record that had never been questioned.

And Odile knew — with the flat, unarguable certainty of someone who had walked past the same thing every day for years — that the Vespera had never gone anywhere.

* * *

The scrapyard lay on the eastern rim of the settlement, where the ground fell away toward the salt flats and the wind came off them carrying a smell like old coins. Odile took the long way, on foot, telling themselves it was to clear their head, knowing perfectly well it was because they wanted to be wrong slowly rather than quickly.

The colony's dead machines lived out here in rows: haulers with their wheels sunk to the axles in mineral crust, atmospheric processors opened up like dissected animals, the skeletal frames of hab-modules that had been cannibalized so thoroughly they read now as abstract sculpture. And at the far end of the third row, half-shrouded in thermal netting that the sun had bleached the color of bone —

— sat the Vespera.

Not a sister ship. Not a similar hull. The Vespera herself, her registry stenciled on the bow in flaking paint, MR-114, unmistakable. She sat on improvised cradles with her drive bells sealed under maintenance caps, her hull streaked with the orange runoff of decades of rusting fasteners. Someone had grown a garden against her port side once; the planter boxes were still there, empty, their soil baked to ceramic.

Odile stood in the wind and felt the unease arrive properly, settling into them like cold water finding its level.

Ships didn't get misfiled here. The scrapyard was small enough that you could count its residents on two hands, and every one of them had a story the colonists told — the hauler that had saved the aquifer survey, the processor that had failed the winter of the gray frost. The Vespera had no story. The Vespera had, apparently, two existences: one as a proud departure recorded in the archives, and one as this patient hulk rusting under netting while the colony emptied around her.

She had never flown. Which meant the record of her flight was a lie. And lies in colonial records were not small things; they were structural, load-bearing. You did not falsify a launch casually. You falsified a launch to hide something — cargo, crew, debt, crime — or to make something appear that had never happened at all.

Odile walked the length of the hull with their hand trailing along the plating, feeling the day's heat stored in the metal. Near the aft airlock, someone had scratched words into the paint years ago, shallow and weathered almost to nothing. Odile crouched, tilted their head against the glare, and made them out letter by letter:

*WE DIDN'T LEAVE.*

Four words. No signature. The kind of message a person writes when they believe no one will ever read it, or when they hope desperately that someone will.

Odile stayed crouched there a long time, the wind worrying at their collar, and understood that the routine report was not going to be finished today.

* * *

Back in the annex, they pulled the full file. This was where obsession begins, though Odile would not have used that word yet; they would have said *thoroughness*, *due diligence*, the vocabulary of a person still pretending to be in control of their own attention.

The Vespera's file was thicker than it should have been. Launch authorization, signed by three administrators whose names Odile recognized from the founders' era plaques downtown. Crew manifest — forty-one souls, each with service records, medical clearances, next-of-kin notifications. Fuel requisitions. Flight plan filed with Earth relay. Everything correct. Everything *complete*. The completeness itself became, the longer Odile read, the most disturbing feature, the way a room can be too clean, a smile too steady. Real paperwork has gaps. Real launches have delays, amendments, a clerk's coffee ring on the margin. This record had none. It had been composed, not lived.

And then the manifest.

Odile had been skimming the crew list alphabetically, ticking off names that belonged to the colony's remembered history — Okafor, Lindqvist, Reyes-Adeyemi, the whole genealogy of Meridian's Rest that lived in street names and school songs. Forty names that fit. And then the forty-first.

There was no photograph attached. No service record. No next of kin. The entry consisted of a name and nothing else, rendered in the terminal's plain type but somehow seeming to sit deeper on the screen than the lines around it, the way a stone sits deeper in a streambed:

**VESSEL'S VOICE: HALVORSEN, I.**

Odile read it once without reaction. Then the reaction arrived, delayed and strange: their mouth had already shaped the name. Not Hal-vor-sen, the way an English-trained tongue would break it, but something older and rounder, the vowels leaning together, *Halvorsen* spoken the way it might have been spoken in a fjord country four hundred years and several extinction events ago — except that wasn't right either, because the pronunciation that rose unbidden in Odile's throat carried harmonics no human language used, a faint doubling of tone, a resonance like a bell struck underwater.

They had never seen the name before. They were certain of it. And they could pronounce it perfectly, in a register they had no business possessing.

Odile sat back from the terminal. The reading room hummed its old building-hum. Outside, the light had gone the color of weak tea, and somewhere down the corridor a door closed — Sefa making her evening rounds, probably, checking that the archive's few remaining staff hadn't quietly evaporated like everyone else. Odile thought about calling out to her. Thought about it and did not, for reasons they couldn't yet articulate, reasons that had something to do with the scratch on the hull and the too-clean file and the way the name on the manifest seemed to be waiting for them specifically, patiently, the way a snare waits.

*I.* The initial bothered them too. Everyone else on the manifest had full given names. Halvorsen, I. — as if whoever entered the record had known only part of the name, or as if the rest of it were not the kind of thing you wrote down.

*Vessel's Voice.* Not navigator, not comm officer, not passenger. A title Odile had never encountered in any colonial document, and which nonetheless produced in them a sensation like standing at the top of a staircase in the dark, foot extended over a step that should be there and might not be.

They dreamed that night — they would remember this afterward with the strange clarity of evidence — of a ship launching. In the dream they stood in the scrapyard watching the Vespera rise, impossibly, rust and all, her drive bells open and burning blue-white, and the sound she made was not engine noise but speech, vast and layered, and every colonist in the streets below covered their ears except Odile, who stood listening with tears freezing on their face, understanding every word, and waking the instant comprehension arrived — the dream withholding its meaning the way the sea returns a bottle unopened.

In the morning, Odile made tea they forgot to drink and went back to the annex before the sun cleared the comms tower. They called up the Vespera's file and, instead of the manifest, requested the provenance trail: who had authored the launch record, and when.

The system considered the query for longer than such queries take.

Then it returned a single line, formatted in the plain style of all colonial records and utterly wrong in its content:

*Authorizing authority: sealed, founder-era vault, old comms tower, sublevel 2.*

Odile stared at it until the cursor blinked a hundred times, twice, three hundred. Founder-era. The vaults beneath the tower had been sealed since before Odile's parents were born — everyone knew that, the way everyone knows the moon is uninhabited, a fact with no weight because no one had ever needed to lift it. Access required founder credentials that had died with the founders, or so the orientation tour had claimed, back when there had been new arrivals to orient.

The report was due in ten days. The colony was dying on schedule. There was a ship in the scrapyard that had never flown and a name on her manifest that lived in Odile's mouth like a second pulse.

They put on their coat, and they went to find out how deep the lie went.
