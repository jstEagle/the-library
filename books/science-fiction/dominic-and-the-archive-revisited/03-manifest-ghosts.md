## 3. Manifest Ghosts

The name would not leave Odile alone.

*Veyra Solenne.* They had written it out eleven times in the journal that night, each iteration in a different hand — their own careful xenolinguistic script, then the loose cursive of their student years, then block capitals like a child learning letters. The name looked different every time and sounded identical every time: two syllables that opened like a door and closed like one behind them.

That was the wrongness of it. Odile had spent twenty-two years on Meridian's Rest and could recite the colony's founding registry from memory — three hundred and six names, every one of them catalogued, cross-referenced, mourned or forgotten as the decades demanded. Veyra Solenne was not among them. And yet when Odile read it off the Vespera's manifest, their mouth had shaped it without hesitation, without the fractional search any unfamiliar name requires. As if the name had been waiting in the muscles of their jaw for a cue.

People did not work that way. Languages worked that way.

So Odile did what they always did with an unanswerable question: they went to the archive. Not the colonial records this time, but the deep strata — the founder-era files that nobody touched because nobody remembered how to touch them, sealed under encryption keys held by administrators who were dust before Sefa was born. The old comms tower's basement annex still had a physical terminal, a slab of pre-colonial hardware humming to itself in the dark like something hibernating rather than dead. Odile had found it years ago during a survey of dying infrastructure and had never reported it, a small hoarded secret in a life otherwise devoted to other people's words.

The terminal woke reluctantly, its screen blooming amber. Odile fed it the name.

The system thought for a long time. Long enough that Odile's tea went cold beside the keyboard, long enough that the wind outside the tower found the broken louvers and played its thin whistle through them, a sound Odile usually recorded with affection and tonight heard as breathing.

Then, a single line:

**ACCESS GRANT — VOICE-KEY MATCH: FAIRWEATHER, O.**

Odile stared at it until the words stopped meaning anything.

They had not entered credentials. There was nothing to enter. The terminal had simply — recognized them. Or recognized something in them. *Voice-key*, though no voice had been offered. Unless the key was not the voice itself but whatever the founders had built into the manifest, into the name, into the impossible fluency sitting in Odile's mouth like a stone swallowed whole.

Below the access line, a directory unspooled: forty-one files, timestamped two hundred and thirty-one years ago, all bearing the same designation — **VAULT 7 / INHERITANCE PROTOCOL / DO NOT WAKE UNTIL NAMED**.

*Named.* Odile sat back. The chair complained beneath them, a small human sound in a room full of waiting machines.

Every instinct honed by two decades of linguistic fieldwork said *stop*. Every story the colony told about its founders — the brave, the far-sighted, the ones whose statues stood eroding in Founder's Square — said the vaults were legend, or worse, that the few ever opened had contained nothing but agricultural schematics and sentimental letters. The founders had been settlers, not cryptographers. Settlers did not seal things beneath comms towers with protocols keyed to names that did not exist.

But settlers also did not build colonies on worlds where the ground itself hummed at frequencies the human ear insisted on hearing as language.

Odile pressed the key.

* * *

The stairwell below the terminal had been hidden behind a false wall so well that the dust on the steps lay undisturbed in a perfect grey carpet, printed only once — by Odile's own boots, now, descending. Their headlamp carved a cone out of the dark. The air tasted mineral and old, like the inside of a bell.

Forty-one steps. Odile counted them without meaning to, the way they counted everything — syllables, heartbeats, the shrinking population figures in the weekly reports. At the bottom, a door of pale composite alloy, seamless except for a recessed panel bearing the same inscription from the directory, and beneath it, smaller, almost apologetic:

**IF YOU ARE READING THIS, YOU WERE ALREADY CHOSEN BEFORE YOU CHOSE.**

"That's not how consent works," Odile said aloud, and their voice cracked strangely in the dead air, too loud, too alive. But their hand was already on the panel, and the panel was already warm, and the door was already opening — silently, on hinges that should have screamed after two centuries, opening the way a mouth opens around a familiar word.

The vault was small. A room, not a tomb, though it felt like both. One desk. One chair. A rack of recording media — physical, archaic, lovingly maintained in climate-sealed cases — and above the desk, mounted on the wall, a speaker horn of a design Odile knew intimately: the same lineage as the listening arrays the Archive had seeded across the continent in the war's first year, the arrays that had taught humanity that the enemy did not speak in words but in *structures*, in meaning folded through time like light through water.

Someone down here had studied the Archive. Someone down here had built a room to listen back.

Odile set their own recorder running — habit, profession, superstition — and lifted the first case. The label was handwritten, the ink faded to the color of weak tea:

**FOR THE ONE WHO DREAMS WITHOUT WORDS. BEGIN HERE.**

Their hands were shaking. They noticed it distantly, the way one notices weather happening to someone else. Because there it was — the thing they had told no one, the thing they had barely admitted in the privacy of their own journal, scrawled and crossed out and scrawled again: *dreamed last night in a language with no human words, dreamed again the night after, and I am afraid of what is doing my dreaming for me.*

Two hundred and thirty-one years ago, someone had written those words' answer and sealed it underground, addressed to a person who did not exist yet.

Odile loaded the first recording.

* * *

It began with silence — not empty silence but structured silence, the kind the Archive used the way humans used punctuation. Then a voice. Not human. Not quite. It moved through the Archive's phonology, that architecture of pressure and pause, and Odile's training rose up in them automatically, parsing, segmenting, translating —

— and stalled, because the voice was speaking *to them.*

Not to humanity. Not to "whoever finds this." The grammar was second-person singular, intimate register, the form the Archive reserved for what humans would call a beloved or a debtor. And threaded through the opening phrase was a name.

*Veyra Solenne.*

Except — Odile played it again, hands flat on the desk, breath held — the voice pronounced it differently than the manifest had. Softer. Older. The way you pronounce the name of someone you have not spoken to in a very long time, someone you are not sure has the right to hear your voice anymore.

And beneath the alien phonology, so faint it might have been imagination, another voice: human, female, layered into the recording like a watermark. Speaking English. Speaking *Meridian dialect*, the creole the colonists had evolved in the first generation and let die in the fourth.

"...if you're hearing this, then the protocol held, and I'm sorry. I'm sorry we couldn't tell you what we took, or why, or what it cost them to forgive us — if they've forgiven us. We wrote this for our heir. Not by blood. By *voice*. The Archive doesn't choose bodies, it chooses listeners, and it chose you before your grandparents were born. Everything we built here — the colony, the treaty, the war — rests on a debt we never paid. You're holding the receipt."

The human voice faded. The Archive's voice continued, patient, immense, folding meaning into structures Odile could feel the shape of but not yet open, like a letter in a language one reads but cannot yet write.

Odile sat in the founder's chair in the founder's vault beneath the founder's tower, and listened to the enemy say their secret name — the name they'd never been given, the name that had appeared on a ship that never flew — and understood, with the cold bright certainty of a translation finally resolving, that the message was not *addressed* to whoever found it.

It had been *waiting* for them. Possibly longer than they had been alive.

There were forty recordings left. Outside, somewhere above, the colony's last lights were going out district by district, and the evacuation window was a number that shrank every day. Odile pressed play on the second file, and the vault filled with voices, and they did not stop listening until their recorder's battery light blinked red and the first grey suggestion of dawn was seeping down the stairwell like water finding its level.

On the last file they played that night — the ninth — the Archive's voice said something new. Something that made Odile stop the playback and sit motionless for a long time in the dark.

It was a single sentence, and Odile could translate exactly half of it. The half they could translate said: *When both fleets come for you —*

The other half contained a word Odile had never encountered in any corpus, any intercept, any dream.

But their mouth, unbidden, already knew how to pronounce it.
