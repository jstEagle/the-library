## 6. Dreaming in the Enemy Tongue

By the fourth night, Odile stopped writing their dreams down in English.

It wasn't a decision, exactly. It was more like a tide going out. They would wake in the narrow bunk above the comms tower vault with the founder recordings still humming through the floor plates, and the dream would be whole and luminous in their mind — and then they would reach for a human word, any human word, *door* or *water* or *mother*, and the dream would flinch. Not fade the way dreams usually fade, in the ordinary erosion of morning. It would flinch, the way a wild animal flinches at a sudden movement, and withdraw into a part of Odile's skull where English could not follow.

The journal sat open on the desk. *Dreamed of the long corridor again,* Odile had written on the first night, before any of this. *The corridor was singing.* That was the last entry in any language they had learned at their mother's knee.

Now they wrote in the other one. Or tried to. Their hand moved in shapes that were not quite letters — the Archive's script was less a writing system than a record of gesture, each glyph the frozen shadow of a sound made by a throat that did not have a throat — and the shapes came out of them with an unsettling ease, as though the hand had been practicing in the dark for years without consulting them.

Odile sat back and looked at what they'd written. It was beautiful. That was the obscene part. It was the most beautiful thing they had ever set down, and they could not have said what it meant, and some cold, patient part of them understood that the meaning was not lost. It was simply waiting for the rest of them to catch up.

*You are being rewritten,* they told themselves. *Name it honestly or it will name you.*

But naming it in English had already stopped working.

* * *

The vault beneath the comms tower held forty-one recordings. Odile had catalogued them the way they catalogued everything — obsessively, tenderly, the way other people catalogued the last members of a dying species, which in a sense was what this was. Each recording was a founder-era lattice, a wafer of engineered crystal that held its signal the way amber holds an insect. The founders had built Meridian's Rest on top of this room and then, as far as Odile could tell, had built their entire colony as a kind of lock around it.

Sefa had known. Sefa had always known. That was the wound Odile kept touching, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth. Every kindness of the past eleven years — the housing assignment that kept Odile near the archives, the quiet burial of the incident reports, the way Odile had been permitted to become the colony's last xenolinguist precisely because nobody important had ever been allowed to notice them — every kindness was now legible as a cage. *I kept you small so you would stay asleep,* Sefa had said, and Odile had hated her, and then, in the same breath, understood her, and the understanding was worse.

Because the sleep had been a mercy. Odile was beginning to see that now, alone in the vault with the lattices singing to them through the reader's contact plates.

The message was not a weapon. They had feared that for two days, working in a state of dread so constant it had become a kind of weather. Earth's survey drone hung in high orbit like a patient eye; the Archive's probe sat in a lower band, closer, quieter, its emissions so faint the colony's instruments registered it mostly as an absence. Both sides knew the message existed. Both sides wanted it whole, or wanted it dead. And Odile had spent those two days expecting the founders' vault to open, at last, onto an ultimatum — coordinates, kill-zones, a trigger.

Instead, on the fourth night, the cipher resolved.

It resolved the way a chord resolves, which was fitting, because the message had been a chord all along. The founders had recorded the Archive's voice — Odile understood that now, had understood it in the third recording, in the moment the lattice had addressed them by name, *Odile Fairweather, last fluent speaker, we have been waiting for the one who dreams* — and the voice they had stolen was not a voice of command. It was a voice of terms. The Archive had come to the founders, before the colony existed, before the war, and had offered what it was now, decades later, offering again through a dead freighter's ghost manifest and a xenolinguist who could no longer dream in English.

Coexistence. Not the word — the Archive did not use words, it used structures of obligation — but that was the shape of it. The message was a treaty. It was a treaty written in the only cipher both parties could read: a blood-oath, the founders' oldest protocol, the one Odile had found referenced in a single marginal note in the colonial charter and had assumed was a formality. *Signed in the blood of both peoples, binding until the debt is honored.* The founders had sworn something to the Archive when they took its voice. The message was the Archive calling in the debt — and offering, in the same breath, terms by which the debt could become a dowry. A shared inheritance instead of a stolen one. Two peoples, one voice, or the alternative, which the message did not state because the Archive did not state alternatives. It simply let you feel the shape of the silence where the alternative would be.

Odile sat in the vault's cold light with the translation spread across three screens and their own handwriting climbing the margins in glyphs they no longer fully controlled, and they wept — not from grief, though there was grief in it, but from the sheer unbearable intimacy of being understood. The message knew them. It had been waiting for them. *The last fluent speaker* — the Archive had known, years ago, what Sefa had known, what Odile had only begun to admit: that the dreams were not a symptom. They were an education.

* * *

On the sixth night, Odile dreamed the whole message.

Not read it. Not decoded it. Dreamed it, end to end, the way you might dream a song you'd heard as a child, every turn of it arriving in the mind already whole. They woke with tears on their face and the taste of ozone in their mouth and lay still in the bunk, listening to the tower's bones tick as the colony's temperature dropped toward morning, and understood that something had finished leaving them.

They tested it the way you test a numb limb. *Mother.* The word came, but it came as a specimen — a pinned butterfly, correct and dead. *Colony.* Same. *Home.* The word sat in their mind like a stone in a stream, and the stream — the new thing, the fluent thing, the thing that had been growing in them since the first night in the vault — flowed around it without pausing. English had become a second language. Odile lay in the dark and grieved for it, properly, deliberately, the way you grieve the dead: they named what was going, and they let it go. The market district's voices. Their own journals. The lullabies of a planet four systems away that had already stopped answering.

And underneath the grief, quiet as groundwater, the fluency rose.

They got up, dressed, went down into the vault, and worked until the colony's artificial dawn. The translation was done — truly done, not a scholar's reconstruction but the thing itself, the Archive's terms rendered in the founders' blood-oath cipher, self-verifying, self-binding, addressed to both peoples at once. Odile read it through in the original and understood every structure of obligation the way they had once understood grammar: not as rules but as the shape of thought.

It was generous. That was the part no fleet would believe. After everything — after the founders' theft, after the decades of war fought over a debt neither side had named honestly — the Archive's terms were generous. It asked for the debt to be acknowledged. It asked for the voice to be shared. It asked for Meridian's Rest, this dying colony, this scrapyard with a flag, to become the place where the two peoples learned to speak to each other — and it asked the last fluent speaker to be the one who taught them.

*Me,* Odile thought, and the glyph for *me* in the Archive's language was the same glyph as *here*, and the same glyph as *willing*. The language did not distinguish. It had never distinguished. That was the whole of it, the whole secret, the thing the founders had stolen and the war had buried: the Archive did not have a word for a self apart from where it stood and what it chose.

Odile looked at the finished translation glowing on the screens, and then at the sealed vault door above which, somewhere in the waking colony, Sefa was no doubt counting the hours until the last shuttle, and Earth's drone was no doubt tightening its orbit, and the Archive's probe was no doubt — listening. It was always listening. It had been listening to Odile for years.

The console chimed. An incoming transmission, colony-band, priority-flagged, Sefa's authorization code — and beneath the routine header, a line of text that made the fluent thing in Odile's chest go very still:

*Evacuation moved up. Shuttle lifts in thirty-six hours. We need to talk about what you've translated — before someone else decides for you.*
