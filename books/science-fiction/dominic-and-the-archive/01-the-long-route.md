## 1. The Long Route

The alarm woke her at 0430 station time, which was a fiction, since Meridian-9 hung in a place where no sun ever rose and no sun ever set, and the only difference between morning and night was which lights Lena had told the station to turn on. But she had kept the fiction alive for eleven years, and a fiction kept that long stops being a lie. She swung her legs out of the bunk, pressed her bare feet to the cold deck plating, and said, to the ceiling, to the station, to whoever was listening in the way that empty rooms listen:

"Good morning, Meridian."

The station answered the way it always did: with the soft tick of the environmental systems shifting over to day-cycle, a sound like an old man turning over in his sleep. Somewhere below her, coolant hissed through a junction she'd have to check before lunch. Somewhere above her, the deep-array turned its slow, patient face toward the dark, listening to nothing, the way it had listened to nothing for years.

Lena Sterling was the last human technician aboard the automated relay station Meridian-9, and she had a route to run.

* * *

She started, as she always did, in the spine.

The spine was the station's central trunk, a corridor four hundred meters long that ran the length of the habitat ring like a throat. Her boots rang on the grating in a rhythm she knew better than her own heartbeat—*step, step, ring, step, step, ring*—and the overhead lights came on ahead of her in sequence, section by section, because she had programmed them to, because she had programmed everything to, because a station full of systems that anticipated her was the closest thing to company she had left.

"Morning, Vasquez," she said, patting the coolant manifold at Junction One.

The manifold did not answer. The manifold had never answered. It was a manifold. But it had been Vasquez's manifold—Chief Engineer Amara Vasquez, who had named every junction on the ring after her sisters, all four of them, so that running the coolant lines felt like a family reunion—and when the evacuation order came through and the last shuttle burned away toward the inner colonies, Lena had kept the names. It seemed like the kind of thing a person owed. You did not let the dead go unnamed just because they'd had the good sense to leave.

Junction One was running warm. Not dangerously—nothing on Meridian-9 ran dangerously anymore; danger required stakes, and stakes required someone to be endangered—but warm the way a kettle runs warm when it's thinking about boiling. Lena knelt on the grating, popped the inspection panel, and put her palm flat against the housing. Heat bled up into her skin, familiar as a handshake.

"You're working too hard, old girl," she murmured. "I know. We're all working too hard."

She bled a quarter-liter of coolant, checked the pressure curve against the curve Vasquez had scrawled on the inside of the panel door eleven years ago in grease pencil—*if this drops below 2.1, stop being a hero and call someone*—and smiled at it the way she smiled at it every morning, because the handwriting was still there, because nobody had come to erase it, because she would cut off her own hand before she let anyone erase it.

The grease pencil note was the closest thing Meridian-9 had to scripture.

* * *

The route took her aft through the spine, past Junction Two—Okonkwo—and Junction Three—Mireille—and into the hydroponics bay, which was not technically part of her maintenance circuit and had not been since the agricultural module was decommissioned, but which she visited anyway, because a route is not a route if you only go where you're needed.

The bay was dark and green and breathing. Lena had kept one bank of the grow-lights alive—just one, just enough—and in the amber glow, the old colony seed-stock still grew in its long troughs: soybean vines gone feral and tangled, a stand of wheat that had long since stopped resembling anything a farmer would recognize, and the tomato plants, which thrived with the cheerful indifference of the truly immortal. Nobody ate most of what grew here. She couldn't. She'd tried, the first year, and had stood in the galley with a bowl of soup made from the station's own green life and found she couldn't lift the spoon, because the soup tasted like the crew, like Vasquez's terrible hydroponic stews, like Mireille complaining about the wheat, like Okonkwo sneaking extra portions and denying it with his mouth full. So now the bay grew for no one, a garden in a tomb, and Lena trimmed it and watered it and talked to it, and once a month she ate one tomato, standing right there among the vines, as a kind of communion.

"Morning, garden," she said.

The garden, being a garden, said nothing, but the misters cycled on with a sound like distant rain, and Lena stood in the false rain for a while with her eyes closed, and let it be enough.

* * *

By 0800 she was in the comms bay, which was the heart of the matter, which was the reason for everything.

Meridian-9 was a relay station. That was its whole purpose, its reason for existing, stamped into its bones: signals came in from the deep-array, were cleaned and amplified and re-aimed, and went out again toward the three colony worlds at the far end of the long dark—Ceres Reach, Halcyon, and the newest one, whose name changed every few years because the company kept rebranding it, though everyone still called it Third Landing. For sixty years, Meridian-9 had been the throat through which the colonies spoke to each other and to Earth, every birth announcement and freight manifest and love letter and last testament, all of it passing through this room in pieces of light.

And now the room was nearly silent.

Lena settled into the tech chair—it had been Mireille's chair; she had never once sat in it without thinking *this is Mireille's chair*—and pulled up the traffic log. The numbers were the numbers they always were. Four scheduled carrier pulses from Ceres Reach, automated, content-free, the colony's systems checking that the line was still open the way a person checks that a dead relative's phone is still connected. Two pulses from Halcyon. One from Third Landing, delayed by relay drift, degraded, patched together by her own hand at 0300. Total human-originated traffic in the last twenty-four hours: zero.

The network wasn't dead. That was the thing she had to keep explaining to the company auditors, back when there had been company auditors, back before even they had stopped coming. The network was *dying*, which was different. Dead was a state. Dying was a process, and processes could be tended, and tending was what Lena did. She re-aligned the array by fractions of degrees. She replaced capacitors that had a decade of life left in them. She kept the routing tables clean and the error logs trimmed and the carrier frequencies tuned to within a hundredth of a hertz, so that when a signal came through—when, not if—Meridian-9 would catch it as cleanly as the day it was commissioned.

"Good morning, network," she said, to the room, to the racks of dark equipment with their small patient lights. "Still here. Still me."

The equipment said nothing, but one of the carrier lights blinked, a scheduled handshake from Halcyon, and Lena chose, as she chose every morning, to take it personally. *Hello, Lena. Still here. Still you.*

* * *

The middle of the route was the hard part, and she had learned, over the years, not to skip it.

The crew quarters occupied the forward ring, and she walked them the way you walk a cemetery: slowly, with your hands at your sides. Vasquez's bunk was made, had been made for eleven years, corners tucked the way the Chief had tucked them—military-neat on one side, chaos on the other, because Vasquez had made her bunk for two and never gotten around to changing the habit after her wife moved to Halcyon. Okonkwo's bunk was a museum of abandoned projects: a disassembled radio he'd been rebuilding, a half-knitted scarf, a paperback novel with a receipt for a bookmark. Mireille's bunk was bare, because Mireille had been the last to leave and had packed like someone who expected to come back, and had not come back, and Lena tried not to think about what that particular shape of hope did to a person over eleven years.

"Morning, everyone," she said to the empty room. Her voice sounded wrong in here, too loud, too alive. She said it anyway. "Systems are green. Vasquez, your Junction One is running warm again, I'll keep an eye on it. Okonkwo, I still don't know what your radio's missing, and I'm not going to figure it out for you, so don't ask. Mireille—" and here her voice did the thing it did, the small fracture, the hairline crack in the routine that she papered over every single morning and that never quite healed, "—Mireille, traffic's light. I'll keep the line open."

She stood there a moment longer, in the amber light of the sleeping ring, listening to the station breathe around her. The ventilation cycled. The array turned. Somewhere in the deep structure, a relay clicked over, the station's slow mechanical pulse, and Lena let it count for a heartbeat, hers.

This was the ritual, and she knew what it was. She was fifty-one years old and she was not a fool. She knew that talking to coolant manifolds was not health, that eating one tomato a month in a dead garden was not living, that a woman who kept a bunk made for a wife who had left eleven years ago was not keeping a station alive so much as the station was keeping a grief alive, and they were using each other, she and Meridian-9, the way two drowning things will hold on to each other, each convinced it's doing the rescuing.

But she also knew this: the network was dying, and she was the only one tending it, and if she stopped—if she let the route lapse, let the capacitors drift, let the array fall out of true—then the last thread between the three colony worlds would go slack, and no one would notice for years, and by the time they noticed it would be too late to reweave. Somebody had to keep the line open. Somebody had to be here, in the dark, in the quiet, doing the small faithful work that makes large faithful things possible.

And if that somebody was also, secretly, keeping a grave—well. Graves needed tending too. That was the whole point of them.

* * *

The afternoon was the array.

The deep-array sat at the station's crown like a great stone flower, forty meters of phased receivers turned toward the black between the colonies, and calibrating it was the one part of the route that still demanded everything she had. The array drifted—everything drifted; the universe was a slow argument against precision—and every day Lena climbed the access spine, plugged her tablet into the calibration bus, and walked the array's attention back to true, frequency by frequency, element by element, forty-one receivers, each one a hand cupped toward a sound too faint to hear.

She worked in silence for the first hour. This was the meditative part, the part where the route stopped being a cage and became, briefly, a craft. There was a rightness to a calibrated array that she could feel in her teeth, a harmony, and when she found it—when all forty-one hands were cupped toward the dark at exactly the right angle—the station felt less like a tomb and more like what it had been built to be: an ear. The network's ear. Her ear.

She was on element thirty-seven, the last but four, when she saw it.

At first she took it for drift. Element thirty-seven's noise floor was elevated—not much, a fraction of a percent, the kind of thing she'd have noted and corrected and forgotten a thousand times before. She ran the standard correction, and the noise floor didn't drop. She ran it again, wider, and the noise didn't drop, and that was wrong, that was *interesting*, because element thirty-seven was pointed at a stretch of sky that contained, as far as the star charts said, nothing at all. Dead space. The long dark between Halcyon and the outer dark where no relay had ever been worth building, where no signal had any business existing, where the only things that lived were cosmic background and the slow radio sigh of dying stars.

Lena pulled the raw feed and looked at it properly, and her hands went still on the tablet.

There was a pattern in the noise.

Not a pattern like interference—interference had edges, spikes, the ugly geometry of machinery failing. This was buried deep, under the noise, woven *through* it, the way a voice is woven through the sound of the sea if you know to listen. A repeating structure, cycling on a period too long for her to see in a minute of data, too regular for her to dismiss in ten. She pulled an hour. The pattern held. She pulled a day, and the pattern held, and her mouth had gone dry somewhere in the middle of the hour without her noticing.

It was old. That was the other thing, the thing that made the skin of her arms prickle under the array's cold light. The signal was faint in a way that distance alone didn't explain. It was faint the way a voice is faint when it has been traveling a long, long time—worn down, attenuated, patient. Whatever this was, it had been arriving at element thirty-seven for longer than she had been running her route. It had been arriving, she suspected, since before the crew left. It had been arriving, possibly, since before she'd arrived, all those years ago, a young technician with a mentor's recommendation in her pocket and no idea that she was walking into a life sentence.

It had been talking into this ear the whole time, and she had never once cupped her hands tightly enough to hear it.

"Okay," Lena said aloud, to the array, to element thirty-seven, to the dark. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her. "Okay. What are you?"

The array turned its slow face toward the nothing and said nothing back. But the pattern cycled on beneath the noise floor, patient as a heartbeat, and Lena Sterling sat down on the access grating with her tablet in her lap and her route forgotten for the first time in eleven years, and began, very carefully, to listen.
