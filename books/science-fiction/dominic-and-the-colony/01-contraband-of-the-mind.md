## 1. Contraband of the Mind

There are three ways to lie to orbital customs on Meridian Colony. You can bribe them, which is expensive and leaves a trail of receipts. You can hide your cargo so well that even a full-spectrum scan comes back clean, which is expensive and leaves a trail of debts. Or you can walk up to the prettiest officer on the inspection deck and tell him the truth about everything except the one thing that matters.

I've tried all three. The third is the only one that doesn't leave me feeling like I've lost something.

The *Kestrel's Regret* — my ship, my home, my floating apology for a life — settled into Docking Cradle Nine at 0400 station time, which is the hour customs officers hate most and smugglers love best. Not because the officers are sleepy. Dominic Vale has never once been sleepy in his life; I'm fairly sure the man sleeps standing up with one eye open like a horse. It's because at 0400 there are fewer witnesses.

"Freighter *Kestrel's Regret*, this is Orbital Customs." His voice came through my comm warm and dry as old paper. "You're cleared for approach, Eastwick. Try not to insult me before breakfast."

"Officer Vale," I said, keying the channel with two fingers while the other hand walked the ship down through the cradle's magnetic grip. "Insult you? I've been composing compliments for six hours. Do you want the one about your jaw or the one about your tragic sense of duty?"

"That's the problem with you, Elodie. Your compliments always have a body count."

I brought her in soft. The *Regret* is forty years old if she's a day, patched in seven places with metal from six different wrecks, and she handles like a drunk aunt at a wedding — but she lands sweet when I ask nicely, and I was asking very nicely indeed, because under my maintenance deck, wrapped in thermal foil and labeled as coolant filters, sat four hundred memory chips. Not data chips. Memory chips. The kind that hold a dead man's last summer, a widow's wedding day, a child's voice saying *mama* for the record. The kind Meridian's Information Purity Act says no private citizen may own, because memories are dangerous, memories are unregulated, memories might contain — and here the Act gets genuinely breathless — *unauthorized historical content*.

God forbid anyone remember anything the government didn't approve first.

* * *

Dominic was waiting at the airlock, and I'll be honest with you, whoever you are: after eleven days alone in the black, I'd have paid real money just to look at him. He stands like he's been assembled rather than born — regulation posture, regulation haircut, regulation gray uniform pressed to the point of aggression. Customs blue trim, senior inspector's bar, boots you could signal satellites with. Twenty-nine years old and already wearing his career like armor he can't take off.

He had his scanner out before my ramp finished cycling. Of course he did.

"Pilot Eastwick," he said. "Manifest?"

"Beacon maintenance run. Relay Seven." I handed him the clearance chit and watched him take it, and I want it noted for posterity that my hands were steady. Mostly steady. "The derelict beacon, top orbit. Somebody has to keep the old girl's lights blinking or every long-haul pilot in the system files a complaint."

"Nobody services that beacon."

"Somebody does now." I smiled at him. "Me. I'm somebody. It's in my file."

He slotted the chit into his reader and I watched his face do the thing it does — nothing, absolutely nothing, a wall of professional blankness that would win awards if they gave awards for it — while the forged authorization sat there in his palm being read by a machine smarter than both of us. The forgery was good. Marn's forger is the best in the colony, and I'd paid enough to buy a small moon. Beacon Maintenance Authority, orbital assets division, signed and countersigned and stamped with a seal that would survive two levels of audit.

Three levels and it dies screaming. But nobody checks three levels on a beacon nobody cares about.

"You know what I like about you, Vale?" I said, leaning against the doorframe of my own ship like I owned the dock instead of renting it by the hour. "You never smile. It's very restful. Everyone else on this station smiles at me like they want something. You just look at me like I'm a math problem."

"You are a math problem." He didn't look up from the reader. "Cargo manifest lists twelve crates of filtration media. Your mass signature is off by four hundred kilos."

"Is it?" I widened my eyes. "Well. Filtration media is dense stuff. Maybe your scale's lonely and wants attention."

"Maybe." He looked up then, and here's the thing about Dominic Vale that I've never said out loud to anyone, including him: his eyes are the color of the sky over Meridian's northern sea right before a storm, gray-green and patient, and when he looks at you he looks *through* you, all the way down to whatever you're hiding, and then — this is the part that undoes me — he decides, privately, silently, whether to say so. "Four hundred kilos of filtration media that isn't there. Or four hundred kilos of something that isn't filtration media. One of those is a clerical error. The other is ten years in the reeducation annex."

"The annex has terrible food," I agreed. "I hear the poetry classes are nice, though."

"Elodie."

Just my name. First name, no rank attached, spoken quietly enough that the dock recorder three meters away wouldn't catch it over the cradle hydraulics. That's how he plays it, every time. Everything official happens in full view of the cameras, and everything true happens in the gaps between them, in the half-second pauses where a man who believes in rules lets a woman who breaks them know that he knows.

And still opens the gate.

"Walk the ship with me," he said, loud enough for the recorder this time. "Standard inspection."

"Officer, I have a schedule—"

"You have a schedule that begins when I say it does. Walk."

So we walked. Through the cargo bay where the real filtration media sat in its honest crates, past the galley where I keep a photograph of a woman I don't remember meeting — more on that later, maybe, or maybe never, some doors even I don't open — back to the maintenance deck, where behind a panel I could have opened for him in four seconds, four hundred human lives sat wrapped in foil, waiting to be someone's again.

He stood in front of that panel for a long time. Long enough that I stopped breathing. Long enough that the station clock ticked over twice.

Then he moved on.

* * *

Here's what you need to understand about the memory trade on Meridian, because everyone acts like it's dirty and almost no one asks why. When the founders came through the jump gate eighty years ago, they came running from a war nobody talks about, and they built their shining colony on a simple principle: *we will forget*. Forget the war, forget the enemy, forget whatever it was that made eight ships' worth of people flee across half a galaxy to squat on a pretty green world and call it theirs. Forgetting became law. Grief became contraband. If your grandmother died in the Exodus and left you a chip with her whole heart on it, you were supposed to surrender it at customs and accept an officially approved memory of her instead — stock footage, government issue, grief by committee.

People don't do that. People hide their dead in their walls and their hearts in their pockets, and every few months a battered freighter like mine carries those hearts up through the dark and trades them along the lanes, grandmother to granddaughter, husband to wife, soldier to the family that was told he never existed.

Am I a criminal? Sure. Put it in the file. But I've delivered a mother's lullaby to a woman who's fifty now and hasn't heard it since she was six, and I've seen what that does to a face, and I will stand in any court in this colony and dare them to call it smuggling with a straight face.

They'd convict me anyway. That's the other thing you need to understand about Meridian. But you knew that. Everyone knows that. Nobody says it, because saying things is also becoming illegal, one quiet ordinance at a time.

* * *

Dominic finished the inspection in forty minutes, which for a full walk-through is either slovenly fast or deliberately slow depending on which of us you ask. We ended up back at the airlock, and he handed me my chit, and our fingers touched for exactly as long as the transfer required and not one millisecond more, which is its own kind of conversation.

"Your paperwork's in order," he said.

"My paperwork is *always* in order."

"Your paperwork is a work of art. I mean that sincerely. Whoever forges for you should be a national treasure." He paused. "If such a person existed. Which I'm not alleging."

"No. You're not alleging anything. You're inspecting." I took the chit and let my thumb drag across his knuckle on the way, watching the muscle in his jaw tighten by a fraction of a millimeter. "Anything else, Officer Vale? Final words? A warning? A poem?"

"One question." He glanced at the dock recorder, then stepped closer, inside the hydraulics noise, close enough that I could smell the station coffee on him and see the tiredness he hides under the posture. "Why the beacon run? You've filed beacon-maintenance clearances twice this quarter. Before this quarter, you'd never touched that relay in six years of flying. Why now?"

And there it was. The question under the question. Because he wasn't asking why I'd suddenly taken an interest in a dead piece of orbital hardware. He was asking why I'd chosen the one cover story that put me, regularly and legally, within boarding distance of the most ignored object in Meridian's sky — and whether I knew something about that beacon that he didn't.

Which was funny, because I didn't. I'd taken the route because Marn told me to, and Marn pays better than God and explains less than gravity.

"Habit," I said. "Old girls need looking after. Beacons too."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I've got that's free."

Something moved behind his eyes — frustration, or worry, or that specific exhausted tenderness he gets when he's spent twenty minutes proving I'm guilty and has to decide, again, what to do about it. He looked at me for a long moment. The dock hummed around us. Somewhere below, Meridian turned, green and lawful and lying.

"Clear to depart, Pilot Eastwick," he said finally, loudly, for the recorder. Then, low, just for me: "Corvane's office requested a copy of every beacon-maintenance filing this month. Every one. I don't know why. I don't think I want to know why. But if I were a woman who flew to dead relays for reasons she won't share—" his voice dropped further, "—I'd be careful. Ash Corvane doesn't request copies. He collects them. And he doesn't collect things he intends to leave alone."

For a second I forgot to be charming. That's the trouble with Dominic Vale: he keeps doing that. Wrapping warnings inside inspections, care inside procedure, until you can't tell where the officer ends and the man begins, and neither, I suspect, can he.

"Dominic." I made my voice soft. "Why do you keep letting me go?"

He was already turning away. Over his shoulder, without stopping, without smiling, in that flat customs-officer voice that the recorder would log as pure procedure:

"Because every time I search your ship properly, Eastwick, I'm afraid I'll find what I'm looking for. And then I'd have to arrest you. And then who'd fly you home?"

The airlock cycled. I stood there a beat longer than a professional should, holding a forged chit and a warning and the particular ache of loving a man whose job is to catch you and whose heart apparently hasn't gotten the memo.

Then I sealed the *Regret*, spun her nose at the dark, and burned for the beacon.

Relay Seven hung in high orbit like a rusted tooth, dead these sixty years, blinking its idiot pattern at nothing. No records say who built it. No records say why. I'd serviced it twice this quarter and thought nothing of it — a job's a job, and Marn's credits spend the same as honest ones.

But as I matched velocity with it and ran the umbilical, my cabin lights flickered. Once. Twice. And through the viewport, in the dead beacon's ancient face, one light that had been dark for sixty years

was on.
