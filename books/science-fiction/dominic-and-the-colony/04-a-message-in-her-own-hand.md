## 4. A Message in Her Own Hand

I didn't sleep. I want to be able to say that was a choice — that I sat up like a sensible woman and decided to face the thing — but the truth is my body refused the offer. Every time I closed my eyes I saw my own handwriting on a screen forty years older than my bones.

So I made tea instead. The good kind, the kind I save for bribing myself, and I carried it down to the freighter's belly where I keep the equipment that would get me shot in most jurisdictions: the decryptor rig Marn swears he's never sold me, the faraday cage lined with insulation from a decommissioned navy server farm, and a chair with a broken spring that has learned the exact shape of my spine through long, bad nights just like this one.

The beacon log fragment sat in my queue like an uninvited guest who'd made himself comfortable.

Let me back up. Because if I'm going to tell this honestly — and apparently honesty is the theme of the evening whether I like it or not — you need to understand what I found out there.

The beacon orbiting Meridian is supposed to be dead. Everyone knows it's dead. It's been dead since before the second generation was born; it's a landmark, a navigation fix, a story parents tell children about the founding days when everything was harder and colder and people built things they couldn't explain. I've serviced it eleven times over six years under maintenance clearances that are, shall we say, creatively sourced. Eleven times I've clipped my tether to its hull, run diagnostics on systems that have nothing left to diagnose, replaced seals that don't seal anything, and billed the colonial registry for work nobody checks because nobody cares.

Dead things are safe. That's the whole appeal of them.

On visit twelve, the hull was warm.

Not much. Two degrees above ambient, which sounds like nothing until you know that space doesn't do "a little bit warm." Space is a place where heat either exists or it doesn't; two degrees means power, and power means someone or something is feeding a machine that no record says anyone built. And then there was the maintenance log — a local buffer, buried three layers deep in a partition that my own diagnostic suite insisted had never existed, dated four decades before my birth, signed with a technician's code and a handwritten annotation.

My handwriting. My particular, ugly, left-slanting hand, the one teachers tried to beat out of me and customs officers squint at on forged manifests. There it was, in a log older than my grandmother's ship, reading: *Check the third relay again. It lies.*

The third relay does lie. I've known that for years. I've never told anyone that, because how do you explain that you have opinions about a relay on a dead beacon?

So. Tea. Decryptor rig. Broken-spring chair. Night.

* * *

The fragment decrypted in stages, which felt less like technology and more like a person deciding how much to trust me.

First layer: routine telemetry. Power draw curves, thermal cycling, antenna alignment records. Boring as a sermon, and I almost relaxed. Then the second layer opened, and boring packed its bags and left.

Coordinates. A set of deep-space coordinates rendered in the old founding-era format, the one modern nav suites won't even parse without a legacy adapter. I happen to own a legacy adapter, because I am a woman of many small crimes and at least one of them pays off every few years. I fed the numbers in and let the suite translate.

They pointed at the jump gate.

Not at the gate itself — at a volume of empty space some nine hundred thousand kilometers sunward of it. A patch of nothing. I ran the numbers twice, then a third time with the paranoia cranked all the way up, checking for sign errors, epoch drift, gravitational precession across forty years of stellar wobble. The correction settled the point even closer to the gate's approach corridor. Whatever those coordinates marked, it sat directly in the path something would take coming *through*.

A date was attached. Founding-era calendar, converted cleanly once I told the rig what year convention to assume. I stared at the conversion for a long time before I let myself understand it, because understanding it meant doing arithmetic I very much did not want to do.

The date was next month.

And not just any day next month. I checked against the almanac out of pure spite, the way you check a horoscope hoping to laugh at it. The date fell inside the jump window — the seasonal alignment when the gate's field geometry opens wide enough for mass transit, the window every freight pilot in the colony plans their year around. The window I had three jobs booked into.

Someone had written down, forty years ago, the exact moment something would arrive through the jump gate. And then they'd hidden it inside a machine everyone believed was dead, behind a signature written in my handwriting.

I put the tea down. My hands had developed a tremor I decided to ignore, the way you ignore weather.

There was a third layer. Of course there was a third layer. The decryption prompt for it wasn't a standard cipher handshake — it was a biometric challenge. Old-fashioned genomic key, the kind the founders used for personal archives before everyone agreed that locking your secrets to your own DNA was a great way to make your secrets die with you.

The rig read the sample I keep on file for forging medical clearances. My genome. Mine, as far as I have ever known.

The lock considered me for four full seconds. Long enough for me to think, quite calmly and from a great distance: *it's going to open, isn't it.* Not hope. Not fear. Just the flat certainty of a woman watching a door recognize her in a house she's never lived in.

It opened.

* * *

The text was short. Whoever wrote it had been in no mood for literature, and I recognized the mood — it's the prose style of someone writing something they expect only a corpse or a successor to read.

> If you are reading this, the primary broadcast failed and the failsafe is active. You were compiled from the archive for this purpose. Do not waste time asking who you were before; the answer will not help you.
>
> Coordinates attached. Date attached. When the fleet comes through, it will ask one question. The colony must answer it truthfully or there will be no colony.
>
> Tell them what we took.
>
> Tell them what we took. Tell them what we took. Tell them what we took.

The phrase repeated eleven times. I counted. Then I counted again, because eleven seemed like a number that should mean something, and I hate a number that means something I can't see.

I sat in the broken chair for what felt like an hour and might have been ten minutes, reading it over and over, looking for a loophole. Some alternate reading. *Compiled from the archive* — that could mean lots of things. Records copied. Data backed up. A message forwarded. People use words loosely. There was absolutely a version of this where I was still me.

Except.

You have to understand what my childhood was, or rather what it wasn't. I came up through the foundling intake on Meridian's south ring, no family record, no origin file, which is common enough down there that nobody asks questions — the whole district runs on people whose pasts have convenient holes. I was told I'd been born on a hauler. There was no hauler. I checked once, years ago, drunk and sentimental, and found that the vessel name in my intake papers belonged to a ship scrapped before I was born. I'd laughed it off. Smugglers get born wrong all the time; it's practically a job qualification. A missing past is a professional asset.

I had built my whole self on top of that hole and called the hole a foundation.

*Compiled from the archive.* Grown, the word wants to be. Seeded. Someone took memories — real ones, someone's real life — and wrote them into a child, and set the child loose in the south ring with a talent for flying and a talent for forgery and a left-slanting hand, and waited forty years for a beacon to wake up and hand her a note.

*Do not waste time asking who you were before; the answer will not help you.*

That line made me angrier than everything else combined. Because it was so *practical*. So considerate. Whoever wrote it knew exactly what I'd do — knew I'd tear my own history apart looking for the seam — and had pre-emptively filed the request under *not helpful*, like a note on a door: *gone fishing, don't wait up.* They'd known me before I existed. They'd known me well enough to manage me.

I got up and paced the cargo bay. Three lengths, turn, three lengths back. The freighter hummed around me, all the familiar sounds suddenly auditioning for the role of evidence. How many of my memories were mine? The taste of the tea? The scar on my knee from falling off a loading crane at nine? Was there a nine? Was there a crane? Was the fall itself an implant, some archivist's idea of texture, a detail added so the vessel would feel real to herself?

Here's the thing nobody tells you about vertigo: it isn't the fear of falling. It's the suspicion that the floor was never there.

I thought about the dreams. I know — I hadn't heard about the children yet, not officially, not then. But the port is a throat and everything slides down it eventually, and for two weeks there'd been whispers at the fuel docks about kids waking up screaming, same nightmare, whole families, eleven of them by the last count. Silver ships in the gate. I'd listened the way you listen to ghost stories: entertained, dismissive, glad it wasn't my problem.

Eleven children. Eleven repetitions of *tell them what we took.*

I stood very still in my own cargo bay while that arithmetic finished itself, and I did not like the sum.

* * *

I did what I always do when the universe hands me something unbearable: I worked the problem sideways.

If I was built to deliver a message, the message needed a delivery system. The beacon was the obvious candidate — but the beacon was dead, officially, and whatever warmth I'd felt on the hull, its transmitter array registered as scrap. I pulled my service records for all twelve visits and laid them out chronologically across the workshop table, because sometimes a pattern only shows itself when you make it sit still.

Visit one through nine: nothing. Dead readings, dead readings, dead readings. Visit ten: a fractional anomaly in the antenna impedance that I'd logged and dismissed as sensor noise. Visit eleven: the anomaly again, slightly larger. Visit twelve: warm hull, hidden partition, my own handwriting.

Something had been waking up. Slowly, on a schedule, in step with — I checked the date stamps against the almanac again, hating myself — in step with the closing approach of the jump window. The beacon wasn't a warning light. It was a countdown, and I had been servicing a countdown for six years like a janitor dusting a bomb.

And the log entry. *Check the third relay again. It lies.* I'd laughed at that, the first time. Now I went and looked at the third relay's actual service history, and here is where I'll admit my voice did something embarrassing in the empty bay, because the relay's serial number, in the old founding format, decoded to a batch designation that included a date code.

Forty years ago. Same era as the log. Same era as — I pulled my own intake file, the copy I'd bought off a records clerk years ago and kept out of sentimentality — same era as the earliest date anywhere in my documented existence.

Whoever built the beacon's insides and whoever built me had used the same parts bin. Or the same hands.

I sat back down. The tea was cold. I drank it anyway, because throwing it at the wall would have felt good for one second and wasteful for all the others, and I am, whatever else is true of me, a woman who does not waste cargo.

*Tell them what we took.* Took *what*? The message didn't say. The coordinates said *where*, the date said *when*, but the substance — the actual confession, the thing worth repeating eleven times in a dead woman's careful hand — that was somewhere else. Another partition. Another lock. Maybe another beacon entirely, out along those coordinates in the empty volume near the gate, waiting in the dark for someone to come ask it questions.

Or maybe the rest of it wasn't written down anywhere. Maybe it was in the only archive that had survived intact, compiled and walking around in a patched flight suit, drinking cold tea, refusing to remember on schedule.

*Do not waste time asking who you were before.*

"Bad news," I said out loud, to the writer, to the empty bay, to whichever founder was listening from wherever founders go. "Asking is the only thing I do."

* * *

Dawn shift started in three hours, which meant the port would wake soon, which meant I had decisions to make in the dark while the dark was still available.

Option one: burn the fragment, scrub the rig, fly my scheduled runs, and spend the rest of my life knowing what I know, which is a special circle of hell I wouldn't wish on a customs officer. Option two: take it to someone official, and I actually laughed at that one, alone, in the dark, the way you laugh at option two. Take it to whom? The colonial registry that doesn't check my invoices? The governor's office, currently — per the dock gossip — very interested in a cluster of children having identical nightmares? Hand Corvane's people a confession from the founders and a date matching the dream, and watch how fast the south ring gets locked down and the word *contamination* starts appearing in bulletins?

Which left option three. Go back to the beacon. Find the rest of the message. Figure out what *we took* means before the date arrives, before whatever is on the other side of the gate arrives, before eleven children finish dreaming something the adults aren't ready to hear.

It was a terrible plan. It required forged clearance, an unscheduled flight, and trusting a machine that lied about being dead, written by people who lied about being founders, addressed to a woman who turned out to be lying about being herself. Every instinct I've honed in fifteen years of smuggling said walk away.

But here's the other thing about smuggling, the thing they don't put in the recruitment brochures: you get very good at recognizing contraband. And I was holding the rarest kind there is. Not chips, not memories, not black-market recollections of somebody's honeymoon. This was contraband of an entire *truth* — a whole civilization's secret, sealed in a hull everyone touches and no one reads, with my face on the manifest.

Somebody had gone to extraordinary lengths to make sure this message got delivered. Forty years of patience. A grown human being, assembled like a parcel. And they'd made one mistake, or maybe it wasn't a mistake — maybe it was the whole design — because they'd made the parcel *stubborn*.

I am stubborn. That much of me is mine. I'd bet the ship on it.

I keyed the rig to start copying the fragment — full redundancy, three drives, one of them going into a place on my person I won't describe in mixed company — and as the progress bar crawled, I caught myself talking to it again, quietly, the way you talk to a co-pilot.

"Tell them what we took," I said. "Fine. But somebody's going to tell *me* first."

The progress bar hit ninety percent and stopped. Then, in the diagnostic margin, unbidden, the rig flagged one final item in the fragment's header — a routing tag, the digital equivalent of a return address, pointing not at the beacon and not at the coordinates near the gate.

Pointing at a name. A broker's handle, retired now, legendary in certain disreputable circles, a man who dealt in identities the way I deal in memory chips.

Marn.

Of course it was Marn. It was always going to be Marn. The man who'd sold me my first forged papers at sixteen, who'd winked and called me "the girl with no yesterday" like it was affection and not a diagnosis.

I closed my eyes, and this time, finally, sleep reached up and took me — and the last thought I had on the way down was that tomorrow I was going to have a conversation with my oldest friend in the world, and one of us had known the truth about me for forty years.

I just didn't know which one.
