## 17. Running the Blockade

The freighter I've spent eleven years keeping alive is dying in my hands, and the worst part is that she's doing it politely.

"Port thruster's gone," Dominic says from the copilot's seat, his voice flat in the way it gets when he's rationing panic like water on a long haul. "Coolant line's bleeding into the number two hold."

"I know. I can feel her sulking." I ease the stick left and the *Vesper Kite* rolls with all the enthusiasm of a drunk aunt being asked to dance. "She never liked blockades. Or me. Or you."

Sef laughs from behind us — a small, startled sound, the kind kids make when they're not sure yet whether they're allowed to be scared. She's strapped into the jump seat with Marn's old jacket wrapped around her twice, swimming in it, her chin barely clearing the collar. Eleven years old and she's already seen more of Corvane's detention cells than most colonists see of the sky.

"How far?" she asks.

I check the plot. The beacon hangs out past the shipping lanes like a tooth nobody pulled, and between us and it: nine patrol cutters, a defense grid warming up its teeth, and one very narrow corridor of nothing that Corvane hasn't thought to watch because no honest ship would ever fly through it.

Good thing we're not an honest ship.

"Fourteen minutes," I say. "Give or take how lucky we feel."

* * *

Here's what they don't tell you about running a blockade: it's mostly boredom punctuated by moments where you briefly understand your own mortality as a physical object, something with weight and edges, sitting in your chest.

For the first six minutes it's all boredom. I take us down through the debris shoals off Meridian's north pole — the graveyard of the founding war, hulls older than the colony itself, drifting in slow orbits that customs charts mark with a single dismissive line: *unprofitable*. Corvane's cutters don't patrol here because there's nothing to tax and nothing to catch. Just ghosts and scrap and, if you know the lanes the way I know them, a winding road straight through the middle.

Dominic watches the scopes. He's good at it — better than me, though I'd sooner eat my own flight gloves than say so. Customs training. Ten years of watching other people's ships for a living means he reads sensor returns like I read faces.

"Cutter, bearing two-seven, high," he says quietly. "He's not looking at us."

"He's looking at the decoy."

Marn.

God, Marn. When we'd lifted from the derelict station, his old hauler — the *Charity Case*, a ship so ugly it had been refused by two scrapyards — had peeled away on a different vector, squawking my transponder code, my forged clearances, my whole criminal biography at full volume for any scanner that cared to listen. *Come get me,* the signal said. *I'm the smuggler you want.*

"He'll be fine," Sef says, and I realize I've been silent too long. Kids notice everything. That's the trouble with them. "He said he's been caught forty times and they always just yell at him."

"They yelled at him because he let them catch him," I say. "There's a difference between getting caught and getting caught on purpose." I don't add the rest: that this time, if the cutter decides yelling isn't enough, there won't be a fine or a lecture. Corvane has declared martial law over a poisoned water supply that was never poisoned, and the penalty for smuggling during martial law isn't a cell. It's a vacuum.

Dominic's hand finds mine on the throttle. Brief. Warm. Then back to the scope, because we are professionals, allegedly.

* * *

At minute seven, the boredom ends.

"Elodie." Dominic's voice changes register entirely — drops into the clipped calm he used reading charges to people whose lives he was ruining. "Two cutters. Breaking formation. They've seen through the decoy — Marn's transponder signature doesn't match the mass profile. They know the *Charity Case* is empty."

On the scope, I watch it happen. Two patrol cutters peeling off the picket line, engines flaring, curving toward the debris shoals. Toward us. And beyond them, Marn's fat, slow, beloved-ugly hauler still broadcasting my name to the dark.

"Marn, come in," I say. "They've made you. Break off, go home."

Static. Then his voice, dry as ever, crackling through the interference like he's commenting on weather rather than warships. *"Home? Girl, I sold my home to pay for your mother's fake identity. This is the first interesting thing I've done in twenty years."*

"That's not funny."

*"Nothing about me is funny. That's why I charge so much."* A pause, and when he speaks again the dryness has thinned, and underneath it is the old man who once sat across a cargo bay table from a frightened nineteen-year-old and told her, gently, that the memories she'd bought were real even if the name wasn't. *"Elodie. The beacon core needs a clean power feed to hand-broadcast. You knew that. You know what I'm offering."*

My throat closes. Because I do know. His ship carries a fusion torch rig — salvage-grade, illegal in four jurisdictions, the kind of raw power draw that lights up every sensor on the grid like a bonfire. If he runs it hot, wide open, screaming — every cutter, every gun on Corvane's grid will look at him and see exactly what they're hunting: a smuggler making a desperate run for the beacon.

And whatever comes for him will not be gentle.

"You could still run dark," I say. "Cut the broadcast, drift, they'd lose you in the shoals—"

*"In the shoals with two cutters already on top of me. No."* Something rattles on his end — he's laughing, or coughing, or both; with Marn it was always hard to tell. *"Listen to me. I spent thirty years selling other people's pasts. Memories, records, identities. Beautiful little lies in pretty packaging. This is the first time anyone's ever asked me to carry the truth instead."* A breath. *"I find I'd like to go out freighted properly, for once."*

"Marn—"

*"Four minutes to the beacon window, girl. Fly."*

The channel clicks dead. On the scope, the *Charity Case* turns — slow, ponderous, magnificent — and burns directly toward the two pursuing cutters, transponder blazing, fusion torch flaring like a second sun.

Sef makes a small sound. I turn my head just enough to see her pressed against the restraint, watching the scope, her eyes enormous.

"Is he going to be okay?"

I have lied to customs officers, to governors, to myself, for eleven years. It should be easy.

"Yes," I say, and put my eyes back on the road, and hate myself with a thoroughness that would impress Dominic.

* * *

Minute ten. The shoals spit us out into open black, and the beacon is suddenly *there* — a splinter of metal against the stars, close enough now that I can see the old damage scars, the antenna spars, the dark windows of a structure no record says anyone built. Fourteen years I've serviced that thing. Fourteen years of believing it was a dead relic, a maintenance contract, a way to pay for fuel and keep flying.

It's a confession. And it's waiting for us.

"Grid's tracking," Dominic says. "They've got firing solutions on the beacon approach. Corvane must have pre-sighted it — he knew where we'd have to go." A pause. "Elodie, the defense grid can reach us before we dock."

"Then we don't dock. We crash elegantly." I'm already computing it, the ugly arithmetic of velocity and distance. "Strap her in tight. Both of you. This next part is going to be less flying and more falling with intent."

The grid opens up.

I've dodged customs interdiction fire before — warning shots, mostly, the polite tap of a state that wants you stopped, not dead. This is different. This is a governor who has staked his career, his story, his entire architecture of lies on the beacon staying silent, and the salvos come in tight, disciplined clusters, flak blossoming across our path like white flowers.

Down. Roll. Up. The *Kite* groans through maneuvers she was never built for, and somewhere below decks something lets go with a bang that I feel through the deck plates and in my teeth.

"What broke?"

"Hold two," Dominic calls. "And hold three. We're venting atmosphere — I'm sealing bulkheads." He's out of his seat, moving aft before I can argue, because of course he is. Ten years of customs discipline and the moment the shooting starts he becomes exactly what he always was under the uniform: a man who runs toward the problem.

A flak burst catches us amidships.

The world goes sideways — literally, the *Kite* spinning, alarms shrieking in a chorus, my hands moving on their own because thinking is too slow now, only muscle and instinct and eleven years of loving this stubborn, patched-together piece of junk. I kill the spin. I lose the port engine. I gain, in exchange, a fire in the engineering section and a view through a new hole in the ceiling of the cockpit: stars, unfiltered, beautiful, indifferent.

"We're hit!" Sef's voice, high and steady at the same time, which is somehow worse than screaming. "We're hit but I'm okay, I'm okay—"

"Stay strapped down," I call back, and my voice comes out steadier than I have any right to expect. "You're doing great. Better than the ship."

Through the forward glass, the beacon grows. And behind us — I flick the rear scope, one glance, half a second —

The *Charity Case* is surrounded.

Three cutters now, boxing her in, running her down in the empty black far from anything that matters. Her torch still burning. Still broadcasting my name, my crimes, my whole invented life, loud enough to pull every eye in the system while we crawl wounded toward the truth.

*"Fly, girl."*

I fly.

* * *

The last thousand meters, I stop pretending it's aviation.

I point the *Kite* at the beacon's service cradle and burn everything she has left — one engine, half a thruster array, and a heart condition. The docking clamps were designed for a gentle approach at two meters per second. We arrive at eleven.

The impact goes through the ship like a bell being struck. Everything not bolted down arrives somewhere else very quickly. My harness bites into my shoulders hard enough to leave bruises shaped like regret. Somewhere aft, Dominic swears with a fluency that suggests his customs career wasted him.

Then: silence. The good kind. Docked. Clamped. Alive.

"Report," I manage.

"Ship's a loss," Dominic says, hauling himself back into the cockpit, a cut above his eyebrow painting half his face red. He grins anyway. "But we're here."

"Sef?"

"Okay," comes the small voice from the jump seat, muffled, fierce. "My teeth hurt but I'm okay."

I unbuckle. My hands are shaking now — they waited until the work was done, considerate things. Through the cockpit glass, the beacon's hull fills the view, scarred and ancient and patient, and beyond it the jump gate hangs in the dark like a held breath.

Because it's happening. Even now, even here, I can see it: a shimmer at the gate's throat, the faint silver bloom of transit space opening. The fleet is coming. Exactly as dreamed. Exactly as scheduled. Right on time for a question nobody in this colony wants asked.

"Do you remember us?"

That's the question. Sef gave it to us in the dark of a derelict station, whispering the end of a dream shared by eleven children, and I have carried it since like a coal in my chest.

Well. I bought a lot of memories in my life. Sold a few too, back when I was young and stupid and hungry. But this one — this one I'm going to give away free.

"Elodie." Dominic's hand on my arm. He's looking past me, through the glass, and his face has gone strange. "The cutters. Look."

I look. Out past the beacon, in the middle distance, the *Charity Case* is still burning — still transmitting — and around her, the three cutters have simply... stopped. Holding position. Weapons cold, if the passive returns tell the truth. And their commander is broadcasting, on open channel, a single repeated message that every ear in the system can hear:

*"Stand down. Stand down. The old man's ship is unarmed. Stand down."*

Someone on those cutters heard Marn's transmission. Someone heard the truth in it — or maybe just heard an old smuggler refuse to run, and found something in that they couldn't shoot at.

Or maybe — and this is the thought that makes my skin prickle — Corvane's people are starting to hear the other thing too. The gate shimmering. The fleet arriving. The countdown finishing itself whether the governor likes it or not.

Marn's voice crackles over the open channel one more time, faint, ragged, unmistakably amused:

*"Told you I'd go out freighted properly. Now get off my frequency and go say what needs saying, girl. I'll keep them company."*

I wipe my face with the back of my glove — when did I start crying? — and turn to the airlock, and to the beacon, and to the broadcast core that the founders sabotaged decades ago because they couldn't bear their own confession.

They couldn't say it. So it falls to the woman they built to say it for them.

Except they're wrong about one thing, whoever they were, wherever they went. Nobody built me to choose this. The choosing is the one part that's mine.

"Come on," I say to Dominic and Sef. "Let's go answer the question."
