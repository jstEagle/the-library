## 21. Two Flags, One Sky

The tent they built for the negotiations was bigger than my school, which is not saying much because our school was four rooms and a leaky roof, but still. It was white and round like a dropped egg, and it sat in the middle of Landing Field Three where nothing had landed for eleven years except supply skiffs and one very lost mail drone.

They called it a "neutral venue." I learned that word from Dominic Vale, who explained it to me while we waited outside on folding chairs. Neutral meant nobody's. Which meant it belonged to whoever walked in first and put their cup of tea down.

"The fleet people don't drink tea," I said.

"Then we've already won," said Dominic, and almost smiled. He was getting better at smiling. For a while there he'd been about as good at it as I was at long division, but that was changing. Elodie Eastwick had something to do with that. When she looked at him, his face forgot how to be an officer's face.

I was there because of the dream. We all were — me and Tomas and the Vey twins and little Anouk who was only six and mostly used her dreaming time to complain about being six. Eleven of us had woken up screaming with the same silver fleet behind our eyes, and now the fleet was real, parked at the jump gate like a patient animal, and the grown-ups had decided that since we'd dreamed them first, maybe we could talk to them best.

Which sounds crazy. It was crazy. But everything was crazy by then, so we fit right in.

* * *

The returning people — the colonists kept calling them "the dispossessed" until someone pointed out you shouldn't call people dispossessed to their faces — did not look like monsters. This disappointed some of the adults, I think. Fear needs a shape, and when the shape turns out to be tired-looking people in grey coats with pale eyes and careful hands, fear has nowhere to sit down.

Their envoy was named Ilan Mereth. He was old in a way that made Governor Corvane look like a boy playing dress-up. His skin was thin as paper and his voice was soft, and when he walked into the egg-tent he stopped first at our row of chairs and looked at each of us, one by one, like he was reading a list he'd written himself a long time ago.

"You are the dreamers," he said.

"We're the children," said Anouk, because she was six and hadn't learned diplomacy yet. "The dream part might be over."

Ilan Mereth crouched down so his eyes were level with hers. "Might be?"

"It's been getting quieter," she said. "Like a radio going out of range."

He stayed crouched there a moment too long. Then he stood up, and his hands shook a little, and I understood — not all at once, but the way you understand things in dreams, whole and finished — that he had been waiting his entire life for a radio to go out of range. That the silence we were hearing was the sound of something he'd carried finally being set down.

"Come inside," he said. "All of you. You don't have to say anything. Just be where the words can find you."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about negotiations: they are mostly waiting.

The first day was nine hours of adults saying words like *sovereignty* and *restitution* and *historical record*, and the second day was more of the same but with worse chairs, and somewhere in there I figured out what my job actually was. It wasn't talking. It wasn't even listening, exactly. It was being a door.

When the colonists' lawyers talked about the founding war, the air in the tent got tight, like before a storm. And when the fleet people talked about the homeworld — *our* homeworld, the one under our feet, the one with our school and our leaky roof and Marn's old fence-shop and the orchards on the south slope — the air got tight a different way, tighter, like a fist closing. And every time the air got too tight, one of us would yawn. Or fidget. Or ask for water. And the air would loosen, just enough, because you cannot clench your fists around a treaty when a six-year-old is asking for juice.

Tomas figured out the yawning thing first. He claimed it was accidental. I believe him, mostly, though after the third day he was doing it with suspicious timing.

But the real work happened in the breaks, in the margins, in the parts that never made it into any official record. That was where the dream lived while it faded.

Because here's the thing about the shared dream: it didn't stop all at once. It thinned out, like fog burning off a field. And in the thin places, it started carrying other things. Not warnings anymore. Memories. Their memories, leaking across whatever bridge we'd become, small ones, ordinary ones.

Anouk came back from a break one afternoon and announced, "Their grandmother's kitchen smelled like burnt honey."

Nobody had asked her anything. She just said it, the way kids say things, and half the room went very quiet, and a woman in a grey coat — Ilan Mereth's aide, whose name was Sera and who never smiled except once — pressed her hand flat against her mouth and said, "It did. It does. The hives are still there. Nobody's tended them in sixty years."

"Well, somebody should," said Anouk, with the moral authority of the very young, and went back to her drawing.

That was the day the negotiations turned. Not because of anything in the official record. Because of burnt honey.

* * *

Elodie Eastwick came to see us on the fourth evening, when the tents were emptying and the field was going gold and purple with the long light. She walked with a limp now, and her left arm was in a brace, and she moved like a ship that had been patched rather than repaired — functional, honest about its damage. She sat down next to me on the folding chairs without asking, which I liked. Adults who ask permission to sit near you usually want something.

"How's the hardest job on the colony?" she asked.

"Boring," I said. "Mostly. Except for the parts where it isn't."

"That's most jobs." She looked out at the egg-tent, lit from within now, glowing against the dusk. "You know what I keep thinking? I spent my whole life smuggling memories. Little chips of other people's lives, past checkpoints, under tables. And it turns out the biggest memory shipment ever moved through this system came through eleven sleeping kids and no paperwork at all." She shook her head. "Customs never had a chance."

"Dominic says you're not allowed to joke about customs violations during active negotiations."

"Dominic says a lot of things." But she said it warmly, the way you say things about people you've chosen. Then she got quieter. "Sef. Can I tell you something true?"

"I'm basically a professional truth-teller now," I said. "Go ahead."

"When I found out what I was — that I was grown from their archives, that my whole life was a delivery route — I hated it. For a while. I felt like a letter that thought it was a person." She flexed her braced hand. "But then I watched you in that tent today. And I thought: the difference between a message and a messenger is that the messenger gets to decide how it's delivered. You kids weren't just the dream. You were the tone of voice. Burnt honey. Juice requests. Yawning at the right moments." She smiled, crooked. "You made them human to us before anyone signed anything. That's not in the beacon's programming. That's yours."

I didn't know what to say to that, so I said nothing, which is also a skill I'd gotten good at. But I kept it. I still have it.

* * *

On the sixth day, the dream changed again.

We were all in the tent — the formal session, the one with the recording devices and the stenographer who had given up trying to transcribe Anouk — when it happened. There was no warning. One minute the fleet envoy was reading a statement about land stewardship, the next, all eleven of us went still at the same time, the way water goes still.

I was there and I wasn't. I was standing in the old dream-place, the one with the jump gate full of silver ships, except the ships were different now. Closer. Clearer. And instead of the question — *do you remember us?* — which had hung over every version of the dream like weather, there was something else. A door. Just a door, standing open, in the middle of nowhere, with warm light coming through it.

And through the door: the smell of burnt honey. The sound of bees. A garden gone wild but not dead, fruit heavy on untended branches.

The dream wasn't asking anymore. It was offering.

I came back to myself and Anouk was holding my hand and Sera, the aide who never smiled, was standing, both hands on the table, staring at us like we were sunrise.

"What did you see?" Ilan Mereth asked softly. "What did you see, child?"

"A door," I said. "Open. With your garden behind it."

And Sera said, to the whole silent tent, "The orchard settlement. On the southern continent. It's been empty since the evacuation. We always assumed—" She stopped. Started again. "We assumed we would take it back or burn it. Those were the two plans. Sixty years of planning, and those were the plans."

"And now?" asked Governor Ashgrove — no, wait, that's wrong, the provisional council chair, a round patient woman named Odile Ferran whose name I only learned later because governors were suddenly out of fashion.

"Now," said Sera, and here is where she smiled, the once, "now I think we plant new hives."

* * *

The dream faded completely on the ninth day.

I know because we'd started keeping count, all eleven of us, comparing notes at lunch like it was a game. Tomas reported his version had gone "all fuzzy, like a vid with bad reception." The Vey twins said theirs had split into two different dreams for the first time ever, which they found upsetting until they realized it meant they were becoming separate people again, at which point they found it upsetting anyway, because twins are like that.

Mine went last. Of course mine did. I'd been the one who drew the beacon, back when drawing it nearly got us all disappeared into Governor Corvane's quiet rooms, so maybe I'd earned the long goodbye.

It came at night, in the dormitory they'd given us near the negotiation site, and it wasn't frightening at all. In the last version, the silver fleet was gone from the gate. The gate stood empty and ordinary, and around it, impossibly, there were stars in daylight, and standing in the middle of it all was a woman I somehow knew was my great-great-grandmother — except she had Sera's face, or Sera had hers — and she wasn't saying anything. She was just closing a door. Gently. The way you close a door on a sleeping room.

She looked at me and mouthed something. No sound. But I read it, the way you read things in dreams:

*Thank you for answering.*

And then I woke up, and it was morning, and I remembered an ordinary dream. Something dumb about a dog wearing my boots. I lay there grinning at the ceiling like an idiot, because it was the first dream in months that belonged only to me.

At breakfast, Anouk announced she'd dreamed about flying fish. Tomas had dreamed about failing a math test, which he claimed was worse than the apocalypse. The twins had dreamed entirely different dreams and argued about whose was better.

Eleven children, eleven private worlds. The bridge had come down, and nobody drowned.

* * *

The last thing I'll tell you about the negotiations is the signing, because everyone always wants to hear about the signing, and because it was smaller than anyone expected.

No grand hall. Just the egg-tent, and two documents — one in each script — and Ilan Mereth and Odile Ferran sitting side by side at a table that wobbled because someone had folded a napkin under one leg and nobody wanted to fix it mid-ceremony in case fixing it broke something else, which honestly felt like a metaphor for the whole colony.

Before they signed, Ilan Mereth asked for one addition. A single line. The lawyers checked it three times. It read:

*Let the children sleep untroubled, and let that be the measure of our peace.*

Odile Ferran read it aloud, and her voice caught on *untroubled*, and in the gallery Elodie Eastwick reached over and took Dominic Vale's hand, and he let her, right there in front of everyone, his commission already surrendered and his future unwritten, and I thought: there. That's the other signature. The one that won't be in the archive.

Afterward, outside, the light was doing that golden thing it does over Landing Field Three in late afternoon, and the fleet ships at the gate had begun, slowly, one by one, to turn toward the planet instead of away from it. Not landing. Not yet. Just turning. Facing us.

Anouk tugged my sleeve. "Sef. Look."

A shuttle was descending — small, grey, unhurried — trailing a request-code the tower read out loud for everyone to hear: *envoy transport, requesting permission to visit the southern orchards. Party of twelve. Bringing bees.*

Bringing bees. Sixty years of war plans, and they were coming home with bees.

I watched the shuttle come down out of the gold light, and I felt the last cold place in my chest — the place where the dream had lived, where the countdown had ticked — fill up with something warm and ordinary and brand new.

"Can we go?" Anouk asked. "To the orchards. Can we go see?"

I looked at Dominic, who looked at Elodie, who shrugged with her good shoulder and said, "Ask your parents. Though fair warning — I'm flying the supply runs out there, and I don't leave anyone behind."

So that was yes.

We ran for the chairs to wait for pickup, all eleven of us, yelling about bees and burnt honey and who got to sit next to the window, and behind us the tent glowed, and above us the sky held two flags' worth of people and exactly one sun, and ahead of us —

Ahead of us, the orchards were waiting, and somebody was finally going to tend them.
