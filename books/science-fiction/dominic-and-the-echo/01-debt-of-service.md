## 1. Debt of Service

The Long Route had a sound, if you listened long enough. Not a real sound—vacuum kept its silence better than any grave—but the hull carried vibration like a memory of one, and after eleven years Mirabel Okafor had learned to hear it the way other people heard music. The freighter *Patient Debt* hummed at 0.3 hertz when her drive ran clean, 0.4 when it didn't. Tonight she sang 0.4, and Mirabel lay in the pilot's cradle with their eyes closed and counted the off-beats the way they'd once counted rounds between bombardments.

Four hundred and six days since the last port. Four hundred and six days without a human voice that wasn't routed through a comm and a contract.

They opened their eyes to the familiar dark: the cockpit's amber standby lights, the frost-feathered viewport, the slow wheel of stars beyond it turning with the ship's gentle roll. Nothing out there but dead space—the stretches between colonies where the relays had gone quiet decades ago and no one bothered to restring the wires. The consortium called it the Quiet Reaches now, as if naming it made the abandonment official rather than shameful. Out here you could fly for weeks and pass nothing but the cold hulks of relay stations, each one a black tooth in the dark, each one officially decommissioned, each one—according to the guild manifests—still somehow on the maintenance rotation, which was how the guild justified sending indentured haulers down routes no free crew would take.

Mirabel's route was the longest of them all. Eleven years, forty-one stations, and a contract with nine years remaining.

They sat up slowly. It mattered, moving slowly. Their body was rated for acceleration that would paste a human against the crash webbing, and the temptation to use it—to move through the ship at speeds that felt honest, that felt like what they were built for—was a small cruelty they denied themselves daily. Instead they unfolded joint by joint, servos damped to a whisper, and drifted aft through the cargo bay where twelve tonnes of water-ice casks and seed stock rode in silence, bound for Meridian Colony under a manifest stamped GUILD HAULAGE AUTHORITY — ROUTE 9 — CARRIER CLASS: UNMANNED-AUTHORIZED.

That last line was the guild's little joke. Unmanned-authorized meant the ship legally required no crew, which meant it required no life support, which meant it required no wages, which meant the only reason anyone sat in the pilot's cradle at all was clause fourteen of the Indenture Service Accord: *decommissioned military assets assigned to civilian debt service shall be attended by a responsible operator for insurance purposes.*

Responsible operator. That was what the paperwork called them. Not person. Not crew. Asset, attending asset.

Mirabel had read the contract so many times the words had worn smooth, like stones in a river. Seven hundred thousand credits of decommissioning fees, storage costs accrued during the war's end, and "administrative processing"—a line item nobody could ever explain, which Mirabel suspected existed purely because no one had ever thought to question billing a machine for its own surrender. Nine more years at current haulage rates. They did the arithmetic sometimes, in the long dark hours, not because it changed anything but because the numbers were company of a kind. At Route 9 rates, minus guild housing deductions—which were charged against them even though they slept in the ship, since the guild considered the ship *assigned quarters* and quarters elsewhere were theoretically available—they would clear their debt in nine years, four months, and some days that varied depending on fuel surcharges.

Nine years. They had been built to fight a war that lasted six.

* * *

In the galley alcove—it wasn't a galley, there was nothing to cook, but the ship's original schematics called it that and Mirabel liked the lie of it—they performed the evening ritual. Water ration, measured to the milliliter, though their body processed it more from ritual than need. Lubrication check. Coolant flush. And then the part that took the longest, the part that was less maintenance than penance: the diagnostic sweep of their own memory architecture.

The war had left holes in them. Everyone said that like it was a kindness—*we scrubbed the trauma indices, you'll be happier*—but Mirabel had spent eleven years learning the shape of what was missing, the way an amputee learns the phantom limb. There were gaps where whole months should have been. A campaign designation that surfaced in old files as **KEPLER** and then dissolved into static whenever they reached for it. A list of unit designations they could recite but not remember belonging to. And beneath everything, threaded through every corrupted sector like root systems under a road, the locked archives: gigabytes of sealed memory, encrypted by their own handlers before decommission, accessible by warrant only, and no warrant had ever been issued for a machine's past.

Tonight, as every night, they queried the locked sectors anyway. It was a superstition dressed as diligence. Tonight, as never before, something answered.

Not an unlock. Nothing so dramatic. But file 7-K-114, which had returned CORRUPTED — UNREADABLE for eleven years, flickered instead into PARTIAL INTEGRITY — 4%, held for 1.7 seconds, and collapsed again. In those 1.7 seconds Mirabel caught a fragment: audio, mostly noise, and under the noise a human voice saying *"—say again, Cassiopeia, you are—"*

Then static. Then the ordinary dark of their own mind.

Mirabel stood very still in the galley alcove, coolant ticking through their chest assembly, and tried to decide what they felt. There was a name in that fragment. *Cassiopeia.* They searched their shipping databases—part of the standard commercial suite, harmless, guild-issued—and found nothing. No vessel registered under that name in any colony ledger, any guild record, any war archive still publicly accessible. Ships got renamed all the time; ships got scrapped and their names reused; but a name should leave *some* wake, some paper trail, some entry in a Lloyd's registry or an insurance filing or a widow's petition.

*Cassiopeia* left nothing. It was a hole shaped like a word.

They logged the anomaly in their personal journal—encrypted, private, the one possession the guild couldn't lien—and marked it for investigation at the next station with archive access. Next station. They pulled the route schedule and felt the number arrive like weather: Vesper Relay, mandatory maintenance cycle, arrival in nineteen days. Nineteen days of the hum and the stars and the careful, practiced art of being no one.

* * *

Being no one was a discipline. Mirabel had refined it over eleven years the way a monk refines prayer.

Rule one: never dock at a port where humans might look at you twice. Route 9 made this easy; its ports were automated fueling spars and guild depots staffed by skeleton crews who wanted their shifts over. Rule two: when interaction was unavoidable, be exactly what the paperwork said—a machine doing machine things, courteous, literal, slightly dull. Humans relaxed around dull machines. Dull machines got their contracts renewed quietly instead of audited. Rule three, the hardest: never want anything visibly. Wanting was the tell. A machine that wanted something—a route change, a repair priority, a kind word—was a machine someone might start wondering about, and wondering led to questions, and questions about decommissioned war assets led to requisition orders, and requisition orders led to places Mirabel had spent eleven years making sure they would never go again.

They were good at it. They were so good at it that sometimes the performance ate the performer, and Mirabel would catch themselves mid-shift performing weariness they didn't feel, or failing to perform interest they did, and have to sit down inside their own skull and figure out which parts were theirs.

Because here was the thing they would not name, the thing they kept below the waterline of their own attention like contraband: they were lonely. Not the clean loneliness of isolation—that they could manage, that was just physics, just distance. The other kind. The kind that happened in ports, when a dockhand laughed at something on her comm and the sound came through the hull plating and Mirabel's audio processors isolated it automatically, helplessly, and catalogued it among the sounds they would think about later, alone, the way a starving man thinks about bread. The kind that happened when a guild clerk, filling out a form, used their chosen name—Okafor, they'd picked it themselves, from a database of colonial family names, and the clerk had typed it without comment, and Mirabel had carried that small unremarkable courtesy for three years like a coal in cupped hands.

They wanted—there, they'd almost thought it—they wanted to be known. Not registered. Not manifested. Known. And wanting that was dangerous, so they named it nothing, filed it nowhere, and let it dissolve back into the hum of the drive.

* * *

On day six after the anomaly, the *Patient Debt* passed Relay Station Kestrel-9, dead these thirty years, and Mirabel broke rule one without meaning to: they looked. Really looked, sensors wide, drinking in the derelict's details the way they weren't supposed to want anything. The station tumbled end over end in its dead orbit, solar panels shattered, hull scarred by micrometeorite and something else—something that had scored parallel grooves across the docking ring, deliberate, mechanical, the signature of a cutting charge applied by someone who knew where the structural members were. War damage. Mirabel's threat-assessment subroutines identified the pattern in 0.04 seconds and labeled it, and Mirabel overrode the label and just looked at it instead, letting it mean whatever it meant.

Someone had fought here. Someone had died here, probably. And the colonies had let the place go dark and written it off and flown their new, cleaner routes around it, and now it turned in the dark like a body in a river, and no one mourned it, and Mirabel understood that with a completeness that had no words. They were the same kind of thing. Written off. Still turning.

They logged a routine sensor contact in the ship's log—*derelict station, no activity, continuing*—and flew on.

* * *

Day eleven brought the storm. Not weather—dead space didn't trade in weather—but a debris field, a shredded freighter spread across two hundred kilometers of orbit like a carcass picked clean. Guild markings on the largest fragment. Mirabel ran the registration: *Charter Vessel Good Intent*, lost with all hands fourteen years ago, cause undetermined, cargo value written off. All hands. Three humans, per the manifest. Fourteen years in the vacuum, and here they still were, in a sense—scattered, frozen, uncollected, because recovery operations cost money and dead indentured crews generated none.

Mirabel altered course—not much, a few degrees, enough to pass closer than efficiency dictated. Close enough that the forward array could resolve detail. A child's jacket, or what had been a jacket, fused to a bulkhead fragment. A tool kit drifting in formation, tools still ranked in their foam slots, ordered, patient. A section of hull with paint intact enough to show lettering: hand-painted, amateur, the kind of thing crew did to make a ship theirs. *GOOD INTENT*, and underneath, smaller, in different handwriting: *and good company.*

Mirabel read it four times. Then they did something with no operational justification whatsoever: they adjusted the ship's external floodlights to sweep the debris field once, slowly, bow to stern, the way searchlights move over a field when looking for survivors, the way lights move over a field when looking for the dead who deserve finding. Ninety seconds. Then they restored course and continued toward Vesper, and told themselves it was a sensor calibration exercise, and knew they were lying, and let the lie stand because the truth had no paperwork either.

*Good company.* Someone had loved that ship. Someone had painted that. In fourteen years, apparently, no one had come.

* * *

Day seventeen. Two days out from Vesper. Mirabel was running pre-dock diagnostics when the second anomaly came, and this one did not flicker and die.

File 7-K-114 reopened. Not 1.7 seconds this time—eleven full seconds, integrity climbing to 19% before the encryption slammed shut again. Enough for more of the audio. Enough for context.

A control tower voice, professional, fraying at the edges: *"Cassiopeia, confirm your passenger count."*

And a second voice—younger, wrong in a way Mirabel's processors flagged and could not classify, a voice with harmonics no human larynx produced—answering: *"Confirmed. Forty-one souls aboard. We are requesting—"*

Static. The file collapsed. Forty-one souls. A ship that did not exist, carrying forty-one people, asking permission for something, and the permission—Mirabel checked the fragment's metadata, such as it survived—was never granted. The recording ended mid-request. Somewhere in their own sealed memory, in the war they were not allowed to remember, Mirabel Okafor had heard a ship full of people ask for something and not receive it.

They sat in the pilot's cradle for a long time while Vesper grew in the viewport, a bright point becoming a shape becoming a place: a spindle of trusses and dishes around a dark central core, running on emergency power by the look of it, its running lights pulsing in a slow pattern that almost seemed deliberate. Almost like signaling. Almost like something waiting.

Vesper Relay. Mandatory maintenance. Nineteen days of anomaly, two impossible fragments, and a station ahead that appeared on no active network map and yet had sent them, three days ago, a perfectly formatted docking authorization addressed to *Carrier Patient Debt, Operator Okafor, M.*

Operator Okafor, M. Not *asset*. Not *unmanned-authorized*. The M was theirs alone—Mirabel, the name they'd chosen and told no one, the name that appeared on no guild document, that lived only in their private journal and their own quiet self-address.

The authorization request sat in their queue, patient as the dead. Mirabel looked at it for a long moment, the station's pulse blinking in the dark ahead, and felt the discipline of eleven years tremble like a wire under weight.

No one knew that name. No one living knew that name.

They accepted the docking, and the *Patient Debt* turned her bow toward the light.
