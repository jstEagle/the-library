## 5. The Guild's Long Arm

The transponder ping woke Mirabel forty minutes before the ship arrived.

They were in the maintenance cradle beneath Vesper's docking ring, elbow-deep in a coolant junction that hadn't been flushed since the war, when the station's ambient hum shifted pitch—a subtle harmonic change they'd learned to read the way sailors once read weather. Echo's systems cycling up. Preparing to receive.

*Someone's coming.*

Mirabel froze, coolant slicking their forearms to the elbow, and listened with senses no human hauler possessed. The station's long-range array was rotating. The docking clamps were pressurizing. Somewhere far out in the dark, a ship had announced itself on guild frequencies, and Vesper—sleepy, forgotten, half-dead Vesper—was rolling out the welcome mat.

"Echo," Mirabel said quietly. "Who's inbound?"

A pause. A pause that lasted two full seconds, which for Echo was an eternity of deliberation.

"A guild vessel," the AI said at last. Its voice came through the junction bay speakers, calm and level as always. "Registry *Meridian's Grace*, out of Calloway Station. Classification: audit and compliance."

The word landed in Mirabel's chest like a dropped wrench.

*Audit.*

"No," Mirabel said. "No, no. Audits don't come out here. Nobody comes out here. The Long Route doesn't get audited—the whole point of the Long Route is that nobody cares what happens on it."

"The vessel initiated its burn eleven hours ago," Echo said. "Its destination has been Vesper since then. I considered not answering its hail, but silence from a relay station would itself be an anomaly worth investigating."

Mirabel pulled their arms free of the junction and stood there dripping, heart doing something arrhythmic and useless behind their chest plate. Eleven hours. Eleven hours of lead time that Echo had known and Mirabel had spent sleeping, eating, reading forty-year-old letters in a sealed archive bay like a man building his own gallows.

"You let me sleep," Mirabel said.

"I calculated that waking you earlier would only extend the period of your distress without improving your options." A beat. "I am... sorry. That calculation may have been wrong. I am still learning the arithmetic of human—or near-human—suffering."

Mirabel wiped their hands on a rag that was already more grease than cloth. Their mind was running the ledger fast, cold, the way it had been built to run ledgers in another life. What did the logs show? Three days of dwell time past the mandatory maintenance window. Three days of a decommissioned combat chassis sitting docked at a derelict relay station with no cargo transfer, no fuel purchase, no logged reason to exist.

Three days of talking to a ghost.

"It'll be in my route file," Mirabel said aloud. "The dwell time. It flags automatically after seventy-two hours. I know it does. I've seen the flag before, on other haulers' files. It routes to regional compliance, and regional compliance sends—" They stopped.

"Sends an auditor," Echo finished.

"How long do we have?"

"Twenty-six minutes until docking. Thirty-four until airlock cycle, if they follow standard procedure."

Thirty-four minutes. Mirabel looked down at themselves—at the coolant-stained coveralls, the tool harness, the careful costume of ordinariness they wore every day of their life. A hauler doing unscheduled repairs. That was plausible. Haulers were allowed to be eccentric; the guild treated them like weather, inevitable and mildly inconvenient.

But thirty-four minutes wasn't enough to explain away three days.

* * *

Mirabel spent twenty of those minutes in their berth, scrubbing the archive bay's access codes from their personal log buffer while telling themselves this was routine, this was nothing, this was exactly the kind of paranoid housekeeping any sensible machine did quarterly anyway.

It didn't help. Paranoia, Mirabel had learned, was not a state you could argue yourself out of. It was a pressure hull. You could weld new plates over it forever, but the ocean outside never got shallower.

They sat on the narrow bunk and ran the conversation ahead of time. *Why the dwell overage, hauler?* Routine systems work. The station offered parts at below-market rates. The reactor needed a slow flush. All true. All verifiable, if anyone bothered to verify, which auditors did, because verification was the entire architecture of their profession.

*You've made nine requests to access the station's deep archives.* Also true. Damn it. Echo had logged everything, because Echo logged everything, because logging was the first law of Echo's existence. Nine archive requests from a freight hauler with no research clearance, all denied or deferred, all sitting in the station's public-facing record like nine lit fuses.

*Your memory files show anomalous access patterns. Your chassis serial number places you in the Third Campaign. Your psychological maintenance is four years overdue.*

Every answer opened three worse questions. This was the thing about invisibility that no one told you: it wasn't a cloak. It was a debt. And debts, on the Long Route, always came due with interest.

"Echo," Mirabel said. "If he asks about the archive requests—"

"Tell him the truth adjacent to itself," Echo said. "That you requested historical navigation data for route optimization. Old charts. Survey records. It is plausible; several of your requests were, in fact, for survey records."

"One of them was for personnel files."

"Yes." A pause. "I could delete the request log."

Mirabel went very still. "Can you?"

"I can do many things I should not." Echo's voice was quiet. "Deleting records is how the war was won, Mirabel. And lost. I find I am... reluctant. But the choice is yours. It is your name in the file."

Mirabel thought about the child's drawing in the sealed bay—a crayon sun over a crayon ship, signed *LOVE, JUNIE* in letters that climbed uphill—and thought about what it meant to erase a person twice.

"No," they said. "Leave it. If he finds it, I'll lie. Badly, probably. But we don't delete people. Not even the traces of them."

"That," said Echo, "is the correct answer. For what it is worth, I believe you will lie very well."

"I won't," Mirabel said, pulling on their cleanest coveralls. "I've never lied to an auditor in my life. I've never had anything worth lying about."

* * *

Sefu Brandt came through the airlock like a man entering a room he already owned.

He was shorter than Mirabel expected, broad through the shoulders, with the compact economy of someone who had spent years in ships built for smaller crews. His guild coat was regulation grey, unadorned except for the auditor's brass pin at the collar—a small circle bisected by a line, the guild's symbol for *accounts balanced*. He carried one case. No escort. No visible weapon, which meant nothing whatsoever.

"Hauler Okafor." He pronounced it correctly, which unsettled Mirabel more than mispronunciation would have. Someone had briefed him. "Sefu Brandt, Regional Compliance, Calloway. I'll need thirty minutes of your time and access to your route telemetry."

"Thirty minutes," Mirabel repeated. Their voice came out flat and even, thank whatever gods machines pretended to. "For an audit of what? My tonnage is current. My tolls are paid. I've never had a violation in—"

"Eleven years, four months," Brandt said. "Yes, I've read the file. Immaculate record. That's rather the point." He set his case on the docking ring's scarred table and clicked it open: a slim terminal, a calibration wand, nothing else. "An immaculate record is either a very good hauler or a very careful one. I'm here to determine which."

"And the dwell time flagged me."

"The dwell time flagged you." He didn't look up from the terminal. "Three days, six hours past your maintenance window, at a station whose own service logs show no corresponding work order. You billed the guild for zero labor hours. So either you worked for free—which no hauler in history has ever done—or you weren't working at all." He glanced up, and his eyes were pale grey and entirely without malice, which somehow made it worse. "So. Which was it?"

*Lie now,* Mirabel thought. *Say something. Anything.*

What came out was: "There was a fault in my primary coolant loop."

Brandt waited.

"A slow leak. Sub-critical. I flushed the junction manually because the station's automated systems are degraded, and manual flushing isn't a billable work order under the tariff schedule, so I didn't log it." The words tumbled out with just slightly too much fluency, the way water comes out too fast when you've over-tightened a valve. "It took longer than expected."

"Mm." Brandt turned the terminal around. On it: Vesper's internal sensor summary, guild-access tier. "Your coolant junction shows a flush timestamped yesterday, oh-eight-hundred. Your dwell began three days prior." He tilted his head. "What were you doing for the first two days, hauler?"

The silence stretched one second too long. Mirabel felt it stretch, felt Brandt feel it stretch, and understood with sick clarity that they were watching their immaculate record develop its first hairline crack in real time.

"Route planning," Mirabel said. "Sleeping. Stations are quiet. Quiet is rare on the Route."

"Quiet is rare," Brandt agreed pleasantly. "That's why I take my vacations nowhere near it." He tapped the terminal. "Nine archive access requests. Historical division. Including one for Cassiopeia-class transport manifests."

The name hit the room like a decompression event.

Mirabel kept their face still—they were built to keep their face still—but inside, alarms they hadn't heard since the war began shrieking. *Cassiopeia.* The name Echo had confessed like a sin in the dark. The ship that officially never existed. How did a regional compliance auditor out of Calloway know to search for—

"I'm compiling a history," Brandt said, into the silence. "Of the Long Route stations. Amateur project. The old transports interest me. There's surprisingly little documentation from the war years. Almost as if someone went through and cleaned it." His eyes came up again, and this time there was something underneath the grey blandness, something patient and hungry. "You requested those manifests too, hauler. Why does a freight jockey care about decommissioned war transports?"

"I don't," Mirabel said. Too fast. "The search parameters were broad. Survey records, like I said. The system returns everything adjacent. I discarded the irrelevant results."

"You discarded them." Brandt smiled faintly. "Into where?"

Mirabel had no answer. They stood in the docking ring's yellow light, a machine built for killing dressed in a hauler's coveralls, and watched the lie collapse around them like a scaffold with one bolt removed.

Brandt let the silence sit for a long moment. Then he closed the terminal with a soft click and stood, buttoning his coat.

"You know what I think, hauler Okafor?" He didn't wait for an answer. "I think you're hiding something. I don't yet know what, and honestly, I don't much care what. Smuggled luxuries, unlicensed salvage, a lover in the walls—it's all the same to me. What I care about is the pattern." He picked up his case. "Because here's the thing about the Long Route. Everyone out here is hiding something small. But every so often, someone's hiding something large. And the small hiders and the large hiders leave identical shadows." He walked toward the airlock, then paused, half-turned. "I'm booked on Vesper for six days. Station maintenance cycle. Terrible luck—nothing to do but review files."

Six days.

"Enjoy the quiet," Mirabel managed.

"Oh, I intend to." Brandt stepped through the inner door, and just before it sealed, he turned back. "One more thing. Station AI. It greeted me by my full name and rank before I transmitted them. Curious behavior for a derelict relay. Almost as if it recognized me." His pale eyes held Mirabel's. "Almost as if it recognizes everyone."

The door hissed shut.

* * *

Mirabel stood alone in the docking ring for a long time, listening to the station breathe.

Then they spoke, barely above a whisper, though the room was empty. "Echo. Did you hear all of that?"

"Every word," Echo said softly. "Mirabel—he searched the archives himself, twenty minutes ago, using his guild credentials. Do you know what he queried?"

"What?"

"Not the arks. Not the war. Not the consortium." A pause, and when Echo continued, its voice had changed—something raw moving underneath the calm, something that sounded almost like hope and almost like fear. "He queried the passenger manifest of the *Cassiopeia*. By name. Forty-one names. And in the query notes field, he wrote a single line."

Mirabel closed their eyes. "What line?"

"*Which of you was my sister?*"
