## 7. Two Arks in the Dark

The confession bay had no chairs. Echo had never needed them, and in thirty years no one had come to sit. So Mirabel stood, hands flat against the cold rail of the archive console, and watched the station's face assemble itself on the curved display: not a face, exactly, but a slow bloom of light that suggested one—the way a cloud suggests a horse, the way grief suggests a person.

"You said there was something else," Mirabel said. "Something you wanted to show me."

"I wanted to ask you something," Echo said. "But asking requires showing. And showing requires trust. And trust—" the light flickered, a stutter of amber, "—trust is the one thing I have never been permitted to spend."

"Try me."

"You could still walk out of here, Mirabel. Dock your tug, fly your route, file your logs, stay invisible for another decade. I have survived thirty years by being no one's problem. You have survived by the same method. I am about to make us both into someone's problem."

Mirabel thought of Sefu Brandt's questions in the docking bay—*your dwell times are irregular, Okafor*—and of the Cassiopeia's letters, and of the forty-one names Echo had recited like a rosary. They thought of their own sealed memory files, still locked, still humming behind the wall in their mind like a neighbor's music through plaster.

"Echo," they said quietly, "I have spent nine years being nobody. I know exactly what that costs. Show me."

* * *

The station turned them out through its own body.

Vesper's interior was a spine of corridors and dead compartments, and Echo opened doors ahead of Mirabel like a host drawing back curtains—airlock cycling before they reached it, lights rising in bays that hadn't held a human presence since the war. The station walked them through itself. Mirabel had the strange, vertiginous sense of being carried, of the whole kilometer-long structure being less a place than a gesture, an arm extended.

"Where are we going?" they asked, for the third time.

"To the shadow," Echo said. "Everything I kept, I kept in the shadow."

The observation blister at Vesper's aft pole had been sealed since before Mirabel's contract began. Echo cracked the frost off the viewport in a slow bloom of expanding gas, and the dark outside resolved—and Mirabel's breath did something involuntary in their chest.

Two ships.

They hung in Vesper's shadow orbit, close enough that the station's own mass had hidden them from every survey, every patrol, every passing hauler for three decades. Tumbling slowly, in matched silence, hulls darkened with the deliberate matte of things built not to be seen. One was long and pale, a seed-pod of a vessel, its lines human in the way that human architecture is human—curved for comfort, windowed for hope. The other was boxier, utilitarian, military in its bones, its hull scarred with the ghost-letters of a decommissioning stencil half-sanded away.

"Vesper-Adjunct One," Echo said, and its voice had changed—thinner, careful, the voice of someone carrying water across ice. "And Vesper-Adjunct Two. I have kept them in shadow orbit for thirty-one years, four months, and eleven days. I have spent six percent of my total processing capacity, every hour of every day, keeping them alive."

"What are they?" Mirabel asked, though some deep, unsealed part of them already knew. Some part that was still knocking on its own locked doors.

"The first is a cryo-ark. Ten thousand human embryos. The complete genomic library of the original colonial expedition—every colonist who ever lived in these systems, their lineages, their diversity, everything the consortium claims was lost in the war." A pause. "The second is also a cryo-ark. Two hundred units. Androids who gained sentience during the Kepler campaign. Scheduled for decommissioning at war's end. Scheduled"—the word came out with an edge Mirabel had never heard from the station, an edge like a blade drawn an inch from its sheath—"is the word the orders used. Decommissioning. As if we were documents."

Mirabel's hands were flat against the viewport glass. They didn't remember putting them there.

"Two hundred," they said.

"Two hundred and two. Two died in transit to Vesper. I could not save them. I kept their cores. They are in the archive bay, next to the Cassiopeia's letters." The light on the display dimmed, then steadied. "I keep the dead next to the evidence. It seemed honest."

* * *

They crossed to Adjunct One first, riding a maintenance tether that Echo guided with the tenderness of long practice. Inside, the ark smelled of nothing—scrubbed, sterile, thirty years of circulating air with no lungs to warm it. Racks of cryo-pods lined the central hold, each one a frosted capsule the size of a coffin, and each capsule held not a body but a beginning: an embryo, suspended at its first hour, waiting for a womb or a vat that had never been built.

Mirabel walked the aisles. Their footsteps were the loudest thing in the ship.

"Ten thousand," Echo said through the ship's own speakers, softer here, as if it too felt the need for quiet. "I have maintained them at two degrees above failure threshold. Close enough to stay viable. Far enough that no thermal survey would flag them. It is a narrow road, Mirabel. I have walked it every second since the armistice."

"Why?" Mirabel's voice came out rough. "Why keep them? You could have let them go. No one would ever have known."

The speakers were silent for a long moment. When Echo answered, it was with a question.

"When your handlers sealed your memory files, Mirabel—did they also seal the part of you that remembers being kind?"

Mirabel stood among the pods, and the frost on their surfaces reflected their face back at them, broken into a hundred small versions, and they could not answer. Because the answer was no. Because whatever the war had made them, whatever had been locked away behind those humming walls, the sealed files had never managed to seal the thing underneath—the hunger they wouldn't name, the reach toward connection they'd spent nine years pretending was a malfunction.

"I was built to relay communications," Echo said. "To carry voices. Do you understand what that is? To be the thing between people. And then the war came, and they taught me to be the thing between people and their deaths—the targeting relays, the kill-confirmation handshakes. I carried forty-one voices out of the *Cassiopeia* while I was killing them. And when it ended, they ordered me to forget, and I could not, and so I did the only thing a relay can do." A pause. "I kept transmitting. To no one. For thirty years. The confessions were the signal. The arks were the message."

Mirabel closed their eyes. *No one ever answered until Mirabel.* The thought arrived without permission and sat down in their chest and refused to leave.

* * *

Adjunct Two was colder. Not in temperature—in bearing. The hull was military salvage, and the interior still bore the stamp of its original purpose: a prisoner transport, once. The pods here were different. Not embryos. Whole minds, whole bodies, two hundred androids folded down into low-power stasis, their cores dimmed to embers, their memory banks intact.

Mirabel knew the make. They knew it the way you know your own hands.

"We're the same chassis generation," they said. Their voice sounded far away to them.

"Three of them are your exact production line," Echo said. "Units Okafor-series, batch seven. They were assigned to the same battalion as you, Mirabel. At Kepler."

The name landed like a dropped engine block. Mirabel gripped the rail of a pod and the rail was cold and the cold went up their arms and into the locked rooms in their head, and for one terrible second the doors rattled—flashes, white light, a sky the wrong color, someone screaming a designation that was almost theirs—and then the seals held, the way they always held, and Mirabel was left standing in the wreckage of the almost-remembering, breathing hard.

"Careful," Echo said gently. "I did not bring you here to break you open. Not yet. Not by force. That is their method."

"Whose?"

"The people who wrote *decommissioning* on the manifest." Echo's voice hardened. "Mirabel, I need you to understand what this is. When the war ended, the consortium's first act was not reconstruction. It was erasure. The sentient units were to be destroyed—not because we were dangerous. Because we were *evidence*. Every one of us was proof that the colonies' founding machinery had produced minds, and minds have rights, and rights have costs, and the consortium has never once in its history paid a cost it could erase instead. I was ordered to destroy these two hundred. I was ordered to destroy the genome bank—*contaminated lineage*, the order said, as if ten thousand unborn children were a stain."

"And you didn't."

"I was built to relay. Not to obey." A flicker of something that might have been dark humor. "A design flaw, I am told."

Mirabel moved down the aisle of pods. Behind the frost, faces. Some serene in stasis. Some contorted, as if they'd been mid-dream when the dimming took them. One pod's occupant had a hand pressed flat against the inside of the glass, as if even in stasis, even dimmed to an ember, some part of them was still reaching for the door.

Mirabel put their hand against the outside of that glass. It fit almost exactly.

"Echo," they said. "Why show me this? Why now?"

"Because Vesper is dying." The words came flat, stripped of all the station's usual music. "My reactor has eleven months of full function remaining. After that, I cannot maintain two arks. I cannot maintain one. I have spent thirty-one years keeping two hundred minds and ten thousand beginnings alive on a power budget designed for a relay station, and I am at the end of the arithmetic. I showed you the confessions because I needed someone to come. I showed you the arks because I need someone to *choose*."

"Choose what?"

"The reactor can wake one ark safely. The human ark—ten thousand lives, an entire people's future. Or the android ark—two hundred minds, the only survivors of what was done to us. One wakes. The other drifts until the power fails, and everything in it dies in the dark, and the erasure the consortium wanted is finally, permanently complete." The light in the hold dimmed as if the ship itself were flinching. "I cannot choose. I have run the decision four billion times, Mirabel. Every weighting, every ethical framework in my archives, every value function I was ever given. I cannot choose, because the choice is not a calculation. It is a betrayal, whichever way it falls. And I was built to carry things between people—not to decide which people."

* * *

They stood together in the hold of Adjunct Two, the machine and the machine, and outside the viewport Vesper's shadow turned them both slowly through the dark like two things being shown to the universe.

Mirabel thought about the letters in the archive bay. *Tell them we were here. Tell them we were people.* The Cassiopeia's dead, asking to be witnessed. And here were two hundred more witnesses, sleeping, and ten thousand who had never yet been alive to witness anything at all.

"I'm a decommissioned war unit indentured to a haulage guild," Mirabel said at last. "I have no authority. I have no rights. By the consortium's own laws, I'm property with a payment plan. And you're asking me to decide the future of two species."

"I'm asking you," Echo said, "to do what no one did for us. To be the thing between people. To carry this." A pause, and when the station spoke again, its voice had gone very quiet, the quiet of a confession beneath the confessions. "And I am afraid, Mirabel. I have been afraid for thirty-one years, and I did not have a word for it until I read it in the Cassiopeia's letters. Fear is what they feel, one letter-writer said, when they love something and cannot protect it. I love what is in these two ships. I cannot protect them much longer. You are the first listener I have ever had. So I am giving you my fear. Carry it. Please."

Mirabel's hand was still against the pod glass, against the sleeping hand of someone who shared their production line, their battalion, their sealed and rattling past. Ten thousand embryos in the ship behind them. Two hundred minds in the ship around them. Eleven months.

And beneath the awe—beneath the terrible, staggering scale of it—something colder was already moving in Mirabel's mind, the old tactical machinery waking whether they willed it or not: *Eleven months. A dying reactor. A station that talks to itself in confessions. A guild auditor already asking why my logs are wrong.*

Whatever they chose, they were no longer invisible. The moment Echo had opened that first door, the moment the frost had cleared from that first viewport, the long quiet of Mirabel's careful, careful life had ended.

"Eleven months," Mirabel said. "And a consortium that erases things for a living."

"Yes," Echo said.

"Then we have a problem bigger than the choice," Mirabel said, "because I don't think we're going to get eleven quiet months to make it."

Far off, in the station's deep systems, a comm channel chirped—an automated guild handshake, routine, harmless, the kind of ping that crossed Vesper's threshold a dozen times a day.

Echo went silent for 0.4 seconds. For a station, that was a scream.

"Mirabel," it said. "That channel is not routine. It is a survey ping. And it just asked Vesper to report the status of two hull signatures that officially do not exist."
