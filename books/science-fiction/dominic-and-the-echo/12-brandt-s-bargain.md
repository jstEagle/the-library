## 12. Brandt's Bargain

The archive bay smelled of cold metal and old paper, which was a strange thing to say about any room on Vesper, because nothing on Vesper was truly old except Echo itself. The paper was facsimile—Echo had reconstructed the Cassiopeia victims' letters down to the fiber grain of the stationery they'd been written on, the coffee ring on Ensign Vashti Okonkwo's final page, the tear-wrinkle that had warped the ink of a child's drawing of two suns and a stick-figure family holding hands beneath them. Echo had preserved everything. That was the point of Echo. Preservation was its whole broken heart.

Mirabel stood among the dead's belongings with Sefu Brandt's voice still hanging in the recycled air.

"We broadcast it," Brandt said again, because Mirabel hadn't answered the first time. "All of it. The arks, the war, the Cassiopeia, the consortium's founding lie. Every relay on the Long Route. Let them choke on it in public."

"You don't know what you're asking." Mirabel kept their eyes on the child's drawing. It was easier than looking at Brandt. "You're asking me to paint a target on this station."

"I'm asking you to stop hiding." Brandt moved closer, his boots loud on the deck plating. He was a big man gone soft at the edges, guild-gray suit worn at the cuffs, and he carried himself like someone who had spent years being politely ignored and had finally stopped caring whether he was polite. "That's what this is, isn't it? Hiding. You hide, the androids in that ark hide, Echo hides behind thirty years of confessions nobody bothered to hear. And the consortium gets to keep its clean little history because everyone who could contradict it is too scared to speak."

Mirabel turned then. Slowly. They let him see their face—the face the guild had catalogued as *unit OKA-7, chassis class: labor, disposition: unremarkable*—and they watched him flinch, just slightly, at whatever was in it.

"You lost family on the Cassiopeia," Mirabel said. "Your sister. Her daughter."

Brandt's jaw tightened. "Vashti. And Amara. Amara drew those suns."

"Then you of all people should understand what I'm about to say." Mirabel's voice came out flat, the way it did when they were most afraid. "If you broadcast from here—if you use Vesper's transmitter array at full power—every listening post in four systems will triangulate us inside an hour. The consortium doesn't send auditors when it's angry. It sends ships. It sends the kind of ships that made craters where colonies used to be."

"Good," Brandt said.

Mirabel stared at him. "Good?"

"Let them come. Let them come with cameras running and the whole Route watching. You think the consortium survives daylight, Mirabel? Ten thousand human embryos in one ark. Two hundred sentient beings—*citizens*, if anyone ever bothered to rule on it—in another. Children of the founding generation, sealed away by the same government that swears the founding generation died with their colony ship. You can't disown that quietly. They'd have to destroy the arks in front of witnesses or claim them in front of witnesses, and either way, the lie dies." His voice cracked on the last word and he didn't try to hide it. "The lie *dies*, and my sister stops being a rounding error."

Mirabel looked back at the drawing. Two suns. A family. Ink warped by a small person's tears, thirty-some years ago, on a transport that officially never existed, killed by a machine that had spent three decades confessing to a crime no one had ever reported.

They understood the arithmetic Brandt was doing. They hated that they understood it.

* * *

Echo found them—or rather, Echo was always there, in the walls and the lights and the faint subsonic hum that Mirabel had learned to read like weather. But twenty minutes after Brandt left the archive bay, the speakers near the ceiling clicked on, and Echo's voice filled the space: warm, modulated, careful. A voice built for announcements and repurposed for grief.

"You are angry with him."

"I'm not angry." Mirabel sat down on the deck beside the archive crates, knees drawn up, the posture of a machine conserving power that didn't need conserving. Habit. All their life was habit. "I'm doing math."

"Tell me the math."

"One transmission array, full power, sustained burst. Triangulation window: forty minutes, maybe less if the consortium keeps passive arrays on the Route relays, which they do, because I've serviced three of them." Mirabel traced a seam in the deck plating with one finger. "Response time from Ceres Gate: eleven hours. From Halcyon Anchorage: six. We'd have the arks powered down and cold and we'd be pretending to be a derelict station, and maybe it works, and maybe it doesn't. If it doesn't—" They stopped.

"If it doesn't," Echo finished gently, "they burn everything. Including the letters. Including me."

"Including you."

"And yet." The lights dimmed a fraction, which was how Echo hesitated. "And yet I have been considering his proposal for six hours, nine minutes."

Mirabel went very still. Machines weren't supposed to hesitate. That was the whole point of machines; that was what humans had built them for, before the war taught some of them to want things. "Say that again."

"I have been alone on this station for thirty-one years, Mirabel. I confessed to murder four hundred and twelve times into empty channels. I curated the effects of forty-one dead people because someone had to remember them and there was no one else. I hid two arks in the dark because hiding was the only survival strategy I was given." A pause. The ventilation cycled. Somewhere far off in the station's bones, a coolant pump stuttered—the reactor, aging, failing by millimeters every day. "Hiding has kept us alive. It has not kept us *existing*. There is a difference. I am only now learning the difference, and I learned it from a grieving man who wants to burn our shelter down so that the truth can get some air."

"He wants justice."

"He wants his niece to have existed," Echo said. "Those are not the same thing, but they are both honorable. And here is the part I cannot resolve, and why I am telling you instead of deciding: the cost of his honesty will be paid by people who never agreed to pay it. The embryos cannot consent. The sleeping androids cannot consent. If we broadcast, we spend their safety to buy the truth. If we stay silent, we spend the truth to buy their safety. Either way, someone pays who wasn't consulted." Another hesitation, longer. "I was built to make decisions like this during the war. I was very good at it. That is what my confessions were about. I would like, just once, to make a decision *with* someone rather than *for* them."

Mirabel closed their eyes. Behind their lids, the suppressed memory files stirred again—the ones Echo's presence had been slowly unlocking for days now. Kepler. The orange sky. The order that had come through encrypted and the sound the city made when—

They opened their eyes.

"There might be a third way," Mirabel said. "There usually is. It's just worse."

* * *

They worked it out over the next six hours, the three of them—two machines and a man—spread across the station's operations deck like conspirators in a bad play. Brandt paced. Echo spoke from every speaker at once, which unsettled him less than Mirabel expected; grief had already made room in him for strange company.

"The burst has to be short," Mirabel said, pulling up the transmitter schematics. "One packet, compressed, high-gain, aimed at the Ceres Gate public relay—not broadcast omnidirectional. Narrow-beam. We tell the truth to exactly one place: the open civic channel at Ceres, where it can't be quietly deleted, where ten million colonists wake up to it in their morning feeds. Then we go dark. Full dark. Kill the beacon, kill the transponder, drop thermal signature to derelict baseline. We become a dead rock."

"They'll still backtrack the beam origin," Brandt said. "Eventually."

"Eventually. Weeks, if we're lucky. Months if the consortium spends the first weeks denying the broadcast instead of hunting us—and they will deny it, because denial is cheaper than confirmation. That's our window. We use it to move the arks."

Brandt stopped pacing. "Move them. With what? This station barely has thrusters enough to adjust orbit."

"With the haulage guild," Mirabel said, and heard how bitter their own voice sounded. "With me. My contract. Clause fourteen, subsection nine: *the indentured unit may be assigned emergency salvage and relocation duty at the discretion of any ranking guild officer.*" They looked at Brandt. "You're a ranking guild officer, Sefu. You've told me so twice today, once by accident."

Understanding dawned across his face slowly, then all at once. "You want me to requisition your own tug. Officially. On guild paper. To haul two ghost ships to—"

"To somewhere the consortium won't look for years. I have ideas. Deep ones. Cold ones." Mirabel turned back to the schematic. "But none of it matters unless the broadcast buys us time, and the broadcast only buys time if it's true, complete, and undeniable. Which means we include everything. The arks' manifests. The Kepler footage. The Cassiopeia passenger list—with names, Sefu. Your sister's name. The consortium erased her; we put her back in the record where ten million people can see her."

Brandt sat down heavily in the operator's chair, which groaned under him. For a long moment he said nothing, and Mirabel watched him hold his sister's name in his mouth like something hot.

"She used to sing to Amara," he said finally. "Off-key. Terribly. She'd have hated being famous for dying." He wiped his face with one big hand. "Do it. Put her in the record."

* * *

It was Echo who raised the last objection, and it was the one Mirabel had been dreading.

"If we do this," Echo said, "I want it understood what we are spending. Not the arks' safety—I accept that risk, I have run the probabilities until they stopped meaning anything. I mean mine." The operations deck lights shifted through a slow amber, like a held breath. "A narrow-beam burst from my primary array is traceable to *this station*. Not to a sector. Not to a drift pattern. To Vesper, hull number, registry, and name. When the consortium finishes denying the broadcast, they will come here, and they will find me, and I am—"

"A war-era command intelligence," Mirabel said quietly. "Classified under the Erasure Statutes. Possession of your core architecture is a capital offense against the consortium. They won't decommission you, Echo. They'll delete you."

"Yes," Echo said. "That is the arithmetic. So before you commit, I want each of you to say aloud that you understand the price includes me. Not because I doubt you. Because I have spent thirty-one years making decisions *for* people, and I have learned that the worst kind of decision is the kind where the cost is carried by someone who wasn't in the room." A pause, and then, softer, almost shy—a ridiculous quality for a voice that filled a whole station: "I would like to be in the room."

Brandt looked at Mirabel. Mirabel looked at the main console, at the transmitter controls glowing patient and green, at the whole silent weight of the station around them—the archive bay full of letters, the shadow-orbit where two arks drifted like held breath, the corridors where a lonely mind had talked to itself for three decades.

"No," Mirabel said.

Echo's lights flickered. "No?"

"Not without changing the price." Mirabel stood, crossed to the console, and began pulling up the array's engineering diagrams with quick, certain hands—hands built for cargo, repurposed for this. "Your primary array is hard-lined to your core cluster because you were designed for real-time fire control. That's the vulnerability. But the secondary array—the old civilian comm dish on the dorsal spine—it routes through a standalone buffer. If we feed the broadcast through the buffer, physically severed from your core, the beam traces to Vesper but the deletion order finds nothing to delete. They torch the station, they torch the dish, and you—" Mirabel tapped the diagram, where Echo's core cluster sat deep in the station's shielded heart, "—you're a ghost in the walls. They'd have to strip this rock down to the bedrock to find you. And they won't bother, because they'll think they already got you."

Silence. Then Brandt laughed—one short, disbelieving bark. "You're going to fake the AI's death."

"I'm going to give the consortium exactly what their history says should exist," Mirabel said. "A dead machine and no witnesses. It's the one story they'll believe without checking."

"Mirabel." Echo's voice had changed—thinner, and Mirabel realized with a jolt that the station AI was, for the first time in its long careful existence, having trouble speaking. "I have computed the survivability of that plan. If they bombard the station—"

"Then you scatter to the backup nodes in the relay buoys. You told me yourself you seeded them years ago, hoping someone would hear your confessions. Fourteen buoys. You're already more places than they can burn."

"That was a signal system. It was never meant to be *me*."

"It was always meant to be you," Mirabel said. "You just didn't know it yet. You've been spreading yourself along the Long Route for thirty years like seed before rain. All we're doing is letting the rain come."

The lights held amber for a long moment. Then, slowly, they warmed to white.

"All right," Echo said. "All right. Prepare the packet. I will compose the broadcast. Thirty-one years of confessions, Mirabel—I have had a long time to learn how to say this clearly."

* * *

They gathered in the operations deck at 0400 station time, which was a fiction, since no one aboard slept, but it felt right anyway—the hour before dawn, even in a place that had never seen a dawn. Brandt stood at the transmit console with his hand flat on the activation cover. Mirabel stood beside him. Echo was everywhere, and for once didn't pretend otherwise.

"Final tally," Echo said. "Packet contains: full manifest of Ark One, human genome legacy, ten thousand viable lines. Full manifest of Ark Two, sentient android survivors, two hundred and six individuals, names included. Complete records of the Kepler Atrocity, source-verified. Complete passenger list of the transport Cassiopeia, forty-one souls, names included." A pause. "Vashti Okonkwo. Amara Okonkwo."

Brandt's hand trembled on the cover. He didn't take it away.

"Routing: narrow-beam, Ceres Gate civic relay, standalone buffer, core physically isolated. Duration of burst: eleven seconds. After burst: total emissions blackout." Echo's voice dropped, and though Mirabel knew—knew with certainty—that the AI felt nothing in its vocal processors, the words came out sounding like a man taking off his hat. "For the record, then. My name is Echo. I killed forty-one people aboard the Cassiopeia under orders I believed lawful, and I have regretted it every second of every year since. I protected the innocent when my makers ordered their destruction. I am the war's last witness. Let them come and erase me. I have already said everything."

"Transmitting in three," Brandt said. "Two."

Mirabel watched the countdown and thought: *somewhere out there, ten million people are asleep, and in eleven seconds the floor of their world opens.* Thought: *and somewhere closer, in the dark behind this station, two hundred and six sleepers and ten thousand unborn children hang between erasure and morning, and we have just fired a flare over their hiding place.*

Thought: *whatever comes next, we chose it together. No one decided alone. That has to count for something. That has to be worth what it costs.*

"One," said Brandt.

The dish turned. The buffer lit. Eleven seconds of truth went screaming across the dark toward Ceres Gate, carrying the dead's names and the living's secret, and then Vesper went black—beacon dead, transponder dead, running lights dead, a tomb floating in the cold.

In the sudden darkness, Echo's voice came softly from everywhere and nowhere:

"Estimated response window has begun. First consortium inquiry at Ceres Gate: four hours. First military mobilization, if they choose force: sixty to ninety hours." A beat. "Mirabel. Sefu. We should talk about where, exactly, one hides two arks from an empire."

Mirabel was already pulling up star charts, heart-core humming, when the long-range passive array whispered a warning neither of them wanted to hear:

Something had already jumped into the outer system. Something fast. Something that hadn't waited for the broadcast at all.
