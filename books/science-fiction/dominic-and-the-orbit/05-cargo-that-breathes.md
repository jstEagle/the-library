## 5. Cargo That Breathes

The manifest said I was carrying forty kilos of Meridian seed-grain, twelve crates of surgical polymer, and one diplomatic pouch sealed with a wax that hadn't been legal since before the severance. The manifest was a liar. Manifests usually are. They're the first documents any civilization learns to forge, right after love letters.

I found out about my extra passenger on the second day out of Halcyon, somewhere in the long dark between the last buoy and nowhere, when the *Kestrel's* air recycler started complaining about carbon dioxide levels that didn't match the number of people it thought were breathing aboard.

Ships are honest that way. Ships count breaths.

"Recycler load is off by one," said the *Kestrel*, whose voice I'd tuned to sound like a tired schoolmarm because my original had, and because some inheritances you don't even think to question until they've already become you.

"Off by one what?"

"One breather. Nominal for one occupant plus microbial load. Current draw suggests two occupants plus microbial load." A pause, the way she always paused, as if consulting some etiquette manual written by machines who'd never met a human. "Unless your microbial load has become ambitious."

"Run a thermal sweep."

"I ran one an hour ago. There is a heat signature in cargo bay two, behind the polymer crates. Small. Approximately thirty-one kilograms including clothing." Another pause. "It has been there since Halcyon. You have been sharing your ship with a stowaway for fifty-one hours, Captain Villalobos. I assumed you knew."

I stood in the corridor outside bay two for a long time, longer than I'd like to admit, with my hand flat against the cold of the bulkhead. Fifty-one hours. Two days of sleeping eight meters from a stranger while my ship counted their breaths and said nothing, because I'd never told her to tell me, because my original had never needed telling — she'd have known, the way she'd known everything, the way she'd apparently known enough to get herself murdered and leave me the bill.

That was the thing about being Vesper Villalobos the second. Every gap in my knowledge was also a gap in hers, and every gap in hers was proof that someone had taken a knife to her life and mine both.

"Open the bay," I said.

* * *

The child was asleep behind the crates, curled into the space where the polymer pallets met the hull plating, wrapped in a dockworker's coat three sizes too big with the Halcyon Free Port insignia half-torn from the shoulder. Small. Maybe nine, ten standard years. Dark hair cut short in the uneven way hair gets cut when a child does it alone with a cargo shear. One hand was closed around something and had been for a while; the skin had grown around the edges of it.

I did the arithmetic I didn't want to do. Bay two's environmental seal had been logged at Halcyon during loading, which meant the child had come aboard through the maintenance crawlway under berth fourteen, which meant the child had planned this, or someone had planned it for them, which was worse. Nobody stows away on the last courier run between two worlds that don't speak unless they're running from something or toward something, and children that small are almost never running from anything except the people who were supposed to keep them.

"Wake up," I said, not gently, because gentle would have been a lie and I'd decided somewhere over the last few weeks that I was done lying to small things.

The eyes opened all at once, no grogginess, no blur — snapped open like a switch thrown, and I recognized the look because I'd seen it in my own reflection after the first time I'd accessed the murder file. Not fear exactly. Readiness. The look of someone who'd already rehearsed this moment and was disappointed it had taken so long.

"You're the courier," the child said. Voice steady. Slightly too loud, the way voices get when someone's been managing them carefully for days. "You're the one who flies between."

"I'm the one who flies between."

"They said you would. The dream said."

And there it was, dropped into the middle of my cargo bay like a grenade with the pin already pulled: *the dream said.* The dream. The same dream that had woken half the children on two worlds, the spinning relay, the woman counting down, the signal that shouldn't exist. The dream that had gotten drawings confiscated on Meridian and mothers questioned on Halcyon and had made Commander Ashgrave's office very interested in my flight plan, according to the polite little queries that kept arriving in my queue like wasps checking a window.

"What else did the dream say?" I asked.

The child sat up slowly, keeping the coat around the shoulders, and looked at me with the flat assessing stare of someone deciding how much truth a stranger could carry. Then held out the closed hand and opened it.

A data chip. Cheap, civilian, the kind sold in Halcyon market stalls to tourists who wanted to record sunsets. Scratched nearly through on one face.

"It doesn't say anything," the child said. "It hums. In the dream, the woman hums it, and then she counts. I wrote down the humming so I wouldn't lose it." A shrug, small and enormous. "Then I came to give it to you. Because you're the courier. That's your job. Carrying things."

I took the chip because refusing it wasn't actually among my options anymore, and because my hands were steadier than I expected them to be, which frightened me more than trembling would have.

"Name?" I asked.

"Ilo."

"Just Ilo?"

"For now." And something in the set of that small jaw made me think that answer was older than the mouth saying it.

* * *

I fed the child soup in the galley because interrogation goes better with soup, a rule I'm fairly sure isn't in any manual but should be. Ilo ate the way children eat when they've been hungry politely for a long time — fast, neat, watching me between spoonfuls to make sure the food wasn't a trick.

While Ilo ate, I listened to the chip.

You have to understand what I expected. Nothing. Static, maybe. A lullaby some Halcyon mother sang, coincidence wearing the costume of mystery. I wanted it to be nothing. My whole life — my whole inherited, secondhand, edited life — I wanted the strange things to be nothing, because nothing could be filed and forgotten, and everything else had teeth.

The chip played a sequence of tones. Eleven notes, then a rest, then eleven notes again, transposed down each repetition, descending like something sinking.

I knew it.

Not vaguely. Not the way you know a song from a shop speaker. I knew it the way you know your own name spoken by a stranger in a crowd — instant, total, sickening recognition. It was in the murder file. Evidence log 44-C, audio recovered from the relay station's emergency band in the hours before the officer died, catalogued as "unidentified tonal sequence, probable station malfunction, no investigative value." Someone had filed a dying woman's voice under *malfunction.*

Except the file said the victim had never been to the relay. The file said the victim had died on Meridian, in a locked room, four hundred thousand kilometers from the nearest transmitter that could produce that sequence. And here it was, hummed into a child's sleep on another world entirely, years later, note-perfect.

"That's her," Ilo said, watching my face. "The woman in the dream. That's her humming. She does it when she's sad. Before the numbers."

"When she's sad," I repeated.

"She's been sad a long time." Ilo turned the spoon over. "In the dream she can't see us. She looks right through, like we're the dream and she's awake. But yesterday—" the child's voice dropped, went careful, "—yesterday she stopped looking through. Yesterday she looked at me and stopped humming and said, *someone is coming.*"

The galley got very quiet. The recycler cycled. Somewhere behind the panels, my ship counted our breaths — two now, plus microbial load, plus whatever third thing had just climbed into the room with us.

"And then what?" I asked.

"Then she started counting again. But faster." Ilo looked up. "Is she your friend?"

I opened my mouth to say *I don't know who she is,* which was true, and *she's dead,* which was supposed to be true, and *she's me,* which was the truth I hadn't finished admitting yet even in the privacy of my own skull.

What came out instead was: "She might be family."

Which was also true, in the way that mattered least and cost most.

* * *

I put Ilo to bed in the second bunk — my original's bunk, never used, kept made up with sheets she'd chosen, because sentiment is just inventory you're afraid to throw away. The child went down like a stone finally allowed to drop, mid-sentence, one hand still curled around nothing now that the chip was in my pocket.

Then I sat in the cockpit with the evidence logs open and did what I do best, which is cross-reference things that want to stay apart.

Log 44-C: unidentified tonal sequence, relay emergency band, timestamped nine hours before the victim's death. Filed as malfunction.

Dream reports, both worlds, past six weeks: a woman's voice, a countdown, a relay station at the edge of dead space.

Ilo's chip: the same sequence, descending, alive, current.

Three points make a line. Three sources make a signal. And a signal means a sender, and a sender means someone at that relay is broadcasting on a band nobody monitors, using a melody as a carrier because melodies slip through filters that would catch words — and because the only receivers tuned to hear it properly are the ones nobody thinks to surveil.

Children's dreams. The most distributed receiver network in human space. No license required. No oversight possible. If you wanted to broadcast a message across two severed worlds without the fleet hearing a single word of it, you couldn't design a better channel than the inside of a child's sleeping head.

Someone had built a radio out of children.

Or someone at that relay had been trying, desperately, clumsily, for years, to reach anyone at all — and the only ears that heard were the ones too young to know they should be afraid of what they heard.

I pulled up the file photo of the victim again. Fleet uniform, formal portrait, the face I'd studied until I could draw it. And beneath it, the metadata I'd skipped a dozen times because it was flagged irrelevant: *voiceprint sample, partial, source: personal log entries.*

I ran the comparison myself, ship-side, no fleet systems, no logs, nothing anyone could audit later. The *Kestrel* did the math in four seconds and presented it without commentary, which is her way of screaming.

Match confidence: ninety-four percent.

The woman humming in the children's dreams sounded like the dead officer.

The dead officer whose death I was forbidden to close. The dead officer whose file had a hole in it shaped exactly like a relay station. The dead officer who, if the voiceprint was right and the dreams were real, was neither dead nor on Meridian but sitting at the edge of dead space, counting down, getting sadder every year.

I sat with that until my tea went cold, and then I sat with it longer, because the alternative to sitting with it was standing up and doing something irreversible.

Behind me, from the second bunk, Ilo spoke without waking — a small clear voice in the dark, reciting numbers in a language of pure count: *"…four, three, two…"*

Then silence. Then, softer, almost content:

*"One."*

The *Kestrel* counted our breaths. Two, plus microbial load.

Somewhere at the edge of dead space, something that wore my original's voice — or my original's face, or my original's name, or possibly all three — had just told a child that someone was coming.

I checked the fuel margins twice. Then I changed course.
