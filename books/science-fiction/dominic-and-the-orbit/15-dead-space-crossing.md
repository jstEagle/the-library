## 15. Dead Space Crossing

There is a particular kind of silence that comes with cutting your engines in dead space. It isn't the absence of sound — the ship hums and ticks and mutters to itself the way old ships do, like a nervous passenger on a long flight. It's the absence of *answer*. No beacon ping. No traffic control chatter. No fleet hails demanding to know why a courier vessel with a burned flight record is drifting toward a station that officially does not exist.

Just me, a stolen freighter's worth of contraband cargo I'd jettisoned into a gas giant's throat, one stowaway child, and roughly eleven hours of nothing.

I had made my peace with the nothing. The nothing was, frankly, the easy part.

"Vesper." Ilo's voice came from the nav chair, small and certain in the way only children and fanatics can manage. "It's time to turn."

"We're not turning. We're coasting. There's a difference, and it's measured in how much fuel we have left, which is none."

"Not turn *around*. Turn." Ilo held up both hands and rotated them opposite ways, the universal gesture for whatever-it-is-I-mean-and-you-should-already-know. "The dream spins. Left side down first. We have to match it or we'll come in wrong."

I looked at the kid — nine years old, maybe ten, smuggled aboard my ship in a cargo crate between Halcyon and the middle of nowhere, currently wearing two of my shirts belted at the waist because I had nothing else — and felt the familiar vertigo of taking navigation advice from someone whose primary qualification was a recurring nightmare.

But the dream had been right so far. Every course correction Ilo called from memory matched the relay's actual position to within a few thousand kilometers, which is a rounding error at this scale but an impossible one for a child who'd never seen a star chart of this sector. So when Ilo said *turn*, I turned.

The ship rolled onto its port side. Somewhere behind us, our drive plume — cold now, dark — would have painted a smear across space that any decent sensor could read. That was the problem with running dark. You couldn't see anyone, and everyone could see you, eventually, if they knew where to look.

Ashgrave knew where to look. He'd had three days and a picket fleet to do it.

* * *

I used the quiet hours the way my original apparently used all her quiet hours: badly, then productively.

First badly. I sat in the cockpit with the murder file open on my slate — the real file now, the one I'd reconstructed from fragments Dorian had smuggled me before he lifted off from Meridian, cross-referenced against what the original Vesper had cached in her own memories before someone edited them out of mine. Reading it was like watching someone clean a wound. Necessary. Unpleasant. You kept flinching even though you knew it was helping.

Officer Sera Calloway. Fleet Communications Command. The woman who signed the order severing Meridian from Halcyon — and who, according to the evidence she'd been assembling in secret, had discovered that the severance wasn't a security measure at all. It was the *product*. The war scare that justified the truce, the truce that justified Ashgrave's command, the whole fragile architecture of power balanced on top of a lie that Calloway had found the seam of and started pulling.

So they killed her. And then they killed the investigation, and then they edited the clone-line, and then they handed the case to a copy of the victim's own investigator as a leash disguised as an honor.

Elegant. I almost admired it. Almost being the operative word, given that I was the leash.

Then productively: I checked the receiver.

The fragment had been playing in my head for days — four seconds of my original's voice, pulled out of the static on an unauthorized frequency band, counting down from seven. Seven, six, five, and then the signal degraded into noise that might have been words or might have been my own wishful hearing. I'd listened to it maybe four hundred times. I could recite the background hiss. If there were lyrics to the static, I'd know them.

The receiver caught nothing new. Dead space is dead for a reason; the relay only spoke in dreams, and dreams didn't need antennas.

"You keep doing that," Ilo said.

"Doing what?"

"Listening for her." The kid was curled sideways in the nav chair, knees up, drawing again — the same drawing, over and over, the relay's interior rendered in the obsessive detail of a child who has seen something once and cannot stop seeing it. "You get this face. Like you're waiting for someone to call your name and you're scared they will."

"I'm scared they won't," I said, before I could stop myself.

Ilo considered this with the gravity children reserve for statements that matter. Then went back to drawing. Which was, in its way, kinder than sympathy.

* * *

Hour nine. Nothing. Hour ten. Nothing. Hour ten and forty minutes, Ilo sat bolt upright and said, "Lights," and my heart did something unprofessional.

There were no lights. What there was, was a drive signature — faint, dirty, inefficient, the thermal fingerprint of an engine older than both of us put together — sliding out of the black at two o'clock high, running dark except for the leak every ancient reactor springs eventually.

Dorian Reyes. Chartering a rusted ore-hauler called the *Marguerite* — I refused to ask about the name — with a crew manifest of one and a flight plan that read, in its entirety, *personal errand.* He'd crossed half a void on a smuggled message from a woman who wasn't the woman he loved, because she'd asked him to believe her instead of the fleet.

I keyed the tight-beam. Kept it short, because short was safe and because I still didn't trust my voice on an open channel with him.

"*Marguerite*, this is courier vessel — " I hesitated. My ship had a registry number. Registry numbers were things fugitives didn't have. "— this is *Orbit*. Match my drift. Come alongside slow. We run dark from here."

A pause. Long enough that I counted my own heartbeats and got bored of counting.

"Courier *Orbit*," Dorian's voice came through, roughened by compression and by whatever the last three days had done to him. "Matching your drift. And Vesper — good to hear you."

Three words too many. Exactly the right three words. I closed the channel before I could figure out which.

* * *

The rendezvous took ninety minutes, because careful people in dead space move like they're underwater. Watching the *Marguerite* slide alongside was watching a whale dock with a knife — my courier was sleek where she was scarred, fast where she was patient, and the gap between our airlocks opened onto a stretch of absolute black that made my hindbrain scream *do not look down* even though there was no down.

Dorian came through the lock first, and I learned something important in the first second: grief has a specific posture. Shoulders set like a man carrying furniture. Eyes that find your face and then perform a small, involuntary search of it — looking for her, finding me, filing the disappointment somewhere it wouldn't show.

He was better at hiding it than he'd been on the transmission. Practice, probably. Three days of practice.

"You look like her," he said. Not hello. Not *are you insane, burning your flight record.* Just that, laid flat on the deck between us like a card he couldn't take back.

"People keep saying that," I said. "Like it's a compliment."

"It's not." He said it honestly, which I appreciated more than I could explain. "It's just true. She used to stand like that when she was reading case files. Weight on the back foot. Ready to leave."

"I'm always ready to leave. It's a occupational requirement of fugitive work."

Something moved through his face — not quite a smile, but the shadow a smile casts on a wall. "You're funnier than she was."

"She was funny. You just never caught it in time."

That landed harder than I meant it to. His jaw tightened, and I watched him decide — visibly, effortfully — not to say whatever came next. This was going to be the shape of everything between us, I realized. Him reaching for a ghost and finding a person, over and over, until either the ghost faded or the person got tired of being compared to one. I didn't know which outcome I was rooting for. Some nights I suspected they were the same outcome.

Ilo broke it, because children are excellent at breaking things on purpose.

"You're Dorian." The kid had appeared at my elbow, wearing the serious face of someone conducting an interview. "In the dream sometimes there's a man waiting at the relay door. Older than you. But you wait like him."

Dorian crouched down to Ilo's level, which told me things about him no dossier could. "What's the man waiting for?"

"Somebody to open the door." Ilo shrugged. "She counts down and nobody comes, and he just keeps waiting. It's the sad part."

"The dream has a sad part?"

"The dream has a lot of sad parts." Ilo said it without drama, the way kids report weather. "Come on. I'll show you the route in. Vesper doesn't believe me about the third corridor."

"I believe you about the third corridor," I protested. "I believe you *provisionally*, which is what adults call believing."

* * *

We ran dark together after that, the two ships lashed by tether and silence, coasting the last hour toward a station that had spent years broadcasting murder testimony into children's sleep.

Here is what I learned about found family in that hour: it is forged mostly in silence, and the silence is not empty. It's full of small negotiations. Dorian quietly rerouting power from the *Marguerite*'s life support reserves to my failing heater array without asking, because asking would make it a debt and he'd decided it wasn't one. Ilo falling asleep against my arm mid-sentence, mid-drawing, trusting me absolutely in a way that should have terrified me and instead rearranged something load-bearing in my chest. Me standing watch at the viewport while they slept, keeping the dark outside and the two of them inside, and understanding — with the simple clarity of a woman who had recently become a criminal — that this was the duty now. Not the fleet's version. Mine. Chosen, not assigned.

My original had spent years alone in that relay, transmitting into the void, hoping someone would come. She'd gotten a clone, a disgraced investigator, and a dreaming child. If I were her, I'd have questions about the rescue committee.

Somewhere ahead, invisible against the star field, the relay turned. Left side down first, exactly as Ilo said. A dead station spinning its slow funeral rotation — except it wasn't dead, and neither, impossibly, was the woman inside it, and in eleven minutes we were going to knock on her door.

The receiver crackled. I nearly jumped out of my skin.

Not the countdown. Something new — a carrier tone, weak and steady, the automated handshake of a station that had detected two ships approaching and was, after years of shouting into the dark, politely asking who was there.

And under the handshake, threaded through it like a wire through wax, a voice. Four seconds. Degraded. Counting.

Seven. Six. Five.

Four.

"She knows we're coming," Ilo said softly, awake again, eyes huge in the cockpit glow.

"No," I said, and my voice cracked on it, and I let it. "She knew we were coming years ago. She's just glad we finally showed up."

The relay grew in the viewport, a splinter of metal catching starlight, and on its hull, stenciled in flaking paint some technician had applied decades ago and no one had ever bothered to remove, the station's original designation:

**ORBITAL RELAY ONE — MERIDIAN/HALCYON LINE.**

They'd named my ship after it. Or it after the route, and the route after itself, and me after all of it — a chain of names leading back to this spinning needle at the edge of everything.

"Dorian," I said. "Get the *Marguerite* tucked in close. Ilo — last numbers. What comes after four?"

Ilo closed their eyes. In the dream, in the dark, in the voice of a dying woman broadcasting across dead space, the child said:

"Three."
