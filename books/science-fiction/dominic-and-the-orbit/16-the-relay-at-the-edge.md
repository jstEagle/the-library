## 16. The Relay at the Edge

The relay looked like a prayer wheel someone had abandoned mid-spin.

That was Ilo's description, delivered from the copilot's chair with the flat authority of a nine-year-old who had been dreaming this place for months and considered themselves the local expert. I had my own description, which was: a coffin with an antenna. A kilometer of corroded lattice turning slowly against nothing, catching light that had traveled too far to be useful to anyone.

No beacons. No running lights. No rescue within eleven years if something went wrong, and Dorian's freighter — the *Marta's Regret*, a name that turned out to be autobiographical — had enough fuel for one approach and no margin for regret of any kind.

"Docking collar's on the equatorial ring," Ilo said, tracing it in the air. "Third door past the broken solar sail. There's a corridor that floods with coolant sometimes, so you have to run."

"You've never been here," Dorian said from behind me. His voice had been rough since the dark crossing, since he'd spent six hours holding our silence while two fleet pickets crawled past us like sharks tasting water. "You've dreamed it."

"Same thing," Ilo said, with the serene confidence of childhood, and neither of us could argue, because we were both there because of a dream.

I brought us in myself. The *Long Route* — my ship, my inheritance, my whole identity compressed into two words — handled the final meters with the grace of a woman being lowered into her own grave. The collar caught. Seals groaned. Somewhere in the relay's bones, something woke up, because the docking lights flickered amber and then, impossibly, green.

"It let us in," Dorian said quietly.

"Or it wanted us to come," I said. "Those aren't always different things."

* * *

The interior smelled like every dead thing I'd ever catalogued on the long route: ozone, cold metal, the sweetish rot of insulation that had given up decades ago. But under it, faint as a pulse, something else. Recycled air that had been cycled one time too many. The smell of a person living alone for a very long time.

Ilo ran ahead through the coolant corridor exactly as advertised — ankle-deep freeze, breath fogging, the child laughing like this was the best day of their life — while Dorian kept pace beside me with a hand near his sidearm and grief written all over his face in a language he thought I couldn't read.

He'd barely spoken to me since the rendezvous. Not coldly. Worse than coldly: carefully. Every sentence measured against some internal template of a woman named Vesper Villalobos, checking my responses against hers, and I could see him flinching every time I didn't match. When I swore at the airlock cycling slow, he looked at me like I'd spoken a language he almost knew. Her original hadn't sworn, apparently. Or she'd sworn differently. It's a strange kind of loneliness, being compared to yourself by a man who loved you first.

"Vesper." He stopped me at a junction where three corridors met, all of them dark except one, where a thin line of light bled under a far door. "If it's her down there—"

"If it's her," I said, "then everything I am is a copy of a woman who faked her own death and let the fleet bury the murder."

"That's not what I was going to say."

"I know. I got there faster." I touched his arm, brief, because I'm not built for comfort and neither, anymore, was he. "Whatever's down there, we came for the truth. Let's not flinch when it has a face."

The door at the end of the lit corridor wasn't locked. It was taped shut — actual tape, cloth-backed, military surplus, layered so many times it had become a kind of seal, a covenant between one person and her own paranoia. Someone had written on it in marker, over and over, until the letters overlapped into gray:

**LISTENING IS NOT ENOUGH. LISTENING IS NOT ENOUGH. LISTENING IS NOT ENOUGH.**

Ilo peeled the tape back with great care, like unwrapping a gift.

* * *

The room beyond was the transmitter core, and it was a shrine.

Cables hung from the ceiling in braided curtains. Every surface — consoles, floor plating, the housing of the main antenna array — was covered in writing. Marker, paint, scratched metal. Numbers, mostly. Sequences. And drawings, hundreds of them, pinned and taped and glued: children's crayon renderings of this very station, spinning, with a woman's silhouette standing in the antenna's throat.

And in the middle of it, in a chair wired directly into the transmitter's control bus, sat a woman who had my face.

She was thinner than me. Grayer. Her hair had gone white at the temples in a way mine wouldn't for thirty years, and her hands — my hands, scarred differently, one finger missing at the second knuckle — rested on a console worn smooth where her palms had rested for years. She was humming. A sequence. Seven notes, then a rest, then seven more.

I knew it before I knew I knew it. Evidence log 44-C. The audio recovered from the murdered officer's personal recorder, timestamped ninety minutes before his death, origin unknown. The file everyone assumed was a corrupted transmission. The file I'd listened to four hundred times in the dark of my own ship, telling myself it meant nothing.

It was a lullaby. It was a countdown set to music. It was her.

Dorian made a sound behind me that I will hear for the rest of my life. Not a word. Something older than words.

Her eyes opened. My eyes, hazed with cataracts and something brighter underneath, found me across the room, and she smiled — my smile, the one I'd seen in mirrors my whole manufactured life, tilted slightly left, and I had never once known where I got it from.

"There you are," she said. Her voice was gravel and static. "You took the long way."

* * *

What do you say to yourself? I've had weeks to think about that question and I still don't have an answer, so you can imagine how well I did in the moment, standing in a dead station at the edge of dead space, looking at the woman whose memories were stitched into my skull like someone else's laundry.

I said: "You're supposed to be dead."

"I'm supposed to be *quiet*," she said. "Different thing. Sit down. No — don't sit, the chairs are all rigged. Stand there. You look good. They fed you better than they fed me." She laughed, and it turned into coughing, and when she wiped her mouth her palm came away spotted red, and Dorian stepped forward like he'd been shot, and she held up her ruined hand to stop him.

"Dorian Reyes," she said softly. "You got old."

"You got *dead*," he said, and his voice cracked clean through the middle of it.

"I got careful. Mostly the same thing out here." She looked at him for a long moment, and then at me, and something passed over her face that I recognized because I'd felt it myself on the long route: the specific pain of loving someone who is a memory. "You came together. In the same ship. Huh." She said it like a math problem she couldn't solve, or maybe one she could, and didn't like the answer.

"The dream," Ilo said, from the doorway, arms crossed. "You sent it."

"I sent it." She said it simply, without apology. "Every night for six years. Broad-spectrum, low-band, buried under the carrier noise so the fleet's filters would read it as background radiation. I aimed it at both worlds because I didn't know which one still had ears. Adults can't hear it — the frequency sits wrong for grown brains, too close to the hum of your own blood. But children—" she shrugged, and the shrug was mine, "—children are antennas. Children have always been antennas. Nobody remembers that because nobody wants to think about what they're receiving."

"You've been broadcasting into kids' heads for six years," Dorian said, and there was horror in it, and I understood the horror, and I also understood her, completely, the way you understand your own handwriting.

"I've been knocking," she said. "For six years. On every door on two worlds. And one of them finally opened." She looked at Ilo. "Thank you for coming. I'm sorry about the nightmares. They were the only honest format left."

"Why hide?" I asked. "Why not just — transmit the evidence? Broadcast the murder file, the war orders, all of it. Why a lullaby? Why a dream?"

"Because evidence gets deleted, Vesper." She said my name like she was testing the weight of it. "I tried. Year two, I pushed the full cache through a maintenance uplink. Forty hours later a fleet corvette 'coincidentally' adjusted its patrol through this sector and swept every band with a purge pulse. They didn't find the station. But they told me they were listening, and that next time they'd bring something bigger than a sweep." She tapped her temple. "So I moved the evidence somewhere they can't purge. Into a format they can't intercept without admitting what they're intercepting. Into the dreams of children on worlds they claim they're protecting." Her smile went sharp. "You can shoot down a courier. You can't shoot down a bedtime story once ten thousand kids know it by heart."

"And now?"

"Now the truce expires in—" she glanced at a wall covered in tally marks, thousands of them, and I realized with a lurch that the tallies weren't days survived; they were countdown numbers, the same ones in the dream, "—nineteen hours. Ashgrave will burn this station the moment his truce dies, because the station is the last witness. So I need a courier." She looked at me. "I need *the* courier. The one they built to carry things and never ask questions. Except you started asking, didn't you? I felt it. In the gaps."

My skin went cold. "The edited memories."

"Somebody had to hold the case," she said. "Somebody the fleet trusted absolutely. Somebody who couldn't testify to anything she hadn't been cleared to remember. They grew you from my tissue and they gave you my routines and my ship and my loneliness, and they cut out everything dangerous, and they thought that made you safe." She leaned forward, and the cables swayed around her like seaweed. "They forgot that a gap is also information. You found the edges of the hole. That's how I knew you'd come. That's why I kept counting."

"What's in the gaps?" Dorian asked hoarsely.

"The whole war," she said. "The manufactured provocation. The officer I supposedly killed — I didn't kill him, Ashgrave did, and I watched, and I ran, and I've been running toward a transmitter ever since. Everything's cached in the relay's core. Everything. Enough to end the truce or end the lie, depending on who hears it first." She coughed again, longer this time, and folded forward over the console, and when she straightened there was blood on her teeth and she didn't bother wiping it. "But I can't broadcast it anymore. The transmitter needs hands on the manual bus now — the auto-rig burned out last year, and my hands—" she raised them, trembling, "—my hands stopped being reliable around the same time my lungs did. Radiation does that. Six years of sleeping inside an antenna."

"So finish it yourself," Dorian said desperately. "Tell us everything. We'll carry it out. We'll—"

"Dead couriers carry nothing," she said. "And I'm already dead, Dorian. I've been dead for six years; the paperwork just hasn't caught up. If I leave this chair, I die in a corridor instead of at my post, and either way the signal stops." She turned to me, and her eyes — my eyes — held mine with a steadiness that had nothing gentle in it. "But you. You're fresh. You're certified. Your biometrics will pass any fleet authentication check, because as far as their systems know, you *are* me. You can sit in this chair. You can hold the bus open while the cache uploads. And when Ashgrave's strike force comes — and it's coming, nineteen hours, maybe less — you'll be the one deciding whether the truth goes out or goes dark forever."

"That's not finishing a case," I said. "That's starting a war."

"No," she said. "Starting a war is what Ashgrave did, twenty years ago, in a room you don't remember. This is just *ending the lie*. Whether it becomes a war after that depends on whether people are better than the men who led them." She smiled, tired and terrible. "I've been betting on the children. You've met them. What's your read?"

Ilo, from the doorway, said: "We already know the whole dream. All of it. Even the bad parts."

The original looked at the child, and then at me, and something in her face finally broke open — not weakness, but the opposite: the exhaustion of a woman who had held everything alone for six years and was, at last, allowed to put it down.

"I'm dying, Vesper," she said, and hearing my name in my own voice from my own face was a vertigo I will never fully climb out of. "Not dramatically. Just — inevitably. I have maybe a day, and most of it hurts. I don't want your pity and I don't have time for your crisis of identity, so here's what I want instead." She pulled her hands off the console, off the bus she'd been holding open for six years, and gestured at the empty seat. At my seat. At the post I'd been flying toward my entire manufactured life without knowing it.

"Finish the case. Close the file. Not the way they wrote it — the way it actually happened. And when the strike force comes through that airlock, you hold the transmitter open, and you make them listen to what they did." Her voice dropped, and for one moment the half-madness fell away entirely and she was just a tired investigator at the end of a very long route. "I spent six years making sure someone would come. Don't make me wrong."

Behind me, Dorian was crying without any sound at all, watching the woman he'd mourned offer him nothing but a mission. Ahead of me, a child waited to see what the adult would do. And in front of me, wearing my face, my original slid out of the transmitter's chair —

— and the console screamed.

Every screen in the room flushed red at once. The tally wall flickered. Ilo's head snapped up, eyes wide, and the child said, in a voice gone small and certain:

"She's early. The countdown changed. It's not nineteen hours anymore."

The original grabbed my wrist with her ruined hand, hard enough to bruise, and grinned through the blood.

"Well," she said. "Ashgrave always did hate waiting. Sit down, courier. We're doing this *now*."
