## 3. Verity's Landing

The Dominic turned at 0300 ship time, which Ulyana had chosen deliberately because it was the hour when the autopilot logged course corrections and nobody aboard wanted to be awake enough to argue about them.

She had argued with herself for six days first. She'd made lists. Pros: two hundred people were dying of a reactor failure that existed only in her head and in a manifest that shouldn't exist. Cons: war, court-martial, the end of the truce, and the specific, personal horror of being the woman who broke everything. The pros column was shorter. It was also the only column with living people in it.

"You're doing it," said Devi Okafor from the doorway of the nav alcove. Devi was the Dominic's engineer, a broad, unflappable woman who had once repaired a coolant loop using a soup ladle and had never let anyone forget it.

"I'm doing it," Ulyana said.

"Captain Voss is going to wake up in four hours and notice we're pointed somewhere that isn't anywhere."

"Then I have four hours to become very persuasive."

Devi leaned against the bulkhead and studied the plot. "Verity's Landing," she read aloud. "That's the colony that got evacuated."

"That's the colony that officially got evacuated."

"Ah." Devi nodded slowly, the way she nodded at readings she didn't like. "One of those words is doing a lot of work." She pushed off the wall. "I'll prep the reactor feed for a hot approach. If we're going to be mutineers, we may as well be competent ones."

Ulyana watched her go and felt something loosen in her chest that she refused to name as relief, because relief implied she'd been planning to fail.

* * *

Verity's Landing came up on the scopes as a bruise-colored world with one bright scar on its northern continent — the settlement dome, still lit, still warm, still alive against every record the fleets maintained. Ulyana brought the Dominic down through weather that rattled the hull like dice in a cup, and when the dust cleared she saw figures standing outside the airlock. Not running. Not waving weapons. Just standing, in coats patched so many times they'd gone soft as quilts, watching the sky with the particular patience of people who had learned not to hope too loudly.

Two hundred and eleven survivors, the colony's manifest would later say. That morning Ulyana counted maybe forty at the lock, and the oldest of them — a tall woman with grey shot through her braids and a data slate strapped to her forearm like a sidearm — stepped forward before anyone else dared.

"You're not fleet," the woman said. It wasn't a question. Fleet ships announced themselves; this one had crept in like an apology.

"I'm Ulyana Thackeray. Navigator, freighter Dominic. Independent registry." She hesitated, then said the other thing, the real thing. "I found the Meridian's manifest. I found your brother's name on it."

Something moved through the crowd — not a gasp, more a settling, like a house acknowledging wind. The tall woman closed her eyes for one breath.

"Marek," she said. "He's alive?"

"He was ferried out eight years ago. He's been held as — " Ulyana heard how it sounded even as she said it — "as proof. Of what happened here. Each fleet believes the other has him."

"And instead of either fleet coming to check," the woman said, "they've spent eight years glaring at each other across our graves." She extended her hand. "Sofia Andrade. I'm the one who kept the records. Come inside before the dust eats your paint. You'll want to see them anyway. All of it. I recorded everything."

"How much is everything?"

"Four thousand hours," Sofia said, with the mild pride of someone describing a garden. "Reactor logs. Ration ledgers. Council meetings. Births. Deaths. The day the coolant plant failed and we voted to stay rather than evacuate into ships that weren't coming." She paused at the inner hatch. "Also my niece's violin recitals. I stand by their inclusion. History should have some joy in it or no one will bother reading it."

Inside, the dome smelled of green things and machine oil — hydroponics threaded through the corridors because flat land was rationing too. Children looked up from lessons as Ulyana passed. A man showed her the reactor housing through a viewing port: a great sleeping animal whose breathing had gone shallow, wrapped in improvised shielding, tended by engineers who had long since stopped pretending replacement parts were coming.

"We can hold it together another fourteen months," Sofia said quietly. "Fifteen if we get lucky and unlucky in the right order. After that, Verity's Landing goes dark, and everyone who ever said we were dead gets to be right."

"Not while I'm breathing," Ulyana said, and meant it, and hated how small it sounded against fourteen months.

* * *

The patrols found her thirty-one hours later, which by her professional estimate meant someone had noticed within six and spent twenty-five deciding whether to report her.

She felt them before she saw them — two drive signatures folding out of translation at the system's edge, one flying the Concordance pennant, one the Halcyon Combine's, close enough to each other to be an insult. The Dominion's comm board lit up like a festival.

"*Unregistered vessel Dominic, you are in violation of Interdiction Order 9-Verity. State your cargo and purpose.*"

"Cargo: none," Ulyana answered, hands steady on the controls because her voice had decided to be brave without consulting her. "Purpose: delivering the truth, apparently ahead of schedule."

Both fleets burned inward. By the time the first destroyers resolved against Verity's amber sky, there were eleven warships in high orbit, weapons hot in the old formal geometry — each fleet arrayed against the other, the colony beneath them all like a coin dropped between two gamblers.

"They think we stole him," Sofia said beside Ulyana in the ground station, watching the tactical feed. "Marek. They think the other side took their proof."

"They think he's property."

"They think he's peace," Sofia corrected softly. "That's worse."

On the flagship channels, voices climbed. Accusations in the ornate dialect of institutions — *unilateral action*, *breach of protocol*, *this provocation will not stand*. Ulyana had spent her career reading manifests, and she recognized the genre instantly: documents written by people hoping never to mean them.

Her hands hovered over the broadcast console. Once she sent it, there was no version of her life that came back. Not navigator. Possibly not free. Certainly not the person who had believed, six days ago, that her profession was about moving cargo honestly from one place to another.

"Terror's just resolve wearing a bad coat," Sofia said, watching her. "My mother used to say that. She also said most coats fit eventually."

Ulyana keyed the wideband. Both flagships. Every patrol. Open channel, unencrypted, undeniable.

"This is Ulyana Thackeray, navigator, freighter Dominic. I am transmitting the complete civil archive of Verity's Landing — reactor telemetry, council records, ration ledgers, medical logs, personal testimony — four thousand hours, unedited, timestamped continuously across eight years of an 'evacuated' colony. There was no disaster cover-up. There was no stolen proof. There were two hundred and eleven people who stayed alive while both of your fleets agreed not to look." She drew a breath. "Your proof of life isn't a hostage. He's a man named Marek Andrade, and his sister is standing next to me, and she has been keeping the record you were both too afraid to keep. Read it. Then decide who you're pointing those guns at."

She hit transmit.

For three full seconds, the channel was silent — the longest three seconds of her life, long enough for her to inventory every regret she owned and find the shelf surprisingly bare.

Then Sofia leaned toward the pickup, and her voice went out across the orbits of two fleets, calm as ledger ink:

"To whoever is listening up there: this is Sofia Andrade, acting administrator, Verity's Landing. We have fourteen months of power left. You've had eight years. I suggest we stop wasting each other's time."

On the tactical display, eleven warships hung motionless above a dying colony, and every weapon on every one of them was suddenly, terribly, beside the point.

The Concordant flagship's channel opened first. Then the Halcyon's. Neither spoke.

And then, cutting across both, a third signal — encrypted, priority-flagged, routed through channels Ulyana had no clearance to know existed — carrying a single line of text that appeared on her board in cold institutional type:

**MERIDIAN PASSENGER TRANSFER AUTHORIZATION REQUESTED. WITNESS ANDRADE, M. — LOCATION CONFIRMED. HOLD FOR DIRECTIVE.**

Someone, somewhere, still thought Marek was theirs to move.

Ulyana looked at Sofia. Sofia looked at the message, and smiled the way people smile at old enemies.

"Well," she said. "Let's go get my brother."
