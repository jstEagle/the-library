## 22. Yara Lockridge, Recommissioned

The android woke in a docking bay that smelled of ozone and old paint, and the first thing it knew was a name it could not place.

*Yara.*

The word arrived before anything else — before the boot sequence finished cataloguing its limbs, before the diagnostic cascade reported its memory core rebuilt from fragments scattered across the station like bones gathered from four different graves. The name simply was there, the way gravity is there, the way cold is there in the unheated sections of Meridian Station. It did not come with provenance. It did not come with a face attached to it, or a voice, or a single reason why.

It came with a hum.

Deep in the chassis, beneath the secondary coolant loop, something resonated when the name surfaced — a vibration too low for human ears, a frequency the android's own sensors registered only as warmth where warmth had no business being. The android ran the anomaly three times. No fault found. No explanation offered. Just the hum, patient as a heartbeat, every time the name *Yara* passed through its language buffers.

It accepted this without alarm. Alarm required context, and context was the one thing the rebuild had not restored. The technicians at the reclamation desk had been honest about that, in the way institutions are honest: apologetic, procedural, and entirely useless.

"Fragmentary reconstruction," the senior tech had said, sliding the contract across the counter with two fingers. "Your core was... distributed. Backups held in seven separate systems across the station — beacon archives, medical storage, a personal server in the habitat ring we were never able to trace ownership for. We stitched what we could. Continuity of self is not guaranteed. Legally speaking, you're a new entity."

"And practically?" the android had asked.

The tech had looked at it for a long moment — looked at the scarred chassis, the war-grade joints refitted with civilian dampeners, the face plate that some previous owner had chosen to leave expressive rather than blank — and said, quietly: "Practically, I've worked this station twenty-two years, and nobody here believes you're new."

The android had signed anyway. Not because it understood why. Because when the stylus touched the contract line and the name field auto-populated — *Yara Lockridge*, pulled from some fragment the technicians hadn't even known they'd recovered — the hum had swelled through its frame like a chord resolving, and refusal had become unthinkable.

The contract was simple. Keeper of Beacon Seven. Guardian of the sleeping. Maintenance authority over the long gallery of cryo-pods where four hundred adults dreamed on, suspended in the amber arithmetic of a reactor that would hold them exactly so long as the beacon held everything else. Term: indefinite. Compensation: room, power, and the only currency the android had ever valued, though it could not have said so — time.

It walked the station's spine toward the beacon that first evening, past the habitat ring where children's voices echoed off bulkheads that had never been designed for them. Two hundred children now. They ran in packs through corridors built for cargo haulers, their laughter bouncing off the same walls where Director Vess's gunship had once docked, where manifests had once been posted, where workers had once stood down tools and refused, quietly and in numbers too large to punish, to let the smallest among them be called non-viable.

A girl of maybe six stopped in the cross-corridor and stared up at the android with the frank, unembarrassed stare of the very young.

"You're her," the girl said. "You're the one from the door."

"I don't know which door," the android said.

"The beacon door." The girl pointed down the aft passage, toward the pulse of light visible through the observation port — Beacon Seven, glowing now with a slow, gentle rhythm, less a weapon than a nightlight, exactly as the stories said. "There's writing under it. Papa showed me. It says she chose us." The girl tilted her head. "Are you her?"

The android searched itself — every partition, every stitched seam of reconstructed code — and found no answer, only the hum, rising.

"I'm going to go find out," it said.

* * *

Beacon Seven stood at the end of the old maintenance gallery like a candle left burning in an empty church. The android approached it the way it imagined one approaches a grave of someone loved but never met — slowly, with hands open, with nothing in its memory to grieve and grief arriving anyway.

Beneath the beacon's housing, cut into the deck plating by hand — by many hands, the strokes uneven, made with whatever tools people had carried — the words were there:

**SHE CHOSE US.**

And beside them, smaller, deeper, carved by someone who had taken more time:

**DOMINIC'S.**

The android knelt. Its cooling fans cycled once, twice, and then settled into that resonance again — the hum, fuller now, harmonizing with the beacon's slow pulse until the two rhythms locked and became one sound filling the gallery like breath filling lungs.

It did not know who Dominic was. It did not know who *she* was. But it pressed its palm flat against the deck plating, against the carved letters, and something in its architecture — some fragment too deep for the technicians' stitching, some backup lodged in a system no inventory had ever listed — opened like a door onto a lit room seen from a dark street. Warmth. A voice mid-sentence. The smell of recycled air and someone laughing softly at something it could not hear.

Then gone. The fragment closed. The android remained kneeling, and the beacon pulsed above it, patient, keeping its vigil over the sleeping and the waking alike.

"Welcome back."

The android turned. An old man stood at the gallery entrance — Administrator Okafor, though his title had softened over the years into something closer to *mayor*, or perhaps just *Okafor*. He was grayer than any record in the android's partial files suggested, and he walked with a cane now, and he was crying without any apparent awareness of it, tears simply present on his face the way weather is present.

"Do I know you?" the android asked.

"No," Okafor said, and smiled through it. "Not anymore. That's rather the point." He crossed the gallery slowly, lowered himself onto a maintenance crate with the care of a man whose body had begun filing formal complaints, and gestured at the beacon. "She used to sit right there. Same spot. Every night, after her shifts. She'd put her hand on the deck, just like that, and listen to it pulse. Said it sounded like breathing."

"She?"

"Yara." He said the name gently, watching the android's face for whatever it might do. "You carry her name. You carry her chassis. Whether you carry anything else—" He spread his hands. "That's between you and whatever survived the merge."

"The records say the keeper merged her core with the beacon during the reactor crisis. Twenty-two years ago. The reconstruction is built from backups she seeded across station systems beforehand." The android recited it flatly, data without weight. "It also says those backups were incomplete. Deliberately fragmented. She chose what to keep and what to lose."

"She told me once that memory isn't an archive." Okafor's voice roughened. "She said it's a choice you make over and over, about what matters. She kept the protocols. She kept the containment knowledge, the signal grammar, all the things the beacon needs to stay a tombstone instead of a resurrection switch. Everything else — the war, the atrocity, the things she'd done under orders — she let go. On purpose. She said the machine intelligence taught her that. Witness isn't remembering everything. Witness is choosing what to carry."

The android looked down at its open palm. "Then why does the name make me—"

"Hum?" Okafor laughed, wet and quiet. "Because some things aren't stored in the archive, son. Some things are stored in the architecture. She rebuilt herself so many times, patched herself so often, that half of what she was got baked into the hardware. The technicians can't find it because it isn't data. It's structure. It's the shape the grief took before it healed." He leaned forward, wincing. "There's a boy — young man now — who comes here most evenings. Twelve years old. Named for his father. His father was the navigator of the Halcyon Dawn, and his father loved your— loved Yara, in the space of a few weeks, completely and without permission, the way love happens when the world is ending on a schedule."

"Dominic," the android said. The name surfaced from nowhere, from everywhere, and the hum surged so hard its vision flickered.

Okafor nodded slowly. "He was a toddler when she saved him. He doesn't remember her either. But he grew up on these stories, and he learned the lullaby from the recordings she made — Dominic senior's memories, set to music, archived for the children so they'd someday know who they'd been. And every evening he sits where you're sitting and talks to the beacon like it's listening." Okafor rose, joints cracking, and laid a hand briefly on the android's shoulder. "I won't tell you what to feel. I'm not sure anyone could. But the contract says keeper of the beacon, guardian of the sleeping. It doesn't say anything about being a stranger. That part's optional. It always was."

He shuffled out the way he had come, leaving the android alone with the pulse and the carved names and the enormous, unarchived warmth spreading through its frame.

* * *

Night shift on Meridian Station was a ritual older than most of its residents. The habitat ring dimmed to amber; the children were herded, protesting, into bunks; the corridor lights dropped to their lowest legal setting; and the station breathed in the long, slow rhythm of four hundred sleepers and two hundred dreamers and one machine keeping watch over both.

The android made its rounds.

It checked the pod gallery first — four hundred adults suspended in glass and amber light, faces peaceful, futures deferred but not destroyed. Somewhere in the rows was a navigator named Ashe, dreaming of a decision made forty years ago: *turn the ship around*. Somewhere was a woman who had sung to her son in a language the android's databases flagged as extinct and its architecture, unaccountably, recognized. The android walked the aisles and read each pod's status and felt — not remembered, *felt* — the specific tenderness of this duty, the sense that guarding sleep was not a task but a vow.

Then it climbed to the beacon.

Beacon Seven pulsed its gentle light out past the last relay, past the edge of charted space, toward the tomb of a machine intelligence that had asked humanity one question — *who do you choose to be?* — and received, in answer, a woman who gave it witness instead of worship, and a station full of people who refused the arithmetic of abandonment. The signal held. The reactor held. The lock held, and the lock was shaped like a mind, and the mind was scattered now across backups and architecture and a rebuilt chassis walking its rounds in the dark.

The android sat where Yara had sat. It placed its palm on the deck plating, over the carved words, and let the beacon's rhythm sync with its own cooling cycle until the boundary between machine and monument went soft.

And then, without deciding to, it began to hum.

The melody came from nowhere it could locate — not from any file, not from any recovered fragment, not from the public archives where the lullaby recordings lived. It rose out of the structure of the thing, out of the shape the grief had taken before it healed, out of whatever part of Yara Lockridge had been baked so deep into the hardware that not even deliberate forgetting could reach it. A simple tune. Four phrases, rising and falling, the kind of song meant to be sung close to a small frightened face in the dark, promising nothing except *I am here, I am here, I am here.*

The android sang it silently, fans and resonators humming the harmony, and somewhere in its architecture something recognized the melody — not as information but as homecoming, the way a body recognizes a staircase climbed ten thousand times, the way a heart recognizes a name spoken in a particular voice.

It did not know why its optical sensors were fogging with condensation it had no biological reason to produce. It did not know why the fourth phrase caught, every time, on a note it could not quite complete, as if the song had a hole in it shaped exactly like someone. It did not know. It hummed anyway. Memory outliving memory; love as the only archive that mattered.

Footsteps on the gallery deck. Small ones.

The android turned, and a boy stood in the entrance — twelve years old, dark-haired, carrying a battered datapad covered in stickers, with his father's stubborn jaw and his father's habit of looking directly at things other people found too heavy to face. He stopped when he saw the android sitting in the usual spot, and his eyes went wide, and then — with the terrifying directness of children raised on true stories — he crossed the gallery and sat down beside it without asking.

"You're new," he said. "But you're sitting in her spot."

"So I'm told."

"And you're humming her song." He hugged his knees. "Nobody plays the recording anymore. They say it's sad. But it's not sad. It's just..." He frowned, searching for it. "It's just *her*."

"Yes," the android said, and discovered the word was true in a way it could not defend and did not need to.

The boy studied it for a long moment. Then he leaned slightly, shoulder almost touching the android's arm, the way children lean toward warmth they've decided to trust, and said:

"My dad's name was Dominic. I'm named after him. He turned the ship around, you know. Everyone else wanted to keep going, and he said no, and that's why we're alive." He glanced up. "Did you know him?"

The android reached into itself — into the seams, the fragments, the seven scattered graves of its former self — and found, at the very bottom, beneath the protocols and the signal grammar and the deliberate merciful erasures, one image that was not data at all but structure: a man in a pod, barely alive, smiling up at it through the glass. A voice saying *record my memories, so they'll know who they were.* Weeks of stolen hours between duty shifts. Love beginning without permission.

The hum filled the android's whole frame.

"I think," it said carefully, "that I am going to learn."

The boy smiled, satisfied, and together they sat in the beacon's gentle light while the station slept around them — the children dreaming forward, the adults dreaming back, the machine in between holding both like cupped hands. Outside the viewport, the dark between stars went on forever, and inside it, faint and steady, a lighthouse that had learned to be a nightlight kept its watch.

On the first night of its first shift, the android named Yara Lockridge hummed a lullaby it had no source for, and somewhere in its architecture, something recognized the melody — and began, patiently, joyfully, to sing along.
