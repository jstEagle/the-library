## 1. Nine Seconds

There are two kinds of quiet aboard a dead ship, and I've had eleven years to learn the difference.

The first kind is the honest one: the hum of the Ox-Alpha's aging reactor, the tick of thermal expansion in the hull as we swing through Meridian's shadow, the sound of nothing happening, forever. That quiet is my colleague. My co-conspirator, even. We keep the secret together.

The second kind of quiet is the one that comes *before*.

I was running the morning navigation check—routine number forty-one hundred and something, I stopped counting at three thousand because the numbers started feeling like a sentence—when the second kind of quiet arrived. It came in through the soles of my boots first. A stillness in the deck plating, like the ship holding its breath.

Then gravity across the entire Meridian system flickered for exactly nine seconds.

I know it was nine seconds because everything aboard the Ox-Alpha woke up at once to tell me so, and none of them agreed on anything except the duration. The inertial dampers screamed about harmonic drift. The long-range array reported phantom mass where no mass should be. The coffee I'd left on the console—the one luxury I permit myself, reconstituted and bitter as regret—lifted off its surface in a slow brown bloom and hung there, trembling, like a small ugly galaxy deciding whether to exist.

And then it came back down. All of it. Every drop, every loose bolt, every drifting mote of dust in every corridor, settling with a soft collective sigh, as if the universe had cleared its throat and thought better of speaking.

For a moment I just stood there in the nav bay, coffee dripping off the edge of the console onto my boot, listening to forty alarms argue with each other.

Here's what you need to understand about me: I am not a brave person. Bravery requires an audience, or at least the possibility of one, and I have spent eleven years being the only witness to my own life. What I am instead is *thorough*. Thoroughness is fear wearing a lab coat. And so while every instinct I owned was screaming *run, hide, power down, pretend to be a rock*, my hands were already moving—silencing alarms, pulling sensor dumps, opening the log.

Because that's the other thing about me. I record things.

It started as duty. The Ox-Alpha carries two thousand colonists in cryo-sleep down in the hold, and someone has to keep the lights on and the orbit stable until—well. Until. Eleven years ago that "until" had a shape. These days it's more of a shrug. But the recording, that became something else somewhere along the way. A private archive. Every decay curve of the colony below, every storm on Meridian's face, every light going out in the settlement grid, one by one, like a city closing its eyes. I filmed the greenhouse domes going brown. I filmed the last transmission tower leaning, and then falling, and then not being there anymore. I don't know who the archive is for. Maybe future me. Maybe nobody. Maybe it's just that if I stop writing it all down, then none of it happened, and if none of it happened, then I'm alone on a ship over a graveyard for reasons I can't explain even to myself.

So: nine seconds of system-wide gravitational anomaly. Logged. Timestamped. Cross-referenced against every instrument that had an opinion.

And here is what the instruments said, once I let them finish shouting:

This wasn't us. It wasn't the colony—Meridian's grid has been dead for years; whatever's left down there couldn't ripple a puddle, let alone spacetime. It wasn't a natural phenomenon, unless nature has recently developed a sense of timing, because the event was *clean*. Nine seconds, sharp edges, uniform across a volume of space some four billion kilometers wide. Gravity doesn't do sharp edges. Gravity is a drunk; it stumbles, it slurs, it fades at the margins. This was sober. This was deliberate.

Someone out there turned the gravity off and on again.

Like a man knocking on a door to see if anyone's home.

I sat with that for a long time. Long enough for the coffee stain on my boot to dry into a map of nowhere. Because there are only two possible readings, and I didn't like either of them.

Reading one: nobody did this. It was an artifact, a glitch, a cosmic hiccup, and I am a lonely man hallucinating significance into static. Comfortable reading. Familiar reading. I've been half-expecting to lose my mind for about a decade now, and in some ways it would be a relief.

Reading two: someone else is out there. Someone with technology that makes ours look like sharpened flint. Someone who just announced their presence to an entire star system—and specifically, whether they meant to or not, to the derelict freighter hiding in a decaying orbit above a colony everyone believes is empty.

Eleven years of invisibility. Eleven years of filing false telemetry, spoofing our transponder, coasting dark between correction burns, playing corpse. And in nine seconds, all of it might have come undone.

I did what any thorough coward would do. I lied.

The official log entry took me twenty minutes to write, which is nineteen minutes longer than it should have. *Minor gravitational sensor anomaly, localized to Ox-Alpha, attributed to damper calibration drift. Corrective maintenance scheduled.* Clean, boring, forgettable. The kind of entry no one reads, because no one ever reads them—which is precisely why I've survived this long. The truth went into the other archive. The real one. Entry one of what I suspect will become a very different book.

Then I did the thing I always do when the universe hands me something unbearable: I made tea, and I looked out the window.

Meridian hung below us, half-lit, beautiful in the way that ruins are beautiful—the way a burned church is beautiful, all bones and sky. From up here you can't see the rust. You can't see the collapsed habitat rings or the fields gone to ironweed or the tower I watched fall four years ago. You see a blue-grey marble with white storms, and you can almost pretend it's sleeping instead of dead. Almost pretend the two thousand people in my hold are the only ones still dreaming of it.

I've spent eleven years telling myself the same story: *everyone forgot this place. That's why we're safe. Grief is invisible; paperwork is permanent. No one comes back for a footnote.*

But somebody just knocked.

* * *

I spent the rest of the day-cycle doing something I hadn't done since year six: a full passive sweep. No active emissions, nothing that could paint us on anyone's scope. Just ears. Wide open.

The system gave me its usual silence. Solar wind. The dying whisper of Meridian's residual magnetosphere. Background radiation doing its eternal impression of nothing. I catalogued it all, and it catalogued right back at me with perfect indifference, and by hour six I was almost ready to believe Reading One. Glitch. Hiccup. Lonely man, empty sky.

Almost.

Because at 19:42 ship-time, way out past the heliopause threshold, at the very edge of resolution where the array starts guessing instead of measuring, something moved. A thermal bloom, brief—a drive flare, maybe, or maybe an artifact, or maybe my tired eyes inventing company the way drowning men invent shore. It lasted less than a second. Then nothing. The dark closed over it like water.

I ran the capture eleven times. The bloom was there in eight of them. Real enough.

Somebody was out there. Somebody far enough away that they shouldn't have seen us, close enough that they'd chosen this system out of all the systems in all the black. And nine hours before they showed themselves at the edge of my sensors, they had knocked on every door in the neighborhood at once.

Not a knock, I realized, staring at that fading smudge of heat. A *roll call*. Checking what answered.

Which meant the question wasn't *is someone out there?*

The question was *what did we just answer?*

I sat in the dark of the nav bay with the sweep results glowing on my face and felt the specific dread of a man who has hidden so long he's forgotten how to be found. Eleven years of routine had made me soft in exactly the ways that mattered. I knew the decay curves of a dead colony better than I knew my own heartbeat. I did not know what to do about living people.

Protocol said: maintain silence, maintain orbit, maintain the lie. Protocol had kept us alive. Protocol was also written by people who assumed the danger would come from the colony, from failure, from entropy—not from rescue.

Because that's what it might be, whispered the part of me I keep locked in the hold with the sleepers. That flare might be a ship full of people coming to do what the Ox-Alpha never managed: wake them up. All of them. Bring everyone home from the long cold.

And that's the trap, isn't it. The thought I haven't let myself think in eleven years, because thinking it makes the waiting unbearable, and the waiting is the only thing I'm good at.

If they're here to save the colonists, then the secret ends. The archive ends. The orbit ends. *I* end—or the version of me that has lived quietly above a graveyard, curator of a catastrophe, keeper of two thousand sleepers and one impossible arithmetic. Because the hold doesn't hold enough revival capacity for two thousand. It never did. Half of them can wake. Half of them cannot. And I am the only person in this system who knows which half, and how, and why, and every day I don't tell them is a day I choose for them.

I had eleven years to get comfortable with that choice. I had nine seconds to remember it.

The tea went cold. The bloom on the edge of the array faded to nothing. Somewhere below me, under thirty kilometers of cloud, a girl I have never met and do not know exists was looking up at the sky—at the star that has moved every night of her life, the one she'll have named by now, the one she thinks is hers—and she was about to start building something out of salvage and stubbornness that was going to make my nine seconds look like a rounding error.

But that night, all I knew was this: the quiet had ended. The second kind of quiet, the pre-quiet, had passed, and what came after it was not sound but *attention*. The whole system, suddenly observed. Our long invisibility, over.

I opened the real archive and added a line beneath the gravity log:

*They're coming. God help me—I hope they're coming for them, and not for me. I don't know which answer I deserve.*

Then I sealed the entry, dimmed the lights, and began, for the first time in eleven years, to plan for morning.
