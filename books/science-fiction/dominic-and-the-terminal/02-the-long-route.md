## 2. The Long Route

The *Kestrel's own ship*—she had named it the *Magpie*, because magpies stole beautiful things and never apologized for it—ran dark and quiet through the long middle of nowhere, that stretch of nothing between Halcyon's glittering shell and the frozen world below where ten thousand people slept inside a machine that was, though she did not yet know it, dying.

She did not know it yet. What Kestrel Eriksen knew, three days out from her last delivery, was this: the long route took eleven days if you nursed your burn, fourteen if you wanted to live to be old, and in all that time there was nothing to do but maintain the ship, sleep, eat reconstituted food that tasted faintly of the packaging, and think.

Thinking was the hazard. Every smuggler knew it. The void between worlds was where men went mad, where women opened their own veins with surgical calm, where the silence got into you like water into a hull. So smugglers developed rituals. Some played cards against themselves. Some talked to their ships' dumb little autopilots until the autopilots seemed to answer. One old runner she'd known on Ceres had memorized epic poetry and recited it aloud in a voice gone hoarse with loneliness, and when he'd finally retired he could no longer speak any other way.

Kestrel's ritual was worse than all of those.

Kestrel reviewed her inventory.

* * *

The chip reader sat bolted to the bulkhead beside her bunk, a black-market unit with its serial number ground away and a hairline crack across the display that she'd never bothered to fix because the crack ran through the corner of the image and never through the center, and the center was what mattered. She fed the first chip in the way she always did: thumb against the edge, a small breath held without meaning to, the click as it seated.

The cabin fell away.

A beach. Not a real beach anymore—there were no real beaches anymore, not since the coastlines had been redrawn by water and war and neglect—but a beach that had existed once, in the childhood of a woman named Mara Eriksen, who had carried it in her head for forty years before deciding that memory was too heavy a thing to lose to death, and so had paid a recording clinic in Rotterdam-Under-Dome an obscene sum to crystallize it.

Mara Eriksen had been Kestrel's mother.

In the memory, Kestrel was six years old, or someone was six years old—the memory belonged to her mother, so the six-year-old was seen from above, from the vantage of a woman looking down at her daughter building something in the wet sand. A wall, maybe. A lagoon. The child's hair was pale in the sun and her concentration was total, tongue between her teeth, and the mother watching felt the thing that mothers feel, which is too large for language and which the recording clinic's technicians had flagged in the metadata as *intensity spike, unresolvable, retained.*

The sea came in. It always came in. That was the shape of the memory: the child builds, the sea takes, the child laughs instead of crying, and the mother stands behind her own eyes thinking, *she'll be fine, she'll be better than fine, she'll build things the sea can't reach.*

Then the memory ended, the way memories do, not with a conclusion but with a thinning, a fade to the gray static of unrecorded time, and Kestrel was back in her bunk with the crack running through the corner of the display and salt water drying invisibly on her cheeks.

She wiped her face with the heel of her hand. She did this every time. She would do it every time again.

That was the arrangement she had made with grief: not to defeat it, not to drown in it, but to visit it on schedule, the way you'd visit a grave. Her mother had died when Kestrel was nineteen—lung rot, from the filtration levels they'd allowed in the lower domes because clean air was priced like wine—and had left her exactly two things: a debt to the clinic that Kestrel had spent five years paying off, and a single crystal chip containing ninety seconds of a beach.

Everything else about her mother lived now only in Kestrel's own unreliable flesh-memory, already blurring. The exact pitch of her laugh. The smell of her hair, which had been some cheap soap whose name Kestrel could no longer retrieve. She had tried, over the years, to record her own memories of Mara, but self-recorded memory was a copy of a copy, softening each generation like a photograph left in the sun. The chip was the last true thing. She kept it in a lined case with eleven other chips, and she thought of them, privately, as her reliquary.

Because here was the secret of Kestrel Eriksen's trade, the one nobody said aloud in the memory markets of Halcyon, where the trade was called *archival services* and conducted in boutiques with soft lighting: everyone who bought a memory was buying back a dead person. Or a drowned coastline. Or a city that stood before the Burn. Or their own younger face. The whole industry, legal and illegal alike, was one vast machine for refusing to let go, and Kestrel had made herself a courier for other people's refusals while hoarding the largest refusal of all in a case beside her bunk.

She was not proud of this. But she was not ashamed either. Shame required believing there was another way to live, and she had looked, and there wasn't.

* * *

On the fourth day she passed over Terminus.

You didn't see it so much as feel it—a gravitational courtesy, a slight lean in the deck plates as the *Magpie* bent her course around the sleeping world. Kestrel floated at the viewport with a bulb of tea and watched it turn below her: white from pole to pole, wrapped in cloud, its surface scabbed with the dark geometry of the colony's structures. The cryo-arcologies. The reactor complex with its cooling fins spread like the wings of something buried alive.

Ten thousand people down there, asleep. They had boarded the colony ships two generations ago fleeing the same ruined Earth that Kestrel's mother's beach belonged to, and when the fleet's power mathematics had come up short—and the mathematics always came up short; that was the first law of colonies—the planners had made the choice that planners make. Half the colonists would sleep. Half would stay awake to run the ships, build the orbital habitats, keep the lights on. The sleepers would dream through the hard years and wake when the account balanced.

The awake half had become Halcyon. The sleeping half had become Terminus. And somewhere in the intervening decades, without anyone voting on it, without anyone even noticing, the arrangement had stopped being temporary. Halcyon orbited fat and bright, its rings studded with gardens and universities and the pale towers of the Council, burning power like it was weather. Terminus slept below in the cold, its reactor ticking down through a maintenance schedule that nobody up top ever asked to see.

Kestrel had asked to see it once. Early days, when she'd still believed systems could be audited. She'd filed a request through proper channels and received, four months later, a form letter thanking her for her interest in colonial infrastructure.

So she'd found another way to look. There was always another way; that was her whole profession.

What she ran to Terminus, in the shipments the customs manifests described as *agricultural diagnostics* or *educational media*, were memories. Small ones, mostly. A wedding. A grandmother's kitchen. The sound of rain—which the sleepers' children, born in the pods or in Terminus's frozen corridors, had never heard except as data. The awake caretakers down there, the skeleton crew of Ilsa-and-people like Ilsa, paid in labor scrip and favors, and Kestrel charged them almost nothing, which was bad business and which she did anyway, because you could not spend your life ferrying other people's dead relatives and remain entirely a creature of arithmetic.

And what she ran back from Terminus, in the other direction, up to Halcyon—that traffic she thought about less, or told herself she thought about less. The elite liked novelties. They liked to *feel* things: authentic grief, authentic terror, the authentic memory of poverty worn like vintage clothing to a party. There were buyers up there who would pay a month's caretaker wages to experience, safely, briefly, the sensation of being cold.

She watched Terminus turn beneath her and drank her tea, and the tea had gone bitter, and she couldn't say whether it was the tea.

* * *

On the sixth day, the loneliness arrived on schedule.

It always did, around day six, right on time, punctual as customs. It came as a kind of pressure behind the sternum, an awareness of the sheer volume of nothing pressing against the hull, and it brought with it the questions it always brought: *Who are you doing this for? Who would notice? If the Magpie broke her heart out here, how long would the universe take to mention it?*

Kestrel had rules for this too. Rule one: no names on the comms. Rule two: no routes repeated within a quarter-year. Rule three—the rule that mattered, the rule she'd carved after Anselm, though she'd have shot anyone who suggested that was why—no attachments. You could love people, fine, love them from orbit, love them at a distance measured in light-seconds and secrets. But you did not give them a way to find you, and you did not give yourself a reason to come back.

She broke rule three in her mind sometimes, out here, in ways that cost nothing and therefore everything. She let herself remember Anselm Voss's voice saying her name—not the council security chief he'd become, but the young archivist's assistant he'd been, with ink on his fingers and a laugh he tried to suppress in the archive stacks because the stacks were sacred. She remembered the winter they'd shared a room in the underlevels of Halcyon, poor as churchmice, happier than either of them knew how to admit. She remembered the morning she'd left, and the specific way he hadn't tried to stop her, and how she had decided, in the shuttle, that his not-fighting was a kindness, and how she had spent the years since occasionally suspecting it was actually just a door closing quietly.

Then she put him away, the way she put everything away, in the lined case with the others.

The trouble with being a courier of memory, she thought, watching the stars hold perfectly still around her, is that you learn exactly how much a person can survive losing. You learn that the self is not a solid thing but a collection, an archive, and that archives can be raided. She had seen buyers who had sold so many of their own memories to pay debts that they'd become polite strangers to themselves, greeting their own reflections like new acquaintances. She had seen a man in the Halcyon markets weep over a chip of someone else's mother, because his own had been seized in a bankruptcy auction—memories counted as assets, under Halcyon law, and assets could be liquidated.

Her mother's face was worth, on the open market, perhaps four hundred credits. Less, probably. Used family memory, common demographic, no historical significance. Four hundred credits for the only true version of a woman who had worked double shifts in the filtration plants so her daughter could breathe marginally cleaner air.

Kestrel had been offered more than four hundred credits for it twice. Both times she had said no with such flat finality that the brokers had actually stepped back, the way you step back from a dog that has gone suddenly, quietly still.

* * *

On the eighth day, she ran the reliquary again, all twelve chips, in order, because day eight was reliquary day and ritual was armor.

There was the beach. There was her mother's voice, recorded accidentally at the edge of a market memory Kestrel had bought as a teenager with her first smuggling earnings—three seconds of Mara calling her name across a crowded dome plaza, *Kestrel! Kestrel, love,* and then the crowd swallowing her. Three seconds. She had played it so many times that she could hear it now without the chip, note-perfect, and understood dimly that this meant the chip was becoming redundant, that her own brain had finally copied it faithfully enough, and that this should have felt like a victory and instead felt like a small death, because the true thing was becoming unnecessary and unnecessary true things get sold.

There was the rain, which wasn't hers—she'd recorded it herself, standing in a Halcyon arboretum during the simulated monsoon season, holding a consumer rig above her head like a chalice while wealthy children shrieked under artificial thunder. There was the taste of real coffee, pre-Burn, purchased from a dying man's estate. There was Earth entire, seen from high orbit by a long-dead pilot, blue and whole and unbroken, a memory so valuable that owning it legally would have required her to be a different species of person.

Twelve chips. Twelve refusals to let go. She sealed the case, clipped it back to the bulkhead, and said aloud, to the empty cabin, in the voice she used for no one:

"Still here."

It was not a prayer. Prayers assumed someone was listening. It was more like a countersignature—the ritual exchange between sentries, *all quiet, all present*, passed down the line of one.

* * *

On the tenth day, the *Magpie* crossed the meridian where the long route bent downward toward Terminus, and Kestrel began her descent checklist, and that was when the message caught her.

Not a message, exactly. Later she would understand it had been a message, routed through three dead drops and a decommissioned relay buoy, compressed and encrypted and disguised as a corrupted telemetry packet from a mining drone. But at the moment it arrived it was simply an anomaly: a flicker on a channel she kept tuned out of habit rather than hope, a fragment of signal where there should have been silence.

She almost discarded it. Protocol said discard. Protocol said anything arriving unsolicited on the long route was either a trap or a mistake, and both deserved the same treatment.

But the fragment resolved, for half a second before the encryption swallowed it, into something odd. Not text. Not voice.

A counting voice. Childlike, patient, mechanical underneath its patience, reciting numbers downward from some enormous figure toward some smaller one. And threaded through the numbers, faint as a watermark, the sound of a red door opening.

Kestrel sat very still in the pilot's chair with her tea going cold in the holder and the descent checklist glowing unfinished on her display, and listened to the fragment loop, and felt the particular prickle along her arms that she had learned, in nineteen years of this trade, to trust above all instruments and all instincts.

Curiosity. The one cargo she could never refuse.

Below her, Terminus turned in the dark, white and silent, ten thousand sleepers dreaming inside a machine that was counting down to something. Above her, Halcyon burned bright and blind. Between them, Kestrel Eriksen hung in her stolen ship with a dead-drop fragment playing in the quiet, and reached out, and decoded it.
