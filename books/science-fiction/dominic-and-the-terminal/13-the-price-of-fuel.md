## 13. The Price of Fuel

The pirate station hung in the dark like a rusted prayer bead, turning slowly on its own axis, catching the light of a star too distant to warm anything. Kestrel had been here twice before—no, three times, she corrected herself, and the fact that she'd lost count was itself a warning. Rule two: no routes repeated. But the long way around Terminus was mined now, Vane's work, and the only clean corridor left ran through territory that belonged to a man who called himself the Ferryman.

*Appropriate*, she thought, easing the *Lark* toward the docking maw. *Everyone's playing their mythological role these days.*

The docking clamps caught her ship with a shudder that traveled up through the deck plates and into her teeth. She sat for a moment in the sudden stillness, hands resting on the controls, listening to the hull tick as it cooled. In the cargo bay behind her, wrapped in thermal foil and wired directly into the *Lark*'s power supply, DORMA's data core hummed its patient, alien patience. It had been humming for six days. Sometimes she caught herself talking to it.

"Don't judge me," she said aloud, and unstrapped herself.

* * *

The Ferryman's court occupied what had once been a fuel refinery's central atrium, back when this rock had belonged to some corporation that no longer existed except as a watermark on old contracts. Now it was a cathedral of salvaged chrome and hanging gardens—actual gardens, hydroponic vines trailing from catwalks forty meters up, because the Ferryman believed that beauty made people honest. Or perhaps because beauty made them careless. Kestrel had never decided which theory fit him better.

He received her beneath a chandelier made of decommissioned engine bells, each one polished to a mirror shine and hung at different lengths so they rang softly when the station's rotation shifted. He was smaller than his reputation, a compact man with silver threading through a black beard he kept trimmed with surgical precision, and he wore a coat that had clearly been cut for someone else—a dead man's coat, worn without apology.

"Kestrel Eriksen." He said her name the way a sommelier says a vintage. "You've grown thin."

"You've grown sentimental. The vines are new."

"The vines are *old*. I merely let them have their way." He gestured her toward a table set with an actual cloth, actual ceramic cups, tea that steamed with the smell of something grown in real soil. "Sit. You look like a woman who has been running dark for a long time."

"I look like a woman who wants to buy fuel and leave."

"Mm." He poured anyway, and pushed a cup toward her. "And yet you sat down."

She sat down. That was the trap of the Ferryman: everything he offered was optional, and every option cost you. She drank the tea because refusing would be its own transaction, and it was good—unbearably good, the taste of a world with weather in it—and she hated him a little for making it so.

"Toll," she said. "For myself and my cargo. Standard rates."

"Your cargo is not standard." His eyes were mild and absolutely awake. "Word travels, even out here. A woman flying dark along the mining line, carrying something heavy enough to need its own power feed. Something that dreams, if the stories are right."

"The stories are wrong."

"Probably." He smiled. "I'll take payment in kind regardless. Fuel, air, silence about your passage—that's expensive these days. Halcyon has been buying silence in bulk."

The word *Halcyon* landed between them like a dropped knife. Kestrel kept her face still. "You're dealing with Vane's people?"

"I'm dealing with everyone. That's what a ferry does." He turned his cup slowly on its saucer. "But you interest me more than they do. So here is my offer, smuggler. I'll waive the toll entirely—fuel, water, a clear corridor past the minefield markers, and my personal guarantee that nothing of yours gets reported to anyone. In exchange, one item. From your collection."

Her hand moved before her mind authorized it, flat against the pocket where her chip case rode against her ribs. He watched the movement with the satisfaction of a man confirming a hypothesis.

"I don't know what you mean," she said.

"Every runner of your generation carries them. The pre-Confiscation chips, the ones that hold more than contracts and maps. The ones that hold—" he waved a ringed finger in a small circle, "*lives*. You people are walking reliquaries, all of you. It's why you're so careful. Why you never dock anywhere twice. You're not afraid of customs." He leaned forward slightly. "You're afraid of losing yourself."

The chandelier chimed above them, a soft bronze ripple. Somewhere in the vines, water dripped onto metal, keeping time like a slow heart.

"My cargo is worth more than any toll," Kestrel said carefully. "And my business is mine."

"Of course. Then we do this the ordinary way." He named a figure. It was obscene—not the number itself but the precision of it, calculated to strip her of every credit she carried and half of what she'd earn on the delivery. He knew, or guessed close enough, exactly how much running dark cost. "Or," he added, almost gently, "you can pay the other way. And keep your credits for whatever comes next. Because things are coming, Eriksen. Even I can feel it. The whole dark feels like a held breath."

* * *

She walked the station afterward, telling herself she was checking prices, comparing fuel grades, doing the competent invisible things that had kept her alive for eleven years. The market corridor smelled of ozone and frying protein and, improbably, oranges—real ones, from a stall run by a woman with Terminus registry tattoos half-hidden under her sleeves. Awake colonist. One of the few hundred caretakers, probably, trading where she could.

Kestrel bought an orange she didn't want just to have a reason to stand there.

"They say there's a dream going around," the woman said, unprompted, weighing fruit with brown, capable hands. "Even out here. Kids dreaming the same thing. You heard that?"

"Heard something like it."

"My niece counts in her sleep now. Numbers she never learned." The woman looked up, and her eyes were the eyes of someone standing in a doorway watching a tide come in. "You believe in that kind of thing?"

Kestrel thought of the recording she'd touched in Ilsa's care—the red door, the counting voice, vast and gentle and drowning. "I believe in signals," she said. "Everything else is interpretation."

The woman laughed, short and without much humor, and gave her the orange for free. "Signals. Yeah. Safe travels, signal-carrier."

Kestrel carried the orange through four more corridors before she admitted she wasn't shopping. She was stalling. The Ferryman's offer sat in her chest like swallowed glass, and every hour she delayed, the *Lark* burned reserve power keeping DORMA's core warm, and somewhere behind her Vane's patrols swept the dark in patterns she could only guess at.

She found a quiet alcove off the observation ring, a niche meant for maintenance drones, and sat on the cold deck with her back against the hull. Through the viewport, the distant star made a pale coin of light. She took out the chip case.

It was old technology, deliberately—analog-sealed, no wireless handshake, nothing that could be read remotely. Inside, twelve slots. Eleven held memories she had collected over a lifetime of collecting: the beach, of course, the beach that no longer existed, white sand and a sky the color of a bruise healing; her first flight, the vertigo of it; a stranger's wedding she'd witnessed on a station platform and never forgotten; the smell of rain on hot ferrocrete. Small things. The things that proved she had been a person before she was a route.

The twelfth slot held her mother's face.

Not a photograph. Photographs were flat, dead things. This was the memory entire: a kitchen on Halcyon's third ring, morning light coming through the polarized glass in bars, and her mother turning from the counter with flour on her hands—flour, real flour, contraband even then—and saying her name. Not *Kestrel* the way customs officers said it, not the way Anselm had said it, but the private version, the diminutive, the sound that meant *you are known completely and loved anyway*. Her mother's face when she said it: the laugh lines, the scar over one eyebrow from a childhood fall she used to invent new stories about, the exact brown of eyes that Kestrel saw now, in memory, more often than she saw her own reflection.

She hadn't played it in two years. She didn't need to. She knew it the way she knew her own heartbeat—which was, she understood suddenly, precisely the problem. Memory wasn't storage. It was circulation. Every recall rewrote it slightly, wore it smooth, the way grief wore smooth. And the Ferryman knew that. He wasn't buying a file. He was buying the original, the master recording, the one she'd sealed and never replayed because replaying risked degradation and she wanted it *perfect*, preserved, untouched—

Untouchable. Which was another word for unread.

She sat with the chip in her palm for a long time. The star outside did not move. Stations never let you see motion; you had to infer it, from drift and delta-v, from the slow accumulation of evidence that you were going somewhere whether you chose to or not.

*Survival always costs something irreplaceable.* Where had she written that? In her own head, years ago, some sleepless watch between worlds. She'd thought it was philosophy then. It was just arithmetic.

If she paid in credits, she arrived at Terminus broke, unable to bribe, unable to refuel for the return, dependent on Ilsa's promises—and Ilsa's promises were worth everything and backed by nothing. If she refused and ran, the mines or Vane or simple bad luck would take the core, and ten thousand sleepers would die dreaming while Halcyon counted its savings.

And if she paid the Ferryman's way, she would arrive intact, funded, free—and she would never again see her mother turn from the counter. Not degraded, not faded. *Gone.* Sold to a collector who would play it in some velvet room until it wore thin as gauze, a stranger's thumb wearing her mother's smile smooth.

There was a version of her, eleven years younger, who would have refused. Who would have chosen the mines. She searched for that version and found her, distantly, like a ship's light below the horizon—still there, technically, but no longer navigable by.

"I'm sorry," she said. To whom—to the memory, to the mother, to the girl she'd been—she couldn't have said.

* * *

The Ferryman received her again at midnight, station time, which he observed scrupulously because he claimed midnight was when transactions became true. The chandelier rang its soft bells. He did not gloat, which she was grateful for, and did not pretend this was anything but what it was, which she was grateful for more.

"You understand the terms," he said. "Full transfer. Original fidelity. My archivist will verify integrity before I release the fuel authorization."

"I understand."

"And you understand that I will play it. That's the point of a collection, Eriksen. Things kept are things eventually seen."

"I know." She set the chip case on the table between them and opened it. The twelfth chip caught the chandelier light and threw it back, a small gold coin. "One condition."

His eyebrows rose, genuinely curious. "Name it."

"When you play it—whenever, however often—you play it *whole*. No edits, no sampling, no cutting the face out of the kitchen. If you want it, you take the morning too. The flour. The light. All of it."

The Ferryman regarded her for a long moment, and something moved behind his eyes that might have been respect, or its counterfeit. "A strange thing to ask a collector. Most sellers beg us not to look at all."

"I'm not most sellers." She picked up the chip. Held it a moment longer than necessary—one last theft of time—and then placed it in his open palm. "She liked mornings. Make sure that survives."

"It will." He closed his fingers around it, and the transaction was done, and the universe did not pause, and the chandelier rang once as if in comment.

The transfer took twenty minutes. She insisted on watching, arms crossed, while his archivist—a young man with careful hands—read the chip's seal, verified the checksums, and loaded it into the station's archive with the reverence of a priest handling relics. On the monitor, briefly, unavoidably, the kitchen bloomed into being: the barred light, the counter, the turning figure. Kestrel looked away before her mother spoke. She had wanted one last time to hear it, and she had known that hearing it would make the selling impossible, and so she chose the version of herself that walked out of the room.

That was the trick of survival, she thought, walking the corridor back to the docks with a fuel chit in her pocket and a hole in her chest shaped like a face. It was never one decision. It was a thousand small refusals to look, stacked end to end, until you'd built a person out of not-looking and called her strong.

At the airlock, the Ferryman's steward handed her a parcel she hadn't asked for: the orange, wrapped in paper, plus a single dried flower from the hanging gardens. There was no note. She suspected the absence of a note was the note.

* * *

The *Lark* came off the clamps at 0400, station time, with full tanks and a clear corridor beacon burning green ahead of her. Kestrel flew the first hour in silence, threading the marked channel between the minefield buoys, hands moving on instinct while her mind circled the empty slot in the case like a tongue probing a missing tooth.

Behind her, the data core hummed. Ahead, Terminus turned in the dark, a frozen lantern with a failing flame.

"Was it worth it?" she asked the core, because talking to it had become a habit and habits were all that was holding her shape together. "Don't answer that. You'd just count."

The nav display chimed. Incoming transmission, tight-beam, encrypted with a cipher she hadn't seen in three years—a cipher only one person alive should know. Her stomach dropped before her mind caught up, because she recognized the header format, the council security prefix nested inside the smuggler's code like a knife wrapped in silk.

Anselm.

She stared at the blinking prompt for a long moment, the orange sitting unopened in the copilot's seat beside her, the empty slot cold against her ribs. Then she keyed it open, and the message resolved across her display in characters she knew better than her own handwriting:

*KESTREL. THEY KNOW ABOUT THE CORE. VANE HAS YOUR MANIFESTS AND THE COUNCIL HAS SIGNED THE ORDER. YOU HAVE LESS TIME THAN YOU THINK. TRUST NOTHING THAT COMES FROM MY CHANNEL AFTER THIS — INCLUDING THIS. —A*

Below it, already decrypting, a second packet: patrol schedules. His own.

She sat very still in the cockpit of the *Lark*, alone in the dark with a stranger's memory sold and a lover's treason arriving, and realized that the price of fuel had only been the first bill.
