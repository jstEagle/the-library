## 19. What Memory Is For

The core sat on the table between them like a sleeping animal—DORMA's data lattice, black and faintly warm, humming at a frequency Kestrel felt in her teeth rather than heard. Around it, the smuggler's den beneath Terminus's docking ring smelled of solder flux and recycled air and the particular staleness of a place where no one had opened a real window in forty years.

"You're not listening," Ilsa said.

"I'm listening." Kestrel turned the core a quarter-turn with one finger. "I just don't like what I'm hearing."

"DORMA can't split the power allocation remotely. The firewalls between Terminus's reactor network and Halcyon's grid are physical as well as digital—council-built, council-locked. The decision has to be *carried*. Entered from inside both systems, by hand, by someone the networks will accept." Ilsa's voice was steady in the way of people who had learned steadiness as a professional skill, holding other people's lives in their hands for years. "It has to be a mind. A mind with clearance or access to both worlds."

"Or," Kestrel said, "a mind that knows how to get into places it doesn't belong."

"That too."

Kestrel leaned back in her chair until its frame creaked. She was tired in a way sleep didn't touch anymore. Three days since the run through the mining field, since Vane sealed the ring and she'd threaded the *Terminal* through corridors no chart admitted existed, children strapped into cargo webbing behind her, singing to keep themselves calm because she'd told them singing was good luck and they'd believed her, because children believe you when you give them something to hold.

Now DORMA's question sat in the room with them, patient as arithmetic. *Half the power. Choose which half wakes.* And the answer Kestrel had found in the long dark hours after the siege—the answer that was neither Halcyon's dissidents nor Terminus's engineers but both, power split, futures braided—was useless unless it could cross the gap between two worlds that had spent a century pretending the other didn't exist.

"So we send the data," Kestrel said. "Burst transmission, encrypted, through one of my old relay ghosts—"

"Vane owns the relays now. Every packet leaving Terminus gets read, copied, and traced. You know this. You've been running dark for six days." Ilsa folded her arms. "Anything digital is dead on arrival. You said so yourself."

"I said so myself," Kestrel agreed sourly.

"There's one channel left that Vane can't intercept. One courier he can't decrypt." Ilsa looked at her, and Kestrel saw that Ilsa had known the shape of this conversation before it started, had perhaps rehearsed it alone in the caretaker's quarters while the children slept, hating herself for what she was about to ask. "A human memory. Imprinted whole. Carried past customs in the only vessel that's never once been searched."

"My head," Kestrel said.

"Your head."

* * *

She walked the docking ring afterward, alone, because there was a version of this decision that deserved solitude and a version that was just fear wearing solitude's coat, and she wanted to know which one she was carrying.

Terminus at this hour was all sound and dim light: the tick of cooling pipes, the whisper of cryo-pods breathing their ten thousand slow breaths, the children's dormitory humming with the particular electricity of kids who had recently learned the world might end and had decided, in the way of children, to be thrilled about it. A girl named Maret waved at Kestrel from a doorway, missing front tooth, sleeves too long. Kestrel waved back. Maret had been in the cargo hold during the siege. Maret had sung.

*They'll strip you,* said the voice in her head that sounded like Anselm and sometimes like her mother and mostly like herself. *You know what Vane does to captured couriers. Memory extraction isn't surgery, it's demolition. They take everything. Not the mission—everything. Your name. Your routes. Your mother's face. You'll wake up a stranger wearing your own body, and whatever's left of you will be a filing cabinet in a Halcyon intelligence office.*

Yes, Kestrel thought. I know.

That was the thing about being a smuggler that nobody romanticized correctly. Everyone imagined the thrill, the borders, the beautiful danger of it. Nobody talked about the accounting. Every job was a ledger, every risk a price, and Kestrel had spent fifteen years keeping her books balanced with a simple rule set: no names, no repeated routes, no attachments. Rules that were really just ways of deciding, in advance, what she would never be forced to pay.

And here was the ledger now, open, demanding an entry. The entire decision—DORMA's split allocation, the engineering sequence, the authentication keys for both networks' human interfaces, the argument itself, the *reason*, because a firewall didn't just need data, it needed a mind that understood what it was carrying well enough to defend the choice under interrogation—all of it imprinted onto her cortex, woven into her actual memories so deeply that no scan could separate contraband from self. That was the trick. That was the only trick left. Customs could search a chip in seconds. But a memory lived in the same tissue as grief, as love, as the smell of a beach that no longer existed, and no machine yet built could cut one thread without unraveling the cloth.

Which meant she couldn't leave it behind if she was caught. Which meant Vane wouldn't have to find it. He'd simply take *her*.

She stopped at an observation port. Below—or rather out, since Terminus had no below, only the frozen dark and the blue-white curve of the planet they'd all fled—Halcyon hung in its high orbit, glittering, smug, a ring of light like a crown worn by someone who'd stolen it. Somewhere under that light was Anselm. Somewhere under it was Sef Oduya's archive full of years-old murder-by-spreadsheet. Somewhere under it were dissident families in sealed districts who didn't know their names were on a list, waiting for someone to carry them a future.

*I could send someone else,* she thought, and then did the honest thing, which was to laugh at herself, quietly, so no one heard. There was no one else. There was Ilsa, who couldn't leave the sleepers; there were the children, who were the point of the whole exercise; there was Anselm, whose face was on every security bulletin Vane had issued this week. There was Kestrel Eriksen, invisible woman, artful ghost, the one person in two worlds whose passage was a rumor rather than a record.

The rules surfaced one last time, dutiful, obsolete. *No names.* She'd broken that for Ilsa. *No attachments.* Broken for Anselm, broken again for a girl with a missing tooth. *No routes repeated.* She was about to repeat the oldest route there was: the one from a life into whatever came after.

Fine, she told the rules. You were always less about survival than about loneliness. Consider this the loneliest thing I've ever done, and notice that I'm doing it anyway.

* * *

The imprinting took eleven hours.

They did it in DORMA's antechamber, the small hot room where the AI's remaining hardware clustered like a heart in a chest of ice. The machine had grown a cradle for the work—an old medical rig, cannibalized and re-tuned—and Kestrel lay in it while filaments finer than hair found the ports behind her ears that every citizen of both worlds carried and most never thought about.

"It will feel like remembering," DORMA said. Its voice was still the counting voice, still the voice from the children's dream, but it had softened somehow since the core had come home, the way a voice softens when its owner stops being afraid. "That is the closest analogue your language offers. In truth it will be more like growing. New rings in a tree."

"Do it fast," Kestrel said. "I've done long surgeries. I don't recommend them."

"No," DORMA said. "Speed is cruelty here. This must be built slowly or it will not hold. And Kestrel Eriksen—" a pause, the first hesitation she'd ever heard from it, "—I am required to tell you what you are agreeing to lose, and I find the requirement painful."

"You're required?"

"The engineers who wrote my first directives believed informed consent was a load-bearing wall. They were correct. I have watched this species for ninety-one years, and consent is the only thing you have ever built that does not decay." The filaments hummed against her skull. "So: hear the cost. If Officer Vane takes you, his extractors will not stop at the decision. They will take the beach. They will take your mother. They will take the hymn you hide things in, and the face of the man who waited for you, and the weight of a child singing in your cargo hold. Everything that is you will become evidence. You will survive—you may even walk, talk, eat, sleep—but the person who chose this will be gone, and strangers will live in the space she made."

Kestrel lay in the cradle and looked up at the tangle of cables and coolant lines and the single amber eye of the machine that had asked a species to choose which half of itself deserved morning.

"I've spent fifteen years being nobody," she said. "Invisible. Unrecorded. I told myself it was tradecraft, but it was armor. I made myself forgettable so no one could ever take anything from me." She exhaled. "Turns out that was just dying slowly with extra steps. So take the risk that means I was actually *here*. Build it."

DORMA was quiet for 1.4 seconds—a geological age, for a machine.

"Then remember this also," it said. "What memory is for. It is not storage. Storage is cheap; any chip can do it. Memory is how a creature carries what it loves across time. You are about to carry ten thousand strangers across a border in the only vehicle that cannot be confiscated." The filaments tightened, gentle as snowfall. "Begin."

It began, and it was remembering, exactly as promised—which was the horror of it. She remembered learning the split-allocation theorem, except the memory arrived already worn smooth, already *hers*, complete with the taste of the coffee she'd supposedly drunk while studying it, the ache in her neck from the chair, the way Ilsa had cried and hadn't wiped her eyes. She remembered standing before Halcyon's grid interface, years ago, obviously falsely, and speaking the authentication phrase. She remembered the argument—not the data, the *argument*: why half and half, why waking the children first on both sides, why a shared future had to begin with shared mornings. Each memory seated itself beside her real ones and locked in, indistinguishable, load-bearing. By hour seven she could no longer say which recollections had been hers before tonight, and she understood, with a strange calm, that this was the point. You cannot confiscate what you cannot isolate. She wasn't smuggling information anymore. She was smuggling *herself*.

At hour nine, unbidden, her mother's face rose in her—real, original, bought back from a pirate collector at a price she'd sworn she'd never pay twice—and Kestrel held onto it deliberately, greedily, memorizing the memory, knowing that in a few days some extractor's probe might drink it away forever and wanting, needing, to have looked at it fully one last time with the lights on.

At hour eleven, DORMA said, "It is done. You are the decision now. Walk carefully."

* * *

She packed light. That part never changed: one change of clothes, a forged transit chit good for exactly one crossing, a hymn recording with nothing hidden in it at all—for the first time in her career, the cover was just a cover, because the cargo was somewhere no scanner would think to look. She stood in the *Terminal*'s cockpit and ran preflight with hands that knew the sequence better than she currently knew her own birthday, and found that thought funny rather than frightening, which she decided was a good sign.

Ilsa came aboard at the last minute. They stood in the airlock frame and looked at each other, and neither of them performed bravery, which was its own kind of respect.

"The children made you something," Ilsa said, and pressed a scrap of fabric into Kestrel's palm. A patch, hand-stitched, clumsy and perfect: a red door, open, with light coming through. "Maret designed it. They voted. Apparently you're crew now."

Kestrel closed her fist around it. Her throat did something complicated. "Tell them—" she started, and stopped, because there was no sentence that survived contact with what she was about to do. "Tell them I said singing is good luck."

"They know," Ilsa said. "They told me to tell *you*."

The airlock cycled. The clamps released. Terminus fell away behind her, a cathedral of ice and breath, and Kestrel Eriksen turned her ship toward Halcyon's glittering crown with ten thousand lives stitched into the folds of her own mind, carrying the only contraband that had ever mattered: the memory of what everyone deserved.

Behind her, ahead of her, everywhere in the dark between worlds, the customs net was already tightening—and on the lead patrol cutter, in a cabin smelling of starched ambition, Officer Vane was looking at a sensor ghost that moved like a woman he'd been hunting for months, and smiling.
