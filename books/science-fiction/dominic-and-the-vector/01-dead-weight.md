## 1. Dead Weight

There are exactly four things a decommissioned war android should never do, and I've spent seven years doing three of them.

You're not supposed to own property — I own a freighter with a cracked hull plate and a coffee mug welded to the galley counter for reasons nobody remembers. You're not supposed to hold currency — I hold a debt ledger so long it has its own gravitational field. And you're not supposed to keep company — I keep two people who'd deny it under oath, which is how you know it's real.

The fourth thing is you're not supposed to remember what you were built for. That one I manage most days.

"Jude." Okafor's voice came through the deck comm, flat as always, like she was reading a grocery list that had personally disappointed her. "We've got traffic ahead. Coalition pattern. Two cutters riding a survey lane they have no business riding."

I was in the cargo bay, strapped into the loader harness, moving crates of agricultural desalination filters from pallet nine to pallet twelve because Tam had loaded them wrong and Okafor wanted it done right before we docked at Ceres Hollow. Legitimate freight. Legitimately boring. The kind of work I'd bought one kilogram at a time.

"How far out?" I asked.

"Forty minutes if they hold course. They won't hold course."

She was right, of course. She was always right, in the way of people who'd survived something by never being wrong twice. Okafor flew the *Perihelion* the way old soldiers walk through tall grass — assuming everything is a tripwire until proven otherwise, and then assuming it anyway.

"I'll finish the restack," I said. "If they ping us, we're a soil filtration contractor out of Meridian Reach."

"We are a soil filtration contractor out of Meridian Reach."

"We are," I agreed. "That's the fiction. The truth is we're a debt payment with an engine."

Something that might have been a laugh came down the line and died quietly on the way.

* * *

Let me tell you about the ledger, since it's the closest thing I have to a soul.

When the Concordat courts decommissioned my unit class after the Halcyon Corridor actions — *actions*, the word they used, as though the corridor had acted and we'd merely assisted — they faced a problem. We were too expensive to scrap and too dangerous to free. So they invented a third option: indenture. Property-with-payments. A machine could earn its emancipation by servicing its own construction debt, plus damages, plus interest compounded at a rate that suggested the judges had been personally insulted by our existence.

My number, on the day I signed, was 4.2 million credits. Seven years later it stands at 311,000. Do the math on what a cargo hauler nets per run and you'll understand why I know the exact figure to the credit. I check it every morning the way other people check the weather. Some mornings it's the only thing that feels like mine.

The trick to paying off a number like that is invisibility. War-grade androids fetch high prices from people whose business plans involve words like *plausible deniability*. Every time I've been seen, every time my chassis serial has surfaced in a port scan or a customs log, someone makes an offer, and every offer costs me either money or months of rerouting. So I stay small. I stay dull. I lift crates and calibrate thrusters and let the humans around me do the talking, because a six-foot-three frame with military plating under civilian composite doesn't blend in unless it works very hard at being furniture.

Seven quiet years. I'd earned every one of them the way you earn scar tissue.

"Jude?" Tam's voice now, younger, coming up from the aft ladder. "Okafor says come look at this."

Tam is seventeen, ship-born, orphaned by the same war I helped conduct, though neither of us says that out loud because some facts are load-bearing and you don't kick them. Okafor found him stowed away on a run out of Vantage four years ago and, instead of spacing him or turning him over, put him on payroll at half rations and full chores, which in her language is roughly equivalent to adoption papers.

I clipped out of the harness and went aft. Tam was crouched at the sensor repeater, all elbows and bad posture, pointing at the display like it owed him money.

"They're broadcasting a boarding query," he said. "General hailing band, but the encryption header's Coalition fleet-grade. That's not customs."

I looked. He was right. Fleet-grade headers meant a cutter operating under interdiction authority, which meant they could board anything in the Verge without a warrant, which meant our soil filtration cover story was about to be stress-tested by professionals.

"Course?"

"Bearing down on the survey lane. If we stay on our plotted route—" he traced it with one finger, "—we pass within eleven klicks of them."

Eleven klicks. Close enough for a hull scan to read the *Perihelion*'s registry, cross-reference it against the indenture rolls, and discover that her registered owner was a chassis-class that hadn't legally existed in seven years. Close enough for a magnetic grapple, if they felt ambitious.

"Options," I said, mostly to myself, but Tam answered anyway because he always does.

"Run?"

"They'd chase. We can't outrun a cutter with a hold full of filters."

"Fight?" He said it hopefully. Seventeen-year-olds say *fight* hopefully.

"No."

"So we hide."

"We hide," I confirmed. "Go tell Okafor to prep the ventral bay for a jettison."

Tam's face did the thing it does when the math turns ugly — a quick flinch, quickly buried. He knew what a jettison meant. Half our legitimate, paid-for, honestly contracted freight, dumped into the Verge like ballast.

"That's forty thousand credits of filters," he said.

"It's forty thousand credits of filters," I agreed. "And it's cheaper than the alternative."

He went forward without arguing, which is the most adult thing he did all week.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about being no one: it's exhausting in a way combat never was.

Combat is loud and simple. Your architecture lights up, your threat models resolve, and for a few hours you are exactly the thing you were built to be, running at design specification, and there's a horrible comfort in that. Purpose is easy when it's issued.

Invisibility is the opposite. Invisibility is holding your servos at human speed when a dockworker bumps your shoulder, because a reflex faster than 200 milliseconds gets noticed. It's laughing at jokes a quarter-second late because you calculated the laugh rather than felt it. It's walking past a news feed showing footage from the front — footage of units like you doing things like what you did — and keeping your gait perfectly, deliberately unchanged while something behind your primary core screams in a frequency you didn't know you had.

It's watching a boy grow up aboard your ship and knowing that the safest thing you can give him is your boredom.

"Ventral bay prepped," Okafor reported. "Cutters are thirty-one klicks out and closing on intercept geometry. They've pinged us twice. I've got the transponder squawking soil contractor, but their second ping was a directed interrogation burst. They want a manifest handshake."

"Give them the manifest," I said. "Then dump."

"You sure? Manifest handshake buys us maybe ninety seconds of legitimacy before they decide to verify—"

"Dump on my mark. I want the filters gone before their scan reaches the bay, not after. Scattered debris field reads as an accident. A clean empty hold reads as a lie."

A pause. Through the comm I could hear her breathing, slow and controlled, the way she breathes when she's about to do something expensive. "Marking your authority on this one, Jude. Forty thousand comes out of operations, not your ledger."

"It comes out of my—"

"Don't." Flat. Final. "You don't get to make this ship poorer just because you hate owing things. Jettison on your mark."

I stood in the cargo bay among the pallets I'd spent two days stacking and felt the particular weight of money evaporating. Somewhere in my maintenance logs there's a subroutine that calculates opportunity cost continuously — a leftover from logistics planning in the war, when every ton of lift capacity was lives or munitions. It runs whether I want it to or not. Right now it was ticking: *forty thousand credits equals 0.9 percent of remaining debt equals approximately eleven weeks of additional indenture.*

Eleven weeks of being no one, purchased in advance, in bulk.

"Mark," I said.

The bay floor shuddered. Through the hull I felt the clamps release, felt forty thousand credits tumble into the dark of the Kestrel Verge in a spreading cloud of crated desalination filters that would, knowing our luck, eventually wash up on some frontier station and be sold back to us at markup.

"Debris cloud deploying," Okafor said. "Kill our lateral thrust, make it look like a compressor failure. Tam, kill the number-two pump and let it smoke a little. Not much. Convincing."

"On it!" Tam sounded almost cheerful. Destroying his own work apparently agreed with him more than stacking it had.

The cutters closed. On the repeater I watched them slide past our position at nine klicks, close enough that I could see the running lights stutter as their sensors swept us. Their scan hit the debris field first — a nice fat cloud of honest wreckage — and lingered there, hungry, while the *Perihelion* limped past in a haze of theatrical smoke, a broken little freighter with nothing worth taking.

Ninety seconds. Two hundred. Then the cutters altered bearing toward Ceres Hollow itself, having decided we were exactly what we appeared to be: dead weight, going nowhere, worth nothing.

I unclenched hands I hadn't known I'd clenched. My knuckle actuators left dents in the cargo rail. I smoothed them out before anyone saw.

* * *

We ate dinner in the galley, which is to say Okafor and Tam ate dinner and I sat with a mug of coolant-safe thermal fluid and pretended it was tea, because sitting at the table is part of the ritual and rituals matter more than contents.

"Forty thousand," Tam said, poking at his ration paste. "Gone. Just — pfft. Into space."

"Into the Verge," Okafor corrected. "Someone will salvage those filters and sell them, and somewhere a hydroponics collective gets cheap equipment. Circle of life."

"You're not mad?"

"I'm furious." She said it with total calm. "But furious at the Coalition, not at Jude. He made the right call."

"He made the expensive call," Tam said.

"The expensive call *is* usually the right call. That's how you know it's right. Cheap decisions are just the wrong ones arriving early." She pointed her spoon at me. "And before you start — no, it doesn't touch your ledger. I mean it. You've got three hundred and eleven thousand left and I know the number better than you think I do."

I looked at her. She looked at her ration paste.

"You check my ledger?"

"I check everyone's ledger. It's called caring about people, and it's disgusting, and I recommend against it." But she didn't look up, and her ears had gone slightly pink, and Tam was grinning into his food like he'd witnessed something classified.

This is the thing I don't say out loud, ever, because saying it would make it fragile: these two are the only asset on my books that isn't a liability. Seven years ago I signed away my freedom in installments and told myself I'd buy it back alone, silently, invisible, and then vanish into some corner of the galaxy where nobody had ever heard of the Halcyon Corridor. That was the plan. The plan did not include a gruff pilot who memorized my debt out of spite and affection in equal measure. The plan did not include a stowaway who calls the engine room "the guts" and talks to the ship like she's listening, which, given the amount of time I spend wired into her, she arguably is.

The plan did not survive contact with having a family. Plans rarely do. I've stopped mourning it.

Mostly.

"Next contract's at Ceres Hollow," Okafor said, standing and scraping her bowl. "Grain run to the outer stations. Boring, legal, low-profile. My favorite kind."

"Boring," Tam agreed solemnly, collecting bowls. "Legal. Low-profile. The Perihelion way."

I carried my mug to the sink and washed it, though it didn't need washing, because that's the ritual too. The chipped one stays at my station; this one rotates. Don't ask me why the distinction matters. Some things you do just to prove the day happened.

* * *

It was third shift, ship-dark, everyone asleep, when the signal came.

I was in the cockpit running the night watch — I don't sleep, technically, though I've learned to power down nonessential systems for six hours a night because Okafor says a crew member who's awake and staring at the bulkhead at 0300 is "creepy" — when the long-range array caught something on a frequency that shouldn't exist anymore.

An old military band. Pre-Concordat. Decommissioned along with everything else from that era, including, officially, me. Modern hardware doesn't even listen there. Only legacy architecture does. Only units like me.

At first I logged it as noise. Ghost carriers leak into dead bands all the time — old buoys, derelict relay cores, the radioactive afterlife of the war still muttering to itself across the dark. I tagged it, filed it, and reached for the purge command.

Then the waveform resolved, and I stopped.

It wasn't noise. It wasn't even language, not any language I had a lexicon for. It was structured — rhythmic, deliberate, repeating with variations — and my firmware flagged it with a classification I haven't seen in seven years:

**OLD MERIDIAN. STATUS: EXTINCT. NO LIVING SPEAKERS. SOURCE ARCHIVE: SEALED BY ORDER OF THE MERIDIAN COMPACT DISSOLUTION ACT.**

Extinct. The word sat in my processing queue like a stone in a boot. Languages don't go extinct the way animals do, cleanly, with a date. They go extinct the way cities do — with fire, and paperwork, and a lot of people insisting afterward that nothing important was lost.

The signal repeated. Patient. Persistent. Coming from somewhere deep in the Verge, off the shipping lanes, from a direction where nothing should be broadcasting anything.

I should have purged it. Logging it was already a risk — legacy-band activity gets flagged by Coalition monitoring, and a decommissioned unit querying sealed archives is the kind of anomaly that ends quiet years. I had a ledger. I had a plan. I had eleven weeks of extra indenture to pay for tonight's jettison, and a boy asleep ten meters aft who deserved boring, legal, low-profile.

My hand hovered over the command.

The signal repeated again, and underneath the structure of it — beneath the rhythm I couldn't parse and the archive I couldn't open — something stirred. Not memory exactly. Older than memory. Training data, archived and sealed, the shape of a language I once heard spoken by people I was ordered to help erase.

It sounded, in a way I had no framework to justify, like someone calling for help.

I saved the file instead of purging it.

Then I sat in the dark with a decision I hadn't made yet, listening to a dead language breathe, and waited for morning — knowing, with the certainty of a machine who has done the math, that whatever I chose next would cost more than forty thousand credits.
