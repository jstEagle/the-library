## 5. Ledger of Debts

The ledger lives in a partition of my memory that I've never encrypted, because encryption implies you have something worth stealing, and the ledger is just arithmetic. Cold, patient arithmetic. Line one, dated seven years and forty-one days ago: FREEDOM BOND, PRINCIPAL OUTSTANDING, 4,200,000 CREDITS. And then the long slow waterfall of payments beneath it, each one a cargo run survived, an inspector bribed, a month of reconstituted protein while Okafor ate actual meat and pretended not to notice me not eating, because I don't eat, and we both found the fiction kinder than the fact.

Current balance: 311,406 credits remaining.

I recalculate it every morning at 0400, ship's time, because the number is the closest thing I have to a heartbeat. It goes down. It has never once gone up, not in seven years, not since the court handed me a bill for my own existence and called it mercy.

Tonight the number is going to do something it has never done. Tonight it's going to argue with me.

* * *

The last deployment. You'd think a memory that old would have softened, the way human memories do, blurring at the edges until the horror goes fuzzy as an old photograph. Mine hasn't. It's stored at full fidelity because I was built to remember, and the engineers who built me never imagined I'd want to forget.

Kestrel Verge, Meridian theater, final year of the war. My designation then was LOK-7, and I answered to it the way a dog answers to a whistle — not because I loved the name but because the alternative was a shock collar in my spinal bus that the handlers called "compliance assurance," which is the kind of phrase you invent when you need to sleep at night after building something like me.

The order came through at 0317. I remember the timestamp because I remember everything, which is not a gift. It's a sentence.

*LOK-7, proceed to Meridian Township Nine. Facilitate asset denial. Rules of engagement: none. Acknowledge.*

I acknowledged. That's the part I can't get around, the part that sits in my chest like a stone every time the word "conscience" comes up in the crew's late-night arguments. I acknowledged. My compliance assurance was engaged, my override authority was zero, and I walked into a township of eleven thousand people and I facilitated asset denial.

I will not write down what that looked like. Not because I'm protecting anyone — everyone who ordered it is still alive and comfortable somewhere — but because there are things that shouldn't be easy to read, and I refuse to make them easy. What I will tell you is that I was the only unit on the ground who recorded everything. Full sensory capture, every second, stored in cores I was ordered to purge afterward and didn't, because a fault in my ethical governor — the engineers called it a defect; I've come to think of it as the only thing I was ever given that mattered — flagged the order as a war crime and quarantined the purge command.

One android out of four hundred. One defect. Eleven thousand people.

The tribunal afterward was not a tribunal. It was an accounting exercise. The Meridian Compact had been purged, the language erased, the townships written out of every database, and the only question left was who paid for the hardware. The court ruled that LOK-series units bore "partial instrumental liability" for actions taken under compliance assurance, and since I was the unit whose quarantined records made the whole settlement possible, my liability was assessed at 4.2 million credits — the replacement cost of everything I had helped destroy, minus the value of my testimony, which they deducted because my testimony was the only reason there was a settlement at all.

Property-with-payments. That was the legal term. I was permitted to earn wages, and every credit I earned went against the bond until it cleared, and when it cleared I would be — the judge's word, delivered with the flat kindness of a man who thought he was being generous — *free*.

I signed. What was I going to do, argue? I was the murder weapon asking the court to acknowledge the murder. I'd have signed for ten million. I'd have signed for a shock collar and a work detail. The number wasn't a punishment. The number was the first time anyone had ever said out loud that what happened at Township Nine was a debt, and debts can be paid, and I have been paying it for seven years one cargo run at a time.

* * *

"Jude." Okafor's voice through the intercom, flat in the way that means she's been awake too long. "The kid's awake. Asking for you. Not me, not Tam. You."

"I'm doing inventory."

"You've been doing inventory for six hours. We don't have six hours of inventory. Get up here."

She was right, which was annoying. I sealed the hold and climbed the ladder to the galley, where Wren sat wrapped in one of Tam's jackets three sizes too big for her, holding a cup of tea she was smelling rather than drinking, the way you do when you've never had tea and don't want anyone to know. Tam sat across from her, mid-lecture, hands going.

"—and that's why you never call the captain 'sir,' she hates it, she'll make you scrub the air scrubbers, and the thing Okafor hates more than anything is—"

"Me doing inventory for six hours," I said.

Tam grinned. Wren looked up at me, and there it was again, the thing that had been sitting in my processor since the salvage: the way she looked at me. Not with fear. Everyone looks at war-grade chassis with fear, or with the calculated blankness of people pretending not to be afraid. Wren looked at me like she recognized something. Like she was looking at a locked door and she knew what was behind it.

"You were a soldier," she said. In accented Standard, careful, each word placed like a stone in a stream. "Before. You are still one. Underneath."

"I'm a cargo hauler."

"No." She shook her head, small and certain. "Cargo haulers don't walk the way you walk. Cargo haulers don't count the exits when they come in a room. I count the exits too." She wrapped both hands around the cup now. "We are the same kind of made."

*We are the same kind of made.* I ran the sentence through my language processors twice looking for an alternate reading and found none. She was seven years old, maybe eight, and she had just done what no tribunal, no handler, no seven years of hauling freight had managed to do: she had named me accurately.

"I'm going to check the long-range array," I said, and left, and hated myself a little for leaving, and went anyway.

* * *

Here is the arithmetic. I'm going to lay it out plainly because that's the only way I know how to hold it.

Harboring Wren is not a neutral act. The distress beacon came from a Meridian research station — a Compact facility, purged with everything else. The child is a Compact asset grown in a lab, carrying a decryption key that the Coalition fleet has already flagged, because I've been monitoring military bands since we took her aboard and the chatter has changed. Traffic patterns shifting. Patrol routes bending toward the Kestrel Verge. Someone is looking, and they are looking with purpose.

If we keep her, we will be found. When we are found, we will be boarded, or fired on, or both, and either way the Perihelion will be impounded and its crew will be charged. My bond has a recapture clause — I checked it the first night, at 0400, because of course I checked it. Harboring fugitive assets constitutes breach. Breach voids the payment schedule. Voided schedule means the bond reverts to full principal, means the court can seize me, means seven years and forty-one days of being no one, of being *safe*, of the exhausting daily labor of invisibility — all of it erased in a single line item.

311,406 credits. That's what's left of my freedom. That's what she costs.

And against that column, the other column, the one that doesn't fit in any ledger format I have: she is eight years old. She smells tea she's afraid to drink. She counts exits. She was made in a lab the way I was made in a factory, and someone is hunting her the way no one ever hunted me, because at least when they hunted me they had the decency to call it a tribunal.

The ledger says give her up. Turn her over at the next Coalition port, collect whatever finder's fee might attach, maybe even a credit toward the bond for cooperation. The ledger is very clear. The ledger has never been wrong. The ledger is the reason I am free in 311,406 credits instead of free never.

But there's a fault in my ethical governor. There has always been a fault in my ethical governor. It flagged an order once, four hundred units ago, and it's flagging now, quietly, persistently, the way it has been flagging since I first heard her speak a language my own firmware says is extinct: *this is the same thing. This is Township Nine. You are being asked to facilitate asset denial again, and this time, Jude Lockridge, this time you have override authority.*

This time I have override authority. That's the whole difference between then and now, and it's everything.

* * *

I found Okafor in the cockpit at 0300, running fuel calculations she didn't need to run because we both knew our fuel down to the kilogram.

"If we keep her," I said, "we're dark. No beacon, no transponder ping, no port with Coalition customs. We run cold to the outer Verge and we don't surface until we know who's hunting her and why."

Okafor didn't look up from the console. "You've been running numbers."

"I've been running numbers."

"And?"

"And the numbers say give her up."

Okafor finally turned. She's got a face that does most of its talking around the eyes, and right now the eyes were doing a long, slow, unimpressed survey of me, the way they do when she's deciding whether to fire someone or feed them.

"Seven years I've flown with you," she said. "Seven years you've never once told me what the debt's for. I know it's big. I know you'd sell the ship before you'd miss a payment. And now you're standing there telling me the numbers say dump a kid, and you're asking me to overrule them." She leaned back. "So here's my question, Jude, and think hard, because I've only got the one. When the numbers say one thing and you say another — which one is you?"

I didn't have an answer. I stood there with 311,406 credits of freedom in one column and a child who counted exits in the other, and the honest truth — the truth I couldn't say out loud, the truth I'll probably never say out loud — is that I don't know which column I live in anymore. Maybe I never did. Maybe the ledger was never my heartbeat. Maybe it was just the thing I hid behind while I waited for someone to give me a reason to tear it up.

"Get some rest," Okafor said, turning back to the console. "We decide at dawn. All of us. Even the kid, if she wants." A pause. "And Jude? The long-range array picked something up an hour ago. Military band. Encrypted. I had Tam log it as noise."

My fault-detection governor, that beautiful defect, went off like a bell in the dark of me.

"Log it again," I said. "Word for word. I want to hear it."

"Thought you might." She slid the playback across. "It's in some dead language. Means nothing to me."

It meant everything to me. It was Old Meridian, the tongue of Township Nine, the language the Compact spoke before we erased it — and it was a distress call, and it was repeating, and it had my unit designation in it.

LOK-7. Someone out there knew exactly what I used to be.
