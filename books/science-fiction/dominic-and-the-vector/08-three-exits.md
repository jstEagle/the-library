## 8. Three Exits

The depot was called Halfway Honest, which told you everything you needed to know about it before you even docked. It sat in the shadow of a dead gas giant like a tick on a corpse, all corroded spars and flickering signage, run by a man named Doss who hadn't looked anyone in the eye since the last war and probably wouldn't start for the next one.

I'd docked here eleven times over seven years. Eleven times, nothing had happened. Fuel, water, contraband noodles that Tam swore by, and out. That was the rhythm of invisibility: boring, expensive, alive.

This time, before our umbilical had even finished pressurizing, Doss pinged me on a private channel with a message I'd never gotten from him before:

*You've got company asking about your ship. Thought you'd want to know. No charge.*

Doss didn't do favors. Which meant either he liked me, which I doubted, or he'd decided we were already dead and wanted a clean conscience, which I believed completely.

"Jude," I said over the ship's comm, "how attached are you to this fuel stop?"

There was a pause — Jude's pauses were always exactly half a second too long, like he was consulting some internal ledger of whether speech was worth the expenditure. "Define attached."

"Asking because there's someone at the station office showing around a picture of the *Perihelion*. And I'm guessing it's not because they want to commission us for a wedding."

Another pause. Then, flat as vacuum: "Coalition or separatist?"

"That's the fun part. Doss says they're not wearing colors. Plain clothes, paid cash, asked about a child."

Silence on the line. In the background of his feed I could hear Tam's music cutting off mid-song, which meant Tam had been listening, which meant Tam had been listening for a while now, which was a whole other conversation I didn't have the energy for.

"I'll pull the docking clamps," Jude said.

"No." I rubbed my eyes under my visor. My eyes were forty-eight years old and they'd earned every one of those years the hard way. "No, we don't run yet. Running looks guilty. Right now we're just another freighter burning fuel we legitimately bought. We sit, we plan, and then we move like we've got somewhere boring to be. Understood?"

"Understood," Jude said, and then, after his half-second: "Okafor. They asked about a child."

"Yeah," I said. "I heard it too."

* * *

Here's what nobody tells you about being captain of a ship with three souls aboard: you don't get to have private panic attacks. You schedule them between cargo manifests and hope nobody notices your hands shaking while you're pretending to recalibrate the water recycler.

I did my panicking in the galley at 0300 station time, drinking reconstituted coffee that tasted like regret and battery acid, when Tam shuffled in wearing that oversized jacket they refused to replace no matter how many holes it grew.

"You're doing the face," Tam said.

"What face."

"The one where you're doing math you don't like." Tam poured themself coffee without asking, which was a liberty, but they'd earned liberties. "How bad?"

"Sit down, Tam."

"That bad."

"Sit down anyway."

They sat. I looked at this kid — sixteen, sharp-eyed, orphaned twice over by systems that chewed through children like ballast — and I thought about Wren asleep two decks down in the spare bunk, curled up small like she was still trying to fit inside whatever cage she'd escaped from. Two kids. One ship. Zero good options.

"We're going to have a crew meeting," I said. "All four of us. Full disclosure. And then we're going to vote."

Tam blinked. "Vote? You're the captain. You don't vote."

"On cargo routes, no. On whether we spend everyone's lives — yeah, we vote." I turned my cup in a slow circle. "Because I'll be honest with you, kid. Every option I've got is bad, and if I pick alone and it goes wrong, that's not command, that's just me deciding whose funeral it is."

Tam was quiet for a moment. Then: "Wren gets a vote too?"

"She's eight."

"She's been through more than most Coalition admirals. She gets a vote."

And that — that right there — was the moment I understood I'd already lost the argument I hadn't started yet. Because Tam was right, and worse, Tam was right for the same reasons I was scared.

"Fine," I said. "Everyone votes. God help us."

* * *

We gathered in the galley at 0600, which was the only room on the *Perihelion* big enough for four people to hate their options comfortably. Jude stood — he always stood, some leftover protocol from a life he didn't talk about — with his arms at his sides like a parade-ground ghost. Wren sat wedged into the corner bench with her knees up, watching all of us with those too-old eyes. Tam sprawled next to her, close enough that their shoulders almost touched. Almost.

"All right," I said. "Full honesty, because you've all earned it and because lying to this crew has a shelf life of about nine minutes. Here's where we stand."

I laid it out on the table — literally, slapping down three data chips like a card dealer with a gambling problem.

"One. The people asking about us at the station office aren't Coalition regulars and they're not flying separatist banners, but Rook's people reached out through back channels an hour ago. Ansel Rook himself. He wants the girl. He's offering enough to clear every debt on this ship, buy new drives, new hull, retire somewhere warm. All we have to do is hand over a child to people who've made it very clear what happens to her after."

Nobody said anything. Wren's hands went still in her lap.

"Two. The Coalition. There's a patrol pattern sweeping this sector — Commander Vey's task force, if the chatter's right. We hail them, surrender the girl, claim we found her drifting and knew nothing. Maybe they believe us. Maybe they take her into 'protective custody,' which is where truth goes to die, and maybe they audit us so hard we lose the ship anyway. But she'd be alive. Probably."

"Probably," Tam repeated, flat.

"Three." I tapped the third chip. "The Maw."

Even Jude shifted at that. Just slightly. A two-millimeter adjustment that, on Jude, was the equivalent of a civilian screaming.

"The Maw is uncharted debris field left over from the Meridian bombardment," I went on. "Two centuries of wreckage, gravitational shear, dead hulks with live munitions, and no nav beacons because anything we send in doesn't come back out to report. Charts call it a navigational hazard. That's generous. It's a meat grinder with pretty dust in it. We go dark, no transponder, thread it on manual, and come out the far side somewhere no one's looking. If we come out."

"And if we don't?" Wren said. First words she'd spoken all morning. Her Standard was accented in ways that didn't map to any living language — vowels that bent like Old Meridian underneath, a ghost tongue leaking through.

"Then it's fast," I said, "and we're together, and nobody sells anybody."

She considered that with the gravity of someone three times her age. "That's the best option?"

"It's the least worst option. Welcome to space, kid."

* * *

I gave them ten minutes to argue before the vote, and they used every second.

Tam argued for the Maw immediately and loudly, on principle, because Tam's principles had never once survived contact with actual danger and Tam didn't care. "We're not selling her. We're not handing her to the people who burned her whole civilization. That's not a menu, Okafor, that's a trap with extra steps."

"Selling her clears our debts," I said, playing devil's advocate because somebody had to stress-test this before reality did it less gently. "Rook's offer sets us up for life. Real life. Not this slow bleed."

"Our life isn't bleeding," Tam shot back. "Our life is fine. Our life is you complaining about the recycler and Jude alphabetizing the tool locker and Wren learning to swear in ship-slang. Selling that for credits isn't a trade, it's a surrender."

Jude spoke then, quiet enough that we all leaned in. "The Coalition option has a variable we can't price."

"Which is?" I asked.

"Commander Vey." He said the name carefully, like handling old ordnance. "Her task force flagged us after the beacon intercept. If she's hunting the girl as an asset, surrender buys nothing. If she's hunting the truth—" He stopped. Recalibrated. "If she's something rarer than she appears, surrender might work. I estimate the probability at under twenty percent."

"And the Maw?" Wren asked him.

Jude looked at her for a long moment. Something moved behind his eyes — I'd seen it once before, back at the research station, when he first parsed her language. Recognition, maybe. Or grief wearing recognition's clothes.

"I can fly it," he said simply. "Manual. If anyone can."

"That's not what she asked," Tam said.

"No," Jude agreed. "It isn't." And he didn't answer it, which was its own kind of answer.

Wren pulled her knees tighter. "Wherever I go, trouble comes. I've watched it happen. The lab, the station, now here. If I stay with you, the Maw might kill you. If I leave, you live." Her voice didn't waver, and somehow that was worse. "I vote for whatever keeps you alive. Even if it's the first one."

The galley went very quiet.

"Absolutely not," Tam said.

"It's my vote—"

"It's a terrible vote and I'm overriding it emotionally," Tam said, and put their hand over hers on the table, and Wren — this child who'd grown up in a lab, who flinched from sudden movement, who'd taught Tam a word for *witness* in a language two hundred years dead — didn't pull away.

I cleared my throat before something happened to my face that captains aren't allowed.

"Votes. Me first, since apparently democracy means I go last in spirit anyway." I held up two fingers. "Maw. I've been running from things for seven years and it's kept me breathing, and I'm not starting a collection of sold children at my age. Jude?"

"Maw," Jude said. No hesitation, no half-second pause. First time I'd ever heard him decide anything instantly. "My reflexes are the only asset that makes option three survivable. Therefore option three is the only honest choice. Also—" and here he paused, actually paused, searching for words like a man digging for something buried, "—I did not spend seven years buying myself to become the thing that sold her."

Well. There it was.

"Tam?"

"Maw. Obviously. Was there ever a question?"

"Wren?"

She looked around the table — at Tam's hand on hers, at Jude standing like a wall that had decided where to stand, at me, tired and gray and forty-eight years of stubborn — and something in her shoulders came down half an inch. Not relaxation. More like a decision to stop bracing.

"Maw," she whispered. "But if we die, I want it noted I voted against dying."

"Noted," I said. "Unanimous. Four for the Maw." I swept the two dead chips off the table and pocketed the third. "Here's how it goes. We fuel up slow and legitimate, file a fake flight plan toward the Ceres relay like good little merchants, and slip dark the moment we're past the depot's sensor fence. Jude preps the manual overrides. Tam, you strip anything that pings. I'll tell Doss we're headed to Ceres, and Doss will sell that information to whoever asks within the hour, which buys us six hours of everyone looking in the wrong direction."

"And if someone's waiting on the far side of the Maw?" Wren asked.

I allowed myself a grim smile and drained the last of the battery-acid coffee.

"Kid, if someone's waiting on the far side of the Maw, they deserve whatever's left of us when we come through."

* * *

We cast off at 0930, transponder chirping cheerful and false, flight plan logged, course set for Ceres like the most boring ship in the Verge. Doss waved from the station office with the enthusiasm of a man waving goodbye to money he'd already spent.

For eleven minutes, everything was routine.

Then Jude's voice came over the comm from the cockpit, calm in the way that meant it wasn't calm at all.

"Okafor. Come look at this."

I strapped into the jump seat beside him. On the long-range display, past the depot's sensor fence, past the ragged glittering edge of the debris field they called the Maw — a formation. Four signatures, running cold, holding position relative to the field's only plausible exit vector.

Not chasing us. Waiting.

"They knew," I said quietly.

"Or they guessed," Jude said. His hands were already moving across the manual controls, waking up systems that hadn't been touched in years, and I watched his posture change — spine straightening, head settling, something old and terrible sliding into place behind his eyes like a weapon being racked. "Okafor. Once we commit to the transit, I can't break off. The shear windows won't allow it. Whatever's in there, we fly through all of it."

I looked at the display. At the four cold signatures. At the churning dark beyond them.

"Jude," I said, "what exactly did you used to do, before you were a freighter pilot?"

His hands didn't stop moving.

"Everything we're about to need," he said, and took us into the dark.
