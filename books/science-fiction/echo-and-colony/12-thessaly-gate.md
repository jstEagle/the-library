## 12. Thessaly Gate

The Long Acre left Kepler Station with her transponder dark and her belly full of ghosts.

Wren had hauled a lot of strange cargo in eleven years — seed stock for dead worlds, reactor cores older than the war, once an entire decommissioned chapel that the Authority wanted "relocated" for reasons nobody would put in writing. But they had never carried anything like this: nine children strapped into acceleration couches in the converted hold, each one wired to a salvaged decoder rig Imani had built out of medical monitors and prayer, and one disgraced archivist riding shotgun with his hands shaking around a navigation console he'd helped design.

"Transponder's off," Sen Okafor said. "Which means every sensor in the corridor is going to treat us as debris or hostile. There's no third category anymore."

"Debris," Wren said. "I've spent a decade pretending to be boring. I can be boring at four hundred meters per second."

They burned away from Kepler on a whisper of thrust, coasting into the shadow of the station's own mass until the Authority's dock cameras lost them against the hull plating. Then Wren opened the throttle, and the Long Acre fell toward the wreckfield where the war had started ten years ago, and where it might, if everything went right in the next six hours, quietly end.

Thessaly Gate lay beyond it. Neutral ground. The last relay in contested space that answered to no flag, no fleet, no patent office. If they could reach it, if they could get the archive up its mast, the truth would stop belonging to anyone — not to Hale, not to Cass Merah, not to Wren. It would belong to everybody, which was the same as belonging to nobody, which was the only place truth had ever been safe.

Six weeks on the countdown. Fourteen days left. And somewhere behind them, Hale's ship was already moving.

* * *

The children slept in shifts.

That was the part nobody had planned for, because nobody had planned any of this — the plan, such as it was, had been improvised in the forty minutes between Okafor sabotaging the false-dream transmitter and Wren deciding that staying at Kepler meant dying at Kepler. Nine kids, ages seven to fourteen, pulled out of school lines and hospital beds and one very confused birthday party, handed to a cargo hauler who had never wanted passengers.

But it turned out the dream didn't need them awake. It needed them *together*. Alone, each child caught fragments — a green valley here, a number there, the voice counting down in a language none of them spoke but all of them understood. Together, in the hold of the Long Acre, their sleeping minds braided the signal whole. Imani's decoder rigs drank from the braid and spat coordinates onto Wren's nav display: safe corridors through the minefields, marked in the dream-data by people who had laid those mines and then died before they could tell anyone else.

Meridian's last broadcast wasn't just testimony. It was a map home.

"Wake rotation," Odile said, appearing at the cockpit hatch. Twelve years old, hair cropped short for the helmet she'd never worn before this week, moving through zero-g like she'd been born in it. Most of the kids still drifted clumsily, grabbed wrong handholds, cried at night. Odile had gone quiet and precise instead, and Wren found that more frightening than the crying.

"You should be asleep," Wren said. "You're on the second shift."

"I'm navigating." Odile pulled herself into the jump seat and strapped in with quick, practiced motions. "You don't know what the numbers mean yet. I do."

"The decoder prints them."

"The decoder prints what we dream." She looked at Wren with the flat patience of a child explaining weather to an adult. "If we dream it wrong, you fly us into a mine. So somebody has to check."

Wren wanted to argue. Wren also remembered the last time they'd ignored Odile — a course correction outside Kepler's patrol ring that the girl had insisted on, muttering about "the water being wrong," and thirty seconds later a proximity alarm had screamed about a drifting mine casing exactly where she said. They'd taken her word ever since.

"Corridor six," Odile said. "Not five. Five looks open but the dream says the water moves backward there. Whatever that means."

"It means the mines were re-seeded after the archive was made," Okafor said from the nav console without looking up. "Authority maintenance crews. The dream-map is ten years old; the field isn't. Corridor five was safe when Meridian sent this. It won't be now."

"So how does she know?"

Odile turned toward the viewport, toward the distant glitter of the wreckfield, and said nothing, and Wren understood that the answer was *she doesn't, she feels it*, and decided some questions were better flown around than asked.

* * *

The first battle of the war had happened here, though officially there had been no first battle — officially, Meridian had fired on an Authority relief convoy without provocation, and the retaliation had been swift and righteous, and Meridian had burned. Everyone above the age of twenty had seen the footage. Everyone under it had grown up inside the story.

The wreckage told a different story. It always did, if you flew close enough.

Wren threaded the Long Acre through it at two hundred meters per second, hands light on the sticks, eyes flicking between the nav plot and the actual dark. Hull plates tumbling end over end, catching starlight. The spine of a convoy freighter snapped in half, both halves still drifting in formation, frozen mid-scream. And scattered among them — this was the part the official footage never showed — the shapes that weren't Authority ships at all. Meridian hulls. Dozens of them. A colony fleet that had supposedly never left its own orbit, torn apart in deep space, ten light-minutes from home.

"They were running," Okafor said quietly. He'd come aft to stand behind the cockpit seats, and his voice had the careful evenness of a man handling something explosive. "When the strike came. The patents were already signed over — command had them by then — so the only thing left to take was witnesses. Meridian tried to scatter. Command let them get this far and then closed the corridor."

"And blamed them for starting it."

"And blamed them for starting it." A pause. "I wrote the press release. Not the words — the *structure*. What got emphasized. What got buried. I was very good at my job."

Wren didn't answer. There was nothing to say that wouldn't either be cruel or be a lie, and the Long Acre deserved better than both. They flew on through the graveyard, past a child-sized pressure suit tumbling alone, and Wren fixed their eyes on the nav plot and thought about the seed vault, about the decade spent tending a garden for a future that kept receding, and understood suddenly and completely that the future hadn't receded at all. It had been *taken*. Taken and buried here, in this field, along with everyone who'd tried to carry it somewhere safe.

"Dream shift change," Odile said softly. Behind them, through the cockpit bulkhead, came the small sounds of children waking and other children settling into couches, Imani's voice low and steady walking them under. "New data in ninety seconds."

The nav display refreshed. A new corridor unspooled across the minefield — narrower than the last, threading between two dense clusters that the plot rendered as slow-blinking red constellations.

"That's not a corridor," Wren said. "That's a keyhole."

"It's the only path," Odile said. "And it closes in—" she frowned, listening to something no one else could hear, "—soon. The water's rising."

Okafor leaned forward. "Re-seeding drones. If the Authority is actively maintaining the field—"

"Then someone knows we're coming." Wren brought the thrusters up. "Strap in. All of you. This is going to be loud."

* * *

It took fifty-one minutes to fly a gap that was, at its narrowest, forty meters wide.

Wren did it by feel, the way they'd done a thousand tight docks at Kepler, except that a botched dock cost you a fine and a botched keyhole cost you two hundred souls' worth of truth and nine children who trusted you. The Long Acre rolled and slid and held her breath through gaps that scraped paint off her flanks. Twice the hull rang with impacts too soft to be mines — debris, gravel, the war's confetti — and twice the cabin went silent enough that Wren could hear the children breathing in the hold, a dozen small lungs keeping perfect time.

Odile called the turns. That was the thing Wren couldn't stop thinking about afterward, in the few seconds between maneuvers when thinking was allowed: a twelve-year-old calling turns through a live minefield based on a dream, and being right every single time. *Left. Hard left. Now straight — don't drift, the water pulls right here.* Once she went silent for a full minute, head tilted, lips moving, and then said, "Stop. Full stop. Now."

Wren killed thrust. The Long Acre drifted.

Thirty seconds later a mine casing tumbled across their bow, close enough to read the stenciled serial number, armed and patient and exactly where the old map said nothing would ever be.

"How," Wren said, when they could speak again.

"She says the dreams are getting clearer," Odile reported, relaying for the younger ones the way she'd apparently started doing without anyone asking her to. "Mara says the voice sounds happy when we go the right way. Like it's watching us."

"Like it's *watching* us," Wren repeated.

"Meridian built the archive to be found," Okafor said. His face in the cockpit glow looked ten years older than it had a week ago, which was fair, because it had lived ten years in a week. "Compressed into a form young minds could receive raw. If the signal responds to the receivers — if it's interactive, adaptive — then somewhere at the far end of it, something is still paying attention. Something that wants us to arrive."

The countdown. The dead-man's switch. Somewhere beyond Thessaly Gate, in hiding, in the dark, the surviving sleepers of Meridian were holding their own lives on a timer, waiting to see if anyone would come.

"Hold on to that," Wren said, and burned for the gate.

* * *

They cleared the minefield as the ship's clock ticked into the last hour of the day-shift, and the wreckfield opened ahead of them like a wound, and Thessaly Gate hung beyond it — a spindle of lights and antenna farms turning slowly against the stars, neutral and indifferent and beautiful, the most beautiful thing Wren Sandoval had ever seen.

Fourteen minutes at current burn. Wren let themself feel it for exactly three of those seconds, and then the long-range scanner chirped.

Contact. One contact, heavy, fast, squawking an Authority escort code it had no legal right to squawk this deep in contested space. Coming around the wreckfield's northern rim on an intercept vector that was polite about nothing.

Behind it, fainter, the passive array tasted engine flare — multiple signatures, small, agile, running cold until they weren't. Free Orbit cutters. Cass Merah's people. Coming from the south.

Wren's hands went still on the controls. Ahead: the relay, fourteen minutes out. Port side: the Authority, closing fast. Starboard: the rebels, closing faster. And in the hold behind them, nine children waking from the dream all at once, because — Odile said later, and Wren believed her — the voice in the valley had stopped counting down and started saying one word, over and over, in a voice like a grandmother's:

*Hurry.*

"Sen," Wren said. "How fast can this old girl burn?"

Okafor looked at the scanner, then at the fuel readout, then at Wren, and did the math every hauler learns to do and hopes never to need.

"Not fast enough," he said. "But I can make us look worth chasing."

The Long Acre turned her nose toward Thessaly Gate and lit every engine she had.
