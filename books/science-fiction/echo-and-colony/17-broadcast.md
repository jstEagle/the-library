## 17. Broadcast

**WREN**

The Long Acre came apart around us as she burned.

Not all at once — she was too old and too stubborn for that — but in pieces, each one announced by a new alarm I no longer had the hands to silence. Port thruster: gone. Coolant loop two: bleeding. The forward cargo bay had been open to vacuum since the wreckfield, and every time we changed vector the whole frame groaned like a shipwreck learning to sing.

Thessaly Gate filled the viewport ahead, a ring of neutral steel and old promises turning slowly against the dark. Ten thousand kilometers out. Then eight.

"Two minutes of burn left," Odile said from the nav seat. She'd stopped crying somewhere over the wreckage of the first battle, and now her voice was flat and precise, the voice of a child who had learned that precision was the only kind of courage available to her. "Then we're ballistic."

"Ballistic is fine." It wasn't fine. Nothing was fine. "Strap in tighter."

Behind us, Hale's cutter held station at exactly the distance of a threat. She hadn't fired since the wreckfield. She didn't need to. The Long Acre was dying on schedule, and all Hale had to do was wait for me to crawl to the relay and accept her version of the truth — sanitized, sequenced, the lie still load-bearing under everything, with Meridian's sleepers moved into Authority custody where they would live, technically, forever, as hostages with medical care.

Cass's edited archive sat on my other drive. Blame Authority command alone. Clean villain, clean war, clean justification for whatever the Free Orbits did next. Cass had looked at me through the comm before she broke off at the wreckfield — after she'd seen the children's faces pressed to the observation glass, twelve small witnesses to her own guns — and said, *You'll do it wrong, Wren. But you'll do it.* Which was either a blessing or a curse, and I hadn't decided.

And then there was the third file. The raw one. The whole thing, unedited, uncompressed by nothing but the children's sleeping minds and ten years of silence.

I'd read all of it now. Every hour of it, in the stolen quiet between burns.

That was the part nobody warned me about. Not Hale, not Cass, not even Okafor, who built the lie and confessed it in a maintenance corridor with his hands shaking. Nobody told me that the raw archive didn't just indict the Authority. It indicted everyone.

Meridian's own council had started it — not the strike, never the strike, but the patent war beneath it. Their lead terraformers had locked the genome-licensing architecture so tight that half the outer colonies were paying Meridian rents just to grow food in soil they'd terraformed themselves. When the Authority demanded the patents be opened, Meridian refused. When the Authority threatened seizure, Meridian's council voted to encrypt the master registry and scatter the keys among their sleepers' own neural archives — holding the future hostage to protect their garden. *We will not surrender the work of three generations,* the last entry said, in the voice of a woman named Amara Voss, *to people who would make the sky a commodity.*

Amara Voss. Odile's grandmother. The voice in the dream.

The Authority had answered that defiance with a lie and a missile aimed at their own relay, and the war had eaten ten years and forty thousand lives. But the first domino — the first greedy, frightened, human domino — had been Meridian's.

If I broadcast the raw archive, I wasn't ending the war with truth. I was ending it with grief, and grief doesn't take sides.

"One minute," Odile said.

I brought up the broadcast console at Thessaly Gate's edge. The relay was neutral the way a courtroom is neutral: it would carry anything, to anyone, without judgment. Three transmission profiles waited on my screen because three different people had made sure they would.

Cass's edit. Hale's sanitization. The raw archive, 1.4 terabytes of testimony, telemetry, confession, and failure.

My hands were steady. That surprised me. Ten years of hauling cargo through contested orbits, ten years of keeping my head down and my manifests clean, and it turned out the steadiest moment of my life was the one where I finally picked a side — except the side wasn't Free Orbit or Authority or even Meridian. The side was the truth, whole, ugly, unedited, offered to both fleets at once with no guarantee anyone would put down their weapons after hearing it.

"Wren." Odile's hand found mine across the console gap. Small, cold, certain. "She's counting again. In the dream. She says —" Odile closed her eyes. "She says *whatever you choose, choose all of it.*"

* * *

**ODILE**

The dream had changed.

It wasn't the valley anymore, or not only the valley. The green hills were still there, and the sky with its two pale suns, but now there were walls around the edges of everything, and beyond the walls, darkness — real darkness, the kind you could drown in. Grandma Amara stood in the middle of the garden with her hands open, and behind her, rows and rows of sleepers lay in white cradles, breathing slow, waiting for someone to say their names.

The countdown floated above her like weather. Six weeks when it started. Now it was hours.

*Odile,* she said, and her voice was the same voice from every night, warm and tired and a little bit scared, which was how I knew she was real, because dreams are never scared. *Whatever he chooses, choose all of it.*

"He's scared," I told her. In the dream I was tall. In the dream I'd been tall this whole time; I think that was her doing, a gift, so I could carry what needed carrying. "He read your part. About the patents. He thinks it'll break everything."

*It might,* she said. *Truth is a tool, not a comfort. We forgot that. We held our work so tight we strangled it, and then men with worse intentions strangled us.* Her hands stayed open. *Tell him we don't ask to be forgiven. We ask to be counted. All of us. Even the ones who were wrong.*

The cradles breathed. Hundreds of them. Thousands. Somewhere in those rows was a woman with my face grown older, who used to braid my hair over a commlink while her daughter — my mother, who I barely remembered — laughed at how bad we both were at sitting still.

"Will you wake up?" I asked. "After?"

*If someone speaks for us,* she said. *That's all the switch ever wanted. Not surrender. Not leverage. A witness.* She smiled, and it hurt, the way true things hurt. *Your friend is almost ready. Be brave one more time, little navigator.*

The dream thinned. The walls went first, then the suns, then the green.

I woke up in the nav seat of a dying ship with my cheek stuck to the restraint padding and Wren's hand hovering over the broadcast console, and I said, out loud, in the real world where voices matter:

"She says choose all of it. And she says —" My throat closed. I made myself finish. "She says they don't ask to be forgiven. They ask to be counted."

Wren looked at me for a long second. Then at the screen. Then at me again.

"Okay," Wren said. Quietly. Like a prayer or a surrender or maybe both. "Okay. All of it."

* * *

**WREN**

Hale's voice came through on the priority channel with four minutes on the countdown clock, smooth as dockside ice.

"Sandoval. You're in range. I know what you're carrying, and I'm asking you one last time — the curated package. The sleepers live under our flag, the record stays sealed, and in five years nobody remembers there was ever a question. That's not a lie. That's anesthesia. Wars end faster when the wound closes clean."

"It doesn't close," I said. "It scars. And scars remember."

"You'd rather they bleed out in the open?"

"I'd rather everybody see the wound." I pulled up the raw archive and watched the progress bar sit there, patient, indifferent. "Even the parts Meridian buried. Even the parts that make your side look reasonable. Especially those."

Hale was silent for three full seconds. For a woman like her, that was a scream.

"You understand what you're doing. The hardliners will call it enemy propaganda. Cass Merah will call it insufficient. Meridian's own council—" She stopped. Recalibrated. "You've read Amara Voss's final entry."

"Every word."

"Then you know you're not delivering salvation. You're delivering a mirror." A breath, and underneath the breath, something that sounded almost like respect. "Do it, then. I'll hold my fleet's fire for as long as my authority means anything. Which may not be long."

The channel closed. On the tactical display, her cutter shifted — not away, not toward. Just still. Holding.

I keyed the relay handshake. Thessaly Gate's ancient transmitter woke up around us, antennas unfolding like something plantlike and patient, and the targeting solution spread itself wide: Authority fleet, Free Orbit flotilla, Meridian's hidden beacon answering at last from its decade of darkness, and the open civil bands where forty thousand families had been listening to war for ten years.

One file. No edits. No mercy for anyone, including the dead.

"Odile."

"I'm here." She climbed out of the nav seat and stood beside me, small and straight-backed, twelve years old and older than the war. "Counting with her. Go."

I pressed transmit.

* * *

**ODILE**

It took eleven minutes for the whole archive to go out. We watched it leave in a thin ribbon of light on the display, and Wren let me narrate the pieces as they went, because Wren said somebody should say the names while they traveled.

"The seed vault schematics. Meridian's founding charter. Council session logs — the vote on the patent lockdown, six in favor, five against, one abstention." Wren's voice never wavered, but their hand found my shoulder and stayed there. "Dr. Imani Reyes's decoding notes. Twelve children's dream testimonies, transcribed. Yours first, Odile. You're the opening statement."

"I know," I said. "She told me."

Telemetry of the first strike — the missile's true origin codes, Authority serials, the relay's death certificate signed by its own murderers. Sen Okafor's confession, recorded in a maintenance corridor, his voice shaking. The counter-log Meridian had kept for ten years, every sleeper accounted for, every heartbeat in the dark. And last, running longest, Amara Voss's final entry, played in full, uncut, her voice filling the Long Acre's broken cabin and the open bands of two fleets and every ship and station in between:

*We will not surrender the work of three generations to people who would make the sky a commodity. If this is pride, let history name it. If this is a crime, let history try it. But let history see all of it — ours and theirs — or none of this was ever worth the dying.*

When it finished, the ribbon of light went out, and the silence came down on us like snowfall.

Wren didn't move. Neither did I. The countdown in my head ticked past the last safe hour and kept going, because the switch didn't want an apology or a treaty. It wanted acknowledgment. It wanted someone, somewhere, to say: *we hear you, we count you, you exist.*

For ninety seconds, nothing.

Then the open band lit up.

Not orders. Not declarations. Just voices — hundreds of them, thousands, overlapping, from both fleets and every station between — people reading the logs aloud to each other, arguing, weeping, saying names. Someone on an Authority cruiser was reciting the council vote tally like a rosary. Someone in the Free Orbit flotilla was playing Amara Voss's last entry on a loop. And threading through all of it, faint and steady, a signal from the dark beyond the walls of the dream:

Meridian's beacon, broadcasting its roster. Four thousand one hundred and six names. Counted. Acknowledged. Alive.

In my head, the countdown stopped.

In the garden, my grandmother opened her eyes.

* * *

**WREN**

I want to tell you it felt like victory. It didn't. It felt like setting down something so heavy you'd forgotten your own shape without it, and discovering that the weight had been holding you together as much as wearing you down.

The fleets went quiet. Both of them. Weapons cold, engines idle, forty thousand tons of war hanging in the black like a held breath. On the tactical display, Hale's cutter turned slowly — not toward Thessaly Gate, not away. Toward her own fleet. Whatever she was about to do, she was going to do it inside her own house.

And then the hardliner squadron broke formation.

Three Authority destroyers, peeling off from the main body, burning hard for Thessaly Gate with their weapons systems waking up one by one on my scope. Behind them, the rest of the Authority fleet hesitated — split, uncertain, a body whose conscience and commands had just stopped agreeing.

"They're coming for the relay," Odile said. Not scared. Just reporting, the way she reported everything now, like a navigator. "They can't let the archive stand."

The Long Acre shuddered around us, hull ticking as the last heat bled out of her bones. No thrusters. No weapons. Eleven minutes of air if the recyclers were feeling generous, and three destroyers inbound.

I looked at Odile. She looked back at me, and in her eyes was the valley, and the cradles, and the woman with my future in her hands, and no fear anywhere in any of it.

"Well," I said, and reached for the relay's emergency channel. "We got the truth out. Now we find out what it's worth."

The first destroyer opened fire.
