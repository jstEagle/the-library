## 20. Meridian Rising

The shuttle came out of the dark at 0400 station time, and every clock on Thessaly Gate was wrong anyway.

That was the thing nobody said out loud in the hours before it docked. Time had gone soft. The countdown that had ruled our lives for six weeks — the dead-man's switch ticking down inside ten thousand sleeping bodies a system away — had stopped three days ago when the fleets stood down and the acknowledgment codes went through. But you could still feel its ghost in the corridors. People kept checking chronometers they didn't need. Kept counting something. As if the silence after might be worse than the ticking.

I stood at Docking Bay Nine with Odile Voss's hand in mine and watched the approach vector burn against the black.

The Long Acre was still in a repair cradle two bays over, her spine cracked, her cargo frame peeled open like a tin can someone had given up on. I'd signed the damage report with a stylus that shook. *Cause of failure: sustained pursuit, weapons fire, one deliberate collision with an Authority interdiction net.* Under *recommended disposition* I'd written: *repair.* Not *salvage.* I wasn't ready to have that argument with myself yet.

"You're squeezing," Odile said.

"Sorry." I loosened my grip. She didn't pull away.

She'd grown up in the last six weeks. That sounds like a lie unless you've seen it happen — a twelve-year-old who carried a message that ended a war, who navigated a hauler through a wreckfield by following a voice only she could hear, who looked at Cass Merah across a breached airlock and made a grown woman put her guns down. Odile hadn't gotten taller. She'd gotten *heavier*, somehow, in the way that matters. Like ballast. Like she'd finally been given weight to match her gravity.

"They're really coming," she said. "You're sure."

"I'm sure."

"Because people keep saying things are sure, and then—"

The bay lights shifted from amber to green. The docking clamps engaged with a sound I felt in my teeth, a long metallic shudder running through the deck plates, and somewhere behind us a crowd of maybe four hundred people went quiet all at once.

Four hundred. They'd come from every ring of the Gate. Haulers in their patched flight suits, Gate staff off shift, rebel fighters who'd surrendered their sidearms at the checkpoint rather than miss this, Authority clerks who'd walked out of their offices without permission because some things outrank orders. Imani stood near the front with her arms crossed tight, like she was holding herself together with friction. Sen Okafor would not be here — he was in custody, awaiting transport to the tribunal at Ceres, and had asked specifically that no one make a fuss. We'd made a fuss anyway, in the way of quiet people: we were all here, which was the fuss.

The outer door cycled. Atmosphere equalized with a hiss like a long exhale held for ten years.

The inner door opened.

They came out slowly. That was what struck me first — how slowly. Eleven survivors of Meridian's sleep section, walking like people learning that floors could be trusted. Medical escorts flanked them, but the escorts had learned quickly that these people didn't need steadying so much as *space*. They moved like the recently resurrected, which is to say carefully, which is to say like each step was a question being asked of the universe and answered, barely, yes.

And then I saw her.

Odile saw her at the same moment. I know because the child's whole body changed — a shiver starting at her shoulders and running down both arms, and her grip on my hand going tight enough to hurt before letting go entirely.

The woman was small. Gray-haired, cropped short the way zero-g sleepers wear it. She wore a Meridian agricultural smock, faded green, the kind I'd seen in archive footage a hundred times while cataloguing seed stock for a colony everyone said was ash. Her name tag read VOSZ, A. — the old orthography, pre-war, before the Authority standardized everything into sameness.

Odile took one step. Stopped. Took another.

The crowd parted for her the way water parts for nothing, just opening, just making room, four hundred people holding their breath for a twelve-year-old crossing thirty meters of deck plate.

Anya Vosz stopped too. Ten meters between them now. The medical escort reached for her arm and she waved them off without looking.

"I know you," Odile said. Her voice carried in the hush, thin and clear. "From the dream. You counted down. You counted me all the way down to—" She stopped. Started again. "Why did you count?"

I will carry that woman's face for the rest of my life. The way it broke open. Not crying, exactly — or crying, but underneath it something else, something like a dam failing in slow motion, ten years of hiding surfacing all at once in front of a child she'd never met but had spoken to every night for a decade.

"Because we were afraid," Anya Vosz said. "We hid, and hiding meant silence, and silence meant we couldn't tell anyone we were alive. So we sent the only thing we could send. Into sleep. Into dreams. Because children receive raw data — adults can't anymore, we edit everything we hear without knowing we're doing it. We needed receivers who wouldn't flinch." She knelt. It took her a while; her knees had been asleep for ten years. "And you didn't flinch. You listened all the way to the end."

"The end was a date," Odile said. "It was scary."

"It was a promise. If nobody came by the date, we wanted you to know it wasn't your fault. That's what the countdown was. Not a bomb." Her voice cracked clean through. "A lullaby. *If no one comes, it isn't because you didn't try.*"

Odile closed the last ten meters in about two seconds and hit her grandmother hard enough to rock the kneeling woman back on her heels, and Anya Vosz wrapped around her and buried her face in the child's hair, and the sound she made was not a word in any language but every person in that bay understood it, because grief and joy had spent ten years in the same sealed room and this was the sound of the door finally opening.

I looked away. Some things you witness better sideways.

Imani had come up beside me. She was crying without any pretense of not crying, which for Imani Reyes was roughly equivalent to screaming.

"We did it," she said. "Barely. Badly. With a broken ship and a stolen decoder and a child's nightmares. But we did it."

"Barely," I agreed. "Badly."

"Okafor's transport leaves tomorrow."

"I know."

"He asked if you'd visit before it goes."

"I will." I watched Odile and her grandmother, the small gray-haired woman rocking back and forth with the girl in her arms, and felt something in my chest that had no clean name. "Imani. What happens to them now? Legally. The sleepers. Meridian's charter was revoked when the colony was declared destroyed."

"That's the reckoning part." She wiped her face with the heel of her hand, unapologetic. "Hale's testimony plus Okafor's plus the raw archive gives the tribunal everything it needs to void the patent seizures and restore the charter. But 'everything it needs' takes months. Years, maybe. There are eleven living claimants to an entire colonial charter and a hundred thousand people who fought a war over its corpse. Somebody is going to argue that survivors don't inherit ghosts."

"Somebody always argues."

"And somebody else," Imani said, nodding toward the far side of the bay, "is already arguing back."

Cass Merah stood at the edge of the crowd, unarmed, in civilian clothes that fit her like a costume she was still getting used to. Since the broadcast she'd done something none of us expected: she'd gone public. Every transmission, every comm channel, the Free Orbit network turned inside out — *here is what I almost did, here is the doctored version I built, here is why I was wrong.* She'd named names, including her own. Half the Free Orbits called it betrayal. The other half were following her into whatever came next, which, if Cass had her way, was a civilian federation with no fleet and no flags and a very long memory.

She caught me looking and gave me a nod. Not a wave. A nod. From Cass Merah, that was a treaty.

Behind her, the Authority delegation waited in their gray uniforms, and at their center, Deputy Director Hale. She'd arrested three of her own commanders two days after the broadcast, frozen the attempted seizure of the relay with a signature and a speech that was already being taught in ethics seminars neither of us would ever attend. She'd confirmed the archive's authenticity publicly, on open channels, knowing — everyone knew — that the tribunal would come for her next. She'd helped sustain the lie for a decade. She'd ended it. Both things were true and she carried them both visibly, like luggage she refused to check.

Our eyes met across the bay. She inclined her head slightly. I didn't return it. I wasn't there yet. Maybe I never would be. But I didn't look away either, and that was its own kind of treaty, unsigned.

* * *

Later — hours later, after the medical intake, after the press that Odile's grandmother faced with the flat calm of a woman who had survived worse than cameras — I found Odile sitting on the floor of Bay Nine beside the observation glass, watching the shuttle that had brought her grandmother still cooling against its cradle.

I sat down next to her. The deck was cold. Neither of us cared.

"She's smaller than in the dream," Odile said.

"Dreams exaggerate."

"No. Dreams get things exactly right and then you wake up and reality has to catch up." She rested her chin on her knees. "She told me what happened. After the strike that didn't happen. They got the sleep section sealed and powered down to minimum and they just... stayed. For ten years. Because coming out meant the war would find them, and staying meant everyone thought they were dead, and they chose being mourned over being hunted." She was quiet a moment. "My mom's going to lose her mind. In a good way. I hope."

"In a good way," I said, and hoped it too. Odile's mother was inbound on the next civilian transport, along with half the parents of the dreamer children, summoned by messages that had taken me three days and most of my remaining credit to send. Some reunions were still ahead. That was the strange arithmetic of the last month — for every debt paid, another family waiting at a dock somewhere.

"Wren." She said my name the way she'd started saying it a week ago, dropping the *Captain*, which I'd never earned and she'd never believed in anyway. "What happens to the garden?"

I blinked. "What garden?"

"Meridian's. The real one. Grandma says they kept the seed vault running even in minimum power — priority list, seeds above people, her idea, she was the agronomist. She says it's mostly dead now. Ten years of brownout." Odile turned to look at me, and her eyes were very steady. "She says someone would have to start it over. From the beginning. From dirt."

I thought about Kepler Station's seed vault, the one I'd tended for a decade. Fourteen thousand accessions. Every seed labeled, logged, loved in the specific joyless way that cataloguers love things — for a future that kept receding. A garden for the unborn. I'd told myself that was enough. That tending potential was a life.

It had never once been enough. I'd just been waiting for permission.

"There's soil under the Meridian domes," I said. "Contaminated, probably. Dead, definitely."

"Grandma says dead soil is just soil that hasn't been argued with yet."

I laughed. It came out rough, unused. "Did she say that?"

"She said something complicated about microbial recolonization and I translated."

We sat there while the bay lights dimmed toward night cycle. Out beyond the glass, Thessaly Gate turned slowly against the stars, and past it, far off, the coordinates where a hidden colony was waking up for the first time in ten years, blinking in the light, afraid and alive.

"Odile," I said. "When the tribunal's done. When your mom gets here and things settle. I'm going to Meridian. To stay. Someone has to argue with the dirt."

She considered this with the gravity she gave everything now.

"I'm coming with you," she said. "Grandma needs an agronomist who speaks dream. And you need somebody who knows how to count down."

I put my arm around her shoulders and we watched the stars wheel, and for the first time since a child woke screaming in a Kepler corridor, I wasn't counting anything at all.

Then my comm buzzed. Imani. Two words:

*"Get here."*

Something in her voice made the cold of the deck go all the way through me.
