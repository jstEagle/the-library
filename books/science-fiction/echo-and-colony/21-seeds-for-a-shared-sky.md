## 21. Seeds for a Shared Sky

The summons came on real paper.

That was the first sign it mattered. Paper was expensive out here — pulped from hydroponic cellulose, stamped with the Authority's seal in ink that cost more per gram than my old hauler's fuel budget for a month. I turned it over twice before I opened it, half expecting it to be another subpoena. There had been so many subpoenas since Thessaly Gate. Everyone wanted a piece of what I'd carried; apparently that included the machinery of justice itself.

*Wren Sandoval is requested to appear before the Joint Commission on the Meridian Atrocity, Session Forty-One, Kepler Station, Assembly Hall C, 0900 station time. Your testimony is voluntary. Your presence is honored.*

Voluntary. Honored. The Authority had learned new words overnight, the way people do after a war ends — suddenly fluent in humility they'd never practiced.

I went anyway. Not because they asked.

* * *

Assembly Hall C had been a cargo bay until three weeks ago. You could still see the scorch marks where container clamps had burned during the occupation, and the commission hadn't bothered to cover them. Maybe that was deliberate. Maybe someone up there understood that a tribunal held in a pretty room would look like theater. Held in a scarred bay, with folding tables and borrowed chairs and the smell of recycled air and old coolant — that looked like work. It looked like people doing the slow, unglamorous job of counting the dead.

And there were so many dead to count.

Session Forty-One was about the first strike. Again. It was always about the first strike now, because everything else flowed from it: the relay Meridian's own command staff destroyed under Authority pressure, the false attribution, the patents transferred by emergency decree while the bodies were still cooling in orbit. Okafor had given them the architecture of the lie in his third day of testimony, laid out like a schematic, because that's how archivists grieve — precisely, in order, with citations.

I sat in the gallery and watched him on the feed. They kept him in custody still, technically a war criminal under the old statutes, though everyone knew how that would end. He testified in a gray jumpsuit with his hands folded on the table, and he never once looked away from the commissioners when he said things like *I authenticated the forgery myself. I knew the telemetry was wrong. I signed it anyway.* Eleven years he'd carried that. He said carrying it had been its own sentence, and the cell was just paperwork.

Cass Merah sat at the petitioners' table, no longer wearing a faction sash. She wore civilian gray too, though hers was pressed, and she spoke for the Free Orbits — or for what the Free Orbits were becoming. She'd stood up in the wreckage of the ambush, after she saw the children's faces through her own targeting display, and something in her had broken open in the good way. The federation charter she was drafting ran to four hundred pages. She read clauses aloud to anyone who'd listen. *No fleet loyalty shall supersede civil authority. No archive may be sealed in wartime. No child shall be conscripted as an instrument of intelligence, however passive.*

That last one was Odile's clause. Cass had named it that, publicly, and Odile pretended to be embarrassed and told everyone at school about it constantly.

The commission asked Cass hard questions. About the attack on the Long Acre. About the edited broadcast she'd prepared, the one that would have blamed Authority leadership alone and left Meridian's own failures — the patent hoarding, the closed-door dealings, the way their terraforming monopoly had squeezed the outer colonies for years before the war — buried under fresh martyrdom. She didn't flinch. She said, *I was going to trade one lie for another, because the other lie was useful. Wren Sandoval showed me what it costs to refuse that trade. I'm still paying it. That's the point of the clause.*

Then the presiding commissioner looked up into the gallery, found me, and said my name.

* * *

They gave me a chair instead of a witness stand, which I appreciated. Stands are for people above the room. Chairs are for people in it.

The questions were gentle, which was worse than hostile. Hostile I knew how to survive. Gentleness kept catching me off guard, kept making me think about things I'd been carefully not thinking about while hauling seed crates and signing affidavits.

Commissioner Adeyemi — a thin, patient woman from the Jovian benches who'd lost two sons in the blockade years and never once mentioned it — did most of the asking.

"You had the raw archive," she said. "You had Cass Merah's version, which might have ended the war faster. You had Deputy Director Hale's version, which might have ended it cleaner. Why neither?"

I thought about the garden. Not the metaphor — the actual garden, the seed vault bed I'd been keeping alive for ten years on a station where the future kept receding like a tide going out. I'd built that life around a simple transaction: tend what you can reach, trust the institutions to handle the rest. The institutions had handled the rest by starting a war on a lie. Every institution in the room had, in some way, been willing to spend the truth like currency.

"Because every version on offer was edited," I said. "And editing was the whole crime. The war started because somebody decided people couldn't be trusted with the whole record. Hale believed peace required the lie. Cass believed justice required a different lie. Both of them were wrong in exactly the same way." I paused. "Meridian's sleepers were dying on a countdown. I could have picked a story that saved them and poisoned everything after. Or I could send the truth and gamble. I gambled."

"It nearly failed."

"It did fail, right up until it didn't. Hale's ship was minutes behind me. Okafor was in a pod being dragged into custody so I could run. If the fleets hadn't gone quiet — if one captain on either side had fired—" I stopped. Recalibrated. "It nearly failed. Yes. I want that in the record. The truth isn't a strategy that works. It's just the only move that doesn't make you the next liar."

Adeyemi wrote something down. Then she slid a folder across the table, and I recognized the weight of it immediately. Real paper again. Official stock.

"The Commission, on behalf of the interim government of the Inner Belt and the provisional federation assembly, wishes to offer—"

"Don't," I said.

She looked up.

"I know what's in the folder. Commendation, restoration of hauling license with priority routing, a seat somewhere — advisory council, truth commission, some body with 'reconciliation' in the title. I've gotten four versions of this letter already. One of them was from Cass, which was the strangest one." I pushed it back across the table, gently, the way you return something fragile. "I'm not refusing because I'm modest. I'm refusing because I've spent eleven years being loyal to things, and every single thing I was loyal to failed the same test. The Authority failed it. The Free Orbits failed it. Even Meridian — the saints, the martyrs — failed it. Their patents helped start this. Their silence let it run for a decade while children dreamed their deaths."

The hall was very quiet. On the feed, Okafor had stopped testifying to watch.

"So I'm done being loyal to flags," I said. "Not to people. People I can still do. But if I take a seat, I become furniture in someone else's structure, and structures are how we got here. What I can do is small and it's mine. I can grow things. I can carry things. I can tell the truth when it's my turn, and stay out of the way when it isn't. That's not a role your government has a title for."

Adeyemi studied me for a long moment. "You understand the trials will go on for years. Some of the guilty will walk. Plea agreements. Jurisdiction fights. The Belt will feel cheated."

"Some of the guilty will walk," I agreed. "That's what happens when you stop lying. The truth doesn't come with a guarantee attached. I learned that at Thessaly Gate with four minutes on the clock." I stood up. "Count the cost honestly, Commissioner. That's all any of us can do. That's the whole job now."

She didn't argue. She closed the folder, and — I'll remember this — she didn't look disappointed. She looked relieved, like someone who'd been ordered to make an offer she'd hoped would fail.

Outside the hall, a reporter from the Gate Feed called my name and I kept walking. Let the record hold the words. Records are better at remembering than I am.

* * *

I found Cass at the dockside canteen that evening, doing what she always did now: arguing cheerfully with three people at once over a drafting table covered in federation charts. She'd gotten better at losing arguments since the wreckfield. She waved me over with a mug.

"They offered you the council seat," she said. Not a question. "Adeyemi told me they were going to. I told her not to bother."

"Thanks for the vote of confidence."

"It wasn't that." She tapped the chart — a map of the old contested orbits, the shipping lanes where I'd spent a decade dodging patrol boats, redrawn now as open civilian corridors. "You're not a builder, Wren. I finally figured that out watching you fly. Builders need the structure more than the outcome. You're a courier. You get the thing from where it is to where it needs to be, and then you let go. If I put you on a council you'd last four months and then hijack your own committee." She grinned. "Besides. I hear you've got a berth booked."

I did. The *Long Acre* was in drydock at Thessaly, patched and re-hulled and stubbornly alive, and in six weeks she'd make her first run under the new open-lane registry: a seed shipment to Meridian. Actual seeds. The vault's first export, decades of quiet tending finally going somewhere that wanted it.

"First bed goes in the valley floor," I said. "Near the spring. Odile drew me a map of it from the dream. Said the light comes down the east ridge around midmorning and the soil smells like rain even in drought." I shook my head. "Ten years I hauled cargo to nowhere. Now I've got a delivery address that only exists because a twelve-year-old dreamed it."

Cass turned her mug slowly on the table. "Okafor's verdict comes down tomorrow."

"I know."

"He'll get time served. Everyone knows it. He'll walk out of that cell and there'll be people who want to shake his hand and people who want to spit on him and he'll have earned both." She looked at me. "He asked for one thing, you know. Before sentencing. Asked that his archive access be revoked permanently. Said an archivist who forged a record should never touch another one. Commission's granting it."

"That sounds like him."

"It's the saddest thing I've ever heard a man ask for." She drained the mug. "Go see him tonight. He won't say it, but he wants to say goodbye properly, and he'll never ask."

* * *

The holding wing smelled like antiseptic and old paint. Okafor sat on the bunk with a data slate he wasn't reading, and when I came to the barrier he smiled the way tired men smile — genuinely, but from far away.

"They told me you refused the honors," he said.

"Word travels."

"In my experience, word travels fastest about the people who decline things. Scarcity value." He set the slate down. "I wanted to give you something before they seal my clearance. Not information — you've had enough of my information. This."

He passed a small object through the transfer slot. An old-fashioned storage chip, unmarked, warm from his hand.

"What is it?"

"The original Meridian garden logs. Not the testimony, not the distress archive — the boring part. Ten thousand days of agricultural records. Soil chemistry, germination rates, failures. Everything they learned growing food on a world that didn't want them yet, written by people who assumed someone would come back and need it." His voice caught, just slightly, and he steadied it. "I kept a copy through the whole war. Couldn't bring myself to destroy it, couldn't bring myself to use it. It felt like it belonged to whoever came next. I think that's you."

I held the chip and didn't trust my voice for a second.

"Wren." He waited until I looked up. "Everyone's calling what you did at Thessaly Gate a miracle. It wasn't. It was a choice, made badly, late, by a frightened person in a broken ship. Which means it can be made again. By anyone. That's the part I need you to keep — not the medal they wanted to give you. The knowledge that it's repeatable. That ordinary people can still pick the true thing over the useful one, even with the clock running." He leaned back against the wall. "Now go home. Plant something."

* * *

I walked back through the station's long spine past the memorial wall they were building — names going up panel by panel, tens of thousands of them, volunteers with stencils working in shifts. Somewhere ahead a child was laughing, and I realized I'd stopped flinching at sudden noises sometime in the last few weeks without noticing.

My comm buzzed. Odile.

*"Wren? We're at the vault. Grandma says the shipment manifest needs your signature and Imani's being weird about the frost-tolerance numbers again. Also —"* a pause, the particular pause of a kid deciding whether to say the thing — *"also the dreams stopped. All of us. Last night. Nobody dreamed anything. Imani says that's normal, that the network's done transmitting, but..."*

"But?"

*"But I kind of miss it. Is that terrible?"*

I stood under the spine lights, holding a dead man's garden in my pocket, and laughed until my eyes stung.

"No, Odile. That's not terrible. That's just missing company." I started walking toward the vault, toward the manifest and the argument about frost tolerance and the ten thousand days of records waiting to be read. "Tell your grandmother I'm coming. And tell Imani to leave the numbers alone — we'll grow what grows."

The line crackled, and beneath it, faint as weather, I could almost hear the valley breathing.
