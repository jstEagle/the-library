## 7. Ana's Choice

Ana Kavanagh had learned early that the council chamber was a room where silence was a currency, and that the people who spoke least were usually the ones spending it most carefully.

She stood at her station along the curved wall of the chamber, tablet in hand, taking minutes she would later sanitize into something anodyne and official. Above her, the great display showed the colony's resource projections — oxygen reclamation curves, protein vats, water tables — rendered in soothing greens. Councillor Adeyemi was speaking about the next supply contract with the tone of a man describing weather.

"—and once the Halcyon Group countersigns, our allocation stabilizes for three years. Three years of certainty." He smiled around the table. "Certainty is the rarest commodity we have."

*And what does certainty cost,* Ana thought, *and who pays it?*

She knew. She had known for four months, since the night she'd pulled the sealed annex from the council archive looking for water rights data and found instead a shipping manifest stamped with her father's registry number. Ten thousand names. The passenger list of the *Meridian*, declared lost with all hands seven years ago, except they hadn't been lost. They'd been in her father's hold the whole time, dreaming in blue light, and the council had known since before she took this job.

She had told herself she was gathering evidence. Building a case. Being careful.

Four months of careful had gotten her nowhere except to this table, listening to men plan a murder as a line item.

"Junior aide Kavanagh," Adeyemi said pleasantly, "you have the minutes?"

"Yes, Councillor." Her voice came out steady. She was almost proud of it.

"Good. One item for your records only." He slid a thin folder across the table toward her, and the motion was casual, unhurried, a man passing salt. "The venting authorization for Hold Seven. It goes into effect seventy-two hours after contract signature. You'll note the schedule aligns with the supply ship's departure window — no witnesses in orbit, minimal network traffic. Tidy."

The word *tidy* sat in Ana's chest like swallowed glass.

"Of course," she said.

She thought of her father's face across the dinner table two weeks ago, older than she remembered, lit by the cheap lamp he'd never replaced because replacing things meant admitting you'd stay somewhere. *I didn't know, Ana. I swear to you, I didn't know what I was carrying.* And she had believed him, which was its own kind of terrible, because believing him meant the guilt belonged to everyone else.

Seventy-two hours after signature. The signature was scheduled for tomorrow at ten.

* * *

That evening she walked the long way home through the agricultural tiers, past the soybean terraces glowing under grow-lamps, past the community kitchen where someone was burning flatbread again — the smell of char and cumin that meant Tuesday. Children ran shrieking between the planters. Two of them stopped their game to stare at her, and one said, not unkindly:

"You're the dream lady's friend's daughter."

"I'm— what?"

"The man on the ship. The one the boy dreams about. Petra." The child pronounced it carefully, like a word from a lesson. "My sister says the counting got faster."

Then they ran off, and Ana stood alone among the soybeans with her heart going like a failing pump.

The countdown was accelerating. Mirei Okonkwo had confirmed it; the whole school had confirmed it. The minds in the hold could feel something coming — some pressure, some closing door — and they were calling out through the only channel they had left: the sleeping children of a colony that was deciding whether to erase them.

At her apartment she laid out her evidence cache on the table like a surgeon laying out instruments. The manifest. The internal memos, each one a small masterpiece of euphemism — *asset consolidation*, *resource rationalization*, *hold remediation*. The venting authorization with Adeyemi's countersignature already dry on it. Four months of careful.

Her comm chimed. Councillor Voss, her mentor, the woman who had hired her out of the academy, who had once told her over bad wine that politics was just ethics with a longer memory.

"Ana. Walk with me tomorrow morning before session?" Voss's voice was warm, tired. "There's something I want you to understand about the Halcyon decision. About why good people make hard choices."

"Of course," Ana said.

Afterward she sat in the dark a long time. Because here was the trap, and she could see all its bars now: if she leaked the cache, Voss would know it was her — who else had access, who else had motive, whose father was the hauler at the center of it. If she leaked it, the colony might panic, the Halcyon contract might collapse, and the same people now dreaming in her father's hold would be blamed for every empty plate and thin air scrubber for a generation. If she leaked it, she would lose everything she had spent six years building, and her father would lose his license, his ship, probably his freedom, and he would forgive her, which was somehow worse.

And if she didn't leak it, then in three days, ten thousand people would die quietly in a cargo hold while the colony ate breakfast, and she would spend the rest of her life being the kind of person who took tidy minutes.

She thought about her father's hands. He had taught her to fly when she was nine, on a rust-bucket trainer over the Jovian yards, and when she'd panicked in a spin he hadn't grabbed the controls. He'd just said, *You already know the answer. Stop being afraid of what it costs.* She'd hated him for it. She'd leveled out.

Ana looked at the cache glowing on her table, all those names, and understood that she had always known the answer too.

* * *

She did not sleep. At 04:12 colony time, she sat down at her terminal and did the work properly, because if she was going to burn her life down she was at least going to do it with clean formatting.

She stripped the metadata. She verified every document twice. She wrote a cover statement in plain language, no jargon, no euphemisms — she refused to use their vocabulary even once. *These are people. Here are their names. Here is the date they were scheduled to die. Here is who signed.*

At the end, she hesitated over one final addition: her own name. Anonymous leaks were safer, and safety had a certain logic. But she thought of the children in the corridors saying *the counting got faster*, and she thought of Voss's tired warmth, and she typed:

*This cache was released by Ana Kavanagh, junior aide to the Colonial Council. I am doing this because I helped keep the secret, and keeping the secret was the crime.*

She attached the Meridian passenger list first — deliberately, so it would be the first thing anyone saw. Not manifests, not tonnage. Names. Ten thousand of them, with ages. Four hundred and eleven under ten years old.

She posted it to the public mesh at 05:03, timed for the morning shift change, when the kitchens and the lift banks and the hydroponics galleries would all be full.

Then she made tea, because her hands needed something to do, and waited for the colony to wake up.

It woke up fast.

By 06:00 the mesh forums were a white noise wall. By 06:30 someone had printed the passenger list's first page and taped it to the council chamber doors — just the names, columns of them, papered over the official seal. By 07:15 there were crowds in Meridian Square, and Ana watched from her window as the crowd split itself down the middle like a cell dividing.

On the east steps: hundreds of people holding the printed names overhead, chanting the count — *ten thousand, ten thousand* — and old Marta Osei from the water reclamation crew standing on a crate shouting that the colony's soul was not a line item. On the west steps: an equal crowd, quieter, angrier in a colder way, holding signs about air and calories and *we can't feed ghosts*. A woman Ana recognized from the protein vats was crying and yelling at the same time: "We buried people last winter! We buried them! Where was the manifest of mercy then?"

Both crowds were right. That was the horror of it. Ana pressed her forehead to the cool window and understood that truth doesn't resolve arguments; it just gives both sides better weapons.

Her comm would not stop ringing. She let it ring.

* * *

They came for her at 09:40, forty minutes before the signing ceremony that would now never happen quietly.

Two marshals, apologetic and firm, and behind them Councillor Voss, who dismissed the marshals with a flick of two fingers and stood in Ana's doorway looking older than she had yesterday.

"You didn't even anonymize it," Voss said. Not angry. Worse — disappointed in the specific way mentors are disappointed, which is to say personally.

"No," Ana said. "Anonymity was how it stayed hidden for seven years. Mine and theirs."

Voss stepped inside. She looked around the small apartment, at the tea gone cold, at the terminal still open. "Do you know what happens now? Halcyon will suspend the contract pending review. The enforcers already in orbit will cite irregularities and move on the hold without any legal cover at all — nothing left to negotiate, nothing left to delay. You may have killed them faster, child."

"Or the crowd makes it impossible," Ana said. "You saw the square. You can't purge ten thousand people in front of ten thousand witnesses. That was the point. That was always the point."

"And your career?"

"Gone."

"Your father's freedom?"

"At risk," Ana admitted. "He'd tell you it's worth it. He taught me to fly, you know. He never once grabbed the controls."

Something moved behind Voss's eyes — grief, or respect, or the exhaustion of a woman who had done arithmetic for so long she'd forgotten numbers used to be people. "The council will vote to censure you within the hour. Some of them will want you charged."

"I know."

"Some of them," Voss said quietly, "will secretly be relieved. Adeyemi has wanted a scapegoat since the annex leaked internally last month. You've handed him one wearing your own name." She paused at the door. "For what it's worth — and it isn't worth much anymore — I read the list. All of it. There was a girl born the year my son died. Same month." She didn't say anything else. The door closed softly, which was the cruelest part.

* * *

The censure vote passed twelve to three. The three who voted no did it publicly, which cost them, and Ana loved them a little for it.

She gave her statement from the chamber floor with the printed names still taped to the doors behind her, visible over the feed to every screen in the colony. She kept it short. "I am not sorry," she said. "I am only sorry it took me four months. The people in Hold Seven have been counting down in our children's dreams because no adult would listen. So listen now. They know we're coming for them. They have always known. The only question left is what we are when we arrive."

Half the chamber applauded. Half sat with folded arms. In the gallery, Mirei Okonkwo caught her eye and nodded once, slow, like a promise.

They escorted her out through the side corridor, and that was where her comm buzzed with a message that stopped her mid-step.

Not from her father. From the docking authority — an automated alert, priority flag, broadcast to council staff automatically, which meant whoever sent it wanted her to see it:

*Unscheduled vessel requesting emergency berth, Docking Ring Two. Registry: Halcyon Group, enforcement division. Manifest classification: PURGE TEAM. ETA: 14 minutes.*

They weren't waiting for negotiations anymore. They weren't waiting for the contract, or the cover, or the tidy window.

Ana started running.
