## 8. Venting Sequence

The alarm woke Petra before the klaxon finished its first wail — which was fitting, because he'd wired that alarm himself, years ago, to wake him gently rather than startle him into a heart attack. It failed at the gentle part tonight.

He was out of his bunk and moving before his brain caught up with his feet, one hand slapping the comm panel. "Talk to me."

"Three ships," said the dock AI, in the placid voice he'd once paid extra for and now wanted to strangle. "Registry: corporate enforcement class. They are requesting immediate docking clearance."

"They're not requesting anything," Petra muttered, pulling up the external feed. Three wedge-shaped hulls hung off the colony's spin axis like knives held very still. Running lights strobing amber — the corporate color for *compliance or consequences*. He'd seen it twice in his career. Both times, someone had lost something they couldn't get back.

His ship sat in Docking Bay Four, umbilical attached, hold full of ten thousand sleeping people the corporation had decided were a rounding error.

He keyed the internal channel. "Mirei. Are you awake?"

A pause, then her voice, rough with sleep and instantly sharp: "I'm awake. The children are already dreaming. Petra — the countdown changed. It's not days anymore. It's hours."

"I know." He was already climbing into the pilot's chair, hands running the pre-flight sequence from memory because his eyes were busy watching three enforcer ships begin their descent. "They came early. Contract hasn't even signed."

"Can you stop them?"

"No." He said it plainly, because lying to Mirei Okonkwo was like lying to gravity — technically possible, practically stupid. "But I can make them work for it. Get to the bay. Bring the kids' dream-records if you can. I'm going to need every voice in that hold."

* * *

Undocking from a station while three gunships watch is an art form. You don't run — running looks like guilt, and guilt gets you shot. You *drift*. You look like a tired freight hauler with a bad attitude coupling who just wants to go about his legally protected business.

Petra eased the *Long Answer* off the cradle with the tenderness of a man lifting a sleeping child, which, he reflected grimly, was exactly what he was doing.

"Cargo vessel Kavanagh," said a voice on the open band, smooth as chilled oil. "This is Enforcement Wing Meridian Compliance. Your hold has been flagged for irregularities. Return to dock and stand down for inspection."

*Meridian Compliance.* They'd named the wing after the ship they'd let die. Petra filed that away in the part of his heart where he kept things to be angry about later.

"Inspection of what?" he asked, keeping his voice bored. "Frozen freight? I've got manifest clearance through—"

"Your manifest has been amended."

"By whom? I didn't amend it."

"That's the irregularity."

He cut the channel before they could hear him laugh, because it was either laugh or scream, and screaming used up air. Behind him, the hold hummed with its stolen warmth — power draws consistent with active cryopods, he'd logged all those months ago, telling himself it was nothing. Ten thousand someones, counting down in blue light.

The first tractor lock grabbed his stern at 0400 station time.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about autopilot: it is a coward. It is built by committees to satisfy insurance actuaries, and when presented with three gunships and a choice between evading and complying, it will always comply, because compliance is cheaper than lawsuits.

Petra killed it.

The *Long Answer* lurched as manual control came online, and somewhere below decks Mirei made a sound over the comm that suggested she'd been thrown against a bulkhead. "Sorry!" he called, though she couldn't hear him through the deck plates. "Doing it the old way!"

The old way meant flying like his grandmother had taught him, back on Ceres, in a scow older than the colony itself: you don't fight the tractor beam, you *argue* with it. A tractor lock assumes your center of mass stays put. So you move your center of mass — venting cold-gas thrusters in sequences no computer would approve, because no computer would approve spending propellant this fast.

The *Long Answer* corkscrewed. The tractor lock shrieked, recalculated, held.

"Petra." Mirei's voice came through now, breathless. She was in the hold. He could hear it — the particular echo of that long blue corridor behind her words. "The children are — oh. Oh, Petra, they're all dreaming the same thing at once. It's loud."

"Loud how?"

"Like a choir. And they're — they're saying numbers."

His blood went cold and then hot. "Course corrections?"

Silence. Then, very quietly: "How did you know?"

Because he'd spent thirty years navigating contested orbits, and there was a language pilots used when they talked each other through bad approaches, and apparently ten thousand sleeping minds had learned it from the ship that carried them. Or maybe they'd invented it fresh, the way children invent everything — but it sounded like approach vectors. It sounded like someone reading him a path home.

"Mirei, I need you to do something terrifying. Read me the numbers. Exactly as they say them."

"You want me to fly you by the dreams of two hundred children."

"I want you to fly me by the dreams of ten thousand people who know this hold better than anyone alive. Yes."

Another silence. Then Mirei laughed — a small, cracked, wonderful sound. "My mother always said I'd end up in a strange profession."

* * *

The second tractor lock caught his port wing at 0411. The third gunship moved to block his escape vector entirely, sliding across his path with the patient menace of something that had never once been in a hurry.

"Vessel Kavanagh. Final warning. Cut engines or we will cut them for you."

"Give me a minute," Petra said, "I'm consulting my navigation team."

His navigation team was asleep. All of them. Had been for seven years.

Mirei's voice threaded through his headset, steady despite everything: "Four-seven-niner... they're repeating it, Petra, four-seven-niner, bearing change..."

"Bearing change four-seven-niner," he echoed, and rolled the ship hard to port, thrusters flaring white against the dark. The tractor lock on his port wing stretched, strained — and snapped, because locks are built for cargo that cooperates.

Alarm bells sang through the gunships. He could picture the enforcer pilots staring at their boards, trying to feed his flight pattern into predictive models and getting garbage, because there was no pattern. There was only a fifty-eight-year-old man with bad knees doing what a chorus of dreaming children told him, one number at a time.

It should have been horrifying. Instead — and he would never fully be able to explain this, not to Ana, not to any inquiry board, not to himself at three in the morning — it felt like being *held*. Like hands on the yoke that weren't only his. Somewhere below him, in the blue-lit dark, ten thousand people who had been cargo for seven years were finally going somewhere, and they were taking him with them.

"There's a gap," Mirei breathed. "Between the second and third ship — they're saying there's a gap, they're saying *now*, Petra—"

"Now," he agreed, and burned everything he had.

The *Long Answer* threaded the needle. Not gracefully — grace was for ships with intact paint — but completely, hull singing, a freighter performing a maneuver that would have gotten a military pilot court-martialed and got Petra nothing but a whoop from Mirei and, distantly, faintly, through whatever impossible channel connected the hold to the colony network, the sound of children laughing in their sleep.

Then the third gunship fired.

Not to kill — corporations hate the paperwork of dead contractors — but a grapple harpoon, slamming into his aft cargo section with a clang he felt in his teeth. Tether line snapping taut. His speed bled away like a scream into a pillow.

"Aft breach risk," the dock AI reported helpfully. "Would you like to hear soothing music?"

"No," Petra snarled, wrestling the stick. "I would like a bigger engine and fewer helpful AIs."

"Petra—" Mirei again, and now her voice had changed. Tighter. Frightened. "The tether — it's attached near the pods' external release housing. If they reel us in, they'll have direct access to the hold. Manual purge ports. Petra, they can vent us from outside."

"How long?"

"At their reeling speed? Eleven minutes."

Eleven minutes. Two hundred children dreaming in the colony school, ten thousand more frozen in his hold, and eleven minutes.

He looked at the tether dragging him backward toward the gunship's belly, and he thought about his daughter, somewhere in the council tower with a stolen evidence cache and her whole career burning around her. He thought about the boy in the dreams — the one who'd said his name, *Petra, Petra, the pilot with the crooked hands* — and how he'd promised, silently, in the blue corridor of his own guilt, that he would come back for them.

"Eleven minutes is plenty," he said, "if I never intended to keep the ship."

"What?"

"Mirei. Get everyone clear of the aft sections. And tell the children—" His throat caught. He swallowed it down. "Tell the children to count with me."

* * *

He blew the aft clamps at 0433.

Not the tether — the *clamps*, the structural bolts holding his own cargo section to the drive spine, the ones every hauler is told never, ever to touch in flight. The *Long Answer* shuddered from stem to stern as the separation charges fired, and then the entire aft third of his ship — cargo hold, grapple harpoon, tether, and eleven years of his life — was drifting free behind him, still chained to the enforcer gunship like a ball and chain.

Let them have it. Let them purge an empty shell. Let them file their reports about cargo successfully contained while ten thousand sleeping passengers rode in his forward compartments, transferred through ducts he'd spent three sleepless nights rerouting, back when the anomaly was still a thing he told himself was nothing.

The gunship's triumphant tractoring of a dead weight gave him four minutes of clean burn. He spent them badly and gloriously, pushing a wounded freighter toward the colony's shadow, where the big guns couldn't follow without shooting through their own customers.

"Petra." Mirei's voice, awed and shaking. "The countdown stopped. The children say — they say the corridor doors are opening. They say someone's coming to walk them out."

"Not yet." He wiped sweat from his eyes with a sleeve that had seen better decades. "Power's still short. We can wake a fraction — I need the release codes, the real ones, and I need the crowd, Mirei. I need the whole colony looking at that hold when those doors open, because the moment the corporation thinks no one's watching—"

"Ana," Mirei said simply. "She'll bring the crowd. She's been building for this."

Below him, in the humming dark, the voices of the dreaming rose — not counting down anymore. Counting *up*. Names, maybe. Or just hope, in numbers.

And on his scope, the two remaining gunships turned toward the colony, running lights strobing amber, coming to finish what they'd started.

Petra set his course straight at them.

"Well," he said to nobody, to everybody, to ten thousand sleepers and one stubborn schoolteacher and a daughter he hadn't earned back yet. "Neither do I."
