## 9. Which Half Wakes

The hold smelled of frost and old plastic, and Petra Kavanagh stood in the middle of it with his hand flat against a pod that was warmer than it had any right to be.

Behind him, the outer hatch groaned. The enforcers had cut through the first seal. He had maybe six minutes — Mirei's voice in his earpiece said five, but Mirei always rounded down to keep him honest, and he loved her a little for it.

"Talk to me," he said.

"Docking ring's packed. Half the colony showed up with thermoses, like it's a festival." A pause, and then, gentler: "Ana's at the front. She's got the passenger list on the big screen. They're reading names out loud."

Names. Of course they were reading names. You could argue about kilowatt-hours until the stars burned out, but nobody could argue with a name read aloud by a child who'd been dreaming it for a month.

He turned back to the pods. Ten thousand of them, racked in columns like a library no one was allowed to open. The power draw told the story plainly enough: he could wake perhaps four hundred before the ship's grid browned out and took the colony's water recyclers with it. Four hundred out of ten thousand. The council's arithmetic had been crueler than that, but not by much.

The dream-children had been counting down for weeks. Now, standing here, Petra understood what they'd been counting toward. Not rescue. *Priority.* The backups had been broadcasting into the colony's network like a drowning swimmer waving one arm at a time, and the smallest arms waved hardest. Children's minds were louder. Children's minds were *hungry*.

A pod near the aisle flickered its status light amber. On the little display, a name: **OKAFOR, TOBIAS, AGE 7**.

Petra went very still.

Tobias. The boy who had been dreaming of him by name. Who had walked up to Mirei in the schoolyard, tugged her sleeve, and said, *The pilot with the crooked hands is going to open the blue door.* Petra looked down at his own hands — scarred across both palms from a docking accident years ago, crooked as weathered branches — and felt something in his chest come loose and refuse to go back.

"You're picking," Mirei said in his ear. It wasn't a question.

"I'm picking."

"The council will call it favoritism."

"The council called it *inventory* last month." He exhaled. "Children first. That's not favoritism, that's triage. Kids wake faster, adapt faster, and every parent on this dock knows exactly how they'd choose."

"And you? How do you choose?"

He ran his thumb along the pod's manual release — a physical latch, mercifully analog, because corporations trusted locks more than people. "I choose the half that can still be surprised by breakfast," he said. "The adults in there made their peace with the dark somewhere around year three of the transit. The kids never did. They've been screaming into our dreams for a month. I'm going to answer."

* * *

The first pod opened with a sound like a long-held breath finally let go.

Frost boiled off the boy inside — small, dark-skinned, curled on his side as if still mid-dream. Petra lifted him out, wrapped him in the thermal blanket he'd stowed under the console an hour ago, and held him the way you hold something you're afraid might be a hallucination.

Tobias Okafor opened his eyes. They were unfocused, gray-rimmed from seven years of cold, and they found Petra's face with terrible precision.

"You're real," the boy said. His voice was rusted. "You're the crooked-hands man. In the dream you were always too far away."

"I'm close now," Petra managed.

"Did we win?"

Petra laughed — a cracked, wet sound that embarrassed him immediately. "Ask me again in ten minutes, kid."

Behind him, the inner hatch blew.

* * *

They came in fast, four of them in corporate gray with stun lances, and the lead one — a broad woman with the patient expression of someone who'd done this many times and been paid well for it — leveled her weapon at Petra without breaking stride.

"Kavanagh. Step away from the cargo."

"He has a name," Petra said. "Tobias Okafor. Age seven. Passenger manifest, Meridian, berth 4-112." He shifted so the boy was behind him, which was absurd — seventy kilograms of smuggler-pilot versus a stun lance — and did it anyway. "You want to vent him, you'll have to say his name while you do it. Out loud. I'll make sure everyone on the dock hears."

The enforcer's jaw tightened. Through the blown hatch, faintly, came the sound of the crowd — hundreds of voices, chanting names. Not slogans. Names. It rolled through the hold like tide.

"Contract says irregular cargo gets purged," she said, but her lance dipped two centimeters, and Petra, who had spent thirty years reading instrument drift, read her.

"Your contract says *cargo*," he agreed. "Look at him. Does that look like cargo to you? Be honest. I won't tell your supervisor."

For a moment — one long, humming moment — the only sounds were the crowd outside and the soft tick of cooling pods.

Then her comm crackled, and a voice Petra recognized as the purge team's commander said something clipped and legal-sounding about *optics* and *live feeds*, and the woman looked past Petra at Tobias, who had poked his head around the pilot's leg and was regarding her with the frank, unimpressed stare of a seven-year-old meeting a monster and finding it smaller than advertised.

"We're standing down," she said into her comm. Then, to Petra, quieter: "Four hundred. That's all your grid can take?"

"For now."

"Make them count." She holstered the lance and turned to her team. "We're done here. Log it as a containment failure. Nobody's fault. Nobody ever's fault."

As they withdrew, one of the younger enforcers paused at the hatch. He didn't look back. But he left his emergency thermal pack on the deck, just inside the threshold, and walked away quickly, like a man leaving flowers at a grave he wasn't supposed to visit.

* * *

By dawn, the hold looked nothing like a hold.

Mirei had organized the waking children onto blankets between the pod racks, fed from the ship's emergency stores — rehydrated rice and fruit paste, which Tobias declared "the best thing I've ever eaten" with the authority of someone whose previous best meal was seven years and one lifetime ago. Ana moved among them with a clipboard, because Ana could not be parted from a clipboard even during a revolution, cataloguing names and next-of-kin and which adults remained sleeping in which racks, building the ledger of the unfinished.

Petra sat on the deck with his back against Tobias's empty pod and watched his daughter work.

"You could have stayed clean," he said. "Council career. Corner office with a view of the algae farms."

Ana didn't look up. "You could have kept decrypting nothing."

"That's fair."

She sat down beside him, shoulder to shoulder, the way they hadn't sat since her mother's funeral. For a while neither spoke. Below them, a girl woke, cried for ten minutes, ate fruit paste, and fell asleep again — and the whole hold seemed to exhale with her.

"They're going to convene an inquiry," Ana said finally. "Your license is gone. Probably worse. I leaked classified material; I'm finished as an aide."

"Mm."

"You're not going to argue?"

"I'm going to teach navigation," Petra said. "To these kids, eventually. To anyone who wants to learn. You don't need a license to draw circles on a slate and explain why the circle is a lie." He nudged her. "And you don't need the council's blessing to rebuild it from the inside. I've read your evidence cache, Ana. You organize chaos better than the council organizes order."

Ana looked at him sideways. "Was that a compliment?"

"It was a navigation fix. Take it or leave it."

She took it. And when Tobias wandered over and climbed into the gap between them without asking, the way children claim territory, Petra put his crooked arm around the boy and thought: *four hundred*. Four hundred awake, nine thousand six hundred still dreaming, and a colony outside arguing itself hoarse about water and watts and worth.

But the argument had changed key. That was the thing. Last week it had been *can we afford them*. This morning it was *which ones do we wake next*, and a question shaped like that has already lost its teeth.

Tobias tugged Petra's sleeve.

"In the dream," the boy said, "there was a door at the end of the corridor. Blue. Behind it there were more doors, lots and lots, and a voice saying numbers. But last night—" he frowned, working at it, "—last night the voice stopped counting. It started saying something else."

"What did it say?"

Tobias leaned close, as if the pods themselves might overhear.

"It said, *the Meridian isn't the only ship*."
