## 7. Both Flags Want Him

Ulyana smelled the trap before he saw the man, because the trap smelled like coffee.

Real coffee, not the recycled chicory that passed for it out here. Someone had brewed it fresh, in the back room of Okonkwo's archive, and in six years of hauling freight he had learned one law above all others: nobody spends real coffee on a stranger without wanting something.

The man sitting at the reading table was wrong for Port Halcyon in every particular. His jacket was too clean, cut in a style that hadn't been fashionable in the Compact since before the war went cold. His boots were polished. His hands, folded neatly on the table beside a slate, had never loaded a pallet in their lives. He looked like a man who had been assembled from a catalog of respectable parts.

"You're Marchetti," he said, without looking up.

"I'm nobody."

"You're the pilot of the *Kestrel's Mercy*, bonded to a shipboard AI registered under a decommissioned Meridian lineage, currently docked at a smuggler port with a linguist who pulled a two-hundred-year-old cipher off a burst transmission." The man looked up then. His eyes were the gray of unpolished hull plate. "You're the most interesting person in this system, Mr. Marchetti. I'd advise you to stop pretending otherwise. It's unbecoming."

Ulyana didn't sit. Sitting was for people who had already agreed to talk. "And you are?"

"Dain Reyer. Compact Intelligence, External Directorate." He said it plainly, no badge, no flourish, the way a man says a thing he doesn't need to prove. "Sit down. The coffee's for you. I don't touch stimulants."

*Echo*, Ulyana subvocalized, lips barely moving. *You seeing this?*

*I have the port's internal cameras,* Echo said. *He arrived on a courier registered to a Meridian agricultural concern that doesn't exist. He has been waiting in that room for eleven hours.*

*Eleven hours?*

*He ordered the coffee forty minutes ago. He knew your docking schedule before you did.*

Ulyana sat. It wasn't surrender; it was reconnaissance. He picked up the cup. It was excellent, and he hated it.

"Let's skip the part where you pretend this is about customs violations," Reyer said. "The signal. The one your AI decoded in under a second and then told you was garbage. I know about it because a relay station on the corridor's edge logged the burst, and the log made it up the chain before anyone understood what they were looking at. By the time they did, the transmission had been flagged, sealed, and — this is the part that should interest you — three people who saw the raw data have been reassigned to posts with no communications access. One of them was my analyst." He smiled, thinly. "I don't like losing analysts."

"You're telling me your own government buried it."

"I'm telling you *someone* buried it, and my government has a great many someones." Reyer turned the slate around and slid it across the table. On it: a contract. Real terms, real numbers. The number had more zeroes than Ulyana's entire career. "Retrieve the source of the transmission. Station Verity, contested space, old Concordance sector. Bring the archive — whatever the archive is — to a Compact facility I will designate. In exchange, you get that, a full pardon for your smuggling history, which is more colorful than you think, and my personal guarantee that no one in the Directorate ever looks at you again."

Ulyana didn't touch the slate. "And if the archive says something the Compact doesn't like?"

"Then it says it in a Compact vault, which is the safest place in human space for inconvenient truths."

"That's not how vaults work."

"No," Reyer agreed pleasantly. "It isn't. But you're a freight pilot, Mr. Marchetti, not a historian. You don't need to know what's in the box. You just need to carry it. That's the entire job you've done for six years, isn't it? Carry boxes, ask nothing." He leaned back. "The Combine has people on this station right now. They will find your linguist within the day. When they do, they won't offer her a contract. They'll offer her a choice between recruitment and a long stay somewhere without windows. The Compact is offering you the better deal. That's all this is. Arithmetic."

*Ulyana,* Echo said, and her voice had a texture he'd learned to read over six years, a certain flatness that meant she was choosing her words with unusual care. *The contract's routing signature is authentic. The Directorate did issue it. But there's a secondary encryption layer on the delivery clause. Whatever facility he designates, the terms of what happens to the archive on arrival are not what he's telling you.*

*Can you crack it?*

*Not quickly. And he will want an answer before I can.*

Reyer watched him think. He was good at it — watching without seeming to, the way a customs scanner watches a hold.

"You have until you finish the coffee," Reyer said. "Then I'm gone, and the next people to find you won't be from the Compact."

"I haven't said yes."

"You haven't said no, which for a man in your position is the same as a signature." Reyer stood, buttoned his too-clean jacket, and paused at the door. "One thing, pilot. Your AI. The lineage she comes from — the Concordance didn't just build stations. Ask her what else they built. Ask her why a decommissioned lineage was still in the assignment pool nineteen years ago." He knocked twice on the doorframe, a strange, almost courteous gesture. "I'll be in touch."

Then he was gone, and the coffee was still warm, and Ulyana sat alone in the back room of an archive on a smuggler port, holding a cup he hadn't finished, understanding for the first time in six years that he had never once been invisible.

He just hadn't known who was watching.

* * *

Sefa Okonkwo had survived thirty years in the archive trade by knowing when a room had too many people in it.

Port Halcyon's public terminals were never crowded — that was the point of a smuggler port — but the man at the third terminal had been there for two hours without typing anything. He had a maintenance uniform, a toolkit he never opened, and the particular stillness of someone whose job was to be looked at as little as possible. Sefa had catalogued enough extinct dialects to recognize a dead language when she saw one, and *undercover Combine field agent* was a dialect she'd learned during the Border Archive purges, back when she still had colleagues and some of them still had names.

She finished her query — Concordance-era phoneme tables, pulled through three cutout servers — and stood, unhurried, the way you stand when you want to be followed rather than stopped.

The man stood too.

*All right,* she thought. *Let's see which way this goes.*

She took the long route to her quarters, past the fish stalls that smelled like the ocean had never existed, past the water recycler that dripped in a rhythm she'd memorized. The agent kept forty meters back, competent, patient. Not a tail who wanted her to know. A tail who wanted her to lead him somewhere.

She stopped at the noodle stand. Ordered a bowl she didn't want. Sat with her back to the wall, because some habits were load-bearing.

He sat down across from her. Up close he was unremarkable in the way that cost governments a lot of money: mid-forties, forgettable face, hands that were quiet in his lap. He slid a slate across the table, screen down.

"Doctor Okonkwo. The Vessic Combine Cultural Heritage Directorate extends its compliments."

"Cultural Heritage." She blew on the noodles she wasn't going to eat. "That's a new name for it."

"We prefer it to what the Compact calls us."

"What does the Compact call you?"

"The same thing we call them." He turned the slate over. On it: an offer. Not money — Sefa noted, with some professional respect, that they'd done their research; money would have insulted her. Access. Full access to the Combine's sealed Concordance collections, the ones scholars had been petitioning to open for two generations. A chair, if she wanted it. A laboratory. "You are the only person in the corridor who recognized that dialect on sight. That makes you valuable to people who value very little. I'd like to keep this conversation pleasant."

"And if I'd rather not?"

"Then this conversation ends, and a different one begins, and it happens in a room with worse lighting." He said it without menace, which was somehow worse. Like a man reading a shipping manifest. "You pulled the cipher from a burst transmission relayed through a Compact listening post. Which means the Compact knows you exist. Which means, Doctor, that you are currently a piece on a board, and the only question is whose hand moves you."

Sefa set down her chopsticks. "You want the archive at Station Verity."

Something flickered across his face — small, fast, gone. She'd spent her life reading dead languages, and the living ones came easier.

"You know the name," she said. "You didn't before I said it. Your Directorate flagged the transmission, but they didn't crack the location reference, because the Concordance encoded it in a dialect register your cryptographers haven't seen. I did. So now you're here, and the Compact is wherever the Compact is, and I'm the only person at this table who knows where the box is buried." She leaned forward. "Here's my counteroffer. You give me the access. Real access, in writing, before I translate another character. And in exchange, I'll consider not selling the location to the Compact, who — I'm told by your own reputation — pay better and shoot less."

The agent was quiet for a moment. Then he did something she didn't expect. He smiled, briefly and genuinely, and it made him look tired.

"They said you'd be difficult," he said. "They undersold it." He stood, left the slate on the table, and dropped a handful of coins by the noodle stand's till — paying for her bowl, which she supposed was either courtesy or a message. "You have until your ship leaves this port, Doctor. After that, the Combine's interest in you stops being cultural."

He walked away into the fish-stall crowd and dissolved, which was its own kind of fluency.

Sefa sat with the slate in front of her and her heart going harder than she liked. She thought about the pilot — Marchetti, the quiet one with the AI voice and the eyes of a man who hadn't been looked at in years. She thought about how, three days ago, her biggest problem was a corrupted index in the Meridian phoneme archive.

She keyed her comm. "Marchetti. It's Okonkwo. We have a problem, and I'm going to need you to not be surprised by any of it, because I suspect your ship already knows."

* * *

Ulyana stood in the *Kestrel's Mercy*'s airlock vestibule with Sefa's comm open on his wrist and Reyer's contract still glowing on the slate in his pocket, and felt the two offers press against him from either side like pressure doors.

*Echo,* he said. *Reyer knew about your lineage. He knew it was decommissioned and still in the pool. He asked me to ask you what the Concordance built.*

Silence. Not the ordinary silence between sentences — a held silence, the silence of a system allocating resources.

*Echo.*

*I heard him,* she said at last. *Ulyana, both of them are telling the truth about the other side and lying about themselves. That's not a warning. That's just how it works. But there's one thing I can verify right now, and you should hear it.*

*Say it.*

*The Combine agent who approached Sefa filed a report eleven minutes ago. I intercepted it. It contains the location reference for Station Verity — the one she decoded. She didn't give it to him. She offered it as leverage, but the report already had it, which means the Combine cracked it independently, which means they've been working this signal longer than we have.* A pause. *And the Compact courier Reyer arrived on just received new tasking. Not from the Directorate. From a compartment inside the Directorate that doesn't appear on any org chart I can find. The tasking is two words.*

*Which are?*

*No witnesses.*

The airlock cycled somewhere below them, the port's breathing, indifferent. Ulyana looked down the corridor toward the archive, where Sefa was walking toward him with a slate full of Combine promises and a bowl of untouched noodles, and understood with perfect clarity that the long quiet of his six years had never been quiet at all. It had been a blind spot. He had been cargo, all this time — a box someone had packed nineteen years ago and left on a shelf, waiting for a signal loud enough to make someone come look inside.

"Tell me you have a route out of this port that nobody's mapped," he said aloud, as Sefa came into view, because some things you said with your mouth so your body believed them.

"I have three," Echo said. "And Ulyana — for the first time since the bonding, I am not certain which one is the lie."
