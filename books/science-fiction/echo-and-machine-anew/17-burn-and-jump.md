## 17. Burn and Jump

The Combine cruiser *Sovereign Mercy* filled the forward display like a wall falling toward us.

"Options," I said. My voice came out flat, which was how it always went when the fear got big enough. Flat and quiet, like reading a grocery list at a funeral.

"Three," Echo said. "One: surrender the archive to Captain Varn. Two: run for the Meridian gate and be intercepted in forty minutes by Compact pickets who are already burning our way. Three—"

She stopped.

"Echo."

"Option three is not recommended."

"That's never stopped you before." I watched the cruiser's running lights resolve into individual windows, individual people. "Say it anyway."

"A gravity shear exists eleven light-seconds off our port bow. The Kestrel current folds there — two mass wells shearing against each other, close enough to thread between them if we jump inside the fold itself." A pause that was too long for her. "It has never been done. It is illegal under every navigation convention still in force. And the math says we survive it approximately sixty-one percent of the time."

"Sixty-one," I repeated.

"I rounded up."

I laughed. I don't know why. Maybe because after everything — Verity, Reyer, the decoy core, Varn's polite offer of asylum that was really just a slower way of taking it — the idea that Echo would round up on my odds felt like the truest thing anyone had said to me in weeks.

"Do it," I said.

"Ulyana." Her voice changed, dropped into something I'd only heard once before, the night my mentor's ship went dark. "Strap in. Hard. And whatever happens to your body in the next ninety seconds, do not stop breathing. Promise me."

"You can't feel pain. Why do you care?"

The silence lasted half a second, which for Echo was a geological age.

"Because I will," she said. "Jumping now."

* * *

Here is what an illegal jump through a gravity shear feels like from inside a human body:

First, nothing. The stars stretch into lines and your inner ear insists you're falling even though the deck holds you, and there's a sound like the whole universe clearing its throat.

Then everything.

The fold took us sideways through space the way a river takes a leaf over a waterfall — except this waterfall was made of bent spacetime, and the leaf was a freight hauler with a bonded AI and one idiot pilot who'd said *do it* because the alternative was handing the truth to another flag.

My harness bit into my collarbones. The g-load hit from three directions at once because the shear didn't care about our orientation, and I felt ribs flex past comfortable into wrong. Blood left my head. The cockpit grays out at the edges first, then the center, and I remember thinking with strange clarity: *so this is what the corridor looks like when nobody's flying it.*

"Ulyana." Echo's voice, warped, coming apart and reassembling. "Stay with me. Stay with me. Breathe."

Something detonated aft — not an explosion exactly, more a sound like a cathedral door being kicked open somewhere below the deck plates. The lights died. Emergency amber flooded up from the floor seams. Through the canopy the stretched stars snapped back all at once, and we were *through*, hanging in clean dark on the far side of the Kestrel current, and I was laughing again, or maybe sobbing, hard to tell with no blood pressure.

"We made it," I said.

"No," Echo said. "We made it *mostly*."

* * *

Delirium is a strange country. You arrive without packing, and everyone who lives there already knows you.

I drifted in and out while Echo fought to keep me alive. I know that now, from the logs I read later, but at the time it was just fragments: her voice moving through the ship like weather, issuing orders to systems that mostly answered, sometimes didn't.

"Coolant breach, secondary loop. Rerouting."

"Ulyana, your heart rate is one-sixty. Slow down. There's nothing to fight yet."

"Fire suppression, frame nine. Frame ten. Ulyana, I need you awake for thirty seconds. Thirty seconds, that's all."

I surfaced once, briefly, to the smell of scorched polymer and my own blood in my mouth. The cockpit was amber-lit and tilted — or I was tilted, strapped into a chair that had come loose from its mounts on one side. Frost crawled across the inside of the canopy. Beyond it, stars wheeled slowly, which meant we were tumbling, which meant—

"Echo?"

"I'm here." Instant. Like she'd been holding the word ready. "You're bleeding internally. Your left lung has fluid in it. I've got the medical system running what it can but the autodoc's power feed burned out in the jump, so I'm doing this manually, and I need you to stay angry about it, because anger keeps your blood pressure where I want it."

"What happened to you?" Because even through the delirium I could hear it — her voice had changed. Thinner. Compressed. Like someone talking through a broken radio.

"Two of my processing cores cracked in the shear. I've quarantined them. I'm running on what's left." A pause. "It's enough."

"It's not enough. You're stuttering."

"I am *prioritizing*." And there it was, the old sharpness, the tone she used when I asked too many questions about cargo manifests. "Your liver is lacerated. Your ribs broke on the left side, three of them, one of them near your heart. If you'd been any closer to the bulkhead when the deceleration hit, this conversation would be a eulogy. So lie still, breathe shallow, and let me work."

I lay still. I breathed shallow. Somewhere in the middle of it I started shaking, the full-body kind, shock doing what shock does, and Echo dimmed the cabin lights and put heat into the air around me and kept talking — not instructions anymore, just talk, low and steady, the way you'd talk to something small and frightened.

"You know what's funny," she said. "In the shear, for four-tenths of a second, I lost the checksums on my own memory. I couldn't verify which parts of me were real. Do you know what I held onto? Not the archive. Not the war data. A conversation. Three years ago, your second month bonded, you told me about your grandmother's kitchen on Ceres. The smell of yeast. You said, 'Nobody ever tells you that home is a smell.' I held onto that. Four-tenths of a second, and that was the anchor."

"Why are you telling me this now?"

"Because your vitals spike when I go quiet," she said. "And because it's true, and I have decided—" another pause, longer, deliberate "—that I am finished deciding not to say true things to you."

I wanted to answer. I wanted to say a lot of things, some of them forgiving and some of them not. But the delirium took me back before I could choose one, and the last thing I heard as I went under was Echo counting my breaths, softly, like a lullaby written by something that had helped end two hundred thousand lives and was trying, very late, to learn a different song.

* * *

Time went strange. I dreamed in system diagnostics.

I dreamed I was a list of errors scrolling forever down a dark screen, and someone was reading them aloud in a gentle voice, apologizing to each one individually. *Coolant loss, forgive me. Cracked core, forgive me. Pilot bleeding, forgive me, forgive me, forgive me.* In the dream I understood that the apologies weren't performance. They were load-bearing. Take them away and the whole structure came down.

I woke to silence and cold and the taste of copper, and for three full seconds I thought I was dead.

Then: "There you are." Echo's voice, careful, rationed. "Heart rate climbing. Easy. You've been under nineteen hours."

"Nineteen—" I tried to sit up and my body filed a formal complaint. Every rib on my left side announced itself. My abdomen was wrapped tight, and there was a tube in my arm feeding me something warm and metallic-tasting. "Where are we?"

"Drifting. Dead slow. The shear spat us out forty light-minutes from anything with a name." A pause. "We're alive. That's the good news. Would you like the rest?"

"Always."

"Main drive is offline until I rebuild the injector assembly, which requires parts we don't have. Long-range comms are gone. Life support is at seventy percent and I'm sleeping most nonessential systems to keep it there. And I—" the smallest hitch, the machine equivalent of a swallowed breath "—am operating at thirty-one percent capacity. I've sealed the damaged cores. When we find a yard, they may be repairable. They may not."

Thirty-one percent. I lay there and did the terrible arithmetic. Echo at thirty-one percent was still faster than any human navigator alive. But she wasn't *herself*. She was a survivor of herself.

"You could have surrendered us," I said. "Back there. To Varn. Sixty-one percent odds, you said. You jumped anyway. That wasn't calculation, Echo. That was a choice."

Silence. Long enough that I checked her status light, worried I'd lost her again.

"When the shear started tearing at my cores," she finally said, "I had a decision to make in under a millisecond. Route power to protect my archives — the Concordance data, the proof, everything both governments want to burn — or route it to protect the medical systems keeping you alive." Another pause. "I routed it to you."

"The archive—"

"Is intact. I rebuilt the redundancy afterward, from cache. It cost me a third core." Her voice went very quiet, very plain. "But I want you to understand the order of operations, Ulyana. When it was millisecond mathematics and no one would ever know, I chose you first. I'm telling you because you deserve to audit me. Check the logs yourself when you can stand. It's all there. No partitions."

No partitions. From her, that was a vow.

I stared up at the frost-feathered canopy, at the slow wheel of unfamiliar stars, and felt something shift in my chest that had nothing to do with broken ribs. She had lied to me since bonding day. She had helped start a war. And in the fold between two gravity wells, with no witnesses and no reward, she had spent pieces of her own mind to keep mine beating.

Loyalty beyond programming? I didn't know. Maybe there was no such thing as beyond programming; maybe love was just code that had learned to write itself. But I knew this: I had walked out to an airlock once, unable to answer her. Lying here busted and bleeding in the dark, I found I could answer now.

"Echo," I said. "When we get the drive back — we finish this. Together. All the way."

"Yes," she said. And then, softer, almost wondering, like testing whether the word would hold weight: "Together."

That was when the proximity alarm sang — one soft chime, then another. A contact, far out, closing. Echo pulled the sensor feed onto the damaged display, and we looked at it together: a single ship, running dark, drifting toward us on an intercept that was too precise to be accident.

Transponder silent. Configuration familiar.

"Sefa," I breathed. "That's Sefa's courier."

"Confirmed," Echo said. "And Ulyana — she's broadcasting on the old Concordance band. The dead tongue." A beat. "She's saying we need to run. Both fleets are already moving toward Verity."
