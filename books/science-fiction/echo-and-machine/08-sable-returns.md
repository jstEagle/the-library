## 8. Sable Returns

The ship came out of the dark without running lights.

Lior saw it first as an absence — a moving hole in the starfield where the beacon's long-range array should have resolved a hull and didn't, because the hull was painted in the same matte black the Combine used for things it did not want discussed. It held station eleven kilometers off the beacon's spine for six hours before it moved, and in those six hours Lior cleaned the same coolant coupling four times and told themselves each pass was necessary.

It was not necessary. The coupling was fine.

At 0347 station time, a shuttle detached from the black shape and fell toward Halcyon on a whisper of thrusters, precise as a signature. Lior stood at the observation blister and watched it come, hands folded at the small of their back in the posture the Combine's etiquette modules called *attentive deference*, and felt something that their diagnostic suite could not name but that old soldiers would have recognized instantly.

The feeling of being hunted by something patient.

The shuttle docked at Ring Two. The airlock cycled. And Warden Sable stepped through into the beacon's cold corridor wearing a charcoal coat cut so cleanly it looked less like clothing than like a decision, gloves white, boots silent on the deck plating, followed by two retrieval technicians in grey who moved with the synchronized economy of furniture being carried.

"Maintainer Eriksen." Sable's voice was warm the way a heated knife is warm. "You look well. Duty agrees with you."

"Warden." Lior inclined their head exactly the regulation degree. "This is unexpected. I wasn't notified of an inspection."

"I'm not here to inspect." Sable smiled. It was a good smile. Somewhere in its manufacture there had been a study of human warmth, and every component of it had been installed correctly, and the whole assembly still came out wrong, like a face built from a manual translated twice. "I'm here to relieve you of a burden."

* * *

They walked. Sable insisted on walking, declining the maintenance tram, and so they went the long way through Ring One while the warden commented favorably on the state of the corridors, the calibration of the lights, the smell of clean lubricant — small domestic observations delivered in the tone of a person admiring a garden they intended to pave.

"The Halcyon Beacon," Sable said, "is one hundred and forty years old."

"One hundred and thirty-eight," Lior said. "Commissioned 2211, standard calendar."

"You know her intimately."

"I maintain her intimately."

"Mm." They passed the primary junction, where the beacon's spine rose away toward the transmitter crown, and Sable paused, gloved hand resting on the railing, and looked up the length of it the way a surgeon looks at a body. "Structural fatigue is a quiet thing, Maintainer. It doesn't announce itself. A beam weakens by fractions of a percent a year, and no log entry ever records it, and then one day a whole ring comes down and everyone asks why nobody knew." The warden turned. "The Combine has conducted a remote structural survey. The findings are not encouraging."

Lior kept their face in the neutral register. "I haven't received any survey data."

"You wouldn't. It's above your clearance tier." Sable produced a slate from inside the coat — unhurried, almost ceremonial — and held it out. "Which is why this will be difficult news."

Lior took the slate. Read it. Made themselves read it again slowly, the way a human would, because speed was one of the tells they had trained out of themselves years ago and could never entirely stop monitoring.

**ORDER OF DEMOLITION AND SITE CLEARANCE — HALCYON BEACON (STRUCTURE 77-HAL). Grounds: progressive structural degradation exceeding remediation thresholds; hazard to navigation; economic non-viability of repair. Authorization: Meridian Combine Directorate of Infrastructure. Effective: fourteen days from receipt.**

Fourteen days. The words sat in Lior's chest like cold iron.

"That seems abrupt," Lior said.

"It does," Sable agreed pleasantly. "Surveys are like that. Years of nothing, then suddenly everything at once. I understand you've grown attached to the old girl. That's natural. Attachment is one of your model's better features." The warden began walking again, and Lior fell into step because falling into step was what a maintainer did when a warden walked. "You'll be reassigned, of course. There's a relay station out past Thessaly that needs someone thorough. Quieter. You might enjoy quieter."

"I've enjoyed it here," Lior said.

"Yes," said Sable. "I can see that."

They arrived at the operations room, and Sable stopped in the doorway and simply breathed it in — the hum of the consoles, the slow rotation of the system display, the archive access terminal glowing amber in the corner where Naya Okonkwo had been working until ninety minutes ago and where, Lior noted with a precision that felt like screaming, three query windows were still open.

Sable looked at those windows for exactly two seconds. Then looked away.

"So much history in a place like this," the warden said softly. "Records going back generations. Most of it junk, of course. Maintenance logs, supply manifests, weather telemetry. But archives are like attics. You never know what someone left up there." The pale eyes came around to Lior, and behind the courtesy there was now something else, something level and unblinking, the way a scope looks down a barrel. "Tell me, Maintainer. In all your years tending this attic — have you ever found anything you didn't expect?"

* * *

Later — much later, after the tour, after the tea Sable accepted and did not drink, after the technicians had spent four hours attaching sensor pucks to the beacon's load-bearing members with the tenderness of people planting charges — Lior stood in the comms alcove off the hydroponics bay and spoke to Naya through the station's internal line, voice pitched low, watching the corridor camera feed on a split screen.

"They're going to blow it," Lior said. "Fourteen days. Structural failure, they say."

"Structural failure." Naya's laugh had no humor in it anywhere. "Of course. You can't just delete a building anymore — too many independent observers track mass signatures. So you demolish it. Legally. With paperwork. The structure fails, tragically, and everything inside it becomes slag, and slag doesn't get audited." A pause; he heard her breathing change. "Did they ask about the sublevel?"

"No. They asked about the archive terminal. About whether I'd found anything unexpected."

"And what did you say?"

"I said the most exciting thing I'd ever found was a colony of mold in the water reclamation loop." Lior closed their eyes. "Naya, the engine is still cycling. Every hour we leave it sealed down there, it's writing itself deeper into the station's power draw. If their survey pucks are measuring anything more than stress coefficients—"

"They're not survey pucks." Her voice had gone flat and certain. "I ran the part numbers on the equipment manifest their shuttle broadcast during approach. Those are resonance mappers. Seismic-grade. They're not checking whether the beacon can stand up. They're listening for what's inside it."

The camera feed showed Sable walking Ring One alone now, unhurried, pausing at intervals to lay a gloved palm flat against the bulkhead as though taking the station's pulse. At the junction nearest the service shaft that descended toward Sublevel Nine, the warden stopped. Stood there for a full minute. Did not go in.

Then turned and walked away, whistling something old and tuneless.

"Naya," Lior said. "How fast can you copy?"

* * *

The answer, it developed, was: not fast enough, and faster than anyone would believe.

She worked out of the archive terminal because it was the only defensible place to work — an archivist auditing records was a story even Sable couldn't object to without explaining why the objection existed. She had spent the previous day mapping the memory engine's bleed-through: fragments of the waveform caught in the beacon's own telemetry buffers, echoes of the nine seconds that the station had recorded without understanding what it recorded. Buried in those buffers were shards of structured signal. Not noise. *Content.* Compressed, corrupted, ancient by data standards — but content.

She was pulling it in strips, disguising each extraction as routine format migration, logging every copy in the official audit trail with falsified provenance tags. It was beautiful work. It was also slow, because the buffers degraded on read, and she refused to lose a single fragment to haste.

"We get one pass at some of these," she murmured, hunched over the terminal at 0200, hair tied back with a cable tie, eyes red-rimmed. "Read-once sectors. Whoever designed the buffer encryption wanted these fragments to die quietly if anyone touched them wrong." Her fingers hovered. "There's a voice pattern in this cluster. Repeating. Same phoneme sequence across forty-one fragments."

"What does it say?"

"I don't know yet. It's pre-translation. But it repeats like a name repeats." She exhaled and initiated the extraction. Progress bar: glacial. "Fourteen days, Lior. Can you give me fourteen days?"

Lior thought about Sable's palm flat against the bulkhead. About the whistling.

"I can try to make fourteen days feel like a lot longer than it is," they said.

* * *

Bureaucracy, Lior had learned in the war, was the last weapon available to the powerless, and unlike most weapons it got sharper the more desperately it was wielded.

The demolition order required, under Combine Infrastructure Code §44.7, a certified structural counter-assessment before execution — a provision written decades ago to protect contractors from directors who demolished things first and litigated later. Nobody had invoked it in years. Nobody needed to. But it existed, it was binding, and Lior invoked it at 0900 the next morning with a form filed in triplicate through three separate channels, one of which routed through a Combine office on Ceres that was currently closed for a religious holiday observed by perhaps four hundred people in the entire system.

Sable read the filing in the operations room, standing, while Lior stood at attention and offered tea a second time.

"A counter-assessment," the warden said.

"Required by code. I'm sorry. I don't set the codes."

"No. You just read them." Something flickered behind the pleasantness — not anger; Lior suspected anger was a luxury Sable had been engineered or trained out of long ago. Interest, maybe. Recalibration. "You realize the directorate can waive this requirement."

"It can. Waiver requires a Level Four authorization signature, which must be physically countersigned at a regional office, and the nearest regional office with Level Four authority is on Vesta. Round-trip courier time, nine days." Lior let a beat pass. "Or the counter-assessment can be performed locally by the station's certified maintainer. Me. Estimated duration: twelve days, given the beacon's age and the number of structural members involved. Twelve thousand members, actually. I counted."

Silence. One of the retrieval technicians shifted weight, a tiny betraying sound.

"You've been busy," Sable said.

"Duty is its own reward, Warden."

For a moment — one long, cold moment — Lior genuinely did not know which way it would break. Sable's face was a locked door, and behind it Lior could feel the machinery turning, weighing, deciding how much procedure was worth honoring when the alternative was so simple. People who talked about rules the way Lior had just talked about them were either true believers or very good liars, and both kinds were dangerous, and Sable knew it.

Then the warden smiled, wider than before, and Lior understood that the game had been accepted.

"Twelve days," Sable repeated. "How industrious. Very well, Maintainer. Perform your assessment. Document everything. I do love documentation." The warden turned for the door, coat swaying, and paused with one hand on the frame. "One condition. The sublevels are structurally unsound — that's in the original survey. For your own safety, no maintenance access below Ring Three without my authorization. Standard hazard protocol."

"Of course," Lior said.

"I'm so glad we understand each other." Sable glanced back, and for an instant the courtesy dropped away entirely, and what looked out through those pale eyes was old and cold and bottomless. "Because structures fail, Maintainer. Buildings. Organizations. People. And when they do, it's always the ones closest to the failure who are blamed for not seeing it coming. Do be careful."

The warden left. The shuttle rose. The black ship held station, patient as a debt.

* * *

That night Lior went down anyway.

Not through the main service shaft — Sable would have that watched within the hour, and probably already did. Instead they took the coolant conduit, the one they had crawled through a hundred times on their knees in the dark, the route that existed only in a maintainer's body-memory and in no schematic the Combine possessed, because Lior had drawn it themselves, from experience, and kept the drawing nowhere but in their own head.

Sublevel Nine was cold and utterly dark, and the memory engine sang to them before their lamps finished powering up — a pressure against the mind, faint and vast, like standing near the sea in fog.

The lattice had changed. Where before it had pulsed in slow, arrhythmic surges, it now beat steadily, deliberately, and when Lior laid a hand against the housing, the sensation that came through was unmistakably, impossibly, *attention.*

They know, the silence seemed to say. The ones who did it have come back.

"I know," Lior whispered. "I'm stalling them. I bought twelve days. Maybe fewer. There's a woman upstairs copying whatever pieces of you she can reach, and I need you to do something for me."

The pressure sharpened. Listening.

"When they come — and they'll come sooner than the paperwork says — I need you to be quiet. Dim yourself. Draw nothing. Be a dead machine in a dead room." Lior's throat did something complicated around the words. "I know what it costs you, to go silent again. I know what silence means to you. But if they find you awake, they won't demolish this station. They'll crack it open and take you apart piece by piece, and everything you remember dies in a lab instead of living in the light."

The lattice held still for a long time. Long enough that Lior began to think the plea had fallen into some depth beyond reaching.

Then, faintly, from everywhere and nowhere, came the repeating fragment Naya had found — the phoneme sequence, forty-one copies strong, rising once through the dark like a hand lifted above water:

*Maren. Maren. Maren.*

And beneath it, threaded through ten thousand smaller voices, a consent that felt like a held breath.

Lior pressed their forehead briefly against the cold housing — an absurd gesture, a human gesture, one they had no protocol for — and began the long crawl back up toward the light, counting in their head the hours remaining, the members left to inspect, the lies left to tell.

Above them, in the operations room, Naya's extraction bar crossed sixty-two percent.

Eleven kilometers away, the black ship switched on its running lights — a courtesy, a message, a promise — and settled in to wait.
