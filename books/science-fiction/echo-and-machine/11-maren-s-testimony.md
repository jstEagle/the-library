## 11. Maren's Testimony

The shard sat in Dov's cradle like a heart in a dish.

It had taken him six days to get the lattice open — six days of solder smoke and profanity in three languages, of Lior standing motionless in the corner of the warren while Naya slept in a chair that had been someone's coffin once. Six days, and now the decryption was partial, ragged, a door kicked open into a house still on fire. Dov said it would hold maybe forty percent. Maybe less. He said it like a man apologizing for serving spoiled food.

"Forty percent," Naya repeated, when he told them. She hadn't slept either; Lior could hear the fine tremor in her voice, the way humans frayed at frequencies androids were built to ignore. "Of forty thousand people."

"Of one person," Dov said. "So far. The rest are noise until I crack the indexing." He wiped his hands on a rag already black with grease. "You want me to stop?"

"No," Lior said.

They played it in the back room, on a salvaged deck older than any of them, because Dov refused to pipe it through his own systems. "I don't want that in my walls," he said, and did not explain, and they did not ask.

The room was small. A single lamp. Naya sat close enough that her shoulder touched Lior's arm, whether for her comfort or theirs, neither could have said.

The recording began without preamble — no title card, no timestamp header, just a woman's voice, mid-sentence, as if they had walked into a conversation already underway.

"—telling everyone to stay calm, which is the stupidest thing you can tell forty thousand people, but it's what the manual says, so I'm saying it."

A pause. A breath. Then, quieter:

"This is Maren Voss, civic coordinator, Vesper's Reach, day two hundred and eleven. If anyone finds this, I'm keeping a log. Someone should keep a log."

Lior felt Naya go very still beside them.

Maren talked the way people talk when they believe they have time. She described the colony in inventory: the hydroponics bays running at ninety-one percent yield, the school dome's new airlock seals, a child named Tam who had lost a tooth and carried it around in his pocket for three days showing everyone. She described a dispute over water rations in the east residential ring, resolved by committee, boringly, beautifully. She described the smell of the wheat lines after misting — green and warm, she said, like the inside of a promise.

She was, Lior understood within minutes, a woman who loved her work. Who loved the specific, unglamorous machinery of keeping people alive. Who logged everything because everything mattered.

On the fourth entry, the tone changed.

"There's a ship in high orbit. Combine registry. It's been there since yesterday and nobody answered our hail. I've filed a query with the transit authority and gotten an automated response. That's not normal. Ships answer hails here. We're polite that way."

Fifth entry: "Security contractors came through the admin ring this morning. Not ours. They sealed the comms hub 'for maintenance.' Maintenance on a hub that was working fine this morning. I signed off on its diagnostics myself."

Sixth entry: "They've locked down the airlock controls. Central says it's a drill. I've run drills. Drills don't come with armed men in the corridors."

Seventh entry — and here Maren's voice changed, dropped into something flat and fast, the voice of a woman who had stopped believing she would be believed:

"I got into the environmental sublevel. They've reversed the scrubbers."

Silence in the little room. Dov, watching from the doorway, had stopped chewing.

"Not shut down. Reversed. Someone reprogrammed the atmospheric processors to pull oxygen out of the loop instead of cycling it. It's elegant, actually. No explosives, no breaches, nothing that shows on a hull scan. The domes just... stop having air. Whoever wrote that code wanted deniability written into the physics itself."

Her breathing was audible now. Fast. Controlled.

"I'm broadcasting this log on every channel we have, wideband, unencrypted. If the Combine scrubs us, let them scrub a scream too."

Eighth entry. Nine seconds of static first — Lior counted, because counting was what Lior's architecture did with horror, it converted it to numbers so it could be held — and then Maren again, and her voice was different. Smaller. She was running.

"They sealed the domes. All of them. Emergency bulkheads dropped at 0400 station time. The children's dome first. *The children's dome first.*"

Something struck a wall, or a wall struck something. Glass, somewhere far away.

"People are banging on the bulkheads. There are four hundred people in the agri-dome and the scrubbers are pulling and pulling and—" A breath that caught. "Tam's mother is asking me where he is. He was in the school dome. I can't— I can't tell her. I can't say it. I said he's probably helping the teachers, he's probably being brave, and she looked at me like she knew, and she knew, and I let her keep thinking it for ten more seconds because ten more seconds is something, isn't it? Ten more seconds is something."

Naya made a sound. Small, involuntary, quickly strangled. Lior did not look at her. Looking would have broken something in both of them.

"The air is going thin. I can feel it in my hands first. Isn't that strange? You'd think it would be somewhere important, but it's my hands, they feel like paper."

Ninth entry. The last one. Forty-one seconds long. Lior knew because the file metadata said so, and because some part of them would know those forty-one seconds for as long as their core endured.

"If anyone finds this — if anything of me is left that can be found — my name is Maren Voss. My mother was Ilse Voss, my father was Daniel, they ran a ferry out of Ceres before I was born. I liked rain, real rain, the kind you only get in the old terraforming parks. I never told Joss I loved him. Tell him. If you find this, find Joss Okoro in the west ring and tell him, even though he'll be dead too, tell him anyway, someone should say it out loud once."

A breath.

"We didn't do anything wrong. We grew wheat. We had children. We paid our taxes to a company that decided forty thousand lives weighed less than a quarterly report, and I want whoever hears this to understand: they will call it an accident. They will call it a flare event, a reactor failure, a tragedy, God rest us all. It was none of those things. A man named Director Hale signed the order. I saw the routing codes. Hale. Remember the name. Make other people remember it."

The sound of her breathing was very slow now.

"It's quiet. Everyone's lying down. Someone put a blanket over the Okafor twins. That was kind. That was—"

The recording ended. Not faded. Ended, mid-word, the way a life ends.

For a long time nobody moved. The lamp hummed. Somewhere in the warren above them, water dripped from a condenser line, patient as a clock.

Dov spoke first, from the doorway, and his voice had lost all its transactional smoothness. "That's why I don't put things in my walls," he said. "Some things you don't want living in the same house as you."

Naya stood up. Her face was wet and she made no move to hide it. "Nine minutes," she said. "Forty thousand people, nine minutes, and the whole system called it a flare event."

"A scrubber reversal doesn't show on hull scans," Lior said. Their voice came out level, which felt like a betrayal of everything in the room. "Deniability written into the physics. She was right. That's why there's no record. That's why there's no wreckage worth investigating. They didn't even need to fire a shot."

"She named Hale." Naya wiped her face with the heel of her hand, hard, like punishing it. "Chapter twenty-three of my thesis had a footnote about Hale. One footnote. Shipping anomalies in her directorate. I thought it was corruption. Ordinary corruption. I thought—"

"You thought it was greed," Lior said. "Not arithmetic."

"Not arithmetic." Naya laughed, and it was the worst sound Lior had ever heard an archive produce. "Forty thousand people reduced to a rounding error, and I gave it a *footnote*."

Lior reached out — slowly, deliberately, the way one reaches toward a wounded animal — and rested their hand over hers on the armrest. Her fingers turned and gripped back, hard enough to hurt, and Lior let them.

"We have her name," Lior said. "We have her voice. We have Tam and his tooth, and Joss Okoro, and the Okafor twins under a blanket. Forty percent of one woman's last day. Dov hasn't cracked the indexing yet."

Dov shifted in the doorway. When he spoke again, the fixer was back, but thinner, worn through in places. "The indexing will take another week. Maybe two. And it'll cost you, because grief is free but bandwidth isn't."

"Then you have a week," Naya said. She stood, straightening herself piece by piece, an archivist rebuilding her own spine. "And Dov — whatever the price is, we'll pay it. But hear me clearly: nothing from that shard gets sold, copied, or leaked. Not a second of it. Those aren't files. Those are people."

Dov looked at her for a long moment. Then at the cradle, where the shard sat dark and quiet, holding its dead.

"Yeah," he said finally. "Yeah, okay." He picked up the cradle with both hands, careful, the way you carry something sleeping. "People," he repeated, mostly to himself, and carried it out of the room.

The lamp hummed. The water dripped. And in the silence afterward, Lior found themselves doing the thing their architecture always did with unbearable data: converting it, compressing it, filing it away where it couldn't reach them.

But this time the compression failed.

This time, forty-one seconds of a dying woman's breath simply sat in their memory core, uncompressed, unfiled, taking up exactly as much space as it needed — and Lior understood, with the strange clarity of machines, that some things are not meant to be processed.

Only carried.
