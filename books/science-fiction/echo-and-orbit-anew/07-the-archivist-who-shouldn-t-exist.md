## 7. The Archivist Who Shouldn't Exist

The Cael Harbor Naval Archive occupied a decommissioned orbital battery, and it wore its history the way old soldiers wear their scars — deliberately, and where everyone could see them.

Gideon came up the access tube on the morning shift, riding a lift built for ammunition crates, his forged papers sweating against his chest inside a courier's jacket two sizes too big. The disguise was good. He'd paid too much for it to be anything else: a Meridian-born freight handler named Tomas Vrek, licensed for bonded cargo runs, cleared for low-priority archive access through a clerical loophole that had cost him another four hundred credits and a favor he didn't want to think about. The papers said Vrek. The face under the cap said Halloran, and that was the part no forgery could fix.

He'd spent three days on Cael Harbor before working up the nerve to come here. Three days walking streets hung with propaganda murals forty meters tall — *ONE PEOPLE, ONE HARBOR* over a stylized wave of human figures — three days listening to dockworkers curse Meridian in the flat, practiced way people curse weather, three days feeling the border inspections in his spine every time a patrol's eyes slid across him and moved on. The war nobody would name lived in this city like damp lived in stone. You couldn't see it. You could feel it in everything.

The archive lobby was marble, or something pretending to be marble, and cold enough that his breath almost showed. A reception desk, a scanner arch, a bored security officer reading a screen behind bulletproof glass. Gideon handed over Vrek's credentials and waited for the machine to decide whether he existed.

It took eleven seconds. He counted them against his heartbeat.

"Access tier three," the officer said, sliding a chit across the counter without looking up. "Reading rooms only. No removals, no copies of classified holdings, no photography. You break a rule, we break your contract. Next."

The chit was warm from the officer's hand. Gideon took it and walked through the arch, half-expecting an alarm, and felt only the faint static tingle of the scanner cataloguing him — biometrics, gait, retinal pattern — into some database he'd never be able to scrub himself out of. Somewhere on Cael Harbor, a file now existed that read *VREK, TOMAS — archive visitor 0847*. And somewhere beneath that file, if anyone ever looked closely enough, was a face that matched a dead man's investigation file on the other side of a war.

He told himself the risk was calculated. He told himself a lot of things lately.

* * *

The naval holdings were on the third ring, down corridors lined with document cases older than either colony's current government. The lighting was tuned to preserve paper rather than comfort people, a dim amber wash that made everything look like it was already being remembered. Gideon passed two other researchers, both elderly, both absorbed, neither interested in a freight handler in a cheap jacket. Tier-three access apparently bought invisibility along with its limitations.

His plan was simple, which meant it was probably doomed. Halloran Prime's final case notes — the ones Gideon carried in his own memory, transcribed word for word because Prime had been paranoid enough to memorize what he couldn't safely store — referenced a shipping manifest discrepancy. The *Perihelion*, a deep-range survey vessel, had drawn drive components from a joint procurement program nine years before her launch. Components that shouldn't have existed. Components whose requisition codes pointed at a program designation Prime had never been able to crack: **CHRONOS-NULL**.

Prime had come to Cael Harbor looking for CHRONOS-NULL. Nine days later, he was dead in a Meridian alley with his skull caved in and his data slates wiped.

So Gideon would do what Prime had done. Search the same catalogs. Pull the same threads. And try very hard not to die at the end of it.

The public terminal for tier-three users sat in a carrel at the ring's far end, a slab of matte polymer with a search interface designed by someone who hated researchers. Gideon settled in, keyed Vrek's chit, and began.

CHRONOS-NULL returned nothing. Not *no results* — nothing. The query field blinked, accepted the string, and produced a blank page with no error code, which was worse. Error codes admitted you'd asked a question. Blankness pretended there had never been a question at all.

He tried variants. Chronal displacement. Temporal drive research. Deep-range propulsion, experimental. Each query landed in the same soft silence. After forty minutes he understood what he was looking at: not a gap in the records but a shape cut out of them, surgical and total, the archival equivalent of a room repainted so many times even the doorframe was gone.

Someone had gotten here first. Someone with better access than a dead detective and deeper reach than a forged courier's chit.

"You're doing it wrong."

The voice came from behind his left shoulder, quiet, unhurried, close enough that Gideon's hand was moving toward the knife at his belt before his brain caught up. He turned.

A woman stood at the end of the carrel, holding a document case against her hip the way other people held sleeping children. She was tall, dark-skinned, somewhere past fifty, with gray threading through close-cropped hair and reading glasses pushed up onto her forehead like a second pair of eyes. Her badge read **OYELARAN, S. — SENIOR ARCHIVIST**, and her expression held no alarm, no suspicion, none of the things a stranger's voice at your back should produce.

Only recognition. Warm, weary, and absolutely certain.

"You're doing it wrong," she said again. "You're searching like the files are lost. They're not lost. They're *kept*. There's a difference, and the difference has a door." She tilted her head. "You always did lead with the obvious queries. I used to tell you patience was a research skill. You never listened."

Gideon's mouth went dry. Every instinct he owned — the ones he'd been born with and the ones he'd inherited from a murdered man — were screaming at once, and they were screaming different things. *Run.* *She knows the name.* *She called you 'you.'*

"I'm sorry," he managed. "You've mistaken me for someone else."

"No," Sefa Oyelaran said. "That's the one thing I haven't done."

She set the document case down on the carrel beside the terminal, deliberately, the way you put down a card you've been holding all game. Then she looked at him — really looked, the way archivists look, cataloguing provenance and condition — and something in her face flickered, grief crossing certainty and leaving both changed.

"The last time I saw you," she said, "you were standing on the launch apron at Anchorage Basin, wearing a survey team jacket with the collar turned up against the wind, and you told me—" her voice caught, steadied, "—you told me you'd come find me when you got back. That you wanted the full procurement history for the drive program, unredacted, because somebody had to keep honest books on what they were about to do out there."

The archive's amber light hummed. Somewhere in the stacks, a page turned.

"That wasn't me," Gideon said quietly.

"I know exactly who you are, Mr. Halloran." She pronounced the name carefully, each syllable placed like an artifact in foam. "You're not him. I can see that now that you're standing in front of me instead of across a table. He had a scar through his left eyebrow. He laughed from the chest. He thought he was funnier than he was." A pause. "You hold yourself like someone carrying weight that isn't yours. Which, I suppose, is precisely accurate."

The knife handle burned against Gideon's palm where his hand still rested near it. He made himself let go. Made himself breathe.

"Nine years ago," he said slowly, "the *Perihelion* launched from Anchorage Basin. Meridian registry. Deep-range survey. It vanished twelve years— " He stopped. Recounted. Felt the floor tilt under the arithmetic. "No. Twelve years ago. The ship was declared lost twelve years ago. But you say you met—"

"I met him nine years ago," Sefa said. "Three years after the *Perihelion* was declared lost with all hands." She watched the confusion land on him and nodded, unsurprised, as if she'd rehearsed this moment for nearly a decade and had long since stopped expecting it to make sense to anyone but her. "You understand my problem, Mr. Halloran? For nine years I have known two impossible facts. One: a man named Gideon Halloran interviewed me about the CHRONOS-NULL program, and he was alive three years after the ship he served on died. Two: he was killed on Meridian six days after that interview, and everyone official insisted the *Perihelion* had never carried anyone by that name."

She picked up the document case again. Held it out to him.

"So I did what archivists do. I kept honest books. And I waited. Because a man who tells you he'll come back for the truth usually means it, even if it takes him longer than he thinks — even if it takes him dying and being issued again, fresh, on the far side of a war, with no idea why his own face makes senior archivists go pale."

The world had gone strange and slow. Gideon heard his own pulse in the silence between her sentences. *Served on.* She'd said it plainly, without drama, and it detonated anyway.

"I wasn't on any ship," he said. "I was decanted eight years ago. I've never left the shipping lanes. I don't—"

"—remember. No. Of course you don't." Her voice gentled, and it was the gentleness that frightened him most, because it was the gentleness people use on the bereaved. "But I checked the launch manifest myself, Mr. Halloran. Before they sealed it. Before they started killing people who checked. Gideon Halloran, mission specialist, chronometric systems. Crew photo thirty-one of thirty-one." She tapped the document case. "You were aboard the *Perihelion* when she jumped. Whatever came back from that jump — whatever they grew in a vat on Meridian and fed another man's memories — some part of you was on that ship. And I have spent nine years wondering which part is standing in front of me right now."

* * *

They walked. She led; he followed, because the alternative was standing in the open arguing with a woman who knew his face better than his own reflection did, and because some deep-buried reflex — his, or Prime's, he honestly couldn't tell anymore — trusted the way she moved through the stacks like a person moving through her own house.

Down a service corridor marked ENVIRONMENTAL SYSTEMS — AUTHORIZED STAFF ONLY, past humming coolant conduits wrapped in frost-silver insulation, to a door she opened with a physical key, brass and antique, absurd among all the colony's biometric locks. Beyond it was a small room that had clearly once been a sensor station: consoles gutted, shelves installed, a kettle on a heating plate, two chairs, and walls of document cases labeled in her own handwriting.

"A dead space," she said, closing the door. "I found it my first year here. The surveillance grid has a hole shaped exactly like this room, and in nine years no one has wondered why. Bureaucracies forget their own blind spots. It's the one mercy they offer." She filled the kettle. "Sit down, Mr. Halloran. You look like a man who hasn't slept since he diverted course for a ghost ship."

The words hit him like cold water. "How do you know about the—"

"The *Perihelion* pinged a distress beacon eleven days ago on a frequency that hasn't been monitored since the war began. Both colonies' navies lit up like festival nights. Do you imagine a senior archivist with my clearance misses that?" She set two cups on the shelf beside the kettle. "Half my colleagues were reassigned within a day. Emergency 'records audits.' Men I've worked with for a decade, gone overnight, doors sealed. When institutions move like that, it means the thing they're hiding has moved first." She glanced at him. "You docked with her. Didn't you. Against protocol. Alone."

"Yes."

"And?"

"They're alive." His voice came out rougher than he intended. "Thirty-one crew. Alive and healthy and *younger*, Captain Voss doesn't remember the last decade, and I have spent eleven days trying to make that sentence stop being true." He took the tea she offered without tasting it. "Tell me you can explain it. Tell me there's a file in this building that makes it make sense."

Sefa lowered herself into the second chair and regarded him for a long moment, steam rising between them.

"There is a file," she said finally. "Several. I've spent nine years assembling them from fragments, misfilings, procurement ghosts, and the private papers of dead men, because the real files were removed before I ever got clearance to know they existed. What I have isn't proof. Proof gets people killed — I can name the people, if you want the list, and you'll recognize at least one name on it." Something moved behind her eyes. "What I have is a confession, Mr. Halloran. Mine. Because I helped build the paperwork for that program, back when I believed the seals on it were routine classification, and I have owed a debt to thirty-one people on a lost ship ever since."

Outside the dead room, the archive breathed — coolant, ventilation, the vast slow metabolism of preserved paper. Gideon realized his hands were shaking around the cup and made them stop.

"Why tell me?" he asked. "You've waited nine years. You could wait nine more. Why hand it to a stranger wearing a dead man's face?"

"Because you're not a stranger." She said it simply, without mysticism, an archivist stating a documented fact. "Because I met you. Because you shook my hand and promised to come back, and I am old enough to know that promises outlive the people who make them — they just need someone to carry them." She leaned forward. "And because whoever killed the man whose memories you're wearing is still alive, still in power, and still cleaning house. My reassignment notice arrived yesterday morning, Mr. Halloran. Effective end of week. Transfer to a polar weather station. They're not going to kill me — killing archivists creates inquiries. They're going to bury me somewhere nothing important is ever filed." A thin smile. "Which means I have approximately four days to give away everything I know, and you are the only person on this colony who might survive knowing it. Clones are inconvenient to disappear. Too many oversight committees invested in you."

The kettle clicked off. Neither of them moved toward it.

"One question first," Gideon said. "Before you show me whatever's in those cases. One question, and I need you to answer it straight, because I've spent eleven days drowning in versions of this and I need one solid floor to stand on."

"Ask."

"When you met him — me — on that apron. Nine years ago. Three years after the ship was lost." He made himself hold her gaze. "Was there anything wrong with him? Anything you noticed and filed away and told yourself was nothing? Because I need to know if the man I'm copied from came back from that jump *whole*."

Sefa was quiet for a while. The frost on the coolant pipes ticked as it melted and refroze.

"He was younger," she said at last. "I noticed it and told myself it was good lighting. He'd been gone three years and he looked like he'd been gone one. I remember thinking survey work must agree with him." Her voice dropped. "And when I mentioned the ship's loss date — casually, just confirming the timeline — he flinched. Not at the date. At the number. Like three years was a figure that didn't fit anywhere in his head, a bill he couldn't account for." She met Gideon's eyes. "I've been an archivist for thirty-one years, Mr. Halloran. I know what it looks like when a record disagrees with itself. Your original was a record disagreeing with itself, walking around in a survey jacket, promising to come back for the truth."

She rose, crossed to the wall of cases, and drew out the one she'd carried all morning — the one she'd been carrying, Gideon suddenly understood, every day for nine years, waiting for a door she couldn't have known would ever open.

Set it on the table between them.

"CHRONOS-NULL," she said. "Everything I have. And Mr. Halloran — before you open it, you should understand that the last man who read these documents with me was dead within the week. Whatever you decide to do with what's in here, decide fast."

Her hand rested on the case's latch. Through the wall, faint and rhythmic, came the sound of the archive's clock, counting down something neither of them had named yet.

Gideon reached past her and opened the case himself.
