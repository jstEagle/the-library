## 12. The List in His Head

The room Sefa Oyelaran had prepared for him was not a room at all. It had been a records vault once — Gideon could see the ghost of it in the reinforced doorframe, the stripped mounting brackets where server racks had lived, the floor bolts polished smooth by decades of archive carts. Now it held a single reclining chair salvaged from a medical bay, a tangle of neural interface cabling that looked older than the war, and a lamp turned low enough that the walls dissolved into shadow.

"You'll want to know what this does before I do it," Sefa said. She moved around the chair with the unhurried economy of someone who had rehearsed this a hundred times in her head and never once out loud. "So you can stop me if it goes wrong."

"And if I can't stop you?"

"Then we'll both learn something." She said it without cruelty, which somehow made it worse.

Gideon sat down in the chair. The padding exhaled dust. Above them, through a grate in the ceiling, he could hear the ordinary sounds of Cael Harbor's underlevels — footsteps, a cart, someone laughing two corridors away — and the ordinariness of it made the room feel more exposed, not less. Anyone could walk by. The most dangerous thing on two colonies was about to happen inside a repurposed closet with a bad seal on the door.

"Explain it again," he said. "Slowly."

Sefa knelt beside the interface console and began pairing cables, her fingers sure. "Your original hid something in his own memory. Not a data chip, not a cipher — those get found. He hid it the way people hide things from themselves. A dissociative construct. Trauma-anchored, probably, because trauma is the one lock nobody picks voluntarily." She glanced up at him. "Halloran Prime knew he was going to be interrogated. Maybe tortured. Maybe worse — memory extraction, if Meridian had the tech then, which they did. So he built a room inside his head and put the list behind a door he couldn't open even if he wanted to."

"And now I want to."

"Now you're going to let me convince you to." She finished the last connection and held up the sensor band — a circlet of dull metal threaded with contact filaments, like a crown designed by someone who hated crowns. "This doesn't read your thoughts. It reads the shape of your attention. When you remember something real, your attention has a texture. When you remember something constructed — a false memory, an implanted one, a locked one — the texture stutters. I follow the stutter."

"To the door."

"To the door." She set the band gently on his head. It was cold. "And then I ask you to open it, and you will have to do that yourself, Gideon. I can find the lock. I cannot turn the key. If I force it, the whole construct collapses and takes everything around it with it — possibly including the man you currently believe yourself to be."

He looked at her. In the lamplight her face was all planes and patience, and he thought: she waited nine years for me. She met my original on a ship that hadn't launched yet, and she watched him die secondhand across nine years of silence, and she still believes this is worth doing.

"What happens," he asked, "if the thing behind the door isn't a list?"

Sefa paused. For the first time since he'd known her — three days, four conversations, one act of treason against her own government — she looked uncertain.

"Then we deal with what's there," she said. "That's the only honest answer I have."

She activated the interface.

* * *

It began as light behind his eyes.

Not brightness — texture. He felt his own mind the way a diver feels current: pressure, direction, the sense of vast volume moving past. Sefa's voice came from very far away, or from very close, he couldn't tell which.

"Tell me what you see."

"A corridor," Gideon said. His mouth worked; he wasn't sure he was speaking aloud. "Grey. Station lighting. Meridian standard."

"That's the surface layer. Your day-to-day recall. Walk it."

He walked. The corridor was his life rendered as architecture — doors on either side labeled with things he knew. *Perihelion manifest.* *Voss interview transcript.* *His mother's face* — no, not his mother, Halloran Prime's mother, a woman whose grief Gideon had inherited like a coat that fit too well. Each door opened easily when he touched it, spilling its contents into the corridor air, and each time Sefa murmured, "Good. Real. Keep moving."

At the end of the corridor there was a junction.

Beyond the junction, the corridor continued — but wrong. The lighting shifted spectrum. The gravity felt half a degree off. And the doors were different: newer, cleaner, unlabeled, arranged with a precision nothing in a human mind had any business possessing.

"There," Sefa said, and her voice tightened. "Do you feel it?"

"It's cold," Gideon said. "Why is it cold?"

"Because he built it cold. Cold keeps visitors out." A breath. "Gideon. Listen carefully. Everything past this junction belongs to him, not to you. When you cross, you may experience it as your own memory. You must keep saying my name. Can you do that?"

"Sefa."

"Again."

"Sefa. Sefa." The name was an anchor line. He gripped it.

He crossed the junction.

* * *

The cold hit him first, and then the smell — ozone and old coffee and the particular antiseptic bite of a Meridian intelligence safehouse, and Gideon knew this place, knew it with his own nerves, because Halloran Prime had known it, had sat in this exact chair nine years ago with a stolen datapad full of names and forty hours before a scheduled debriefing he did not intend to survive honestly.

He was watching himself watch. That was the horror of it. The memory played from inside Prime's skull, and Gideon stood behind his own eyes like a passenger in a vehicle already moving.

Prime's hands were shaking. On the desk: a printout, actual paper, because paper couldn't be remotely wiped. Two columns. Gideon tried to read the names and the memory blurred them deliberately, the way a dream blurs text, and he understood dimly that this was the lock working exactly as designed.

*"You can't memorize four hundred names,"* Prime muttered to the empty room, and his voice was Gideon's voice, and hearing it was like swallowing glass. *"But you can bury them somewhere they'll never dig."*

The scene folded. Time lurched — the safehouse became a transit car, the transit car became a stairwell, and in each location Prime was doing the same thing: repeating the list. Aloud, silently, in patterns. Pairing names with images, images with sensations, sensations with pain. Building the construct brick by brick while Gideon rode inside him, helpless, feeling every repetition carve grooves into a brain that would one day be scanned, sequenced, copied —

— copied —

"Oh," Gideon said aloud, in the vault, in the chair, tears running freely down his face. "Oh, that's what I am. That's how he did it."

"Gideon." Sefa's voice, sharp now. "Stay with me. Say my name."

"Sefa." But he kept talking, because the memory was still unfolding and stopping now might mean losing it forever. "He didn't hide the list in his head. He hid it in *mine*. He requested the clone early — before the murder, before anyone knew he'd need one. He spent three weeks imprinting the list into his baseline memory architecture so deep that when they cloned him, the copy carried it forward automatically. I'm not a witness to the crime, Sefa. I'm the *filing cabinet*."

"Yes," Sefa said softly. "I think that's why you were made. Now — the door. Do you see it?"

He saw it.

At the center of the folding scenes, beneath the repetitions, there was a door. It was not architectural. It was made of the moment itself — the moment Prime had chosen as the anchor, the worst moment he owned, sealed shut with everything he felt about it. Gideon approached it and the closer he came the more he felt it: not fear exactly, but *grief*, dense as lead, radiating off the door like heat off a reactor housing.

Behind this door was the moment Prime had decided to die for the list. Gideon could feel that too. The decision was the hinge.

"I can't," he heard himself say. "Sefa, I can't open this. It's not mine. It's his grief. I've been carrying it for nine years and I don't even know — I don't know which parts of missing her are mine—"

"Whose?" Sefa asked, gentle and merciless.

"My mother's. *His* mother's. I grieve a woman I never met. I love a city I've never walked. Every morning I wake up homesick for a life that was *stolen*—" His hands were fists against the chair arms. Somewhere far away the sensor filaments were reading the storm of his attention, and Sefa was watching the storm, and he realized distantly that his rage was useful to her, that emotional turbulence was exactly what cracked constructs open, and he hated her a little for knowing that, and loved her a little for waiting until he understood it himself.

"That's the key," she said. "Not despite that. *Because* of that. The door is made of his grief. You are the only person alive who grieves with the same nervous system. Open it, Gideon. Open it as him. Open it as you. There's no difference anymore, and that's not a tragedy right now — it's the mechanism."

He reached for the door.

* * *

The pain was total and brief, like a star going nova inside his skull.

Every name came through at once. Four hundred and twelve names, both colonies, paired with faces, voices, the smell of specific rooms, the sound of specific rain — Prime had encoded them in sensory chaos so that no interrogation could ever flatten them into data, and now the chaos flattened *Gideon*, poured through him like a river through a broken dam. He screamed. He was aware of screaming. He was aware of Sefa's hands on his shoulders, grounding him, her voice cutting through in fragments:

"—with me — count backward — seven — six —"

He counted. The names kept coming anyway. A Cael Harbor dockworker named Imre Vashti. A Meridian schoolteacher named Odile Ferran. Children. The list had children on it, and the children were not on the list to be saved — Gideon understood this suddenly, viscerally, the way you understand a fall after it's happened — the children were on the list of the *other* colony. The ones who would sleep through the jump and wake to a world that had been reorganized around their absence.

"No," he gasped. "No, that's not — he wouldn't—"

"He would," Sefa said, grim. "He found it. That's why he died. Keep counting."

Five. Four.

The memory was changing now, degrading as the construct collapsed, and Gideon grabbed at it desperately — names slipping, faces smearing, whole blocks of the list dissolving into static. He clawed at them. Held maybe half. The rest went out like lights in a building, floor by floor, and the last thing the construct showed him before it died was Prime himself, standing in the safehouse doorway on the last night of his life, looking back at the desk, at the paper, at the buried thing, and saying — clearly, quietly, in Gideon's own voice:

*"If anyone finds this, I'm sorry it's you."*

Three. Two. One.

White.

* * *

He surfaced slowly, like a man rising from deep water.

The vault ceiling. The lamp. Sefa's face above his, drawn and damp with sweat, her hand still gripping his shoulder hard enough to bruise.

"Talk to me," she said.

"Four hundred and twelve names," Gideon rasped. His throat felt scoured. "I think I have... two hundred? Maybe less. The rest collapsed with the construct." He laughed, and it came out wrong, wet and thin. "Nine years of being a dead man's filing cabinet and I come out of it half-shredded."

"Half is more than either government has." Sefa pressed a water flask into his hand. "Can you stand?"

He tried. The room tilted and settled. In the small viewport-slit near the ceiling, Cael Harbor's artificial dawn was bleeding grey-gold into the underlevels, and Gideon watched it with the strange double vision that would probably never leave him now — seeing the sky both as himself and as a dead man who had once stood in a safehouse and traded his life for a piece of paper.

"Sefa." His voice steadied. "The names I kept. They're organized. Both colonies, ranked — priority tiers. Prime didn't just steal the lists. He stole the *selection criteria*. Age brackets, genetic markers, skill classifications. It's a blueprint for who gets to exist."

She was quiet for a long moment.

"Then we got what we came for," she said finally. "More than. Gideon — a partial list plus the criteria means the pattern can be reconstructed. Given time, given access to census data, the missing names become arithmetic instead of secrets."

"How much time?"

Her answer was interrupted by the door.

It didn't open so much as cease to be closed — the lock cycling with a soft administrative chime that Gideon recognized from a dozen border inspections, the sound of authority arriving politely. Sefa was on her feet before the panel slid wide, one step in front of him, and Gideon had the mad impulse to pull her behind him instead, except his legs were still shaking and the room was still tilting.

Two figures in the corridor. The first wore Cael Harbor security grey, expressionless, hand resting on a sidearm out of protocol rather than threat.

The second wore civilian clothes cut too well for a station underlevel, and smiled with the particular warmth of a man who has never once needed to raise his voice.

"Gideon Halloran," the man said. "Or should I say — welcome back. We've been expecting the excavation to finish." He stepped inside uninvited, glancing around the gutted vault with mild professional interest, and his eyes when they found Gideon's were almost kind.

"My name is Crane. Deputy Director Crane. And I'm afraid we need to talk about what you just remembered — because so help me, son, I was there when they wrote it into you."
