## 14. Deputy Director Crane

The summons came on cream-colored paper.

That was the first wrong thing. In nine years of working the Meridian–Cael run, Gideon had received exactly three pieces of physical correspondence: a tax notice, a union ballot, and a condolence card from the cloning authority when Halloran Prime's estate was formally transferred to him — *your grief is our grief*, the card had said, in a font designed by someone who hated both grief and cards. Everything else lived in the network, encrypted, logged, timestamped. Paper was what you used when you didn't want a record. Or when you wanted the record to be beautiful.

The envelope bore no stamp, no return address. Just his name in copperplate ink — *Gideon Halloran* — and beneath it, in smaller script: *Deputy Director's Office, Bureau of Transit Integrity.*

Transit Integrity. He'd never heard of the bureau. That was the second wrong thing. A government agency you hadn't heard of was either trivial or enormous, and nothing about the weight of that envelope felt trivial.

He read it four times in his cabin aboard the *Long Answer*, docked at Meridian High with two pallets of seed grain and a crate of someone's grandmother's letters riding in his hold. The message was courteous to the point of seduction. It thanked him for his "civic diligence" in responding to the distress ping from the vessel *Perihelion*. It noted that his "initiative reflected credit on the independent carrier community." It requested — *requested*, the verb doing all kinds of quiet work — his presence at an interview regarding "standard post-incident protocols," at his earliest convenience, travel and lodging provided.

It was signed: *A. Crane, Deputy Director.*

Gideon sat with it for a long time while the station's docking clamps ticked around him like a cooling engine. He thought about the phrase *post-incident protocols*, which implied there were protocols, which implied this had happened before. He thought about how the Perihelion's crew — thirty-one people biologically younger than their own service records, a ship whose jump drive reached backward into flesh — had been in Meridian custody for eleven days now, sealed behind quarantine bulkheads on a station deck that officially did not exist. And he thought about the last line of the letter, which he'd skipped past twice because it read like pleasantry:

*I feel I know you already. We have mutual friends, of a sort. Some of them are you.*

He should have refused. He knew that even then, some cold little auditor in the back of his skull flagging the risk assessment in red. Men like him — men made from other men — did not get invited to deputy directors' offices for pleasant conversations. They got invited when they'd already been weighed, measured, and found either useful or inconvenient.

But the letter also said something else, buried in the fourth paragraph, and it was the thing that got him off the ship and onto the shuttle: *We would very much like to close your original's case. We understand how much it has cost you to carry it open.*

Nobody outside the file knew he carried it. Nobody official, anyway. The murder of Halloran Prime was nine years cold, formally unsolved, quietly shelved. Whoever wrote this letter knew not just that Gideon had inherited the investigation but that he'd never put it down — that he worked the long route partly because the route passed through every place his original had ever stood, and standing where a dead man stood was as close as a copy could get to mourning him.

So he went. Because the letter knew him. And because the only thing more frightening than walking into a trap set by someone who studied you was admitting that being studied felt, horribly, like being known.

* * *

The Deputy Director's office occupied the top floor of a building in Meridian's administrative ring that looked, from the outside, like it had been designed to be forgotten — gray stone composite, no signage, windows tinted against the colony sun. Inside, though, everything was warm. Real wood paneling, salvaged from Earth ships two centuries dead. A carpet so deep his boots left prints. Lamps turned low and amber, the whole room lit like a study rather than an office, like a place where difficult things were discussed over brandy and then gently forgotten.

And books. Actual paper books, hundreds of them, floor to ceiling. Gideon clocked them the way he'd been trained to clock cargo manifests — by value and by threat. First editions. Technical manuals from before the war. A shelf of naval histories with the Cael Harbor imprints still visible on the spines, which was either a collector's indifference to politics or a very deliberate message about who owned what knowledge.

"You're punctual." The voice came from behind the desk before the man rose to meet him. "I appreciate that. Punctuality is honesty about time. Most people treat appointments as aspirations."

Anton Crane was not what Gideon had prepared for. He'd built the man in his head during the shuttle ride — sixtyish, gray, hard-eyed, the bureaucratic carnivore with the handshake that counts your fingers. Instead: a broad-shouldered man in his fifties with silver going soft at the temples, a face built for warmth, laugh lines actually earned, dressed in civilian wool the color of wet slate. He crossed the room with his hand extended and gripped Gideon's forearm, pilot-fashion, instead of shaking it.

"Gideon Halloran. Finally." He held the grip a half-second longer than courtesy required, studying Gideon's face with an expression that wasn't quite appraisal and wasn't quite affection. "You have his eyes. Not his face — they corrected the jaw, the nose, the whole craniofacial package, you're taller than he was — but the eyes came through untouched. I argued for that, you know. At the design review. Everyone else wanted full cosmetic divergence, deniability, the works. I said no. I said the eyes stay."

Gideon kept his voice level. "Why?"

"Because a man should be able to look at his own work and recognize it." Crane released his arm and gestured him toward the chairs by the low table. "Sit. Tea? It's actual Ceylon, don't ask what I traded for it. The colonies produce a hundred synthetic approximations and every one of them tastes like apology."

"I'm fine."

"You're not, but that's a separate conversation." Crane poured two cups anyway, set one within Gideon's reach, and settled into the chair opposite with the ease of a man who had never once entered a room unsure of his welcome. Up close, the warmth had architecture. Every relaxed line of him was load-bearing. Gideon had spent nine years reading shippers and smugglers and border inspectors, and he knew performance when it was this good; the question was what the performance was *for*.

"Let me say the quiet part first," Crane said, "so we can spend our time on the interesting parts. You diverted a licensed freighter against protocol to intercept a vessel declared lost twelve years ago. You then transported thirty-one unauthorized personnel from that vessel to a station under false manifest classifications — 'salvage,' if I recall your filing, which was creative, by the way, listing living people as hull plating. You have since made inquiries through channels that are monitored, accessed records that are sealed, and asked questions in port bars that were heard by people who report to me." He smiled, and it reached his eyes, which somehow made it worse. "By any reasonable standard, you are in a great deal of trouble."

"And by yours?"

"By mine, you did exactly what you were designed to do." Crane lifted his tea, blew across it. "Which raises a fascinating question I've been turning over since the Perihelion pinged: did you divert because you chose to, or because the choice was installed? Don't answer. I genuinely don't know, and I helped build you, which should tell you something about how well anyone understands what comes out of that process."

"You helped build me." Gideon said it flat, testing the shape of it.

"I commissioned the project. Twelve years ago. Before the war ended, technically — the authorization predates the cease-fire by six weeks, which is one of those details historians will trip over someday." Crane said it the way another man might discuss a garden he'd planted. "Halloran Prime was dying professionally, you understand. Not literally — that came later — but his investigation had hit the wall every investigation eventually hits: the wall where the truth stops being information and starts being a liability. He needed to get the evidence out of his own head and somewhere it couldn't be interrogated, tortured out of him, or simply deleted along with him. Cloning was the answer. Memory-faithful inheritance. A dead-drop that walks around and pays its own fuel bills."

"That's what I am. Storage."

"What you are," Crane said mildly, "is the only reason the truth survived its owner. I'd call that a promotion over storage."

Gideon's hands were steady in his lap. He'd learned steadiness young — or rather, he'd woken up with it, one of the borrowed competencies, Halloran Prime's twenty years of keeping his voice flat across interrogation tables folded into his nervous system like ballast. But underneath the ballast something was moving. "You said the authorization predates the cease-fire. You're telling me the cloning program is a war program."

"I'm telling you everything on this side of the war is a war program. Including the tea, probably." Crane set down his cup. "But let's not get ahead of ourselves. I brought you here for three reasons, and I'll give them to you in ascending order of importance, because I find people listen better when they know the structure."

He raised one finger. "First: the Perihelion crew. They're safe. Comfortable, fed, medically supervised. Captain Voss is recovering — her memory disruption is significant but not, we believe, permanent. I want you to hear that from me directly, because I know you've been imagining the worst, and I'd rather have your trust than your fear. Fear makes people stupid. Trust makes them useful."

"Useful to whom?"

"To the situation. Second—" a second finger "—you're not in trouble. Formally, your diversion never happened; I've had the logs amended. The bureau you received my letter from doesn't appear in any public registry, and it won't, because its function is to make sure certain events never require registries. You've stumbled into the largest containment operation in colonial history, Mr. Halloran, and the fact that you're sitting in this chair drinking Ceylon instead of lying in a holding cell should tell you precisely how well you've conducted yourself."

"And third?"

Crane leaned back. For a moment — just a moment — the charm dimmed, like a lamp turned down on a rheostat, and what showed underneath was older and colder and much more tired. Then the lamp came back up.

"Third," he said, "is that I liked your original. Genuinely. He was stubborn, self-serious, allergic to authority, and completely incorruptible, and in twenty years of this work I can count the incorruptible ones on one hand and still have fingers left to hold my cup. When he died, I lost the best investigator either colony ever produced, and I have spent nine years wondering whether the program that preserved him also killed him. Whether we handed a man a secret too heavy to carry and then acted surprised when the carrying crushed him." His eyes went to Gideon's, and they were Halloran Prime's eyes, and looking into them was like looking into a mirror angled at the past. "So before we talk business, I want to ask you something, and I want you to answer honestly, because I'll know otherwise. Do you remember his death?"

The room went very quiet. Somewhere below, the station's ventilation breathed.

"Yes," Gideon said.

"Not the file. Not the scene photographs. Do you *remember* it. The way he experienced it."

"The office. Evening shift change, so the corridor cameras cycled blind for ninety seconds. He'd pulled the drive telemetry two days earlier and hidden it where the excavation couldn't reach — where *I* couldn't reach, at first, he walled it off deeper than the inheritance layer. He knew someone was coming. He'd stopped locking his door, which I think was deliberate; he wanted whatever happened recorded on his own terms. There was a smell — machine oil and someone's reheated noodles from the break room. Two shots. He didn't see the face. He saw shoes. Gray, institutional, the kind issued in—" Gideon stopped. He hadn't known he knew that. The knowledge surfaced like a body coming up through dark water. "The kind issued in facilities run by the Office of Fleet Continuity."

Crane watched him without blinking. "Do you know what's remarkable?" he said softly. "Everything you just described matches the forensic record except the shoes. The shoes aren't in any file. The corridor camera caught the killer from the knees up — deliberately, we assume; whoever did it knew the camera angles. So the shoes exist in exactly one place in the universe." He tapped his own temple. "In there. Inherited. Which means the single most valuable piece of evidence in a nine-year-old murder has been walking around inside your skull this entire time, on a mail run, hauling seed grain and grandmothers' letters, and you didn't even know you had it."

"Is that why I'm here? The shoes?"

"No. The shoes are a detail, and details are for investigators, and I'm hoping to retire you from that profession." Crane rose, crossed to the window, and looked out at the planet turning below — Meridian's northern hemisphere in its long afternoon, cloud systems scrolling over farmland that fed four million people who had no idea their government maintained a bureau that unmade events. "Here's why you're really here, Mr. Halloran. Your original died because he found something true, and the people around him decided the truth was more dangerous than he was. I was not the person who made that decision, but I was in the room, and I did not stop it, and I have carried that for nine years with considerably less grace than you'd expect from a man with this much practice at composure." He turned back. "I am offering you the chance to finish what he started — openly, this time, with resources, with protection, with me. The Perihelion. The drive. The whole buried architecture. All of it, laid out for you, if you'll work it with me instead of around me."

Gideon didn't move. "And if I work it around you anyway?"

"Then we're having this same conversation in a year, except I'll be older, you'll be tired, and someone else will probably be dead." Crane smiled again, and this time it didn't reach anywhere at all. "I'd rather skip the sequel. The first one was excellent. Tragic structure, unforgettable ending. I'd hate to see it adapted twice."

He picked up a slim folder from the desk — paper, of course, everything in this room was paper, everything in this room was deniable — and held it out.

"This contains the access credentials, the clearance authorizations, and the location of the facility where your original hid the telemetry. Consider it a down payment on trust. Read it tonight. Sleep on it. Come back tomorrow and tell me yes." His grip closed on Gideon's forearm again, warm and firm, the pilot's grip, the brother's grip. "For what it's worth — and I know it isn't worth much coming from me — I'm glad the eyes survived. Someone should remember him accurately. Even if the remembering is wearing a different jaw."

Gideon took the folder. It was heavier than paper had any right to be.

At the door, Crane spoke once more, casually, the way men deliver the sentences they've been aiming at you all along.

"Oh — one small thing. The shoes. Gray, institutional, Office of Fleet Continuity issue." He was pouring himself more tea, not looking up. "You should know that I wear that exact pair. Have worn them for fifteen years. Half the senior staff does; it's the standard fitting." He glanced up, and his face was all warmth again, open as a lit window. "I mention it only so you understand how evidence works, Mr. Halloran. A shoe is not a man. Ask anyone who's ever been convicted by one."

The door sealed behind Gideon with a sound like a held breath released.

He stood a long moment in the amber-lit corridor, the folder in his hand, his original's heartbeat loud in his borrowed chest. Then he opened the folder right there, because he had never once in his life — in either of his lives — been able to wait until nightfall.

Inside, on the first page, above the clearance codes, was a note in Crane's handwriting. Personal. Almost tender.

*He trusted no one, and he was right to. Start where he stopped. — A.C.*

And beneath it, a coordinate set for a place Gideon had flown over a hundred times without knowing: a decommissioned relay station on Meridian's southern continent, dark for nine years.

Dark, the file noted, *since the week Halloran Prime died.*
