## 20. Two Lists

The excavation chamber smelled of ozone and old paper.

That was the strange thing about Sefa Oyelaran's archive — everything in it was digital, every record a ghost of light in a lattice of quantum storage, and yet she had insisted on filling the room with physical books, real paper, the smell of decay and ink. "Memory needs anchors," she'd said when Gideon asked. "You can't dig into a man's head in a clean room. Clean rooms are where you forget things."

So he sat in a chair that creaked, under a lamp with a tungsten filament, surrounded by ten thousand volumes no one would ever read again, while thin filaments of conductive gel cooled against his temples and Sefa watched him from behind a console older than either colony's war.

"Third pass," she said. "Tell me what you see."

Gideon closed his eyes.

The dark behind his eyelids was not empty. It was never empty anymore. Since the first pass — since Sefa had threaded her way past the clone-scrubbing protocols and found the sediment of Halloran Prime still layered inside him like fossils in shale — he had been living with the debris field of another man's mind. He saw the murder now in fragments: the office on Meridian's Spindle Ring, the rain against the window that wasn't rain but coolant condensation, the smell of burnt coffee and copper. He saw the last file Halloran Prime had opened. He saw the name at the top of it.

But tonight, on the third pass, something else came up.

"It's not one list," he said aloud, and heard his own voice go flat with shock. "Sefa. It was never one list."

"Slowly," she said. "Describe it as it comes."

"There are two documents." The images assembled themselves as he spoke, grainy and half-lit, like footage recovered from deep water. "Same format. Same encryption signature — Meridian Naval Intelligence, Directorate of Continuity Planning. But they're mirrored. One is marked *Meridian Vault Selection — Priority Wake Order*. The other is—" he paused, because saying it made it real, and real things could not be unmade— "*Cael Harbor Population Assessment. Post-Event Viability.*"

Sefa did not move for three full seconds. Then she leaned forward, and the lamplight caught the gray at her temples, and Gideon understood that she had suspected this for nine years and had hated herself for being right.

"Read me the first one," she said quietly. "The wake order."

He read.

It was not a long document. That was the horror of it — how short it was. Four hundred thousand names would have been too many to hold in a single human memory, so whoever built the list had built it as a hierarchy: categories first, then quotas, then names. *Essential technical personnel and dependents: 60,000. Genetic diversity pool, randomized within approved parameters: 90,000. Cultural continuity cohort — artists, teachers, archivists: 15,000. Remaining allocation distributed by cryostasis seniority.*

One hundred sixty-five thousand names. Out of four hundred thousand sleeping in Meridian's vaults beneath the Ashfall Shelf.

"The rest," Gideon said, and his voice cracked on it, "the rest are listed under a single heading." He made himself look at it. Made himself read it. "*Non-selected population: to remain in suspension indefinitely. Power allocation redirected to selected cohorts post-jump.*"

"Indefinitely," Sefa repeated.

"The vaults run on the same grid as the jump facility. After a single-use displacement event, there isn't enough power for everyone. They knew that going in. The drive wasn't just a weapon or an escape hatch — it was a triage machine. You get one jump. One rewind. And whatever future you buy with it, you buy for the people on your list."

He opened his eyes. The lamp hummed. Somewhere in the stacks, a cooling fan ticked like a slow clock.

"And the second document?" Sefa asked, though Gideon could see she already knew.

"Cael Harbor's version. Same structure. Same categories — they copied each other, Sefa. These two governments that won't even speak, that let a war sit between them like a corpse in a doorway — their continuity planners were using the same template. Maybe trading notes before the silence. Maybe the silence started *because* of the notes." He rubbed his eyes and the gel smeared cold across his brow. "Cael's list is smaller. A hundred forty thousand. Their vaults are deeper, their grid is older. And their non-selection heading is worse."

"Say it anyway."

"*Non-selected population: to be written out of record following Event. Historical continuity to begin from selected cohort.*" Gideon laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, jagged, nothing anyone would mistake for humor. "They weren't just planning to let them die in the dark. They were planning to erase them from the archives. Four hundred thousand people, and their own government was going to delete them so thoroughly that no one born after would ever know they'd existed. No graves. No names. Not even the grief."

* * *

Sefa poured tea from a dented thermos and neither of them drank it.

They sat across from each other at a reading table scarred by decades of pens, and between them lay the only physical object the excavation had produced: a single sheet of archival flimsi on which Sefa had transcribed, by hand, the category headers from both lists. Two columns. Mirrored structures. Different handwriting, if you counted the typography of different bureaucratic software as handwriting — and after nine years in the archives, Sefa did.

"They were racing," she said at last. "That's what nobody wanted to admit, back before the silence. Both colonies were building the same machine for the same reason — the vaults were always a stopgap. You can't keep four hundred thousand people frozen forever. The power costs alone compound past any economy's horizon. Every year of suspension is a year closer to the day you have to choose who wakes and who doesn't. The drive was supposed to make the choice unnecessary. Rewind the biological clock of an entire population, buy them centuries, and nobody has to die."

"But it only works once."

"It only works once." She turned the flimsi sheet toward him. "And the moment both sides understood that — the moment the physics became undeniable — the drive stopped being a lifeboat and became a throne. Whoever fires it gets to decide what humanity looks like afterward. Not just who lives. Who *counts*. Who is remembered. You don't win a future, Gideon. You write one."

Gideon stared at the two columns until they blurred.

"My original found these lists," he said. "Both of them. That's why he died. Not because he found the ship, not because he figured out the drive — because he had proof that both governments had already decided, years ago, which of their own people deserved to exist. He was carrying evidence that would have burned down both administrations at once. Meridian couldn't let him publish it. And Cael—" he stopped.

"And Cael couldn't let him publish it either," Sefa finished. "Which is why the assassin has never been caught. You've been looking for a killer from one side of the war. There isn't one. Or rather — there are two, and they've never met, and they used the same gun."

The words settled over the room like ash.

Gideon thought about the case file he had inherited — not read, *inherited*, the memories laid into his developing brain cell by cell while the original's body went into the ground. He had spent nine years treating that file as a mystery to be solved, a debt to be paid. A man murdered mid-investigation; a clone commissioned to finish the work. It had felt like justice deferred. It had felt like a thread leading somewhere.

Now he saw the shape of the thing whole, and the shape was obscene. He was not a detective. He was a courier. Halloran Prime had known he was going to die — had *prepared* for it, hidden the lists inside his own neural architecture where no search warrant could reach, and then arranged for a copy of himself to walk around carrying them. Living dead-drop. Evidence with a heartbeat. The governments hadn't killed the investigation when they killed the man. They'd just changed its address.

And the new address was him.

"I need to know the names," he said. His voice sounded distant to himself, like a transmission from far away. "Not the categories. The names. If I'm going to do anything with this — burn it, broadcast it, bury it — I have to know whose lives are sitting in my skull."

"You understand what you're asking." Sefa's eyes were steady and very tired. "A full extraction of the encoded manifests. Your original didn't hide them as data, Gideon. He hid them as *experience*. Names tied to faces, voices, places — the only encryption that survives a memory wipe, because it doesn't look like a file. It looks like a life. To pull them out cleanly, I have to make you relive every introduction. Every handshake. Every conversation he had with every person on both lists, in enough sensory detail that your brain will not always be able to tell whether it happened to you or to him."

"How long?"

"Days. Maybe more. And there's a cost I can't promise to avoid. People who carry this much borrowed memory start losing the borders. You'll wake up some morning not sure whether you kissed a woman named Anneliese or watched Halloran Prime kiss her. Whether your fear of deep water is yours or his. Identity is a story the brain tells to keep itself coherent, and we are about to pour a second story into the same vessel."

Gideon looked at his hands. They were his hands — he was almost sure they were his hands — broad-knuckled, a scar on the left thumb from a cargo clamp incident that had happened to *him*, on *his* route, in *his* ninth year flying the lane. Except now he also remembered a scar on the right wrist he had never earned, and a woman named Anneliese whose laugh he could hear as clearly as his own heartbeat, and a night of rain-that-was-not-rain in an office that smelled of copper.

"Anneliese is on the list," he said suddenly. The knowledge surfaced whole, unbidden, the way a drowned thing surfaces. "Meridian's list. Cultural continuity cohort. She was a composer. He interviewed her eleven days before he died, and he liked her, and he put her name in his head along with eighty-nine thousand others, and he never told her why he was asking about her family, her history, her bloodline." He pressed his palms against his eye sockets until the darkness sparked. "She's walking around Meridian right now, Sefa. Teaching. Writing music. And she's on a list in a dead man's skull that decides whether she wakes up."

"That's what the lists *are*," Sefa said softly. "That's what power does with people. It turns them into entries. Categories. Quotas. 'Genetic diversity pool, randomized within approved parameters' — do you hear it? Even the mercy is arithmetic."

* * *

They worked through the night cycle, and the night after that, and the night after that.

The excavation was nothing like he had imagined. No pain, at first — just immersion, the archive's interface dissolving the walls of the reading room and replacing them with Halloran Prime's yesterdays. Gideon sat in the creaking chair and shook hands with strangers. He sat in interview rooms and heard himself ask questions in another man's cadence. He walked the corridors of Meridian's Continuity Directorate wearing another man's security clearance, and the corridors were quiet in the way of places where terrible decisions are made by people who have made peace with them.

By the fourth day, the borders began to blur exactly as Sefa had promised.

He dreamed of Anneliese and woke grieving. He dreamed of a Cael Harbor dockworker named Tomas Reyes — no relation, he checked, no relation, though the surname snagged in him like a fishhook — and woke angry on behalf of a man he had never met. He dreamed of the launch bay of the Perihelion, twelve years ago, and woke with the taste of engine coolant in his mouth and the absolute conviction that he had seen someone important there, someone whose face slid away from him every time he reached for it, like a name on the tip of the tongue.

On the fifth day, Sefa printed the lists.

Not all of them — printing three hundred thousand names would have taken the archive's ancient fabricator a week and a forest's worth of flimsi. She printed the category headers, the quota tables, the first hundred names of each selection tier, and the final authorization lines. And she spread them across the reading table under the tungsten lamp, two documents side by side, and stepped back as if standing at a grave.

Gideon walked the length of the table slowly, reading.

The two lists were twins. That was the thing he couldn't stop seeing. Strip away the colony seals and the different fonts and they were the same document, generated from the same cold logic, refined across the same silent years. Meridian's planners had weighed genetic diversity against technical skill. So had Cael's. Meridian had allocated slots for artists and teachers, a bone thrown to the idea of a civilization worth waking up into. So had Cael's. Both had dated their final revisions within months of each other. Both had signed off through channels so deeply buried that even most of each government's own leadership likely didn't know the documents existed.

Two worlds that had severed contact rather than say each other's names had spent nine years independently writing the same death sentence.

"They're not enemies," Gideon said. His voice came out hoarse. He cleared his throat and tried again. "Look at this, Sefa. Enemies diverge. Rivals compete — they build *different* machines so they can beat each other. These lists aren't competition. They're convergence. Both colonies hit the same wall — the vaults, the power curve, the one-jump limit — and both colonies reached for the same solution, and neither one hesitated. The war, the silence, the border inspections, the propaganda murals — none of it slowed them down for a second. Because the war was never the point. The war was *cover*. As long as everyone was watching the frontier, nobody was watching the planners."

"Containment," Sefa agreed. "We talked about this. The silence keeps the populations from comparing notes. From learning what the other side knows. Because if a Meridian dockworker ever learned that Cael Harbor's archivists held the same sealed files, held the same lists, the secret stops being containable. Two governments keeping the identical crime is a conspiracy. One population discovering it is a revolution."

Gideon picked up the Meridian sheet. The flimsi was cool and slightly damp from the fabricator, and it trembled in his hand, or perhaps his hand trembled and the flimsi simply told the truth about it.

Eighty-nine thousand nine hundred and twelve names. He carried them now, all of them, filed in the sediment of a dead man's devotion. Anneliese Voss-adjacent in the cultural cohort — no, not adjacent, alphabetically near, that was all, that meant nothing, except that his mind kept drawing lines between names, kept building families out of strangers, because that was what the lists refused to do and someone had to.

He set the sheet down and picked up Cael's.

One hundred forty thousand and six. And here, in the technical personnel tier, third column, eleventh row, a name he recognized from a service record he'd pulled during the Perihelion investigation: *Reyes, T., atmospheric systems tech, Cael colonial fleet, seconded to joint drive program, 12 years prior.* A man who had helped build the machine that rewound time, deemed essential enough to save, and then — Gideon checked the cross-reference his original had buried alongside the name — reassigned, disappeared from records, status unknown. Saved on paper. Erased in practice. Even the chosen were not safe from the choosers.

"This is what my original died holding," Gideon said. "Not a scandal. A mirror. Proof that if either colony fired that drive tomorrow, the difference between them wouldn't matter. Meridian saves its hundred sixty-five thousand and lets the rest go dark, and Cael does the same, and afterward there are two little kingdoms of the chosen floating in the dark, each one certain it's the last best hope of humanity, each one built on a foundation of people who were never given a vote." He looked up at Sefa. "There's no good side in these pages. There's just power, and the arithmetic power makes when nobody's watching."

"No," Sefa said. "There's something else in these pages. Look again."

She touched the bottom of the Cael document, where the authorization signatures ran in a vertical stack. Most were cipher-keys, anonymous designations. But the final line — the countersignature required to activate the plan, the name that turned intention into order — was a plain-text rank and a partial identifier, redacted everywhere except the archive copy Halloran Prime had stolen:

*Deputy Director, Continuity Planning — [REDACTED] — authorized to execute upon window confirmation.*

The same title appeared on the Meridian list. The same redaction pattern. The same blank space where a face should be.

"One office," Gideon breathed. "Both colonies have a Deputy Director of Continuity Planning. Same program, same structure, same— " He stopped. Something cold moved through him, slow and total, like deep water closing over the head. "My original interviewed people in that office. Eleven days before he died. He was pulling threads in the Continuity Directorate, and the lists were the loom, and whoever holds that deputy directorship on *either* world had every reason to—"

The archive door chimed.

Sefa went still. In nine years, Gideon had learned, that door had chimed fewer than twenty times, and never without warning, never without an appointment coded days in advance. She crossed the room and keyed the internal display, and Gideon watched her face do something he had never seen it do: go white.

"What is it?"

"Priority transmission. Meridian diplomatic pouch, routed through Cael customs under a cultural exchange flag." Her voice was carefully level, the voice of a woman who had survived purges by sounding boring. "It's addressed to *the visiting investigator*. By name. Our name — the false identity you entered on." She looked back at him over her shoulder. "Gideon. Nobody knows that name. I built that identity myself, from scratch, nine years ago, and I have never transmitted it anywhere."

The chime sounded again, patient, polite, obscene in its courtesy.

Gideon stood among the two lists — three hundred thousand names spread across a scarred table under a dying lamp — and understood that the window of being unseen had just closed. Somewhere, a man in a well-tailored uniform was reading a report about a freight pilot who diverted to a lost ship, and smiling, and reaching for a comm channel.

"Answer it," he said.

Sefa's finger hovered over the key. "Whatever this is," she said, "it means they've known where you were longer than you've known yourself."

The screen lit.
