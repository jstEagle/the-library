## 24. Betrayal at the Archive

The knock came at 04:12, station time, and it was too polite.

Gideon had been awake anyway. He'd spent the last six hours in a rented sleep-cubicle above the archive district, cross-referencing Sefa's fragments against the list still buried in his skull, and he'd learned to read Cael Harbor's rhythms the way you learn a sleeping partner's breathing — the freight elevators groaning at midnight, the water recyclers cycling at three, the particular silence of a colony that pretended it wasn't listening to itself.

The knock was two soft raps. Then a voice, warm and bureaucratic: "Archivist Oyelaran? Records audit. We apologize for the hour."

Sefa was three floors down in the deep stacks, working. Gideon was supposed to be gone by now — that had been the plan, the plan they'd made together over cold tea and stolen bandwidth: extract what they could, copy everything twice, and Gideon would be on a shuttle to the dark lanes before Cael's morning shift change. But the second copy was still compiling. The drive's displacement mathematics didn't compress well; Sefa had warned him the full dataset was the size of a small city's municipal records.

Two soft raps again. Then, without waiting: the sound of the door seal cycling.

Gideon killed the cubicle's lights and moved to the wall panel, thumb finding the maintenance access Sefa had shown him on his first night — *every building in Cael Harbor is older than its government*, she'd said, laughing, *so every building has more doors than the government knows about*. The panel slid open onto a crawl space that smelled of coolant and forty years of dust.

Through the gap between panel and frame, he saw them enter: four figures in the grey of Cael Internal Security, moving with the unhurried confidence of people who already owned the room. Not raiders. Auditors. That was worse.

"Where is the archivist?" one asked the empty cubicle, though the question wasn't really a question. Another was already at the terminal, and Gideon watched the man's hands move across the interface with terrible fluency — not searching for the research, but *sorting* it. He knew what he was looking at. He knew which files mattered.

They'd known all along. Gideon felt the understanding arrive whole, the way a hull breach announces itself as cold before it announces itself as sound. Cael Harbor hadn't granted him asylum out of sentiment or schadenfreude. Their intelligence had wanted the drive — Voss had said so, Juno had said it more bluntly — and Gideon had told himself he understood the bargain. Sanctuary for access. A trade between wary parties.

But a trade requires both sides to want different things. Cael had wanted exactly what Meridian wanted. There had never been a bargain. There had been a leash, and he'd been walking at the end of it, grateful for the collar because it was warm.

He crawled.

* * *

The crawl space ran the length of the archive annex like an artery, and Gideon followed it toward the deep stacks, elbows and knees on grating, the list in his head pulsing with every heartbeat — four hundred thousand names on each side, and now his own name added to the ledger of the betrayed. At the junction above Stack Nine he found a vent grate and looked down into the reading room where he and Sefa had spent eleven nights rebuilding the truth from fragments.

She was there. They had her seated at the long table, and she was — this was the thing he would remember later, the thing that would keep him up in derelicts and dark lanes for months afterward — she was *calm*. Hands flat on the table. Spine straight. Watching the security officer across from her with the patient attention of a woman who had spent her career waiting for documents to finish copying.

"You understand," Sefa was saying, "that I'm a civil servant. I process requests. If there's been an irregularity in my access logs—"

"Dr. Oyelaran." The officer's voice was gentle, almost kind. "We know who you are. We've known since the Perihelion launched. Do you imagine we lost track of the only person who ever walked off that ship before it jumped?"

Gideon went cold against the grating.

"The manifest," he breathed, too quiet for anyone to hear. The impossible crew manifest the Perihelion's AI had logged — thirty-one names, thirty-one living crew. And apparently a thirty-second name that had never appeared anywhere, an archivist who shouldn't exist, who had recognized him not as Halloran Prime but as someone she'd met before launch. Sefa had been aboard. Sefa had *left* before the jump. Which meant someone had let her leave, and whoever that was had spent nine years keeping her close enough to watch and far enough away to be deniable.

"We're not going to hurt you," the officer continued. "You've done valuable work. More than you know. The Directorate has been trying to reconstruct Oyelaran's original displacement models for a decade — your fragments just saved us nine years of failure." He slid a slate across the table. "All we need is confirmation that the secondary dataset is complete. The one your guest has been compiling."

Sefa's eyes flicked upward — once, briefly, toward the vent — and then back to the slate, and in that flicker Gideon saw her do something extraordinary: she decided, in real time, to spend herself. Her face went carefully blank, the blankness of a woman buying seconds with the only currency she had left.

"It's incomplete," she said. "The displacement harmonics require the launch-day calibration constants. Those were stored aboard the Perihelion. Your copy is useless without them."

It was a lie. Gideon knew it was a lie because he was carrying the calibration constants in his own hippocampus, tucked beside the names, another gift from Halloran Prime's final desperate months. But it was a beautiful lie, a structural lie, a lie with load-bearing walls — because it meant Cael couldn't act on their stolen data until they took the Perihelion itself, and taking the Perihelion meant fleets, and fleets meant time.

The officer studied her for a long moment. Then he smiled, and it was Crane's smile, the same smile worn by a different empire, and Gideon understood that this was simply what power looked like everywhere: patient, warm, and already holding the answer.

"Thank you, Doctor," the officer said. "You'll be comfortable in protective custody. We have a great deal to discuss about launch day."

They stood her up. They walked her out. She did not look at the vent again, and Gideon pressed his forehead against the cold grating and grieved her in advance, because he already knew — the way you know things in this war between colonies that wouldn't even name themselves — that he might never be able to come back for her.

Then he did the harder thing. He crawled away.

* * *

Juno Reyes was where he'd told her to be, which was nowhere near anything important: a public washroom in the transit concourse, third stall, running the water so the acoustic dampers would blur her voiceprint. She was seventeen years old and aging backward, and when Gideon pushed into the stall she looked up at him with those too-old eyes and said, "You have the face."

"What face?"

"The face Voss gets. When the ship changes under her feet." Juno shut off the water. "Sefa's taken, isn't she. I heard the concourse alerts. Internal Security doesn't run audits at 4 a.m. unless somebody's already lost."

"They have her. They have most of the data." Gideon crouched to her level, which put him eye to eye with a girl who had been a decorated pilot before the Perihelion jumped and was now, biologically, younger than the license she no longer legally possessed. "How much of it is useless?"

"None of it, if they get the ship." Juno's jaw tightened. "But the copy — yours, the partial compile — how far did it get?"

"Sixty percent. Maybe seventy. Enough of the drive architecture to understand it. Not enough to fly it."

"Enough to know the split-jump math exists?"

Gideon paused. They hadn't told Juno about the split. It lived in the sixty percent like a splinter — a theoretical solution Sefa had flagged with a single annotation: *displacement can be divided across sequential jumps; cost scales nonlinearly; see crew survival projections.* See crew survival projections. Four words doing the work of a graveyard.

"Why?" Juno asked, watching his face. "What's in the sixty percent?"

"Hope," Gideon said. "With a price tag."

"Everything worth stealing has one." She stood, suddenly all business, seventeen years old and nine years a pilot, and pulled her jacket on. "So here's what I know, since nobody tells me anything and I listen anyway. Cael's impound authority has a priority shuttle leaving for the Perihelion's holding orbit at 06:00 — joint custody transfer, Meridian liaison aboard, big show of cooperation. Which means the docks are locked down except for one thing."

"Which is?"

"Mail." Juno grinned, and for a second she was actually a kid. "You told me yourself. Nobody guards the mail. Two colonies at each other's throats for nine years and they still honor the courier seal, because if they don't, the whole pretense falls apart and everyone has to admit there's a war. Get us on a mail skiff and we're invisible."

"I'm not a courier anymore. My credentials died with my asylum."

"No." Juno reached into her jacket and produced a chit — physical, old-fashioned, stamped with the orbital post authority's ring. "I've been carrying letters for you this whole time, Halloran. Every stop we made, I watched how you did it. The seal isn't checked against a person. It's checked against a route." She flipped the chit. "Route 7. The long lane. Yours."

He stared at her. "You stole my mail seal?"

"I *preserved* it. There's a difference, and it matters legally, probably, somewhere." She pressed it into his hand. "Voss is still in quarantine medical, sublevel two. We go now, we take the mail skiff, we collect the captain, and we're in the dark before Cael realizes their auditors audited the wrong silence."

"And then where? We can't fly the Perihelion. We can't fix the drive. We're three people with a partial file and a stolen seal."

Juno's grin faded into something steadier. "Then we find the one place nobody will look for us. Somewhere with no value, no vaults, no archives. Somewhere even two governments agree isn't worth a bullet." She shrugged. "You're the one who knows the dead routes. Take us somewhere dead."

* * *

Sublevel two smelled of antiseptic and recycled air, and getting into it required Gideon to do the thing he hated most: walk through a checkpoint wearing a borrowed face. Not a disguise, exactly — Cael's surveillance had catalogued him thoroughly during his weeks of asylum, but the lockdown had pulled every camera's attention toward the archive district and the impound yards, and the checkpoint guard was nineteen and exhausted and much more interested in the priority-transfer traffic stacking up overhead than in a tired man in a maintenance coverall escorting a juvenile through medical.

"Family visitation closes at five," the guard said.

"He's my uncle," Juno said, with the flawless confidence of a born liar, and squeezed Gideon's arm with genuine affection, which was either brilliant tradecraft or the truest thing anyone had done for him in weeks.

Quarantine medical was a ward of glass-walled rooms, and in the fourth one, Captain Ilsa Voss sat upright on a narrow bed, reading a paper book — an actual paper book, contraband in all but name — and when she saw Gideon through the glass she closed it on her finger to hold her place and mouthed one word:

*Finally.*

Getting her out took eleven minutes and one small fire. Juno started it — a thermite pen against a supply closet's electrical conduit, nothing dangerous, lots of smoke — and in the evacuation shuffle Gideon walked Voss out through the service corridor wearing a spare medic's coat, the captain moving with the brisk unremarkability of a woman who had spent decades being ignored in corridors. Her rewound body was maybe thirty now; her eyes were fifty-five and had seen a murder.

In the service stairwell, breathing smoke-tinged air, Voss stopped him with a hand on his chest.

"Sefa?"

"Taken. Protective custody." He couldn't soften it. She deserved better than softening. "They knew about her from the beginning, Ilsa. Before the launch. Someone let her off the ship, and someone's been holding her leash for nine years."

Voss absorbed this the way she absorbed everything: completely, and without visible movement, like ballast settling. "Then she's alive because she's useful. Useful people can be reached." She started down the stairs. "And the data?"

"Partial. Sixty percent. Enough to know the shape of the answer, not enough to build it."

"Then we steal the rest from the people who stole it first." She glanced back at him, and there it was — the ghost of the captain who had interviewed Halloran Prime nine years ago, the woman whose testimony existed only in a dead man's memory and a clone's inherited grief. "Halloran. Whatever you're blaming yourself for right now, put it down. You can carry it again after we're clear."

* * *

The mail skiff *Long Answer* sat on Pad 41 like something forgotten on purpose — a stubby, scarred, utterly unglamorous hauler with the orbital post authority's faded ring on her flank and a cargo bay full of sealed letter-tubes bound for relay buoys nobody serviced anymore. Juno had her hatch open and her pre-flight half done before Gideon and Voss cleared the fence line, and she waved them aboard with the urgency of a woman who had already talked her way past two dockhands and did not intend to spend the credit.

"Cleared for departure under Route 7," she reported, sliding into the pilot's seat, hands finding the controls like they'd grown there. "Nobody even looked at the seal. You were right, Captain. The mail is sacred."

"The mail is *ignored*," Voss corrected, strapping into the second seat. "There's a difference, and it's the only reason we're alive."

Gideon took the third seat — the observer's chair, the chair where Halloran Prime had once sat on a different ship in a different life, and the memory arrived uninvited, as it always did: his original's hands on the same style of harness, the same view of a docking bay receding, the same feeling of leaving a place that had decided, quietly, to become a trap.

Cael Harbor fell away beneath them, its propaganda murals shrinking into geometry, its cryosleep vaults — four hundred thousand sleepers, a future the colony couldn't afford — hidden somewhere under the ice like a heart under ribs. Behind them, in the archive district, Sefa Oyelaran was teaching her captors everything she knew, buying time with truth the way she'd bought it with lies, and Gideon marked her location in his mind the way sailors mark reefs: not because he could avoid the loss, but because he refused to pretend it hadn't happened.

They cleared the traffic shell. Juno threaded the departure lanes with a precision that made Voss raise her eyebrows — the eyebrows of a captain recognizing real talent — and then the skiff dropped into the dark lanes, the unpatrolled spaces between the colonies where the stars didn't care who governed what, and the comm went quiet, and for six minutes nobody spoke.

It was Voss who broke it.

"They'll come after us," she said. "Not hard, not yet. They'll let us run, because running confirms we have something worth chasing, and because they want to see where we go. Both of them. Meridian will pick up the trail within a day — Crane will make sure of it. Cael will follow at a distance and wait for us to lead them to whatever we think we've found." She turned in her seat. "So the question, gentlemen and pilot, is: where do we go that's worth less than the chase?"

"Nowhere," Gideon said.

Juno laughed. "Bold strategy."

"I mean it. There is nowhere. Everywhere valuable is theirs, and everywhere worthless can't help us. We have sixty percent of an answer, a stolen mail seal, and—" he touched his temple "—a list neither government knows I've finished excavating. That's the entire inventory." He looked out the viewport at the debris fields ahead, the slow-turning wreckage of a war neither colony would name, hulls and hulks drifting in orbits that had outlived everyone who'd fired the shots. "Except there's one thing they don't have. One thing nobody has."

"The other forty percent," Juno said.

"The Perihelion," Voss said, at the same moment, and the two answers met in the air between them.

"My original hid the calibration constants in my head alongside the names," Gideon said slowly, following the thought as it built itself. "Sefa lied to buy time, but the lie was true in one direction — Cael's copy *is* incomplete without launch-day data. And the launch-day data is aboard the ship. In the drive's own memory. In the AI's logs." He turned to look at them. "We don't need to steal the research back. We need to steal the ship."

"That's the plan you're proposing?" Juno's voice cracked upward, seventeen again despite everything. "Steal a captured experimental vessel from a joint colonial impound yard, guarded by two governments who both want it badly enough to burn an archivist for it?"

"Yes."

"With what crew? Three people?"

"One of whom flew her for six years," Voss said quietly. "One of whom is the best young pilot I've ever seen. And one of whom," she nodded at Gideon, "has been carrying this ship's secrets in his skull since before he knew they existed. That's not nothing, Reyes. That's the exact crew the job wants."

Gideon looked at the debris field turning slowly outside the glass — the graveyards of the unnamed war, the silent testament to what two colonies did when they each decided they alone should choose the future. Somewhere behind them, Sefa was in a cell. Somewhere ahead, the window was closing — nineteen days, then eighteen, then fewer — and two fleets were already mobilizing toward a ship that could wake one world or erase the other.

And in his head, calm as a filed document, the list waited: eight hundred thousand names, and the principle his original had died holding, the one thing he'd passed on intact.

*No list written by power survives contact with the people on it.*

"Plot us a course," Gideon said. "Somewhere dead. We're going to need time to figure out how to take her back — and I think I know where Halloran Prime left us a head start."

Juno's hands were already moving. "Where?"

"The last place he ever sent a message." Gideon met her eyes in the reflection off the canopy. "The Perihelion's original launch site. The one place both governments pretend doesn't exist, because admitting it exists means admitting what launched from it."

The *Long Answer* banked into the debris field, a mail skiff carrying no mail, three fugitives carrying everything, falling toward the dark.
