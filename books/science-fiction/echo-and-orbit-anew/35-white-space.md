## 35. White Space

There was no sound in the jump, and that was the first mercy of it.

Gideon had expected noise — the scream of the drive, the groan of a hull being unmade, something to match the violence of what they'd done. Instead the moment the window closed around them, everything went quiet the way a held breath is quiet. The Perihelion's bridge dissolved into white. Not darkness — white, total and soft, like standing inside the memory of snow.

He was alone.

He knew he wasn't — Voss had been at the interface, Juno strapped into the auxiliary seat, thirty-one crew members scattered through the ship with their hands folded and their eyes closed, volunteering their years — but the white space had no room for any of them. It gave him only himself, and then, gently, it began to give him more.

* * *

The first fragment came without warning, the way a smell can arrive before its source.

He was standing on a plain of frost-furred concrete under a sky the color of old tin, and the ground beneath his boots was humming. He knew that hum. Every child raised on Meridian knew it — the vibration of cryopods cycling through their ten-thousand-year maintenance rhythm, four hundred thousand hearts beating at one beat per century.

The vault doors were opening.

Not all at once. In rows, in waves, like a field of flowers deciding together that it was spring. Gideon walked down the central aisle — or the vision carried him; there was no difference here — and watched the pods crack their seals one by one. Frost sublimated off the lids in long silver banners. And inside each pod, a face.

He had carried those faces for weeks now, ever since the memory excavation had torn the lists out of the hidden rooms of Halloran Prime's mind. But the lists had been names. This was worse. This was names with eyelashes. Names with chapped lips and sleep-creases on their cheeks. A woman whose pod read *Oyelaran, T.* — Sefa's mother, though Sefa had never said so, though Gideon had known it anyway from the way she'd flinched at the surname. A boy of maybe nine standard years, curled on his side the way children curl when they expect to be woken gently, and who was.

The boy opened his eyes. Looked directly at Gideon. And smiled.

*You came,* the smile said. Not *you saved us.* Just *you came.* As if Gideon were someone expected. As if this had been an appointment kept rather than an arithmetic survived.

Gideon tried to speak and found he had no mouth, or no need of one. The vault ceiling rolled back above them — a kilometer of engineered stone parting like a curtain — and the tin-colored sky poured in, and it wasn't tin at all anymore. It was dawn. Real dawn, Meridian's slow amber dawn, the kind that took forty minutes to finish because the colony's sun rose at the pace of patience.

Four hundred thousand people stood up inside the same hour. Gideon felt it as pressure, as weather, as a change in the gravity of the world. They came out of the vault blinking and holding each other's sleeves, and nobody told them what had happened, and nobody had to. They could read it in the empty streets, in the abandoned checkpoints, in the propaganda murals already whitewashed to bare gray by hands they'd never identify. Their governments had planned to choose which of them would wake. The choice had been taken away from every list ever written, and given instead to a ship full of people who had agreed to be children again so that strangers could be born into morning.

The woman Oyelaran found the boy with the sleep-creased cheek and lifted him onto her hip, and he weighed nothing, and she wept without sound, and Gideon understood suddenly that the silence wasn't absence. It was reverence. The whole waking vault was quiet the way churches are quiet.

He wanted to stay. The white space let him want it for a long time — longer than any real moment should have lasted — and then it took even that away, kindly, the way you take a blanket off a sleeping child.

* * *

The second fragment was Cael Harbor, and it broke him differently.

He came awake inside it already moving, already running down a corridor he recognized from the archives — the deep levels, the sealed naval stacks where Sefa Oyelaran had waited nine years for a man who didn't yet exist to come looking. Except the corridor was different now. The blast doors stood open. The warning placards about unauthorized access had been pried off the walls, leaving pale rectangles of unfaded paint, little ghosts of secrecy.

And the light coming down the stairwells was wrong for Cael Harbor. Too warm. Too gold. Cael's sun was a hard white star that made everything look interrogated. This light was amber. Meridian's light.

Both skies at once. That was the thing the white space did, the impossible arithmetic it performed without apology: in these fragments, both colonies saw the same sky. Not the same *kind* of sky — the same one. As if the war's last casualty had been the horizon line itself, and the jump had healed it.

The Cael vaults were deeper than Meridian's, older, carved into basalt before the war, and the pods down there were squat and utilitarian, stamped with serial numbers instead of names. Four hundred thousand colonists who had gone under during the rationing crises, promised a future the colony couldn't afford to give them all — promised, in fact, that most of them would never be asked to wake at all. Gideon walked the aisles and the pods opened and the people came out rubbing their arms, disoriented, asking questions in Cael dialect, and the answers came back to them not from officials but from other wakers, from the crowd itself, a rumor ripening into truth:

*The drive fired. Both vaults. Nobody chose. Everybody woke.*

An old man — old before suspension, one of the first-generation volunteers — stood in the center of the floor with his fists on his hips and laughed until he had to sit down on his own open pod. Around him, people were doing the mathematics out loud. *If everyone woke, then no one decided. If no one decided—* and they couldn't finish the sentence, because finishing it meant admitting that the thing they'd been told was impossible had turned out merely to be unpaid for.

Gideon moved among them unseen, or seen and unremarked, or perhaps remembered — he couldn't tell which, and the white space offered no ruling. At the far end of the vault, a wall screen still flickered with the last propaganda feed the government had pushed before its channels went dark: a mural-animation of two worlds turning away from each other, *STRENGTH THROUGH SEPARATION* scrolling beneath. Someone had thrown a boot at it. The screen showed the two worlds turning toward each other now, stuttering on a loop, half-broken, half-right.

A girl tugged his sleeve. She had Cael Harbor's flat vowels and Meridian's amber light on her face, and that combination should have been impossible, and here it simply was.

"Did you know them?" she asked. "The ones who paid?"

"I knew some of them," Gideon said, and his voice worked here, and it cracked here.

"Were they scared?"

"Yes."

"Did they do it anyway?"

"They voted," he said. "That's the whole story. They voted."

She nodded with the terrible seriousness of children processing debt, and ran back into the crowd, and the crowd closed over her, and the vault filled with the sound of four hundred thousand people learning each other's names — because that was the first thing they did, Gideon realized. Before food, before shelter, before the obvious logistics of a world that had just doubled its population overnight, they introduced themselves. To strangers. To the other side's strangers. Names, handshakes, the small liturgy of *I exist, you exist, we appear to be in this together.*

No list written by power survives contact with the people on it. His original had recorded the principle in the deepest layer of himself, and here it was, made flesh and breakfast queues and introductions. Power had written two lists. The people on them had torn both up by the simple act of existing simultaneously.

* * *

*

Between the fragments there was nothing, and the nothing was not empty.

Gideon floated in it and understood — the way you understand things in dreams, instantly and without evidence — that the white space was not a place. It was the interval between two readings of the same event. The drive hadn't thrown them anywhere. It had displaced them along an axis the universe keeps private, and what he was experiencing was the ship's passage across the seam, and the fragments were not visions. They were drafts. Possible mornings, being weighed against each other by something that had no word for justice but kept reaching for it anyway.

Or they were projections — the drive interfacing with his rewired memory, showing him the futures his own mind needed to see in order to survive the crossing. He genuinely could not tell. He suspected no one could. He suspected that Voss, somewhere in the white beside him, was seeing her own fragments: the Perihelion's first launch, maybe, or her crew young and loud in the mess hall, or the face she'd seen in the launch bay — Crane's face — finally, permanently, made small.

It doesn't matter, he told the white space. Whether it's real or projected, it's true.

And the white space, if it heard him, answered with a third fragment.

* * *

This one was smaller. Domestic. Almost nothing.

A kitchen. Meridian construction-standard, prefab walls, a window with amber light in it. Two mugs on a table. One of them steaming.

Juno Reyes sat at the table, and she was — Gideon's chest seized — she was *older*. Twenty-something, twenty-five maybe, hair cut short and practical, a pilot's calluses on her hands, the calluses she'd been aging backward away from. She was drawing. On actual paper, with an actual pencil, tongue between her teeth in concentration. She was drawing a freighter — a mail-and-freight runabout, boxy and beloved — and she was drawing it well, because of course she was; she'd flown one through debris fields at seventeen going on twelve.

Across the table, a man sat reading case files. Gideon couldn't see the man's face. He understood, with a calm that frightened him more than any horror could have, that the man was him. Some version of him. A version that existed after, in a world where after was permitted.

Juno finished the drawing. Tore it carefully along the perforation. Slid it across the table.

"For your cabin," she said. "So the route knows you're coming back."

The other Gideon took it and pinned it to the wall beside the case file shelf, and the drawing hung there — a child's rendering of a ship, suns drawn as circles with rays, two suns, one amber and one white, sharing the same paper sky —

and the fragment ended.

* * *

Gideon surfaced into the white space gasping, though there was no air to gasp and no lungs demanding it. He knelt — or existed in the posture of kneeling — in the featureless light, and he let himself feel all of it at once, because there was nowhere to hide it and no reason left to try.

Grief first, because it deserved to go first. Thirty-one people were paying for those mornings with the only currency they had. Voss would wake a young woman wearing an old captain's eyes, and everyone she'd commanded would be children, and the navy that gave her her rank would be a museum piece. Juno had insisted loudest at the vote — *I've got the most years, I can afford it* — as if years were a budget and childhood a rounding error. Gideon had refused to decide for them. He clung to that refusal now like a handrail. Whatever the white space was showing him, whatever futures it was letting him preview, none of it had been purchased with his authority. It had been purchased with theirs, freely, by ballot, in the last democratic act either colony had seen in a decade.

Then wonder, because grief alone couldn't hold the weight.

Both vaults. Both skies. The fragments might be lies, might be anesthesia, might be the drive's cold machinery dressing the crossing in comforting pictures — but they were also *possible*. That was the revelation underneath the revelation. For nine years the secret had been maintained on a single premise: that the drive could only serve one list at a time, that scarcity was written into its physics, that someone must choose. Crane had built a whole philosophy on that premise, had murdered for it, had built *Gideon* for it. And the premise was wrong. Or it had been wrong all along and only the crew of the Perihelion, voting in a dim mess hall with the fleets converging, had been brave enough to test it.

Neutrality isn't genocide by delay, Gideon thought at the white space, at Crane, at whoever was listening. Neutrality is refusing to let anyone else write the list either. It just costs more than tyrants are willing to pay.

The crew was willing.

* * *

Time in the white space was a rumor. He had no clock, no chronometer, no pulse to count. He may have lived a year in those fragments or a fraction of a second. But near the end — he felt it as nearness, the way you feel a shore before you see it — the fragments began to thin, and the white began, very slowly, to acquire texture.

First a hum. The familiar maintenance-rhythm hum, except it wasn't a vault this time — it was the Perihelion's own hull, singing at the frequency of a ship re-entering the universe's agreement about time. Then a warmth against his left side, directional, sourceful: a star. Then weight, gentle as a hand settling on his shoulder, dragging him back down into the ordinary tyranny of mass.

And threaded through all of it, faint and growing, a sound he knew better than his own name: the tick and shuffle of a mail-and-freight runner's systems coming online. Fans. Pumps. The little domestic heartbeat of a working ship.

The white space thinned to gauze. Through it, the last fragment flickered — not a future this time, but a present arriving: the bridge resolving around him, Voss at the interface, and Gideon's breath caught, because Voss was *young*. Round-faced, bright-eyed, early twenties, a stranger wearing a captain's expression like a coat cut for someone bigger. Her hands still rested on the drive interface, steady, sure, and when she turned to check on him her eyes were Ilsa Voss's eyes entire — thirty years of command compressed into a face that had never commanded anything.

"Gideon." Her voice was younger too, lighter, and it cost her something to make it carry the old authority. "Emergence in sixty seconds. Are you—"

"I'm here," he said.

Behind her, strapped into the auxiliary seat, Juno stirred. Smaller now. Eight years old, maybe nine, swimming in her harness, blinking at the bridge with a child's enormous attention. She looked at Gideon, and Gideon braced himself for terror, for the scream he'd have screamed at that age upon waking small in a strange century.

Instead she smiled the exact smile from the vault fragment — *you came* — and said, "Did it work? Did both of them wake?"

"We're about to find out," Voss said.

The forward display cleared. Stars steadied from streaks to points. And there, hanging in the black like two lanterns set out on the same windowsill, were Meridian and Cael Harbor — close, closer than they'd been in twelve years of silence, both of them ringed by the wreckage of fleets that had arrived too late to stop anything, both of them dark at the government frequencies and *blazing* at the civilian ones, thousands of overlapping transmissions, panic and jubilation indistinguishable at this distance, the electromagnetic sound of four hundred thousand people per side waking up and wanting, immediately and desperately, to talk.

Voss leaned toward the display. The young face, the old eyes.

"Open a channel," she said. "All of them. Every band."

The comm board lit up like the vault doors had — in rows, in waves, like a field of flowers deciding together that it was spring — and the first voice to come through the static was asking, over and over, in two dialects at once:

*"Is anyone out there? Is anyone else awake? Please — is anyone out there?"*

Gideon Halloran, clone, dead-drop, storage solution, the man who had carried a murder and two lists and a principle through nine years of inherited grief, reached out and keyed the transmit.

"Yeah," he said. "We're here. We're all here."
