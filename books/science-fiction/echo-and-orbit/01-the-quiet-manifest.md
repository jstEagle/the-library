## 1. The Quiet Manifest

The colony had been dead for eleven years, officially, and Xanthe Okonkwo had spent nine of them listening to it breathe.

They sat in the navigation bay of the *Echo* with the lights dimmed to their usual amber, watching Meridian turn beneath them like a coin someone had dropped into a well. The station's hull caught the light of the system's K-class star and threw it back in long, slow glints—once every forty seconds, a flash, like something down there still trying to signal. Xanthe knew better. It was just geometry. It was always just geometry.

But they logged it anyway.

*Day 3,287. Station rotation nominal. Solar reflection event at 0412 station time. No emissions. No thermal anomalies above background. Population status: unknown.*

That last word was the whole archive, really. *Unknown.* Not deceased, no matter what the corporate filings said. Not lost with all hands, no matter what the memorial plaques on Ceres Station said. Unknown, because Xanthe had been there when the transmissions stopped—not stopped, that was the thing, not stopped but *cut*, mid-sentence, mid-word—and because they had never once believed that five hundred sleeping bodies in cryo-trays two kilometers below the observation ring had simply evaporated.

Xanthe typed the entry with two fingers, the way they did everything, and then did what they did at the end of every log: opened the hidden partition.

It lived inside the nav system's dead sector, a slice of memory the manufacturer had walled off after a recall nobody bothered to complete. Nine years ago Xanthe had found it the way you find anything worth keeping—by accident, by stubbornness, by refusing to accept that a machine was finished just because a company said so. Now it held four hundred and eleven hours of audio. The last transmissions from Meridian Colony, pulled raw off the relay bands before the corporation scrubbed them from every public buffer in the Reach.

Five hundred voices, give or take. Most of them were automated: station telemetry, cryo-bay status pings, the soft administrative hum of a place going about its business while its owners decided its business was over. But some of them were people. A night-shift tech named Okafor who sang to the incubators. A woman whose name Xanthe had never learned, reading her daughter bedtime stories over the family band even though the daughter was asleep in tray 114 and would not hear them for another decade or ever. And on the final night—the cut-off night—a man saying, very calmly, *"They're telling us the transfer is routine. If anyone receives this, we did not agree to—"*

And then nothing. Eleven years of nothing, except for Xanthe, alone in the dark above it, writing *unknown* three hundred times a year.

"You're doing the thing again."

Sefa stood in the hatchway, grease to the elbows, hair tied up with what appeared to be a zip tie. She had the particular walk of an engineer who has already fixed three things today and is looking for a fourth.

"I'm navigating," Xanthe said.

"You're communing." Sefa dropped into the second chair, uninvited, which was how family worked aboard the *Echo*. "We're holding a parking orbit around a graveyard, and you're having a conversation with it."

"Somebody should talk to them."

Sefa went quiet at that, which she almost never did. She looked out at the glinting station for a moment, jaw working. "Idris wants to move on. Next contract's at Thessaly Gate, and we're burning margin sitting here."

"We stay through the anniversary."

"I know." She said it like a complaint and meant it like a promise. "Nine years. You'd think I'd stop asking."

"You could stop asking."

"Where's the fun in that?" She stood, squeezed Xanthe's shoulder hard enough to mean it, and left. The bay settled back into its amber hush.

Xanthe waited until her footsteps faded, then touched the hidden partition one more time—not to add anything, just to feel it there. Four hundred and eleven hours of proof that Meridian had been murdered politely, with paperwork. Proof that somebody, someday, might need.

They told themselves, as they told themselves every year, that keeping it wasn't dangerous. That a dead sector in a dead ship's nav core was the safest grave in the Reach. They almost believed it.

The audit notice arrived eleven days later, and it arrived the way death arrives in the modern age: as a form.

Xanthe found it buried in the *Echo*'s message queue at 0300, unable to sleep, running their nightly passive sweep of Meridian's bands out of habit rather than hope. The queue flagged it green—routine, automated, harmless—but Xanthe had spent nine years learning to read what corporations said underneath what they said, and the subject line made their hands go cold before they'd finished parsing it.

**MERIDIAN ASSET REVIEW — VESSEL DATA REQUEST — RESPONSE REQUIRED WITHIN 72 HOURS**

Vantage Consolidated Recovery Group. The subsidiary that had owned Meridian Colony outright, that had filed the loss report, that had declared five hundred sleepers unrecoverable and written off the largest cryogenic asset in colonial history as a tax event. Xanthe had read those filings so many times they could recite the language in their sleep: *total loss of asset value; no viable recovery pathway; survivors: none.*

The body of the notice was worse than the subject line.

*In accordance with ongoing asset reconciliation, all vessels within the Meridian survey volume are required to submit complete registry manifests, flight history, and cargo declarations for audit compliance. Non-response will be interpreted as salvage claim and handled accordingly.*

Standard boilerplate. Sweep-the-volume language. Except for the third paragraph, where some clerk's query had survived the template:

*Note: Registry cross-reference indicates active transponder registration for vessel ECHO (hull class: long-range salvage freighter) listing crew member X. OKONKWO. No flight history exists for this vessel prior to cycle 4,118. Please confirm vessel provenance and crew manifest accuracy.*

Xanthe read it four times. Then a fifth, standing up, pacing the six meters of the navigation bay, reading it again.

No flight history. Of course there was no flight history. The *Echo* had never flown, not on any ledger anywhere. Idris had bought her as scrap—a burned-out freighter with a falsified hull number and a past nobody would admit to—and rebuilt her in a blackyard orbiting a moon nobody visited. Officially, the *Echo* was a ghost. Unregistered routes, laundered contracts, a ship that existed only in the spaces between audits. That invisibility had kept them alive for nine years. It had let them sit over Meridian, year after year, watching, logging, witnessing, without anyone asking why a salvage freighter cared about a dead colony.

And now a form, generated by an algorithm, had noticed the seam. *Crew member X. OKONKWO.* Their name, sitting in a database, attached to a ship that had never existed, orbiting a colony where everyone was supposed to be dead.

Someone would look at that flag. Maybe just a clerk closing out a queue. Maybe someone else. Someone who remembered that X. Okonkwo had also appeared—briefly, nine years ago, in the personnel records of Meridian Colony before the records were purged. Someone who wondered what a former Meridian systems tech was doing on a ghost ship parked over the ghost colony for nine consecutive cycles.

Xanthe sat back down. Their hands were steady, which surprised them. Inside, something was moving—something between the loneliness they had carried for nine years and something newer, sharper, with teeth.

For nine years the witness had been the loneliest job in the universe, and they had done it gladly, because being unseen was the price of seeing. Nobody hunts a ghost. Nobody interrogates a rumor. Xanthe had made themselves small enough to fit inside the silence, and the silence had protected them, and in exchange they had kept faith with five hundred sleepers who didn't know they had a witness at all.

But the silence had a hole in it now, and the hole was shaped exactly like their own name.

They thought about deleting the archive. Four hundred and eleven hours, gone in a keystroke, and maybe the flag would die as a clerical error, maybe the clerk would shrug, maybe—

No. Even thinking it felt like a second murder. Those voices were all that was left of the truth, and if Xanthe erased them, then Meridian would finally be dead, properly dead, dead the way the corporation had always wanted: with no witnesses, ever.

*Response required within 72 hours.*

Xanthe opened a draft reply and stared at it for a long time. Then they closed it, unopened, and did the only thing that made sense. They walked aft, past the sleeping racks, past Bo's darkened medbay with its too-many-empty-beds smell of antiseptic and grief, and knocked on the captain's door until Idris opened it with the expression of a man who already knew the news was bad.

"We have a problem," Xanthe said. "Someone knows the *Echo* exists."

Idris looked at them for a moment. Behind him, on his desk screen, the same audit notice glowed green and routine and harmless.

"Yeah," he said quietly. "And that's not the worst part. There's a ship inbound. Transponder says *Vantage*. ETA nineteen hours."
