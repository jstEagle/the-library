## 3. Which Half Wakes

They board us at 0400, ship's time, which is a courtesy. Vantage doesn't need the dark to take what it wants; the dark is just habit.

I hear them before I see them—magnetic boots on the Echo's hull, a rhythm like knuckles on a door nobody wants to answer. Six of them in corporate grey, faces behind visors that make them look less like people than like decisions. Their lead carries a slate with my name already on it. Not Xanthe Okonkwo, navigator. Xanthe Okonkwo, *asset*. There's a difference, and the difference is everything.

"Your flight logs," the lead says. "All data pertaining to Meridian Colony. You'll be compensated."

Compensated. As if the last nine years were a debt they intended to settle all along, and I'd simply failed to invoice.

I hand over the decoy archive—the one I built for exactly this morning, three hundred hours of static and dead air dressed up as transmissions. The lead scans it, nods once, and something in their posture loosens by a degree I'm not supposed to notice. But I do. I've spent nine years learning to read silences over Meridian; I can read one across a boarding bay.

They're not here for the archive alone.

* * *

They let me walk back to navigation under escort, which tells me they still think of me as crew. That lasts until I pass the medbay and see Bo pressed against the bulkhead, two of the greys flanking her, and one of them holding her tablet—her *personal* tablet—with the family registry open on its face.

The registry lists next of kin among the sleepers. Bo's sister. Anaya Bo-Ademola, pod 114, status: *recoverable*.

Recoverable. The word lands wrong before I understand why. Recoverable implies recoverable *by someone*. It implies value.

"You're not erasing them," I say. It comes out flat, almost bored, and I hate how steady I sound when nothing inside me is.

The lead turns. Behind the visor, a pause I can price. "Meridian Holdings appreciates your discretion these past years, Navigator. It made this simpler."

"Simpler."

"The colony holds eleven hundred and six souls in suspension." They recite it like inventory. "Of those, five hundred and forty-one carry active corporate debt instruments—education bonds, relocation liens, medical financing. Those debts survive cryostasis. Wake them, put them to work, and the holdings are solvent again within a decade." A beat. "The remainder are, frankly, negative assets. Resuscitation costs exceed projected return."

Negative assets. Five hundred and sixty-five people, filed under a minus sign.

"And the negative ones?"

The visor tilts toward me, and I realize they think I'm asking out of curiosity. Maybe they think I'm asking because I want in.

"Thermal reclamation," the lead says. "The pods are self-contained. We vent the hold sections and the units cycle down cleanly. No mess. No remains. On paper, the colony died nine years ago anyway. We're just closing the account."

On paper. Everything about Meridian has always lived on paper—the manifest that never flew, the colony that was written off, the audit that flagged my name like a blood spot on a ledger. Nine years I told myself the corporation had forgotten. They hadn't forgotten. They'd been waiting for the paperwork to ripen.

Bo makes a sound against the bulkhead, small and strangled, and every instinct I have goes to her—and then past her, to Sefa in engineering, to Idris on the bridge, to the Echo herself, old and stubborn and the only home I've ever chosen.

"Where's my crew?" I ask.

"Confined pending completion. Cooperate, and they're released at Meridian orbit. Resist—" The lead lets the sentence die, which is its own kind of sentence.

* * *

Navigation bay. My bay. Two escorts at the door, the lead at my shoulder while I pull up the charts they demand: approach vectors, relay positions, the debris field where the colony's comm array used to sing.

And underneath the charts, running quiet as a second heartbeat, the real archive. Nine years of transmissions. The colonists' last voices before the freeze—jokes, arguments, lullabies, a woman named Anaya reading her sister a story over a bad line. Proof they were alive when the corporation signed the death certificate. Proof they're *still* alive, if anyone cares to look.

The lead wants it. I can feel the wanting coming off them like heat. The decoy bought an hour at most; their auditors will crack it by Meridian orbit, and then the questions stop being polite.

So here is the arithmetic they've handed me, and I turn it over and over because there's no version where it comes out clean:

Give them the true archive, and the Echo sails home whole. Sefa keeps her hands on engines she loves. Idris keeps his command, such as it is. Bo loses her sister to thermal reclamation and spends the rest of her life knowing I helped close the account. And the other half of the colony—my half, the half I've been keeping company in silence all these years—vents into the dark as a rounding error.

Or.

There's a protocol buried in the navigation system, one I installed myself four years ago during a maintenance window nobody audited. Emergency wake, colony-side, triggered from here. It would open every pod in the hold—all eleven hundred and six, no triage, no ledger. It would also draw power through the Echo's own drive core to do it, because the colony's grid died with its crew and the pods have been sipping from our umbilical ever since, a fact I have never once entered in a log.

Full draw. Core burnout. The Echo goes dark between the stars, engines slagged, stranded in the one place in the universe our names were never supposed to appear.

No ship. Maybe no rescue. Certainly no anonymity—I'd be broadcasting the whole thing wide, every relay I ever seeded, the evidence and the crime and my own name attached to both.

Idris would call it madness. Sefa would call it murder—of the ship, which for her amounts to the same thing. Bo wouldn't call it anything. She'd just look at me with her sister's name burning behind her eyes, and that would be worse than any argument.

The lead checks a chronometer. "Navigator. The archive."

My hand is on the console. Both options live under my palm—the archive's release key, the wake protocol's dead-man trigger—and the strange thing, the thing I'll remember for the rest of whatever life I have left, is how quiet it gets inside me at the end. Nine years of logging silence over Meridian, and now the silence is mine, and it isn't lonely at all. It's full. It's five hundred and sixty-five people who were never supposed to matter, plus one sister in pod 114, plus three misfits who chose me without knowing what I was carrying.

You don't get to keep everything. I learned that young. The question was always what you refuse to lose.

I think about the manifest with my name on it. A ship that officially never flew, carrying a navigator who officially never existed. The corporation came back to erase the evidence, and the evidence is standing right here with a hand on the console.

Let them erase me, then. Let them try.

"Here's your archive," I say, and I don't touch the release key. I press the other one, and hold it, and feel the Echo shudder beneath me like something waking up.

The lead's visor snaps toward the readouts. "What did you—"

"Emergency wake," I tell them. "All pods. Every single one. You can put that in your ledger."

Alarms bloom across the board. Power pours out of the drive core in a river I can't call back, and somewhere below my feet, in the frozen dark of the hold, eleven hundred hearts are being asked to start again.

The lights die first. Then the engines, with a sound like a held breath finally let go. The Echo drifts, blind and burning through her own reserves, and in the last seconds before the main board goes black I route everything remaining into the transmitter and start the broadcast—every relay, every frequency, the archive and the orders and my voice, saying the words the corporation never wanted said aloud:

*They're alive. They were always alive. Come and see.*

Then darkness, and the long fall toward Meridian, and out of the static—a signal rising to meet us.
