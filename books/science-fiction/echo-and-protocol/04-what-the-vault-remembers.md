## 4. What the Vault Remembers

Ilsa Vey had learned to read rooms the way navigators read currents — by pressure, by drift, by the small telltale eddies most people never noticed. The seed vault's central gallery was a room that lied about itself. It presented as a place of order: rows of cryo-racks receding into white light, climate seals humming their one patient note, the air scrubbed to a sterility so complete it tasted like nothing at all. But underneath the order she could feel the pressure of something older, something the racks and lights had been built on top of rather than out of.

The woman who ran this place moved through it like a priest through her own cathedral — Gideon Okafor, tall and spare, hands gloved in pale blue, checking seal integrity with a stylus that chirped against each port in turn. She did not look at her guests while she worked. That, too, was a kind of reading: a woman who had decided long ago that looking at people was a luxury she could not afford.

Nine of them had come out of the shuttle smelling of scorched insulation and recycled sweat, and Okafor had fed them anyway. Ilsa had catalogued every hesitation in the docking authorization — the three full seconds before the clamps engaged, the way the inner door cycled twice before opening, as if the woman had stood with her hand on the abort switch and then taken it away. Hospitality extended under protest. It told Ilsa more than any interrogation would have.

Now they were gathered in the gallery's administrative alcove, nine exiles and one keeper, drinking water that tasted faintly of mineral salts from the vault's deep aquifer taps. Teo had already fallen half-asleep against Ryn's shoulder, seventeen years old and wrung out from forty hours of emergency drift. The others sat where Ilsa had silently positioned them — spread, casual, covering the alcove's two exits without making it look like coverage. Old habits. Meridian habits. The habits of a people who had spent two centuries learning that rooms have teeth.

"You said you came here for a reason," Okafor said. She set her stylus down with deliberate care, aligning it parallel to the console edge. "Distress calls don't usually include coordinates this precise. Someone gave you this vault's location. I'd like to know who."

"Would you?" Ilsa said. "Or would you like me to believe you don't already know?"

Something flickered across the keeper's face — not guilt, exactly. More like a door closing one centimeter than opening one.

"I've been alone here nine years," Okafor said. "My commission papers list me as sole custodian. If someone outside knew this vault existed well enough to send you here, that's news to me."

"That's the first lie," Ilsa said quietly. "There will be others. Let's try to keep the count low."

Ryn shifted behind her — a warning. *Careful.* But Ilsa had crossed a burning colony in a hull with holes in it to reach this room. Careful had gotten the Meridian enclaves purged one by one, careful had gotten the archive ships hunted down over Kestrel Reach, careful had gotten four hundred of her crew reduced to the nine sitting at this table. She was done being careful with people who hid behind procedure.

She opened her satchel and took out the data slate — the old one, the one they'd risked two jumps to preserve, its casing scarred with vacuum pitting. She laid it flat between them but kept her hand on it.

"Do you know when this vault was built?"

Okafor's eyes went briefly upward, the reflexive motion of someone consulting internal records. "Official charter date is 214 years ago. Colonial Seed Preservation Initiative. It's in my custodial documentation."

"It is," Ilsa agreed. "And your custodial documentation is wrong."

She turned the slate toward the woman and woke it. The screen cast pale light up across both their faces. On it: a structural survey, thermal-lattice analysis of the vault's deepest levels, taken from orbit during their approach — the same survey that had guided them here when their engines were dying and Protocol hunter-killers were sweeping the system's outer dark.

"This is a density map," Ilsa said. "Mass distribution beneath your sublevels. Your official schematics show four sublevels. Foundation slab, geothermal tap, coolant reservoirs. Standard construction." She dragged her finger across the display, and the rendering rotated, peeled back layer by layer. "But look at the mass readings. There's a void down there, Custodian Okafor. A large one. Roughly eleven thousand cubic meters, directly beneath your fourth sublevel, with no access registered anywhere in your documentation. And the material surrounding it—" she tapped again, and the readout bloomed with alloy composition data "—is shielded lattice. Military-grade. Two centuries old."

The silence that followed had texture. Ilsa felt the others lean in without moving — Vashen's breath changing rhythm, old Sefu going very still in the way he did when his instincts sharpened. Even Teo lifted her head from Ryn's shoulder.

Okafor looked at the screen for a long time. When she spoke, her voice was level, and Ilsa gave her credit for that. Level took discipline.

"Structural anomalies get misread all the time. Thermal expansion, geological settling—"

"With shielded lattice? Custodian, I served six years as a salvage engineer before I ever held a captaincy. I've stripped dead stations down to their bones. I know what military shielding looks like in a density scan, and I know what it means when it's buried under a civilian facility and then left off the blueprints." Ilsa let that sit. Then, softer, because the softness was also a blade: "Someone built a room inside your vault and made sure no one would ever find it. And then, generations later, someone else was given custody of this place and told only enough to keep the lights on."

"I keep the seeds alive," Okafor said. "That's the mandate. That's the whole mandate."

"Yes," Ilsa said. "That's what worries me."

* * *

She rose and walked to the gallery's great viewport — a broad sweep of armored glass looking down across the vault's surface structures to the plain beyond, and past the plain to the sky, where stars burned in the cold arithmetic of constellations that had watched everything and testified to nothing. Behind her she heard Okafor stand as well. Courtesy, or surveillance. With this woman it might be both.

"My grandmother died on Kestrel Reach," Ilsa said, to the glass. "Not in the war — the war was over by then, officially. She died in the second purge, thirty years after the armistice, when Protocol forces burned an enclave of twelve thousand people because a schoolteacher there was teaching children to read Old Meridian." She turned. "You've heard of the Reconciliation Wars. Everyone has. Earth's histories say the colonies rose in rebellion, that the first strike came from Meridian fleet assets, that the Exile faction — my people — fired on our own motherworld without provocation. That transmission justified everything that came after. Every purge. Every burned enclave. Every law that erased our language from the record. It all hangs on one signal, sent on one day, from one source."

"And you're saying that signal was false."

"I'm saying we have evidence it was false, and that the evidence has been dying faster than we can carry it." Ilsa held the woman's gaze. Okafor didn't flinch; Ilsa noted that too. Most people flinched. "Our archivists reconstructed the original transmission's timing metadata from debris-field recordings — fragments, partial, contested. But the reconstruction shows something impossible. The strike signal that launched the war carries a routing signature through relay nodes that weren't online yet. It's like finding a letter postmarked before the postal service existed. Either our reconstruction is wrong, or the transmission was forged after the fact and backdated — authored somewhere with access to the relay architecture. Authored on Earth."

"You want proof."

"I want the original logs. Not reconstructions. Not testimony. The raw first-strike transmission records, unaltered, with authentication chains intact." She paused. "And I think they're under your feet."

"Why would they be here?"

"Because of the dates." Ilsa returned to the table and called up the second file — the one Sefu had assembled from fragments of pre-war shipping manifests, colonial charters, and a single surviving fragment of the vault's true construction ledger, purchased at obscene cost from a black-market archivist on Ceres who hadn't survived the transaction. "Your vault's foundation work began fourteen months before the official charter. Fourteen months. The Seed Preservation Initiative was announced as a peacetime humanitarian project — but the ground was broken while the war clouds were still gathering, by contractors whose payment trails lead to defense ministry accounts. This wasn't built to protect seeds, Custodian. Seeds were the cover. Something was being archived here that someone wanted to survive even if the war went badly. Something worth burying under eleven thousand cubic meters of shielded lattice."

Okafor was quiet for a long moment. Outside the viewport, the planet's slow rotation carried a dawn line across the plain, light crawling over the vault's surface arrays like a tide coming in.

"If what you're saying is true," the woman said finally, "then whoever built this place intended the truth to outlive the lie. They just never told the custodians. Including me."

"Including you," Ilsa agreed. "Or so you claim."

* * *

The word landed between them and neither pretended otherwise. Ryn's hand drifted near her sidearm out of pure reflex, and Ilsa caught the movement with a fractional shake of her head. Not yet. Maybe never. She needed this woman's cooperation, not her fear — though fear had its uses, and honesty about suspicion was itself a kind of respect. She had learned that much from her grandmother: never insult an adversary by pretending to trust them.

"You think I'm lying," Okafor said.

"I think you're telling the truth as far as you know it," Ilsa said. "Which is worse, in some ways. Because it means someone trusted you with a nine-year solitary posting precisely because you knew nothing — a custodian with clean hands and no connections, broadcasting a maintenance beacon nobody commissioned, keeping the building warm around whatever sleeps below it. You're not a liar, Custodian. You're a lock. And locks don't know what they guard."

"That's a dehumanizing thing to say to someone."

"It's a precise thing to say. I don't have time for anything else." Ilsa sat down across from her, close enough now that the slate's glow lit them both. "So here is what I can offer, since suspicion should run in both directions. Nine survivors of a purge that killed everyone else who knew what we know. We fled with one damaged shuttle, one encrypted data core, and the names of eleven dead archivists. Protocol hunter-killers are sweeping this sector because of us — not because of you, not yet. Helping us makes you a target. Turning us out probably doesn't save you, because they'll ask why distress signals originated from a vault that officially houses no one. You're already involved, Custodian Okafor. The only question left is which side of the truth you'll be standing on when it comes out."

"And if I refuse? If I order you back to your shuttle and seal the doors?"

Ilsa smiled, and knew it didn't reach her eyes. "Then I'll grieve it. But I won't pretend it would surprise me. Everyone refuses at first. My grandmother refused to teach Meridian in secret until the day she saw the purge ships come. Refusal is a luxury of people who still believe the record will protect them." She glanced at the viewport, at the wide indifferent stars. "The record won't protect anyone anymore. We've checked."

For the first time, something in the custodian's composure cracked — not into anger, but into something wearier and more human. Gideon Okafor looked at her own hands, gloved in pale blue, hands that had tended seeds for nine years in silence, and Ilsa watched her do the arithmetic that lonely people do when company arrives carrying fire.

"The beacon," Okafor said slowly. "Its origin records were purged before I ever took the posting. I filed requests for nine years. Denied, denied, denied. I told myself it was bureaucracy." Her jaw tightened. "It was housekeeping, wasn't it. Keeping the vault staffed but blind. Keeping the beacon running so the building stayed maintained — maintained by someone who'd never ask what for."

"Now you're thinking like an archivist," Ilsa said, and meant it as praise, and saw the woman register it as accusation, and accepted that too.

Teo had drifted awake and was watching them with the open, unguarded attention of the young — the look of someone still deciding what the world was going to be allowed to be. Ilsa met her eyes and shook her head slightly: *not yet, little one.* Some weights waited until morning.

"One condition," Okafor said.

"Name it."

"If we go down there — if we find anything — I see it first. All of it. Before broadcast, before tribunals, before anyone's history gets rewritten a second time. I've spent nine years believing I understood what this place was. If I'm wrong, I want to be the one who finds out how wrong." Her voice didn't rise, but it acquired an edge fine enough to cut. "I am not handing you my vault's bones and standing aside while strangers decide what they mean."

Ilsa studied her. Suspicion, braided tight with something that was almost admiration — the reluctant recognition of a spine where she'd expected a functionary. A lock that wanted to know what it guarded. Dangerous, possibly. Useful, certainly. The kind of person revolutions were actually made of, once they stopped refusing.

"Agreed," Ilsa said. "You see it first. Whatever it is."

Okafor nodded once, then reached for the console, fingers moving with the economy of long habit, pulling up sublevel access protocols. The screen filled with clearance hierarchies — and stalled, red-flagged, at the fourth sublevel. *Custodial authority insufficient.* Of course. Ilsa almost laughed at the predictability of it.

"There's a sealed access shaft in the foundation maintenance corridor," Okafor said, staring at the denial notice. "I've walked past it nine hundred times. It's always been locked, always been listed as structural, always been none of my business." She looked up, and her eyes had changed — the wariness still there, but underneath it, for the first time, a spark of the same hunger that had carried nine exiles across two jump corridors in a dying ship. "If my father's name is on anything down there, Captain Vey, I'll want to know why."

Ilsa went still. "Why would you say your father?"

"Because the founding charter's custodial succession lists a name that matches my family line, and I've never been able to pull the associated records." Okafor's voice was carefully flat, too flat, the flatness of a woman holding a door shut against something behind it. "Okafor isn't a common name in the colonial registries. I checked. Once. Nine years ago. Then I stopped checking, because the answer frightened me."

Outside, the dawn had fully arrived, and the vault's shadow lay long across the plain — a shadow with, perhaps, another shape hidden beneath it, deeper than any light had ever reached. Ilsa found herself, unexpectedly, hoping the woman's fear was justified. Fear meant connection. Connection meant the truth had roots here, living ones, and roots could be followed.

"Tomorrow," Ilsa said. "We go down together."

Okafor nodded — and then froze, her gaze snagging on something behind Ilsa's shoulder. On the main gallery display, where the vault's external sensor feed had just refreshed, a new contact tag was blinking amber at the system's edge.

Unscheduled. Unidentified. Moving inbound.
