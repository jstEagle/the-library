## 14. Teo's Run

The fuel skiff smelled like every bad decision Teo had ever made.

It was old — older than the vault, maybe older than the war — and it smelled of ozone and rust and the particular sourness of a life-support scrubber that had been patched one time too many. The seat was molded to the shape of someone else's body, someone long dead, and when Teo settled into it his spine found grooves that didn't match him at all, as if the chair itself were warning him off.

He ran the preflight anyway. Third time. His hands knew the sequence now better than they knew their own names.

"Skiff One, you are cleared for departure," Gideon's voice said in his ear, coming through the vault's ancient comm relay with a faint crackle underneath, like paper being crumpled very far away. "Teo — listen to me. You go out, you top off the shuttle's tanks from the reserve pod at marker seven, and you come straight back. No detours. No sightseeing."

"No promises," Teo said, because if he said anything sincere he'd have to think about what he was actually doing, and thinking about that would stop his hands from working.

In the skiff's tiny rearview pickup he could see the others gathered at the airlock glass: Ilsa with her arms crossed, wearing the expression she wore when she'd lost an argument but wanted everyone to know she'd been right; Dario standing slightly apart, holding a battered slate against his chest like a shield; Gideon herself, small and still, her face unreadable behind the visor of her surface suit.

Teo raised one hand. Nobody waved back. That was fine. That was how serious people said *come home alive*.

He keyed the release and the vault spat him into the dark.

* * *

The debris field had once been something else. Gideon had told them about it on the second night, in her flat archivist's voice: a shipyard, before the Reconciliation Wars. Kilometers of orbital scaffolding where freighters had been born whole from their keels up. Then the first strike came — the forged strike, the lie that started everything — and the yard died in an afternoon, and nobody ever came back to bury it properly.

Two centuries later its bones still turned slowly around the planet, a halo of shattered trusses and fuel bladders and hull plating the size of city blocks, all of it drifting on trajectories so old they'd become almost lawful. Almost predictable. Almost safe.

Almost.

The skiff threaded into the field at a quarter thrust, and Teo did what Ilsa had drilled into him for six hours straight until his eyes crossed: he flew by rhythm, not by sight. The debris rotated in slow, overlapping cycles. A girder that blocked your path now would be gone in ninety seconds; a gap that looked open would close like a mouth. There was a music to it, Ilsa said, a waltz in nine-four time, and if you could hear the beat you could dance through anything.

Teo couldn't hear any music. What he heard was his own pulse, loud and stupid, and the tick of micrometeorites against the skiff's forward plate like fingernails on a coffin lid.

But his hands were steady. That surprised him. Somewhere between the fourth simulation and the real thing, the fear had stopped being a wall and become a floor he stood on.

"Marker seven is bearing two-seven-zero relative," Dario's voice said over the channel. It was strange hearing him from the vault instead of beside him. "Reserve pod should be visible on your port scan in — "

"There." Teo saw it: a fat, scarred cylinder lashed inside a cage of broken scaffolding, its reflective coating long since burned away, looking less like fuel storage than like a grub in a web. He swung the skiff wide to line up his approach, and for three seconds — three stupid seconds — he let himself look past it, out through a gap in the wreckage at the planet below.

It was beautiful. That was the trap of it. Blue-white cloud spirals, the brown seam of a mountain range, the vault's little cluster of lights on the night side like a handful of coins dropped on black cloth. He'd grown up on stations and transports and the inside of other people's emergencies. He had never had a sky of his own. For three seconds he just hung there and looked.

Then the proximity alarm screamed.

* * *

Later — much later, when he told the story and retold it until the telling wore smooth — Teo would say he felt the drones before he saw them, which wasn't true, but it captured something true adjacent: the way the hair on his arms lifted under his flight suit, the way the alarm's pitch changed from *attention* to *run*.

They came out of the planet's terminator like a fistful of thrown knives. Six of them, wedge-shaped, matte-black, running dark except for the cold blue slit of their sensor arrays. Protocol hunter-killers, autonomous wing, the kind the exiles' stories were full of. They swept into the debris field in a spread formation and began to burn everything that reflected radar.

The first target was a derelict tug two hundred meters off Teo's bow. It simply ceased to be — a flash, a bloom of expanding shrapnel, a pressure wave that rocked the skiff even through vacuum, because the tug's residual atmosphere vented all at once and shoved everything nearby.

Teo's body did the flying. His mind caught up afterward, in fragments.

*Go quiet.* Ilsa's voice in his memory, from the drills. *Kill your transponder, kill your running lights, drop into the debris and become garbage.*

His hand slapped the transponder dead. He cut thrust to nothing and let the skiff drift, tumbling gently, among the wreck-mass. Through the canopy he watched the drone wing work its way across the field, methodical as harvesters. Flash. Flash. A fuel bladder went up in a silent orange flower. A section of hull plating, big as a plaza, was sliced into confetti by something he didn't see fire.

And then one of the drones turned toward him.

Not toward his position — toward his *signature*. Teo understood it with a cold clarity that felt almost calm: the skiff's thermal mass was wrong. Everything around him was cold, two centuries cold, and the skiff was warm from his run, warm from his own body breathing inside it. Against the frozen graveyard he glowed like a coal.

The drone's blue sensor slit brightened.

It began to accelerate toward him, threading the debris with a fluid confidence no human pilot could match, and Teo did the math without wanting to: thirty seconds, maybe twenty-five, and then he would be a flash too, another entry in a field full of them, and the shuttle would never get fueled and the jump window would open and close and the truth would die in a sealed vault under a dead man's name.

He thought, absurdly, of the phrase Dario had taught him three nights ago, over reconstituted rice. *Senneth ara vey.* Sleep well, little star. A Meridian goodnight. Teo had laughed when he learned it, because it sounded like a song, because Dario sang it half under his breath while he worked, because it was the first sentence of a dead language that had ever been addressed to Teo personally, like he was worth putting to bed safely.

*Senneth ara vey,* he thought, to himself, to the skiff, to whatever happened next.

The drone was four hundred meters out. Three-fifty.

Then the comm crackled, and Dario's voice filled the cockpit — not panicked, not performative, but level in a way Teo had never heard from him, level the way deep water is level.

"Teo. Don't move. Don't touch anything. I'm going to talk to them."

* * *

What Teo learned afterward, in pieces, was this:

Protocol autonomous wings answered to a command hierarchy, and every hierarchy had a root authentication chain, and Dario Sen had spent eleven years as a Protocol signals analyst memorizing chains the way other men memorized prayers. The codes he carried were weeks out of date — stolen, aging, half-burned — but authority, like milk, doesn't spoil all at once.

Dario sat in the vault's comm room with Gideon watching over his shoulder and Ilsa standing behind him with a wrench, and he broadcast on the wing's command frequency in flawless Protocol cipher, identifying himself with a handler's authorization tier he had no right to, and he told the drones a story.

The story was this: this sector was not under attack. This sector was a live exercise. The debris field was a training environment seeded with simulated targets, and the skiff adrift within it was exercise property, instrumented, monitored, and *not to be touched*, because a weapons strike on exercise assets would trigger a review board and the reviewing officer was named, ranked, and cited in the message header with a signature block so dense and so correct that only a machine checking against a machine would bother to verify it.

And the machines checked. And the machines hesitated.

On the skiff's scope Teo watched the lead drone's acceleration curve flatten, then bend, then swing wide. It passed him at six hundred meters, close enough that he could see the pitted ceramic of its hull, close enough that he could imagine the warhead nested in its belly like a sleeping animal. Its blue eye swept across the canopy and moved on.

The rest of the wing broke formation and began burning targets again — but on a different vector, away from him, away from marker seven, performing the fiction Dario had written for them with perfect mechanical sincerity.

"They're accepting the exercise frame," Dario's voice said in Teo's ear, and there was strain in it now, sweat Teo could hear. "But the wing will report back. When it does, the story dies. You have maybe ten minutes. Get to the pod, get the transfer done, and get behind mass. Go."

Teo went.

He brought the skiff alive out of its drift and flew the last approach to marker seven hot and low, weaving through wreckage he would have refused to look at an hour ago, because fear was a floor now and he stood on it and moved. The reserve pod's coupling took his docking collar on the second try. Fuel lines woke with a shudder he felt through the whole frame of the skiff, and the transfer gauge began to climb.

Ninety seconds for a full top-off. It felt like a year.

Through the canopy, upside down and sideways as the skiff cycled through its docking attitude, Teo watched the drone wing work. And then he saw the thing that made his stomach drop through the deck: the lead drone had stopped. It hung motionless amid the wreckage, sensor array flaring bright, and it was pointing — not at Teo, not at the pod — back along the path of Dario's transmission, back toward the source, running the verification that Dario had said would kill the story.

"Uh," Teo said. "Dario. Your machine is doing homework."

Static. Two full seconds of static, which in Teo's new arithmetic was a geological age.

"I see it," Dario said finally, tight. "It's querying the review board identity. That identity was retired eight days ago, which means the query will bounce, which means — " A breath. "Which means I have one more card and it's a bad one."

"What's the bad card?"

"The truth," Dario said, "shaped like a lie."

What he transmitted then, Teo only understood later, replayed in the vault's mess while everyone pretended not to stare at him. Dario dropped the handler's cipher entirely and sent a plain-text burst on the wing's maintenance channel — the channel used by ground crews, by technicians, by the unglamorous caste of Protocol that kept the killing machines fueled and calibrated. And what he said, in the flat idiom of a tired technician filing a fault report, was that the exercise ordnance in this sector had been misconfigured, that a recall-authentication error had armed live warheads against friendly exercise assets, and that the wing was to return to its carrier immediately for munitions inspection, priority flag amber, technician's responsibility assumed under his own certification number.

It was a confession. That was the genius of it, or the madness. Machines trusted self-incrimination; it fit no attack pattern they knew. A technician flagging his own catastrophic error was the most believable signal in the universe, because no one would invent blame they didn't have to carry.

The lead drone's sensor array dimmed. It rolled, aligned on a return vector, and burned for high orbit. One by one the rest of the wing followed it up and out, and the debris field went quiet again, settling back into its two-century waltz, and Teo sat alone in a rusting skiff with a fuel gauge climbing toward full and blood singing so loud in his ears he could barely hear the all-clear.

"Transfer complete," he managed. "Coming home."

Nobody answered right away. Then Gideon's voice, quieter than he'd ever heard it: "Come home, Teo."

* * *

The docking clamp closed behind him with a sound like a held breath released, and when the inner hatch cycled open the whole crew was there — Ilsa first, grabbing his flight suit by both shoulders and shaking him once, hard, which from Ilsa was an embrace; then Gideon, who put her hand briefly on top of his helmet and left it there while the seal hissed open; then the others, pressing in, all of them talking at once, and Teo stood in the middle of it grinning like an idiot with his legs shaking and let himself be loved loudly.

Dario hung back at the edge of it. He always did. But when the crowd thinned he stepped forward, and Teo saw the state of him properly for the first time: gray-faced, hands trembling, a thread of red running from his nose over his lip where the pressure of the transmission burst had popped something small and stubborn. He'd wiped at it and missed, and the smear had dried rusty on his chin.

"You're bleeding," Teo said.

"It's nothing. Capillary — it happens with high-power bursts sometimes." Dario tried to smile and didn't quite land it. "You flew well."

"You talked well." Teo looked at him — really looked, the way he hadn't let himself before, because trusting Dario had been a vote and votes aren't the same as faith. "That last message. The technician thing. You flagged yourself. You gave them your certification number, your name, everything. If they trace that burst —"

"They'll trace it," Dario said simply. "Tonight, probably. And by tomorrow every Protocol net in the system will have my face and my file and a warrant with my name on it." He shrugged, and the shrug cost him something; Teo watched it cost him. "I've been waiting eleven years to spend that name on something worth buying."

The mess went quiet around them. Ilsa had stopped moving. Gideon had gone very still by the lockers.

"So that's it," Ilsa said slowly. "No more codes. No more borrowed authority. Whatever you had that was theirs — you just burned all of it. For us."

"For the archive," Dario corrected, but his voice cracked on the word, and everyone heard what it actually meant.

Teo reached out and smeared the dried blood off Dario's chin with his thumb, the way his mother used to, a lifetime ago, on a station whose name he could barely remember. "Meridian doesn't waste anything," he said, using the phrase Dario had taught him, badly pronounced, probably offensive, definitely sincere. "Not even blood."

Dario laughed — a short, startled, wounded sound — and for the first time since the escape pod, he looked like he belonged somewhere.

It lasted four seconds. Then the vault's main console chimed, an alert tone none of them had heard before, and Gideon crossed to it in three strides and read the screen, and the color left her face the way heat leaves metal.

"That's the beacon," she said. "Someone's answering it. Someone who isn't Protocol." She turned, and her eyes found each of them in turn. "It's a Meridian handshake. Old cipher. Full authentication." Her voice dropped. "There's a ship inbound, and whoever's flying it knows exactly who we are."
