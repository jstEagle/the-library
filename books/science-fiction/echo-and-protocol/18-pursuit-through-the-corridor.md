## 18. Pursuit Through the Corridor

The corridor opened at 0400 ship's time, a seam of pale light stitched across the dark between Mars and the Belt, and Ilsa Vey watched it bloom on the nav display with the particular hatred she reserved for beautiful things that wanted to kill her.

"Window confirmed," Teo said from the pilot's cradle, voice too bright, the brightness of someone holding fear at arm's length. "Sixty-one minutes before it collapses. Corridor transit, if the math holds—forty-three minutes to Ceres."

"And if the math doesn't hold?"

"Then we're a smear in a very expensive tunnel." He grinned at her over his shoulder. He was nineteen years old and had learned to fly on smuggler wrecks in the Meridian Exile, where the difference between a good landing and a bad one was usually measured in survivors. "Relax, Captain. I've done worse with less."

She hadn't told him about the interceptor yet.

It had appeared eleven minutes ago at the edge of long-range: a Protocol hunter-killer, lean as a drawn blade, running dark until it wasn't. It had lit up its drive the moment their shuttle cleared the vault's debris cloud—which meant it had been waiting, which meant someone had known their launch window, which meant the coordinates that had burned sublevel four were still burning somewhere in Protocol's systems like a coal that wouldn't die.

"Contact is accelerating," Dario said quietly. He sat strapped into the sensor station, his stolen codes arrayed across his console like a card player's hand he no longer trusted. His face was still gray from the venting—the whole crew had watched Eden glitter out into vacuum an hour ago, forty years of Gideon's seeds blooming and dying against the stars while the shuttle threaded the sensor-blind chaos they left behind. It had worked. They were alive because of it. But Ilsa had seen what it cost Gideon to give the order, and she carried that cost now too, folded into her chest alongside everything else. "Intercept in thirty-eight minutes. They'll have weapons lock well before we reach the mouth."

"Options," Ilsa said.

"Run faster," said Teo.

"We're at ninety-four percent rated thrust. The hull took debris damage in the venting."

"Pray harder?"

"I'm serious, Teo."

"So am I." He turned back to his board, and the grin fell off him like a mask coming loose. "Captain, we can't outrun them. That's a *Vigil*-class. She'll be on us before the halfway mark. Our only play is to not look like what we are when they catch up."

Dario's hands went still over his console.

Ilsa saw it happen—the exact moment he understood what she was asking, and what it would cost. "You said you had spoof codes left."

"I said I had *one* spoof code left." His voice was flat. Careful. "One. The drone wing over the debris field—that burned through most of my library. What's left is a single Protocol fleet transponder signature. A *Vigil*-class signature." He laughed, short and bitter. "They built these hunters with a master identification key so friendly units could tell each other apart in a furball. Somebody in Fleet Logistics sold a copy of that key eight years ago. I bought it with money I didn't have, from a man I hope is dead."

"Will it hold?" Ilsa asked.

"It will hold for exactly as long as no one looks too closely." Dario turned in his chair to face her fully, and Ilsa saw the exhaustion carved into him, the static burns still healing along his jaw from the drone wing, the strange new steadiness underneath all of it. Nine days ago she'd wanted him spaced. Now he was the only reason any of them had a chance. "Here's the problem, Captain. If I spoof our transponder as a *Vigil*, and there's a real *Vigil* sitting right behind us—and there is—then the corridor traffic control at Ceres sees two ships claiming the same identity. One of them is legitimate. One of us gets flagged, boarded, and shot. And the real one has better paperwork than we do."

"Then we don't claim the same identity," Ilsa said slowly. An idea was forming, cold and clean, the kind of idea that came from twenty years of running. "We claim theirs *first*. We enter the corridor ahead of them, broadcasting their signature. When they arrive, *they're* the anomaly. Two ships, one ID—but we were here first. Their own traffic control protocols will treat the second arrival as the spoof."

Dario stared at her. Then he started laughing—not bitter this time, but wild, disbelieving. "That's insane. That's using their own bureaucracy as a weapon."

"Their bureaucracy is the biggest weapon they have. Might as well point it backward." She unbuckled and moved forward to stand behind Teo's cradle. "How fast can you get us to the mouth?"

Teo looked at the nav plot. Looked at the interceptor's projected track. Ilsa watched him do the arithmetic every pilot dreads—time versus distance versus the gap between brave and dead.

"If I burn everything," he said, "we hit the mouth four minutes ahead of them. Four minutes isn't much."

"It's everything," Dario said. "Four minutes means we're inside the corridor, inside their own traffic lattice, logged and acknowledged under their signature before they can contest it. After that, shooting us means shooting their own transponder record. Some Protocol gunner would have to explain that in a tribunal."

"Some gunner might decide not to explain anything," Ilsa said. "Everyone strap in. This is going to be ugly."

* * *

It was uglier than ugly.

Teo flew the approach like a man throwing himself down a staircase on purpose. The shuttle's damaged hull groaned through the full-thrust burn; alarms Ilsa had never heard before woke up and started screaming; somewhere aft, a coolant line let go and Gideon Okafor—quiet, stubborn Gideon, who had spent nine years alone among seeds and silence—crawled into the crawl space without being asked and sealed the rupture with her own two hands and a patch kit designed for greenhouse plumbing.

Ilsa heard her voice on the intercom from inside the ductwork, calm as Sunday: "Coolant pressure stabilizing. Whatever you're doing up there, keep doing it."

The interceptor came up behind them like a fist closing. On the tactical display it stopped pretending to be subtle—running lights blazing, weapons arrays cycling, hailing them on open channels with the flat, procedural menace of Protocol authority: *Unidentified vessel, this is PCV Admonition. You are entering a restricted jump corridor. Kill your drive and prepare for boarding or you will be fired upon.*

"They're not even reading our transponder yet," Dario said, fingers flying. "Too far out, too much debris scatter. Thirty seconds until they get a clean return." He loaded the stolen signature, hesitated one heartbeat—Ilsa watched him hesitate, watched him understand that this code was the last artifact of his old life, the last tool of the man he'd been—and burned it. "Signature active. We are now, officially, the PCV Admonition."

"That's poetic," Teo said through gritted teeth. "That's disgusting. I love it."

"*Admonition*, your transponder identifies you as a Protocol vessel," the hail continued, confused now, the voice losing its certainty. "Confirm authorization code."

Dario fed them a response string assembled from fragments of three different stolen cipher sets. It would buy seconds, not minutes. "They're checking," he muttered. "They're always checking. Nobody in Protocol trusts anybody else in Protocol, thank God."

The corridor mouth swelled in the viewport—a ring of distorted starlight, the jump threshold shimmering like heat haze over a road. Behind them, the real *Admonition* closed to firing range. Ilsa felt the deck change pitch under her feet as Teo threaded the last debris filaments of the seed cloud, the ship rolling through glittering drifts of Gideon's sacrificed harvest, wheat and rice and ten thousand vanished cultivars catching the drive flare like snow in headlights.

"Twenty seconds to threshold," Teo said. "They've got a firing solution. I'd bet my life on it. In fact—"

"In fact you already have," Ilsa said. "Fly."

What Teo did next, no trained pilot would have dared, and Ilsa understood later that this was precisely why it worked. Protocol trained its pilots to respect thresholds—to approach jump corridors on standardized vectors, at standardized velocities, because the corridor's shear gradients punished improvisation with death. Teo had never been trained by Protocol. Teo had learned to fly in the Exile, where the rules were written in wrecked hulls, and so instead of the standard approach he cut the drive for six full seconds, let the shuttle fall silent and ballistic through the last stretch of debris, dropped below the interceptor's firing solution geometry entirely—and then slammed the drive back to full burn on a vector that grazed the corridor's shear boundary close enough that the nav display bled red warnings across every panel.

The ship screamed. Every rivet screamed. Ilsa's vision grayed at the edges as the acceleration crushed her into her harness, and through the viewport the threshold light smeared into a streak, and behind them—

Behind them the *Admonition* fired.

Two lances of coherent light crossed the space where they should have been, where a standard-vector target would have been, and missed by a margin measured in heartbeats. The second shot clipped them anyway—a glancing strike along the dorsal spine that punched through the outer hull skin, cooked a relay bus, and filled the cabin with the smell of scorched insulation and the sound of Gideon swearing fluently in Old Meridian from inside the coolant ducts.

And then the threshold took them.

Transit was forty-three minutes of stretched light and pressure behind the eyes, the universe folded down to a tunnel of gray. In the corridor, nothing could follow. Nothing could fire. Nothing could touch them except the mathematics they'd brought with them—and Teo had flown them in four minutes ahead of their own shadow, logged at Ceres traffic control as the PCV Admonition, first arrival, priority clearance, under a signature that would take Protocol lawyers days to untangle.

Days they did not need. They needed minutes.

* * *

Ceres resolved out of transit haze at 0447, scarred and spinning, the neutral relay station hanging off its axis like a jewel on a wire. The shuttle came through scorched along the dorsal line, one maneuvering thruster fused solid, coolant venting in a bright feather that traffic control flagged immediately.

"PCV Admonition, you are showing damage inconsistent with your flight plan," the relay controller said, bored and suspicious in equal measure.

"Our flight plan encountered complications," Dario transmitted, and Ilsa could hear him smiling. "Requesting emergency berth. Priority one."

A pause. Somewhere in the relay's bureaucratic guts, two records of the same ship were colliding, and clerks were waking up all across the system to deal with it. Ilsa watched the clock on the nav display count down the corridor window's collapse behind them—watched, a minute later, the plot register the real *Admonition* arriving at Ceres's perimeter, transponder screaming its legitimacy into a traffic lattice that had already given its name away.

She imagined the conversation happening on that bridge. She wished she could watch it.

"Berth seven," the controller finally said. "Medical team standing by. And *Admonition*? Expect a full customs review."

"Looking forward to it," Dario said.

They came in slow, on thrusters and prayer, Teo nursing the wounded ship down the relay's docking spine with hands that shook only after the magnetic clamps caught and the deck went still beneath them. For a long moment nobody moved. The cabin hummed. Coolant dripped somewhere aft. The smell of burnt insulation hung over everything like incense.

Gideon emerged from the ductwork gray-faced and patched with sealant, carrying the archive case against her chest the way she had carried it for nine days—like a child, like a bomb, like the only thing in the universe that mattered. Her eyes found Ilsa's across the cabin.

"Are we here?" Gideon asked. "Is this real?"

Isla checked the chronometer. Eleven minutes remained on the corridor window. Outside the viewport, Protocol cutters were already launching from the *Admonition*, burning hard for the relay, legal attachés and marines bundled together in the same hulls because Protocol had never seen a difference between law and force.

Eleven minutes. Maybe twelve before the first cutter docked.

"It's real," Ilsa said, and unbuckled. "Get the archive. Move."
