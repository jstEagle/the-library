## 21. Testimony

They gave her three days' notice and a tailor.

The tailor was a small, nervous woman from the Ceres administrative ring who measured Gideon for a formal tunic in the Protocol gray that made her feel like she was being dressed for her own funeral. Gideon declined the gray. She asked instead for undyed linen — vault cloth, the plain weave she'd worn for nine years — and when the tailor protested that it would read as provocation before the tribunal, Gideon said, "Good."

Ilsa helped her braid her hair that morning, standing behind her in the cramped quarters the relay station had assigned them, working the strands with the same unhurried precision she used to run pre-flight checks. In the mirror, their eyes met.

"You don't have to say anything you're not ready to say," Ilsa murmured.

"Yes, I do." Gideon watched her own reflection hold steady. "That's the entire point of me now."

Teo sat cross-legged on the bunk, fidgeting with the pilot's wings Dario had carved for him out of shuttle hull scrap. "What if they shout at you?"

"Then I'll know my voice still works," Gideon said, "because theirs will be the ones going hoarse."

Dario stood by the door in his borrowed civilian coat, hands folded, wearing the particular stillness of a man who had spent his career in rooms like the one she was about to enter. "They'll open with your parentage," he said quietly. "It's standard. Establish bias before testimony. They'll want the gallery thinking of your father's face when they hear your words."

"My father's face." Gideon turned from the mirror. She had spent nine years unable to remember it clearly, and then one night in sublevel four, staring at the founding charter, it had come back all at once — the crooked smile, the ink stains on his fingers, the way he'd hummed Meridian lullabies off-key because he'd learned them from her mother and never quite mastered them. "Good. Let them see it. He had an honest face. That's why they chose him to hang the lie on."

* * *

The tribunal chamber had been a water reclamation auditorium before the war made Ceres neutral ground, and it still smelled faintly of mineral and old pipes. Seven adjudicators sat on a raised dais beneath the relay's patched-together flag — no Earth colors, no colonial banners, just the plain white of neutrality, which after everything struck Gideon as either the most honest symbol in the system or the most cowardly.

The gallery was full. Delegates from six fleets. Journalists with hand-cranked recorders because the Protocol delegation had demanded signal silence. And in the third row, exactly where she could see them: Ilsa's straight-backed stillness, Teo's wide eyes, Dario's careful blankness. Her people. Her family, sitting in public light for the first time since she'd found them burning out of the dark.

The chief adjudicator was a woman named Oyelaran, silver-haired, with the patient expression of someone who had spent thirty years listening to powerful men explain why documents were missing. She read Gideon's designation into the record: *Witness Gideon Okafor, custodian of Seed Vault Erebus-7, daughter of Tomas Okafor.*

There it was. The word *daughter*, laid down like a card.

A Protocol legal attaché rose from the second row — Voss, Dario had warned her, the handler's counterpart in the legal arm, a man with a voice like oiled machinery. "Before testimony begins," Voss said, "the tribunal should be reminded that the witness is the direct descendant of the individual convicted of authoring the first-strike transmission. The archive she carries has not been independently authenticated. We submit that her testimony must be weighted accordingly."

Oyelaran didn't look up from her slate. "Noted, Attaché Voss. The tribunal is also aware that the conviction of Tomas Okafor rested on evidence sealed by the same office that now requests we distrust unsealed evidence. The witness may proceed."

A ripple through the gallery. Gideon stepped to the witness floor and found the microphone too tall; a technician adjusted it while the whole room watched her fumble, and she decided to let them watch. Let them see a woman who had spent nine years talking only to machines learn, awkwardly, to talk to people again.

"State your name and function for the record."

"Gideon Okafor. For nine years I maintained the seed vault at Erebus-7. Before that, I was a conservation geneticist. I was assigned alone because the vault's operating budget had been cut to a single custodian and a maintenance beacon nobody would tell me the origin of." She paused. "I've since learned why the origin records were purged. I'd like to begin there."

Voss shifted. "The witness will confine herself to questions—"

"The witness," Oyelaran said mildly, "has been granted open testimony under Neutral Procedure Nine. Continue, Custodian."

Gideon breathed. Under the gallery lights, she could almost pretend she was back in the vault's long galleries, speaking to the seeds, narrating her days aloud because silence for nine years is its own kind of erosion. That trick had saved her mind once. It steadied her now.

* * *

She began with the beacon.

"For two hundred years, official history says the first strike against the Meridian colonies was authorized by a transmission signed by Tomas Okafor, transmitted from Erebus-7's command layer. That history says he acted alone, that he was tried and executed within the year, and that the vault was converted to seed storage afterward as a gesture of reconciliation." She let the words sit. "Every part of that story is true except the important parts.

"The vault's foundation level contains the original transmission logs. Not copies. The primary array, preserved in vacuum-sealed stasis since before the Reconciliation Wars. I have held the physical medium in my hands. The authentic log shows that the first-strike order originated not from Erebus-7 but from the Office of Colonial Security on Earth, routed through a relay chain designed to make it appear local. My father's signature on the forged copy was traced from a sample taken during a routine inspection eight months before the attack. He never touched the transmission console that day. The duty roster proves it. The roster is in the archive. It always was."

"How convenient," Voss said, "that this archive survived perfectly intact for two centuries beneath a facility whose very purpose—"

"Attaché." Oyelaran's voice didn't rise, but the room temperature did. "You will have opportunity for cross-examination."

"I welcome it," Gideon said, and meant it, and saw Voss register that she meant it, and saw something flicker behind his composure that might have been the first honest thing he'd shown all day.

She testified for four hours.

She walked them through the breach of the foundation seal — Dario's keys, her father's charter, the door that had waited two hundred years for someone with both the bloodline and the reason to open it. She described the authentic first-strike log in the flat, precise language of a scientist cataloguing specimens, because if she let herself speak of it any other way she would start shouting and never stop. She named the routing relays. She named the cipher signatures. She named, slowly and deliberately, watching each syllable land in the gallery like stones dropped into still water:

"Deputy Director Marcus Hale, Office of Colonial Security, who authored the forgery order. He is alive. He retired to Luna with honors. Auditor-General Sefu Ndiaye, who sealed the original investigation and destroyed the duty roster's public copy. Alive. A statue of him stands in Meridian City — a city built by the people his lie burned. Director Corinne Vashti, who signed the execution order knowing the trial evidence was contested by her own forensic division. Alive, as far as I know, though I note for the record that three of her former aides died unexpectedly in the past decade, and I would respectfully suggest the tribunal look into that before she does."

The gallery erupted. Oyelaran let it run for eleven seconds, then rapped the dais.

Voss was on his feet. "These are accusations against serving and former officials of the Earth government, made by the daughter of a convicted traitor, based on documents no independent party has examined—"

"Then examine them," Gideon said.

The simplicity of it stopped him mid-breath.

"That is my entire testimony, Attaché. Not faith. Not grief. Not a daughter's loyalty. An invitation. The archive is in the custody of this tribunal as of last week. Authenticate it. Break the cipher signatures — you'll find they match the private key registry of the Office of Colonial Security, which your own government has never denied possessing. Check the duty roster against personnel records. Check the relay chain against traffic logs the colonies kept independently during the war — the Meridian Exile kept their own records, Captain Vey can testify to that, records Earth never knew existed. Every thread I've given you can be pulled without trusting me for one second. So don't trust me. Trust the arithmetic."

She turned from Voss deliberately and faced the full gallery.

"I am the daughter of the man your history calls the first traitor. I grew up carrying that name like a stone in my chest. When I found the truth in my own vault's foundation, my first instinct — I'm ashamed to say — was to protect myself. To stay invisible. To keep broadcasting a beacon no one answered, because invisibility had kept me safe my whole life." Her voice caught, and she let it. "Nine survivors taught me otherwise. They taught me that a lie doesn't get weaker with age. It gets load-bearing. Two centuries of dead were stacked on top of what happened in that archive, and every year the stack got heavier, and everyone pretending it wasn't there called the pretense peace.

"So no, I am not here to recant. I am here to inherit. My father's name has been a weapon pointed at the truth for two hundred years. Today I take it back and point it the other way."

* * *

Cross-examination lasted two more days.

Voss was good. Gideon gave him that. He came at her from angles she hadn't prepared — the cloned beacon, the defector whose loyalties had been in question, the fact that she had vented forty years of irreplaceable genetic material to cover an escape. He tried to make the seeds the story. *A custodian who destroys her own vault,* he said, *is either a fanatic or a fabricator.*

"A custodian who destroys her own vault," Gideon answered, "is someone who finally understood what the vault was for. Seeds aren't the harvest, Attaché. They're the promise. And a promise means nothing if the lie that killed billions stands. I'd vent them again. Ask the colonies whether they'd trade the war's end for my wheat stocks. Take a vote. I'll wait."

Someone in the gallery laughed — a short, startled sound, quickly smothered, but it spread the way laughter does in solemn rooms, loosening something. Even Oyelaran's mouth moved at one corner.

The hardest hour came on the second afternoon, when Voss produced the trial transcript of Tomas Okafor and read the final statement aloud — her father's voice, transcribed, defending himself in words that had been dismissed as the ravings of a guilty man. *Check the roster. Check the relays. I never sent it. Whoever did wanted a war, and I was simply the shape the lie needed.*

Gideon had read that transcript forty times in the weeks since Ceres. It still hit her like decompression.

"Do you believe your father was innocent," Voss asked, "or do you need to believe it?"

And here was the trap, the real one, the one beneath all the others. Because the honest answer was complicated. She thought of the charter with his name on it. Of the lullabies sung off-key. Of the possibility — the one that kept her up at night — that innocence and guilt were never the right instruments for measuring what had been done to him.

"I believe the evidence," she said finally. "My father taught me that. Whatever else he was, whatever the tribunal ultimately rules about the margins of his life, the forgery was not his. That's not belief. That's the duty roster, the relay chain, and the cipher registry. Belief is what your office has been asking the system to substitute for those things for two hundred years. I decline."

* * *

On the third evening, the tribunal recessed to deliberate authentication, and Gideon walked out of the chamber into the long corridor where her family waited, and discovered that her legs had opinions about the day's work. Ilsa caught her elbow without ceremony.

"You named three directors and dared them to check your math," Ilsa said. "In front of six fleets."

"It seemed efficient."

Teo was grinning so hard it looked painful. "When you told Voss to take a vote about the seeds — the Luna delegation's aide wrote it down *twice*. I watched him."

Dario said nothing at first. He fell into step beside her, and when they reached the viewport overlooking the relay's docking arms, he stopped and looked out at the traffic — colony ships, medical transports, the first unarmed freighters to cross the old front lines in a generation.

"Hale's lawyers filed a motion an hour ago," he said quietly. "Demanding the archive be excluded as evidence. Chain-of-custody objections. They're also requesting a gag order on all witnesses pending review." He glanced at her. "It won't work. But it tells us something. Men file motions like that when the arithmetic is starting to scare them."

"Then the arithmetic is working."

"There's more." Dario hesitated — a rare thing for him now; he'd spent his defected months learning to stop hesitating. "Ndiaye's office released a statement this afternoon. Denying everything, obviously. But the denial misstated the date of the duty roster by six days. The real date was never public. Only someone who'd seen the sealed original could get it wrong that way." He let that land. "He's seen it recently, Gideon. Within the last few years. Which means somewhere in the Office of Colonial Security, someone has been keeping the original close enough to touch."

Gideon looked out at the docking arms, at the ordinary miracle of ships moving without fear, and felt the old vault-custodian instinct stir — the one that checked seals twice, that trusted nothing to have stayed closed overnight.

"Slow," she said. "Imperfect. Two hundred years of lie doesn't unravel in a week."

"No," Ilsa agreed. "But it's unraveling. I can hear it."

Behind them, down the corridor, a clerk came hurrying with a slate in her hand and a strange expression — half apology, half alarm — calling Gideon's name. The tribunal, she said, had finished early. The authentication results were in.

All of them.

And one of the seven adjudicators, the clerk added, was refusing to sign.
