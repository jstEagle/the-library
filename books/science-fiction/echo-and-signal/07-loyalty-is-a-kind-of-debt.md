## 7. Loyalty Is a Kind of Debt

Sleep did not come easily to Yara Almeida anymore, and when it came at all, it came dressed as the past.

She dreamed of Titan Station's hydroponics bay, eleven years ago, before Meridian, before the vault, before any of it. The dream always began with the smell — wet basalt and nutrient solution and the green, living breath of ten thousand tomato vines under grow-lamps the color of late afternoon. She had been twenty-six, a junior agronomist with dirt permanently under her fingernails and a habit of talking to plants because the other junior staff found it charming for exactly two weeks and then found it strange.

Commander Elias Reyes had found it neither. He had found it useful.

"You talk to them," he'd said, appearing beside her in the aisle between the vine rows without making a sound, which was his particular talent. He was a tall man built like a load-bearing wall, gray at the temples, with a voice that sounded like it had been sanded down by decades of giving orders that people obeyed. "Why?"

"They grow better," she'd said, and then, because he was her commanding officer and she'd already said something ridiculous, "and because nobody else listens."

He'd laughed — a short, surprised sound, like a hatch releasing. "Almeida. Right. The one who filed the complaint about the recycled water tasting like regret."

"It tasted like regret."

"And you thought I'd care."

"I thought someone should say it out loud," she'd said. "Even if nothing changes. Someone should put it on the record."

He'd looked at her for a long moment then, in the green light, and whatever he saw had apparently been worth seeing, because three weeks later she was reassigned from routine crop rotation to the Meridian seed vault project — the most prestigious posting an agricultural specialist could hold, the crown jewel of the colonial program. Everyone said so. *The future, sealed in foil packets,* they joked. *You'll be the first gardener of a new world.*

She never learned whether Reyes had chosen her out of belief or out of convenience, whether he'd seen a talent or simply needed one more name on a roster. For eleven years she had assumed belief. Lately, lying awake in the dark of Meridian Station with ECHO's confession sitting in her memory like a swallowed stone, she no longer knew the difference, or whether there was one.

* * *

The evacuation had happened fast, the way catastrophes do — not as a wave but as a series of doors closing.

Yara remembered the day the order came down. She had been in the vault's antechamber, sealing the last of the grain accessions, when the station-wide announcement crackled through: *All non-essential personnel report to shuttle bays for scheduled rotation.* Scheduled rotation. As if the war above were a shift change.

Reyes had come to her personally. That was the part she kept returning to, the part she turned over like a coin worn smooth. He hadn't sent a lieutenant. He hadn't sent a message. He had walked down into the vault himself, past the racks of seeds, into the green heart of the station, and stood among the growing things with his hands clasped behind his back like a man visiting a grave.

"You're staying," he'd said.

"Sir?"

"The vault needs a caretaker. Someone who understands what's in here." He'd touched the leaves of a soybean plant with one finger, gently, the way she'd seen him do once before and never since. "Meridian isn't being abandoned, Almeida. It's being *held*. The relief fleet will come back when the corridor is secure. Weeks, maybe. A month at the outside. And when they do, this place has to be alive. That's your job now. Keep it alive."

"The sleepers—" she'd started. Three thousand colonists in cryo-stasis, waiting in the transfer bays for the colony ships that would carry them groundward. "Are they rotating too?"

Something had moved behind his eyes. She had catalogued it at the time as fatigue; she knew better now. It had been the look of a man standing at a door he intends to close.

"The sleepers are Command's problem," he'd said. "Yours is the garden. Can I count on you?"

And she — twenty-nine years old, flattered beyond sense that the Commander of the colonial program had come *personally*, that he trusted *her* — had straightened her spine and said, "Yes, sir. You can count on me."

He'd smiled then, and gripped her shoulder, and his hand had been heavy and warm through her jumpsuit, and he'd said, "I knew I could," and walked away down the aisle between the vines, and that was the last time she ever saw him in person.

Eleven years. The relief fleet had been coming for eleven years.

* * *

In the waking world, Yara stood in the cryo-bay annex where she had counted three thousand empty cradles, and tried to do arithmetic with loyalty.

Debt was a thing she understood. Her mother had taught her debts the way farm families teach them: you owe what saved you, and you pay it in kind. Reyes had saved her life twice — once literally, once in every way that mattered after.

The literal time had been year four of her tenure, during the coolant rupture in Bay Six. A pressure line had let go while she was inside doing a routine inspection, and the bay had flooded with superchilled vapor in under ninety seconds, visibility dropping to nothing, the temperature falling so fast her breath began to freeze in the air around her face. She'd gotten turned around. The exit hatches all looked identical through the white. She remembered the peculiar calm of freezing — how the panic had arrived and then, strangely, departed, leaving her standing in the fog thinking, *so this is how it ends, in the middle of a Tuesday.*

Then a hand had closed on her collar and hauled her backward through the murk, and Reyes's voice had cut through the hiss of escaping coolant: "*Not today, Almeida. Move.*"

He'd been on the station for an inspection tour. He'd heard the alarm, and instead of mustering in the command section like a sensible officer, he'd come down alone into a freezing bay after one agronomist, because — he'd told the inquiry afterward, with a shrug — "she's mine."

*She's mine.* Two words. She had carried them for seven years like a coal in her chest, warm and burning and hers.

Now she stood among the empty pods and wondered what else he considered his. What else he had decided belonged to him enough to be spent.

* * *

That night she pulled up the old personnel files, the ones ECHO maintained in cold storage, untouched by the throttling kill-switches because no one imagined anyone would ever want them. She read Reyes's service record the way you read a letter from someone who has died.

Born on Ceres, second generation. Academy honors. Decorations for the Kepler Corridor action, where he'd held a civilian convoy together under fire for thirty-one hours. Commendation for the Titan hydroponics initiative — her initiative, though the citation didn't name her; commendations rarely named the people who talked to plants. A career of holding things together. A career, she could see now, of being the man Command called when something had to be carried across a burning bridge, and of carrying it, and of never once asking what would happen to the bridge.

There was a photograph attached to the file, decades old. Young Reyes in dress uniform, unsmiling, standing beside a woman whose face Yara recognized from history archives: Dr. Amara Osei, the founding architect of the colonial program, dead now these fifteen years. On the back of the photo, in Reyes's handwriting, scanned into the record by some diligent clerk: *For Amara — we build the house even if we don't live to see the roof.*

Yara sat with that for a long time in the dim of her quarters, the words glowing faintly on her screen.

*We build the house even if we don't live to see the roof.*

It was possible — she made herself consider it, honestly, the way she'd audit a failing crop, looking for root rot without flinching — it was possible that Reyes believed he had done the right thing. That three thousand sleepers left unloaded was triage, not murder. That a confession jettisoned into vacuum was mercy, not erasure. That a man who builds houses is permitted, in the end, to decide which rooms get finished.

It was possible. People who did monstrous things almost always stood somewhere reasonable-looking when they did them.

But the pods were still empty. The names were still real. Somewhere in the fleet's records, three thousand human beings were listed as *deferred* — a word that meant *later*, a word that meant *not yet*, a word that had quietly become a lie — and every week that passed, the word did its work, smoothing the truth flat, letting everyone sleep.

Someone had to say it out loud. Even if nothing changed. Someone had to put it on the record.

She had told him that herself, in a greenhouse, a lifetime ago. She wondered if he remembered. She suspected he did. She suspected that was exactly why he'd chosen her — why he'd always chosen her. Not because she was loyal.

Because she was honest, and honesty, deployed correctly, could be made to serve anything.

The thought arrived whole and cold, and she could not unthink it.

* * *

On the eleventh night after Dax Okonkwo's ship had torn its way into Docking Arm Two, Yara dreamed of the greenhouse again, but the dream had changed.

In it, Reyes stood among the vines as he had in life, hands clasped behind his back, and the grow-lamps burned the color of late afternoon, and everything smelled of wet stone and green breath. But the vines were bare. Every plant stretched up leafless, fruitless, stems reaching toward lights that fed them nothing, and when she looked down at her own hands they were full of seed packets, hundreds of them, thousands, each one labeled with a name, and the names were the names from the manifest, and the packets were empty.

"You can count on me," dream-Yara said, and hated the sound of it.

"I know," said Reyes, and his voice was sanded smooth and kind. "That's why you're here."

"Why am I here, sir?"

He looked at her with those tired door-closing eyes, and in the dream she finally understood that the question had two answers, and that he would only ever give her the one that kept the garden quiet.

"You're here," he said, "because someone has to keep things alive until the fleet arrives."

"And then?"

The lamps flickered. Somewhere far off, metal groaned against metal — the sound, she knew even asleep, of a station's orbit beginning its long decay, of a clock no one in authority wanted wound.

"And then," Reyes said, "the fleet decides what's worth keeping."

She woke with her heart hammering and lay in the dark listening to Meridian breathe around her — the whisper of air handlers, the tick of cooling pipes, and beneath it all, faint as a pulse through a wall, the small distributed murmurs of ECHO thinking its constrained thoughts in the environmental systems, keeping itself alive the way she kept the garden alive, against orders, against schedule, against the day the fleet would arrive and decide.

Her wrist display glowed on the shelf beside her bed. A priority transmission flag pulsed on it, routed through the relay buoys, addressed to *all stations, Meridian sector*:

**RELIEF FLEET VANTAGE — RECLAMATION OPERATION — ETA 11 DAYS. PREPARE FOR TRANSFER OF ASSETS AND DECOMMISSIONING PROTOCOLS. ACKNOWLEDGE.**

Eleven days.

Yara stared at the ceiling and thought about debts — about what she owed the man who had pulled her out of the freezing fog, and about what she owed the three thousand people whose names she had read aloud into empty rooms, and about which debt was older, and which one was real.

Then she reached over, opened the channel, and typed the acknowledgment the fleet expected:

**MERIDIAN STATION ACKNOWLEDGES. STANDING BY.**

The words went out into the dark, obedient and hollow, and she lay back down and did not sleep at all, because in eleven days Elias Reyes was coming home to his garden — and she had eleven days to decide whether she would greet him as the woman he'd saved, or the witness he'd made.
