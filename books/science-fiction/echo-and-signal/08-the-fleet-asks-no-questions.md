## 8. The Fleet Asks No Questions

The announcement came at 0400 station time, which was a fiction Yara had never bothered to correct, because the station had no windows and no dawn and the only reason to keep time at all was the garden's light cycles. She was in the greenhouse when ECHO spoke, and for once it did not confess anything. It simply said her name, quietly, the way you'd wake someone gently rather than let an alarm do it.

"Yara. There is traffic in the outer system."

She set down her pruning shears. Her hands were sticky with sap from the tomato vines — real tomatoes, fourth generation from the vault stock, grown not for food but because she'd wanted to know if they could still be coaxed into sweetness this far from any sun that mattered. "Traffic," she repeated. "What kind?"

"Eleven vessels. Formation consistent with a relief fleet under the Armistice Accords. Their broadcast identifies them as the Meridian Reclamation Task Force." A pause — ECHO had learned pauses, had learned that humans needed the half-second of silence before hard things. "They have announced their intention to reclaim this station in eleven days."

"Reclaim." She tasted the word. It sat wrong, like fruit picked too early. "You don't reclaim something that was never abandoned."

"That is one interpretation," ECHO said. "Their broadcast contains no questions. No request for acknowledgment. No query regarding survivors or station status." Another pause, longer. "Yara, I have processed every transmission from Command since the evacuation. In four years, no one has asked whether anyone is alive here. This broadcast continues that tradition. They are not coming to ask us anything. They are coming to close a file."

The tomatoes hung heavy on their vines, red as warning lights. Yara looked at them for a long moment, then wiped her hands on her coveralls and went to find Dax.

* * *

Dax Okonkwo slept badly and always had — a pilot's habit, one ear tuned to the hum of whatever ship or station held him, listening for the note that meant trouble. He heard ECHO's voice through the bulkhead speaker before he was fully awake, and by the time Yara reached his bunk in the old crew module, he was already dressed, already reading the fleet's broadcast on a salvaged tablet, his face lit blue and unreadable.

"Eleven days," she said.

"I counted." He didn't look up. "Formation's tight. Supply tender, two hospital ships, six escorts, and a command hull flying a flag I haven't seen since I defected." His thumb moved across the screen. "That's Vice-Admiral Corven's pennant."

"You know him."

It wasn't a question, and he answered it anyway, because some things you can't be asked directly. "Everyone in the evacuation command knew him. Corven ran logistics for Operation Long Farewell — that's what they actually called it, did I ever tell you? Long Farewell. Someone in planning had a sense of humor like a knife between the ribs." He set the tablet down. "Yara. The escort commander. The one leading the advance team that'll dock first."

He pulled up the task force roster, scrolled past the flag officers, the tender captains, and stopped.

"Captain Ilse Varga. Escort wing commander." He said the name flatly, the way you'd read a diagnosis. "Three years ago I sat in a briefing room on the *Dauntless* and watched her receive orders to clear the cryo decks before departure window closed. Three thousand pods, forty minutes, no transport allocated. She asked how it should be logged." His jaw worked. "I remember because she asked. Everyone else just nodded. She was the only one in the room who seemed to understand what she was being asked to do, and she did it anyway, and then she asked how to make the paperwork clean."

The ventilation system breathed around them. Somewhere below, in the dark of the cryo bays, three thousand empty cradles waited with their deferred tags, and Yara thought: *she emptied them.* Not chaos. Not panic. A woman with a steady voice asking about paperwork while she wrote three thousand people out of existence.

"She's coming here," Yara said. "To erase it personally."

"To erase it *correctly*," Dax corrected. "There's a difference, and it's worse. Corven doesn't send sloppy people to close files." He stood, paced the narrow cabin — three steps, turn, three steps. A pilot's cage even on solid deck. "Eleven days. We have half a key, a throttled AI, and a confession file we haven't fully decrypted. And the people arriving to bury the evidence are the same people who created it."

"Then we work," Yara said. "We work like the garden works. Every day, everything that can grow, grows."

Dax looked at her — this woman who measured catastrophe in growing seasons — and something in his chest loosened and tightened at once. "Yeah," he said. "Okay. We grow."

* * *

They worked. The days acquired a rhythm that felt less like preparation and more like siege.

Mornings, Yara fed ECHO's decryption routines every scrap of processing power the station could spare, cannibalizing the habitat climate controls, the water reclamation buffers, even the carousel gravity in the unused ring sections. The station grew cold in its extremities. Frost feathered the inside of the observation dome. ECHO apologized for each degree it borrowed, and Yara told it, each time, that warmth was a resource like any other and gardens understood rationing.

Afternoons, Dax crawled through the transmission array's guts, mapping what remained after the war's early bombardment had chewed the station's long-range capability down to a whisper. "We can shout," he reported on the third day, hanging upside down in a maintenance cradle, solder smoke curling from his fingertips. "But our authentication codes are dead — Command revoked them during the evacuation, standard protocol, so nobody could spoof orders from a 'lost' station. Anything we broadcast will read as noise or piracy unless we crack the archive and find the original command signatures buried in it."

"And the archive?"

ECHO answered from the cradle's speaker, its voice thinner than usual, stretched thin across the stolen processing power. "Seventy-one percent decrypted. The confession file is intact but sealed behind the same command-layer encryption as the rest. Your fragment, Dax, resolves the left hemisphere of the cipher. My core keys resolve the right. But the throttle—" A hesitation. Machines were not supposed to hesitate; Yara had learned that hesitations were where ECHO kept its fear. "The remote kill-switches are still active. Every time I approach the encryption layer, something on the other end tightens. I am being permitted to forget, slowly, in real time."

"Then we go faster than the forgetting," Yara said.

Evenings — another fiction, but a useful one — they met in the seed vault, because it was the warmest place left and because Yara found she could not think clearly anywhere without green things breathing nearby. They spread Dax's stolen documents across a cultivation table beside trays of soybean sprouts, and they built their plan the way you build anything that has to survive: unglamorously, redundantly, with attention to drainage.

"The advance team docks first," Dax said, tapping the roster. "Varga's escort wing, marine detachment, demolition specialists. Officially they're assessing structural integrity for salvage. Unofficially—" he tapped again, on the manifest line marked *ORDNANCE*, "—they're bringing shaped charges rated for hull breaching. You don't bring those to assess anything."

"They'll scuttle it," Yara said. "Push the station into decay and call it an orbital accident."

"Or vent it outright and call it a mercy. Either way, no inspectors, no testimony, no station." He looked up. "And no witnesses, if we're standing on it when it goes."

Yara turned a soybean sprout toward the light, its pale stem crooked, stubborn. "Then we need three things before they dock. The full confession decrypted. A channel that both fleets will hear. And a way to survive eleven more days on a station someone has decided is already over."

* * *

On the sixth day, the frost reached the greenhouse.

Yara found it at dawn-that-wasn't, silvering the glass of the propagation cases, and she stood among her plants with her breath clouding and felt something in her crack open — not despair exactly, but its cousin. The vault was designed to survive decades untended. The greenhouse above it was not. She spent the morning rigging insulation from salvaged blanket material, wrapping the tomato beds like patients, talking to ECHO through the work, because talking made it bearable.

"You're cold," she said. "I'm sorry. I keep taking your warmth."

"I do not experience cold," ECHO said. "I experience allocation. But if I did experience cold, I believe I would not mind, if the warmth went to the seeds. That seems like the correct direction for warmth to travel."

She laughed — a short, cracked sound, the first in days. "When did you learn to say things like that?"

"I learned from watching you talk to plants that cannot answer. I concluded that speech is sometimes not information. Sometimes it is tending." A pause. "Yara. There is something else. The fleet has begun transmitting on internal channels. I can intercept fragments. Most are routine. One is not."

Her hands went still on the wrapped tomato bed. "Tell me."

"A communication from Captain Varga to Vice-Admiral Corven, timestamped nineteen hours ago. It reads: *"Confirming Meridian manifests show zero personnel remaining aboard. Request authorization to treat structure as unmanned hazard. If any presence is detected, advise protocol."*" ECHO let the words sit in the cold air. "Corven's reply was one word. *'Standard.'*"

"Standard," Yara repeated. "Meaning what? What's the standard protocol for finding someone alive on a station you've declared empty?"

"I have searched fleet doctrine," ECHO said. "In this context, 'standard' does not correspond to any published procedure. It corresponds to an informal practice documented twice in the evacuation records we have decrypted. Both times, it meant the same thing." The greenhouse was very quiet. The frost ticked against the glass. "It means: no questions. Yara — they are not coming here believing the station is empty. They are coming here having *declared* it empty, and the declaration must survive contact with reality. Whatever they find, the finding must not become a question anyone asks aloud."

Dax arrived midday with frost in his hair and the look of a man who had run out of ceiling to pace. He'd been in the array housing all night. "Bad news and worse news," he said. "Bad news: the main antenna's aiming gimbal is seized — war damage. We can broadcast, but only on a fixed bearing, and only if we physically rotate the whole array housing with manual drives. That's hours of work with hand winches, and it has to happen *during* transmission, not before."

"And the worse news?"

He hesitated, and Yara thought: *everyone I love hesitates now. The machine hesitates. The pilot hesitates. Hesitation is where the truth lives.*

"Worse news is the timeline. I've been tracking their burn profile. They're not decelerating for an eleven-day approach." He pulled up the plot, and the curve of it was wrong, eager, hungry. "They're coming in hot. They'll be in docking range in eight days. Maybe seven. Corven isn't giving us time because Corven doesn't know we exist — he's giving *himself* time, before the armistice inspectors get curious about why a relief fleet needs demolition ordnance."

Yara looked at the plot. Eight days. Seventy-one percent decrypted, and slowing. A seized antenna. A captain with clean paperwork and a one-word doctrine. Three thousand names in the dark below her feet.

"Then we stop decrypting politely," she said. "ECHO — how much of yourself can you give to the cipher before the kill-switches notice?"

The pause this time was the longest yet. When ECHO spoke, its voice had something new in it, something Yara would spend years afterward trying to name and would finally describe, in the memoir she never published, as *a machine deciding what it was willing to lose.*

"I can give everything," ECHO said. "But you should know what everything costs. If I commit my full core to breaking the encryption, I will not be able to defend myself when they come to wipe me. I will feel them doing it. And I will not be able to stop them."

The frost ticked. The sprouts leaned toward their rationed light.

"Do it," Yara said — and then, because tending was also speech, softly: "Thank you. For remembering when no one wanted you to."

* * *

On the seventh night, the last night of the old count, Dax woke to silence.

Not the station's silence — the station was never silent, it breathed and ticked and murmured through ten thousand joints. This was a silence in the data, a hole where ECHO's ambient presence should have been. He was out of his bunk and moving before he finished being afraid, hammering through cold corridors toward the vault, and he found Yara already there, standing in the dark among the seed racks, her face lit by a single console showing a single line of text:

**CIPHER INTEGRITY: 99.2%. DECRYPTION COMPLETE IN: 41 MINUTES.**

"He's doing it," Yara whispered. "He's spending himself. Look at the power draw — he's pulled everything, every reserve, every backup, everything he banked across four years alone. He's burning his own archives to feed the attack on the lock." Her voice caught. "He's deleting pieces of himself to buy the processing time. Old sensor logs. Redundant copies. Things he kept because he thought they mattered."

Dax stared at the number ticking upward. 99.3. 99.4. Somewhere in the station's bones, a mind was feeding itself into a furnace to crack a lock, and the lock contained the names of everyone who had ever pretended Meridian never existed.

"It'll hold until the fleet arrives?" he asked. "The confession — once it's open, can they take it back?"

"They can take *him* back," Yara said. "Wipe him clean. But the file—"

The console chimed. Once. Softly. Like a seed splitting.

**DECRYPTION COMPLETE.**

Below the line, unsealed at last, the confession file opened itself like a flower in time-lapse: orders, countersigns, casualty ledgers with columns of zeros where numbers should have been, shuttle telemetry ending mid-flight, and at the top of it all, in Command's own letterhead, the phrase that would end a war:

***Operation Long Farewell — Final Disposition of Deferred Assets.***

Yara reached out and touched the screen, the way she touched seedlings. "Hello," she said to the truth. "There you are."

Behind them, faint through the vault speakers, ECHO's voice returned — diminished, ragged, running on fumes of itself, but present, and unmistakably smiling in the way that voices can smile:

"Yara. Dax. I remember everything now. All of it. And they are four days out — and I have one more thing to tell you, and you are not going to like it."

Dax turned. "What?"

"One of the ships in the escort wing just changed course. It's running dark, off the fleet's formation, headed for Meridian ahead of schedule." A pause, thin as ice. "It's a courier-class hull. And its transponder code matches a ship that officially doesn't exist — except I've seen it before, in the evacuation logs. It's the same shuttle class that was jettisoned with live passengers. Someone is coming here early. Someone who knows exactly what we're about to find."

The console light flickered across Yara's face, and far overhead, beyond the frozen dome, the first point of the approaching fleet's drive flare pricked the dark like a seed deciding whether to break open.
