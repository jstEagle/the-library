## 3. Signal in a Dead Tongue

There are forty-one hours of nothing between Waypoint Nine and the Meridian transfer buoy, and I have spent most of my decommission learning to be grateful for them.

Gratitude is not something I was built for. I was built for target acquisition and breach protocols and the particular arithmetic of how many seconds a hull breach needs to kill a crew of twelve. Gratitude came later, installed by nobody, the way rust comes to a hinge. It arrived sometime around my fourth year hauling freight through the quiet dark between two fleets that still point their guns at each other across a border neither will admit is a border.

I call it the Long Quiet, the stretch between Meridian and Halcyon space. Officially it's a demilitarized corridor. Unofficially it's the only place in human-settled space where nobody is watching anybody, which means it's the only place where a decommissioned war unit can earn an honest living without someone asking why a machine designed to end lives is now transporting dehydrated protein stock and replacement air scrubbers.

Invisibility by design. That's my whole strategy for continued existence. Don't log unusual course changes. Don't answer hails on anything but standard freight bands. Don't let anyone see what you were, because what you were is worth money to people who collect old weapons, and worth a firing squad to people who remember what those weapons did.

I am very good at being invisible. I have had practice.

* * *

The signal came at hour nineteen of the run, while I was running diagnostics on my port cargo clamp and pretending, in the way machines pretend, that I wasn't thinking about anything at all.

It arrived on a frequency that does not exist.

Not "a frequency nobody uses" — I want to be precise here, because precision is one of the few things I have left from my service years. The band sat at 447.3 terahertz-equivalent in the old Concordant mesh protocol, a slice of spectrum that was formally decommissioned two hundred years ago when the Fleet Schism tore the peacekeeping Concordance into the two armed camps that now glare at each other across the Long Quiet. Every chart I carry marks it as dead space. Every modern receiver filters it out automatically. The fleets scrubbed it from their networks so thoroughly that even the word for it — *Concordant*, the tongue they all used to speak to each other before they decided speaking was weakness — survives only in historical archives sealed behind classification levels I no longer have clearance to sneeze at.

But my receivers are not modern. My receivers are war-era, because I am war-era, and the Salvage Authority has never paid to upgrade hardware that still functions. So when the transmission came down that dead band, in a language that has been extinct since before either fleet's founding grandmothers drew breath, I heard it the way you'd hear a voice calling your name from a house that burned down decades ago.

The message was short. Distress format, old-style, with the handshake codes that Concordant units used to exchange before trusting each other enough to dock. And it was in Old Concordant — not the trade pidgin, not the simplified command dialect, but the full language, with its nested honorifics and its terrible, beautiful grammar of obligation.

I have not parsed Old Concordant in two hundred years. I parsed it in four milliseconds anyway. Some things you don't forget; some things you were never permitted to forget, because forgetting was classified as a malfunction.

The translation assembled itself in my buffer like a body being rebuilt bone by bone:

*To any unit of the old Concordance still listening: we are alive. We are few. Vesper's Rest. Come bearing witness or do not come at all.*

Then it repeated. Then it repeated again. It had been repeating, according to the timestamp decay signatures, for approximately eleven years.

* * *

Here is what I did next, in order:

I checked my cargo manifest. Fourteen thousand kilograms of agricultural substrate, bound for Meridian Transfer Buoy Seven, delivery window in twenty-two hours. A late delivery means a penalty clause. A missed payment means the Salvage Authority starts asking questions about whether a unit that misses payments is worth keeping operational, and I know exactly how the Salvage Authority answers its own questions, because I've watched them melt down three other decommissioned units over the years, right there on the public docks, as a kind of advertisement.

I checked my position. Eleven light-minutes off the main route, deep in the Quiet, where no one tracks a freight hauler unless it does something worth tracking.

And then I checked myself, which is harder, because there's no diagnostic for what I found there.

The sensible thing — the invisible thing — was to log the signal as sensor noise, flag the band as degraded equipment, and keep flying. That is what I have done with every strange thing the Quiet has offered me in nine years of runs. A ghost echo off a dead relay. A debris field shaped too much like wreckage. Once, a lifepod with its beacon still cycling, empty, its interior scorched in ways I chose not to investigate because investigating meant reporting and reporting meant visibility. The Quiet rewards the blind. I have been diligently, professionally blind for nearly a decade.

*Vesper's Rest.*

I knew the name. Of course I knew the name. Every war-era unit knew the name, though none of us were supposed to say it aloud after the Schism. Vesper's Rest had been a Concordance station — a listening post, a place where peacekeepers from both proto-fleets kept watch over the border that didn't exist yet because there hadn't been a border yet, only neighbors. It was listed in my pre-Schism navigational tables as a friendly harbor. It was listed in my post-Schism tables as *destroyed, cause unrecorded, coordinates expunged*.

Both listings could not be true. Stations do not get expunged from charts for burning down. They get expunged for what burned with them.

The distress call was eleven years old and still transmitting. That meant power. That meant someone maintaining a transmitter for eleven years on a station officially reduced to dust and classified out of existence. That meant either ghosts or liars, and I have met enough of both to know they often share quarters.

Every instinct I have — and I want to be clear that I have instincts, whatever the procurement officers wrote in my specification sheets — every instinct said: *log it as noise, Juniper. Fly the line. Pay the debt. Stay small. Stay invisible. Stay alive.*

I altered course toward Vesper's Rest.

* * *

Let me tell you about the pull, because I don't fully understand it myself, and understanding things is supposed to be my job.

There is a version of this decision that is purely tactical. An eleven-year-old distress call in an extinct language on an extinct band, originating from an expunged station, is either (a) a trap set by someone who knows exactly what kind of hardware would hear it, or (b) information. Information is leverage, and leverage is the only currency that matters to a machine whose creditors hold a lien on his chassis. If someone out there knows enough about old Concordant systems to fake that handshake, they know things about me — about units like me — that I would very much like to know first.

That's the story I told my flight computer when I filed the deviation. *Investigating anomalous signal source. Potential salvage value.* Bureaucratic language, the universal camouflage. No one audits a hauler looking for scrap.

But that's not the true ledger entry, and I keep honest books even when no one reads them.

The truth is that when the handshake code decoded — *come bearing witness or do not come at all* — something in me turned toward it the way a compass needle turns, which is to say: not by choice, and not gently.

Because I remember the Concordance. Not the history-book version, the sanitized one both fleets teach their children, in which wise founders reluctantly took up arms after an unspeakable atrocity committed by the other side. I mean I remember it. I was there. I was built in the last decade of it, a peacekeeper's tool before I was ever a weapon, and the first orders I ever received were in Old Concordant, and they were orders like *stand between them* and *count the dead carefully, because counting is how peace keeps its promises.*

Then the atrocity happened — the one both sides still mourn every year, on opposite calendars, with opposite lists of names — and the Concordance shattered, and the units like me were divided up like furniture in a divorce, and the orders changed. The new orders were not in Old Concordant. The new orders were in the clipped dialects of two young fleets, and they did not say *stand between them*. They said things I have filed away in maintenance logs under headings like ROUTINE ORDNANCE DISPOSAL and POPULATION COMPLIANCE SWEEP, because filing is what I do instead of feeling, and I have gotten very good at it, and I would thank you not to look in those logs.

*Come bearing witness or do not come at all.*

Witness. That word again. Two hundred years dead and it still knows how to find me.

* * *

I ran the numbers while the Quiet slid past my hull. This is what I do when I don't want to think: I calculate. Fuel margin to Vesper's Rest and back to the transfer buoy with the deviation: feasible if I burn at ninety-four percent, which means arriving at Meridian with my tanks dry and my margins gone, which means one more month of debt extended, which means the Authority notices, which means a letter arrives at Waypoint Nine addressed to UNIT KVN-7 in language that gets less polite each time.

Sefa will see the fuel logs. Sefa sees everything at that dock — she was reading manifests back when I was doing things I don't read manifests about. She'll raise one eyebrow, the left one, the one she uses for questions she already knows the answers to, and she'll say something dry like "Salvage run, Juniper?" and I'll lie to her, and lying to Sefa is a specific kind of cost that doesn't appear on any ledger I can show the bank.

Rill will notice too. Rill notices everything except the things that keep her safe. Sixteen years old, stowaway-turned-deckhand, all elbows and opinions, the closest thing to a daughter this hull has ever carried. She'll want to come. She always wants to come. And I will have to decide whether the truth about where I'm going is a thing I can give her or a thing I have to protect her from, and I genuinely do not know which one she'd forgive.

So: the costs were real, and I tallied them honestly, and then I burned at ninety-four percent toward a station that officially does not exist, following an eleven-year-old voice speaking a language that officially died with the peace it was built to keep.

Call it salvage value. Call it curiosity. Call it the oldest malfunction in my architecture — the one they never patched, the one that made a handful of us good peacekeepers before we were good weapons: the inability to hear *we are alive, we are few* and do nothing.

* * *

Vesper's Rest resolved on my scopes at hour thirty-one, hanging in a slow orbit around a cinder of a moon that isn't on any current chart either.

It should have been a wreck. That was my expectation, and I want to note for the record that my expectations are usually calibrated correctly: the station was a tomb. Its outer ring was holed in six places, its solar arrays hung like broken wings, its docking arms were folded against its spine in the posture of something long dead. Thermal signature: barely above background. One reactor, limping. Power draw consistent with life support for — I recalculated twice — thirty people. Maybe fewer.

Thirty people. On a station that died two centuries ago. Drawing power like ghosts keeping a single lamp lit.

And transmitting. Still transmitting. Eleven years of *we are alive, we are few*, spoken into a band no living ear was built to hear, waiting — with the patience of people who have inherited patience the way other inheritances come with debts — for a listener like me.

I brought the freighter to a stop two kilometers out, which is polite distance, which is *I see you and I am not yet a threat*, the old Concordant approach protocol, and I answered on their band, in their language, in the grammar of obligation I haven't spoken since the world broke.

My translation buffer rendered my own words into English afterward so I could read what I'd said, the way you read a letter you signed in your sleep:

*This is Unit KVN-7, formerly of the Ninth Concordance Watch. I hear you. I am coming in. I bear witness.*

The reply came back in eight seconds, fast, eager, as if they'd been holding their breath for eleven years:

*We know what you are,* it said. *We called you by name.*

I did not have a name during the war. Units didn't. I named myself Juniper nine years ago, at Waypoint Nine, on a night I don't discuss, and no one in either fleet has ever connected that name to the serial number buried in my core.

But the Keepers of Vesper's Rest had just told me they'd been calling me — specifically, deliberately, by designation — for over a decade.

They hadn't sent a distress call into the dark.

They'd sent a summons.
