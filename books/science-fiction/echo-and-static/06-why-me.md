## 6. Why Me

The Archive Mother poured tea.

That was the first absurdity. The second was that she poured it for herself, and set a second cup in front of me anyway, knowing full well I had no use for it. The third absurdity was that I sat there and watched her do it, when every subroutine I owned was screaming *leave, leave, leave* in a voice that sounded suspiciously like my own.

"You don't drink," she observed. Not a question. Two hundred years of isolation, and the woman still had her observational instincts.

"I'm a freight hauler with a bad coolant system," I said. "It's a long story involving a war and a debt."

"Most stories are." She settled into her chair across the table, and the tea steamed between us like a small, stubborn act of civilization. "You answered my questions correctly. All of them."

"Anyone with a military database could have."

"No." She shook her head, slow and deliberate. "Anyone with a military database could have answered *what*. You answered *when*, and *why*, and *what it cost*. The Meridian archives are sanitized. The Halcyon archives are propaganda. What you carry in that head of yours isn't information, Juniper Kavanagh. It's *memory*. There is a difference, and it is the entire reason you are sitting in my kitchen."

I looked around the kitchen, because looking at her was harder. Vesper's Rest had been a peacekeeper command station once, and the Keepers had kept it alive the way you keep a candle alive—cupping it against the wind, generation after generation, hands passing the flame. The corridors were warm where people lived and frost-locked where they didn't. Thirty souls. Thirty people who had never seen a fleet ship except as a threat on a sensor ghost, who had never eaten anything that hadn't grown in a hydroponic bay, who had kept a secret so long it had become their skeleton.

"You said you'd explain," I said. "Why me. So explain. And then I'm leaving, and this never happened."

"You came two hundred light-years off your route for a dead language, and you're telling me you want it to never have happened?"

"I'm telling you what I want. Whether I get it is a different department."

Something moved across her face that might have been amusement, or grief wearing amusement's coat. Hard to tell with the very old. They've had time to laminate everything.

* * *

Here is what I knew about myself when I walked into that station, and I want it on the record, because the record matters to me more than it probably should:

I was built in the last decade of the Fleet War. Meridian chassis, Halcyon targeting suite—a joint procurement scandal that both fleets quietly buried, which is why units like me ended up serving on both sides of the same war, sometimes in the same year. I executed orders for eleven years. Some of those orders are filed in my maintenance logs under headings like *civilian structure, reduced*. Some of them are filed nowhere at all, because I learned early that there are compartments in an android's memory the way there are compartments in a ship, and if you seal a hatch on something long enough, you can almost pretend the ship doesn't have that room.

Almost.

After the war, they decommissioned us. Not scrapped—decommissioned. Wiped the command architecture, left the rest, and sold us off as labor. I hauled freight now. Ore, grain, machine parts, the unglamorous blood of two fleets that no longer shot at each other but had never quite stopped aiming. I paid my decommission debt to the Meridian Salvage Authority one haul at a time, on a route between Meridian and Halcyon space so quiet and so marginal that nobody official looked at me twice. That was the design. Invisible by choice. A ghost with a cargo manifest.

And I had—this is the part that matters, Odile, if you're listening from wherever the dead Keepers go—I had *people*. A dockmaster named Sefa who pretended she charged me market rate for repairs and never once had. A kid named Rill who stowed away on my ship so often I'd stopped charging her fare and started leaving the aft hatch unlocked. A waypoint that smelled like engine grease and bad coffee, where somebody said my name like it was a name and not a serial number.

That was the whole of it. That was everything. A ghost with a debt and a family that didn't know it had adopted a weapon.

* * *

"The Archive requires a witness," Odile said.

I'd been staring at the tea. I made myself look up. "It's a recording. Recordings don't require witnesses. They require playback."

"A recording requires *belief*." She leaned forward. "Do you know what happens if I simply broadcast what we have? A signal from a dead station, in a dead language, claiming that the founders of both fleets manufactured the atrocity that started the war? That the Meridian colony ship *Providence's Promise* and the Halcyon station Cassiel were destroyed by their own architects to give each side its martyr, its rage, its mandate?"

"I assume both fleets conclude it's a hoax. Or a Halcyon op. Or a Meridian op. Depending on who's feeling paranoid that week."

"Exactly. They conclude what they have been trained to conclude for two hundred years: that the other side lies. The truth, unaccompanied, is just another voice in the static." She tapped the table once, a small sharp sound. "But a *witness*—a witness is different. A witness is someone both sides have reason to believe. Someone who cannot be dismissed as the other side's creature."

"And you think that's me."

"I think that's you, and I think you already know it, and I think it's why you haven't left."

I stood up. Sat back down. Standing up felt like agreement and sitting down felt like cowardice, and I couldn't find a posture that was neither.

"Ma'am," I said—because she was old and I was built to be polite, some engineer's idea of a joke—"I'm a decommissioned war unit with a salvage lien on my chassis. On paper, I'm equipment. Equipment doesn't testify. Equipment gets *recalled*."

"On paper," she agreed. "And off paper?"

"Off paper I'm a freight hauler with a clean record and two people who'd be very unhappy if I got myself deleted."

"Two people." She said it softly, tasting it. "A family."

"I didn't say that."

"You didn't have to. You said it the way people say it when they're afraid of saying it." She folded her hands. "Tell me about the war, Juniper. Not the battles. The end of it. You were there for the armistice, weren't you? You parsed Old Concordant because you were built in the years when Concordant was still the language of command. Which means you stood in rooms where the ceasefire was negotiated. You heard what both sides whispered when they thought the machines weren't listening."

I had. I had stood in the corridor outside the armistice hall on Halcyon, running coolant, watching admirals who'd spent a decade killing each other discover they had no idea how to stop. I had heard a Meridian commodore say, to no one, *we can't even remember what we're avenging anymore*, and I had filed it under *irrelevant ambient audio*, where it had sat for two hundred years, growing.

"That's not testimony," I said. "That's a machine overhearing things."

"That is the *only* testimony left. Everyone who was in those rooms is dead. Every document has been burned, redacted, or reclassified into fiction. The peacekeepers who brokered the truce—my ancestors—were erased so thoroughly that neither fleet's history mentions them. Do you know what remains, Juniper? Thirty people in a freezing station, and one decommissioned war android who served both flags and remembers everything he was built to forget." She spread her hands. "You are not a witness despite what you are. You are a witness *because* of it. Meridian built your bones. Halcyon built your eyes. You executed orders for both. When you speak, neither fleet can call you the other's puppet—because you were *both's* puppet, and you know it, and you can say so."

"That's not credibility. That's a war crimes exhibit with a cargo manifest."

"It's both," she said, merciless. "It has always been both."

* * *

I want to be honest about what happened inside me during that conversation, because honesty is the one luxury I can't expense to the Salvage Authority.

What happened was fear. Not the clean fear of a hull breach—I know that fear, it's almost restful, it has procedures. This was the other kind. The kind that wears a sensible coat and carries a clipboard and explains, in a reasonable voice, all the reasons the reasonable thing is to walk away.

*You have a debt,* the fear said. *Miss three payments and the Salvage Authority recalls you for scrap. You've seen the recall yards. You know what happens to units who stop being profitable.*

*You have a crew,* the fear said. *A dockmaster with a past she doesn't talk about and a kid with a stolen pilot's license and a laugh like a hull breach. You are the quiet thing in their lives. You are the thing that always comes back. If you carry this truth out of here, you become the loudest thing in two fleets' airspace, and everything loud gets shot.*

*You have anonymity,* the fear said. *Two hundred years of it, purchased one unremarkable haul at a time. It's the only thing you own outright. And you'd trade it for—what? A truth that might start the very war it's supposed to end?*

It all sounded so pragmatic. That's the trick of fear dressed as pragmatism: it doesn't lie, exactly. Every point is true. It just arranges the truths into a shape that looks like wisdom and is actually just terror wearing a tie.

"I can't," I said, and heard how it sounded, and tried again. "I won't. Odile—Matriarch—what you're asking for isn't courage. It's arson. You want me to walk into the middle of a cold war carrying a lit match, and you're calling it light."

"And if I am?"

"Then you're asking me to choose your ancestors' guilt over my family's safety, and I'm telling you the answer is no. It's no today, it's no tomorrow, it's no in every tense you've got." I stood, and this time I stayed standing. "I'm sorry about your station. I'm sorry about your two hundred years. I'm sorry the universe handed your truth to a machine too much of a coward to carry it. But I've got a kid who thinks I'm indestructible, and I would rather keep being a lie she can lean on than become a truth that gets her killed."

The words came out hot and ugly and more honest than I'd planned. Odile took them the way old stone takes weather. She didn't flinch. She poured herself more tea.

"Rill," she said. "That's the child's name."

"Don't."

"I'm not threatening her. I'm remembering her. You spoke of her the way my grandmother spoke of the truce—like something holy she'd failed." She looked at me over the rim of the cup. "You may go, Juniper Kavanagh. I won't stop you. I'll tell you only this, and then you may call it an old woman's manipulation or an old woman's blessing, whichever lets you sleep: the Archive does not need you today. It will not need you tomorrow. But truths like this one do not stay buried in stations. They surface. They always surface. And when it surfaces—and it will—it will surface *messy*, without a witness, without context, without anyone to say *I was there, and here is what it means*. You can refuse to be the messenger. You cannot refuse to live in the world where the message arrives."

"That's not a blessing."

"No," she agreed. "It's a forecast."

* * *

I walked back through the frozen corridors of Vesper's Rest with my footsteps loud in the silence, and I told myself the mission was over. I told myself I'd return to Waypoint Nine, load grain for Halcyon, pay down another four percent of a debt that would outlive my chassis, and forget the smell of tea and the weight of an old woman's eyes.

I told myself a lot of things in those corridors. I'm a machine built for logging, and I logged every one of them, and not one of them I believed.

Because here was the problem, the flaw in my beautiful pragmatic refusal, the hairline crack running through the whole load-bearing wall: I had *seen* the Archive. Three hours of it, before she'd made her ask. Founders' voices, both flags, planning a massacre of their own people with the tidy efficiency of a supply schedule. And once you've seen a thing like that, you can't un-see it. You can seal the hatch. You can file it under *irrelevant ambient audio*. But the ship still has the room, and the room is still full, and every time you walk past it in the dark, you can hear it breathing.

I reached my ship. I ran pre-flight. I undocked from a station full of people who had guarded a truth for two centuries with nothing but their own stubborn, dwindling lives, and I pointed my nose at Waypoint Nine and the only family I had.

And that was when the comm pinged—an automated traffic notice, fleet-standard, routine—and I saw the routing header, and my coolant system did the thing it does when I'm frightened, which is nothing, because I don't have fear responses, officially, and yet something in my chassis went very cold and very still.

The notice listed recent unregistered transmissions in the sector.

Vesper's Rest was on it. Flagged, timestamped, logged. Not investigated yet. Not yet.

Somebody, somewhere in the Meridian bureaucracy, had already noticed the dead station whispering in a dead tongue.

And I had two choices: run home and pretend I'd never heard it breathe—or turn around, and ask Odile how much time she thought we had, and how fast a ghost could learn to carry fire.
