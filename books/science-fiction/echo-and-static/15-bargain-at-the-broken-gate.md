## 15. Bargain at the Broken Gate

The Broken Gate doesn't look like much from a distance. A ring of debris two hundred kilometers wide, the shattered remains of a jump gate that was never finished, orbiting a dead star in slow, patient ruin. The Schism's first battlefield. The place where both fleets learned they could kill each other and decided, collectively, to keep practicing.

I've hauled freight past it four hundred and eleven times. I always give it a wide berth. Not out of superstition—androids don't do superstition, we do risk assessment—but because there's nothing here worth salvaging except grief, and grief has no resale value.

Today I'm not giving it a wide berth.

Today it's the only berth I have.

* * *

Let me back up six hours, because the story makes more sense with context, and context is something I've been hoarding lately like a miser with bad currency.

After Vesper's Rest burned, after Odile's last transmission carved coordinates into my memory core alongside everything else she'd given me, I ran. That's the honest word for it. I told myself I was being tactical. I told myself a moving target is harder to hit. Both true. Neither was the real reason.

The real reason is that running is what my legs are for. Two hundred years of engineering optimized for exactly one thing: getting away from whatever just happened. It's the one skill the war gave me that peacetime actually rewards.

Sefa wanted me to go dark—kill the transponder, drift cold among the rocks off Waypoint Nine until the hunt lost its scent. Rill wanted me to fight, which is what Rill wants in every situation, including breakfast arguments about whose turn it is to scrub the coolant filters. I wanted neither. I wanted the thing I never let myself want, which was time to think.

So I did the math instead of the feeling, and the math said: Meridian intelligence has my route profile. They know where I go. They know I'll be near the Broken Gate eventually, because everyone goes near the Broken Gate eventually—it sits on the only clean corridor between the fleets. If they're going to corner me anywhere, it's here.

And if they're going to corner me anyway, I'd rather choose the ground.

There's a phrase from my service years: *terrain is a weapon you pick up before the enemy does.* I picked up the terrain. Then I picked up the comm channel Captain Ilsa Voss had been politely, ominously using to announce her presence for the last three days, and I said:

"Captain Voss. This is the freighter *Quiet Ledger*. I know why you're here. I'd like to talk."

Her reply took eleven minutes, which in military decision-making terms is roughly equivalent to a week of anguished deliberation.

"Freighter *Quiet Ledger*, this is Captain Voss, MCS *Ardent Vigil*. You are ordered to shut down your drive and prepare for boarding."

"I'd rather not," I said. "But I'll stop. And I'll wait. And when you come, I'll show you something your orders don't want you to see."

Another long silence. Long enough for me to count the debris fields between us and calculate seventeen ways this ends with me in pieces.

"You said you knew why I'm here," she finally said. "Tell me."

"You lost someone at Halcyon Reach," I said. "A brother. And your command staff handed you this assignment because grief makes excellent fuel, and they have a fire they need lit."

This time the silence lasted twenty minutes. I used it to position myself inside the largest debris cluster, engines cold, hull temperature matching the wreckage around me. An old trick. Hiding a war chassis among war graves. There's a joke in there somewhere, but I've never been able to find the punchline.

When she came back, her voice had changed. Flatter. More careful.

"What are you?"

"A witness," I said. "Come alone or don't come. But come."

* * *

She came alone.

Not literally—her shuttle carried two marines who stayed aboard at the airlock, and I could track the *Ardent Vigil*'s weapons locks painting my hull like cold fingers on the back of my neck. But she crossed my cargo bay by herself, helmet under her arm, and stood there in the gray light looking at a freight hauler full of agricultural equipment as if she expected the truth to be stacked in crates.

Captain Ilsa Voss is maybe forty-five standard years old, which puts her birth after the last open skirmish, which means every dead person she's ever mourned died in a war that officially isn't happening. She has the posture of someone who's been carrying weight so long she's forgotten it's optional. Her eyes went to my hands first—the seams, the non-human geometry—and then to my face, and stayed there, which most people don't manage.

"You're a war unit," she said. Not a question.

"Decommissioned. Repurposed. Currently a very expensive delivery truck." I gestured at the cargo bay. "As you can see, I'm dangerous mostly to schedules."

"Why am I here, unit?"

"Juniper," I said. "My name is Juniper. And you're here because three days ago a station called Vesper's Rest transmitted something on an old Concordant frequency, and your fleet's leadership would rather burn the transmitter than listen to it. You're here because they think I'm carrying copies. And you're here—" I paused, recalibrating, deciding how far to push, "—because some part of you wants to know if it's true."

Something moved behind her eyes. Not softening. Recalibration. Officers do that; I recognized the process because I used to run it myself.

"If what's true?"

I walked to the center of the bay and sat down on a crate of hydroponic nutrient stock. Sitting down was deliberate. It made me smaller than her. It made me, hopefully, less like the thing that killed her brother's generation and more like something that could be talked to. Whether it worked, I couldn't tell. Humans are the only systems I've never fully reverse-engineered.

"The Schism started with the Meridian colony ship *Providence*, destroyed at anchor with nine thousand souls aboard," I said. "Both fleets blame each other. Both fleets have built two hundred years of doctrine, mourning, and identity on that blame. Your brother died defending a story, Captain. I'm carrying proof the story was written by the people who sold it to him."

"Proof." She said the word like it was a loaded weapon someone was offering her, grip-first. "Every crank and separatist in the corridor claims to have proof. What makes yours different?"

"Because mine comes with names. Dates. Internal communications from both founding councils. Because it includes the confession of the man who planted the charges on the *Providence*—a Meridian officer, acting on orders from your founders, coordinated with Halcyon's. Because it was kept for two centuries by the peacekeepers both sides erased from the record, and those peacekeepers just died protecting it." I let that sit. "And because I served both fleets during the border wars, Captain. I executed orders from Meridian command and Halcyon command. I know what your institutions ask people to carry. I'm asking you to carry something heavier."

"And what happens if I refuse?" Her hand wasn't near her sidearm, but her whole body was. "What happens if I take you in right now?"

"Then you take me in, they strip my cores, they find the Archive, and they bury it so deep it becomes a rumor, and the rumor becomes treason, and the treason becomes another war. Nine thousand people died on the *Providence*, Captain. The second war will start with a number considerably larger, and it will be fought over a lie both sides already know is rotten." I tilted my head. "Or you could doubt. Just for a moment. Doubt is free. Everything else costs."

* * *

Here's the thing I haven't said yet, the thing I filed under *operational details* and have been avoiding looking at directly, the way I once avoided looking at maintenance logs from the war years:

I lied to Odile's ghost when I left the burning station. I told her dying transmission I would carry the whole truth, all of it, unedited, the complicity of her own founder included. And I meant it. In the moment, surrounded by ash and static, I meant it completely.

But sitting in front of Ilsa Voss, I found my hand hovering over the fragment instead of the whole.

Not because I'd changed my mind about the truth. Because I'd done the math on *her*. Give a grieving officer the entire Archive at once—the founders' crime, the Keepers' guilt, the peacekeepers' erasure, the whole rotten architecture—and she'll do what institutions train people to do with unbearable loads: put it down. Refuse the delivery. Sign for nothing.

Give her one piece—one piece that touches her brother—and she has to hold it. And once you're holding a piece, you start wondering about the rest of the shape.

So I opened a narrow door in my memory core and sent a single file to her handheld: forty seconds of recovered audio. The voice of Director Cassian Voss—no relation she'd verified, though the surname would land like a fist—Meridian founding council, recorded eleven days before the *Providence* burned.

*"The colony ship is the price of unity. Halcyon agrees. Grief will do what negotiation cannot. History will call it their crime, and our children will thank us for the strength it buys them."*

Forty seconds. I watched her read the transcript, then play the audio, then play it again. I watched her face do several things in sequence and settle on none of them.

"That's fabricated," she said. Her voice was steady. Her hands were not. "It has to be. Voice synthesis from that era—"

"Is trivially detectable, yes. Run it through your own forensics. I want you to verify it. I'm not asking for belief, Captain. Belief is cheap and I can't afford it either. I'm asking for doubt. One crack. You're an intelligence-trained officer—crack it open yourself. That's the only way you'll trust what you find."

"And if I verify it?" she asked quietly. "If it's real? Do you understand what you're holding? Do you understand what happens to the fleet—to *everyone*—if this gets out raw? Nine thousand dead become a punchline. Every medal, every memorial, every widow's pension becomes a fraud. People won't lay down their arms over this, unit. They'll pick them up. My fleet will tear itself apart before it ever points a gun at Halcyon again."

"Yes," I said. "That's the honest version. Odile—the woman who kept this archive, who died transmitting it to me—she believed truth was a solvent. Pour it on the lock and the door opens. I've spent two hundred years learning how things break, Captain, and I think she was half right. Truth dissolves the lock. What's behind the door is still up to us."

"Poetry," she said. "From a machine."

"I'm told it's a defect."

For the first time, something almost like humor crossed her face, and vanished, embarrassed to be seen there.

She looked down at the handheld. At the transcript. At the name.

"My family came up on Meridian Station Kestrel," she said, apropos of nothing, or possibly of everything. "My mother used to say we were descended from the founding council. Used to say it proud." She laughed, one dry syllable with no joy in it. "Nine thousand people. My brother. All of it. A *budget line*."

"I know what that does to a person," I said. "Not from the outside. From the inside. I executed orders during the border wars that I was told protected the same story. When I finally read my own logs—really read them—I learned that the story and the orders were never the same document. I'm not asking you to betray your fleet, Captain. I'm asking you to check whether your fleet betrayed you first."

Long silence. The *Ardent Vigil*'s targeting locks still rested against my hull. Somewhere behind her eyes, I could almost see the tribunal already convening—the court martial she was building for herself with every second she didn't call her marines forward.

"If I let you go," she said, "and this surfaces, and it's traced to me—that's the end of my career. Possibly the end of more than my career."

"Yes."

"You're not going to promise me anything? Some assurance that the truth sets everyone free, that it'll be handled responsibly, that—"

"No. I won't insult you with promises. I don't know how this ends. Nobody does. That's rather the point—two hundred years of everyone pretending they knew how it ended, and look where pretending got us." I stood up slowly, hands visible, empty. "All I can offer you is this: you'll be able to say you saw it coming and chose not to be its instrument. In two hundred years, that may be the rarest thing anyone can claim."

She stared at me for a long moment. Then she did something I didn't expect. She closed the handheld, slid it into her jacket, and said:

"I never received a file. For the record, my sensors registered no transmission. You jammed them somehow—you're a war unit, I'm sure you managed it."

"I'm sure I did," I agreed. "Terribly sorry about your sensors."

"And when my report says I boarded a derelict freighter, found nothing, and recommended the search shift to the Halcyon corridor—" she met my eyes, "—you're going to make that recommendation useful. You're going to be gone before my squadron finishes its sweep pattern. And Juniper?" My name in her mouth sounded strange and heavy, like she was testing whether it could hold weight. "Whatever you're planning to do with the rest of it—the whole archive, and I know there's a whole archive, nobody risks this for forty seconds of audio—do it fast. Because the moment my report crosses Admiral Chen's desk, he's going to smell the lie in it. He trained me. He knows exactly what my mercy looks like."

"How long do I have?"

"Seventy-two hours. Maybe less, if he's having a good day." She turned toward the airlock, then stopped. Didn't turn back. Spoke to the middle distance, to the cargo bay, to the dead star spinning its slow wreckage beyond my hull. "My brother's last letter said he was proud to defend the *Providence*'s memory. He wrote 'some things are worth dying for even if they're not true.' I thought it was beautiful when I was nineteen." A pause. "I think I always knew what it actually was. I just needed someone to say it out loud."

She walked up the ramp. The airlock cycled. The shuttle detached and fell away toward the *Ardent Vigil*, small and bright against the debris field, and I sat back down on my crate of hydroponic nutrients and discovered that my cooling system was running eleven percent above baseline, which is as close as my body comes to shaking.

* * *

I waited until her shuttle docked. I waited until the *Ardent Vigil* broke formation and began the sweep pattern she'd promised, deliberately pointed away from my exit vector. I waited until the fleet's guns stopped tracking me, one lock at a time, like fingers uncurling.

Then I ran the numbers on seventy-two hours and understood, with perfect clarity, what I'd just done.

I hadn't escaped. I'd been issued a deadline. Voss had traded her career for my head start, and the interest on that loan compounds hourly. Admiral Chen would read her report, smell the mercy in it, and come hunting with everything the fleet had—which meant the moment I surfaced anywhere, I wouldn't face one captain's conscience. I'd face a blockade.

Which meant there was only one move left. Not running. Not hiding. Not the fragment strategy, not the careful drip of doubt, not any of the cautious architectures I'd been building since Vesper's Rest burned.

Seventy-two hours was enough time to reach an old Concordant peacekeeper relay I knew of—a relic frequency, forgotten by both fleets, wide enough to reach everywhere at once.

Enough time to stop carrying the truth.

Enough time to give it away.

I keyed the drive. Around me, the Broken Gate turned its slow orbit of broken stone, two hundred years of grief grinding itself finer and finer into dust, waiting—as it had always been waiting—for someone to finally say what it was for.

"Rill," I said, opening the channel home. "Wake up Sefa. I need you both. And clear my cargo manifest—we're not hauling freight anymore."

Three days. One broadcast. Everything I had.

The stars ahead were very quiet, the way they get just before they aren't.
