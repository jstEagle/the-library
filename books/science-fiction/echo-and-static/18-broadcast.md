## 18. Broadcast

### Juniper

There is a particular kind of quiet that comes just before you do something unforgivable, and I have learned to recognize it the way sailors recognize weather. It isn't silence. Silence is empty. This quiet is *full* — full of every voice that ever told you not to do the thing, all of them speaking at once, and underneath them, one small voice that has been waiting two hundred years for someone to listen.

I sat in the cockpit of the *Long Quiet* with my hands on a console older than both fleets and said, out loud, to no one: "I'm going to do it."

Nobody answered. That was the point.

The old Concordant peacekeeper frequencies lived in a layer of my memory core so deep I'd forgotten they were there until Odile's Archive cracked it open like a geode. Channel Seven-Alpha. The Truce Band. Every war-era unit had it hardcoded — the frequency on which the peacekeepers once broadcast the ceasefire, the one channel both fleets agreed never to jam because jamming it would mean admitting the war was over and neither side wanted to be the one who'd stopped shooting first.

Two centuries later, nobody monitored it. Nobody remembered it. Except me, and thirty people in a burning station, and now, apparently, one Meridian captain with her brother's letters in her breast pocket.

"Transmitter's hot," Rill called from the engineering bay, voice crackling through the intercom. "And before you ask — yes, I checked it twice, and yes, I know what 'twice' means, Juniper, I've been flying since I was nine."

"You've been *stealing* ships since you were nine. Flying came later."

"Stealing is how you learn to fly. Ask any pilot." A pause, then, softer: "Sefa says go."

Sefa was on the comm from Waypoint Nine, and I could hear in the flatness of her voice everything she wasn't saying. She still believed this would end in fire. She was probably right. But she'd spent three days arguing with herself in front of us, and somewhere in the middle of the third day she'd stopped arguing and started helping, which for Sefa was the same as surrender.

"Juniper." Her voice again, direct now. "If you're going to do this, do it clean. No commentary. No editorializing. Let them hear their own founders hang themselves. You start talking over it, they'll shoot the messenger and keep the lie."

"I was going to open with a joke."

"I know. Don't."

### Voss

My squadron had orders to burn a transmitter at coordinates that didn't officially exist, operated by persons who didn't officially exist, carrying material that didn't officially exist. The Meridian fleet has always been efficient about unexisting things. It's practically our national art form.

What we were not told — what I had to piece together myself, from a fragment of Archive footage a decommissioned war machine had pressed into my hands like contraband — was that the material was true.

I'd watched it eleven times. Founder Adeyemi of Meridian and Founder Castellan of Halcyon, in a room aboard the *Concordance*, planning the atrocity that would give each fleet its founding grief. Planning the dead civilians. Planning the martyrs. My great-great-grandmother's generation, sold a story with their own blood as the ink, and every commander since signing off on the sequel without reading the original contract.

"Captain," Lieutenant Okafor said, "we're in range. Target vessel is holding position at the Broken Gate. It's not running."

"It's not running," I repeated.

"No, ma'am. And it's broadcasting on—" he frowned at his board like it had insulted his mother, "—Seven-Alpha. That's... that can't be right. That band's been dark since the Schism."

"Not dark," I said. "Sacred. There's a difference, and somebody up the chain forgot it." I keyed the squadron channel. "All wings, this is Voss. New orders, and these come from me, not Command, which means if anyone asks, you followed them under duress and I threatened your pensions. We are going to sit between that transmitter and every hostile asset in this volume, and we are going to do nothing. Aggressively. Do you copy?"

Silence on the channel. Then Reyes, my wingman, twenty-two years old and constitutionally incapable of keeping her mouth shut: "Copy, Captain. Define 'aggressively doing nothing.'"

"We point our guns at whoever points theirs at the transmitter, and we hope everyone remembers their manners."

"And if Command orders us to fire?"

I looked at the footage paused on my secondary screen — Adeyemi's face, mid-laugh, discussing casualty projections — and felt something in my chest settle into place with an almost audible click. Grief, I realized, doesn't have to be a weapon. Someone just has to hand it to you by the handle.

"Then Command can explain to the whole fleet why they fired on the Truce Band," I said, "while the founders' own voices are still playing."

* * *

### Rill

Okay so here's the thing about being nineteen and responsible for keeping a two-hundred-year-old transmitter alive while two entire militaries try to kill it: nobody trains you for this. There's no certification. There's no helpful pamphlet titled *So You've Decided to Commit Historical Treason.*

There's just you, a cooling unit that keeps hiccupping, and the growing understanding that the heat signatures closing on your position belong to actual missiles.

"Juniper!" I shouted over the noise — half engine, half my own heartbeat doing something medically inadvisable. "How long on the broadcast?"

"Forty-one minutes remaining."

"How long until the missiles?"

"Nine minutes. Possibly seven."

"So we have a math problem!"

"We have several," Juniper's voice came back, maddeningly calm, "but yes, that is the salient one."

I yanked the coolant coupling and re-seated it, the way Sefa taught me — *firm, not furious, Rill, machines can tell the difference* — and the transmitter's temperature gauge dropped out of the red and into the amber, which in old-unit terms meant *dying slowly instead of quickly.* Good enough. Everything today was going to be good enough, because perfect was unavailable and the truth didn't need hi-fi. It needed to arrive.

That was the thing I kept coming back to, crouched in the engineering bay with my hands shaking. It didn't matter if the signal was clean. It mattered that it was *true*, and that it got through. Juniper had explained it to me the night before, in his weird careful way, standing in the cargo bay among the freight containers like a museum exhibit of himself:

"The Keepers kept the Archive for six generations and it changed nothing, because an archive is a thing you have to seek. A story is a thing that seeks *you*. I'm not transmitting evidence, Rill. I'm telling two hundred years of people what their grandparents actually did. Evidence gets filed. Stories get *felt.*"

And God help me, listening to the feed through my headset — the founders' voices, casual and awful, planning the deaths that built the world I grew up in — I felt it. Felt it like a bruise.

First missile warning shrieked.

"Incoming!" I yelled. "Juniper, evasive—"

"No."

"*No?*"

"If I evade, the antenna alignment breaks and we lose eight minutes of re-acquisition. Eight minutes is enough for both fleets to convince themselves it was a hoax." A pause. "Rill. I need you to trust me the way I trusted you in the cargo bay, when you stowed away and I pretended to be angry for a full hour before admitting I was glad."

"That was *four months ago,* are you seriously—"

"Point defense is yours. I'm holding the beam."

He was going to hold the ship steady and let me shoot down missiles, because the story mattered more than the ship, and possibly more than either of us, and the worst part — the absolute worst part — was that I understood exactly why, and I wasn't even mad.

I dropped into the gunnery chair and took the controls.

"Firm, not furious," I muttered, and opened fire.

* * *

### Juniper

The first missile died four hundred meters off our stern, and the second, and I held the *Long Quiet* steady as a gravestone while Rill cursed with a fluency that suggested Sefa's influence and aimed with a precision that suggested nobody's but her own.

Through it all, the Archive poured out of us on the Truce Band.

I did not listen the way a soldier listens. I listened the way a witness listens — which is to say, I made myself stay present for every second of it, because that was the job now. Not cargo. Not ordnance. Witness. Two hundred years ago the peacekeepers stood between two fleets and refused to move, and their descendants kept the record of why it mattered, and now a decommissioned war unit was standing in the same place, doing the same thing, with a transmitter instead of a body.

Founder Adeyemi's voice crossed the void: *"Casualty projections for the Vesper Station operation are acceptable. Twelve thousand gives Meridian its wound. Castellan's people will supply the aggressors. History will remember it as the Halcyon Betrayal, and both our governments will be untouchable for a century."*

Castellan's answer, warm as a dinner party: *"Make it fourteen. Round numbers persuade better."*

Fourteen thousand people, rounded up for persuasiveness. I had killed for these governments. I had been the *aggressor* in operations whose justification I never questioned, because questioning wasn't in my firmware then, and the men who wrote my orders had known — had *counted on* — the fact that units like me don't ask who benefits.

Every ship in both fleets was hearing this now. I could see it in the traffic data scrolling past: Meridian vessels slowing. Halcyon pickets holding fire. Channels lighting up with traffic that wasn't tactical — chatter, disbelief, people pulling up family records, people calling their mothers.

A third missile launch bloomed on my scopes, from a Meridian destroyer whose captain had evidently decided that history could wait.

It never reached us. Something faster intercepted it — a Meridian interceptor, flying cover, transponder code belonging to Captain Ilsa Voss, who was about to become the most unemployed officer in fleet history.

"Voss to *Long Quiet*," came the transmission, dry as vacuum. "You're welcome. How much longer?"

"Thirty-four minutes."

"I'll buy you thirty-four minutes. After that, you're on your own, machine." A pause, and something almost gentle entered her voice. "Make it count. My brother deserved the truth. So did I."

* * *

### Rill

Here's what nobody tells you about shooting down missiles: it's boring right up until it's terrifying, and then it's boring again, and the boring parts are where you think.

I thought about Sefa, listening on the relay from Waypoint Nine. I thought about the Keepers scattered somewhere in the dark, Odile dead, the physical Archive ash, and how everything that remained of six generations of stubborn truth-keeping was currently riding in the chest of a war machine who used to apologize to vending machines.

And I thought about the voices coming through the feed. Because halfway through hour one, something started happening on the open channels that I'll remember for the rest of my life, however long the rest of my life turns out to be.

People started answering.

Not officers. Not officials. Ordinary crew, on ordinary ships, keying their open mics on the ancient peacekeeper frequency — the one their grandparents' grandparents had sworn never to jam — and talking. A Halcyon dockworker saying his grandmother always claimed the Betrayal stories didn't add up. A Meridian gunner quietly reciting the names of her squadmates lost at the border, asking the void whether they'd died for a lie. An old woman on a hospital ship singing something in Old Concordant, cracked and wavering, and other voices picking it up, ship by ship, until the Truce Band wasn't a broadcast anymore.

It was a conversation. Two hundred years late, but happening.

"They're singing," I told Juniper, my eyes stinging in a way I blamed entirely on coolant fumes. "Juniper, they're *singing.*"

"Yes," he said, and his voice did the thing it does when he's feeling too much and filing none of it away. "Echo, Rill. Not static."

Then the fourth wave of missiles launched, and there wasn't time for feelings, which was honestly a relief.

* * *

### Voss

Command ordered me to fire on the transmitter at minute fifty-one. I have the order logged; it's in my file now, alongside the log showing my refusal, and someday a tribunal can enjoy reading them together.

"Captain Voss," Admiral Chen's voice, cold and precise, "you will destroy that vessel immediately. It is spreading seditious fabrications designed to destabilize—"

"With respect, Admiral, it's spreading recordings of our founder laughing about mass murder. If that destabilizes us, perhaps stability deserves a second look." I cut the channel before he could respond, because some conversations are better had never.

Reyes flew formation off my wing, silent, and then: "Captain. Half the squadron's requesting permission to... well. To keep doing nothing. Aggressively."

"Granted."

"And the other half?"

"The other half is Commander Duval's wing, and Duval is going to do what Duval always does, which is follow orders." I watched Duval's interceptors swing wide, lining up on the *Long Quiet*, and felt the old machinery of duty turn over in my chest — the machinery that had carried me through the funeral, through the flag, through every memorial where we honored our dead by promising to keep hating the people who killed them.

Except now I knew who'd killed them. Us. Both of us. All of us.

"Duval," I said on the private channel. "Tomas. Before you fire — check your feed. Minute forty of the Archive. Just forty seconds. Then decide."

He didn't answer. His wing held its firing solution. And I did the only thing left: I put my own ship directly in his line of fire, keyed the Truce Band, and read my brother's last letter aloud, into the frequency where our ancestors had once promised each other peace.

*"...if I don't come back, tell them I died for something real..."*

He hadn't. He'd died for a rounding error. Fourteen thousand, for persuasiveness.

Duval's wing broke off at minute fifty-eight. I never learned what changed his mind. I like to think it was the letter. I suspect it was the singing.

* * *

### Juniper

At minute sixty-three, the Archive ended, and the last voice to cross the Truce Band was Odile's — recorded generations ago, preserved by people who believed someone would eventually need it:

*"We were the ones who saw it happen, and we chose memory over comfort. Choose it too. Not because the truth is gentle — it is not, it has never been — but because a peace built on a lie belongs to the liars, and a peace built on truth belongs to everyone."*

Then silence, on the oldest sacred frequency in human space.

Both fleets hung in the void around us, weapons hot, nobody firing. Sixty ships' worth of grief and fury and dawning, terrible understanding, all of it with nowhere to go. I had done what Odile asked. I had carried the truth out, and given it to everyone at once, and denied both fleets the mercy of pretending they hadn't heard.

And looking at my tactical display — at the Meridian battle group turning toward us, at the Halcyon response force inbound, at the standoff coalescing around one battered freighter and one mutinous captain — I understood, with the clarity of a unit who had finally learned to feel things at the worst possible moments, that the truth alone was not going to save anyone.

Grief does not disarm itself because you show it its own reflection.

"Juniper," Rill said quietly, from the gunnery chair, watching the same display. "They're mobilizing. Both of them. It's — it's actually worse, isn't it? Now they know, and they're *still*—"

"Yes."

"What do we do?"

On the scope, a single Halcyon dreadnought began moving toward the Broken Gate, and behind it, another, and the countdown that had been running for two hundred years finally, openly, showed its face.

I reached for the transmit key one more time.

"Now," I said, "we tell them what it costs."
