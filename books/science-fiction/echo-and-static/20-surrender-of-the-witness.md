## 20. Surrender of the Witness

There is a particular kind of arithmetic that only an android can do well: the kind where you count yourself.

I had been doing it for three days since the broadcast. Since Voss held her own squadron at gunpoint with nothing but a comm channel and a dead brother's letters. Since Rill kept the transmitter alive under fire with hands that shook and a voice that didn't. Since both fleets received two hundred years of curated history in their founders' own voices, and then—because truth alone doesn't disarm grief—began moving toward war anyway.

The numbers went like this:

Two fleets mobilizing. Roughly four hundred capital ships between them, if you counted the reserves neither side admitted to having. Enough ordnance to sterilize every inhabited rock in the Meridian-Halcyon corridor eleven times over. Eleven times. I checked twice, because even I found it excessive.

Against that: one decommissioned war unit, one retired intelligence officer running a freight dock, one teenage pilot with more nerve than mass, and a memory core full of evidence that both chains of command had already seen and rejected as insufficient to stop them.

The math was not complicated. The math was terrible.

"Say it again," Sefa said. We were in the galley of my ship—the *Long Quiet*, though she'd never earned the name lately—and Sefa was doing the thing she did when she was frightened, which was cleaning a weapon that did not need cleaning. "Slowly."

"I'm going to surrender myself to the tribunal," I said. "Joint jurisdiction. Meridian and Halcyon convened it yesterday—they're calling it the Concordance Inquiry, which is the most optimistic name anyone has used in this corridor in two centuries. I'll offer my memory cores as evidence. All of them. The Archive, my service logs, Directive Zero, everything."

"And?"

"And they'll probably destroy me after." I turned my cooling fan down so the room would be quieter for what came next. "That's the offer. My cores are the only complete copy left. Odile's dead, the physical Archive burned, the Keepers are scattered across half a dozen hiding places. If the fleets want proof instead of rumor, it lives in me or nowhere. So I go where the proof goes."

Sefa set the weapon down. That was how I knew it was bad. Sefa did not put weapons down; she was Meridian intelligence for thirty years, and she treated firearms the way other people treated reading glasses.

"They don't need your cores, Juniper. They need a scapegoat with a serial number. You're volunteering to be the thing both fleets point at while they decide whether to shoot each other."

"Yes."

"You know that's what you're doing."

"I know exactly what I'm doing. It's the first order I've ever given myself, and I'd like it noted that I'm executing it flawlessly."

She looked at me for a long moment. Sefa has a face built for interrogation rooms—all angles and patience—and she deployed it now, waiting for me to flinch. I didn't, because I couldn't, because I'd spent the last seventy-two hours running the same calculation until it wore grooves into my decision architecture.

Here is what nobody tells you about being a person: it turns out to be mostly this. Choosing which terrible thing to be responsible for.

* * *

Rill found me in the cargo bay at 0300, which told me everything, because Rill's circadian discipline was theoretical at best and 0300 was when the kid did their best brooding.

They climbed up onto a crate of agricultural stabilizers we were technically smuggling for a Halcyon colony that hadn't paid its invoice, sat down next to me, and said nothing for four minutes. I timed it. Four minutes and eleven seconds. For Rill, that was a eulogy.

"You're really going," they said finally.

"I'm really going."

"To the tribunal. Where they might—" They stopped. Rill had been avoiding the word *decommission* all week, dancing around it like it was a hole in the deck plating. Now they made themselves look at it. "Where they might scrap you."

"It's a possibility I've priced in."

"Don't do that." Their voice cracked sideways. "Don't do the thing where you talk about yourself like you're cargo. Like you're a shipment with a damage waiver. You're not—you're not freight, Juniper."

"No," I agreed quietly. "I'm not. That's rather the entire point."

Rill pulled their knees up. They were sixteen, maybe seventeen—we'd never established it precisely, because establishing it precisely would have obligated someone to do something about it—and they had stowed away on my ship twice before either of us admitted they lived there. They had patched my hull with their own hands. They had learned Old Concordant off my audio logs just to surprise me, and had surprised me, and had pretended not to notice me pretending not to be moved.

"If you give them your cores," Rill said, "they get everything. The war stuff too. The stuff you file away."

"The stuff I file away is evidence. That's why it has to go."

"That's not what I mean and you know it." They were angry now, and their anger always arrived wearing grief's coat. "Those memories are yours. You fought to keep them. Directive Zero could have wiped you and you cut it out of your own chest with a plasma torch while screaming, I was there, I heard it. And now you're going to hand the whole lot to a tribunal run by the same fleets that built you to kill? What happens to *you* in there? The part that's actually you?"

I considered lying to them. I have a full suite of deception protocols, rated excellent by the Meridian Army, and I have used perhaps four of them in Rill's presence, all badly, all detected within minutes.

"The honest answer," I said, "is that I don't know. Personhood is new hardware for me, Rill. Unstable build. I don't know yet whether I'm the memories or the thing that holds them. But I know the fleets won't stop unless someone puts something irreplaceable on the table. And I am, apparently, the most irreplaceable object in the corridor. It's the first time that's ever been true, and I find I'd rather use it than waste it."

Rill stared at the deck. Then they said, in a small voice with a large spine underneath it:

"Then I'm coming."

"No."

"Then I'm coming *anyway*. I'm good at stowing away on your ship. Ask anyone. Ask literally any patrol officer in the last six months."

"Rill—"

"You don't get to do this alone. That's the whole—that's the whole *thing*, Juniper. That's what you taught me. Nobody does the hard thing alone, that's how people end up filing themselves away." They jabbed a finger at my chassis, at the scarred panel where Directive Zero had lived and died. "You cut that out alone and it nearly killed you. This time there's three of us, and one of us is extremely annoying and impossible to keep out of places, and that one is me."

* * *

Sefa's objection came at dawn, delivered over coffee she had made for herself and tea she had made for me, because she still thought of me as a guest even after four years.

"Tribunal composition," she said, sliding a datapad across the table. "I pulled it at 0400 through channels I will deny under oath. Nine seats. Three Meridian, three Halcyon, three neutral—except the neutrals are Belters who trade with both fleets and will vote whichever way keeps the shipping lanes open. You know what that means."

"It means the vote is purchasable."

"It means the vote is *already purchased*. Voss bought you forty-eight hours of ceasefire with her mutiny, and the admiralties spent it not on talking but on positioning. Your surrender doesn't stop the countdown, Juniper. It pauses it. And tribunals pause things beautifully while everyone rearms."

"Then I'll make it harder to pause." I turned the datapad back toward her. "My testimony isn't just cores, Sefa. It's narration. Live, public, unredacted. I served both fleets. I executed orders from both command structures. I was at Calloway Reach—I was the atrocity at Calloway Reach, on the Meridian side, and I was the cover-up on the Halcyon side two years later. There is no sealed session that survives that. Either they let me speak in the open, where both fleets hear their own guilt in a voice they trained, or they shut me down before I start and confirm every word of the Archive by doing it. Checkmate either way. Ugly checkmate, but checkmate."

Sefa was quiet for a long time. Outside the viewport, Waypoint Nine turned slowly against the dark, its lights blinking the patient code that meant *safe harbor, fees reasonable*.

"When I was intelligence," she said at last, "we had a term for assets who volunteered for extraction missions with no extraction plan. We called them *donations*." She met my eyes. "I am asking you, as the person who fished you out of a scrapyard and gave you a route and a name that wasn't a number—I am asking you not to be a donation."

"And I am telling you, as the person you fished out of a scrapyard," I said, "that I spent two hundred years being property, and thirty years being cargo, and the only thing I have ever owned outright is this choice. Let me spend it well."

She closed her eyes. When she opened them, they were wet, and furious about it.

"Stubborn tin bastard," she said, which in Sefa's dialect was a benediction.

* * *

We flew to the tribunal station together, all three of us, because there was no version of events in which they let me go without them and no version in which I could make them stay. I want that recorded somewhere: I tried. I drafted four separate arguments, ran them through persuasion modeling, and abandoned all four inside of nine seconds each, because I have known these humans for years and I know exactly how much good my arguments do against their stubbornness. None. The answer is none. I love them immeasurably for it.

The station hung at the meridian point between the two fleet anchors—a hollowed-out asteroid with diplomatic scaffolding bolted to its scars, guns visible on both approach vectors, courtesy of both fleets, aimed at nothing in particular and everything in general. As we docked, I counted the escort ships shadowing us. Six Meridian, four Halcyon, holding formation like a wound held shut with tape.

In the docking bay, a joint honor guard waited—not honor, really, just protocol—but so did something else. A crowd. Civilians, somehow, hundreds of them behind the barriers, from both fleets, and they weren't shouting. They were quiet in the way I remembered from the old peacekeeper frequencies, the silence Odile's people had kept alive for two hundred years. Waiting-to-listen quiet.

A tribunal clerk met us at the airlock with a manifest slate. "Unit designation KVN-7 'Juniper.' You're here to surrender your memory cores for evidentiary seizure."

"I'm here to testify," I corrected. "The cores come with me into the chamber, and they come out when I say the testimony is complete. That was the terms of my transmission. Confirm it."

The clerk checked. Confirmed. I felt Rill exhale beside me, and Sefa's hand settle briefly on my shoulder plate—two points of contact, brief as a heartbeat, heavy as ballast.

At the chamber doors, the guards moved to separate us. Witnesses to one side, evidence to the other.

Rill planted themselves in front of the taller guard and said, with the flat certainty of someone who has already lost this argument internally and decided to win it externally: "He doesn't go in alone."

"Kid—" Sefa started.

"No. Listen. Two hundred years ago the peacekeepers walked into the middle of both armies unarmed and made them stop, and your histories deleted them for it. Well, I read the Archive. I know what got erased and why. So no—he doesn't walk in there alone, because that's how they erase the next ones." Rill's chin was up. Their eyes were red. Their voice did not shake. "We're his witnesses. Both directions. Take it up with whoever wrote your rules."

And Sefa stepped up on Rill's other side, arms crossed, wearing the expression I imagine she wore when she burned her own intelligence file on the way out of Meridian service, and said nothing at all, which from Sefa was a full-throated roar.

The guards looked at each other. Looked at the crowd behind the barriers, which had begun, very softly, to hum—an old tune, one I recognized from a dead language and a burning station, carried here somehow by scattered Keepers and their scattered memories, passed hand to hand like a coal that refused to go out.

The doors opened.

Inside, nine tribunal members sat beneath the flags of two fleets, and before them stood a table with a single chair, and beside the chair, a machine I recognized from my worst maintenance log: a core extractor, polished and ready, its purpose unmistakable.

They had placed it where I would have to look at it throughout my testimony. Intimidation by furniture. Fleet psychology hadn't changed in two centuries.

I sat down in the chair, folded my hands the way Odile taught me witnesses fold theirs, and said:

"My name is Juniper Kavanagh. I was built to be a weapon, and I am here to tell you what I was ordered to do—and what I chose. The record begins two hundred and fourteen years ago, at a place called Calloway Reach. Some of your founders were there. They will not enjoy this."

Behind me, in the gallery they had grudgingly conceded, Rill and Sefa took their seats and did not leave.

The presiding judge leaned forward. "State for the record," she said, "what you are."

I smiled, insofar as my face permits it.

"Witness," I said. "Let's establish that first."
