## 9. Halcyon's Open Door

Halcyon Reach came up on the Kestrel's scopes like a held breath let out.

Verdance was green the way a greenhouse is green — managed, rationed, every hectare of arable soil accounted for in ledgers older than Petra's grandmother. Halcyon Reach was green the way a jungle is green. From orbit it looked almost reckless: forests running to the coasts, river deltas braiding silver through wetlands nobody had bothered to pave, cities clustered along the equator like beads on a string, their lights warm and unrationed.

Petra had been making this run for eleven years and had never once landed planetside. The contract was dock-to-dock: cargo down, cargo up, forty hours in Highport while stevedores with union patches and easy laughter moved the freight. Verdance paid in seed stock and rare earths; Halcyon paid in manufactured goods, medical supplies, and — this was the part that always caught in Petra's throat — fruit. Actual fresh fruit, flash-frozen but still recognizably extravagant, packed in crates marked with a smiling sun logo that Verdance's trade ministry pretended not to notice.

The docking clamps engaged with a sound like a handshake.

"Kestrel, welcome to Halcyon Highport," said a voice on comms, female, unhurried, pleasant. "You're cleared for Berth Nine. Customs will be along shortly — no rush. Governor Reyes has asked to see you."

Petra sat very still in the pilot's chair.

Eleven years. Forty-odd crossings. In all that time, the sum total of official attention the Kestrel had received from Halcyon Reach amounted to a customs form, a stamp, and once — memorably — a complaint about a mislabeled crate of soil inoculants. Governors did not ask to see haulers. Governors did not know haulers existed.

"Say again, Highport?" Petra keyed. "You have a request from—"

"Governor Ilsa Reyes," the voice repeated, with the faint amusement of someone enjoying a small joke. "She's read your traffic logs, Captain Blackwood. She'd like to discuss them over dinner. Tonight, if you're free. She insists you bring an appetite."

* * *

Petra spent two hours deciding what to wear, which was two hours more than they had spent on any decision since leaving Verdance, and hated themselves for every minute of it. In the end they wore the same field jacket they always wore, brushed clean of Meridian dust, over the one shirt without a frayed collar. Grandmother Dravenmoor had taught them that clothes were armor you chose, and that the trick was choosing armor that looked like nothing at all.

The governor's residence occupied the top third of a tower in the capital city of Solmar, and Petra rode an elevator up through clouds that smelled — actually smelled — of rain and blossoms. Verdance's air was filtered and recycled until it tasted of nothing. Here the atmosphere itself seemed to be showing off.

Ilsa Reyes met her in a room with an entire wall open to the sky.

She was smaller than her portraits, silver-haired, with laugh lines she had clearly decided to stop fighting somewhere around her sixtieth year. She wore civilian clothes — a plain gray tunic, no insignia — which was its own kind of uniform. She crossed the room with both hands extended and took Petra's in both of hers, and her grip was warm and dry and exactly strong enough.

"Captain Blackwood. Eleven years, forty-one crossings, and not one word of complaint filed against you or your ship. Do you know how rare that is?" She laughed, and it was a good laugh, unforced. "Come. Sit. The food is from my own garden, which means if it's terrible you're obligated to lie to me."

They ate on the open terrace. Grilled river fish, flatbread, a salad of greens so vividly alive they seemed to hum. Petra ate carefully, aware of watching eyes even when none were visible, and Reyes talked — about the harvest festivals, about her grandchildren, about the absurd politics of the port authority — with the practiced ease of a woman who had learned that the fastest way to make someone comfortable is to make yourself boring.

It was nearly an hour before she set down her glass and said, lightly, as though remarking on the weather:

"So. Tell me about the Meridian."

Petra's fork stopped halfway to their mouth.

Reyes watched them with kind, patient eyes. "You transmitted a query packet to our deep-space archive division nine days ago. Requesting pre-Fracture shipping manifests, launch records, anything referencing a vessel called Meridian. Your encryption was good. Our archivists are better." She smiled, and there was nothing sharp in it, which somehow made it worse. "I'm not angry, Captain. I'm delighted. I've been waiting my whole career for someone to find that ship."

"You knew about it." Petra's voice came out flatter than they intended.

"I knew the name. Every schoolchild on Halcyon knows the name — we just call it a legend. The ship that launched the founders. The ark that carried our grandmothers' grandmothers across the dark." Reyes leaned back. "We tell it as a story. You people on Verdance don't tell it at all, do you? Interesting, that. Two colonies, one origin, and only one of us kept the memory." She tilted her head. "Or only one of us was allowed to."

"The signal," Petra said. "The distress call. Did your archives—"

"We never received it. Whatever frequency it's on, it doesn't reach us — or doesn't reach anyone who'd admit hearing it." Reyes's expression sobered by degrees, a tide going out. "But if what your query implies is true — if the Meridian is out there, intact, with its sleepers and its genetic archive—" She stopped herself, and Petra watched her do it, watched a woman who commanded a world deliberately rein in her own voice. "Then everything changes. For both of us."

"Both of us," Petra repeated.

"Captain." Reyes folded her hands on the table. "Your colony is starving. Don't look surprised — we read your grain futures the way you read our freight manifests. Verdance's topsoil is failing, your Conclave knows it, and your brother—" Petra went rigid, and Reyes had the grace to pretend not to notice, "—your brother Sefen's archival requests suggest the Conclave knows it too. The Meridian's archive could save them. Could save all of us; our gene lines are narrow too, we've just hidden it better behind abundance." She spread her hands. "I want to help. Fully. Ships, crews, salvage engineers, whatever the rescue requires. No conditions."

"No conditions," Petra said.

"One request." Reyes smiled again, rueful, self-deprecating. "Not a condition — a request. When the time comes to wake them... remember that five thousand of those sleepers are Halcyon's children. Their names are in our founding rolls. Their descendants are sitting in this city right now, eating dinner, not knowing their great-great-grandparents are frozen in the dark waiting for someone to come." Her voice softened into something that sounded, genuinely, like grief. "Don't let Verdance's panic decide their fate. That's all I ask. Fairness."

It was a beautiful speech. Petra recognized beauty when they heard it; they'd grown up on Verdance, where beauty was rationed too, and had learned young that the most beautiful things were often the most expensive.

"Governor," Petra said slowly, "the manifest splits evenly. Half bound for Verdance, half for Halcyon. The ship's own AI won't authorize waking either half without living authority from both worlds."

Something flickered behind Reyes's eyes — fast, gone before it could be named. Not surprise. Recalculation.

"An AI," she said. "How quaint. And how convenient, that it wants permission from governments that have spent two centuries pretending each other don't exist." She refilled Petra's glass herself, leaning close, and her voice dropped to a register meant for one person only. "Here is what I know that you may not, Captain. Verdance will never cooperate. Never. Whatever your Conclave told you when you left — whatever promises they made about shared rescue — the moment they understood what the Meridian holds, they started planning to take it alone. They've already recalled three ships from the long patrol. Ask yourself where they're going."

Petra kept their face still. Inside, something cold began to move. Three ships. Sefen hadn't mentioned ships.

"I'm offering you the only workable alliance on the table," Reyes continued, straightening, warmth restored like a lamp relit. "Help me wake the Halcyon half — legally, properly, with full documentation — and I'll send everything I have to save the other half afterward. On my honor, which on Halcyon is worth something. But sequence matters, Captain. My half first. Then theirs. That's not betrayal. That's arithmetic."

"And if Verdance moves first?"

Reyes held her gaze, unblinking, and for one unguarded instant Petra saw the thing underneath the warmth: a woman who had governed a paradise for twenty years and knew exactly what it cost to keep it green.

"Then Verdance gets nothing," she said pleasantly, "and I'll have done my duty to my own people. Which is, after all, what you're trying to do for yours." She raised her glass. "To the Meridian. May she finally come home."

Petra touched their glass to hers because refusing would have been a statement, and statements were ammunition.

* * *

They gave Petra a suite in the tower instead of the berth aboard the Kestrel. They gave Petra a tour of the archive division, where courteous staff pulled pre-Fracture records within minutes — minutes, after Petra had spent weeks coaxing fragments from grandmother's sealed boxes. They gave Petra a comm channel direct to the governor's office, priority-coded, and a credit chit with no stated limit, and a standing invitation to dinner.

Everything was given. Nothing was asked — except the one thing, restated nightly in different beautiful words: my half first.

On the fourth night, unable to sleep in a bed softer than any bed had a right to be, Petra stood at the suite's window looking down at Solmar glittering below, and made themselves say it honestly:

*You could say yes.*

That was the seduction of it, and Petra was honest enough to feel the pull. Verdance had answered the Meridian discovery with silence, then refusal, then — if Reyes was right — warships. Halcyon answered with light, food, open doors, a hand extended across two centuries of darkness. One world treated Petra as a traitor-in-waiting; the other treated them as a returning hero. Any reasonable person would choose the open door.

And the door was open precisely because Reyes believed Petra would walk through it carrying five thousand lives as a gift.

Petra thought of Vesper's voice in the dying dark of the Meridian, patient beyond all reason, asking only: *Will the gardeners come?* Not Verdance's gardeners. Not Halcyon's. The gardeners. As if the ship remembered a time before the worlds had split, when tending life had been a single profession with a single loyalty.

Grandmother's voice, from long ago, in the seed vaults: *"A gardener doesn't ask which sprout deserves the water, little one. A gardener asks what the garden needs."*

But the garden needs water now, Petra thought, and there are two hands holding jugs, and each one says pour mine first.

Their comm chimed. Priority channel — Reyes's office. Petra's stomach tightened.

"Captain Blackwood." It wasn't Reyes; it was an aide, crisp and neutral. "The governor wanted you to know immediately: our long-range array has detected drive signatures entering the region. Three vessels, Verdance registry, military transponders disabled. Estimated arrival at the Meridian's coordinates in eleven days." A pause, professionally apologetic. "The governor asks whether you'd like Halcyon to dispatch an escort for your return trip. She notes that time may now be... a factor in your sequencing decisions."

Petra looked out at the beautiful, generous city, glowing all the way to the horizon, and felt the walls of the open door pressing gently inward.

"Tell the governor," Petra said, "I need to make a call."

They keyed the encrypted relay to Sefen's private address — the one he'd sworn was clean, the one he'd used to warn them home — and waited while the light crawled across the dark toward a brother who might already be declared void, on a world already sending warships to a graveyard full of sleeping children.

The channel opened. Static. Then Sefen's voice, low and ragged and urgent:

"Petra — thank the stars. Listen to me. Before you trust anyone — before you trust *me* — you need to hear what I found in the sealed archives. It's worse than the Fracture. It's so much worse than the Fracture."
