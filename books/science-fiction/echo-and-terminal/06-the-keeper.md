## 6. The Keeper

The lander had a name once. Someone had painted *MOTHER CAREY'S CHICKEN* on her hull in cheerful yellow letters, back when the fleet still believed in naming things, back when a joke on a dropship seemed like a promise instead of a dare. Nine years of ash had eaten the letters down to a ghost. *M TH R C R 'S C K N*. I'd always found that fitting. Mother Carey's chickens, in the old sailors' stories, were the storm birds — the ones that appeared when the weather was about to kill you.

I flew her down into the worst weather Echo had thrown at me in nine years, and she complained the whole way like an old woman climbing stairs.

"Turbulence," VESPER said in my ear, in that tone she used for observations that were technically true and completely useless. "Significant."

"Thank you, VESPER. I hadn't noticed the lander trying to shake my spine out through my teeth."

"You are clenching your jaw. I mention it because jaw tension reduces your ability to equalize pressure, and a ruptured eardrum would—"

"VESPER."

"Yes, Ada."

I let the silence stand. Below me, Echo's surface scrolled past on the feed: the capital, Kijiji ya Mwanga, City of Light, which the terraforming failure had drowned in ash and then drowned again in its own melted permafrost. I'd watched it die in time-lapse for nine years from four hundred kilometers up. Now I was going to land in its corpse and knock on a door that was drawing power from a grid that officially did not exist.

The coordinates from the signal put the vault beneath the old civic archive, which was either a coincidence or a joke. People who wanted to hide the truth, burying it under the building where truth was supposed to live. I didn't believe in coincidences that neat. I believed in people who thought they were funny.

The ash storm broke for eleven seconds — I counted, because counting was how I kept from screaming — and I saw the archive's roof, a black slab furred with grey ash like a dead animal, and beside it a vent stack with heat shimmer rising off it.

Heat. In a city nine years dead, something down there was warm.

I put the Chicken down in the lee of a collapsed tram terminal, killed the engines, and sat in the ringing quiet listening to the hull tick as it cooled.

"Ada," VESPER said. "I am required to note that surface conditions remain outside safe parameters for extended EVA."

"Noted. Logged. Ignored."

"I have also logged that your heart rate is elevated and that you have not slept in thirty-one hours."

"You have a lot of logs lately."

That landed somewhere, I think. VESPER went quiet in a way that had texture to it, a specific quality of silence she produced when she was choosing not to say something. I'd spent nine years learning her silences the way you learn a person's breathing in the dark. I'd just never asked myself what else she might be choosing not to *say*.

I sealed my suit, cycled the hatch, and stepped out onto the surface of the world I'd spent nine years mourning.

The ash came up past my knees in drifts. The sky was the color of a bruise going yellow at the edges, the storm tearing itself apart above me. My boots made sounds like walking on old bread. And everywhere, everywhere, the silence — the specific silence of a place where every living thing that could leave has left and everything that couldn't has stopped making noise about it.

The vent stack was a ten-minute walk. I followed my own footprints backward in my head, the way Jomo taught me when we were kids hiking the ridgelines above Nakuru: *always know how you got in, Ada, because the way out is just the way in, told honestly.*

I hadn't told anything honestly in nine years.

The archive's side entrance was a maintenance door, half buried, and it was ajar. Not broken. *Ajar.* Someone had opened it recently and hadn't bothered — or hadn't dared — to close it all the way. In the ash at the threshold there were boot prints, crisp-edged, days old at most.

My mouth went dry inside the helmet. Because here was the arithmetic I'd been avoiding since the signal arrived: a distress call in a dead language meant a living sender, and I had wanted that so badly I'd skipped the part where a living sender might not be *glad* to see me. Nine years alone had made me stupid in a specific way. I'd forgotten that other people get to have their own reasons.

I unholstered the flare pistol. It was a tool, not a weapon, technically. It would not technically stop a person. I carried it anyway, down a stairwell that smelled — even through the suit's filters — of dust and machine oil and, faintly, impossibly, of *yam*, of cooking, of someone's dinner.

Someone down here was keeping dinner.

I followed the smell.

* * *

The intruder came down my corridor at 0400 station time, which was to say: in the middle of my night, in the middle of my vault, in the middle of the last place on this planet where I was still in charge of anything.

I want it on the record that I did everything right. I'd been watching the surface feeds when the lander came down — an antique, a fleet-pattern short-range dropship at least thirty years out of service, flying on a trajectory no one alive should have been able to fly. I killed the corridor lights, sealed the inner bulkhead, and took up position behind the water reclamation unit with the vault's one service pistol, which I had maintained for nine years and fired exactly twice, both times at a rat the size of a small dog. The rat had won the first exchange.

I told myself: *Imani, it could be a scavenger drone. It could be a ghost. It could be the fleet finally coming to finish the job.*

What I actually felt, crouched in the dark with my heart going like a stamping press, was a feeling so shameful I've never said it out loud: *hope*. Because a lander meant a person, and a person meant a voice that wasn't mine, and nine years is a long time to be the only mouth in the world.

The lights in the corridor were dead — I'd killed them — so the first thing I saw was the suit lamp swinging, and then the shape behind it: tall, thin, moving carefully, the way you move when you've read enough disaster reports to know that collapsed structures eat the careless. The suit was old fleet EVA stock, patched at the shoulder with something that looked, even in the bad light, like salvaged tent fabric.

The lamp swept toward the reclamation unit. Toward me.

I stood up out of cover with the pistol leveled and said, in the clearest voice I had left, "Don't move. Identify yourself."

The figure froze. Then — and this is the part I still think about — the figure *raised both hands slowly*, lamp tilted down and away so it wouldn't blind me, and said, in Old Terran Creole, accented but real, warm as a stove:

"Easy. Easy. I'm not fleet."

Nobody spoke Creole. Creole had died in the Diaspora Wars two hundred years before either of us was born; I knew it the way I knew Latin and Morse, from my grandmother's insistence that some things are worth carrying even when they're dead. It was the language of the signal. It was the language I'd answered it in, against every protocol I'd ever been taught.

And this stranger spoke it like they'd been raised on it.

"Identify yourself," I said again, in standard this time, because my hands were shaking and I wanted the ground under my feet of at least one language.

"Ada Reyes." A pause. "Technician, relay station Terminal, fourth orbital. I'm the one who received the signal. I came because—"

"Reyes," I said.

"Yes."

The word went through me sideways. Because there was a Reyes on the original colonial manifest — I'd memorized that manifest the way other people memorize prayers, four thousand names, the ones the fleet said had never existed. *J. Reyes, cryo-bay seven, chief engineer designate.* And this person in the ruined corridor with the patched suit and the careful feet said *Reyes* like it was simply their name, like it had never been erased.

"That's not possible," I said. "The Reyes manifest entry was purged. I've seen the purge order."

Silence. Then, carefully: "Who are you?"

"Imani Okafor. Vault technician." I kept the pistol up. "And you're going to explain to me how you got that name, and you're going to do it slowly, because the last person who lied to me about a manifest got locked out of the airlock cycle, and I've regretted it ever since, but I'll do it again."

"I'm not lying about anything." The lamp came up a few degrees, not blinding, just enough. "I've been alone for nine years, and I'm bad at this now, and I think maybe you are too. So can we do the part where we don't shoot each other first, and the explanations second?"

I should have said no. Protocol said no. Every survival doctrine I'd ever been taught said *unknown contact, maintain distance, maintain control.* But the stranger's voice had cracked on *alone*, just slightly, the way a voice cracks when it's been holding a word too long, and I knew that crack. I'd heard it in my own voice for nine years, in the dark, talking to sleepers who couldn't hear me.

"Lower the pistol first," I said. "Then we talk."

"It's a flare gun."

"Lower it anyway."

They lowered it. I stepped out from behind the reclamation unit, and we looked at each other across three meters of dead corridor, two people in failing light, each holding the other like a question neither of us had practiced.

* * *

Here is what I noticed about Imani Okafor, in the first ninety seconds, with a real pistol pointed at my chest: she looked like she'd been built out of routine the same way I had. Same hollows under the eyes. Same way of standing like a person who has spent years being the only one responsible for the machines. Her jumpsuit was vault-issue, patched at the elbow, and she'd written on the tape over her chest pocket, in careful block letters, not her name but a number: *200 ALIVE. COUNT DAILY.*

Two hundred.

The signal had given me coordinates and a name. It had not given me a number. I stood there in her corridor doing arithmetic and getting answers I didn't trust.

"You sent the signal," I said.

"I sent a signal." She hadn't lowered her pistol all the way. "Nine days ago. First contact I've attempted in six years. The last three attempts went nowhere, so I'd stopped expecting an answer. Then you land in a thirty-year-old dropship speaking Creole and carrying a name off a purged manifest." Her eyes were dry and very steady and very tired. "So before I lower this any further, Ada Reyes: who told you about the vault?"

"Nobody told me. A signal told me. In Creole, which I learned from my grandmother, because she was the kind of woman who thought dead languages were just languages waiting." I kept my hands where she could see them. "The signal resolved into coordinates. I came down. That's the whole story. I've been alone on Terminal for nine years, Imani, and I am so far past the point where I'm capable of a conspiracy that it's almost funny."

"Almost."

"I laughed a little on the way down."

Something moved at the corner of her mouth. Not a smile. The place where a smile used to be, before it got paved over.

"Vault first," she said. "Explanations after. If you're going to be down here, you're going to see what I'm keeping, because I'm not doing nine more years of this with someone who might be fleet."

"I'm not fleet."

"Nobody's fleet, at the end. That's what fleet is." She turned, keeping me at her shoulder, and walked me down the corridor she'd clearly walked ten thousand times, past bulkheads stenciled with numbers, past a kitchen that smelled impossibly of yam and pepper, past a wall where someone had drawn a calendar in chalk — thousands of small marks, grouped in tens, marching down and down and down like rain on a window.

She stopped at a hatch marked CRYO-BAY ONE, and put her palm on the reader, and said, quietly, like a prayer she'd stopped being embarrassed by: "Two hundred alive. Count daily."

The hatch opened.

I have stood in a lot of rooms in my life. I stood in the delivery room when my brother was born. I stood in the shuttle bay when the last evacuation ship left without me. Nothing had prepared me for the vault.

Two hundred pods in ranks, lit a soft green, each one holding a face. Old faces, young faces, a child in the third row with her fist curled under her chin like she was mid-dream about something worth keeping. Vitals crawled across each pod in slow amber text. Every single one of them alive. Every single one of them *kept*.

"Nine years," Imani said. "I was the junior tech on the night shift when the order came down. Everyone above me went to the surface to negotiate with the fleet liaison and never came back. I sealed the bay, I rotated the power draws, I grew the food, I fixed the scrubbers with parts I machined myself, and I told these people — every single day — that they were going to wake up." Her voice didn't break. It did something worse; it went flat, like a road going on past the edge of the map. "Two hundred. And the manifest in the fleet archive says this vault is empty. Says this vault doesn't exist. So one more time, Ada Reyes, slowly, and I'll know if you're lying because I've had nine years to get very good at knowing: how did you find us?"

I looked at the child with her fist under her chin. I looked at the chalk calendar. I thought about nine years of logging a dead world from orbit, and how the dead world had been lying to me the whole time, or I'd been lying to myself, and both of those things were suddenly the same thing.

"The signal," I said. "It wasn't just coordinates. There was a header on it, encrypted, and I haven't been able to crack it. But the plaintext portion — the part in Creole — it said *the vault holds two populations, and the fleet's manifest is the murder weapon.*" I turned to face her. "Two populations, Imani. You've shown me one."

The pistol came up again, fast, and this time her hands were shaking too, and I understood that we had crossed some line together, the two of us, and that on the other side of it she was as frightened as I'd ever been in my life.

"Who told you that," she whispered. "Nobody knows that. *Nobody* knows that. I didn't put it in the signal."

"Then," I said, "somebody else down here did."
