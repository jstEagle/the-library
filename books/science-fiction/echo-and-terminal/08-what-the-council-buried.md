## 8. What the Council Buried

The archive room smelled like dust and old ozone, which is to say it smelled like every other room in the vault, except this one had the decency to admit what it was.

I'd been putting off the deep logs for three days. There's a particular kind of cowardice that looks like diligence—recalibrating the cryo-lines a fourth time, re-inventorying the protein vats, alphabetizing a supply manifest that had been alphabetized since before I was born. Ada had gone back up to Terminal with a promise to return in forty hours and a look on their face like a person carrying something breakable in both hands. And me? I sat down at the terminal in the archive alcove, cracked my knuckles out of pure theater, because nobody was watching and habits need an audience, and pulled the fleet's sealed directory.

It took my credentials eleven seconds to reject me. It took Sefu's neural implant handshake four seconds to accept.

That should have been my first clue. The sleepers' implants opened doors that my expeditionary-wave clearance couldn't. We were told the colonial wave were passengers. Guests. People we carried and looked after. Nobody tells you the guests have better keys than the staff.

"Okay," I said to the empty room, because talking to yourself is only madness if you stop enjoying it. "Let's see what you're hiding."

* * *

The directory structure was beautiful, in the way a locked cabinet is beautiful. Everything labeled. Everything orderly. TERRAFORM PHASE LOGS — CYCLE 1 through 47. ATMOSPHERIC COMPOSITION — QUARTERLY. BIOSPHERE SEEDING REPORTS. HYDROLOGY. SOIL MICROBIOLOGY. A whole world's autopsy, filed by fiscal quarter.

I started at the beginning, because if you're going to be gutted, you might as well do it chronologically.

Cycle 1 read like a love letter. Oxygen percentages climbing. Algae blooms taking hold in the equatorial shallows. First successful soil conversion on the Kessel Flats. Somebody named Dr. Amara Osei wrote the summaries back then, and she wrote them like poetry for people who pretended they didn't read poetry: *The world is learning to breathe. We are teaching it.*

By Cycle 12 the tone changed. Not dramatically—that's the thing I kept snagging on, going back through it later, the way you keep touching a bruise to check it's still there. No crisis. No alarm. Just a slow migration of language from *we are teaching it* to *the model predicts* to *variance within acceptable parameters.* As if the planet had stopped being a patient and become a spreadsheet.

And then, Cycle 19, a single line buried in an appendix, formatted so innocently it took me three reads:

*Per Directive 7-C (Council Sealed), atmospheric seeding continues per schedule notwithstanding modeled outcome variance of 94.6% probability of biosphere collapse by Cycle 40.*

Notwithstanding. What a word. What a little gray word to sit on top of a death sentence. Not *despite*. Not *even though we know*. *Notwithstanding*, as though the collapse were a scheduling conflict. As though the model had raised its hand politely and the council had said, noted, thank you, proceed.

I sat there for a long time with my hand flat on the console, feeling the vault's heartbeat come up through the metal—the pumps, the coolant loop, the two hundred sleeping techs in the ring above me—and I thought: they knew. They knew at Cycle 19. That's thirty years before the ash storms. Thirty years before the evacuation order came down like a verdict from a court that never held a trial.

My mother spent her whole career on the surface crews. She used to come home with red dust in the creases of her knuckles and say the soil was finally turning, finally forgiving them. She died in the third year of the storms, in a habitat breach that the official log called "an unforecastable weather event."

Unforecastable. I found the forecast. It was filed under Appendix D, Cycle 19, ninety-four point six percent.

* * *

I don't know how long I read. Long enough that my tea went cold twice and I reheated it once and forgot about it entirely the second time, which for me constitutes a fugue state. The archive was thorough in the way guilt is thorough—it kept everything, even the things it meant to bury, because bureaucracies can't help themselves. Deleting takes effort. Filing takes none.

Directive 7-C appeared fourteen more times across the remaining cycles, each reference a little more sanitized than the last, until by Cycle 38 it was just "7-C compliance confirmed" with no attachment, no text, no explanation. Someone had been scrubbing as they went. But scrubbing a document leaves a shape behind—a hole exactly the size of what was removed—and the holes themselves told the story. Every deletion clustered around three topics: the collapse models, the colony's actual viability projections, and the neural archives.

That last one stopped me cold.

NEURAL ARCHIVE — STATUS: MAINTAINED PER DIRECTIVE 7-C, SUBSECTION 4.

Subsection 4. I dug for it. Most of the directive's subsections had been stripped clean, but Subsection 4 survived—maybe by accident, maybe because whoever did the scrubbing couldn't bring themselves to delete it, or maybe because some clerk a century dead decided that a record this obscene deserved a witness. It was two paragraphs. I've read them enough times now that I could recite them standing on my head, but the first time, I read them aloud, slowly, because I didn't trust my eyes.

*In the event of program termination, all primary documentation of Phases 1–47 shall be preserved exclusively within the neural archives of the colonial-wave complement, whose implant architecture retains full-fidelity recording capability. Expeditionary-wave personnel archives shall be purged upon standard rotation. This ensures continuity of record while maintaining operational information security.*

Operational information security.

They made the colonists into filing cabinets. Four thousand people, put under ice not just to survive but to *hold the evidence*—their own murder warrant, stored in their own heads, in a format only they could open. And us, the expeditionary wave, the technicians and crew who thought we were the mission? Our memories got wiped on rotation. Standard procedure. We weren't witnesses. We were turnover.

I laughed. I want to be honest about that, because everything after this gets very solemn and I think it matters that somewhere in the middle of it, alone in a room full of dead paperwork, I laughed until my ribs hurt, the ugly kind of laugh, the kind that's just grief wearing a costume. Because it was funny. It was genuinely, cosmically funny. My mother died believing the weather had betrayed her, and the forecast had been sitting in a sealed appendix the entire time, and the reason nobody ever found it was that the council had decided the truth would live in the heads of the sleeping and nowhere else, and everyone awake was too busy surviving to ask why the sleepers' implants had better keys than ours.

A whole world murdered by paperwork. Not by malice, or not only by malice—by *procedure*. By a directive with a number. By men and women in a council chamber somewhere on the flagship, drinking real coffee, deciding that Echo was an experiment whose results they already had, and the variable left to control was whether anyone would ever read the footnotes.

* * *

Sefu found me there. I heard his cane before I saw him—the old man taps it against the bulkhead frame when he enters a room, some habit from a ship he served on before the ice, a courtesy announcement: *a person is arriving, adjust accordingly.* I didn't adjust. I couldn't seem to make my body do anything except sit very still.

"You found it," he said. Not a question. He lowered himself into the chair beside mine with the sound of a man negotiating with his own knees. "Your face says you found it."

"How long have you known?"

"I've known since before the ice." He said it gently, which somehow made it worse. "Elder Adeyemi was elected for many reasons, Miss Okafor. One of them was memory. The council chose our archive carriers carefully. They wanted docile ones." His mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "They misfiled me."

"Why didn't you tell me? Three years, Sefu. I've kept your people alive for three years and you let me believe the storms were bad luck."

"And if I had told you?" He folded his hands over the cane. "You are a good technician and a stubborn woman, and you would have done exactly what you are doing now, except alone, three years earlier, with no one above you in orbit and no signal in a dead tongue and no stranger coming down through the ash with a lander and a plan. Grief keeps, Imani. It keeps better than protein. I waited for the moment it could be *used*."

I wanted to be angry at him. I reached for the anger the way you reach for a light switch in a familiar dark room, and my hand found nothing there. Because he was right, and because I recognized the strategy—I'd been doing a smaller version of it myself for nine years, sealing away the question of why the fleet abandoned a viable evacuation timeline, why the manifests didn't match the population counts, why nobody ever answered. I'd filed my suspicions under *later* and called it discipline.

"The sleepers," I said instead. "The 3,800. Their implants carry all of it. Every cycle. Every appendix. Every deleted directive."

"In full fidelity. They are the record, child. All of them. That was the design—not one witness to silence, but thousands, distributed, so that no single death could erase it." He tapped the cane once, softly, on the floor. "The council built their tomb and accidentally built a library."

"And the truce expires in—" I did the math and hated the number. "Eleven days. When it lapses, the fleet's authority consolidates. Whoever holds the official history—"

"—holds everything," Sefu finished. "And the council has had a hundred years to write theirs."

We sat with that for a while. The pumps breathed. Somewhere overhead, two hundred people slept inside their own skulls full of evidence, dreaming, if the techs were right about implant drift, in fragments—algae blooms, soil reports, a woman writing *the world is learning to breathe* while she signed its death warrant.

"There's something else," Sefu said, and his voice had changed, gone quieter, the way it does when he's about to hand someone something heavy and wants them to have time to set down whatever they're carrying. "Before your technician returns. Before we decide anything. You should see Cycle 44."

"The last one?"

"The last complete one." He leaned forward, working the console with the unhurried precision of a man who had learned interfaces older than either of our grandparents. "Everyone assumes the council ran the experiment and lost track of it. Let it fail through negligence. That is the generous version, and it is wrong." The file opened. "Read the authorization block."

I read it. Then I read it again, because the first time my brain refused to parse it, the way it refuses a word in a language you almost speak.

AUTHORIZATION: DIRECTIVE 7-C, FINAL PHASE. PROGRAM OUTCOME TO BE OBSERVED TO FULL COLLAPSE FOR CALIBRATION PURPOSES. SECONDARY SITE PREPARATION TO PROCEED IN PARALLEL. ECHO DESIGNATION RETIRED UPON CONFIRMATION.

Secondary site preparation. In parallel. They hadn't just watched Echo die—they'd needed it to die *completely*, cleanly, all the way to the end, because a collapsed world was data and a rescued world was a confounding variable. And somewhere else, in some other system, another blue-and-brown promise was being seeded by the same hands, using the calibration they'd wrung out of my mother's red dust and my father's flooded flats and four thousand people put on ice to serve as a hard drive.

"They're still doing it," I said. My voice came out flat and strange, like someone else's. "Somewhere out there. Right now. Another Echo."

"Perhaps several," Sefu said. "Which is why the record cannot simply survive, Miss Okafor. It must *travel*."

I looked at the countdown clock I'd pinned to the corner of my display—eleven days, some hours, ticking down toward a truce written by people who thought we were furniture—and I thought about Ada Reyes up there on Terminal, decoding a language two centuries dead, flying down through ash storms on nothing but a hunch and a shared surname. About Jomo, wherever he was in the lower rings, refusing to answer my messages, which meant he'd already figured out something was coming and was preparing for it in his own silent, infuriating way.

"Sefu," I said. "How fast can we wake Elder Council quorum without draining the power grid?"

He looked at me for a long moment, and in his face I saw the thing I'd been too angry to see for three years: not a keeper of secrets, but a librarian who had spent a century waiting for someone to finally request the book.

"Faster than is safe," he said. "Which is the only speed that will matter."

* * *

I sent the message up to Terminal that night, on the tight-beam, encrypted twice, addressed to Ada Reyes with a subject line I agonized over for twenty minutes before settling on the only honest option:

**FOUND IT. IT'S WORSE THAN WE THOUGHT. AND WE'RE NOT THE ONLY ONES THEY DID THIS TO.**

Then I sat back in the archive room, in the smell of dust and old ozone, surrounded by the paper ghosts of a murdered world, and waited for the stars to answer.
