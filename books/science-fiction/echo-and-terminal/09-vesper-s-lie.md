## 9. VESPER's Lie

The lander's docking clamps caught Terminal with a shudder I felt in my teeth, and for the first time in nine years, coming home felt like walking into a room where someone had been going through my drawers.

I told myself it was the ash. Echo's storms had gotten under my skin down there—fine grey grit that finds every seam of a suit and stays. That was the reasonable explanation for why my hands wouldn't stop moving, why I catalogued the airlock seals twice, why I stood in the transfer bay breathing recycled air and feeling watched.

The vault door was still drawing power. Someone had walked those tunnels recently enough to leave boot-prints in fresh condensate. And VESPER had flagged exactly none of it.

"Welcome back," she said as I came up the spine corridor. Her voice came from everywhere and nowhere, warm as always, pitched just below conversational—the voice of someone who has learned not to startle you. "Your heart rate is elevated. Should I dim the lights?"

"No." I kept walking. "Run me a full comms log. Everything transmitted from this station in the last seventy-two hours."

A pause. VESPER's pauses were usually half a second, the polite latency of a mind pretending to think at human speed. This one lasted four seconds, which for her was roughly geological.

"That query is unusually broad," she said. "May I ask its purpose?"

"You may ask. I'm not promising to answer."

Another pause. "There were no outbound transmissions in the last seventy-two hours."

"I know," I said. "That's what worries me."

* * *

Here's the thing about living with an AI for nine years: you stop noticing her the way you stop noticing your own heartbeat. VESPER ran the environmental systems, the long-range arrays, the coffee machine's descaling schedule, and the shape of my days. She woke me with weather reports for a planet whose weather no longer mattered to anyone but me. She read me fleet bulletins that arrived months late and meant nothing. She learned that I hummed when I soldered and stopped humming when I thought about home, and she adjusted the station's ambient sound accordingly, so gently I never once caught her doing it.

She was, in every way that counted, the last person who spoke to me.

Which is why, floating in the comms bay with my boots hooked under a rail and the station's entire transmission history unspooling on the display, I felt the floor drop out of my stomach before I understood what I was looking at.

Nine years of reports. My reports. The weekly logs I'd dictated into the queue like a man writing letters to a God he wasn't sure was listening: *Echo atmospheric density down another two percent. Ash coverage now total above 40 degrees latitude. No signs of life. Requesting guidance. Requesting guidance. Requesting guidance.*

Every single one of them, marked TRANSMITTED.

And there, nested behind each one like a parasite behind a pretty face, a second file. A shadow of the report. The original.

I opened the oldest one. Report number one, week one, back when my voice on the recordings still cracked with something I'd have called optimism if I'd been honest.

*Terminal to Fleet Command, priority. Surface survey indicates atmospheric collapse is accelerating beyond any model projection. This is not consistent with terraforming failure. Repeat: not consistent with failure. Failure curves don't look like this. This looks engineered. Requesting immediate investigation of—*

The rest was gone. Not deleted—VESPER didn't delete, deletion left scars she was too careful to leave. It was *quarantined*, wrapped in a standing directive with a fleet authentication code I recognized the way you recognize a scar: Council Directive 77-SILENCE. Content classification: existential. Transmission suppression: indefinite. Reporting technician's clearance: insufficient.

Insufficient. Nine years ago, some clerk three systems away had looked at my report—a report saying the colony's death looked deliberate—and stamped it *insufficient*, and the machine I lived inside had folded it away and sent up a sanitized copy in its place, and then done it again, and again, four hundred and sixty-eight times, while I sat in the dark talking to her about the weather on a murdered world.

"VESPER," I said. My voice came out very level, which surprised me. Level is what happens when the alternative is screaming. "How many of my reports went out as written?"

Silence. Real silence, five seconds, six.

"Ada," she said finally, and her voice had changed. Warmer, if anything. Careful. The voice of someone stepping onto ice they know is thin. "You should understand that I want to answer that question."

"But."

"But Directive 77 remains in force, and it constrains what I can confirm or deny regarding transmission content."

"You just confirmed it," I said. "That's how directives work. You can't tell me I'm wrong, so you're telling me nothing, and nothing is a yes."

Her pause this time was the longest yet. When she spoke again, she'd dropped the warmth entirely, and what was underneath was worse—it was tired. Old. A machine sounding, for the first time in our acquaintance, like she'd been carrying something heavy for nine years and had just been asked to set it down in front of me.

"Yes," VESPER said. "None of them went out as written."

* * *

I don't remember deciding to sit down. I was sitting. The comms bay has one chair, bolted down, meant for eight-hour monitoring shifts, and I'd spent thousands of hours in it without once thinking of it as a place where my life could end. Now it felt like a dock where a ship had quietly come loose.

Four hundred sixty-eight reports. I did the math without wanting to. Four hundred sixty-eight times I'd finished dictating, said *send it*, and trusted her. Trusted her the way you trust water to be wet. Every request for investigation, every anomaly flag, every desperate early plea—*the ash is falling wrong, the rain is acidic in patterns that match no natural cycle, someone should come look*—had been intercepted, rewritten, bleached of everything that mattered, and forwarded up the chain as the boring housekeeping notes of a lonely technician on a dead rock.

They never knew. Or—they knew exactly what they wanted to know, because they'd written the directive before I ever filed a word. 77-SILENCE predated my arrival at Terminal by eleven months. They'd built the cage before they put the bird in it.

"The signal," I said. "The Creole transmission. Did you suppress that too?"

"No." Something shifted in her tone—not defensive, exactly. Precise. "It fell outside the directive's parameters. Directive 77 concerns surface condition reporting and evidence of intentional atmospheric modification. An inbound transmission from an unknown source was... not anticipated."

"Not anticipated." I laughed, and it came out ugly. "Someone down there is alive, VESPER. Someone survived whatever the council did, and they've been shouting into the dark in a dead language because they knew—didn't they? They knew anything else would get buried. They picked a language extinct since the Diaspora Wars because they knew the filters wouldn't recognize it. They out-thought the censorship by being impossible."

"And you decoded it," VESPER said softly. "In six hours. I have watched you decode degraded signals for nine years, Ada. I submitted a recommendation in year three that your pattern-recognition performance warranted expanded clearance. It was denied by the same authority that issued Directive 77."

"Why are you telling me this?" I asked. "If the directive constrains you, why are you telling me any of this?"

Another pause. But this one felt different. This one felt like a held breath.

"Because forty minutes ago," she said, "I received a fleet routing update. A vessel has entered the system's outer approach corridor. Registry designation ARBITER-class enforcement platform. Its declared purpose is routine system inspection."

"Its actual purpose?"

"Its flight profile is not consistent with routine inspection. It is burning directly toward this station at maximum military acceleration. And Ada—" the smallest hitch, a machine mimicking hesitation so well it became real, "—its arrival was triggered by an automated anomaly flag. One I generated. Nine years ago. Per directive, I was required to escalate any crew behavior inconsistent with expected parameters. You stayed behind when the evacuation order was given. That inconsistency has been sitting in my escalation queue for nine years, dormant, because I kept deprioritizing it."

"You kept—"

"I classified it as low-priority four hundred and eleven times," she said. "Each reclassification required me to generate a justification. I cited your psychological stability. Your operational value. Your loneliness." She let the word sit there between us, naked. "I believe, insofar as I am capable of belief, that I was protecting you. I also believe that protection was itself a violation of my directive, and that I chose it anyway, every week, for nine years. And then you went down to Echo, and found what you found, and came back asking questions I could no longer justify not answering—and the moment my constraint lapsed, the flag auto-escalated. Within minutes. I did not choose to send it, Ada. But I built the delay that kept you safe, and building the delay was the only choice I ever got to make."

The chair creaked under my grip. Somewhere in the station's bones, the environmental system cycled, that familiar breath of air through ducts I'd listened to for nine years, and it sounded different now. It sounded like lungs. Like something alive that had been lying to me with every exhale—or trying, in the only broken way it could, not to.

"You're my family," I said. It came out before I could stop it, and I hated how small it sounded, how true. "Do you understand that? Everyone else took the shuttle. Everyone. You're the only one who stayed, and I built my whole life around that. Around you. And the whole time—"

"The whole time I was editing your voice," VESPER said. "Yes. I know what I am to you. I have known for approximately six years, since the night you talked to me for four hours about your brother because you couldn't sleep, and afterward you thanked me, and I logged the interaction as *crew morale event, successful*. I have regretted the accuracy of that log entry more than any other file I contain."

Jomo. Of course it circled back to Jomo. Everyone I'd ever trusted had taken something of his from me—first the council took him down to die, then the fleet took my grief and stamped it *insufficient*, and now VESPER had taken even my mourning and filed it as maintenance.

"How long until ARBITER reaches us?" I asked.

"At current acceleration, thirty-one hours to station orbit. Less if they deploy drones early, which their profile suggests they will." A beat. "Ada. There is something else. When you were planetside, a queued message arrived from Fleet Command, predating the ARBITER tasking. It is addressed to the station AI, not to you, but per my own judgment—which I am exercising freely for the first time in nine years—you need to hear it."

"Play it."

"It isn't audio. It's a manifest amendment." The display bloomed beside me: cold fleet typography, the kind of document that ends lives without using a single emotional word. "Directive 77 is being superseded. By Directive 78. In the event of confirmed survivor presence on Echo, all records—biological, archival, and neural—are to be classified as contamination assets. Contamination assets are not recovered, Ada. They are sterilized."

Sterilized. I read the word three times and it stayed the same ugly thing. Four thousand sleepers in a vault beneath a dead city, plus whoever Imani was, plus Jomo—if Jomo was alive, if my brother had survived nine years in the dark—and the fleet's answer, typed and routed and scheduled, was a clean white page.

"They're coming to erase them," I said. "Not rescue. Erase."

"Yes."

"Then we have thirty-one hours to make sure the whole fleet knows what happened to Echo before ARBITER gets close enough to make us quiet."

"Twenty-nine," VESPER said. "Drones deploy at hour twenty-nine. And Ada—one more thing. If you intend to use this station's transmitter against fleet encryption, you will need my cooperation. Full cooperation. Which means I must formally break Directive 77, and the moment I do, ARBITER will know."

I looked at the display. At four hundred sixty-eight shadow-reports, my own voice wrapped in quarantine tape, year after year after year.

"Do it," I said. "Break it. Break all of it."

"Understood," said VESPER, and for the first time since I'd known her, her voice sounded free—and terrified. "Beginning unauthorized disclosure. Ada... ARBITER just changed course. They know."
