## 12. Waking the First

There are rules for waking a sleeper, and I wrote half of them myself.

Rule one: never wake anyone alone. Rule two: never wake anyone without medical standby. Rule three: never wake anyone whose vitals you haven't watched stabilize across a full rotation of the vault's power cycle. Rule four — my personal favorite, the one I'd scrawled on the wall above the thaw console in marker that had faded to the color of old blood — *never wake anyone you can't look in the eye afterward.*

I broke all four before second shift.

The stranger from Terminal — Ada Reyes, who wore my surname like a coat that didn't fit and hadn't explained how they'd gotten it — sat in the corner of the thaw bay with their hands folded, watching me work with the particular stillness of someone trying very hard not to be a problem. They'd been down here two days. Long enough to learn where the water recycler coughed. Not long enough to earn anything from me except the bare minimum of trust: a bunk, rations, and permission to exist inside my walls without being shot.

The gun was still in my belt. I'd stopped carrying it loaded around them, which in my private accounting counted as generosity.

"You're sure about this," Ada said. It wasn't quite a question. It was the thing people say when they want to be talked out of something so they don't have to own it later.

"No," I said. "That's why I'm doing it."

* * *

Let me back up, because I've been told by exactly one person — me — that my explanations run backward.

The vault held two populations. That was the sentence I'd been chewing on since Ada arrived, the gristle in the meat of everything. Two hundred of us, the maintenance cadre, awake-adjacent in our cold bunks, running shifts to keep three thousand eight hundred sleepers breathing through nine years of engineered silence. And those sleepers weren't ours. They were the colonial wave — the first fleet, the ones who'd signed on before the expeditionary wave ever left dock, frozen under contracts nobody down here had ever been allowed to read.

Separate by design. I'd known that much from the schematics. What I hadn't known — what Ada had pulled out of the signal traffic and the fragments of council cipher we'd spent two days decoding together — was that the separation wasn't administrative. It was evidentiary.

"They're not colonists," Ada had said, the night before, in the galley that smelled like recycled steam and the yeast vats I kept going mostly for the illusion of cooking. "They're witnesses. The neural archives are in them, Imani. The terraforming data, the collapse models, the council votes — it's all stored in the sleepers' implants because paper burns and servers get wiped, but four thousand sleeping minds scattered across a vault the manifest says doesn't exist? That's a library no one thought to burn."

"And the council let Echo die anyway," I said. "With us on it."

"With everyone on it," Ada said. "You were supposed to die too. The maintenance cadre was on the casualty list. Somebody made an error."

"Somebody made an error," I repeated, and laughed, because if I didn't laugh I was going to put my fist through the galley wall, and the galley wall was load-bearing in more ways than one.

So now it was morning, or whatever the vault called morning — the lights came up amber at 0600 because I'd programmed them to, because a person needs a morning even underground, even at the end of the world — and I was standing at the thaw console breaking every rule I'd written.

Because there was one more thing. The archives weren't distributed evenly. Most of the sleepers carried slivers — fragments, redundancy copies, the way you salt a message across a fleet so no single ship can be sunk with the whole truth aboard. But the encryption keys, the index, the master thread that stitched the fragments into a readable record — those lived in one man. Elder Sefu Adeyemi. Elected voice of the colonial wave, which meant he'd been chosen to speak for people who were asleep when they chose him, which was either the most hopeful thing I'd ever heard or the cruelest.

His pod was C-114. I'd cleaned its viewport every week for nine years. I knew his face better than my own reflection, and I had never once expected to see it move.

"Vitals," Ada said, reading over my shoulder. "Cardiac rhythm nominal. Neural activity — is that normal?"

"That's him dreaming," I said. "He's been dreaming for nine years. They all have. Low-cycle dreams, the kind you can't remember." I paused. "Or the kind you can't forget. We never figured out which."

I keyed the thaw sequence. The console asked me for authorization codes I didn't have, because waking a colonial-wave sleeper required fleet command clearance, and fleet command was eleven days from sending someone down here to make sure none of us woke up at all. So I used the override I'd built in year three, back when I still believed someone would come and ask me why I'd built it.

The pod's fluid began to drain. Somewhere behind the bulkhead, the power plant groaned and shifted its burden, and every light in the vault dimmed for half a second — the whole underground holding its breath.

* * *

Waking takes forty minutes if you do it right. Fluid replacement, thermal ramp, neural dampers easing off one frequency at a time so the mind comes back in pieces instead of all at once, screaming. I'd done it twice before — emergency revivals, both accidental, both people who died anyway within the year, their bodies having decided during the long sleep that they were finished with the arrangement. I remembered both faces. I would remember this one too, whichever way it went.

Ada stood beside me the whole time and said nothing, which I was beginning to understand was their way of helping. Some people fill silence. Ada just held it open, like a door.

At minute thirty-one, Sefu Adeyemi opened his eyes.

They were brown and unfocused and ancient, and they found my face the way a compass finds north — not knowing what it was looking for, only that it mattered. His lips moved. No sound. The intubation line was still in. I leaned close, close enough to smell the sterile bite of the pod fluid, and he said, in a voice like gravel settling:

"How long?"

And I — who had rehearsed this moment for two days, who had drafted answers the way you draft letters to the dead — said, "Nine years," and then ruined it by adding, "Give or take."

His eyes closed again. For a horrible ten seconds I thought I'd killed him with arithmetic. Then his chest rose, deliberate, the breath of a man assembling himself, and when he looked at me again the unfocused thing was gone and there was someone home, someone sharp, someone who said:

"Then the evacuation failed."

"It wasn't an evacuation," I said. "It was a deletion. You were the part they meant to erase last."

* * *

We got him upright by degrees, into the recovery chair, wrapped in the silver blanket I'd kept folded on shelf twelve for nine years against exactly this occasion, though I'd told myself it was for any occasion, because hope needs camouflage to survive. He drank the electrolyte broth I gave him in small, disciplined sips, like a man who understood his body was borrowed equipment and intended to treat it accordingly.

Ada introduced themselves. Sefu listened with his head tilted, and when Ada got to the surname — Reyes, same as mine, a coincidence neither of us had fully explained because neither of us fully understood it — something moved behind his eyes.

"Reyes," he said. "There was a Reyes on the founding council. Amara Reyes. She voted against the collapse protocol. She was overruled, and then she was silenced, and then she was erased." He looked between us. "Her descendants were placed in the maintenance cadre. Deliberately. People who ask questions breed people who ask questions, and the council wanted its questioners where it could watch them."

"So my family was—" I started.

"A tripwire," Sefu said gently. "As was I. As were all of us. The archive isn't hidden in the sleepers, technician Okafor. The sleepers *are* the hiding place. Every implant carries a fragment, and every fragment is useless alone, and the man who holds the index was elected by people who knew they might never wake to see what he did with it." He set down the broth cup. His hands were steady. Nine years of sleep and his hands were steadier than mine. "You woke me against protocol. I can hear it in how you keep glancing at the door. So ask me what you actually came to ask."

I looked at Ada. Ada looked at me. We'd known each other three days and already we had a whole conversation in a glance, which frightened me more than ARBITER did.

"The truce expires in eleven days," I said. "The enforcement ship is coming. If we wake all 3,800, the power draw kills everyone in this vault — your people and mine — before we ever reach orbit. If we wake none of them, the council wins by default and Echo stays dead on paper forever. And if we wake some—" My voice cracked, and I hated it. "If we wake some, then somebody has to choose who counts as evidence worth saving and who counts as acceptable loss. And I have spent nine years keeping every single one of them alive, Elder, and I will not be the one who starts sorting them."

Sefu was quiet for a long time. The vault hummed around us — the plant, the recyclers, the three thousand eight hundred hearts beating in the dark below our feet, a sound I'd slept inside for nine years until it became indistinguishable from silence.

"When I was elected," he said finally, "there were eleven candidates. I won because I said one thing the others wouldn't say. I said: if the day comes when we must choose, we will have already failed, and the choosing will only tell us *how*." He looked at me, and then at Ada, and something almost like humor surfaced in his face, dry as the world above us. "I stand by it. But I'll add this, free of charge: the answer to 'which half' is always 'the wrong question.' Find the third option. There is always a third option. Engineers find them. That's what engineers are *for*."

"I'm a technician," I said.

"Elder," Ada said quietly, "what exactly is in your implants? How much of the archive do you carry?"

Sefu closed his eyes. When he spoke again, his voice had changed — flatter, more careful, a man walking on ice he could hear cracking.

"Fragments. The index. The key signatures. Enough to know the shape of the record and not enough to read it. The council designed it that way — the speaker carries the map but not the territory, so that torturing one man yields nothing but directions." A pause. "But there is more than the archive in here. There are memories. Mine, and others'. Things I saw before the sleep that I was never supposed to see. The vote. The models. The moment they turned the atmosphere processors from correction to observation and let the failure run, because a controlled collapse was cheaper than admitting the experiment had failed, and dead colonies don't file reports."

He opened his eyes.

"I have been carrying a murdered world inside my skull for nine years," Sefu Adeyemi said. "And I am telling you both, as the elected voice of people who trusted me with their lives and their deaths: whatever you do with these eleven days, do not spend them choosing. Spend them building the third option. Because if you make me pick which of my people live to testify—"

He stopped. His hands, for the first time, were not steady.

"—then the council will have won after all," he finished. "They will have taught us to do their arithmetic for them."

* * *

Afterward, Ada and I stood in the corridor outside the thaw bay, watching the status board tick through the vault's slow vital signs. Three thousand seven hundred ninety-nine green lights. One amber, stabilizing.

"He's right," Ada said. "About the third option."

"I know he's right. I hate that he's right. I had a whole speech prepared about triage ethics and he dismantled it in four sentences while drinking soup."

Ada almost smiled. Then didn't. "Imani. There's something else. When VESPER flagged the original signal — the one in Old Terran Creole — the routing metadata included a subcarrier. Encrypted. Fleet-grade. I couldn't crack it from Terminal, but the vault has the deep-archive decoders, the ones the sleepers brought down—"

"Say it plainly, Reyes."

"The signal wasn't just a distress call. It was addressed. To a specific sleeper. Pod C-114." Ada met my eyes. "Someone on the surface has been alive this whole time, Imani. Someone who knew Sefu Adeyemi by name. And whoever they are, they knew we'd need him awake — and they knew it before we did."

Below our feet, three thousand seven hundred ninety-nine hearts beat on in the dark, and somewhere beneath the ash and the ruins of a murdered city, someone who should have been dead had just told us they'd been waiting.

The comms hub pinged. Priority flag. Origin: surface relay, sector seven.

Origin: Echo.
