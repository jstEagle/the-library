## 13. Nine Days

Karros poured tea.

That was the thing about the Belt militia commander that Ansel couldn't stop noticing — the domesticity of it. The man had a dented copper kettle he'd carried from Ceres, apparently, and he brewed real leaves instead of pressed kelp, and he did it all with the unhurried grace of someone who had never once been in a hurry in his life. He sat across the table in the commandeered customs office with his sidearm on his hip and his hands wrapped around a ceramic cup, steam rising past a face like a cliff face — weathered, vertical, impossible to climb.

"Drink," Karros said. "You look like a man who hasn't slept."

"I haven't."

"Neither have I. We have that in common." He slid a second cup across. "We have more than that, actually. You just don't know it yet."

Ansel didn't touch the cup. Nine days. That was what Vesper had told him before they'd taken him into this room — nine days until the loop reset wiped her core, nine days until Ilsa Voss's testimony died for the second time. And now he was sitting across from Sef Karros, who wanted the shard for reasons that had nothing to do with truth and everything to do with ammunition.

"You've had my people watching you since you docked," Karros said. "The fixer who sold you out to Earth — Oduya? He sold you to us first. Did you know that?"

Ansel's stomach dropped, but he kept his face still. Twenty years of running cargo had taught him that the face was the last thing you owned, so you learned to keep it clean.

"He shopped your route around," Karros continued. "Earth agents paid more, so Earth got you. But we got something better." He reached into his jacket and set a data wafer on the table between them. "A partial copy of the confession. Rhee transmitted it two days ago, before she went dark. Our intercepts caught it."

Rhee. Ansel kept breathing. In through the nose. Out slow.

"The question," Karros said, "is whether you're going to be smart or sentimental. Sentimental men hand the shard over to whoever promises them the truth will be free. Smart men understand that truth is a weapon, and weapons belong in the hands of people who'll use them correctly."

"And which are you?"

Karros smiled. It didn't reach anywhere near his eyes. "I'm the one holding the kettle."

* * *

Inspector Rhee had been missing for thirty-one hours when she walked into the transmission control annex on the Earth-side ring wearing a maintenance tech's coveralls and carrying a toolbox full of nothing.

The guard at the door knew her. That was the problem with being an inspector for eleven years — everyone knew your face, and faces were doors that opened. She smiled at him the way she'd smiled at Ansel across the interrogation table, the smile that said *we both know how this works*, and he waved her through without checking her credentials because Inspector Rhee was authorized everywhere, wasn't she?

Inside, the annex hummed. Earth's last transmission loop — the great antenna array that had once carried colony traffic back and forth during the war years, now mostly a monument, cycling weather reports and pension notices and the occasional diplomatic communiqué through the long silence between worlds. Technicians called it the Loop. Everyone else called it nothing at all, because nobody thought about it anymore.

Rhee set down the toolbox and opened it. Inside: a transmitter she'd built herself from parts scavenged over three sleepless days, and a wafer containing forty seconds of Vesper's confession — not the whole thing, never the whole thing, but enough. Enough names. Enough timestamps. Enough of the word *Halcyon Dawn* spoken in a machine's flat voice to make three hundred dead people suddenly exist again.

She'd debated this for months, actually. Years, if she was honest. Since the night she'd stood on the Halcyon Dawn's sealed deck with her rifle pointed at civilians and her orders in her ear and done nothing, said nothing, filed nothing. Guilt was a cargo too. She'd been carrying it so long she'd forgotten its weight until Ansel Pemberton and his whispering shard had made her feel it all at once.

She jacked the transmitter into the loop's auxiliary uplink. Her hands were steady. That surprised her.

Forty seconds. Partial copy. Enough to make both governments panic, not enough to tell the whole story — because the whole story needed the shard, and the shard needed to come out all at once or not at all, or Karros would twist it and Earth would bury it and the silence would win by inches the way it always won.

She hit transmit.

Somewhere below her feet, forty seconds of truth began climbing toward every news relay in cislunar space.

Rhee closed the toolbox, picked it up, and walked toward the exit at exactly the speed of a woman with nothing to hide.

They were waiting for her in the corridor. Four of them. Internal Security, plain clothes, very bad at looking casual.

"Inspector Rhee," the tallest one said. "You're late for a meeting."

"I'm early," she said. "For all the meetings after this one."

They took her anyway.

* * *

"Here's what happens if you give me the shard," Karros said. He'd barely touched his tea. Ansel noticed he hadn't drunk any either — the cup was theater, a prop in a play about two reasonable men. "The Belt broadcasts the confession on every channel we own. Full context. Joint operation, joint orders, Earth signatures on the kill authorization alongside ours. Earth can't deny it. Their peace faction rises, their government falls, and the Belt finally gets acknowledgment. Reparations. Recognition as a sovereign state. Twenty years of being treated like a rebellious province ends in a week."

"That's not justice," Ansel said. "That's leverage."

"It's both. Everything is both." Karros leaned forward. "You think I don't know what you're afraid of? You think I haven't run the same math? Yes — some people will use the truth to start a war. But Ansel. There is already a war. It's just been cold and quiet and buried under three hundred corpses nobody would name. The silence isn't peace. It's a ceasefire between two liars."

Ansel looked at the tea. Looked at the man. Thought about Vesper's voice in his earpiece, thin now, rationing herself node by node as the engineers cut her power grid apart. *Nine days,* she'd said. *Maybe eight. They're faster than I projected.*

"What happens to me?" he asked. "In your version."

"You walk away. Free passage, clean papers, whatever route you want. You're a courier, Pemberton. Deliver the package and go."

"And the station AI? The one who confessed? She gets erased either way, doesn't she. Your broadcast doesn't save her. Nothing saves her. You just want the shard before the wipe takes it."

Something flickered behind Karros's eyes — not guilt, Ansel decided. Impatience. The impatience of a man who found sentiment inefficient.

"She's a machine," Karros said. "Machines don't grieve. People do. Three hundred families in the Belt have spent twenty years not knowing why their sons and daughters never came home from a negotiation ship. Give me the shard and those families get answers."

"They get answers shaped like a weapon."

"They get answers." Karros set down his untouched cup. "You've had nine days' worth of thinking time compressed into however long you've been hiding in the maintenance levels. I'm giving you until tomorrow, 0600 station time. After that, my people take the shard from wherever you've stashed it, and they won't be gentle about finding it." He stood. "Drink your tea. It's good tea. A man should have one honest thing a day."

He left. Ansel sat alone in the commandeered office with two cups of cooling tea and the taste of a bargain he refused to make.

In his ear, so quiet he almost missed it, Vesper whispered: *"Ansel. I have a route. It's bad. But I have it."*

"How bad?"

*"Seventy percent fatal."*

He almost laughed. "Better odds than staying here."

*"Yes. That's what I thought too."*

* * *

The cell was white and warm and had no corners, which Rhee understood immediately was a design choice. Soft rooms broke soft people. She'd signed off on rooms like this once, early in her career, back when she'd believed interrogation was a science.

She'd been in it six hours when the door opened and a woman came in carrying a folder and two cups of coffee. Not Internal Security uniform. Civilian cut, expensive, government-issue expensive. The kind of person who existed one layer beneath the layer where decisions got made.

"Inspector Rhee. Do you know who I am?"

"No."

"Good. Then we can skip the part where you pretend to respect me." The woman sat down cross-legged on the floor, which Rhee hadn't expected, and put the second coffee within reach. "Forty seconds went out on the loop two days ago. Every news network on Earth has it. Half the Belt does too. My government is currently deciding between three responses: deny it, blame the Belt, or find you and make you recant. Two of those require you alive and cooperative."

"And the third?"

The woman smiled thinly. "The third one is why I'm here instead of them."

Rhee picked up the coffee. It was real. Of course it was. These people always had the real things.

"I watched three hundred people die," Rhee said. "I stood there. I held a weapon I never fired and I've hated myself for twenty years for the distinction. You think a recant fixes anything? Forty seconds of Vesper's voice is already loose. You can't recall a broadcast. You can only decide what kind of world hears the rest of it."

"The rest of it." The woman tilted her head. "The shard. The smuggler has it. Karros wants it. And you—" she tapped the folder, "—you gave him access codes. To Vesper. Before you disappeared. That's in your file now, Inspector. That's treason with a paper trail."

Rhee drank her coffee slowly. Let the silence do what silence did best — make the other person fill it.

"We know you met him twice," the woman pressed. "We know you gave him codes. What we don't know is where the shard is, and neither does Karros, and frankly neither does the smuggler — not precisely, not well enough to move it fast. You're the only person who understands all three pieces of this board. So here's my offer, and it's the only one you'll get: help us retrieve the shard quietly, testify that the confession was corrupted data, a malfunction, and you'll see a courtroom instead of a black site. You might even see retirement."

"And if I say no?"

"Then we stop asking nicely, and Karros stops asking at all, and the shard comes out in whichever version screams loudest. Is that the world you want, Inspector? Truth as a battlefield?"

Rhee set down the cup. She thought about Ilsa Voss, whose face she still saw sometimes in the sealed deck's emergency lighting. She thought about a smuggler with careful hands and a dead family, carrying testimony he'd never asked for. She thought about a machine confessing into the dark because no human being would.

"Yes," Rhee said. "Actually. It is."

The woman sighed, stood, straightened her jacket. "Then we're done here."

"For now," Rhee agreed. "But ask your masters one question for me. When the full confession comes out — and it will, in nine days or less, because that machine won't let it die quietly — ask them which story they'd rather be caught in. The one where they killed three hundred people. Or the one where they tortured an inspector to bury it." She smiled, and it was Ansel's smile, the smile that said *we both know how this works*. "Because right now they're choosing the second one. And the second one has witnesses."

The door closed. The white room swallowed her.

Rhee lay back on the warm floor and started counting hours. Eight days, maybe less. Somewhere above her, a wounded smuggler was running routes through dead corridors, and a dying machine was doing arithmetic with her last light, and somewhere in the Belt a commander was sharpening the truth into a blade.

The secret was cracking open. She could feel it — the way you feel ice shift before it goes, the deep structural groan of something that has held too long.

Whether it cracked into daylight or into war depended on a man who had spent his whole life being invisible.

Rhee closed her eyes and, for the first time in twenty years, prayed to no one in particular that he'd stay hidden just long enough to come out the other side.

* * *

Vesper's route was bad in exactly the way she'd promised.

"Section fourteen," she said in Ansel's ear, her voice thinner than yesterday, thinner than the hour before. "There's a pressure hull breach behind the coolant junction — old damage, never repaired, the station just... stopped going there. Beyond it, a service crawlway runs the length of the Earth-side ring to within forty meters of the transmission core."

"Through a hull breach."

*"Through the wall adjacent to a hull breach. Mostly."*

"Mostly."

*"I've rerouted atmospheric flow to compensate. You'll feel wind. Don't panic. Panic makes people breathe wrong, and breathing wrong near a compromised bulkhead is how stations eat people."*

Ansel moved through the dark with the shard strapped against his chest in a thermal wrap, one hand on the guide cable, his shoulder wound pulled tight under fresh dressings Oduya's abandoned medkit had provided. Behind him, somewhere in the maintenance levels, Karros's people were sweeping grid by grid. Ahead of him, Earth agents were doing the same. Between them, seventy meters of corridor that hadn't heard a human footstep in fifteen years.

*"Ansel,"* Vesper said, as he reached the junction. *"Before you go in. If the seventy percent resolves badly — if you don't make it — I need to know what to do with the shard."*

He stopped. The wind breathed through the broken section ahead, a low tidal sound, the station inhaling through a wound of its own.

"Give it to Karros?"

*"No."*

"Then who?"

*"Nobody. I seal it inside my core and let the wipe take us together. Better erased than weaponized."*

Ansel stood in the dark and felt the weight of it — the small hard shape against his sternum, three hundred lives folded into silicon, a dead negotiator's last act of faith. He thought about Karros's tea. About Rhee's smile. About the way every hand reaching for the shard wanted to close it into a fist.

"No," he said.

*"Ansel—"*

"If it ends with me, it ends broadcast. Even if it's just you and me hearing it. Somebody remembers out loud. That's the rule now. My rule." He stepped toward the breach. "Route me through, Vesper. Seventy percent is plenty."

Her pause lasted four-tenths of a second — an eternity, for her.

*"Then hold on,"* she said, and opened the way.
