## 3. Static Is a Weapon

The manifests came to Jude the way confessions come to priests: late at night, in a language nobody wanted to speak aloud.

They had pulled seven years of cargo records for Station Meridian's docking ring—every hauler, every pallet, every tonnage entry from the quarter of the massacre. On paper it was the dullest reading in the system. Grain substrate. Coolant slurry. Medical isotopes with half-lives shorter than the voyage. And threaded through all of it, like a hairline crack through glass, was static.

Not noise. Jude had spent enough years listening to corrupted telemetry to know the difference. Noise was random, democratic, indifferent. This had *shape*. It clustered around specific timestamps—always within minutes of the decompression event that officially never happened—and it always resolved, when Jude ran it through spectral decomposition, into the same signature: a carrier wave folded inside itself like a hand closing over a mouth.

They sat in the hauler's cramped data bay with a mug of tea gone cold and their sister's accident file open on one screen and forty children's pooled dream-fragments on another, and they watched the two datasets agree.

*Corridor 9-C.* The dream-children said corridor 9-C. The static signature peaked at corridor 9-C's junction sensors. The manifest showed a cargo transfer logged there at 03:14 station time—a transfer of nothing. Zero mass, zero volume, zero return. A shipment of absence, billed and paid.

Jude's hands were steady. That surprised them. Seven years of steadiness, drilled into them by every investigator who'd ever told them that a clean record was the only armor worth wearing. *Don't make waves, Underhill. Don't get named. Invisible investigators close cases; visible ones become cases.*

Their sister had been visible. Their sister was dead. And thirty-nine children—no, forty-one now, the dream still spreading, two new names added just this morning—were carrying the massacre in their skulls like shrapnel no surgeon could reach.

Jude traced the zero-mass shipments backward. Not to Meridian's suppliers. Not to any corporate entity with a name and a logo. To a shell leasing company, and through it, to a single recurring line item: field maintenance, array calibration, *atmospheric conditioning services*—billed monthly, for seven years, to a vessel registered out of Ceres.

Their own vessel.

Jude looked up from the screen at the hauler's familiar hum, the vibration in the deck plates they'd slept on for a decade, and felt the floor tilt under a truth that had been sitting in the room with them the whole time. The erasure weapon wasn't aboard Meridian. Meridian was where it *fed*. The source—the transmitter, the folding wave, the hand over the mouth—had been riding with them. In the array mast they'd never questioned. In the calibration cycles scheduled by someone else's remote authority. Every time Jude docked near Meridian, every time they took a claim in contested orbit, they had been the bellows pumping static across the station's memory banks.

They hadn't just failed to see it. They had *carried* it.

"Oh," Jude said quietly, to the empty bay, to the cold tea, to a dead woman who couldn't hear. "Oh, you clever, cowardly bastards."

* * *

Ondine Marsh arrived at 06:00 sharp, which was how Jude knew something had already gone wrong.

Marsh didn't do early. Marsh did punctual, polished, and precisely as helpful as her contract required—no more. She came up the docking tube in a charcoal suit that probably cost more than Jude's annual claim settlements, smiling the smile of a woman who had rehearsed bad news until it looked like good news.

"Underhill." She set a slate on the galley table without being offered a seat. "I've been asked to inform you that your investigation is being reclassified. Corporate security matter. You'll be compensated for your time, of course. Generously."

"Reclassified," Jude repeated. "Meaning buried."

"Meaning *resolved*." Marsh's smile didn't flicker, but her eyes did the accounting—they always did, little ledger movements behind the courtesy. "The children's testimony is inadmissible. Shared dreaming is not evidence, it's folklore. And frankly, Underhill, the families would prefer this end quietly."

"The families haven't heard from you in seven years."

"The families will be *grateful*." She tapped the slate. A document bloomed: a settlement offer, a non-disclosure clause, and beneath both, in language so clean it took Jude a full second to parse its obscenity, a medical annex. *Cognitive remediation services, voluntary, fully funded, recommended for all minors exhibiting post-traumatic confabulation.*

Remediation. Jude read the term twice. It wasn't therapy. Therapy left scars and stories. This was the same signature from the manifests, packaged clinical, dosed per child. A targeted wipe. The dream-memory wouldn't fade—it would be *excised*, root and branch, and the children would wake up lighter, emptier, officially healed.

"You're going to take what's left of them," Jude said.

"I'm going to give them peace." Marsh gathered her slate, and at the airlock she paused—not turning back, speaking to the frame, to the station beyond, to whoever might be listening. "You were never part of this, Underhill. That was always your best quality. Don't ruin it now."

The airlock cycled. Jude stood in the galley with the settlement offer glowing on the table and understood, with terrible clarity, the shape of the next twelve hours. Once the remediation orders were signed—once the first child was dosed—there would be no witnesses left who remembered. No case. No names. Just static, forever, and Jude's clean record gleaming on top of the grave like frost.

They had spent seven years staying invisible. Invisible people don't stop executions. They just get to say afterward that they didn't know.

Jude knew.

* * *

The array mast was cold. It always was—unshielded metal drinking the dark—but tonight Jude felt the cold as something earned, something honest, after years of sleeping beside a lie.

They climbed the maintenance ladder with a hardline spool over one shoulder and the children's pooled fragments compressed onto a crystal core in their breast pocket, right over the heart, which felt dramatic and also exactly correct. Below them, the hauler's running lights made small orange constellations against Meridian's bulk. Above, the transmission array spread its branches against the stars like a tree that had forgotten it was supposed to bear fruit instead of silence.

Jude jacked in. The system recognized their credentials—ten years of invisible, dutiful service, all of it vouching for them now—and opened the uplink without complaint.

They started with the names.

That was the decision they'd made somewhere between the galley and the ladder, and it held: not the analysis first, not the spectral proof, not the elegant demonstration of the erasure signature. Names. Forty-one children remembering forty-one names the official record had deleted, spoken in sleep, in unison, across three settlements. *Ana Reyes-Okafor. Tomas Ilic. Priya Chandrasekaran.* Each name paired with its fragment—corridor 9-C, the sound of a hull singing before it broke, a teacher's voice counting heads and coming up short. Then the manifests. Then the static, decoded, played raw, ugly as a confession. Then everything.

The broadcast went out wideband, unencrypted, addressed to every settlement, every relay, every cheap receiver in contested orbit. There was no taking it back. There was no version of tomorrow where this hadn't happened.

Jude was halfway through the seventh name when the deck plates changed their song.

Boots. Many boots, in step, coming up the docking tube with the particular rhythm of people authorized to skip politeness. Jude kept transmitting. Kept speaking the names aloud into the cabin mic, voice steady, because the words mattered more than the voice delivering them, because somewhere out in the dark a child in a bunk might be hearing her grandmother's name said back to her by a stranger, and that stranger owed her the dignity of not rushing.

"Underhill!" Marsh's voice, distorted through the hatch speaker. "Step away from the console. This is corporate security jurisdiction. You are interfering with licensed equipment."

"It's my equipment," Jude said, and read the eighth name.

The hatch blew its seals. Grey suits flooded the gantry, four of them, then six, moving with the bored efficiency of professionals doing an unpleasant errand. One grabbed Jude's arm. Another went for the console, and Jude didn't fight him—fighting meant stopping the transmission, and stopping was the one thing they would not do, not for a clean record, not for freedom, not for anything.

So they let the hands take hold. They let themselves be turned, cuffed, walked toward the gantry rail while the array behind them kept singing the names into the dark. They let Marsh appear at the edge of the chaos, slate in hand, face finally showing something real—not triumph, but the tight annoyance of a woman whose tidy solution had just developed a leak.

"You could have taken the settlement," she said.

"My sister died in corridor 9-C," Jude said. "There was never going to be a settlement."

The officers pulled them down the ladder, past the galley, toward the tube, and Jude went willingly, bonelessly, almost gratefully—as if arrest were a door they'd been leaning against for seven years and it had finally, mercifully, opened. Behind them the transmission light burned green. Ahead, whatever came next: charges, trial, the long machinery of consequence.

It didn't matter. The names were loose in the system now, riding every relay, landing in every ear. Forty-one children had remembered, and one invisible person had finally been seen doing something worth seeing.

At the bottom of the tube, through the viewport, Jude caught one last glimpse of Meridian's ring—and saw, in the residential windows, lights coming on. Whole corridors waking at 06:40, faces pressed to glass, watching the hauler's array pulse.

They were listening. All of them. And somewhere among those lights, Jude knew with a certainty that felt like inheritance, a quiet eleven-year-old named Edmund Voss was already awake—already reaching, in the way only he could, for everyone else.
