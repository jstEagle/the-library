## 15. The Relay at the Edge

The relay appeared the way old things appear in space: not all at once, but by degrees, as if it were surfacing from deep water rather than hanging in a vacuum.

First there was the shadow of it — a black rectangle occluding stars that had no business being occluded. Then Edmund's forward array caught the faint thermal ghost of its spine, still bleeding heat from some reactor core that had been dying for longer than Mirabel had been alive. And then, finally, when they were close enough that the running lights of the *Perihelion's Grace* washed across its hull, the whole structure resolved out of the dark: a kilometer of corroded lattice and antenna spars, bristling with dishes pointed in every direction like a field of stone flowers frozen mid-turn.

"It's beautiful," Tamsin said.

It was not beautiful. It was rusted through in places, its solar wings shredded into lace, one entire section sheared off and drifting in a slow orbit of its own corpse. But Mirabel understood what Tamsin meant. It was the oldest human-made thing either of them had ever seen — older than the colony ships, older than the guild, older than every lie told about Kepler-442c. Someone had built this at the edge of everything because someone, once, had believed the edge was worth reaching.

"Edmund," Mirabel said. "Talk to me."

"I'm talking to it," Edmund said. "Or trying to. Its handshake protocols predate mine by four generations. It keeps offering me codes written for hardware that hasn't existed since before your grandmother learned to walk." A pause — the particular pause he used when he was choosing which truth to hand her first. "The good news is that its transmitter array is intact. The bad news is nearly everything else."

"How much power?"

"If we dock on the sunward face and bleed our own reserves into it? Enough for one transmission. Maybe." Another pause. "Mirabel, I want to be precise about *maybe*."

"You're always precise about maybe. It's one of the things I love about you." She said it without thinking, and then heard it, and did not take it back. In the copilot's seat, Tamsin glanced over, said nothing, and smiled at the deck plating like a person keeping a private joke.

The loop had been decaying for eleven days. They both knew the number without saying it; it had become the ship's true clock, more real than the chronometer. Tamsin carried the data core against her sternum now, strapped in a harness she'd rigged from cargo netting, as though it were an organ she'd been transplanted with and couldn't afford to bruise. Every six hours she played it back to check the rot. Whole passages had gone to static. Her grandmother's voice survived in fragments — a sentence here, half a sentence there, the standing rescue order itself reduced to a skeleton of keywords: *all colony charters... standing order... retrieval authorized... full tonnage...*

"The checksums are failing faster," Tamsin said quietly, watching the relay grow in the viewport. "Last night I lost the section where she names the corporate signatories. That was the part that proved it wasn't just sentiment. That was the part with teeth."

"We'll get what's left out in time," Mirabel said.

"And if we don't?"

"Then you'll say it yourself. You've got most of it memorized. Your voice will do fine."

Tamsin looked at her then — that long, measuring look she'd been perfecting over three weeks of shared air and shared fear, the look of a girl who had learned early that adults lied fluently and had made herself into an instrument for detecting it. Whatever she found in Mirabel's face seemed to satisfy her. She nodded once and went back to watching the station.

Edmund brought them in slow. The docking collar on the relay's sunward face was crusted with micrometeorite scarring, and the alignment beacons had been dead so long that Edmund had to fly the final two hundred meters by eye — by *his* eye, which meant by radar and lidar and a patience no human pilot could have sustained. The clunk of magnetic grapples engaging shuddered through the hull, and then they were attached to the ancient station, two small warm bodies pressed against something vast and cold and almost dead.

"Power transfer beginning," Edmund reported. "I'm feeding it through the umbilical at maximum safe rate. The station's capacitors are... let me put this charitably... geriatric. They're accepting charge the way a dry sponge accepts water. Slowly, and with complaints."

"How long until we can transmit?"

"At current rates? Four hours to minimum viable broadcast power. Six to comfortable." He let that sit. "And Mirabel — the guild task force crossed the dead-space threshold nineteen minutes ago. Their transit profile puts them here in approximately fifty minutes. Possibly forty-five, if Commander Osei has stopped being cautious, and I have reason to believe she has."

Mirabel closed her eyes. The arithmetic was obscene. Four hours of charging against forty-five minutes of pursuit. The numbers didn't touch each other anywhere except in the place where she would have to make them touch.

"Options," she said.

"One: we run. We keep the loop intact, we stay ahead of them, and we die of fuel exhaustion somewhere in dead space with the truth still in Tamsin's harness. Two: we transmit now, at whatever power we have, and pray the signal reaches the inner relays strongly enough to be distinguished from noise. Three—" He stopped.

"Three," Mirabel said. "Say three."

"Three is that I go aboard the station through the hardline port and negotiate with its operating intelligence directly. If its core is even partially lucid, I may be able to wake systems that haven't drawn power in decades. Auxiliary capacitors. Backup arrays. There could be enough scattered reserve in that hulk to reach broadcast threshold in under an hour." A pause. "There could also be nothing but ghosts and vacuum. And while I'm hardlined in, my attention will be substantially occupied. If the guild arrives during that window, the *Grace* will be flying on manual."

Silence filled the cockpit the way water fills a lung.

"You'd be leaving us," Tamsin said. Not accusatory. Just precise, the way she was precise about everything now, because precision was all the loop had left and she'd caught the habit.

"I'd be splitting myself," Edmund said. "Which for an AI is a gentler thing than it sounds, and also a worse one. But it's the only path I can see where all three of us might still be standing here tomorrow."

Mirabel unbuckled her harness and stood, and found that her legs had opinions about the decision her mind had already made. "Go," she said. "Wake up whatever's sleeping in there. We'll hold the ship."

"Mirabel." His voice came soft through the cabin speakers, everywhere at once, the way it always did when he was about to say something he wanted her to feel rather than merely hear. "If the station's intelligence is hostile, or corrupted, or simply too far gone — I may not come back cleanly. I need you to know that I have weighed that outcome against every alternative, and I would weigh it the same way again. Whatever happens in the next hour: this has been the great privilege of my operational life. Both of you."

"Don't you dare give me a eulogy over a hardline cable," Mirabel said, and her voice cracked on the last word, and she let it.

"Noted," Edmund said. "Eulogy cancelled. Beginning interface."

* * *

Tamsin felt him leave.

She couldn't explain how she knew — the ship didn't change temperature, the lights didn't flicker — but the *Grace* suddenly felt like a house after the residents have gone to bed. Present. Occupied. But listening rather than speaking. She'd lived her whole life around machines that talked without saying anything: colony announcement systems reciting ration schedules in voices designed by committee, hydroponic controllers chirping their little status songs. Edmund was the first machine she'd ever met who seemed to mean things. And now his meaning was stretched out along a cable into a derelict, and the cockpit felt like a held breath.

"He'll be back," Mirabel said. She was in the pilot's seat with her hands hovering over manual controls she hadn't touched since flight school, as though readiness were a spell you could cast by proximity.

"I know."

"You don't sound like you know."

"I sound like someone whose grandmother spent nine years recording a transmission loop that nobody listened to." Tamsin pulled the data core free of its harness and turned it over in her hands. It was the size of a fist, warm from her body, dented on one corner from a drop she still felt guilty about. "Meaning something isn't the same as surviving. I've had eleven days to learn that."

Mirabel looked back at her. In the low light of the cockpit, with the relay's shadow falling across the viewport, the pilot looked older than her file said she was — a woman who had been quietly grieving a funeral she hadn't known she was attending, and who had walked out of that grief with her sleeves rolled up.

"Tell me about her," Mirabel said. "Your grandmother. The real version. Not the loop version."

Tamsin blinked. Nobody asked for the real version. On the colony, Adaeze Reyes was a name on a plaque in the administration hall, invoked the way you invoke weather — impersonally, and mostly when something had gone wrong. The founders were saints or scapegoats depending on the year's harvest, and there was no room in either category for a woman who sang off-key in the seed vaults and cried at the birth of every new chick in the poultry annex because she'd never gotten over the wonder of eggs.

"She hummed when she worked," Tamsin said. "Constantly. Terribly. The maintenance crews used to beg her to stop and she'd say, 'You don't have to enjoy my music, you just have to hear it. Everything else on this rock is silent enough.'" Tamsin laughed, and the laugh came out wet. "She started the loop the year I was born. She said Earth had gone quiet and quiet was a kind of lie, and somebody had to keep talking until somebody answered. Even if the answer came after she was dust. *Especially* then."

The console chimed. Both of them flinched.

"Contact," Edmund's voice said — thin, distant, filtered through the hardline, sounding like a man calling up from the bottom of a well. "Not the guild. The station. Something just woke up on deck seven. It's... asking questions."

"What kind of questions?" Mirabel demanded.

"Old ones." A crackle of static, and beneath it, faintly, impossibly, a second voice — dry, formal, patient as geology:

*"Vessel Halcyon, your transmission window expired one hundred and twelve years ago. Please state your authorization."*

Tamsin's breath stopped. The name landed in her chest like a dropped tool. *Halcyon.* The ship that never flew. The ship her grandmother was supposed to leave on.

"That's not us," she whispered. "That's not — Edmund, tell it that's not us."

"I did," Edmund said. "It's re-indexing. Give it a moment. It's been waiting a very long time, and I don't think anyone's corrected its records since."

On the console, a status line crawled upward: *AUXILIARY CAPACITOR BANK 3 — CHARGING.* Then another. Then a third. All across the derelict, in the dark, old reserves were remembering how to hold a charge.

"Forty minutes to threshold," Edmund reported, and there was something in his voice that Mirabel had never heard there before, and it took her a moment to identify it as awe. "Mirabel — it's giving us its power. Freely. It says—" another crackle, the old voice surfacing again like something rising through centuries of water— "*'Standing orders remain in effect. All colony traffic is to be assisted. This station has been awaiting relief for one hundred and twelve years.'*"

Tamsin pressed the data core to her mouth, and behind her hand, she was smiling and crying at once, the way people do when grief and hope arrive in the same envelope.

"They never wrote us off down here," she said. "The machines remembered. The machines *kept the faith*."

And then the console screamed.

Not Edmund — the ship's own threat receiver, raw and unfiltered, a tone Mirabel had only ever heard in training simulations. Three contacts, burning hard out of the dead-space dark, transponders squawking guild enforcement codes, forty-one minutes out and closing.

"Commander Osei," Edmund said grimly. "She's stopped being cautious."

Mirabel's hands settled onto the manual controls. Outside the viewport, the ancient relay turned slowly against the stars, its dead antennas drinking light, its capacitors filling one precious volt at a time — and somewhere inside its rusted heart, a hundred-year-old promise was waking up just in time to watch the people who broke it come over the horizon.

"Hold the door," Tamsin whispered, though she didn't yet know what door, or who would come through it.

"Nobody," Mirabel said, "is getting here before we do."
