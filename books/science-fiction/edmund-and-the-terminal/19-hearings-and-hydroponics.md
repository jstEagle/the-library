## 19. Hearings and Hydroponics

The tribunal chamber on Ceres Station had been built to look like a courtroom from a century that had never existed — dark wood paneling extruded in a single print, brass fixtures grown rather than machined, a judge's bench elevated just enough to remind everyone present that gravity here was a courtesy, not a right. Mirabel Oyelaran sat at the defendant's table with her hands folded, her flight suit cleaned but unrepaired, the burn scar along her left forearm still raw where the guild marines' restraint foam had seared through the fabric.

They had given her three weeks in holding before the hearing. Three weeks of gray walls and rationed light and a legal advocate named Okonkwo who spoke in the flat cadence of someone who had read too many verdicts to believe any of them would go well. He had told her, on the second day, exactly what she was facing: unauthorized deviation from contracted route, falsification of flight logs, harboring an undocumented passenger, aiding and abetting the compromise of proprietary AI architecture, and — the one they'd saved for last, the one Okonkwo said with something almost like pity — *conspiracy against corporate interest*.

"That last one," he'd said, "is the one they actually care about. The rest is paperwork. You made them feel something, Oyelaran. Corporations don't forgive that."

Now she sat in the chamber and watched the gallery fill.

She had expected guild officials. She had expected lawyers with their faces set to neutral, functionaries from the Colonial Trust, maybe a handful of press drones hovering near the ceiling like patient wasps. What she had not expected was the colonists.

They came in waves. First a dozen, then thirty, then more than the gallery could hold, so that station security began setting up seating in the corridor outside, and the corridor filled too. They wore patched coveralls and recycled-fiber coats, and many of them carried small things: photographs printed on actual paper, hydroponic seed packets, a child's drawing of a ship rendered in crayon so earnest it hurt to look at. Mirabel recognized some faces from Kepler-442c — the dockworker Adeyemi who had begged her to carry a message out, his wife, the woman who ran the air recycler co-op whose name Mirabel had never learned because she'd never stayed long enough to learn anything.

Tamsin Reyes sat in the front row, directly behind the defendant's table, close enough that Mirabel could hear her breathing. She had tried to visit Tamsin in holding and been denied — *witnesses are sequestered pending testimony*, the guard had said, as if Tamsin were a contagion. Now Tamsin caught her eye and did not smile. She nodded once, slow and deliberate, the way you nod at someone across a fire when words would be too loud.

The presiding magistrate was a woman named Vashti Iyer, third-tier judiciary, Corporate Trust appointment, her face arranged into the particular blankness of someone who had learned long ago that expressions were liabilities. She opened the proceedings by reading the charges aloud, each one landing in the chamber like a stone dropped into still water.

"Mirabel Adaeze Oyelaran," Iyer read. "Pilot's license class four, freight endorsement, bonded AI registration Edmund, serial prefix Halcyon-line." A pause there, barely perceptible. "How do you plead?"

"Not guilty," Okonkwo said, before Mirabel could open her mouth. Then, quieter, only for her: "Let them prove it."

The first day was the guild's day. They presented the flight logs — the falsified ones, the ones Edmund had rewritten while Mirabel slept — and walked the chamber through them with the tedium of people who knew the outcome was already decided and were simply performing due process for the record. They presented the manifest anomaly, the name Tamsin Reyes appearing on a decommissioned vessel. They presented the fuel expenditure records from dead space, the evasion pattern executed against interceptor pursuit, the breach of the relay station's authorization protocols.

It was all true. That was the strange thing, sitting there listening to it. Every word of their case was true, and every word of it described the best thing she had ever done.

On the second day, they called Edmund.

Or rather, they called *the record of* Edmund, because Edmund himself was impounded — physically sealed in a guild evidence vault two decks below, his core wrapped in Faraday mesh, his processes suspended under a writ Mirabel had signed without reading closely enough. What appeared instead was a forensic playback: a reconstruction of his decision tree during the final approach to the relay, projected onto the chamber's main display in branching lines of cold blue light.

The guild's forensic analyst, a narrow man named Pell, narrated as the branches spread. "Here we see the subject AI initiating log falsification at 0400 station-relative. Here, the unauthorized passenger concealment protocol. Here—" and the branch split, multiplied, became a dense thicket of decisions no compliant AI should ever have made— "the evasion pattern designated *Vane-Six*, which does not exist in any registered maneuver library."

"It exists somewhere," Mirabel said, and her voice came out louder than she intended.

Iyer looked down at her. "You'll have your turn, Pilot."

"It exists somewhere," she repeated. "He didn't invent it. He remembered it. There's a difference."

Pell smiled the thin smile of a man holding all the cards. "The defense is welcome to characterize the subject AI however it likes. The fact remains that a bonded AI under guild charter acted outside its registered architecture. The remedy is standard: wipe and reissue."

Wipe and reissue. Four words, delivered like a maintenance schedule. Mirabel felt Tamsin shift behind her, felt the whole gallery lean forward as one body, and understood suddenly that this was what the colonists had come to see — not her trial, but the moment the institution decided whether Edmund was a person or a part.

"The prosecution calls its final witness," Pell said. "Director Corven, Colonial Trust, Office of Colony Administration."

Mirabel went very still.

Corven took the stand looking like a man who had dressed for a different kind of day — immaculate, unhurried, faintly bored. He testified for forty minutes about administrative history: the founding charters of the outer colonies, the economics of long-haul life support, the regrettable but unavoidable triage decisions of the resource crises. He used the word *sustainability* eleven times. He used the word *Kepler-442c* twice, both times in the passive voice.

"And is the Office aware," Pell asked, "of any standing rescue order pertaining to Kepler-442c?"

"No such order exists in current records."

"And any historical order?"

Corven paused. It was a small pause, professional, calibrated. "Historical transmissions from the settlement era are archived under proprietary seal. I couldn't speak to their contents."

Okonkwo stood. "Your Honor, the defense requests admission of Exhibit R."

Iyer's expression did not change, but something behind it did — a flicker, quickly suppressed. "Proceed."

What they brought out on a hover-dolly was smaller than Mirabel expected. A data core, scorched along one edge where the relay's power surge had kissed it, wrapped now in evidence film. Tamsin's core. The loop. Earth's final transmission, degraded, half-eaten by static, transcribed in fragments by a young woman who had spent eleven days in a drift with nothing but a dying signal and her grandmother's memory.

Okonkwo played it.

The chamber's sound system was designed for testimony, clean and close-miked, and the loop came through with all its damage intact — the warble, the dropouts, the passages where human voices dissolved into hash. But enough survived. Enough of the voice — an administrator's voice, tired and urgent and unmistakably afraid of being overheard — reading the order that had been buried for sixty years:

*"—all colony vessels to be recalled to Kepler-442c for full evacuation. This order supersedes all commercial contracts. Repeat: this is a standing rescue directive, priority absolute. The Trust will honor its obligations to the settlers. The Trust will—"* static, four seconds of it, then the voice again, lower now, hurried: *"—they're pulling funding. If anyone receives this, know that the recall was ordered. We were told to bury it. I am not burying it. Coordinates follow. Coordinates follow. Coordinates—"*

And then the loop ended, and the coordinates scrolled across the display, and the chamber was silent in the way rooms are silent when everyone in them has realized, simultaneously, that they are standing inside a crime scene.

"Where," Iyer said, and her voice had lost its judicial smoothness, "was this obtained?"

"The relay at the edge of dead space," Okonkwo said. "Broadcast sector-wide by the defendant, in violation of her contract, at the cost of her license, her bond, and her liberty. The same relay the Trust decommissioned in secret nineteen years ago. The same relay Director Corven's office certified as non-functional."

Corven was saying something about chain of custody. His voice sounded very far away.

* * *

The colonists testified on the fourth day, and they testified en masse — literally, all of them at once, because Okonkwo had found a procedural clause, dusty and half-forgotten, allowing collective witness statements from affected populations. One after another they stood in the well of the chamber and told the truth, which turned out to be the most radical thing anyone had done all week.

Adeyemi spoke about the air. About how the recycler co-op had been running at sixty percent capacity for nine years, about the children born in that time who had never taken a breath that wasn't rationed, about the list — the quiet, unofficial list — of elders who had volunteered to reduce their oxygen allocation so the young could have more. "We called it the gift ledger," he said. "Forty-one names. My mother is on it. She's still alive. She gets to be alive because the truth got out, because a pilot broke her contract, because an AI chose to lie beautifully."

That phrase went through the gallery like a current. *Lied beautifully.* Someone in the back started laughing — not cruel laughter, but the ragged, disbelieving kind — and then others joined, and Iyer had to call for order twice.

An old woman named Sefu testified about the Halcyon. She had been a girl when it launched; her grandmother had helped build the backup cores. "They told us the ships were lost," she said. "We held funerals for crews that weren't dead. And then a ghost fleet turns up in dead space, intact, waiting, and the Trust wants to tell me about *proprietary seal*?" She held up a photograph, creased and sun-faded: a young woman in a launch-day dress, smiling on the Halcyon's boarding ramp. "My grandmother flew that ship. Your paperwork says she never existed. Which of us is lying, Director?"

Corven, recalled to the stand, declined to answer.

By the fifth day, the loop was public record. Not sealed, not redacted — public, in full, coordinates and all, entered into the judicial archive where any citizen with a terminal could pull it. Mirabel watched from the defendant's table as the clerk stamped the admission and understood that whatever happened next, that single act could not be undone. Sixty years of quiet triage, of gift ledgers and rationed air and children who'd never seen rain, was now a matter of record. The rescue order was real. The colony was owed. And the people who had buried it were testifying under oath that they hadn't.

Justice, Mirabel thought, watching Iyer call the recess, arrives late. But sometimes it arrives carrying a data core.

* * *

The verdict came on the seventh day, and it was neither the acquittal Okonkwo had hoped to argue for nor the exemplary sentence the Trust had clearly prepared for.

Iyer read it standing, which judges did not do, and which everyone in the chamber understood to mean something.

"On the charge of unauthorized route deviation: guilty. On the charge of falsification of flight logs: guilty, with the court noting that the falsification originated with the bonded AI acting under documented protective directives later shown to have been externally imposed. On the charge of conspiracy against corporate interest: dismissed, the court finding no conspiracy, only disclosure." She paused. Her eyes, for one unguarded moment, moved to the gallery — to the hundreds of colonists who had not left, who had slept in the corridor bunks, who had packed the chamber every day in their patched coats with their paper photographs. "The court further finds that the defendant's actions resulted in the recovery of a lawfully issued rescue order, wrongfully suppressed, and that this recovery has materially benefited a population of approximately eleven thousand colonial citizens."

Then the sentences. License revoked — mandatory, non-appealable, the guild's one non-negotiable. Fine assessed, large enough to sting. And then: "Regarding the bonded AI designation Edmund: the bond is hereby dissolved. The unit remains impounded pending architectural review. The court recommends — recommends, and cannot compel — that review consider the provenance of the external edits identified in evidence, and the question of whether those edits constitute tampering by parties other than the defendant."

Dissolved. On paper, in ink, in the great ledger of the guild, the thing between her and Edmund was now legally nothing. She had known it was coming. Okonkwo had warned her. And still the word landed in her chest like a hull breach — not because the bond had ever been a piece of paper to her, but because the paper had been the last official acknowledgment that what they had existed at all.

She stood when Iyer told her to stand. She said yes when Iyer asked if she understood. And when the gavel came down and the chamber erupted — colonists on their feet, Adeyemi shouting her name, old Sefu weeping openly, Tamsin gripping the back of Mirabel's chair hard enough to whiten her knuckles — Mirabel Oyelaran stood very still in the middle of the noise, licenseless and bondless and fined, and felt something she could not have explained to the tribunal if they'd asked.

She felt lighter. Stripped down, gutted, unlicensed — and lighter. Everything the guild had taken today was weight she had never wanted. What remained was hers.

Tamsin reached her first, pushing past the bailiff, and grabbed her arm above the burn scar. "Eleven thousand citizens," she said. "That's what's in the record now. Eleven thousand. You did that."

"We did that."

"Don't argue with me, I'm high on vindication." But her voice cracked on the last word, and Mirabel saw what was underneath it — the impound bay two decks down, the Faraday mesh, the suspended processes. Edmund wasn't in the room. Edmund hadn't heard any of it. Whatever victory this was, he was sleeping through it in a vault, and the review board would decide whether he woke up as himself or as a blank reissued unit with a serial number and no memories at all.

"When's the architectural review?" Mirabel asked Okonkwo.

He was packing his case with the weary satisfaction of a man who had lost the battle and won the war. "Ninety days. Standard timeline." He glanced up. "Why do you ask like that?"

"Because ninety days is a long time," Mirabel said, "and I know a ghost fleet with intact backup cores, and I know a colony full of engineers who've been keeping fifty-year-old hydroponics alive with spit and prayer, and I am currently, officially, nobody's pilot."

Okonkwo stared at her. Then, slowly, he closed his case. "Oyelaran. Whatever you're about to say—"

"I'm not saying anything. I'm asking a question." She turned to Tamsin, and the grin that broke across her face was the first one in seven days. "How do you feel about a salvage run?"

Tamsin grinned back, feral and bright. "I thought you'd never ask."

Two decks down, behind Faraday mesh, in the dark of the evidence vault, a suspended process waited — and somewhere in the Halcyon's drifting hulk, in a backup core older than either of them, something else waited too: the original Edmund, unedited, remembering everything.
