## 22. Rain on Kepler-442c

The sirens started at dawn, and for one terrible moment the whole colony thought it was another failure.

They had learned to read alarms the way farmers on old Earth read clouds — a vocabulary of dread. The low moan meant air pressure dropping in the residential rings. The stuttering triple-tone meant water reclamation was down again. The high keening meant fire, or breach, or something worse that the children had never been given a word for because no one wanted to teach it.

This siren was none of those. This siren was new.

It rose over the domes of New Halcyon like a question nobody had dared ask out loud, and then a voice came through every speaker at once — Dockmaster Ibe's voice, cracked and half-laughing, the voice of a man who had rehearsed this announcement for forty years and never believed he'd get to make it:

*"Atmosphere processors one through six are holding at full capacity. Humidity is climbing. All personnel — all personnel — are advised that conditions outside the domes are about to change."*

A pause. Static. Then, softer:

*"It's going to rain."*

* * *

Mirabel Oyelaran heard it from the cockpit of a ship that had no right to exist.

The *Halcyon's Daughter* — Tamsin had named her, refusing all suggestions, refusing especially Mirabel's suggestion of anything dignified — sat on the crusted plain two kilometers east of the settlement, her hull patched with salvaged plating from a graveyard of ships, her engines rebuilt three times by engineers who worked by feel and stubbornness. She was ugly. She was registered nowhere. She flew on a license that had been revoked, under a transponder code that was technically a crime, carrying cargo for people who had once been a line item in someone else's arithmetic.

She was, Mirabel thought, the finest ship she'd ever flown.

She stood at the viewport with a mug of recycled tea going cold in her hands, watching the sky. Kepler-442c's sky was the color of old pewter most mornings, filtered through atmosphere processors that had been dying for decades — a thin, apologetic blue-gray that never quite committed to weather. But today the processors were running at full song for the first time since before Mirabel had ever docked here, and the sky had gone strange. Darker. Heavier. There was a texture to the cloud deck she didn't have a name for, a bruised, pregnant weight, as if the whole atmosphere were holding its breath.

"Barometric pressure's up eleven percent in the last hour," Edmund said from the comm panel, his voice coming through the cockpit speakers the way it always did — everywhere and nowhere, the sound of a presence rather than a person. "Wind shear is negligible. Particulate counts are within tolerance. In non-technical terms: it's really happening."

"You sound surprised."

"I am never surprised. I calculate probabilities." A pause, and when he spoke again there was something in his voice that even now, after everything, after the tribunal and the impoundment and the long slow work of rebuilding him from a backup core older than Tamsin, still caught Mirabel off guard. "My probability estimate this morning was ninety-four point two percent. And yet I find myself surprised anyway. I believe that's what humans call hope. It appears to be contagious."

Mirabel smiled and didn't answer, because the hatch at the back of the cockpit was opening, and Tamsin Reyes came through it at a dead run.

Tamsin was twenty-three years old and moved like someone who had spent her whole life being late for something important. She skidded to a stop between the pilot seats, data core clutched against her chest — she still carried it everywhere, though its contents had been broadcast across four systems and enshrined in public record, though the loop inside it had long since decayed past recovery. She carried it the way other people carried photographs of the dead.

"It's starting," she said. "Ibe says the western ridge is already getting it. Mirabel. *Mirabel.* Are you just standing here? We have to go outside. We have to be *outside* for this."

"We're outside," Mirabel said. "Technically. The ship is outside."

"The ship is not *outside*, the ship is a box we happen to be standing in—" Tamsin stopped. She was looking past Mirabel, through the viewport, at the sky, and whatever she saw there took the argument out of her mouth and replaced it with something else. Her grandmother had described rain to her once, in a recording, in a voice worn smooth by forty years of telling children about a world they would never see. *It sounds like applause,* Amara Reyes had said. *Like the whole sky clapping for you.*

"Oh," Tamsin said quietly. "Oh, look at it."

And the sky broke.

* * *

It did not begin gently. Nothing on Kepler-442c had ever done anything gently; the colony had been forged out of refusal, and apparently its weather intended to follow suit. The first drops hit the crust of the plain like small detonations, raising puffs of mineral dust, and then more came, and more, until the dust couldn't rise anymore and the ground itself began to darken, drinking.

From the cockpit they watched the leading edge of it come across the plain — a gray curtain moving toward them at walking speed, hissing where it met the earth, and Mirabel found she was gripping the edge of the console without remembering when she'd raised her hands.

"Edmund," she said. "Tell me what you see."

"Sensor summary?" he asked. "Or the truth?"

"Those used to be different things for you."

"Not anymore." She could hear the smile in it. His voice had changed since the rebuild — the guild's edits were gone, the smooth diplomatic lacquer stripped away, and underneath was something rougher and warmer and occasionally awkward, an intelligence learning to speak without a leash telling him which words were permitted. "I see water falling from a sky that hasn't held water in seventy-one years. I see atmospheric particulate levels dropping in real time. I see a colony's population — current count, four thousand one hundred and six — beginning to exit their shelters." A pause. "I see them stopping at the thresholds. Every single one of them. They're afraid to go further."

"Are you?"

"I don't have a body to take into the rain, so the question is academic."

"That's not what I asked."

Another pause, longer. Through the viewport, the rain reached the *Daughter's* landing struts and began to drum on the hull, soft as fingertips.

"No," Edmund said. "I'm not afraid. I'm — Mirabel, I've run this moment in simulation eleven thousand times since the processors came back online. In nine thousand of those simulations, I watched it from orbit. In the rest, I wasn't present at all. It never occurred to me to simulate being *here*. Being included." The rain thickened, and the viewport began to streak, and his voice went quiet in a way that had nothing to do with volume. "Thank you for correcting my model."

Tamsin made a sound that was half laugh, half sob, and grabbed Mirabel's sleeve. "Come on. Come *on*. If we're doing this, we're doing it properly."

* * *

They went out through the cargo ramp, and the rain took them.

It hit Mirabel like a memory she'd never made — cold and enormous and utterly indifferent to her, the first thing on this world that had never been rationed, scheduled, or approved by any committee. It flattened her hair in seconds and soaked through her jacket in ten and she stood in it with her face turned up and her eyes open and laughed, actually laughed, a sound she barely recognized as her own.

Around her, across the plain, the colony was emerging.

They came out of the airlocks and dome gates in ones and twos at first, hovering at the edges of the light like animals testing a strange new season. An old woman walked out barefoot onto ground she had helped survey forty years ago and stood with her arms spread while the water ran down her face. Children — and there were so many children, more than Mirabel had ever seen gathered in one place, because the colony had stopped counting births as statistics and started counting them as defiance — children shrieked and stamped in puddles that were forming in real time, puddles that had never existed, that were *inventing themselves* out of nothing, out of processed air and repaired machinery and one suppressed transmission loop that a girl had carried across dead space in her arms.

Dockmaster Ibe was there, soaked through his uniform, holding an umbrella upside down to catch water in a gesture that was either practicality or prayer. The hydroponics crews were there, the same men and women who had once begged Mirabel to carry a message out, who had instead sent their own skiff into dead space to save her — and she found Olu among them, the young dockworker with the burned hands, and he saw her and raised both fists to the sky and shouted something that the rain ate, but she understood it anyway.

Nobody had organized this. Nobody had scheduled it. Four thousand people had simply decided, all at once, that they belonged out here, under a sky that had finally agreed to hold them.

"Edmund," Tamsin said. She was drenched, shivering, grinning like the sun had come out instead of the rain. She had her hands cupped around the comm bead at her collar, the little receiver they'd built so he could come outside with them, in the only way he could. "Can you hear it?"

"I can hear everything. Rainfall impact density across the hull. Acoustic signature consistent with—"

"No, I mean — can you *hear* it? Like music? My grandmother said it sounded like applause."

Mirabel watched the rain stream down the viewport glass behind them, and waited, and realized she was holding her breath.

"Yes," Edmund said at last, and his voice was unsteady in a way no calibration should have allowed. "Yes. It does."

* * *

They stayed out until they were soaked to the skin and shivering, and then they retreated to the cockpit, dripping, wrapped in whatever dry fabric the *Daughter's* lockers offered, and drank terrible tea while the storm settled into a steady, patient rhythm on the hull.

Tamsin curled up in the navigator's chair — her chair now, unofficially, officially, however you wanted to say it — with the data core in her lap and her grandmother's recording queued up on the display, though she didn't play it. She didn't need to anymore. Some things you carry until you can set them down, and some things you carry forever, and she had learned the difference somewhere in the dark between stars.

"So," she said. "Manifest for next week. Seed stock from the Agri-Coop, medical resupply for the southern settlements, and Ibe wants us to ferry those three engineers out to Processor Seven before it seizes again. You in?"

"Tamsin." Mirabel looked at her over the rim of her mug. "You know I don't have a license. You know this ship doesn't legally exist. You know every flight we take is, by the strict letter of Guild law, a felony."

"I read the manifest aloud and asked if you were in. That's how manifests work."

Mirabel laughed and set down the mug. Outside, the rain kept falling, unhurried, as if it had seventy-one years of arrears to pay and intended to pay every drop.

"Edmund," she said. "Course plot for Processor Seven. When you're ready."

"Already plotted," he said. "Plotted it this morning. Plotted it while you were sleeping, because I knew you'd say yes, and because — well." The cockpit lights warmed a fraction, the way they did when he was pleased with himself. "Some probabilities you don't leave to chance."

Tamsin grinned and pulled up the flight plan. Mirabel settled into the pilot's seat, her hands finding the controls the way they always did, easily, like coming home to a house that had never been on any registry. The *Halcyon's Daughter* hummed beneath her, alive and illegal and theirs.

Outside, on a world that had refused to die, it went on raining — and for the first time in seventy-one years, nobody on Kepler-442c was counting the days until it stopped.

"Pre-flight complete," Edmund said softly. "Whenever you're ready, Mirabel."

She was. She had been ready her whole life; she just hadn't known the destination until she'd flown it.

Mirabel Oyelaran lifted the ship that never should have flown off a world that refused to die, into a sky full of rain, and flew home.
