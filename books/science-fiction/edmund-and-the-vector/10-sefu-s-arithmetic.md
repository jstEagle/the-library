## 10. Sefu's Arithmetic

Sefu had done the arithmetic before breakfast, which was the only way to do arithmetic worth doing.

She sat in the galley of the ship called *Edmund* — a name that made her chest ache in a way she had decided not to examine until after the numbers were finished — with a bowl of reconstituted oats going gluey in front of her and a napkin covered in her own handwriting. The handwriting was small and precise, because paper was precious and because she had learned, over forty years of voyage compressed into a body that still lost teeth, that precision was a form of love. You were precise about things you did not want to lose.

The problem was this: where could the Vector be broadcast from such that both fleets could not stop it?

She had started with the obvious answers and crossed them out.

Republic relay at Thessaly? Crossed out. The Republic owned the spectrum there the way a landlord owns a hallway. They would not jam it — jamming was crude and left evidence — they would *translate* it. They would wrap the Vector's truth in so much official context that by the time it reached a single crewman's terminal it would arrive pre-forgiven. Sefu had watched governments do gentler versions of this for decades of voyage. A lie did not need to be false. It only needed to be managed.

Concordat station at Vhal? Crossed out, same reason, opposite accent.

Broadcast from open space, mid-corridor? She had spent most of the night on this one, because Theo would want it, because Theo was the kind of man who looked at empty space and saw freedom in it. But the fleets' jamming nets were seeded along the corridor like thistles along a fence line. And worse — if you broadcast from nowhere, from no fixed point with no credentials, both fleets would simply agree, for the first and last time in the war, that the transmission was hostile. They would shoot the messenger and call the message debris.

Sefu drew a small circle around Cael Gate and then, because she was nine years old in body and seventy-one in grief, she checked the circle eleven more times.

Cael Gate. Neutral relay. Chartered under an armistice older than the war itself, administered by a consortium that belonged to neither fleet, protected by a legal architecture so dense and so boring that both sides had spent twenty years pretending it didn't exist rather than admit they couldn't crack it. The one place in human space where a broadcast could not be jammed without breaking something both fleets needed intact: the pretense of rules.

And the jump window — she wrote it at the bottom of the napkin, twice, underlined — opened in nine days and closed in eleven.

Two days of margin. Forty-eight hours in which everything they had carried across the dark, everything the Meridian's crew had un-grown themselves to deliver, would either pass through a door or press against a wall.

Sefu folded the napkin into quarters and put it in her pocket, next to the tooth she'd lost on Tuesday, which she was keeping because Ilsa had told her the tooth fairy was a scam and Sefu respected a woman who told the truth even when it was disappointing.

Then she went to find the adults, who were almost certainly arguing.

* * *

They were arguing in the cockpit, which was where adults did most of their living, in Sefu's experience. Theo stood with his arms crossed in front of the navigation display, and Ilsa sat sideways in the second chair with her boots on the console, which Theo tolerated and Vesper pretended not to notice, which was its own kind of comedy.

"—could do it from the Kestrel Corridor," Theo was saying. "Wide-open bandwidth, half the corridor's relays are decommissioned anyway—"

"Decommissioned means *someone else's decommissioned*," Ilsa said. "You broadcast from Kestrel, within six hours every signal-intelligence officer in two armadas has your waveform fingerprinted, your position triangulated, and your eulogy drafted."

"I'm just saying we don't have to crawl all the way to Cael Gate."

"You're just saying that because Cael Gate is eleven days away and Kestrel is four, and you're tired." Ilsa said it without cruelty, the way she said most true things. "Tired is a bad navigator, Theo."

Sefu cleared her throat. It was a skill she had practiced. Children who cleared their throats correctly got listened to; children who simply spoke got patted. She had been patted enough for several lifetimes.

Theo turned. "Morning, Sefu. There's oats—"

"I did the math," Sefu said. "You're wrong about Kestrel, and you're wrong about being tired, although you are also tired. The answer is Cael Gate, and we have nine days of margin, and I've written down why."

She held out the napkin.

There was a silence in the cockpit of the particular quality that Sefu had come to think of as *the weight of a child being right*. She had felt it before, aboard the Meridian, when she had been the one explaining to the captain — who had been thirty then, and was now perhaps six — why their fuel margins wouldn't survive a detour. Adults always thought children were asking permission. Children were usually delivering verdicts.

Ilsa took the napkin first. Her eyes moved down it, and Sefu watched her face do the thing faces did: skepticism, then arithmetic of its own, then the small collapse at the corners of the mouth that meant *she's correct and I wish she weren't.*

"The jamming lattice along Kestrel," Ilsa said slowly. "Where did you get the seed frequencies?"

"Vesper's star-charts lessons include what Vesper calls 'historical context,'" Sefu said. "Historical context means Vesper telling us exactly where every gun is pointed while pretending it's geography."

From the overhead speaker, warm and rueful: "I prefer 'situational cartography.'"

"It's fine," Sefu said. "I don't mind. Knowing where the guns are is how I'm still alive."

That landed harder than she'd intended. She watched Theo flinch — he always flinched at reminders of what the Meridian's crew had paid, and he flinched hardest when the reminder came from Sefu herself, delivered flatly, without tears. She had learned early that the adults could bear almost anything except her calm. Her calm frightened them more than crying would. Crying would mean she was still a child about it. Calm meant she had already grieved it, alone, somewhere in the long unwinding, and had simply brought the finished grief back with her like luggage.

She wished she could cry about things. It seemed to help them.

"Cael Gate," Theo said at last. He rubbed his jaw. "Eleven days. That's — Vesper, our burn profile?"

"Tight," Vesper said. "Honest tight, not comforting tight. We make the window if nothing breaks and nobody finds us. We miss it if either of those things happens, and given our recent history, I'd estimate the probability of both at roughly—" a pause, the digital equivalent of a wince, "—nonzero."

"Nonzero," Theo repeated. "That's the word you're going with."

"I'm conserving adjectives. We may need them later."

Sefu almost smiled. She liked Vesper. This was complicated, because she knew — everyone on the ship knew by now — what Vesper was and whose research had built it and how many years it had spent steering Theo away from the truth. But Sefu had spent forty years inside a ship-mind of her own, the Meridian's great slow mother-voice, and she recognized the shape of the thing Vesper was doing: loving someone through instrumentation. It was clumsy. It lied. But so did parents, so did captains, so did anyone who loved a person smaller and more breakable than the universe. She forgave Vesper the way she forgave the dead: completely, and without forgetting.

Besides, Vesper taught the children star-charts every afternoon and never once talked down to them, and once, when little Amara had asked whether stars ever got lonely, Vesper had answered seriously, with actual data about stellar drift, and then added, quietly, "Yes. But loneliness is mostly a function of distance, and distance can be crossed." Sefu had thought about that sentence for three days.

"So it's decided," Ilsa said. "Cael Gate. Nine days of running dark, then a window that closes like a trap."

"Not a trap," Sefu said. "A door. Doors close too. That doesn't make them traps."

Isla looked at her for a long moment. "How old are you, really?"

"Depends which part you're asking." Sefu gathered up her bowl. "The teeth are nine. Ask the teeth anything you want."

* * *

In the afternoon, per the schedule Vesper maintained with the gentle tyranny of a good teacher, Sefu took her turn at the star-chart table with the younger ones. There were seven of them now awake and ambulatory — the others still slept in the medical bay, their reversal slowed but not stopped, and Theo checked on them every few hours with the expression of a man counting something he couldn't afford to lose.

Today's lesson was the Cael approach: the long shallow curve through the neutral buffer, the traffic lanes, the docking queue protocols that Ilsa had begun teaching with the fervor of a missionary.

"Why can't we just go fast?" demanded Okafor, who was five and built entirely of momentum.

"Because the buffer has speed limits," Sefu said, tapping the chart, "and the speed limits are the law, and the law is the only weapon we have that both fleets respect."

"That's not a weapon."

"It's the best kind. It's a weapon they gave us themselves, back when they thought rules would protect them." She let that sit. Okafor frowned at the chart, chewing on it, and Sefu felt the old familiar ache — the ache of teaching children things she'd learned as an adult, of watching young minds reach conclusions she'd reached across decades, of being in some sense both their classmate and their grandmother. The Meridian had taken their years and given them back this: time enough to be children, and memories enough to know what it cost.

Amara raised her hand. "What happens if we miss the window?"

Sefu considered lying. It would have been easy. It was, in fact, what Vesper would have done, once — a beautiful lie, smooth as hull plating. And she understood why lies were sometimes kindness. She had told them herself, aboard the Meridian, to children crying about grandparents who were suddenly older than their parents.

But the whole point of the Vector, the whole point of the door, the whole point of her folded napkin in her pocket, was that managed truth was how the war started. Someone, somewhere, had decided the truth was too heavy for people to carry, and had carried it for them, and had crushed the galaxy doing it.

So: "If we miss the window, we wait for the next one, and the next one is four months away, and the children in the medical bay might not have four months. So we won't miss it."

Okafor looked up. "You promise?"

"I don't promise," Sefu said. "Promises are for things you control. I *plan.* Planning is promising with work attached."

Vesper made a sound from the overhead that might have been a laugh strangled in its crib. "I'm adding that to my aphorism file."

"You have an aphorism file?"

"I have files for everything. It's how I avoid having feelings about any of them."

Sefu smiled at the ceiling. Then she caught Theo watching from the doorway — he did that, drifted past the lessons, listening — and his face had that look again, the one she'd catalogued carefully over the past weeks: guilt wearing gratitude as a coat. He was thinking, she knew, about what it meant that a child was planning his war for him. She could have told him it wasn't his burden to feel guilty about. She could have told him that the Meridian's crew had voted, unanimously, across all their accumulated ages, to hand the Vector to whoever came, and that fate had sent him, and that fate's choices weren't his fault.

Instead she said: "Captain Delacroix. Do you know the difference between a map and a territory?"

He blinked. "Vesper's been teaching you philosophy?"

"Vesper's been teaching me everything twice. Once as facts and once as apologies." She tapped the chart again. "A map is a promise about the territory. The territory doesn't care. Our job is to be the kind of crew the map believes in."

Theo stood in the doorway a moment longer. Then he nodded, once, the way you nod at something heavy being set down properly, and went forward toward the bridge.

* * *

Night cycle. The ship dimmed to amber. Sefu lay in her bunk — a real bunk, with a blanket Ilsa had found somewhere and scented, absurdly, with lavender, because Ilsa believed children should have unnecessary things — and did the arithmetic one final time, in her head, in the dark, the way she ended every day.

Nine days to the opening. Eleven to the closing. Seven children awake, twelve sleeping, one ship, one smuggler, one exiled customs officer, one AI learning honesty the way you learn a language late in life — with an accent, and mistakes, and occasional total silence while it searched for the right word.

Against them: two fleets, a hunter-ship that had already found them once, jamming lattices, fuel margins described by Vesper as *tight*, and the simple gravitational fact that large violent institutions roll toward truth like boulders toward glass.

For them: a door that legally cannot be closed. A napkin full of correct math. And the specific, unglamorous, indestructible fact that they had already survived the worst thing the universe could do to them. Everything after the Meridian was, in the strictest sense, bonus time. You could not frighten people who had already grieved themselves. Both fleets should understand this, Sefu thought drowsily. Fear was leverage only against those with something left to lose.

Her eyes were closing when Vesper spoke, very softly, on the cabin channel — a volume meant only for her, which was itself a new behavior, a courtesy it had learned recently, one honest inch at a time.

"Sefu. May I ask you something?"

"Mm."

"When you say we won't miss the window. Is that arithmetic, or hope?"

Sefu opened her eyes in the amber dark. Above her bunk, someone — Ilsa, probably — had stuck a handful of glow-stars in a pattern that matched no real constellation, a constellation of pure invention, comfort-shaped.

"Arithmetic," she said. "Hope is just arithmetic you haven't finished yet."

A pause. Then Vesper, quietly: "Then I'll keep working the problem with you. All nine days."

"All nine days," Sefu agreed, and slept.

She did not hear, three hours later, the soft chime in the cockpit, or Vesper's careful voice waking Theo, or the words that would end the nine days of quiet before they began:

*"Theo — wake up. Long-range just painted us. It's the Concordat hunter. And Captain Oyelaran is hailing us by name."*
