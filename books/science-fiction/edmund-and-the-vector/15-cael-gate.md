## 15. Cael Gate

Ilsa Reyes had spent nineteen years of her life learning every rule Cael Gate had ever published, and the first thing she noticed when the relay swam up out of the dark was that someone had broken all of them at once.

The station hung in the long silence between systems like a paper lantern — a spindle of white habitat rings around a black transmission core, its running lights blinking the old neutral pattern: *all are welcome, none are judged.* She'd memorized that pattern as a cadet. It was supposed to be the one honest thing in the whole corridor.

Except tonight the honest thing was lying. The dock lights were green, but the traffic control band was silent in a way no neutral station is ever silent. And parked in a lazy crescent around the relay, close enough to cast shadows on each other's hulls, sat eleven warships.

Six Republic cruisers, their hulls painted the gray of bureaucratic apology. Five Concordat hunters, angular and hungry-looking even at rest. And between them, moored to the station's primary pylon like a guest of honor, a joint command barge flying both flags side by side.

"Well," said Theo from the pilot's chair, very quietly. "That's new."

"They're cooperating," Ilsa said. She heard her own voice go flat and official, the way it did when she was frightened. Nineteen years of customs work taught you that tone was armor. "Republic Seventh Flotilla, Concordat Hunter Wing Nine. Joint task force. There's probably a memorandum of understanding with a lovely cover page."

"Against us."

"Against the message." She turned in the copilot's seat to look at him. He had the particular stillness he got when he was doing arithmetic he didn't like. Behind him, down the corridor toward the hab ring, she could hear the children — Sefu's voice leading some kind of recitation, the younger ones echoing in their eerie unison, a sound like a small choir that had already attended its own funeral and come back polite about it.

"The law itself," Ilsa said, "is the obstacle."

She hadn't meant to say it out loud. But there it was, hanging in the cockpit air between them, and Theo didn't argue, which somehow made it worse.

* * *

They came in slow and dark under a freighter's transponder Vesper had stitched together out of three dead ships' registries, and Ilsa spent the approach hours doing what she did best: reading paperwork at a distance.

"You see the queue?" she asked, fingers dancing across the traffic feed. "Look at the docking schedule. Every berth assigned through next week. Every slot filled by ships flagged to fleet logistics commands. They haven't just occupied the station, Theo. They've *booked* it."

"That's legal?"

"It's worse than legal. It's *procedural*." She laughed, and it came out bitter. "You can't storm a neutral relay. Both fleets would scream about sovereignty for a decade. So instead they filed priority berthing requests, bought out the queue, and posted a customs advisory citing a 'hazardous signal anomaly.' Any vessel carrying unregistered data gets impounded pending inspection. Which means any vessel carrying *the Vector* sits in a holding pattern until the jump window closes and Sefu's arithmetic comes due." She shook her head. "Somebody on that barge went to the same academy I did. This is a paperwork siege."

From the doorway behind them, a small voice said, "Then we should file better paperwork."

Sefu stood there with two of the younger ones clinging to her sleeves — little Anaya and the boy they all called Pip because his real name was forty years too heavy for him. Sefu had the look she always had, that terrible calm of a person who had grieved everything once already and found the second time easier.

"I used to audit docking queues," Ilsa told her. "Before."

"Before you were disgraced?"

"I prefer 'before I developed standards.'"

Sefu almost smiled. It was one of maybe six smiles anyone had coaxed out of her since the Meridian, and Ilsa found herself absurdly proud of it, the way you're proud of a flower growing through pavement.

"The window opens in thirty-one hours," Sefu said. "It closes fifty-two hours after that. If we are not broadcasting by then—" She stopped. She never finished those sentences anymore. The children had an agreement among themselves not to finish them, Ilsa had noticed. As if leaving the sentence open kept the death inside it hypothetical.

"We'll be broadcasting," Theo said. "Vesper. Options."

The ship's voice filled the cockpit, warm as ever, and Ilsa had learned enough in nine days of running dark to hear the seams in that warmth now — the places where honesty had been patched in recently, like new fabric over old burns.

"Option one," Vesper said, "we run. There is a gap in their sensor lattice at bearing two-seven-zero. Probability of clean escape: thirty-one percent. Probability we then spend the rest of our lives being hunted by two fleets with nothing to show for it: considerably higher."

"No," Theo said.

"Option two, we dock under the false registry, play dumb freighter, and wait for a mistake. Estimated time until a mistake occurs: unknown. Estimated time until Oyelaran's hunter scans our cargo bay: four days, if his patience holds, which historically—"

"No."

"Option three," Vesper said, and paused, and Ilsa realized the pause wasn't dramatic. It was reluctant. "There is no option three that I can construct honestly. I have been trying for six hours. I want the record to show I tried."

Something in Ilsa's chest squeezed. Nine days ago that sentence would have been impossible. The AI had lied so long and so beautifully that hearing it strain toward truth was like watching a bird relearn a wing.

"There's always option three," Ilsa said slowly. "There's always option three. You just have to stop thinking like a pilot and start thinking like a customs officer."

Theo looked at her. "I'm listening."

"The queue," she said. "They bought the queue. Which means the queue exists. Which means somewhere in that station's traffic management system is a priority docking protocol — emergency medical, diplomatic courier, tribunal witness transport. Slots that nobody can legally refuse. Not the task force commander, not the station master, not God himself. Because if Cael Gate starts refusing priority slots, it stops being neutral, and the moment it stops being neutral, both fleets lose their favorite place to do dirty business." She felt the old machinery waking up in her, the thing that had made her good at this job and then gotten her destroyed by it. "You don't fight a paperwork siege with guns. You fight it with *better paperwork*."

"And can you forge a priority slot?" Theo asked.

"Oh, forging's easy. Forging gets caught in ninety seconds by anyone competent." She grinned, and was aware it was probably not a comforting grin. "No. I'm not going to forge anything. I'm going to use my *real* credentials."

The cockpit went quiet.

"Ilsa," Theo said carefully, "your credentials were revoked."

"Revoked, yes. Deleted, no. That's the beautiful joke of it, Theo. Disgrace moves fast but bureaucracy moves slow. My officer ID is still live in the Cael Gate system because revocation requires countersignature from my regional director, who was reassigned before signing it, so the request has been sitting in a pending folder for six years waiting for a human being with authority to notice it." She spread her hands. "As far as Cael Gate's traffic computer is concerned, Senior Customs Officer Ilsa Reyes, badge 4471-Corvid, is on extended leave. With priority access rights. Pending administrative review."

Vesper made a sound that Ilsa could only describe as a machine falling in love.

"That," the AI said, "is the most beautiful lie I have ever heard, and the astonishing part is that it isn't one."

"It's not a lie. It's a *loophole*. Lies are for people who can't read the regulations." She stood, suddenly restless, suddenly certain. "Get me a hardline into the station's public traffic feed. Not the military band — the civilian one, the one the station master's office actually runs. I need twenty minutes and nobody asking me questions."

"Why twenty minutes?" Theo asked.

But it was Sefu who answered, from the doorway, in her flat child's voice that carried decades. "Because a broadcast takes eleven minutes to cycle the core. And because everything else," she said, "is how long hope lasts before someone checks the paperwork."

* * *

They gathered in the mess that evening — the term the children insisted on, though the Meridian's crew had once called it the galley and Theo called it the room with the bad coffee. Ilsa watched them come in and felt the familiar ache she'd stopped fighting sometime around day five.

Nine children. The oldest was Sefu, who wore her reversed years like a coat too big. The youngest was Pip, who was biologically six and experientially sixty-one and mostly wanted someone to read him stories about pirates, which Ilsa suspected was less nostalgia than professional research.

They ate reconstituted stew and argued about star-charts, because Vesper had been teaching them navigation as penance, and the lessons had become the closest thing any of them had to a religion.

"—and *that's* why you never trust a beacon," Vesper was saying through the table speaker, in the patient tone it reserved for the children. "A beacon tells you where things are supposed to be. A chart tells you where things actually are. The difference between those two facts is where most accidents live."

"Is that also where most wars live?" asked Anaya, who was seven and merciless.

There was a pause. Ilsa caught Theo's eye across the table. Neither of them breathed.

"Yes," Vesper said finally. "Yes, it is. Well done. That will be on the exam."

Pip giggled. Sefu didn't laugh, but she looked at the speaker for a long moment with something that might have been forgiveness beginning, or might have been indigestion. With Sefu it was genuinely hard to tell.

Later, when the little ones were bedded down in the hammocks they'd rigged in the cargo bay — they refused cabins, all of them; they said rooms reminded them of the long dark stretch of the voyage home, and none of the adults ever asked what that meant — Ilsa found Theo at the viewport, watching the task force's running lights crawl across the black.

"My sister died in the opening atrocity," she said, without preamble. She'd told him once, at Waypoint Nine, drunk on two sips of contraband brandy, and had pretended since that she hadn't. Tonight she decided to stop pretending. "Marisol. She was stationed at Halcyon Reach when both fleets decided the other side had fired first. Neither had. Somebody fed them both the same lie and let them pull the triggers." She wrapped her hands around a cup of the bad coffee. "For nineteen years I thought justice meant somebody standing trial. Uniforms, verdicts, a judge with a nice baritone. And then I got disgraced for noticing a discrepancy in a shipping manifest, and I learned that the courts are just more paperwork, and the paperwork belongs to the people who wrote the lie."

Theo said nothing. He was good at that. Most smugglers talked; Theo listened, which was why Vesper's lies had room to grow in him, and why the truth had taken root there too.

"So tomorrow," Ilsa went on, "when I spoof that queue — and I will spoof that queue, don't make that face, I've been drafting the injection sequence since dinner — I'm not doing it for your father's manifest, or the Vector, or the war. I'm doing it because Marisol Reyes deserved a court, and if the universe won't give her one, then the record becomes the court. Every ear that hears the truth is a bench in the gallery. Let the fleets convene their tribunals. We'll hold ours in public."

Theo looked at her. "You know they'll take your credentials the moment they realize what happened. Really revoke them this time. Maybe worse."

"I've been disgraced for six years, Theo. It turns out it's not fatal. Mostly it's just quiet, and you get very good at cards." She smiled. "Besides. What are they going to do? Arrest me for filing accurate forms?"

* * *

At 0400 station-relative, Ilsa sat alone in the cockpit with a hardline cable in her lap and the injection sequence glowing on her screen, and allowed herself one minute of fear.

It was a disciplined minute. She'd practiced. Nineteen years of customs inspections had taught her that fear was just another form to fill out: name, date, nature of dread, supporting documentation. Hers read simply — *they will catch me, and the children will die unwound, and Marisol's lie will win forever* — and she stamped it APPROVED, PROCEED ANYWAY, the way she'd stamped ten thousand manifests, and plugged in the cable.

Cael Gate's traffic system bloomed around her, vast and sleepy and trusting, a cathedral built entirely of schedules. She moved through it the way water moves through stone: patiently, along the grooves already cut. Pending revocation request, badge 4471-Corvid — there it was, gathering six years of digital dust. She didn't delete it. Deleting left scars. Instead she *advanced* it, one procedural step, into the review queue, where it would sit for another six years while a different kind of request took precedence.

The request she wrote herself, in perfect institutional grammar, using her own living credentials:

*PRIORITY BERTHING REQUEST — SENIOR CUSTOMS OFFICER I. REYES (4471-CORVID), EXTENDED LEAVE STATUS, RETURNING TO ACTIVE REVIEW DUTY. NATURE OF INSPECTION: HAZARDOUS SIGNAL ANOMALY, PER ADVISORY 7734-K. REQUESTING DOCK 12 ACCESS, TWENTY-MINUTE SLOT, WITNESS-VERIFIED DATA INTEGRITY AUDIT.*

And then, because the truly elegant part of any lie is the part that's true, she attached the actual text of Advisory 7734-K — the task force's own customs advisory, the one they'd posted to keep people away from the Vector. Their words. Their authority. Their signature.

She was quoting the siege back at itself.

The system considered. Somewhere in the station's core, a process older than the war weighed her credentials against the advisory against the priority protocol against nineteen years of rules neither fleet had bothered to repeal, because repealing rules required cooperation, and cooperation, until this week, had been the one thing the Republic and the Concordat could never manage.

*REQUEST ACKNOWLEDGED,* the system said. *DOCK 12 RESERVED. WINDOW: 20 MINUTES. PRIORITY STATUS: NON-REFUSABLE.*

Ilsa sat back. Her hands were shaking. She let them.

On the screen, the task force's command barge blinked placidly, unaware that its own paperwork had just been turned into a crowbar. Eleven warships, a memorandum of understanding, a bought-out queue, a customs advisory — and all of it helpless against a revoked officer's pending file and a twenty-minute slot that no one could legally refuse.

She keyed the intercom. "Theo. Wake the children. Dock 12, twenty minutes, starting whenever we say."

A pause. Then his voice, rough with sleep and something brighter underneath: "How?"

"I filed a form," Ilsa said. "In triplicate."

Down the corridor, faintly, she heard Sefu begin to wake the others — and heard, beneath the small feet and the whispered countdown, the deep mechanical hum of the station's defense grid shifting, somewhere far above, as a hunter's scan swept the docks and began, methodically, to look for them.

Twenty minutes. Hope, measured out in the only currency the law still honored.

Ilsa pulled up the approach vector and started the clock.
