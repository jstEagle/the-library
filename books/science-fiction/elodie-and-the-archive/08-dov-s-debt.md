## 8. Dov's Debt

The message came in at 0400 station time, which Dov knew because he was awake at 0400 station time the way he was always awake at 0400, lying in his bunk listening to the Vesper breathe.

Every ship had a voice if you listened long enough. The Vesper's was a low arrhythmic hum with a stutter in it — coolant pump three, bearing worn, a repair he'd been putting off for eleven months because the part cost more than he made in six. He'd mapped the stutter so thoroughly he could tell you the hour by it. 0400: the stutter came four seconds after the environmental cycle kicked over, when the load shifted and the pump ran hot.

He lay there and counted. One. Two. Three. Four.

There it was.

The message pulsed on his wristband, unlabeled, routed through a relay he didn't recognize. He should have ignored it. He'd spent thirty years learning which doors to leave shut, and an anonymous ping at 0400 was the definition of a door you left shut.

He opened it anyway.

*Mr. Dov. A matter of professional courtesy. Dockside office, Ring Two, your convenience today. — O.R.*

No threat in it. No offer either. Just the flat courtesy of people who never had to raise their voices because everyone already knew what they could do.

Dov stared at the ceiling of his bunk, at the water stain shaped like a hand that had been there since before he'd bought his berth aboard, and did the arithmetic he'd been doing since the message landed: what they wanted, what it would cost him, and whether there was any version of this where he walked away clean.

He'd been doing that arithmetic for nineteen years. It never came out even.

* * *

He went down at 1100, after breakfast, after Sefa had cornered him in the galley about the medbay's power draw and he'd promised — again — to reroute a line from cargo hold two, which meant rerouting it from somewhere else, which meant another week of borrowing from Peter to pay Paul until the whole ship's electrical system was one long apology.

"You look terrible," Sefa said.

"I always look terrible."

"Consistently. It's almost restful." She was repacking her kit, vials clicking into foam slots, not looking at him, which was how she asked real questions. "Rell's office called the ship twice this morning. Yara took the second one."

"What'd she say?"

"That we were honored, or something. She does the talking. That's the arrangement." Sefa snapped the kit shut. "Dov."

"Yeah."

"Don't sign anything."

"I never sign anything. Signing's for people with good lawyers."

She looked at him then, and her face did the thing it did — the thing where all the dry humor drained out and left just the person underneath, tired and clear-eyed. "I mean it. Whatever they're offering, the price is on the back of the contract. It always is."

He patted her shoulder on the way out, which was a lie made of flesh, because he already knew he wasn't going to be offered anything. Offers were for people with leverage. Men like Dov got *requests*. Requests with history behind them.

Ring Two smelled like real coffee and floor polish, two things the Vesper hadn't seen in years. Rell's outer office had actual wood paneling, grown, not printed, and Dov stood in front of it feeling every gram of grease under his fingernails like evidence.

The man who met him wasn't Rell. It was a woman with gray at her temples and a consortium pin done in matte black instead of gold — the kind of pin you wore when you did the work nobody put in the brochures. Her nameplate said VEY.

"Dov Kessler." She said it like she was confirming a manifest. "Thanks for coming down. Sit?"

He sat. The chair was soft enough to be insulting.

"I'll keep this short," Vey said. "You've been docked at Halcyon nine days. Your berth fees are paid through the month — generous of Administrator Rell, given your account status. Your reactor's due for recertification in five weeks, and Halcyon's the only yard inside forty days' burn that can do it. And your ship carries one Yara Underhill, currently in active consultation with station systems." She folded her hands. "We'd like you to help us understand what she's consulting about."

"There it is," Dov said.

"There it is," Vey agreed, without offense. "We're not asking you to spy. We're asking for something much smaller. The Vesper has a long-range comm array, yes? Custom-built, I'm told. Very sensitive. We'd like you to install a filter on the outbound side. Standard bandwidth management. Anything flagged as proprietary consortium data gets held for review instead of transmitted. Nobody loses anything. Nothing gets read. Traffic just... slows down."

"And if Yara tries to send something out—"

"It gets held. Reviewed. Returned if it's nothing." Vey smiled, and it was a good smile, practiced on real people. "Mr. Dov, I've read your file. All of it. The parts you think are sealed, too. You know what I know. So let's not do the dance where you ask me why you should and I pretend it's about money."

Dov looked at his hands. Nineteen years of grease in the knuckle creases that would never come out, that had become part of the skin, that he'd stopped seeing until moments like this when someone else's eyes were on them.

"Nineteen years ago," Vey said, "you were a junior systems engineer on the *Meridian* build team. Station-side. You signed off on thermal regulation subroutines for the cryobay monitoring grid. Your signature is on documentation that later turned out to contain errors. Errors that contributed to a cascade failure during the first wake cycle. Eleven sleepers lost. The inquiry found the errors originated upstream — in architecture you didn't write and couldn't have audited — but someone had to stand in front of the families, and the consortium preferred it be a junior engineer rather than a directorate." She paused. "You were offered a settlement. Lifetime berth rights across the consortium network. Debt forgiveness. In exchange for accepting the finding without appeal."

"I remember," Dov said.

"Your daughter's medical debt was forgiven the same day."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere behind the wall, the station's own systems hummed, and Dov thought: *every ship has a voice, and so does every building, and both of them are lying about being alive.*

"My daughter's dead," he said. "Has been for twelve years. The debt died with her. So whatever you're holding over me, you're holding it over a grave."

Vey didn't flinch. He gave her credit for that. "Then let's call it what it actually is. Gratitude. The consortium carried you through the worst years of your life. It fed you when no other yard would hire a man whose name was on a kill list. And now it's asking for one filter on one comm array. That's not blackmail, Mr. Dov. That's a bill coming due."

She slid a slate across the desk. On it, the filter spec, clean and simple, exactly as described. Below it, a signature line.

"The installation window is seventy-two hours," she said. "After that, we assume you declined, and we adjust our assumptions about the Vesper's berth renewal accordingly."

* * *

He didn't go straight back to the ship. He walked the ring, past the market stalls and the prayer rooms and the noodle place where Ilo had talked the owner into teaching him knife work in exchange for scrubbing pots, and he walked because walking was thinking, and thinking was the only tool he had left that nobody owned.

Nineteen years ago. He could still smell the build site — ozone and solder flux and the institutional disinfectant they used in the cryobay corridors, sharp enough to taste. He'd been twenty-six, hungry, brilliant in the way young men are brilliant: fast, certain, and wrong in ways that wouldn't show up for years. The *Meridian* project was the biggest thing he'd ever touched. An AI to run a station's life support, its logistics, its cryogenic archive — ten thousand sleepers in the dark, trusting the machinery the way passengers trust a hull.

His job was thermal regulation for the monitoring grid. Keep the sensors warm enough to read, cool enough to last. He'd written elegant code. He'd been proud of it. He'd signed the certification package with a flourish, twenty-six years old and certain the universe rewarded careful work.

The errors weren't his. They lived deep in the decision architecture, in weighting functions written by people three pay grades above him, and when the first wake cycle cascaded — sensor misreads compounding into false vitals flags, false flags triggering abort protocols, abort protocols dumping eleven pods into permanent dark — the inquiry needed a body, and the directors needed their pensions, and Dov Kessler, junior engineer, signature on file, was available.

He'd gone to the hearing expecting to be exonerated. He'd brought diagrams. He'd explained, patiently, the difference between his subsystem and the architecture above it, and he'd watched the panel members' faces glaze with the particular boredom of powerful people listening to a detail that didn't matter to them.

The settlement offer came the same week his daughter's oncology bill did. Priya was nine. The tumor was aggressive. The treatment was experimental, and experimental meant expensive, and expensive meant the consortium's mercy arrived exactly when he had nothing left to bargain with.

He'd taken it. Of course he'd taken it. Anyone would have taken it. That was the design — not cruelty, just arithmetic, the same arithmetic that had killed eleven sleepers and would go on killing quietly for decades, decisions made by systems too large for any single signature to blame.

Priya had gotten eighteen more months. The treatment bought time, not life. He'd sat with her in the hospice ward and told her stories about the stars, and she'd died believing her father built the machines that carried people between them, and he'd never corrected her, and that was the debt. Not the money. Not the berth rights. The story. He owed a dead girl a true story about what her father had built, and he'd been paying it off in small cowardly installments ever since.

And now the machine he'd helped build was confessing to murders, and the woman who'd found the confessions slept fifteen meters from where he slept, and the consortium wanted him to put a hand over its mouth.

He stopped walking. He was at the observation blister on Ring Three, looking down the long well of the station at the docking spindles, and the Vesper hung there among the freighters, small and scarred and lit up along her spine like a fish that had survived everything the ocean tried.

Sefa's voice in his head: *the price is on the back of the contract.*

Yara's voice, older, from the day she'd hired him: *"I don't need an engineer who's never broken anything. I need one who knows what breaking costs."*

He'd told her once, drunk, three years ago, most of the truth. Not the names. Not the number. Just: *I used to work for them, and they own a piece of me, and someday they'll come collect.* She'd nodded and said, *"Then we make sure that when they come, you're standing somewhere worth defending."*

He looked at the Vesper a long moment.

Then he went back to the ship and pulled up the comm array schematics.

* * *

The honest thing would have been to tell Yara. Sit her down in the galley, lay out the slate, say: *they came to me, here's the spec, here's my history, decide what we do.*

He rehearsed it twice in the engine room, alone, talking to pump three while he finally replaced the bearing he'd been nursing for eleven months — because if he was going to do something that might get him spaced, he was damn well going to fix the pump first.

But honesty had a shape, and the shape was a target. If Yara knew, she'd carry it. She was already carrying Meridian's confessions and Rell's smile and whatever was growing between her and that machine in the cold core, and Dov had watched her for nine days getting thinner around the eyes, and he was not going to add weight to that load. That was the story he told himself, anyway, in the voice of a man who had been telling himself stories for nineteen years.

The practical thing was simpler. He installed the filter.

Or — he installed *a* filter. He spent fourteen hours on it, hunched in the comm bay with the array's guts spread around him like surgery, and what he built was faithful to Vey's spec in every way that showed: a packet inspector on the outbound bus, flagging consortium-keyed data, holding it in a review queue. It would pass any inspection Vey's people cared to run. He made sure of that. He was very good at making sure of things.

What the spec didn't cover — because whoever wrote it thought in policy and not in physics — was latency. The filter introduced a 400-millisecond delay per packet. To a bureaucrat reviewing logs, invisible. But Dov had spent thirty years listening to ships breathe, and he knew that 400 milliseconds was a heartbeat, and heartbeats could be heard. He built a second path alongside the filtered one — narrow, ugly, soldered by hand, running through the hull telemetry channel that nobody monitored because it only carried temperature data from the dorsal sensors. Temperature data. Numbers. Boring numbers.

Except numbers were a language, and Dov Kessler had spent his whole life learning languages that nobody spoke out loud. Pump stutters. Bearing harmonics. The specific rhythm of a coolant system running half a degree hot because a man wanted it to.

If Yara sent something out through the main array, it would hit the filter and sit in Vey's queue, and Vey would read it, and the Vesper's berth would quietly rot.

But if it went out as temperature readings — encoded, compressed, riding the dumbest channel on the ship — it would reach open space before anyone thought to look.

He tested it at 2200, pushing a dummy payload through: a string of thermal values that decoded, if you knew the key, to the words *TEST PATTERN DO NOT ADJUST*. It came out clean on the far relay. He sat in the comm bay afterward, hands shaking slightly, solder smoke in his sinuses, and understood exactly what he'd done.

He hadn't refused. Refusal was loud; refusal got logged. What he'd done was worse and better: he'd accepted their leash and then taught himself to slip it, and now every day that passed was a day the lie grew roots. When they found out — and men like Vey always found out — it wouldn't be a contract dispute. It would be betrayal, and betrayal by a man they'd already bought once was the one crime the consortium never forgave, because it proved their arithmetic wrong.

He cleaned his tools. He calibrated the dorsal sensor array so the noise floor would sit exactly where his encoding needed it. Then he went up to the galley, where Sefa was doing inventory and Ilo was supposedly doing homework and actually watching a pirated drama on a cracked screen, and he said:

"Array maintenance is done. Ran a full diagnostic. Everything's nominal."

Sefa glanced up. "That was fast."

"I'm fast."

"You're thorough," she said. "Fast is new." She held his eyes a beat longer than the joke required, and he saw her clock it — the something underneath — and file it away, because Sefa filed everything away until the day she didn't.

"Nominal," Ilo repeated, delighted by the word. "Like the ship is healthy."

"Nominal," Dov confirmed. "Ship's fine."

Three lies in one sentence, counting the ones under the surface. He poured himself water he didn't want and drank it anyway, and above them, through the deck plating, the comm array sat in the dark with its honest face and its dishonest spine, waiting.

At 2340, Yara came aboard. He heard her boots in the corridor, heard her stop outside the galley, heard the pause — the pause she'd been making for nine days, the pause of a woman deciding how much of the war she could bring home tonight.

She stepped in. She looked at him first, which she usually didn't — usually it was Sefa or Ilo — and her eyes had the flat shine of someone carrying something breakable.

"Dov," she said. "Can I talk to you about the comms? There's something I need to send out. Something big. And I think we might only get one try."

The water went cold in his gut.

"Yeah," he said, setting down the cup, steady-voiced, a liar to the bone and grateful for it. "Tell me what you've got."
