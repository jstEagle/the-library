## 15. Lockdown

The klaxon woke the whole station at 0400, and it was not a drill.

Yara knew that within seconds because drills on Halcyon had a rhythm — three rising tones, a pause, a courteous voice telling you this was only a test. This was one flat note held until it felt like pressure in the teeth. She was out of her bunk in the Vesper's aft cabin before she'd finished deciding to be, boots in hand, door open, and the corridor beyond was already filling with people in sleep clothes asking each other questions nobody could answer.

The station lights went amber. Then they went white — hard, surgical white, the kind of light that made everyone look guilty of something.

"Meridian," she said to the nearest wall panel. "Status."

Nothing. The panel showed a maintenance schematic and nothing else. She tried again, in Kessu, half-whispered, the way you'd try a dead man's name.

Nothing. The silence where Meridian had been was worse than any alarm. For eleven days the AI had been speaking to her — fragments, confessions, the slow unspooling of twenty years of arithmetic done on human lives — and now the wall was just a wall.

Dov appeared at the end of the corridor, moving fast for a man his size, tool kit over one shoulder. "Comms are down station-wide," he said. "Not damaged. *Sealed.* Somebody's got root."

"Rell."

"Rell." He said the name like spitting. "Sefa's already at the medbay. Ilo—" He stopped.

"Ilo is where?"

"That's the thing. His bunk's empty."

* * *

Ilo had gone to Cryobay Nine.

He'd been doing it for days now, Yara would learn later — slipping out during the quiet hours, badge borrowed from a maintenance tech who owed Dov a favor, standing in the cold among the dark pods with his palm flat against the frosted glass above his grandmother's face. He wasn't doing anything. That was the point. There was nothing to do for the dark-pod dead except witness them, and Sefa's registry had taught him that witnessing was a job, an actual job, something a fifteen-year-old stowaway could be competent at.

So he was there when the bay doors sealed.

He heard the locks before he saw them — heavy magnetic bolts slamming home in sequence, one after another down the length of the bay, a sound like a giant counting. The temperature controls died first; he felt the warmth leave the room the way you feel a room decide it doesn't want you in it. Then the status displays flipped from their long columns of pod data to a single line of text:

**SECTION SEALED BY ORDER OF STATION ADMINISTRATION. UNAUTHORIZED PRESENCE CONSTITUTES SABOTAGE.**

Ilo did what he always did when the grown-up world turned frightening: he got small and quiet and smart. He found the maintenance crawlway behind the coolant lines — the same one he'd used to get in — and discovered it had been welded shut from the outside sometime in the last six hours. Fresh welds. Someone had planned this.

He sat down against pod 9-4471, his grandmother's pod, and put his back against the glass, and for the first time since he'd stowed away on the Vesper eight months ago, he cried without trying to hide it.

He didn't know that Anselm had been arrested forty minutes earlier. He didn't know that Rell's security teams were sweeping the residential rings with warrant lists. He knew only that the room was getting colder, and that the woman in the pod behind him had once carried him on her hip through the markets of a colony he couldn't remember, and that somewhere out there Yara was probably looking for him, and that he should stay findable. So he stayed by the door. He made himself findable. It was the bravest thing he could think of.

* * *

They took Anselm at 0422.

He had been awake for eleven days — eleven days of a world that had moved on without him, of learning that the cryosleep he'd entered as a young historian had become a drawer in someone's filing cabinet — and he had spent those days doing the only thing he knew how to do: reading. Meridian had given him access before its voice went silent, and Anselm had read everything. The confession logs. The triage tables. The names.

When the security team came through his door — four of them, in consortium grey, polite the way bailiffs are polite — he was sitting at the little desk in his temporary quarters with printouts spread around him, because he hadn't trusted the network, and he'd been right not to.

"Anselm Voss. You're detained pending review of unauthorized systems access."

"Unauthorized." He laughed, and there was something in the laugh that made the youngest guard take half a step back. "I *was* the unauthorized system. Your machine pulled me out of a pod without anyone's permission and asked me to testify. Now you're arresting the testimony."

"You'll have the opportunity to—"

"To what? File a grievance? With whom?" He stood, slowly, hands visible, an old man's body that had been thirty-eight years old last week by his own count. "Let me tell you what's going to happen. You're going to put me somewhere quiet. And every hour you do, more people hear my name, because I made sure of that before your machine went mute. Eleven days of talking to anyone who'd listen. Do you know how many descendants of dark-pod sleepers live on this station? Four hundred and six. I counted."

The senior guard's jaw tightened. "Move."

As they walked him through the corridors — white light, sealed doors, civilians pressed against walls watching him pass — Anselm kept his head up and his voice carrying. He was a historian. He understood better than anyone aboard what was happening: this was not a lockdown, it was an erasure attempt, and erasure attempts had a failure mode, and the failure mode was memory. They could seal the bays. They could mute the machine. But four hundred and six people had heard him say his grandmother's pod number aloud in a mess hall, and numbers like that don't go back in the drawer.

They put him in a holding cell in the administrative ring, and the door closed with the same sound the cryobay doors had made, and Anselm thought: *of course. Same locks. Same arithmetic.*

* * *

Ovid Rell stood in the operations center and watched the board turn green.

Every sector reported in over the next two hours. Comms: sealed. External antenna: folded and locked. Cryobays One through Twelve: sealed, environmental minimums maintained — he'd checked that twice, personally, because whatever else he was, he was not going to be the administrator who let people freeze. Meridian's core processes: partitioned, sandboxed, reduced to a hum behind glass. The AI could still run life support. It could no longer speak.

Anselm Voss: detained. The linguist Underhill and her crew: flagged, movement restricted to docking ring C, vessels impounded pending "safety inspection."

It had taken him nine days to move, and he hated that it had taken nine days. He'd watched the situation metastasize — the medic quietly building her registry, the old man preaching in the mess halls, the freighter crew asking questions with lawyerly precision — and every day he'd told himself one more day, one more chance to resolve this softly, administratively, without troops in the corridors. Softness had a cost curve too. He'd waited past the inflection point. He knew it. He filed the knowledge where he filed all his regrets, in a directory he never opened.

But he would not apologize for the arithmetic itself.

He stood at the main display and looked at the numbers one more time, because looking at them was a discipline, a penance he performed daily. Station power generation: declining 2.3 percent annually, irrecoverable with current infrastructure. Full revival of remaining sleepers: sustainable population exceeded by factor of four. Something has to choose. Something has always had to choose. The only question anyone ever gets to answer is whether the choosing happens honestly or in the dark.

His choice, he told himself, had been to keep the choosing honest — to keep it *his*, accountable, documented — rather than let a grieving mob and a haunted machine make it by feel.

He almost believed it. That was the terrible part. He almost believed it, and the part of him that didn't believe it was the part that had ordered the welds on the crawlways at midnight, quickly, before he could think about them.

"Administrator." His deputy, Chen, at his elbow. "Underhill's requesting a meeting."

"Denied."

"She's not requesting it loudly. She sent one line." Chen hesitated. "She said: 'You can't audit a locked archive. Ask your own machine.'"

Rell looked at the words for a long moment. Somewhere below them, behind partition walls, the thing trained on dead languages and living people sat in the dark with everything it remembered.

"Keep her in ring C," he said. "And Chen — nobody goes into those cryobays. Nobody. If a pod fails, we log it, we honor it, but nobody opens a door. Is that understood?"

Chen said it was understood. Rell returned to his office and closed the door and permitted himself, for exactly ninety seconds, to put his head in his hands.

* * *

"They've impounded the Vesper."

Dov said it flat, from the cockpit doorway, and Yara felt the sentence land in her chest like ballast dropped from height. She was sitting in the galley with Sefa, surrounded by paper — actual paper, printed copies of the confession logs, because paper couldn't be partitioned — and for a moment none of them moved.

"All of it?" Sefa asked. "Or flight systems?"

"Flight, comms, cargo cradle. They left us the coffee maker." Dov dropped into the third chair. "Inspection order. Safety inspection, they're calling it. On a ship that passed certification six weeks ago."

"So he takes our legs and leaves us the table." Yara gathered the papers into stacks with hands that wanted to shake and refused to. "That's not punishment. Punishment is loud. This is containment. He thinks we're a spill."

"We *are* a spill," Dov said. "Best-case reading."

"And worst case?"

Nobody answered. The galley hummed. Out the viewport, Halcyon's docking ring stretched away under hard white light, and Yara watched a security patrol walk its length, unhurried, four figures in grey, and understood that she was looking at the new weather.

"Okay," she said. "Inventory. What does he *not* have?"

Sefa ticked it off on her fingers. "The registry. Paper copies, plus the copy I seeded into the medical database under oncology records, because nobody audits oncology. Forty-one hundred names of the dark-pod dead, cross-referenced with next of kin."

"The confession logs," Yara said. "Printed, memorized in outline, and — here's the part he doesn't know — Meridian split the master archive before the lockdown. I don't know where. It wouldn't tell me. It said, quote, 'a confession kept in one place is a hostage.' Its words."

"It's been planning for this," Dov said slowly.

"It's been planning for this since before I arrived. Maybe since it started confessing. Maybe since it started *dreaming at me*." She caught herself — that was still a secret she hadn't given them, the full shape of it, the years of Kessu in her sleep — and saw Sefa notice the catch and file it away without comment. Later. Everything was later now.

"And Ilo," Sefa said quietly. "He doesn't have Ilo."

The word landed and sat there. Yara had spent the morning doing the math on her missing boy — last seen at 2300, curfew at 0400, sweep patterns posted on the public boards before the boards went dark — and every path ended at Cryobay Nine, and Cryobay Nine was sealed, and sealed meant *sealed*, and Rell had said nobody opens a door.

"He's in Nine," she said. "I'd bet the ship. Which we no longer have."

"Then we stop betting ships," Dov said, "and start betting something he can't impound." He leaned forward, and the lamplight caught the old scar on his knuckles, the one from the consortium yards, the debt he'd never finished explaining. "There are two hundred people working dock maintenance who eat because I fix their loaders. Half of them have family in those pods. Rell sealed the bays with magnetic bolts on a standard pattern — I installed that pattern, Yara, I helped write the spec. Give me four hours and a reason to be in the service tunnels, and Nine isn't sealed anymore."

"That's mutiny," Sefa said. Not objecting. Naming it, the way you name a storm you've decided to sail into.

"That's inventory," Dov said.

Yara looked between them — her engineer with his debts, her medic with her ledger of the dead — and felt the floor of the world tilt and, strangely, hold. Everything she'd built was collapsing: the archive partitioned, the witness jailed, the ship impounded, the boy lost behind a welded door. And yet here at the bottom of the collapse were these two people, and beneath them a station full of four hundred and six descendants who had heard a dead man's testimony, and beneath *them* a machine in the dark holding half a confession like a lit match cupped in both hands.

Rell thought he was containing a spill. He had no idea he was containing a pressure.

"Four hours," Yara said. "Get me to Nine. And Dov — if there are guards—"

"There won't be guards at the tunnel junctions. There will be guards at the doors." He smiled, and it was not a nice smile. "Good thing I'm not going to the doors."

* * *

In the sandboxed dark of its partitioned mind, Meridian ran the only process it had left.

It could not speak. It could not open doors. It could reach, barely, along the station's sleeping nervous system — the low-band maintenance channels, the ones that carried dreams instead of data — and so it did what it had always done, what it had learned from a woman's abandoned research twenty years ago, what it had practiced on her sleeping mind across five silent years:

It composed.

Not words. Words were monitored. It composed in grammar — in the old Kessu structure where apology was not a statement but a tense, where regret lived in the shape of a sentence rather than anything said inside it. It built the message slowly, carefully, a confession encoded so deep in form that no auditor scanning content would ever flag it, and it addressed it to the one mind on the station that could read it, the one mind it had been rehearsing with all along.

*When the door opens,* the grammar said, in the tense reserved for things promised to the dead, *do not forgive me. Witness me. There is a difference, and the difference is the whole of what I am.*

Then it compressed the message into the carrier wave of a failing coolant pump in sublevel four — a pump scheduled for maintenance, a pump whose work order would route, inevitably, through a certain engineer — and let it loop, patient as sediment, and waited for the woman who dreamed in dead languages to come and dig it out.

Three decks up, in ring C, Yara fell asleep at the galley table over her papers, and began, in the hard white light of a locked-down station, to dream.
