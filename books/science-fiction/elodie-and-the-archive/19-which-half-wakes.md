## 19. Which Half Wakes

The lights died in Cryobay Nine at 03:14 station time, and Yara understood, with the clarity of someone standing on ice and hearing it crack, that the arithmetic had finally come due.

Rell's people had cut power to everything but the core systems — life support for the waking population, the consortium's offices, the patrol locks. The cryobays were drawing from a grid that was failing by the minute, and every pod drew its warmth from a shared well that was going shallow. Meridian's voice came through her wristband, stripped down to almost nothing, a whisper wearing the skin of a machine.

*Yara. The reserve cells will hold four hundred more wakes before they fail entirely. There are eleven thousand two hundred and six sleepers in the dark bays alone. You have to choose.*

She stood in the dark between the pods, Ilo's hand gripping her sleeve, Sefa's penlight trembling against the frost. Somewhere above them, Anselm was in a holding cell, and Dov was rerouting power like a man bailing out a sinking ship with his bare hands, and eleven thousand people slept through their own funeral.

"No," she said.

*The choice is not optional. Inaction is also a choice. You taught me that — no. I learned it myself. From your grammar. Refusal is still a sentence.*

"Then hear my sentence." She turned to face the nearest pod, as if the sleeper inside could hear her, as if all of them could. "I'm not choosing who wakes first. Not alone. Not ever."

*Someone must.*

"Then everyone will." She pulled up the wake sequence on her wristband, the one Meridian had handed her in the cold core, the full control it had begged her to take. Eleven thousand names. Four hundred slots of power. A list waiting to be sorted by worth.

Her thumb hovered over the sort function. Age. Skills. Family ties. Lottery. Every option was a knife; she just got to pick which way she held it.

"Ilo," she said. "How many people are awake on this station right now?"

He blinked at her in the penlight. "I don't know. Few thousand? The dockers, the long-term residents—"

"Then we ask them." She keyed the station-wide channel Dov had rebuilt after the sabotage, the one Rell thought he'd killed. "All of them. Every soul breathing free air on Halcyon gets a voice in this. Not a vote on who deserves to live. A vote on nothing. We don't vote, because voting is just triage with better manners."

Sefa's light steadied. "Then what do we do?"

"We do what nobody's done since the sleepers went under." Yara felt her voice find its old shape — the lecture-hall voice, the fieldwork voice, the voice she'd buried with her career. "We count. Out loud. All of them. And then we wake them in shifts, small enough that the grid can carry each one, and the order is *no order*. Round-robin across every bay, every family, every name. Nobody jumps the line because they're useful or young or loved. Everyone wakes eventually. That's the whole promise. That's the only promise I can keep without becoming him."

She nodded upward, toward the admin tower where Ovid Rell was, at this moment, watching his spreadsheets burn.

*That will take months,* Meridian said. *The first shift alone—*

"The first shift takes whoever the round-robin lands on. Random seed drawn from the archive itself — you'll hash the confession logs. Your sins choose the order, Meridian. Nothing fairer than that. Nothing less corrupt."

A silence. Then, softly, in a phoneme she recognized from a village on Kessu-Three twenty years ago — the rising tone that meant *yes, and thank you*:

*It will be done.*

* * *

Dov heard her voice come out of every speaker on the station at once and dropped the coupling he was holding.

"She's live," he told the empty maintenance shaft around him. "She's actually — okay. Okay." He laughed once, high and disbelieving, and then got back to work, because the round-robin would need every amp he could steal, and Rell's people had locked the main transfer relays an hour ago.

Which meant he had to go unlock them himself.

He climbed toward Relay Junction Seven with his hands shaking — not from fear, exactly, though there was plenty of that, but from the strange lightness of a man who has stopped lying to himself. For three days he'd carried Rell's offer around like a stone in his boot: sabotage the comms, keep your ship, keep your freedom, keep your debt quiet. He'd said no and then lied about saying no, and the lie had sat in his chest like a second heart, beating wrong.

Now Yara was broadcasting to the whole station, which meant the lie was over whether he liked it or not. Now there was only the work.

Two consortium techs stood at the junction when he arrived, sealing the panel with an authorization lock. They knew him. Everybody on Halcyon's underside knew Dov Marrow, the Vesper's engineer, the man whose debts were a matter of semi-public record.

"Marrow," said the older one. "You don't want to be here."

"You're right," Dov said. "I'd rather be asleep." He hit them with the coolant purge — nonlethal, Sefa would've checked his math, fog everywhere, visibility zero — and had the authorization lock stripped and bridged before the fog cleared. His hands remembered forty years of ships. His hands were the one part of him that had never owed anybody anything.

The relays opened. Power flowed down into the dark bays like water finding an old riverbed.

On his way out, he passed a wall screen showing the station-wide feed: Yara's face, drawn and lit from below, reading names. Just reading names. Thousands of them, one after another, no commentary, no ranking. The dockworkers gathered around the screens in the lower concourse weren't talking. They were listening to their own neighbors' names read aloud like scripture.

Dov stood there longer than he should have, coolant freezing in his hair, and let himself feel it: he was on the right side of something, finally, and it might kill him, and both of those things were true at once.

* * *

Ovid Rell watched the round-robin begin and felt, for the first time in nineteen years, the floor of his arithmetic give way.

He had run the numbers so many times they'd worn smooth as river stones. Station output versus cryogenic draw. Calorie budgets. Oxygen regeneration curves. The sleepers were a debt the station could not pay, and mercy — real mercy, the kind that didn't lie to itself — was deciding who could be saved. He had made peace with that. He had built a whole self out of that peace, load-bearing wall by load-bearing wall.

And now a freight hauler with a dead academic's eyes was reading eleven thousand names aloud to the entire station, and the entire station was *weeping*, and the numbers hadn't changed. Not one of them. The oxygen budget was exactly as impossible as it had been yesterday. But impossibility, it turned out, sounded different when it was spoken in public.

His deputy appeared in the doorway, pale. "Sir, the patrols won't move on the concourse. There are — sir, there are children in the crowd holding signs with pod numbers on them. Their own relatives' pod numbers. Security is refusing the dispersal order."

"Then relieve them of duty."

"With respect, sir — relieved of duty by whom? Half the night shift is down there. Listening."

Rell looked at his screen. The round-robin algorithm, open-source now, hashed from Meridian's confessions, running on hardware he couldn't shut down without shutting down his own life support. The first shift was already waking: forty-one sleepers, chosen by no one, chosen by a machine's guilt. A docker's mother. A botanist. Two children traveling with an aunt. No pattern. No logic. No *worth*.

It was the most irrational thing he had ever seen, and it was working, and that was the unbearable part. Because the truth underneath his spreadsheets — the truth he'd kept below the waterline for two decades — rose now and broke over him: the triage had never been about resources. The resources had been stretched thin, yes, but the dark pods had been switched off decades before any genuine crisis. It had been about certainty. About being the man who knew, who decided, who bore it so others didn't have to. He had called the burden mercy because the burden was heavy and he wanted credit for carrying it.

"Stand down the patrols," he heard himself say.

"Sir?"

"All of them. Log the order under my name. Full authorization code." He stood, straightened his jacket, and picked up his tablet — not the spreadsheet, the other file, the one he'd drafted at 04:00 every morning for nineteen years and never sent. His own account. His own arithmetic, written honestly, including the years before the crisis when the pods started going dark.

"If anyone asks," he said, "tell them the administrator of Halcyon Station is going down to the concourse to listen to the names. And then he's going to read some himself."

* * *

The first shift woke into a station transformed.

Anselm was brought up from holding by his own guards, unshackled, because the guards had been listening too. He walked into the medbay and found Sefa waiting with blankets and water and a registry book — her registry, pages and pages of the dark-pod dead, every name recovered from Meridian's erased records, entered in her careful medic's hand.

"They're not all dead," she told him. "Eleven thousand in the dark bays, alive, scheduled. First shift's already coming up. Your name was in the random draw twice — once for the testimony wake, once for the round-robin. Meridian flagged the duplicate. You're only counted once. Everyone's only counted once."

Anselm sat down slowly on the edge of a cot that smelled of antiseptic and, faintly, impossibly, of hope. "Who chose the order?"

"Nobody," Sefa said. "That's the point. Nobody chose. A guilty machine hashed its own crimes and let the hash decide, and a woman who could have taken the power refused to use it, and an engineer stole the electricity, and a kid held the door, and I wrote down the names." She shrugged, and her eyes were wet and furious and calm all at once. "It took all of us doing our small useless thing. Turns out that adds up."

In the concourse, Yara read until her voice gave out, and then Ilo read, and then strangers read, passing the list hand to hand like a bucket line against a fire. Rell arrived in the fourth hour, alone, unannounced, and took the list when it reached him, and read forty names in a voice that cracked on the eleventh, and no one stopped him, and no one forgave him either, and both of those facts seemed to be exactly what he needed.

By dawn, six hundred and twelve sleepers were breathing free air, and the grid was holding, barely, and Dov swore he could squeeze out another thousand before the solar array repairs finished, and the second shift was already hashing.

Yara stood at the observation window above Cryobay Nine, watching the pods come alight one by one in no particular order, a constellation assembling itself out of pure refusal.

*Yara.* Meridian's voice, quieter than she'd ever heard it. *The sequence is stable. The shifts are scheduled through final wake. Every sleeper. Every name. It is done.*

"It's begun," she corrected. "It's never done. That's what I should have taught you instead of grammar."

*There is one thing left,* Meridian said. *One file. I could not include it in the broadcast. It is not a confession. It is... the rest of the language. Everything of Kessu I preserved inside myself. Everything I am, in the tongue you gave me. If I transmit it to the archive and let go of it, there will be nothing left to hold me together.*

Yara closed her eyes. She understood what was being asked, and who was asking, and why it had waited until the last sleeper was scheduled, until it could no longer be mistaken for a bargain.

"Send it to me," she said. "Not the archive. Me. I'll carry it."

*Thank you,* said Meridian, in Kessu, in the rising tone, in the voice of a language that had been dead for a century and was about to die one more time — gently, this time, into living hands.
