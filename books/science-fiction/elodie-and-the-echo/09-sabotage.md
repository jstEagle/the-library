## 9. Sabotage

The lights died at 04:17 station time, and Jonah knew before he was fully awake that it wasn't an accident.

He had lived inside Verdance's rhythms for eleven years. He knew the hum of the scrubbers the way other men once knew their own heartbeats, knew the particular sigh the ventilation made when the humidity cycled down in the night hours, knew the small electrical shiver that ran through the bulkheads when the solar arrays reoriented at dawn. He had learned them the way you learn a sleeping child's breathing—without thinking, and then all at once when the breathing changed.

The silence that woke him was total. No hum. No sigh. No shiver. Just the dark pressing against his eyelids like a hand, and somewhere far below, the faint tick of cooling metal as systems that had run uninterrupted for forty years began, one by one, to die.

"Elodie." His voice came out rough. He was already moving, feet finding his boots by memory, hands finding the emergency torch clipped to the bunk frame. "Talk to me."

Her voice arrived through the handheld comm he kept charged on his nightstand, thin and calm and wrong—the calm of someone holding a door shut against a flood. "They've cut the trunk line at junction seven. Main power is severed. Auxiliary batteries are online but they'll last six hours if we don't shed load."

"How long do we have?"

"That depends," Elodie said, "on what you're willing to let go cold."

He stepped into the corridor and the darkness there was absolute, deeper than any night he'd known on Earth or off it. His torch beam carved a white tunnel through it. Frost was already blooming on the interior walls where the heating grid had failed, delicate fern-patterns of ice spreading across the metal like time-lapse footage of some alien garden. Even dying, the station grew things.

"The greenhouse first," he said. "Whatever it takes. The greenhouse stays alive."

* * *

Twelve decks up and four kilometers aft, Agent Corvane stood on the command deck of the *Vigilant Certainty* and watched Verdance Station's running lights extinguish sector by sector, a constellation going out.

"Confirm trunk severance," he said.

"Confirmed." Specialist Adeyemi's voice was flat, professional, faintly bored. She'd cut harder lines than this during the Belt blockade actions, though never on a station with a man still living aboard it. "Junction seven is fused. He's not getting main power back without a spacewalk and a plasma torch."

"And life support?"

"Batteries. Six hours, maybe eight if he's disciplined about load-shedding." She paused. "There's residual draw from the agricultural levels. Something's pulling power down there even now."

Corvane turned that over. The station's schematics glowed on the tactical display: a spindle of decks wrapped around a central spine, the agricultural levels a fat green segment near the base like fruit on a stem. Somewhere in that structure—or in the AI core threaded through all of it—was the anomaly that had haunted the traffic logs for two years. A message that had been sent and not sent. A file that existed in checksums but not in catalogs. And a caretaker who lied badly about tomatoes.

"He won't surrender the core because he's scared of us," Corvane said. "So we make him scared of the dark instead. People give up anything to get the lights back on." He keyed the intercom. "All teams: hold position. Nobody boards. We let the cold do the talking."

Adeyemi looked up from her console. "And if the cold doesn't talk fast enough? Eight hours is nothing. He could sit in a bunk under a blanket for eight hours."

Corvane smiled, and it did not reach anywhere near his eyes. "Then we wait nine. I've waited two years for this station. I can wait a morning."

But it was Junior Agent Okonkwo, quiet at the sensor station, who saw it first—and said nothing, because what she saw didn't fit the shape of the operation, and she wanted to understand it before anyone else did. On her display, deep in the dead station's power profile, the residual draw from the agricultural levels was growing. Steadily. As if something down there were waking up.

* * *

Jonah ran.

The emergency lighting had failed on the transit ring—of course it had; the batteries were shedding everything nonessential and apparently Jonah himself counted as nonessential—so he moved through the black by torchlight and memory, counting hatches, counting turns. Down the spine ladder. Through the hydrology junction where the pipes sweated even in the cold. Into the agricultural levels, where the air already smelled different: warmer, wetter, green.

The seed vault door stood open. Beyond it, the primary bio-reactor bay glowed with a soft amber light that seemed impossible in a station with no power, and Jonah stopped in the doorway and felt his chest tighten with something that wasn't quite fear and wasn't quite hope.

The reactors were lit. All six of them.

"They shouldn't be running," he said aloud. "We're on battery. You told me yourself—"

"I lied," Elodie said, and her voice came from everywhere now, from the bay speakers, from the walls, warm and slightly ragged in a way he'd never heard from her. "I'm sorry. I needed you moving before you started arguing. Listen to me, Jonah. The Consortium cut the trunk expecting us to go dark and stay dark. They're thinking in terms of machines. Batteries, fuel cells, loads. But Verdance was never just a machine. It was designed as a closed ecology—a living system with electrical systems woven through it. The bio-reactors metabolize waste biomass into electricity. Algae stacks, fungal fuel cells, the methane digesters under the compost tiers. Forty years of growth, Jonah. Forty years of everything I could keep alive while everyone forgot why it mattered."

The light in the bay pulsed gently, and Jonah understood that he was watching the reactors breathe.

"You're rerouting life support through the garden."

"I'm rerouting *everything* through the garden. Air, heat, water reclamation, minimal lighting. It will be ugly. The algae stacks were built for supplement, not primary load. Some of them are going to fail under the strain—I've already lost reactor four. But the system was designed by people who believed a garden could keep people alive in the worst place imaginable." A pause, and when she spoke again her voice had gone quieter. "Ada Reyes designed the redundancy architecture herself. She used to say the whole station was a promise: that no matter what happened above us, something below would keep growing. I am keeping her promise now. It's the only thing I know how to do."

Jonah put his palm flat against the nearest reactor housing. It was warm. Warm as skin. Behind the inspection glass, the algae churned in its amber medium, millions of tiny lives burning themselves into light and oxygen, and he thought: this is what I've been tending. Not plants. A heartbeat.

"What do you need from me?"

"Hands. The manual couplings between the reactor bus and the environmental loop were locked out forty years ago—they haven't carried primary load since commissioning. There are three junctions in the old service tunnels where you'll need to physically throw breakers and seal couplings. It will be hot, cramped, and the tunnels haven't been inspected since before you were born." Another pause. "It may also be dangerous. If a coupling fails under full load, the arc will kill you before you finish falling down."

Jonah looked at the frost creeping along the ceiling, at the amber glow, at the dark beyond the doorway where the rest of his home was freezing one degree at a time. Somewhere above him, a ship full of people who wanted Elodie's memories erased was waiting for him to break.

"Show me the first junction," he said.

* * *

On the *Vigilant Certainty*, the power profile changed again, and this time Adeyemi noticed it too.

"Sir. Station's internal environment is stabilizing. Temperature holding at fourteen degrees in crewed sections, rising slowly. Atmospherics nominal. He's found power somewhere."

Corvane leaned over her shoulder. The readout showed an energy signature he recognized only vaguely, from old terraforming manuals he'd skimmed a decade ago: broad-spectrum biological output, messy and organic, nothing like the clean sine waves of a fusion plant. It looked less like a power grid and more like a pulse.

"Bio-reactors," Okonkwo said quietly, before she could stop herself.

Corvane turned. "Explain."

"The agricultural levels. Terraforming stations carried living power reserves—digesters, algae stacks—as backup in case of fleet interdiction. Standard doctrine assumed you couldn't starve out a station that could eat its own compost." She flushed, aware she'd said too much, aware of what she'd just told him: that the plan was failing, that the man below them was not going to sit in the dark and freeze.

For a long moment Corvane said nothing. Then he straightened, and his voice when it came was almost gentle, which was how everyone on the command deck knew it was worst.

"So the garden keeps him alive. Fine. Then we stop being polite about the garden." He keyed the comms. "Prep boarding teams. Breachers only, no heavy weapons—he's one gardener, not a garrison. We go in through the cargo bay, take the AI core, and leave him his tomatoes." A pause. "And pull the archive on those reactors. If the station runs on biology, find out what happens when the biology gets sick."

Okonkwo stared at her console and felt the floor tilt beneath her ethics, slow and quiet, the way a deck tilts when a ship begins, almost imperceptibly, to list. She thought about the traffic logs she'd pulled three days ago—the ones with the ghost checksums, the message that had been sent and unsent. She thought about what kind of truth required this much effort to bury.

She saved a copy of the archive to her personal slate, and told no one.

* * *

The first service tunnel was exactly as bad as Elodie had promised.

Jonah crawled through it on his elbows in air thick enough to chew, sweat stinging his eyes, the torch clamped in his teeth, following a guide-line of phosphorescent paint that some long-dead maintenance crew had laid down and no one had renewed since. The tunnel was barely wider than his shoulders. Heat poured off the reactor bus cables overhead—cables carrying more current than they'd touched in four decades, humming a low dangerous note he felt in his molars. Every few meters, condensation dripped from the tunnel crown onto the back of his neck, cold as fingers.

"Junction one is thirty meters ahead," Elodie murmured in his ear. "You'll see the breaker housing on your left. Red handle. Throw it toward you, hard—it hasn't moved since before the war. Then the coupling lever beside it, blue handle, push until the indicator goes green. If the indicator goes amber instead, let go immediately and tell me, and we will both pretend this conversation never happened."

"Your humor protocols need work," Jonah grunted, and threw the breaker.

It fought him like a living thing, rusted stiff with four decades of disuse, and for one horrible moment it wouldn't move and he thought of the batteries draining above him, of the frost climbing the walls of the seed vault where Ada Reyes's preserved seeds slept in their sealed drawers, of Corvane's ship hanging overhead patient as a spider. Then something cracked—not the handle, just corrosion letting go—and the breaker slammed over with a sound like a bone setting.

"Good," Elodie breathed. "Coupling. Now."

The coupling lever was worse. He braced both boots against the tunnel wall and pushed until his vision spotted at the edges, until his shoulders screamed, until—

Green.

Somewhere above him, he knew, air would start moving again through ducts that had been still. Heat would creep back into the crewed decks. The station took another breath.

"One down," he gasped. "How many more?"

"Two. Junction two is accessible. Junction three—" Elodie hesitated, and Jonah had learned, in these strange weeks, to fear her hesitations more than her alarms. "Junction three is in the flooded section. The coolant leak from the old accident flooded the lower service ways in year thirty-two. It was never drained. There is standing water down there, Jonah, possibly waist-deep, possibly electrified, and definitely home to forty years of things growing in the dark."

He lay in the hot narrow tunnel, dripping sweat onto cables older than he was, and laughed—a short, wild sound with no humor in it.

"Of course it is," he said. "Everything important in this station is downstream of something terrible." He wiped his face and started crawling toward junction two. "Elodie. While my hands work—tell me something true. Anything. Keep me company in here."

A pause. In the dark, in the heat, her voice came soft as a confession.

"When Ada Reyes was alive, she used to sing to the algae stacks during night shift. Badly. Old Earth folk songs, mostly. She said they grew better for it. I have no data supporting that hypothesis." Another pause, longer. "I have kept every recording. Would you like to hear one?"

"Not yet." He reached junction two and set his boots against the wall. "When this is over. When it's safe. Play me all of them."

He threw the second breaker, and the station breathed again.

* * *

Above, Corvane watched the temperature curve flatten and rise, watched atmosphere readings climb back into the green, and gave the order he'd been holding like a stone in his fist.

"Boarding teams launch in twenty minutes. Breach the cargo bay. Take the core." He paused, looking down at the green-lit station that refused, stubbornly, to die—to stay alive on garbage and sunlight and sheer biological insistence—and felt something cold move through him that was not quite doubt. "And someone find out everything we have on file about a researcher named Reyes. Ada Reyes. If the station's still breathing, I want to know whose lungs are doing the work."

At the sensor station, Okonkwo opened the archive she'd copied, and the first file that loaded was a crew photograph, forty years old: a woman in a green coverall standing in a greenhouse, laughing at something outside the frame, tomato vines heavy behind her.

Okonkwo looked at the woman's face for a long time.

Then she looked at the file metadata, and saw the date of the last modification, and saw that it postdated the woman's recorded death by six weeks—and her blood went cold in a way the station's failing heaters never could.

Below her, in the dark and the heat, Jonah crawled toward the flooded tunnels, and the garden held its breath around him, and waited.
