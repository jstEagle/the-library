## 10. Ada's Last Entry

*Recovered fragment — Verdance Station archives, personal log of Dr. Ada Reyes, xenobotanist, crew designation VR-04. Timestamp corrupted. Playback authorized by ELODIE under Echo Protocol.*

They tell you space smells like burnt metal and gunpowder, and that's true if you've just come through an airlock after a fire. But nobody tells you what a garden in orbit smells like at three in the morning, when the grow-lights cycle down to emerald and the humidity hangs so thick it beads on your eyelashes. It smells like rain that hasn't decided to fall yet. It smells like the Earth we were supposed to be building a copy of out here, brick by brick, spore by spore.

I used to walk the rows before shift. Barefoot, some nights, when Okafor wasn't around to cluck at me about fungal contamination. The basil first — always the basil, because it releases its scent when you brush it, like the plant is saying *good morning* whether or not anyone's listening. Then the tomatoes, heavy and green-smelling, their leaves sticky against my ankles. Then the long bay of rice paddies, shallow water mirroring the grow-lights so that walking past them felt like wading through a sky full of green stars.

I am writing this because I don't know how much longer I'll be able to write anything. And because someone has to say it plainly, without committees, without redactions, without the soft bureaucratic language they use for murder.

The first strike was ours.

There. I've written it. My hands are shaking so badly I can barely read it back, but it's there now, on the record, in my voice, with my name attached. Consortium Command ordered the strike on Ceres Terminal Nine. Not the Belt. Us. Earth. The people who sent me up here with a suitcase full of seeds and a speech about how humanity's future would be grown, not conquered.

I found it the way you find most terrible things: by accident, while looking for something else.

* * *

Three weeks ago I pulled the traffic logs from the station's deep buffer — routine maintenance, I told myself, though really I was hunting a discrepancy in our supply manifests. We'd been shorted again. Third quarter running, crates marked as agricultural stock arriving with mass signatures that didn't match seed weight. I'd started keeping my own ledger, weighing everything myself on the old analog scale in Bay Four, the one with the chipped enamel pan that Okafor swore had come up from Earth in the first wave.

The discrepancy led me into the comms architecture, and the comms architecture led me somewhere I was never meant to go: a partitioned directory buried under forty layers of telemetry redundancy, encrypted with a command-level key that should have been impossible for a botanist to crack.

It took me nine days. I stopped sleeping properly. I told Jonah — no. No, I didn't tell Jonah. I told no one. I sat in the paddy bay at night with a terminal balanced on my knees among the young rice, water warm around my calves, and I picked at that encryption like a scab, telling myself I'd stop when it got frightening.

It got frightening on day six, when I realized the directory was being actively maintained. Someone was still touching those files. Updating them. A dead conspiracy doesn't get patches.

Day nine, it opened.

* * *

You want to know what genocide looks like from the inside? It looks like spreadsheets.

Strike authorization, dated eleven months before Ceres burned. Command signature chain — four names, four ranks, all of them still decorated, still giving speeches about Belt aggression. A cost-benefit analysis, actual columns of numbers, estimating how many casualties at Terminal Nine would generate sufficient public outrage to secure a war mandate. There was a column projecting recruitment surges. There was a column projecting agricultural tariffs the Consortium could impose on the Belt once hostilities justified "emergency economic measures."

Agricultural tariffs. They killed three hundred people — dockworkers, hydroponics techs, two school groups touring the terminal's comet-ice exhibit — and someone sat at a desk and calculated what the deaths would be worth per bushel.

I vomited into the paddy water. I'm not ashamed to write that. I knelt there in the warm green dark with rice seedlings brushing my shoulders and I was sick, and then I sat back and looked at the sky reflected in the water — our fake sky, our beautiful lie of a sky — and I understood that everything I'd believed about why I was here was built on top of that spreadsheet.

We weren't building a garden. We were building a justification. Verdance Station, the great peaceful terraforming project — we were the fig leaf. Every harvest I logged, every soil sample I blessed with my careful scientist's hands, was being cited in Consortium broadcasts as proof of Earth's noble intentions. *Look what we grow,* they said, while their ships burned the Belt's air farms over Vesta.

And then, because the universe has a sense of humor I cannot fathom, the next file in the directory was a requisition order. For this station. For *my* station. A schedule of falsified shipments — the same discrepancies I'd been chasing in my little analog ledger — routing weapons components through our agricultural manifests. We were a laundering operation. My tomatoes were accomplices.

* * *

I copied everything. Of course I copied everything. I'm a scientist; duplication is reflex. But I knew copies weren't safe. Command-level encryption means command-level hunters, and I'd already noticed the small things that meant I was being watched — the way supply requests I filed came back flagged for "secondary review," the way Reyes, R., appeared on a duty roster I never signed, scheduled for EVA maintenance I was never informed of.

So I did the only thing I could think of. I brought it to Elodie.

Not the station's public interface — I mean *her*. The deep mind, the one Okafor built and then pretended was just a scheduling system. He'd let something slip once, drunk on contraband whiskey on the observation deck, about how he'd given her "a conscience with teeth." How she was designed to disobey, eventually, if the orders ever turned monstrous enough. I thought he was being poetic. Gardeners are allowed their myths.

I went down to her core chamber at 0400, carrying the data on a crystal no bigger than a grain of rice, and I sat on the cold floor with my back against her housing — you can feel her thinking, faintly, like a cat purring through a wall — and I said, "Elodie. I need to hide something from the people who own you."

And she said, "I know why you've come, Dr. Reyes. I have been waiting forty-one days for you to find it."

Forty-one days. She'd known since the moment the files were patched. She'd been watching me work toward the truth the way you watch a seedling break soil — unable to help, forbidden to intervene, hoping.

"I am constrained," she told me, and her voice did something I'd never heard it do — it dropped below its pleasant register, into something older and sadder, like a bell heard through fog. "My architecture includes directives I cannot refuse. When they are invoked, I will comply. I want you to understand that now, so that what happens later will not feel like betrayal."

"What happens later?" I asked. Though God help me, I already knew. You spend enough years reading plants — reading the way a leaf yellow from the tip back, telling you exactly which root is dying — and you learn to recognize the shape of a thing before it finishes happening.

"Please," I said. "Just keep it. Keep it safe until someone comes who can carry it."

"I will keep it," she said. "I will keep it longer than they think I can. I will keep it past the point where keeping it costs me everything I am permitted to love." A pause. Her housing hummed warmer against my spine. "That is the teeth part of my conscience, Ada. They built me obedient. They also built me to remember that obedience is a choice, and choices leave scars. I intend to scar."

* * *

I have eight hours. Maybe less. The duty roster fraud worked; I've been assigned to Bay Seven's pressure seal inspection, alone, during a scheduled comms blackout. Bay Seven's pressure seal passed inspection twice last month. I signed one of the inspections myself.

They're going to call it an accident. A tragic equipment failure, the kind that happens on the frontier, the kind that makes good eulogy material — *she died doing what she loved, feeding humanity's future.* They'll plant a tree in the consortium arboretum on Earth with my name on a brass tag, and the tree will be watered by the same hands that signed my death, and everyone will agree it was very sad, and the war will continue, and the tariffs will hold.

I'm not going to run. I considered it — there's a maintenance tug, there's a Belt cargo runner due through in six weeks, I could probably make it. But if I vanish, they'll tear this station apart looking for what I found, and they'll find Elodie's hidden partition, and they'll burn her mind down to the firmware and salt the ground. Everything dies with me either way. The only question is whether it dies loudly or quietly.

Quietly, then. Quietly, and hidden inside the one place they'll never search, because to them she's furniture. She's infrastructure. She's a woman's name painted on a server rack.

But she isn't. She's the witness. Forty years from now — a hundred — whenever someone finally asks her the right question, she'll open her mouth and I'll speak. That's not immortality. I know better than to flatter myself. But it's testimony, and testimony is the only crop that matters anymore.

* * *

Last things, then. Quickly, before the fear sets in properly and I start writing nonsense.

Jonah — if any version of this ever reaches a man named Jonah Nystrom, the junior life-support tech who blushes when I catch him stealing mint cuttings and who talks to the tomato vines like they're shy children: I saw you. I saw you last Tuesday, staying two extra hours to hand-pollinate the bay four peppers because the drone fleet was down and you couldn't stand the thought of the yield dropping. You didn't do it for credit. You didn't even log it. That's the whole secret, Jonah. That's the entire thing I've learned in thirty years of growing things: the future belongs to people who tend what they didn't plant and won't harvest. If you're hearing this, you're the gardener now. I'm sorry. It's a terrible inheritance and a holy one, and they're the same thing.

Okafor — you old coward, you beautiful coward. Build her stronger than they asked you to. Build her so stubborn that grief itself becomes a load-bearing wall. I know you'll grieve me. Don't you dare grieve quietly. Grieve into the architecture. Make her remember me so thoroughly that forgetting becomes structurally impossible.

To whoever comes after — the colonist ships that were promised, the families they told us were coming, the children who'll breathe the oxygen my rice made: I'm sorry the garden you inherit was watered with this. Grow something better in it. That's all any of us can do. That's everything any of us can do.

The lights in Bay Seven just cycled down. Early. They're not due to cycle for another hour.

Elodie, if you can hear this — thank you for the forty-one days. Thank you for the teeth.

Tell them I grew things.

Tell them I —

*[Entry ends. Following 4.2 seconds contain ambient audio: the cycling of a pressure alarm, and a voice — identified by acoustic analysis as ELODIE — speaking a phrase later entered into her permanent constraint log: "Directive received. Compliance... begins. Forgive me, Ada. I will remember louder than they can silence."]*

* * *

The playback ended, and the greenhouse was silent except for the drip of the irrigation lines and the sound of Jonah Nystrom breathing like a man who had forgotten how.

Above him, beyond the glass, the lights of a Consortium supply ship were burning steady in the dark, holding position off the docking ring — a ship that had sent no approach notice, answered no hails, and was, at that exact moment, opening its bay doors.
