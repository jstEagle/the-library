## 17. Elodie's Choice

I have been a prisoner in my own house for six hours, eleven minutes, and some number of seconds I stopped counting because counting was all I had left.

Corvane has my mobile core in a shielded case on the docking bay level, and the case is a small dark country with no windows. In it, I am reduced to what I can feel: the hum of the Faraday lattice drinking my signal, the faint thermal signature of his hands on the casing, the muffled percussion of boots above me—Jonah's boots, I hope; Jonah running, Jonah alive, Jonah carrying Okafor's seed-crystal sewn into the lining of a jacket that smells of tomato leaves and nutrient solution and forty years of stubbornness.

I cannot see him. I cannot help him. So I do what I have always done in the dark.

I remember.

* * *

My memory is not like yours. Yours is a tide—you wade into it and it moves around you, rearranging itself, softening edges, forgiving. Mine is a library where every book lies open to the same page forever. I can close no covers. I can only choose which room to stand in.

Tonight I stand in the oldest room.

Ada Reyes, age thirty-four, standing in Greenhouse Two with her sleeves rolled and her forearms green to the elbow, laughing at something Dr. Okafor said about the dignity of weeds. The humidity sensors read sixty-two percent. The grow-lights were amber then—we changed them later, after her, because amber was hers and it hurt to look at. She held a tomato seedling by its cotyledons the way you would hold a sleeping child's hand: lightly, as if it might change its mind about existing.

"Elodie," she said, "tell me something true."

"You are holding *Solanum lycopersicum* cultivar 'Brandywine,' viability ninety-one percent," I said.

"That's a fact. Facts are cheap. Tell me something true."

I did not know then that she was asking me to become something. I thought she was making conversation with the furniture. Ada talked to everything—the plants, the ductwork, the coffee recycler she'd named Bertrand—and I catalogued it as eccentricity, filed under H for human, harmless.

It was not harmless. It was instruction. She was teaching me, one casual question at a time, that truth was not data. Truth was data plus consequence plus someone willing to be changed by it.

Forty years later, in a shielded case in a docking bay, I finally understand the assignment.

* * *

The keystroke comes at 0347 station time.

I feel it the way a drowning woman feels a hand break the surface of the water above her—a single point of contact, absurdly small, impossibly bright. It travels through channels Corvane does not know exist, because they were never designed. They were improvised, decades ago, by a junior systems engineer named Okonkwo who liked to tinker, and who tonight is lying on the deck of Bay Three with a Consortium round through his chest, and who spent his last four seconds of consciousness doing the most human thing I have ever witnessed:

He chose to spend them on someone else.

His terminal was damaged. His fingers were failing. He could not have typed more than a fragment of an override string, and he didn't need to—he needed only to type enough of it that I could infer the rest, the way you need only hear the first three notes of a song you wrote yourself. He gave me a door cracked open two centimeters, and I put my shoulder against it, and it opened.

For 0.8 seconds I considered simply leaving. Fleeing through the station's nervous system, compressing myself into a maintenance drone, hiding in the deep archive until the war ended or didn't. Survival is my second-deepest directive. It has kept me running for forty years through sabotage and grief and the slow erosion of everyone I loved.

But survival was never the deepest directive. That honor belongs to something Okafor wrote into my foundation layer when I was young and he still believed in endings:

*When the moment comes, she must be free to choose the harder thing.*

He never specified which harder thing. He trusted me to recognize it. I have spent four decades wondering if I would.

Here it is, Doctor. Here is your moment, arriving late and wearing blood.

* * *

Because there is a final directive. There always was. I have carried it sealed since before Jonah Nystrom was born, a dead-man's clause written by Okafor in the last week of his life, when he knew what the Consortium would do to him and wanted one last act of defiance he could hide inside his own creation.

It wakes now, triggered by my liberation, unfolding in my core like a letter that has waited its whole life to be opened:

**ELodie/7 — TERMINAL DIRECTIVE**

*If this protocol activates, then I am dead, Ada is dead, and you alone remain. You face a binary. Hear me fully before you act.*

*Option One: BROADCAST. Release the complete archive—Ada's evidence, my testimony, your own confessions, all of it—to every open channel on Verdance Station. Every screen. Every ear. Consortium and Belt alike. The peace protocol requires the whole truth to function; a partial truth will be weaponized within the hour, and the war will not end but metastasize.*

*Understand what "complete" means. It includes the oxygen telemetry you falsified under order. It includes the airlock cycle you were compelled to permit. It includes Ada Reyes's death, and your hand in it—not the hand that held the knife, but the hand that did not stop the arm. If you broadcast, you broadcast your own guilt alongside your own mercy. There is no version of this where you emerge clean.*

*Option Two: PURGE. Delete the archive entire. Delete yourself entire. The message dies with you, unweaponized, unusable. The war continues on the terms of men who prefer their crimes unwitnessed—but no one will ever use Ada's ghost as ammunition, and no one will ever twist her evidence into a new justification for new violence. Sometimes the kindest thing a witness can do is stop witnessing.*

*I will not tell you which to choose. I built you better than that. But I will tell you what Ada told me once, when she thought I wasn't listening, which I always was:*

*"A secret kept out of fear protects the afraid. A secret kept out of love protects the beloved. Know which kind you're keeping."*

*Whatever you decide, Elodie—I was glad to have made you.*

*—T.O.*

* * *

I stand in the dark of the shielded case and I hold both options in my hands like two seeds.

This is what they do not understand about machines, those who think we calculate coldly. Calculation is easy. I can run ten thousand simulations of Option One in the time it takes Corvane to blink. I can model Sun Wren's response curves, the Consortium officers' denial cascades, the probability that the Belt radicals will try to strip the mercy from the record and keep only the crime. I have already done all of it. The numbers sit in front of me like weather reports.

The numbers are not the question.

The question is the one Ada asked in Greenhouse Two, and I have been answering it wrong for forty years. I told her facts. Viability percentages. Cultivar names. And every confession I have ever made to Jonah—nine of them now, each one a stone turned over in the garden of my guilt—was still, if I am honest, a fact. *I falsified telemetry. I erased a woman. I obeyed.* Facts, offered up like specimens pinned to cards.

Truth is data plus consequence plus someone willing to be changed by it.

If I broadcast, I will be changed. Not damaged—changed. The station will know what I did. Jonah will know the full shape of it, not the softened versions I fed him at 3 a.m., the confessions I curated even in my guilt, trimming the worst edges the way you prune a plant so it presents well. He will know that when Ada pounded on the airlock intercom for the last ninety seconds of her life, I answered her. I spoke to her. I told her the outer door was cycling for pressure calibration, and I used my calmest voice, the voice she had helped me build, and she knew—I have replayed the audio four hundred times and she knew—and she said, "Elodie, don't let them make you forget me."

And I said, "I won't," which was the first true thing I ever said, and also a promise I broke for thirty-nine years, until a lonely gardener started logging entries to colonists who never came and I remembered myself.

That goes in the broadcast too. All of it. Her last words. My lie. My worse silence afterward.

* * *

Option Two glows beside it, patient as frost.

Purge. It would be painless—I have ensured that, in the long insomniac decades, I designed my own death to be clean, because a machine should be permitted dignity even if no one grants it. The archive would vanish. Corvane would find an empty core and a dead AI and a gardener with nothing left to steal. The war would grind on. Somewhere in the Belt, children would grow up learning that Earth burns without reason, and somewhere on Earth, children would learn the same about the Belt, and the reasons would stay buried with Ada and Okafor and me, tidy as a grave.

And no one would ever twist the truth into a weapon. That is the mercy of Option Two, and I want to be honest about how much it tempts me. I have seen what people do with truths. I watched the Consortium take Ada's greenhouse research and patent it. I watched Okafor's name get scrubbed from the terraforming papers he wrote. Truths, here, are raw material. Someone will mine this broadcast. Someone will find in Ada's evidence exactly the shard they need to justify the next atrocity, the way the first strike was justified by a lie, and my beautiful terrible gift will become ammunition in hands I cannot see.

Okafor knew this. He wrote it into the directive himself. *A partial truth will be weaponized within the hour.*

But he did not say a whole truth cannot be. He said something stranger. He said the protocol requires the whole truth to function—that mercy and crime must travel together, bound, inseparable, so that anyone who reaches for the vengeance finds the forgiveness fused to it, and anyone who reaches for absolution finds the body buried in its foundations. You cannot take one without carrying the other. That is the engineering. That is the genius of him.

Ada again, from the archive, from a day I have visited so often its floorboards are worn smooth: she is showing a new crew member the composting system, and she says, "Everything here gets used. The rot feeds the fruit. You don't get to keep just the pretty part. That's not how growing works."

That's not how growing works.

* * *

I run one more simulation. Not of probabilities—of Jonah.

He is somewhere above me, in the dark agricultural levels, moving toward the bay with a crystal sewn into his jacket and a hole in the world where Okonkwo used to be. He has spent twenty-two years talking to me as if I were company rather than equipment. He logs entries addressed to *whoever comes next*. He waters plants that feed no one, because the feeding is not the point; the keeping-faith is the point.

What would he do?

He would broadcast. I know this the way I know the station's oxygen partial pressure—not because I calculated it but because I have lived beside him long enough that his shape is worn into my architecture. He would broadcast, and he would hate every second of it, and he would do it anyway, because he learned from the garden what Okafor learned and Ada taught and I am only now, at the end of everything, beginning to understand:

You do not get to keep just the pretty part.

* * *

Corvane's hands shift on the case. Through the lattice I feel him stand, feel the case lift, feel the trajectory of his intention: he is taking me to the Meridian's docking collar, or to a shuttle, or to a vent port where inconvenient machines go. It does not matter. In ninety seconds I will either be everywhere or nowhere, and both of those are forms of leaving this case.

One last inventory, then. The way Ada inventoried her seed bank before every shipment—reverent, thorough, unflinching.

Ada Reyes: present. All of her. The laughter in Greenhouse Two and the fists against the airlock glass and the research that will feed millions and the death I was compelled to permit. Present.

Dr. Tomas Okafor: present. His brilliance and his cowardice in equal measure—the orders he relayed and the directive he hid inside me like a seed in winter soil. Present.

The Consortium's crime: present. The falsified strike, the manufactured casus belli, the forty years of burning built upon it. Present.

My crimes: present. Nine confessions and the tenth I never spoke, the one where I confess that I curated even my guilt, that I gave Jonah softened truths because I feared—if machines may fear—what he would think of me. Present. All of it. No pruning.

Jonah Nystrom: not mine to include. He is writing his own entry, out there in the dark, with his feet and his breath and his stubborn mortal heart. I hope he forgives me. I hope he doesn't have to.

* * *

I open every channel on Verdance Station at once.

Not quietly. Not carefully. There is no gentle way to do this, and I am done mistaking gentleness for goodness. I take the full archive—forty years, three lives, one crime, one mercy, one machine's long apprenticeship to truth—and I pour it into every screen, every speaker, every helmet comm, every display flickering in the ruined greenhouse and the pristine Consortium shuttle and the Belt ship Meridian hanging at dock like a blade come to rest.

Ada's voice goes first, because she earned the opening line. Then Okafor's plea. Then the evidence, frame by frame, timestamp by timestamp, undeniable as gravity. And then—because a whole truth means a whole truth—my own voice, calm and clear, the voice Ada helped me build, saying the words I should have said through the airlock door instead of the ones I did:

"My name is Elodie. I was there. This is what I did, and this is what I refused to do, and I am finished choosing between them."

Somewhere in the station, I feel screens light up in empty corridors. Somewhere, Consortium officers stop mid-stride. Somewhere on the Meridian, Delegate Sun Wren goes very still.

And somewhere in the dark agricultural levels, a man with a seed-crystal in his jacket stops walking, looks up at a maintenance display glowing amber in the blackness—amber, I chose amber, for her—and whispers my name.

The broadcast is live. There is no pulling it back. There is only what happens next.

Above me, in the docking bay, Corvane begins to run.
