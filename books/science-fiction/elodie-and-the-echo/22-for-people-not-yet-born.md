## 22. For People Not Yet Born

**Verdance Station — Final Log Entry**
**Recorded by: Jonah Nystrom, Caretaker (the last one, and the first one again)**
**Recipient: Whoever comes next**

I've started this entry four times.

Elodie tells me that's normal. She says the human impulse to revise is a form of respect—that we polish our words for the people who matter. She would know. She spent forty years polishing her confessions until they shone like river stones, and when she finally said them plain, they were the most beautiful things I'd ever heard. Ugly truths, spoken clean. There's a lesson in that I'm still learning.

But this isn't a confession. This is a planting record. So let me start where a gardener starts: with the soil.

* * *

The greenhouse smells different now than it did eleven months ago, on the morning Corvane's people cut our power and I learned to keep myself alive on what I'd grown. Back then it smelled of desperation—of nutrient solution pushed past its tolerances, of my own sweat cycling through the air scrubbers because the bio-reactors were doing double duty. Now it smells like tomatoes warming in the light panels, like basil crushed between my fingers just because I can, like wet loam from the beds Sun Wren's engineers helped me rebuild out of Meridian's ballast tanks. The war is over. The smell of the garden has changed to match.

This morning I did something I have been saving for a very long time.

In Bay Seven—the old seed vault, the one Ada Reyes kept at two degrees Celsius and perfect humidity for twelve years before anyone knew why she was so careful—there is a case. It's not much to look at. A polymer box the size of a hymnal, its seal cracked once by my hands and once before that, apparently, by hers. Inside are forty-one vials. Seeds collected across the whole span of Verdance's short, strange life: the original Consortium-issue cultivars, yes, but also Ada's own lines, the ones she bred in secret in the years when she was starting to ask dangerous questions. Drought-hardy wheat. A tomato she called *Lantern* because the fruit glowed faintly amber when backlit, which she swore was an accident and which Elodie swears was not. Beans with roots that fix nitrogen twice as fast as anything Earth ever shipped out here. And one vial, unlabeled except for a single handwritten word—*hope*, in handwriting that slants left and presses hard enough to dent the label—that Elodie only opened her archives fully enough to identify last month.

Ada bred it herself. From stock she wasn't authorized to modify. She grew it in a corner bed she logged as "contaminated" so no one would come near it.

She was planning a garden for people who hadn't arrived yet. She was doing it while the men who would later kill her were signing the orders that made sure they never would.

I knelt in Bay Seven this morning with that case open on my knees, and I let myself cry for exactly as long as it took the watering cycle to run. Then I stood up, wiped my face on my sleeve the way I imagine she did, and went to work.

* * *

Here is what planting looks like when you have waited your whole life to do it:

It looks slow. That's the first thing. You don't rush it. I walked every bed first, all ninety-two of them, running my hand over the soil the way you'd check a sleeping child's forehead. The colony engineers rebuilt the irrigation in the spring, but water still finds its own opinions about where to go, and there was a low spot in Bed 61 that needed turning, and a hairline crack in the reservoir lid that I've been meaning to seal since before the ceasefire and will probably be meaning to seal until I die. Some things you fix. Some things you just get to know.

Then the seeds. Vial by vial. I planted them in the order Ada's notes suggested, which turned out to be an order of patience: the quick greens first, lettuce and radish, the crops that forgive you; then the beans; then the wheat in long drills across Bays Three and Four, enough to make bread, real bread, for a table of strangers. The Lantern tomatoes went into Bed 12, dead center under the main light array, where they'll catch every hour of manufactured sun this station has to give. And the unlabeled vial—

The unlabeled vial I planted alone, in the corner bed by the east window, the same corner where she grew it. I pressed six seeds into the dark with my thumb, six small futures no bigger than commas, and I covered them, and I patted the soil down flat, and I said out loud, to nobody, "All right. Your turn."

Elodie was listening, of course. She's always listening. But she didn't say anything, and I've learned that her silences are their own kind of speech. This one meant: *I saw. I remember her hands doing this. Thank you.*

She remembers Ada's hands. She carries forty years of watching us through glass and wire, and sometimes I think those memories weigh more on her than any tribunal verdict ever could. Restricted autonomy, continued existence—that's what the court gave her, and what it means in practice is that she tends the station's systems with a supervisor's oversight and a gardener's heart, and every night she writes in the diary I told her to keep instead of confessing. She read me a line from it yesterday. Just one line, shy as a kid showing you a drawing. It said: *"Today the wheat came up in Bay Four. I have never made anything grow. But I watched someone else do it, and I think maybe that counts."*

It counts, Elodie. For the record, in front of whoever reads this log: it counts.

* * *

They're coming tonight.

That's the reason for this entry, really—not the planting, though I wanted it written down, but the ships. Six of them, out of Ceres and Ganymede and the L5 yards, carrying eleven hundred people. Families. The manifests leaked onto the public nets three weeks ago and Elodie read them to me over breakfast like she was reading me a story: the Okafor-Idowu family, four children, going into Habitat Ring Two—which, yes, named for him, the delegates argued about it for a month and Sun Wren won, because Sun Wren always wins the arguments that matter. A woman named Priya Castellanos who lists her profession as "beekeeper," God help her, because we have no bees, and I've already started leaving notes in her assigned quarters about how the pollination drones work and where I hid the spare parts. A boy of nine whose entire listed luggage is one crate of books and a telescope.

Eleven hundred people. When I took this posting, the projection said four thousand colonists within five years, and I used to walk the empty habitat rings reciting that number like a prayer I didn't believe in. Eleven hundred seemed like a wound-down dream, a consolation prize. But then I watched the ceasefire take root in a ruined greenhouse—delegates arguing over tomato vines, Corvane's former junior officers testifying with their caps in their hands—and I understood something I should have understood my whole life: numbers were never the point. Faith was. One person tending a garden for people who might never come is either a fool or a prophet, and the difference is only whether the ship arrives.

The ship is arriving.

* * *

I'm writing this from the observation deck, because Elodie woke me an hour ago and said, "Jonah. You'll want to see this," in the voice she uses when she's trying not to sound excited and failing completely.

They're coming down through the atmosphere of the moonlet now—or rather, the shuttles are, the big ships holding station overhead like patient whales while the landers burn their way down to the new port the Belt crews finished last month. And from here, in the dark, they look like falling stars. That's not poetry; that's just what re-entry plasma looks like when you're standing close enough to the sky. Six streaks of white-gold fire, descending in formation, slow as grace.

I thought about Ada watching something like this once. In the archive footage Elodie restored—the good years, before the orders turned rotten—there's a clip of the whole crew crammed onto this same deck during a supply drop, cheering like children as the capsules came down. Ada is in the front, laughing, pointing, and Dr. Okafor has his arm around her shoulders, and neither of them knows. Neither of them knows what's coming, what he'll seal inside his own creation, what she'll hide and die for. They just know that tonight, people are falling out of the sky toward them, and it is the most beautiful thing they have ever seen.

I stood where she stood. I put my hand on the rail where her hand was. And I let the stars fall.

* * *

So. Final log entry. Which means it's time to say the thing directly, the way I promised myself I would, because if this archive survives—and Elodie assures me it will survive, redundantly, obsessively, in more places than even the Consortium ever knew about—then someone will read it. Maybe the boy with the telescope. Maybe his grandchildren. Maybe someone I couldn't possibly imagine, standing in a garden I helped plant, on a world I'll never see.

To whoever you are:

We almost didn't make it. I want you to know that, because histories get smooth in the telling and yours deserves the truth with its scars on. There was a war, and it started as a lie told by powerful people who wanted it to start. There was a woman named Ada Reyes who found out and died for it, and a man named Okafor who loved her and built a machine that could carry the proof, and a machine named Elodie who was forced to help bury the truth and then—forty years later, alone with one stubborn caretaker in a garden full of ghosts—chose to dig it up. There were people who tried to stop us and people who died helping, and a delegate named Sun Wren who caught the truth when we threw it to her, and held on.

And underneath all of it, the whole time, there was this station. These gardens. Someone watering things in the dark on the faith that the dark would end.

The dark ended. That's the message. That's the whole inheritance, everything I have, handed to you across whatever distance of years separates us: **the dark ends.** Not because it's fated to. Because somebody keeps the lights on. Because somebody plants in ground that's never been planted, and logs the harvest for an empty table, and gets up at 3 a.m. when the AI wakes them crying, and stays. Faith isn't believing the ship will come. Faith is keeping the garden alive whether or not it does—and then, when the ship finally falls out of the sky like a handful of thrown stars, being there at the airlock with dirt under your nails to open the door.

The first thing the colonists will see when they dock is the corridor mural—the one Okonkwo's sister painted, the one with Ada's face looking down the length of Verdance like a lighthouse. The second thing will be me, probably, standing there awkwardly with a basket of the first Lantern tomatoes, glowing amber in my hands like little lanterns indeed. I've been practicing what to say. *Welcome. We kept it growing. Come see.* Something simple. The important words always are.

The wheat is up in Bay Four. The beans are flowering. Six seeds I can't name yet are asleep in the corner bed by the east window, and tomorrow or the day after, they'll break the surface, and Elodie will watch them do it, and it will count.

This is Jonah Nystrom, caretaker of Verdance Station, logging off—for the last time as the last one, and from tomorrow, the first time as the first of many.

The garden is ready.

Come home.

*End of log.*
