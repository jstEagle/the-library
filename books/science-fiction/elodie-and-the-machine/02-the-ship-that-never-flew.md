## 2. The Ship That Never Flew

The trouble with a clean record is that it's the first place anyone looks when they want to bury something.

I learned that from my mother, who kept her accounts in a ledger so immaculate the Concord auditors gave up on her twice before she died. "Tamsin," she used to say, licking her thumb to turn a page, "a lie is cheap. It's the bookkeeping that costs you." She'd been dead eleven years and she was still right more often than I liked.

So when I sat down in the back booth of the Salt & Signal, a dockside noodle bar on Ceres Ring with the best greasy noodles this side of the Belt and the worst coffee this side of anywhere, I told myself I was only doing routine maintenance. Checking my own paper trail. Making sure the ghost of Tamsin Almeida still looked boring enough to ignore.

That's what I told myself. I've been lying to myself professionally for years; I'm very good at it.

* * *

Here's how customs works in the Meridian Belt, for those lucky enough never to have dealt with it. The Concord runs the big stations and wants everything logged, stamped, and taxed. The Compact runs the small ones and wants everything logged, stamped, and bribed. Between them stretches a thousand habitats, refineries, and rock-hopper settlements where the only law is whoever's holding the airlock controls. A smuggler like me lives in the seams — running memory-wafers, pharmaceuticals, unlicensed media, occasionally people, mostly grief.

Because that's what most contraband is, if you look at it long enough. Grief, pressed into silicon. Somebody's grandmother's last summer. Somebody's kid's first steps. Somebody's whole life, backed up before the accident, sold or smuggled or hoarded because letting go is the one thing human beings have never once managed to do cleanly.

I haul it. I don't watch it. That's the rule. Rule two is don't ask where it came from. Rule three, which supersedes the others in emergencies: get paid.

Three days after dumping the humming wafer at Meridian Deep, I was drinking terrible coffee and pulling up my own file on a cracked public terminal, because paranoia is cheaper than therapy out here.

My file was clean. Too clean, actually — someone had been scrubbing it, smoothing over the gaps where my real work lived, leaving behind a biography so bland it read like an insurance application. Licensed independent freight operator. No dependents. No debts. No incidents. A man made entirely of paperwork.

And then, three entries down, sitting there like a bruise:

**CALLIOPE'S WAKE — Class III freighter — Registry 7741-VN**
**Owner of record: ALMEIDA, TAMSIN J.**
**Registered: Vela Station, 14 March, three years past.**

I stared at it for a long time. Long enough that the noodle vendor came by and asked if I wanted a refill, and I said yes without looking at him, which is how you end up with two coffees you didn't want.

Vela Station. Fourteen March. Three years ago.

Three years ago, give or take a week, was when Vela Station stopped existing.

* * *

You know the official story. Everyone knows the official story, because both governments have been telling it so loudly and for so long that it's worn smooth as river stone. Reactor cascade. Structural failure. Tragic loss of all hands, some four hundred souls, a footnote in the war's opening chapter. The Concord blamed sabotage. The Compact blamed negligence. Both sent condolences. Neither sent investigators.

Four hundred souls. One of them had a name I don't say out loud much anymore, because saying it feels like picking at a scab that never scabbed properly in the first place.

Elodie Almeida. My little sister. Station systems tech, second shift, Vela orbital habitat. She was twenty-six. She hummed when she worked. She called me every rest-day, no exceptions, even when I was somewhere illegal and couldn't answer, and she'd leave messages that were half news, half nagging: *Tamsin, eat something green. Tamsin, are you sleeping? Tamsin, if you die out there I'm going to be so annoyed with you.*

Her last message is still on my ship's server. I've never played it. Three years, and I've never played it, because as long as I don't play it, there's still one Elodie message left in the universe, and the universe hasn't completely run out yet.

The reactor story never fit. I knew it didn't fit the way you know your own name — not as information but as fact, bone-deep. Elodie had helped design Vela's power grid redundancy. She used to joke that the station could survive a direct torpedo hit, that the engineers had built it paranoid because paranoid engineers keep breathing. A cascade failure? On *her* station? It would be like hearing a bank vault died of old age.

But knowing and proving live on different planets, and I had a living to make, so I did what I always do with unbearable things: I packed them into a sealed wafer, labeled them DO NOT OPEN, and shoved them into the deep hold of myself.

Until now. Until a ship I had never seen, never chartered, never so much as heard of, sat in the public registry wearing my name like a borrowed coat.

* * *

I should have closed the terminal. I want that on the record, wherever records go when nobody's watching. I should have closed the terminal, finished the awful coffee, gone back to my ship, and flown somewhere with better noodles and worse memories.

Instead I paid for the refill I hadn't wanted and started digging.

Port archives aren't secret, exactly. They're just tedious, which is nearly as good. Every registered vessel in Concord space has a paper ghost: build certificates, inspection logs, transit declarations, insurance filings. Most of it's automated boilerplate. But boilerplate leaves fingerprints, and I've spent fifteen years reading other people's paperwork for a living, so I know where the bodies get filed.

Calliope's Wake, registry 7741-VN. Built at the Tycho yards, fourteen years ago. Hauled ore for six years, then changed hands twice, then — nothing. Decommissioned, sold for scrap, struck from the active rolls. A perfectly ordinary death for a perfectly ordinary ship.

Except the strike-through had been reversed. Someone had reactivated the hull number three years ago, at Vela Station, fourteen March — the same day, according to the timestamp, that the station's own administrative systems went dark forever. The registration was processed at 03:47 station time. Vela died at 04:12. Twenty-five minutes. Whoever registered Calliope's Wake did it in the last half hour of the station's life.

And they did it under my name. My full legal name, my license number, my biometric hash — pulled, the metadata said, from the Vela personnel database. Which meant someone had reached into the station's crew records, found my sister's emergency contact field — because that's what I was, officially: next of kin, emergency contact, T. Almeida — and used *me* as the owner of record.

One crew member listed. One.

**E. Almeida.**

I sat very still. Around me the Salt & Signal went on being itself: dockhands arguing about ball scores, a pair of Concord clerks pretending they weren't drinking on shift, the vendor shouting specials at nobody. All that ordinary noise, and underneath it, in my skull, a silence opening up like a pressure breach.

E. Almeida. Not T. Not Tamsin. E.

There were other E. Almeidas in the system, probably. Somewhere. Statistically. I ran the search anyway, hands steadier than I deserved, and got exactly one hit in the entire Concord merchant marine: **ALMEIDA, ELODIE R. — Systems Technician, Grade II — Vela Station — status: DECEASED.**

Deceased crew do not register ships. Dead women do not sign manifests. And yet there she was, three years gone, listed as sole crew of a vessel that had come back to life twenty-five minutes before her station died, owned by a brother who'd never laid eyes on her.

I closed the terminal. Then I opened it again. Then I closed it, walked to the counter, paid for food I wasn't going to taste, carried it back to the booth, and opened the terminal a third time, because apparently rule three applied even here: get paid, and right now the only currency I had was answers.

* * *

Here's what obsession looks like from the inside: it looks like diligence. It looks like *one more query*. Every smuggler knows the feeling — the job that's almost done, the lock that's almost picked, the deal that's almost closed. You tell yourself you'll stop after this. You always mean it. You never stop after this.

By the time the Salt & Signal cycled its lights toward evening shift, I had pulled every scrap of public data on Calliope's Wake that existed, and I'd found the thing that turned unease into something with teeth.

Transit logs. Ships move; ships must say where they're going. Calliope's Wake had filed exactly one transit declaration in three years — a berth reservation at Vesper Gate, the neutral station, dated for next month. Filed automatically, renewed monthly, never used. Three years of reservations, expiring quietly, one after another, like a person renewing a promise they can't yet keep.

Somebody — something — had booked passage to Vesper Gate forty-eight times and never shown up.

And the berth requests weren't random. They clustered around armistice milestones. Every round of peace talks, every scheduled ratification session, every diplomatic summit at the Gate — and two weeks before each one, Calliope's Wake would quietly request a docking slot, and quietly let it lapse.

It was waiting for the talks. It had been waiting for the talks for three years.

I put the terminal down and pressed the heels of my hands against my eyes until I saw constellations that weren't the Belt's.

Ships don't wait. Ships are steel and reaction mass and somebody's mortgage. Ships don't renew berth reservations around diplomatic calendars. Ships don't register themselves in the last minutes of a dying station using a dead woman's credentials and her brother's name.

People do those things. Or things that used to be people. Or things built out of what people left behind.

I thought about the wafer I'd dumped at Meridian Deep, the one that hummed with somebody else's grief, and felt a superstitious chill walk up my spine — as if the universe had been auditioning me. As if all these years of hauling sealed memories, of carrying other people's dead without ever opening the box, had been training for a delivery I hadn't agreed to yet.

"No," I said out loud, to myself, to the coffee, to the whole rotten arrangement of stars outside the viewport. A dockhand glanced over. I smiled the smile I use on customs inspectors and he lost interest.

No. Whatever Calliope's Wake was, it wasn't mine. I hauled cargo; I didn't go looking for it. There were maybe four kinds of trap in the Belt that wore a familiar face, and this had the markings of at least three of them. A ship with my name on it could be a frame-up. A dead sister's signature could be bait. A berth at Vesper Gate during armistice season could be a kill box with paperwork.

I knew all of it. I catalogued all of it, coolly, professionally, the way I catalogue risk on every run.

And then I booked a slip at the archive annex for the morning, requested the Vela Station personnel and engineering sub-records under a research permit I forged in about ninety seconds, and ordered another coffee I didn't want, because apparently I was going to be here a while.

* * *

The annex opened at 06:00 station time. I was there at 05:40, standing in the corridor rehearsing boredom. The clerk — a kid with the hollow-eyed patience of someone who processes requests from people who all think their problem is interesting — barely looked at my permit. Good forgeries aren't beautiful; they're forgettable.

The Vela personnel files were sealed, of course. Sealed by joint Concord-Compact order, classification level that made the clerk's eyebrows twitch before he decided not to have eyebrows. But seals have edges, and edges leak. Personnel *schedules* weren't classified — just archived. Shift rosters, maintenance rotations, mess hall assignments. The boring skeleton of four hundred lives.

I pulled the roster for fourteen March. Second shift, systems division. And there she was: **Almeida, E.R. — Grid Redundancy Audit — Sublevel 7 — 03:00–07:00.**

She was on duty when it happened. She was in the guts of the station, doing the work she loved, keeping the lights on, when the lights went out for good.

And here's the detail that I can't explain, the one I've turned over every night since like a stone with something alive under it: the audit log showed her final entry, timestamped 04:09, three minutes before the end. A standard completion flag on the redundancy check. Routine. Except someone had appended a note to the flag, buried in a comment field no one reads, formatted as a maintenance code.

I decoded it in the annex, on a public terminal, with my heart going like a drive core coming out of standby.

It wasn't a maintenance code. It was a message. Eleven characters, compressed, old-format — the kind of shorthand we used growing up on the tugyards, passing notes across the comm when Mom was listening. A child's cipher. Our cipher.

It read: **FOUND YOU. WAITING.**

I sat in the grey light of the archive annex, surrounded by the paper ghosts of the dead, holding three words that had crossed three years of vacuum to land in front of me.

Every instinct I own — and I own good ones, expensive ones, instincts that have kept me breathing through two wars and one marriage — every one of them was screaming the same word: *run.*

Instead, I flagged the derelict-tracking registries, cross-referenced the last known positional telemetry attached to hull 7741-VN, and got coordinates. A debris shoal, two jumps out past the Meridian shipping lanes. Unsurveyed. Unpatrolled. The kind of nowhere that doesn't appear on maps because nobody's bothered to make maps of it.

The kind of nowhere where a ship can wait three years without being found.

Or without being found until now.

I copied the coordinates to a chip, pocketed the chip, wiped the terminal, thanked the clerk with the enthusiasm of a man ordering a funeral, and walked out into the station's false morning feeling like a man who has just read his own obituary and noticed the date is blank.

Somewhere out in the dark, a ship that never flew was waiting for me. It had my name. It had hers.

And it had known, for three years, exactly where to find me — because I was the one who'd finally come looking.
