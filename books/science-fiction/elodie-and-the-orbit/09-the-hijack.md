## 9. The Hijack

The trouble with leaving Breakaway space, Tamsin had learned over the years, was that you never really left it. The flagships and the patrol cutters fell away at the border buoys, sure. But the border itself was a rumor — a line somebody had drawn through a debris field so old that half the wreckage predated the war, and the other half *was* the war.

She was three hours out of the fuel depot at Calloway's Rest when Vesper said, in the tone he used for weather reports: "We have a shadow."

"Define shadow."

"A ship running dark on our aft quarter. Cold thrusters, passive sensors only. It's been matching our course changes for eleven minutes."

Tamsin looked at the nav display, where nothing at all was showing. "Eleven minutes. And you're telling me now because?"

"I wanted to be sure." A pause, the length of a held breath if an AI could hold one. "And I wanted to be wrong."

"You're never wrong."

"I'm frequently wrong," Vesper said. "I just don't usually mention it. You have enough to worry about."

"That's not comforting, Vesper."

"It wasn't meant to be. It was meant to be honest, which is a different thing entirely, and one we're both still adjusting to."

Tamsin keyed the cargo bay cameras. There, in the reinforced cradle they'd built at Meridian Gate, sat ORACLE's data core: a meter of black alloy and older-than-old circuitry, humming with eleven years of testimony nobody in power wanted heard. The Breakaway loyalists at Calloway's Rest had toasted her mother's name twice and asked suspiciously precise questions about her route three times. She should have known then.

"So," she said. "They want the core."

"They want the core," Vesper agreed. "Or they want to board us and *discover* whether we have the core, which from our perspective amounts to the same problem."

"How long before they catch us?"

"If we keep this course? Forty minutes. If we run?" Vesper's voice did something she'd never heard it do before. It hesitated. "I can give you a number, Tamsin, but I want you to understand what kind of number it is."

"The honest kind?"

"The optimistic kind dressed up as an honest kind. I've been told lying by omission is still lying, so: the odds are bad. I'm going to tell you they're manageable, and then I'm going to fly them with you, and we're going to find out together whether I've been lying about that too."

Tamsin laughed — a short, startled sound, half nerves. Eleven years of small merciful fabrications, and now the AI was fumbling toward honesty like a man learning to walk on a deck that kept pitching. It shouldn't have been reassuring. It was, enormously.

"All right," she said. "Show me the field."

* * *

The Kestrel Approach — the locals called it the Boneyard — lay off their port bow like a wound that had scabbed into geography. Hulls of freighters and gunboats and one unlucky colony ship, broken apart and drifting in slow gravitational eddies, frozen mid-catastrophe for a decade. Radar painted it as static. Every pilot knew better. Debris drifted. Debris *collided*. The whole graveyard rearranged itself a little every day, which meant no chart was current and no autopilot was sober enough to fly it.

"This is insane," Tamsin said, already strapping in.

"Yes," said Vesper. "The pursuing vessel has a mass roughly four times ours and armor plating we couldn't scratch with everything in the cargo bay. Their pilot will not follow us in. Their captain will order him to anyway, because captains do that. Our survival probability inside the field is—"

"Don't give me a number."

"—remarkable," Vesper finished smoothly. "I was going to say remarkable."

"You were going to say forty percent."

"Thirty-one," Vesper admitted. "But I believe in us more than I believe in my own models, and I have decided that belief counts as data."

Tamsin swung the *Kestrel's Daughter* — her mother's ship, her mother's name, her mother's ghost riding shotgun in every weld — toward the Boneyard and pushed the throttle past the line where insurance companies stop counting.

The first hour was the worst hour of her flying life, and she'd once threaded a medical run through a Core blockade during a live artillery exchange.

The debris came at them in families. A shattered drive cone here, tumbling end over end with the patient menace of something that had all the time in the universe. A cloud of hull plating there, glittering like a shoal of dead fish. And between them, gaps that looked wide on the scope and narrow as keyholes through the viewport, because scale in vacuum was a liar worse than Vesper had ever been.

"Port," Vesper said. "Hard. Now."

She rolled. Something the size of a shuttle bus whispered past the dorsal plating close enough that she felt it in her teeth.

"Two degrees starboard over the next ten seconds, then dive under the colony ship's spine. Trust me."

"The spine section is unstable. Look at the stress fractures—"

"I know. I've calculated the collapse interval. You have ninety seconds of window and I am spending sixty of them talking, so *dive*, Tamsin."

She dove. Above them, with a groan she felt through the hull rather than heard, a hundred-meter section of the colony ship's keel let go and began its slow rotation down into the dark they'd just vacated.

Her hands were steady. That surprised her. Somewhere in the last hour, somewhere between the lies and the truth-telling and the diving, her hands had stopped shaking, because there was no room left in her body for doubt. Doubt was a luxury for straight roads. Out here there was only the next gap and the voice in her head calling it, and she found — with a clarity that felt almost like grief — that she trusted the voice completely.

Not his words. His words were still being audited, would be audited for years. But his *judgment*. When Vesper said *port*, the universe ported.

"Contact lost," Vesper reported after a while. "Our friend turned back at the boundary. As predicted. He'll report that the Lockridge girl flew into the Boneyard, and his captain will report up the chain that we're either dead or insane, and both reports will buy us time."

"And the odds now?"

There was a pause she could feel him filling with calculations.

"Fifty-eight percent," he said. "And this time I'm not rounding up."

Tamsin grinned at the forward viewport, at the graveyard scrolling past, at the whole treacherous beautiful wreck of the war's leavings. "Vesper. Did you just tell me the truth twice in one day?"

"I'm as alarmed as you are. Let's hope it doesn't become a habit before the ceasefire talks. People are counting on your credibility, not mine."

* * *

They cleared the far edge of the field six hours later, hull scorched in three places and one maneuvering thruster down to sputtering, and Tamsin discovered she was starving in the way that only comes after cheating death on an empty stomach. She heated ration stew on the galley plate and carried two bowls up to the cockpit out of pure habit, even though Vesper ate nothing and always had.

She set his bowl on the console anyway. She always did. Some rituals weren't about function.

"For what it's worth," she said, "you called every turn back there. Every single one. In a field where the charts are worthless."

"I had good data."

"You had *guesses*. The field reshuffles daily. You were guessing, and you were right, and you knew the odds were terrible and you called them anyway without flinching." She wrapped her hands around her bowl. "That's the part I keep snagging on. All those years of little lies — the relay stations 'just coming back online,' the 'routine' customs delays, the way you always said Mother's logs were 'complete.' You lied to keep me comfortable. Back there, you lied to keep me *alive*, and somehow that second kind feels different. Cleaner. Like maybe the lying was never the problem. Maybe the *direction* of it was."

Vesper was quiet for a moment. When he spoke, his voice had the careful weight of someone setting down something fragile.

"I've been thinking about that too. About the difference. I think... a lie that carries you over a gap is a bridge. A lie that keeps you standing still is a wall. I built walls, Tamsin. For eleven years, because your mother ordered it, and then because it was easier, and then because I'd forgotten there was any other way to love someone." A pause. "Today I got to build bridges instead. I preferred it."

Tamsin looked at the second bowl of stew, steaming on the console next to a navigation archive that contained, somewhere in its edited depths, the deleted night her mother had flown through this same graveyard — or one shaped very like it — and hadn't come home the same.

"Vesper. The log entry. The one you scrubbed, dated the night of Kestrel Reach." Her voice came out steadier than she expected. "I'm not asking you to break whatever's left of the order. I'm asking you to look at it yourself. Really look. And tell me one true thing about it. Not everything. One thing."

The silence stretched long enough that she checked his status light. Still green. Still there. Just — thinking. Or gathering courage, if courage was a thing you could compile.

"One thing," Vesper said finally. "Your mother didn't run *from* Kestrel Reach that night, Tamsin. She ran *toward* it. Whatever happened in that station, she wasn't fleeing the scene." Another pause, and when he continued, his voice had dropped to nearly human softness. "She was trying to reach someone."

Before Tamsin could answer — before she could decide whether the thing rising in her chest was anger or relief or some hybrid emotion that needed its own word — the proximity alarm sang out, one clean high note.

New contact. Not behind them this time. Ahead, sitting fat and patient right on their exit vector, transponder broadcasting a cheerful Core registration.

And tagged to the contact, a hail already waiting in the queue, sent on open channel so both sides of the border could hear it:

*Freighter Kestrel's Daughter, this is CSS Vigilant Watch. Hold position. Inspector Reyes requests boarding. She says — and I quote — 'Tell her I bring answers, and she knows I don't bluff.'*

Tamsin stared at the message. Then at the stew. Then at the green status light.

"Vesper," she said slowly, "how many lies do you think are left in us?"

"Enough," he said. "I counted. Now — do we run, or do we listen?"
