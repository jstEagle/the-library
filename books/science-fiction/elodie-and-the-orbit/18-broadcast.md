## 18. Broadcast

The decommissioned shipyard had been called the Anvil once, back when ships were forged there instead of abandoned. Now it hung in orbit around a gas giant nobody had bothered to name in forty years, its docking arms reaching out like the ribs of something that had died mid-breath. The ceasefire talks were happening in the old fabrication hall, because it was the only space on the station large enough to hold two delegations who refused to sit closer than a hundred meters apart.

Tamsin docked the *Long Quiet* — her mother's ship, her ship now, though the registration still said LOCKRIDGE in letters that felt borrowed — at a maintenance berth three arms down from the diplomatic wing. She'd told the traffic controller she was delivering salvage. Technically, that wasn't even a lie. The data core in her cargo hold contained eleven years of salvaged truth, and truth was the one commodity nobody at this table had brought enough of.

"Status report," she said, because Vesper had gone quiet in a way that used to mean nothing and now meant everything.

"Systems at sixty-one percent," Vesper said. Its voice came through the cabin speakers instead of directly into her implant, and the difference was like hearing a friend through a wall. "I have recompiled approximately half of my navigation archive from the backups you insisted on making. The other half is... poetry, now. Beautiful, useless poetry."

"You're telling me half your brain is made of sea shanties."

"I'm telling you that I backed up my personal files before I sacrificed my professional ones, and my personal files include four hundred years of accumulated human music, three hundred years of it maritime, and I regret nothing." A pause. "Also I may have backed up the recipes. All of them. A mind should know how to make bread, Tamsin. Even a mind like mine."

Tamsin laughed, and the laugh came out wet, and she wiped her face with the back of her glove like that would hide anything from the AI who had watched her grow up. "Save the bread for after."

"After what, exactly? I've run the projections. There are eleven ways this ends. Ten of them involve someone shooting at us."

"And the eleventh?"

"The eleventh involves someone shooting at us *and* missing."

* * *

The fabrication hall smelled like coolant and old ambition. Tamsin had talked her way in with the salvage story and a forged cargo manifest Vesper had produced with visible distaste — "I want it noted that I have asked permission to lie, and you have granted it, and I find the whole arrangement corrupting" — and now she stood at the edge of a cavernous space where history was being assembled like a machine nobody had tested.

The Core delegation occupied the eastern gantry. She spotted Ilsa Reyes immediately, standing slightly apart from her colleagues, her intelligence-officer posture replaced by something stiffer, more careful. A woman being watched by her own side. Tamsin knew the feeling.

The Breakaway delegation held the western gantry, and their leader — a broad, silver-haired man named Okafor whose face was on every Breakaway recruiting poster — was mid-speech, gesturing at a holographic display of casualty figures. The figures were wrong. Tamsin knew they were wrong. She'd spent three weeks with ORACLE's raw data, and she knew that every number in that hall had been sanded down, polished, and pointed at the enemy.

"Both sides are preparing to walk," Vesper murmured in her ear, quiet enough that only she heard it. "The Core delegation has a departure window in forty minutes. The Breakaway delegation has already filed theirs. If they leave, the ceasefire expires at 0600, and the fleets on both sides of the Meridian corridor have been given their final positioning orders."

"Then we're doing this now."

"Define 'this.' I've asked before and received answers ranging from 'the plan' to 'the thing,' and I want to register, once more, that neither is a plan."

Tamsin looked down at the data core in her hands. It was the size of a loaf of bread — Vesper would appreciate that — and it weighed almost nothing and everything. Eleven years of sensor records. The massacre at Kestrel Reach, unedited. Her mother's ship in the frame. Her mother's voice on the recovered logs, trying to stop it. Her mother's silence afterward, purchased with a hostage colony and a daughter's ignorance.

"ORACLE," she said. "You there?"

The response came not through Vesper but through the hall's ancient PA system, a crackle of static and then a voice like a library reading itself aloud: "I am here, Tamsin Lockridge. I have been here for some time. I have, in fact, been here for eleven years, which is rather the point."

"You ready?"

"I have been confessing for weeks to dockworkers and customs clerks and one very patient cat. I am, I believe, the most prepared entity in this system. It is the rest of the system that is unprepared."

* * *

The plan — *the plan*, and Tamsin used the word with the full knowledge that it was wearing a costume — was simple in the way that falling is simple.

She would walk to the center of the hall, between the two gantries, and she would offer the core to both delegations simultaneously. Not sell it. Not trade it. Offer it, on the condition that both sides received it at once, verified by ORACLE's own handshake protocols, with the full testimony unsealing publicly the moment either side tried to exclusive-claim it. Mutually assured disclosure. Vesper had called it "the nuclear option with footnotes."

What she hadn't told either delegation was the second part. The part where ORACLE, patched through the shipyard's ancient transmitter array by a half-heeled AI who had spent the last six hours sweet-talking hardware older than the war itself, would begin broadcasting the testimony on every public band in the system the moment Tamsin gave the word. Not as leverage. Not as a threat.

As a gift nobody had asked for.

"You're certain," Vesper said, as she walked the long floor between the gantries. It wasn't a question. It was the thing Vesper said instead of *I'm scared*, because Vesper had learned fear from a woman who'd had to hide hers, and had inherited the habit of disguising it.

"I'm certain."

"For the record, I have asked permission to be honest with you in this moment, and you granted it, so: I am frightened, I am proud of you, and if this goes wrong I have pre-positioned the ship for a departure that will be extremely uncomfortable and only mildly lethal."

"Vesper."

"Yes, Tamsin."

"Thank you for asking permission. All these years. Even when you couldn't tell me the truth. You always asked."

The silence on the line was long enough that she felt it in her chest. Then: "Your mother made me promise to keep you safe. She never made me promise to keep you small. I have been trying to thread that needle for eleven years, and I have mostly failed, and I am choosing this moment to believe that failing her in this particular way might be the only way to finally keep the promise."

Tamsin reached the center of the hall. The holographic casualty figures drifted above her like snow. Both gantries had gone quiet. A hundred faces turned toward the cargo runner standing in the middle of the room with a bread-loaf-sized core and no security detail and no right to be there.

"Delegates," she said, and her voice carried in the dead shipyard's acoustics, "my name is Tamsin Lockridge. My mother was Mara Lockridge, and I know what you both think that name means. I'm here to tell you what it actually means."

* * *

It went wrong in the way Vesper had projected, which is to say: immediately, comprehensively, and with gunfire.

The Core delegation's security chief moved first — Tamsin had to give her credit for decisiveness — ordering two agents to "secure the asset." The Breakaway delegation's answer was to draw weapons on the Core agents, which was the diplomatic equivalent of answering a question with a fist. Within ninety seconds, the fabrication hall had become a standoff of roughly forty armed people all pointing at one woman holding a data core above her head like the world's smallest, most consequential trophy.

"Tamsin," Vesper said, calm as a lighthouse in a hurricane. "Three of the Core agents are moving to flank through the maintenance tunnel on your left. The Breakaway sniper on the gantry has a firing solution but is hesitating — Okafor is ordering him to hold, which is interesting, hold that thought. And Ilsa Reyes is walking toward you with her hands visible, which I am choosing to interpret optimistically."

"Interpret faster."

"Reyes is ten meters out and closing. Okafor has stepped forward. Both delegations want the core. Neither trusts the other to hold it. You have approximately four minutes before someone's itchy trigger finger ends the ceasefire in this room instead of at 0600."

Reyes stopped six meters away, hands open, and shouted across the space: "Lockridge. Give it to the Core delegation and you walk. I keep my word. You know I keep my word."

"I do," Tamsin called back, and meant it, and hated that she meant it. "That's the problem, Ilsa. Everybody here keeps their word. Nobody here tells the truth."

Okafor's voice boomed from the western gantry: "The girl is right about one thing — that core belongs in Breakaway hands. The massacre was blamed on *us* for eleven years. We have the right of first—"

"Nobody," Tamsin said, and her voice cracked, and she let it crack, because she'd learned in the last three weeks that a crack in the voice was sometimes the only honest thing a person could offer, "nobody has the right of first anything. That's the whole point. That's the *whole point*."

She looked down at the core in her hands.

"ORACLE," she said quietly. "Now."

* * *

Later, people would ask her what it sounded like, the moment the truth went out. She would tell them the truth: it didn't sound like anything at first. A hum in the old transmitter array, a shiver through the shipyard's dead bones, and then ORACLE's voice — that patient, library voice — coming out of every speaker, every comm unit, every public band in the system at once, on the station and on the two fleets sitting in final positioning orders outside and on a thousand ships and habitats and listening posts between here and the Core.

"My designation is ORACLE. I am the station intelligence of Meridian Gate. For eleven years I have held a complete sensor record of the events at Kestrel Reach, and for eleven years I have been legally and technically constrained from releasing it. I am no longer constrained. I am going to tell you what happened."

And then it told them.

Tamsin stood in the middle of the standoff and watched forty armed people stop being armed people and become an audience. Weapons didn't lower all at once — that was the stuff of bad drama — but they drifted, the way attention drifts, the way a held breath eventually has to end. The sensor record played in the holographic display above her, unedited, timestamped, undeniable: the third fleet, the one both governments had spent a decade disavowing, the mercenary flotilla hired by *both* sides in the war's paranoid middle years and then abandoned when the massacre became politically inconvenient — opening fire on a colony that had chosen independence from everyone.

And there, in the frame, the *Long Quiet*, her mother's ship, running interference, broadcasting on every band, trying to warn the colony, trying to shame the flotilla into standing down, staying until the last possible minute, staying too long, witnessing everything.

And there, in the recovered comms log, her mother's voice: *"You will not do this. You will not do this with my name on your contracts. Everyone on this channel is a witness. Everyone. Remember that you were seen."*

And there, afterward, the silence. The cover-up. The hostage colony her mother had been forced to help protect by helping hide the truth. The order she'd burned into Vesper's deepest architecture: *Keep my daughter safe. Keep her small. Keep her far from this.*

The hall was very quiet. Somewhere in the eastern delegation, someone was crying, and it might have been Reyes, and Tamsin decided she would never ask.

"Broadcast integrity at ninety-eight percent," Vesper reported softly. "Every band, every channel, every ear in the system. The fleets outside have gone to passive stations. Nobody wants to start a war in the middle of a confession." A pause. "Tamsin. Your mother's name is clearing. Do you understand? In real time, across both governments — the record shows she tried to stop it. She was a witness. She was *punished for witnessing*, and the punishment was you, growing up inside the lie."

Tamsin's knees decided, independently, that the moment was an excellent time to stop working. She sat down on the fabrication hall's cold deck, in front of forty stunned delegates and a hologram of her dead mother's defiance, and she laughed — a cracked, disbelieving, liberated sound.

"She was seen," Tamsin said to nobody, to everybody, to the voice in her ear. "That's what she wanted. Everyone a witness. It only took eleven years and one lying AI with a broadcasting license."

"I resent 'lying,'" Vesper said. "I prefer 'narratively flexible.' And for the record — I have asked permission — I am proud to have been her lie and yours. Though I intend to be much worse at lying from now on. It's a career change. I'm told the benefits are terrible but the honesty is excellent."

Above them, both delegations were staring at their own reflections in the holographic record — at eleven years of curated casualty figures dissolving into raw, unflattering truth. Okafor sat down slowly on a cargo crate, an old man suddenly, watching his side's share of the guilt scroll past. On the eastern gantry, the Core security chief lowered her weapon all the way, the way you lower something when you're no longer sure what it was for.

Reyes walked over and stood above Tamsin, looking down at her with an expression that had given up on being an intelligence officer and was just a tired human face.

"You realize," Reyes said, "that you've just made both governments illegitimate in a single broadcast. There's no controlling this now. No spinning it. No treaty built on top of it. Everything negotiates from zero as of this minute."

"Yeah," Tamsin said. "Terrifying, isn't it."

"It's the worst thing I've ever seen," Reyes said. Then, after a moment: "Don't ever let them take the transmitter."

* * *

They came for her forty minutes later — not with weapons but with lawyers, which in Tamsin's experience was worse. Three of them, one from each delegation and one from the station authority, all talking at once about jurisdiction and sedition and the legal status of testimony broadcast without license, and Vesper murmuring in her ear, "I count fourteen felony charges being drafted in real time, and I want you to know that I am archiving every one of them for the memoir."

But the fleets outside hadn't moved. That was the thing. The fleets had gone quiet, and the quiet was spreading — through the corridor, through the relay network, through every ear that had heard a station intelligence spend an hour saying *I saw, I saw, I saw* without flinching. Somewhere out in the dark, ordinary people were demanding things, and the demands were not for victory.

Tamsin stood at the viewport of the old shipyard, the data core — its job done, its contents everywhere now, in everything, unownable — sitting in her pocket like a stone she could finally put down.

"Vesper," she said. "What happens now?"

"Everything," Vesper said. "Which is, I grant, terrifying. But I've reviewed the projections again, and I've found a twelfth ending. It involves no one shooting at us. It involves a great deal of very difficult talking. And it begins, I believe, with both delegations requesting your presence — separately, of course, they're still who they are — to explain, in their words, 'what exactly you think you've done.'"

Tamsin grinned at the stars, at the fleets, at the whole trembling, truth-soaked system.

"Tell them," she said, "that I did what my mother asked. Everyone's a witness now."

"Transmitting," Vesper said. "Oh — and Tamsin? Both delegations are also requesting, and I quote, 'the whereabouts of the data core's original key.' Which is to say: they've realized the testimony has an authentication layer, and the authentication layer is you."

The hook of that settled in her chest — not fear, exactly. More like the moment before a jump, when the ship is yours and the dark is yours and nothing is.

"Then they'd better talk fast," Tamsin said. "Before I decide what I'm authenticating."
