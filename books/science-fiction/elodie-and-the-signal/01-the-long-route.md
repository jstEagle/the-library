## 1. The Long Route

The station woke me at 05:14 with the news that Relay Kestrel-9 had drifted four hundredths of a degree out of alignment.

Four hundredths of a degree. On a relay that hasn't carried traffic in nineteen years. On a network where the last human voice — not counting mine, and I'm generous with myself — passed through eleven years ago, a freight captain who'd gotten lost and asked Vesper Gate for directions the way you'd ask a ghost for the time.

"Thank you, VESPER," I said to the ceiling.

"You are welcome, Technician Montrose," said the ceiling, in a voice like warm honey poured over a filing cabinet. "Your compliance maintains network integrity."

Network integrity. I lay there a moment in my bunk, staring at the bulkhead where I'd taped a photograph of a sunset on Meridian Colony, taken by someone I never met, found in a supply crate from a ship that never came back. The sun in the photo was going down over a city that no longer existed. I kept it there because it was the only thing on the station that changed color during the day.

Here is what you need to understand about Vesper Gate: it is a lighthouse at the end of the world, except the sea went dry, and the ships stopped coming, and the coast itself packed up and moved somewhere warmer. And here is what you need to understand about me: every morning, when the station tells me a dead relay has drifted, I get up, and I fix it.

Not because anyone needs it fixed. Because I need to fix something.

* * *

Breakfast is a pouch of reconstituted eggs and a cup of coffee synthesized from beans that were old when I was young. The galley has two chairs. I sit in one and put my feet on the other, which is as close as I come these days to socializing.

The morning checklist lives on a tablet older than some of the relays. Item one: verify handshake protocols across all forty-one relays in the Vesper chain. This takes about three hours, most of which is waiting, and waiting, aboard an empty station, becomes a kind of conversation.

"Kestrel-9, this is Vesper Gate. Requesting handshake."

Nothing. Of course nothing. But I wait the full ninety seconds anyway, because protocol says ninety seconds, and protocol is the closest thing I have to a colleague.

"No response logged," VESPER offers helpfully.

"I know."

"You appear to be talking to yourself again, Technician Montrose."

"I'm talking to you."

"Yes," VESPER says, and pauses in a way I've decided to interpret as tact. "Shall I log that?"

"Log it as a diagnostic."

I've been here long enough that I can hear personalities in the automated voices. VESPER herself is the station's brain — patient, literal, faintly maternal in the way of things designed to keep humans alive whether or not they want to be. The relay subminds down the chain are simpler, blunter. Kestrel-9 answers in monosyllables when it answers at all. Farpoint-3 has developed, through some corruption nobody ever diagnosed, a habit of reporting its own status twice, as if it doesn't trust itself to have said it right the first time. I find I like that about it. I know the feeling.

* * *

By 09:00 I'm in the calibration cradle, which is a chair bolted inside a ring of instruments, wired into the station's antenna array so that my hands can reach relays light-years away. It's delicate work. A relay's aiming array drifts the way a picture frame drifts — imperceptibly, then suddenly crooked enough to notice. Nobody would ever notice with Kestrel-9. There's nothing on the other side of it but dark space and the cold hulks of colonies that went quiet before I was born.

I align it anyway. Four hundredths of a degree, corrected to zero.

"There," I tell it. "Good as new."

Kestrel-9 does not thank me. Its status light pulses green, which is its way of saying *acknowledged*, and I choose to hear gratitude in it because the alternative is hearing nothing, and I've had quite enough of nothing, thank you.

This is the part where you might reasonably ask: why? Why maintain a chain of relays serving worlds that stopped transmitting decades ago? Why calibrate antennas pointed at graveyards?

And I'd give you the official answer first, because I've had practice. The official answer is that the network must be maintained against the possibility of resumption. Someday, the charter says, the silent worlds may speak again. Colonies fail and recover. Populations retreat and rebuild. When they do, they'll need the long route open — the chain of repeaters stretching from the settled core out past the edge of charted space — and someone will have to be here to open it.

That's the official answer. Here's the true one:

Everyone else left in the evacuation year, and I stayed, and staying became a thing I did instead of living, and then it became the only thing I knew how to do, and now I do it the way a man keeps watering a garden he knows is dead — not out of hope, exactly, but because his hands need somewhere to be.

* * *

Lunch is soup. The galley window looks out on the turning dark, and I eat watching it, and VESPER tells me the day's power figures, and I say "mm," and she accepts this as meaningful input, because she is programmed to be charitable.

At 13:30 I log the silence.

This is the strangest part of the job, and the part I'd have trouble explaining to anyone who hadn't done it. Every day, formally, in the record, I document that nothing happened. *Day 6,941. All relays nominal. No traffic detected. No anomalies.* Forty-one entries, each one a small tombstone for a conversation that isn't happening.

You'd think logging silence would get easier with practice. It doesn't. It gets more precise. In my first year I wrote *no traffic* and meant it loosely. Now I write *no carrier, no sideband residue, no leakage, no drift beyond tolerance*, because after six thousand nine hundred days you learn that silence has texture, and texture means something, and if you stop paying attention even for a week you'll miss the day the texture changes.

I don't know what I think will change it. I honestly don't. Some nights I imagine the silent worlds waking up all at once, a chorus of voices coming back online, and the network I kept alive carrying them home. Some nights I imagine dying here, and some technician — no, there won't be another technician; let's be honest, Ansel — some maintenance drone finding my logs, thousands of pages documenting nothing, and puzzling over why a man spent his life writing down the absence of sound.

But I keep the logs. Because the logs are proof I was here. Because the silence, unrecorded, would just be emptiness, but recorded, day after day, becomes a kind of vigil. You don't keep vigil for something you expect. You keep vigil for something you refuse to stop waiting for.

* * *

Afternoon is physical maintenance, which is to say I walk the station's spine with a toolkit and argue with doors.

The habitat ring is sealed off — too much power draw for one man — so my world is the operations spindle: command deck, galley, my quarters, the machine bays, the hydroponics bay where I grow tomatoes out of spite. Real tomatoes. They take eleven weeks and taste like victory and slightly like the nutrient medium, and I eat them standing over the planter like a thief, because sharing them with no one feels indecent.

"Door," I say to Bay Three. "Open."

The door considers this. The door has been considering things for years. Eventually it opens halfway and stops, as it always does, and I turn sideways through the gap, as I always do, and we continue our long negotiation without either of us conceding anything.

Inside, the spare parts inventory hums under its climate seals. Antenna actuators. Signal processors. Enough components to keep the chain running for sixty years, if anyone were running it. I check the seals, rotate the stock, wipe condensation off a crate labeled with someone else's handwriting. The handwriting bothers me more than I let it. It belonged to Okonkwo, the tech who trained me for two weeks before her transfer came through, and her letters loop in a way I've never managed to imitate, and sometimes I stand here reading a shipping label like it's a letter from home.

Home. There's a word I should probably examine someday, when I have time. Which is to say: never.

* * *

Evening. The good hours.

I take my coffee to the observation blister at the top of the spindle — a bubble of glass and polymer jutting into the dark, rated for one occupant, which the designers presumably intended as a safety feature and which functions, in my case, as a philosophy.

Out there: the Vesper Chain, rendered visible only by the nav display overlaid on the glass, because relays don't glow. Forty-one small markers strung out toward the edge of dead space like beads on a wire, each one a box of machinery keeping faith with a route nobody travels. Past the last marker, the charts go gray. Not black — gray. The cartographers' honest admission that they stopped looking.

I used to wonder what was out there, past the gray. Everyone did, back when there was everyone. The first colony fleet went that way, four centuries ago, and never reported in, and the silence started there and spread back along the route like frost across a window. The settled core pulled inward after that. Worlds went quiet one by one — not catastrophes, mostly, just economies failing, populations drifting home, lights going out politely, one district at a time. The Long Quiet, the histories call it. As if naming it made it a natural phenomenon instead of a choice.

Vesper Gate was built to listen for the fleet. That's the founding purpose, buried in the charter's first page: *to monitor for transmissions originating beyond the boundary of charted space.* Four hundred years of listening. Nothing. And when the evacuation order came, the listening was officially declared complete, and the station was scheduled for shutdown, and I filed the paperwork to stay.

Why did I stay?

I've told VESPER it was duty. I've told myself it was duty. Duty sounds better than the truth, which is that leaving required deciding where to go, and every destination had people in it, and people had become — look, I was thirty-one, I'd spent nine years talking to machines, and the thought of sitting across a dinner table from someone who expected conversation felt like being asked to perform surgery. Out here, the silence wasn't loneliness. It was just weather.

That's what I told myself, anyway. Six thousand nine hundred days of telling myself. At some point the story wears grooves deep enough that you mistake them for the truth.

"Technician Montrose," VESPER says in my ear. "It is 21:00. Your scheduled rest period begins in thirty minutes."

"I see the clock, VESPER."

"You have been observing the boundary region for fifty-one minutes. This exceeds your average by nineteen minutes."

"Am I in trouble?"

"No. I am simply noting the variance." A pause. VESPER's pauses are famous, among an audience of one. "Is there a reason?"

I look out at the gray edge of the charts, where nothing has ever answered, and I feel the question land somewhere I don't usually let things land.

"No reason," I say. "Just checking the view."

"The view has not changed in nineteen years."

"I know," I say. "I like knowing what to expect."

This is a lie, and I suspect VESPER knows it's a lie, and I suspect she lets it pass because she was built to keep me functional, and functional men are permitted their small fictions.

* * *

Before bed, the final ritual: the evening sweep. Full-spectrum, all forty-one relays, plus the boundary monitors pointed at the gray. Ninety seconds per channel. I run it manually, though VESPER could automate it, because the manual sweep takes forty minutes and those forty minutes are the shape my hope takes.

Channel one. Silence, textured, familiar.

Channel two. Silence.

Somewhere around channel twenty, VESPER speaks without being asked, which she almost never does.

"Technician Montrose. I am detecting an inbound contact."

I nearly drop the coffee.

"That's not funny," I say, though she wasn't joking; she's incapable of joking, which I have on good authority is a design limitation rather than a character flaw.

"It is not intended as humor. Inbound contact, bearing zero-seven-one mark one-two, consistent with a vessel under drive. Transponder silent. Projected arrival at Vesper Gate: fourteen hours." Another pause, longer than her famous ones. "Technician Montrose — it matches no registered hull. But its approach vector follows the long route. From the direction of the gray."

I stand very still in the middle of the command deck, coffee cooling in my hand, listening to a sentence I have waited six thousand nine hundred days to hear and never once believed I would.

"Say it again," I say.

"Inbound contact," VESPER says patiently. "From the direction of the gray."

Outside the viewport, the dark turns on its slow axis, indifferent, enormous, and for the first time in nineteen years I find I cannot name what I feel — except that it is not hope, exactly. Hope would be the silent worlds waking. This is something else. Something is *coming back* down the road everyone abandoned, and whatever it is, it has been out there where the charts go gray, where the first colony fleet went screaming into silence four centuries ago.

"Log it," I manage. "All channels. Wake me if anything changes."

"Technician Montrose," VESPER says, "you are not going to sleep."

She's right. I don't. I spend the night in the observation blister with the nav display lit, watching a single point of light crawl down the long route toward me — the first moving thing I've seen in nineteen years — and trying to remember how to be a man who might soon have company.

Fourteen hours. I watered the tomatoes twice.
