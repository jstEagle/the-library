## 5. What the Relays Heard

The Meridian's data core was stored in a compartment behind the galley, which told you everything you needed to know about survey ship design priorities. Fuel, water, food, and only then, somewhere down the list between spare gaskets and emergency flares, the entire recorded memory of your mission.

I had to stand on a counter to reach it. This was the indignity of my current existence: I was, by rank, experience, and accumulated scar tissue, the most senior officer in human space aboard this vessel, and I could not reach the top shelf.

"Captain," said Okonkwo — Dee now, eight years old, sitting cross-legged on the galley floor with a ration bar she was eating in the methodical way of someone who had once planned logistics for a crew of twenty-two and took feeding seriously — "do you want me to get that?"

"I want you to pretend this isn't happening," I said, and hauled the core down with both hands.

She laughed. It was a child's laugh, bright and unguarded, coming out of a woman who had once told me, deadpan, over the corpse of our navigation array, that we would laugh about this someday or we wouldn't laugh at all. The dissonance hadn't stopped being strange. I suspected it never would.

The data core slotted into the Meridian's analysis station with a satisfying clunk. The station itself was built for adult hands, so my feet dangled off the edge of the chair like a kid playing pilot, and if Ansel Montrose happened to walk past the open hatch and see me like that, well. He'd already seen me fall off a ladder yesterday. A captain builds her legend out of what survives.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about being eight again: your body is a stranger who has moved into your house and rearranged the furniture. My hands were too small for the console keys. My arms tired after twenty minutes of fine manipulation. When I concentrated too long, some ancient childhood habit surfaced — swinging my legs, chewing my lip, humming — behaviors I had spent thirty years training out of myself, all of them waiting patiently in the basement like furniture under sheets.

But my mind. My mind was intact. Mostly. That was the horror of it, when I let myself look at it straight: I remembered dying. I remembered the radiation wash off the pulsar, the alarms going soft and apologetic as systems failed one by one, the specific weight of knowing it was over. And then I remembered waking up small, in a ship that smelled of antiseptic and childhood, surrounded by my crew — my crew, all of them children now, all of them remembering their own deaths with the eerie calm of people who have already grieved themselves.

We had talked about it, the first night. Sat in a circle in the mess like a support group for the impossible, and compared notes. Nobody panicked. That was the strangest part. Panic requires a future to be afraid of, and we had, in some sense, already used ours up.

Now I pulled the flight data, because grief is a luxury and mystery is a job.

* * *

The Meridian's last survey leg had taken us through the Vesper Gate corridor — the string of relay stations threading the dead worlds, the network Ansel Montrose had tended alone for longer than anyone should tend anything alone. Our instruments had logged every relay as we passed. Standard procedure. Ping, listen, log, move on. The relays were supposed to be silent. They'd been silent for decades. That was the whole tragedy of the corridor: a nervous system with nothing left to feel.

Except.

I ran the raw telemetry three times before I let myself believe it, and then a fourth time because believing it and understanding it were different animals entirely.

Every relay we passed had spoken.

Not loudly. Not in any band anyone monitors anymore, because why would you monitor a graveyard? But buried in the noise floor of each relay's carrier wave — underneath the automated handshake protocols, beneath the maintenance chirps, in the layer of static that every engineer since the invention of radio has been trained to ignore — there was structure.

A pattern. Repeating. And here was the part that made my skin crawl in a body that apparently still knew how to crawl: the pattern changed. Relay to relay, as we passed, it shifted. Not randomly. Progressively. Like a sentence being spoken word by word across a chain of stations, timed — precisely, deliberately timed — to our passage.

We weren't overhearing something. We were being *read aloud*.

"Dee," I said. "Come look at this."

She climbed onto the adjacent chair, all elbows and gravity, and squinted at the waveform display. Even aged backward, she had the eyes of a woman who'd spent twenty years reading instrument panels. Thirty seconds of silence. Then, quietly, in her old voice — the one that surfaced sometimes, the adult cadence wearing a child's throat like an ill-fitting coat:

"That's not noise."

"No."

"That's *modulated*. Look at the interval spacing. It's prime-numbered." She traced the pattern with one small finger. "Who primes a noise floor?"

"Nobody," I said. "That's the point. It's a flag. It's saying: *this is not natural, pay attention.*"

Dee was quiet a moment. Then: "How long has it been doing this?"

That was the question, wasn't it. I pulled the historical logs — the Meridian's outbound records from six years ago, when we'd passed through the same corridor heading rimward. And there it was. Nothing. Clean static, honest static, the static of machines minding their own business in the dark.

The signal was new. Or newly awake. Either way, it had started sometime in the last six years, in a network where the only living human presence was one technician on one station at the far end, calibrating dead equipment and logging silence.

Logging silence. I thought about that phrase, which Ansel had used without irony, and felt the first cold thread of a suspicion I wasn't ready to pull.

* * *

I worked through what passed for night aboard the Meridian — the lights dimmed to simulate a cycle our bodies no longer trusted. Sleep came in shallow doses; eight-year-old bodies need more of it than they admit, and mine kept dragging me under while my mind clawed back up, insisting on one more pass through the data.

By "morning" I had the whole architecture laid out on the main display, and I sat looking at it the way you look at a diagnosis.

The signal propagated along the relay chain in sequence, but its origin point wasn't any relay. Tracing the timing offsets backward — accounting for light-lag, propagation delay, the drift in each station's clock — every thread converged on a single bearing. Out past the last mapped relay. Out past the last *anything*. Into the region the charts marked with a cartographer's euphemism: DEAD SPACE. As if naming it dead made it safe.

Something at the edge of that emptiness was speaking through the relays. Using them. The dead network wasn't dead; it was an instrument, and something had picked it up and learned to play it, and the first song it chose to play was a greeting timed to the passage of a survey ship full of people who were about to die.

Or — and this thought arrived with the gentleness of a knife — the song didn't greet us. The song *did* something to us. The de-aging, the impossible survival, the memories folded wrong: none of it accidental. None of it random. We passed through the corridor, the signal touched us, and we woke up younger with our futures still in our heads.

Cause and effect. Signal and response. I'd been treating our transformation as a catastrophe that had happened *near* a mystery. It was time to consider that it might be the mystery itself. That we weren't survivors of an anomaly. We were the anomaly's output.

"You've been up all cycle."

Ansel stood in the hatchway holding two bulbs of tea, and I realized I'd been talking to myself for the better part of an hour, narrating my findings aloud in the particular mutter of a scientist alone with a breakthrough. Embarrassing. I took the tea anyway.

"The relays are talking," I said. "All of them. In sequence. To us, or about us, or — possibly — *with* us. And it's coming from out there." I pointed at the display, at the convergence point floating in the charted void like a hole cut in the map. "Edge of dead space. Past everything."

He set his tea down unread and leaned into the display, and I watched him read it — really read it, the way he did everything, with the total unhurried attention of a man who had spent decades listening to things nobody else bothered to hear. His face did something complicated. Recognition, maybe. Or the look of a man hearing a sound he'd been half-hearing for years and finally having proof he wasn't mad.

"This modulation scheme," he said slowly. "The handshake layer. It's using the old maintenance protocol. Pre-collapse encoding. Nobody's used that in — " He stopped. Recalculated. "In sixty years. Maybe seventy."

"So whoever's speaking learned it from the relays themselves."

"From *my* relays." His voice had gone quiet and strange. "They've been listening to the maintenance chatter. For years. Learning the language from — " He laughed, short and hollow. "I talk to these stations. Every day. Status reports, calibration calls, log entries. Out loud, sometimes, when the silence gets thick. I've been broadcasting a one-man conversation into the dark for eleven years, and something out there has been *taking notes*."

There it was. The thread I'd been circling, pulled clean. I looked at him — this gaunt, gentle, stubborn man who had stayed behind when everyone else evacuated, who kept a dead network alive out of what I was beginning to suspect was less duty than devotion — and I understood, with cold clarity, exactly how large this was.

It hadn't found us by accident. It had found us because of him. Because one man refused to stop speaking into a silence everyone else had accepted, and something vast and patient at the edge of everything had heard him, and answered — not him first, but the next ship that came through. Us.

"Ansel," I said. "Your logs. All of them. I need every transmission Vesper Gate has sent or received in the last decade. Especially the ones you think don't matter."

"Especially those," he agreed grimly. "That's always where it lives." He was already moving toward the comm station, then stopped, turned back. Something vulnerable crossed his face and was filed away almost fast enough. Almost.

"In my future," I said, before I could stop myself — because it kept happening, these leaks from a life I remembered and he didn't, "you found this too. You're the one who found it. You traced it faster than I did." I smiled, and it hurt. "You always were better at listening than I am."

He looked at me for a long moment. Eight years old, sitting in a chair too big for me, telling him about a future he'd never lived. Anyone sane would have flinched away. Instead he said, softly:

"What happens to us? In the future you remember?"

And I opened my mouth to answer — and found, to my absolute horror, that I couldn't. There was a shape where the answer should have been. A warm, important, load-bearing shape. But the details were gone, smudged out like wet ink, and when I reached for them the reaching itself hurt, a dull ache behind my eyes like a memory physically tearing loose.

"I don't know," I whispered. "I knew this morning. I know I knew."

The display hummed behind us, patient, pointing outward. The signal waited at the edge of dead space, and it was taking things from us even now, even here, even at this distance — spending our pasts like currency, and I was starting to understand that whatever it wanted, it intended for us to pay our way forward.

Ansel put his hand briefly on my head — an absurd gesture, paternal and tender and completely wrong for what I remembered of us, and exactly right for what we currently were — and went to fetch his logs.

Eleven days. That was how long the jump window held, according to the orbital mechanics I'd have to confirm later. Eleven days to decide whether we followed that signal to its source, or sealed the corridor and let the dark keep its secret.

I looked at the coordinates glowing on the display, and for the first time since waking up small, I wasn't afraid.

I was *aiming*.
