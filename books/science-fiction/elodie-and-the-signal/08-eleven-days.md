## 8. Eleven Days

Day one of eleven began with me taking a plasma torch to my own home.

There's a particular kind of madness in that, and I want it on the record that I recognized the madness even as I committed it. Vesper Gate had been my whole world for nineteen years — its corridors, its hum, the particular way the coffee recycler gurgles at 0400 like an old man clearing his throat. And now I was stripping it for parts, harvesting the only place that had ever loved me back so I could patch up a ship full of children who used to be adults.

The station AI — I'd named her MARGOT years ago, because a voice that dry deserved a name with some warmth in it — objected immediately.

"Removal of the secondary antenna array will degrade local navigation capability by forty percent," she said.

"MARGOT, nobody's navigating here. The last ship through this corridor was a freighter that took a wrong turn in '61."

"Statistically improbable does not mean impossible."

"You know what's impossible? Getting to the edge of dead space without that array." I cut the last bolt free and felt the array come loose in my hands like a tooth. "Besides, if we're right about what's out there, you'll have more traffic than you can shake a sensor boom at."

MARGOT paused in that way she did when she was deciding whether to argue or simply log my stupidity for posterity. She logged it. She always logged it.

* * *

The Meridian was worse than I'd hoped and better than I'd feared.

Worse, because a survey ship that's been through whatever the Meridian went through doesn't just need repairs — it needs forgiveness. Her hull plating was scarred in patterns that didn't match any damage model I knew. Her fuel lines were intact but her fuel wasn't; whatever had happened to her crew had happened to her chemistry too, and half my first day aboard was spent running diagnostics on propellant that read, on three separate instruments, as being slightly older than it should have been.

Better, because children are lighter than adults, and every kilogram mattered.

That was the arithmetic I kept coming back to during those eleven days. Twenty-two passengers at thirty kilograms each instead of seventy-five. A captain who weighed less than her own flight suit had. The universe had taken something monstrous from these people and handed back, as a kind of insult, a margin.

I tried not to think of it as a margin. I mostly failed.

* * *

Teaching children to be passengers turned out to be the strangest work of my life, and I once spent six months teaching a cargo-handling AI to appreciate sarcasm.

They were good students. That was the unnerving part. They sat in the mess hall — my mess hall, which now smelled permanently of reconstituted protein and crayon, because someone had found crayons somewhere and I'd stopped asking where — and they listened like professionals. Because they were professionals. Eight-year-old hands folded on eight-year-old knees, and behind their eyes, forty-year-olds doing the math.

"The acceleration couches will take most of it," I told them, drawing the launch profile on a wall panel with a grease pencil. "But there'll be a stretch around the second relay where you'll feel like an elephant is sitting on your chest. That's normal. Breathe shallow, count backward from ten, and do not panic."

A boy named Okafor — he'd been the Meridian's engineer, and even at eight he couldn't stop fiddling with things — raised his hand. "What if the elephant is real?"

"Then we have bigger problems than the jump window."

Serious nods all around. That was the other unnerving part. No giggling. Children should giggle. These ones took notes.

Elodie stood at the back with her arms crossed, watching me teach, and I caught her doing it again — that thing where she looked at me like I was a photograph of someone she'd known. Not grief exactly. More like the ache you get hearing a song from a country you can't return to.

"You're good at this," she said afterward, in the corridor outside the mess.

"I've had nineteen years of talking to an audience of one, and she's a computer. Low bar."

"That's not true." She said it quickly, almost sharply. "In the — " She stopped herself. She always stopped herself there, at the edge of the future she remembered, like a person catching themselves before stepping off a ledge. "You're good at this," she said again, softer.

I wanted to ask. Every day I wanted to ask what she remembered, what version of me lived in her head, whether he was better than I was or just different. I never asked. Partly because it felt like reading someone's diary. Mostly because I was afraid of the answer.

* * *

Day four: I cannibalized the long-range comms booster and Elodie rewired it better than I could have.

That deserves unpacking. I'm a technician. I've spent two decades making dead machines whisper. But Elodie — eight years old, standing on a crate to reach the coupling, tongue between her teeth in concentration — handled circuitry like she'd been born holding a soldering iron, which, in a sense, she had been, just in a body that no longer existed.

"Hold that," she said, and I held it, and she ran a junction line with a steadiness that made my chest hurt.

"You know," I said, "most kids your age are learning fractions."

"I know fractions." A pause. "I knew calculus. It's still in here somewhere." She tapped her temple with the soldering iron — a safety violation that would have gotten any adult crew member written up — "like water behind ice. I can feel it but I can't pour it."

"What's it like? Forgetting?"

She didn't answer right away. When she did, she didn't look up from the board. "It's like the station before you fixed the relays. Everything's still there. It just doesn't answer anymore."

I held the coupling very still for a while after that.

* * *

By day six, the routine had a shape. Mornings: hull work, the two of us in vacuum suits or out on the gantry, passing tools and insults. Afternoons: passenger drills with the crew-children, evacuation routes and brace positions and the elephant-on-the-chest breathing exercise, which Okafor had started calling "the elephant prayer" and which had spread through the others like a benign virus. Evenings: the mess hall, twenty-two small people eating, and me and Elodie at the end of the table doing the day's math on napkins.

Fuel margins. Mass budgets. Window timing. Eleven days folding down into ten, into nine.

"You keep looking at me like that," she said one evening, not looking up from her napkin equations.

"Like what?"

"Like you're trying to memorize me."

I considered lying. I'd gotten decent at silence over the years, but lying to her specifically seemed like a bad investment. "Maybe I am. You said your memories are going fastest. Seems fair somebody keeps a copy."

Her pencil stopped moving.

"In my future," she said carefully, "you never said things like that."

"What did I say?"

"Nothing. That's the point. He was — " she searched for it, this child's face trying on adult frustrations like clothes cut for someone else — "kind. Careful. He never said anything that might cost him anything. I loved him anyway. I think I loved him partly because of it. But sitting here, eight years old, eating protein cubes with a man who makes jokes about his own loneliness?" She shook her head. "He wouldn't have said maybe I am."

"Maybe he would have, eventually. People change."

"He ran out of time to." She went back to her equations. "Don't be him. Be whoever this is."

I want to be honest about what that did to me. It broke something open that I'd welded shut a long time ago, and it terrified me, and I smiled anyway, because she was eight years old and grieving a marriage I'd never had, and someone had to be steady at this table.

"Deal," I said. "But whoever this is gets to be bad at feelings sometimes."

"Noted," she said. "Filed under existing conditions."

* * *

Day seven, MARGOT found something wrong with the Meridian's drive that nearly ended everything.

"A structural resonance in the main engine mount," she reported, her voice coming through the suit comm flat as ever. "Probability of catastrophic failure under sustained burn: significant."

"How significant?"

"I would prefer not to quantify it while children are listening."

The fix meant pulling a damping assembly out of Vesper Gate's own maneuvering thrusters — the last working piece of heavy machinery the station had left — and machining it down to fit. Fourteen hours of work. Elodie did the calculations; Okafor held lights and asked questions that were, frankly, graduate-level; I did the cutting, sweating inside a suit in a bay that got colder as the station gave away another piece of itself.

Somewhere around hour eleven, elbow-deep in coolant housing, I realized I was humming. Some old song, half-remembered. And Elodie was humming harmony.

"You know this song?" I asked.

"No," she said. "But you hummed it yesterday too. And the day before. Apparently it survives the translation."

"Survives what translation?"

"Whatever turned me into this." She gestured at herself, the whole impossible situation, with a wrench. "Everything important about you is getting through, Ansel. Even the parts I supposedly forgot. Especially those."

I didn't have anything to say to that, so I cut metal, and she hummed, and the elephant of the deadline sat on both our chests, and it was — I'll say it, since nobody else will — it was the best day I'd had in nineteen years.

* * *

Day nine, the memory loss arrived on schedule and knocked the legs out from under us.

It was little Vashti, the navigator, who flagged it — came to me in the corridor with tears she was furious about having, and reported, in the clipped diction of a career officer trapped in a child's body, that she could no longer remember her mother's face. Only that she had one. Only that it had mattered.

By evening, three more of them had lost names. Their own names, in two cases. We started writing identity tags and pinning them to shirts, and the sight of it — twenty-two children wearing laminated cards that said CHIEF ENGINEER and ASTROGATION and HELM — was either the saddest thing I'd ever seen or the most stubborn, and I decided it was the second one, because the first one would have stopped me working.

Elodie took it hardest, and hid it best.

"My turn soon," she told me on the gantry, night nine, both of us watching the stars do nothing, beautifully, the way they always did. "Vashti's losing her past. I'm losing my future. Fair's fair."

"Your memories of the future. Not — "

"Not yet." She said it plainly. "But the pattern's clear. It goes newest first, then deepest. The future goes, then the adult life, then..." She shrugged, small shoulders in a big suit. "Then whatever's underneath. Except you."

"Except me?"

"Except you." She turned to look at me, and in the gantry lights her face was eight years old and ancient at once. "I've tested it. Every night I run the inventory. My mother's maiden name — gone as of Tuesday. My commissioning date — gone. The taste of real coffee — gone, which is tragic. But you?" She tapped her sternum. "You're load-bearing. Whatever took everything else left you standing. I don't know if that means something or if it's just cruelty with a sense of humor."

"It might mean the important stuff sinks last."

"Or it might mean the important stuff is what gets taken last," she said. "Right before the end."

I had no argument for that. So I did the thing the man in her future apparently never did — I said the true thing out loud.

"Then I'll spend what's left of it well."

She looked at me for a long moment. Then she laughed — actually laughed, a child's laugh bursting out of an old soul, bright and startled — and said, "There he is. Whoever this is."

* * *

Day ten. Final checks. The Meridian sat in the docking cradle patched and ugly and alive, wearing Vesper Gate's donated organs like a transplant patient in ill-fitting clothes. Fuel: sufficient. Mass: within tolerance, barely. Passengers: drilled, tagged, frightened, brave.

I walked the station one last time that night, though I told myself it was a systems check. The empty rooms. The stripped bays. MARGOT's core, dimmed to standby, waiting out the decade alone with nothing but logs and silence.

"You'll keep the lights on minimum cycle?" I asked her.

"I will keep the lights on," MARGOT said, which was not the same thing, and we both let it go.

"One more thing," she added, as I reached the airlock. "Anomalous activity on the deep-band receiver. Origin: edge of dead space. Duration: intermittent. Content: none."

My stomach dropped through the deck plating. "None? Or none decoded?"

"Those categories have recently become difficult to distinguish." A pause, the length of which I did not enjoy. "Also: Crew member Okonkwo and crew member Reyes have been observed removing materials from the Meridian's stores and concealing them in the station's lower cargo ring. Estimated concealment time: three hours ago. I judged it relevant."

I judged it relevant too. Because I knew those names — the two oldest souls in the smallest bodies, the two who'd gone quietest during the drills, the two who'd voted, silently and with their eyes, against every departure rehearsal.

And because the lower cargo ring was where I'd stored the Meridian's backup navigation core.

Eleven days had become one, and somewhere below my feet, two children who used to be senior officers were taking the future apart piece by piece.
