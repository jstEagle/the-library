## 14. The Cost of Answering

The decoder finished at 0347 station time, which by then was a purely theoretical concept, since none of us had slept in anything resembling shifts and the Meridian's children kept hours that could best be described as feral.

I knew it was done because the console chimed — a soft, polite chime, the kind of sound that says *I have news* without specifying whether the news is a lottery win or a hull breach. I had learned, over twenty-two years of listening to machines, that the politeness of a chime is inversely proportional to the quality of what follows.

Elodie was asleep in the copilot's chair, curled up with her knees to her chest, one hand still resting on the data slate like she'd fallen asleep mid-sentence. She'd been working the translation matrices for fourteen hours straight, driving the Meridian's linguistics engine through the signal's folded structure with the patience of someone defusing a bomb one wire at a time. Eight years old. Fourteen-hour shift. In my experience, most adults couldn't manage that, and most adults hadn't captained a deep survey ship either.

I almost didn't wake her. I stood there for a good minute watching her breathe, thinking: whatever this says, once she reads it, we can't unread it. There's a version of tonight where we just don't look. Where we turn the ship around and go home and live out our lives not knowing, which is its own kind of mercy, the cheap kind, the kind you regret at three in the morning forever.

But she'd already read half of it before she fell asleep. And Elodie Voss did not strike me as a woman — or a girl — who would forgive being spared.

"Hey," I said, and touched her shoulder. "It's done."

She came awake the way she seemed to do everything: instantly, completely, no fog, no grogginess, eyes open and aimed at me like small searchlights. For a second she didn't know where she was — I saw it flicker across her face, that lost look the younger ones got when they surfaced from sleep into bodies that were wrong — and then it settled into recognition, and then something warmer than recognition, and then she covered all of it with captain's neutrality, which on an eight-year-old face looks less like authority and more like a kid determined to be taken seriously at show-and-tell.

It should have undercut her. It never did. That was the thing about Elodie that I don't think I ever managed to say out loud: the age was a costume she'd been stuffed into, and you could see the seams, and behind the seams she was exactly, precisely herself.

"What does it say?" she asked.

"It says," I said, pulling up the decoded text, "that we've been having a conversation with something that doesn't know it's killing us."

* * *

Here is what the linguistics engine pulled out of the signal, after fourteen hours of chewing on a message that had been folded through time like a letter hidden inside another letter:

It wasn't language, exactly. It was instructions. Or maybe a better word is *grammar*. The entity at the edge of dead space — the Listener, the kids had started calling it, and the name stuck because nobody came up with anything better and because it was true — had been broadcasting continuously for centuries, and what it broadcast was a description of itself. Not a greeting. Not a warning. A specification. The way a machine ships with a manual, except the manual was written in temporal gradients and the only copy was embedded in relay chatter across forty dead systems.

And the manual said, in essence:

*Everything that approaches me arrives earlier.*

That was the whole mechanism. No weapons, no malice, no intent in any direction we'd recognize as intent. The reversal field wasn't a defense. It was more like weather. The entity existed in a state where time moved toward it the way water moves downhill — everything slid backward as it approached, smoothly, inevitably, until it reached the source and became new. The de-aging of the Meridian's crew hadn't been an attack. It hadn't even been noticed, as far as we could tell. They had sailed into a current and the current had done what currents do.

"They died," Elodie said quietly, when we'd read it through twice. Her finger rested on the line of decoded text describing the field gradient. "In the corridor. I remember — I remember the reactor breach, I remember the radiation, I remember Okafor sealing the aft section with himself on the wrong side of it." Her voice didn't shake. It went very flat and very careful, the way voices go when shaking isn't affordable. "We died. All of us. And then we woke up here."

"Yeah," I said.

"So it put us back."

"It put you back *earlier*. Back to before the damage. Before—" I stopped, because the next part was the part that mattered, and I wanted to say it right. "Before the ages you are now. You're eight. You were thirty-something when the Meridian launched. The field didn't stop at repairing you. It kept going. It's probably still going, at some rate too slow to feel, and the closer you get to the source, the faster it runs."

Elodie looked down at her hands. Small hands, unscarred, the knuckles soft. I watched her flex them, testing them against her memory of other hands.

"Collateral kindness," she said. "Or collateral damage."

"That's what I said."

"I know." She glanced up. "You mutter when you work. You've been muttering for two days. I've started keeping a list."

There it was again — that disorienting intimacy, arriving out of order. She knew my habits from a future I'd never lived, and I was learning them fresh, and somewhere in the middle those two knowledge sets kept colliding like waves in a harbor, and neither of us ever quite knew which version of knowing we were standing in.

"The rest of it," I said, and pulled up the final section of the decode. "This is the part you're going to hate."

* * *

The rest of it was this: the entity wanted to talk.

Not in the way the old colony fleet had talked to it — though that was in the record too, buried deeper in the fold, the distress call from the first ships, four hundred years old, still unanswered in any way its senders would have understood. The entity had received that call the way a mountain receives rain. But now, apparently, something had changed. Something about the Meridian's passage — alive, structured, deliberate — had gotten its attention in a way four centuries of silence hadn't. The signal's outer layers, the ones timed to the relays, weren't a lure. They were an *invitation*, repeated patiently across decades of dead air, waiting for anyone to come and answer.

And the invitation included terms.

To communicate — really communicate, bidirectionally, at bandwidths that meant something — you had to enter the field. Deeper than the Meridian had gone. The entity's attention, its capacity to perceive and respond, scaled with proximity, and proximity scaled with reversal. There was a gradient curve in the decode, and I sat with it for a long time before I showed it to Elodie, because the curve was honest in the way mathematics is always honest, which is to say merciless.

At the boundary where meaningful communication began, a human adult aged backward at roughly a year per hour.

Deeper in, where full exchange became possible, the rate climbed past a year a minute.

And at the center — at the point where the entity's attention was total, where you could ask it anything and receive anything, where four hundred years of questions might finally get answers — the curve went vertical. At the center, you didn't grow younger. You arrived. You became new. You became, in the gentlest possible phrasing the math allowed, *unborn*.

"It's not a trap," Elodie said. We were in the Meridian's mess, because the mess had the biggest table and because the other nineteen children were there eating reconstituted stew, and Elodie had insisted on briefing them together, no exceptions, no decisions made over their heads. "I want everyone to understand that. It's not lying to us. It's not baiting us. It told us exactly what it is. It's just—" She searched for the word.

"A place where the rules are different," said Devi, who at nine was the oldest of them now and had appointed herself the group's translator of adult concepts. "Like walking into deep water. The water isn't attacking you. You just can't breathe there."

"Exactly like that," Elodie said. "Thank you, Devi."

A boy named Tomas — six, solemn, formerly the Meridian's chief engineer and currently missing most of his vocabulary for engines — raised his hand. "If we go close enough to talk," he said slowly, hunting each word, "we get small. Smaller than this?"

"Yes," Elodie said.

"And if we go all the way—"

"You don't go all the way," I said, before I could stop myself.

Twenty-two faces turned to me. Nineteen of them belonged to children. Two of them — Elodie's and Devi's — belonged to people who had been adults recently enough to recognize evasion when they heard it.

"You don't go all the way," I said again, softer. "Nobody goes all the way. That's not a plan, that's just dying with extra steps."

"With extra steps backward," Devi said, and there was a ripple of laughter, quick and startled, the kind children produce in the worst moments because their bodies haven't learned yet that grief is supposed to sit on laughter and keep it down. Even Elodie's mouth twitched. Then she made it stop.

"Ansel's right," she said. "The center is off the table. But I need everyone to hear the whole picture, because you're crew, and crew gets truth." She turned back to the table display and called up the gradient curve, glowing green and terrible above the stew. "Here's the whole picture. Somewhere along this curve is a distance where we can talk to it and survive the talking. Maybe. The math says the window exists. The math also says the window is narrow, and the margin for error is—" she checked the figure, though she knew it, I could tell she knew it, "—about four minutes of drift. Total."

Silence. Nineteen children looking at a graph that described their own erasure as a function of distance.

Then Priya, five years old, former xenobiologist, current owner of a stuffed rabbit she refused to explain, said: "But it's *listening*. Nobody's talked to it in four hundred years. Somebody should say hello."

And that — that was the moment I understood we were actually going to do this. Not because of the math. Because a five-year-old with a rabbit thought the vast thing at the edge of dead space deserved a hello, and every other person at that table nodded like she'd said the obvious.

Terror dressed as reunion. That was the shape of it. The entity wasn't offering us death; it was offering us *homecoming*, in the most literal sense — a return to beginnings, a folding-back into the warm dark before names. Every instinct in the human animal looked at that curve and felt something horribly like comfort. Come closer, the field whispered, in its wordless way. Come back. Be new. And the horror wasn't that it lied. The horror was that part of you wanted to believe it.

* * *

After the briefing, while the children were being herded toward bunks by Devi with the brisk tyranny of a nine-year-old den mother, Elodie stayed at the table, staring at the curve.

"Four minutes of drift," I said.

"Four minutes." She traced the green line with one finger, stopping well short of the vertical section, the way you'd keep your hand back from a stove. "Ansel. If I'm reading this right — and I am — then whoever aims the reply has to hold position inside the communication band manually. The Meridian's autopilot can't compensate fast enough at that gradient. Temporal shear plays hell with inertial damping. It'll have to be flown."

"Floated," I corrected. "Flown implies a cockpit. The comms array's external. Whoever holds the beam steady sits outside the hull, in the band, taking the field at communication depth."

She didn't say anything. She didn't have to. We both looked at the curve, at the region marked *comm band*, at the aging rate printed beside it in the linguistics engine's tidy, indifferent font.

One year per hour.

For an adult, four minutes of drift tolerance meant four minutes of margin before the drift became unrecoverable. For an adult holding station for the hours a transmission would take — the numbers stopped being survivable somewhere around minute ninety. I ran it twice hoping I'd made an arithmetic error. I hadn't. The universe, it turns out, checks your work.

"We'll figure it out," I said. "There's shielding configurations, there's—"

"Ansel." She said my name the way she always did when she was about to dismantle something I'd built, gently, the way you take apart a child's block tower so it can be built better. "Look at the curve again. Really look at it."

I looked. And I saw what she'd seen, and my stomach dropped through the deck plates and kept going.

The comm band's aging rate wasn't constant. It scaled with mass. With cellular turnover. With — there was no delicate way to put it — with how much life you had left to run backward. A body with fewer years in its ledger drained slower. The field took a percentage, not a sum.

An adult in the band, transmitting: dead in under two hours.

A child in the band, transmitting: hours of margin. Possibly enough. Possibly exactly enough.

"Oh no," I said. "No. Absolutely not. Don't even—"

"I haven't said anything yet."

"You're saying it with your *face*."

"My face," Elodie said, "is the face of a captain doing arithmetic," and she turned the display toward me, and there it was in the linguistics engine's tidy font, and there was nowhere for either of us to hide from it.

Outside the viewport, far off, the dark between stars seemed slightly darker than it should have been — the direction we were pointed, the direction we'd been pointed for eleven days, the direction of the thing that had waited four centuries for someone to say hello, and that had told us, honestly, patiently, in the grammar of gradients, exactly what the hello would cost.
