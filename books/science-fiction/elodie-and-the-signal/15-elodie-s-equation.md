## 15. Elodie's Equation

The numbers woke her before the alarm did.

Elodie had fallen asleep in the survey lab with the Meridian's flight data still scrolling on the display, her cheek pressed against a console that hummed at the frequency of a machine thinking. She was eight years old, and she had dreamed of nothing, which was itself unusual — for weeks now, sleep had been a corridor of futures, each one ending in the same white room where Ansel Montrose stood with his hands open and his face full of grief he wouldn't explain.

Tonight: nothing. Just dark, and then the numbers, patient as tide.

She sat up on the cot they'd given her — too big, everything aboard Vesper Gate was too big now, built by and for people who had been allowed to keep their height — and pulled the data slate onto her knees. The screen's glow caught the fine hairs of her arms. Child's arms. She had stopped flinching at them sometime around day four.

*Run it again,* she told herself, the way she used to tell herself things when she was forty-one and commanding a ship through a debris field with half her thrusters gone. *Slowly. Slowly is how you find the lie.*

The signal from the edge of dead space had resolved over the past two days into something almost legible. Not language — not yet, maybe never — but structure. A lattice of pulses folded through time the way a hand folds paper, each crease a moment that had already happened being offered again. The entity — Ansel called it *the listener*, and the name had stuck because it was kinder than anything else they could have called it — did not speak forward. It spoke backward. Every message it sent arrived before it was sent, wrapped around events like a hand around a stone.

And every message it sent carried a cost.

She had watched the de-aging curve a hundred times now, plotted against distance from the boundary. It wasn't linear. That was the first thing she'd understood, lying awake in a body that fit her badly, listening to the small sounds of twenty-two children breathing in the berthing compartment down the corridor. The reversal field didn't fade gently with distance. It fell off like a cliff — steep, steep, steep, and then flat. There was a shell around the thing at the edge of dead space, a boundary layer where time ran backward between seconds, and beyond that shell, space was merely space. Ordinary. Forgiving.

They had crossed that shell without knowing it. The Meridian had died out there — she remembered dying, remembered the cold coming up through the deck plates and into her chest like water finding its level — and something vast and patient had gathered them up and poured them back down their own hourglasses. Collateral kindness. Collateral damage. The distinction, Elodie suspected, mattered mostly to the ones being unmade.

*So don't cross the shell.*

It seemed obvious once she thought it. Everything obvious is obvious only after someone bleeds for it.

She pulled up the field model Ansel had built — he was better at this than she was, or had been; her astrophysics was eroding along with everything else, draining away in the night like bathwater, and what remained was instinct wearing the costume of knowledge — and she began to work the problem the way she'd worked problems her whole adult life, which was to say: stubbornly, on insufficient sleep, with a stylus she kept losing in the folds of a jumpsuit three sizes too large.

The question was simple. Could you answer the listener without entering the reversal field?

Not flee it. Not silence it. *Answer* it. Because silencing it was no longer on the table — they had tried to model that too, and every path to shutting the relay down passed through the same shell, the same backward-running seconds, the same dissolution. And answering it felt right in a way Elodie couldn't defend mathematically but trusted anyway, the way she trusted a heading more than a map. The thing at the edge had spent centuries holding a human distress call like a cupped palm holds a moth. It hadn't crushed it. It had *kept* it. You didn't silence something like that. You introduced yourself.

But an answer required transmission, and transmission required proximity — or so the first models said. The signal was faint, folded through time, and to unfold it — to reply in kind — you needed to stand inside the field where time bent, where your words could be creased into the past as well as the future.

Except.

Except the field wasn't uniform. She'd seen it in the flight data and dismissed it as noise, and then seen it again last night and dismissed it as wishful thinking, and now, at three in the morning station-time, with a child's heart beating quick and light in her chest, she stopped dismissing it.

There were gaps in the shell.

Not holes — seams. Places where the reversal gradient thinned to nearly nothing, narrow corridors of ordinary time threading through the boundary layer like veins of quartz in granite. They moved. They drifted along the shell's curvature, opening and closing on timescales of minutes, the way pressure systems opened and closed over an ocean. But they existed. And one of them — she checked the timing three times, then a fourth, because hope made a poor mathematician — one of them would pass directly across the Meridian's approach vector eleven days from now, during the jump window, and hold open for approximately ninety seconds.

Ninety seconds of ordinary time, threaded through the edge of unmaking.

Inside that seam, a transmitter could fire a tightly focused, tightly aimed burst — a needle, not a shout — along the seam's axis, deep into the field, without the ship or its crew ever crossing the threshold. A message delivered by letter-slot instead of by walking through the door.

Elodie sat very still, the slate glowing against her knees, and let herself feel the shape of it: *a way through.* For perhaps ten seconds she simply breathed, eight years old and older than she had ever been, while somewhere below her feet the station's ancient systems ticked through their endless maintenance of a silence nobody had asked them to keep.

Then the second half of the equation arrived, the way the second half always arrives: late, unwelcome, and correct.

Aim.

The seam was ninety seconds wide and drifting. To hit it — to put a focused burst down a corridor of ordinary time narrower than a ship's airlock — the transmitting array would have to be aimed manually, continuously, corrected in real time against the seam's drift. No automated system aboard the Meridian could do it; the targeting computers were forty years old and had been designed to track comets, not cracks in causality. It would have to be a person. A person at the external array housing, on the hull, with a manual alignment rig and their hands on the controls and their eyes on the drift readout, making micro-corrections second by second for ninety seconds while the ship held position at the very lip of the shell.

At the lip of the shell.

Elodie pulled up the radiation model and felt the floor of the thought give way beneath her.

The boundary layer didn't just reverse time. It *leaked*. Where ordinary spacetime ground against the reversed, the friction threw off particles that had no business existing — she'd found them in the Meridian's hull logs, exotic decay signatures the survey team's instruments had flagged and filed under *anomaly, unresolved* back when the crew had been adults with careers ahead of them. Radiation, yes, but radiation with teeth. The dose rate at the shell's lip, outside the seam, in the region where the array operator would have to sit with her hands on the manual rig —

She ran the number. Ran it again. Checked the units twice, because a wrong unit was the most common way good people killed themselves with arithmetic.

An adult human, in a standard EVA suit, at that exposure, for ninety seconds: fatal. Not eventually-fatal, not years-later-fatal. Fatal *now*, cellular architecture collapsing mid-task, hands failing before the burst fired, a corpse drifting at the edge of dead space with the message unsent. The suit would protect against heat and vacuum and nothing else. Nothing else mattered there.

Her first thought — the professional thought, the captain's thought, the thought she was proud of — was: *then we don't do it. We turn around. We go home. Eleven days is enough to reach the inner relays, and the inner relays are enough to reach inhabited space, and we file a report and let a fleet come with shielding and robots and time.*

Her second thought arrived before the first had finished, and it was smaller, and quieter, and it wore her own voice:

*But I'm not an adult anymore.*

She looked down at her hands. Small hands. Hands whose cells were new, recently turned over, freshly minted by whatever process had reached into the Meridian and run its crew backward through themselves. Child tissue was different. Not magic — she refused magic; she had lost too much to magic to grant it any jurisdiction — but different. Higher cellular turnover. More active repair pathways. DNA that had recently demonstrated, empirically, an ability to be rewritten and survive. The de-aging hadn't just shrunk them. It had *renovated* them, cell by cell, and the renovation was recent, and the radiation damage that would kill a forty-year-old body in ninety seconds might — *might*, the word was load-bearing, she made herself look at it squarely — might buy a child's body a fighting chance. Damage, yes. Burns, sickness, maybe worse. But survivable. Possibly. If the exposure stayed under some threshold she could calculate but not verify, if the seam held its full ninety seconds, if the suit held, if the drift corrections stayed within tolerance, if, if, if.

The word *might* was load-bearing, and underneath it, holding up the whole cathedral, was another word she didn't want to read: *me*.

Because there were twenty-two children aboard, and she was the only one who remembered being a captain, and the calculation didn't care about rank — it cared about mass, and cellular age, and exposure geometry — but command did. Command was the thing she had left when everything else drained away. Her astrophysics went, and her navigation went, and the names of her officers went, and the taste of the wine she'd drunk at her retirement dinner went, but *command* stayed, sitting in her small chest like a coal that wouldn't cool. She could not order a child to do this. She could not ask one. She could not even let one volunteer, because volunteering was a thing children learned from adults, and these children had learned it from her, and that made every raised hand hers.

Which left, with a neatness she distrusted deeply, one candidate.

She got up. The deck was cold through her socks. In the corridor, the station lights ran their slow cycle, dimming toward the false night that Ansel had programmed decades ago and never had reason to change. She walked toward the observation blister without deciding to, her feet remembering routes her mind had already surrendered, and found him there — of course she found him there; he was always either in the relay core or here, at the window, keeping his vigil over the dark.

Ansel Montrose stood with his hands clasped behind his back, watching the stars do their patient, indifferent work. He heard her come in — he always heard her; she'd stopped wondering how — and turned, and his face did the thing it always did when he saw her: a flicker of recognition so heavy with borrowed grief that it took her breath. He looked at her the way you look at a photograph of someone you loved who has since changed beyond knowing.

"You've been working," he said. "I can see it. You get this line—" he touched the space between his own brows, "—right there."

"I need you to check my math," Elodie said. "And then I need you to hate me for it."

He didn't laugh. He knew her well enough now — nine days of shared work, of her mourning a version of him she'd loved in a future that no longer existed, of him learning to be known by a stranger wearing a child's face — to know that she wasn't joking.

She showed him the slate. She talked him through it: the seams, the ninety-second window, the needle-burst, the manual aim. His face moved through wonder and dread in the same order hers had, and she watched him arrive at the radiation model and stop, the way she had stopped, the way anyone would stop at the foot of that particular wall.

"Fatal," he said quietly. "For an adult. Ninety seconds at the lip, that's—"

"That's a coffin with a view," Elodie said. "Yes. I know. I ran it first."

"And for a child—"

"Might." She held up a hand before he could speak. "I know what *might* weighs. I'm the one carrying it. But Ansel — listen to me. Listen to me the way you'd listen to a briefing, not the way you listen to me, because those are different and I need the first one right now." She stepped closer, and the size difference between them — he was tall, and she came up to his elbow, and the universe had arranged this meeting of minds with a cruelty it probably considered whimsy — made her tilt her head back to hold his eyes. "Nobody else can do this. Not the crew — they're children, real children now, and half of them have forgotten how to read a drift readout. Not you — you're the pilot, the engineer, the only adult left, and if you die at that array the mission dies with you and so does everyone on this ship. Not a robot — we don't have one that can aim, and we can't build one in eleven days. It's a person, at the lip, with their hands on the rig. And the only person whose body can plausibly survive the dose is a body that was recently taken apart and put back together by the thing we're trying to talk to."

His jaw tightened. "You're describing yourself."

"I'm describing the only viable operator," Elodie said, with the precision she used when precision was all that was holding her upright. "The fact that the only viable operator is me is a coincidence of biology. I'm choosing to treat it as one."

"It's not a coincidence," Ansel said. "You know it isn't. You worked the problem until the answer was you. That's not math, Elodie. That's—"
