## 20. What Was Forgotten

The airlock cycled, and the first thing I felt was cold.

Not the clean cold of vacuum — I'd left that outside, folded into the suit like a letter nobody would read. This was a different cold, the kind that lives inside a person, the kind that comes from somewhere you can't point to. It started in my chest and worked outward, and by the time the inner door opened I was shaking so hard that two pairs of small hands caught me under the arms and carried me the rest of the way in.

I remembered the suit. I remembered the array. I remembered the walk itself, mostly — the hull under my boots, the stars bending wrong at the edge of everything, the way my own voice sounded strange in my ears as I talked to someone over comms, someone patient, someone whose name was—

*Someone whose name was.*

And there it stopped. There was a shape where a name should have been, the way there's a shape on a wall where a picture hung for years. I could see the outline of it. The pale rectangle of absence. I knew someone had talked me across that hull. I knew their voice had been the last thing I heard before the radiation took the future first and then started on the rest, peeling my memories off in reverse like wallpaper. I knew — because I had known, before, in a life I could no longer fully account for — that I had loved them.

The knowing was all that was left. The love had survived. The person had not.

They laid me on the med-bay cot, and I looked up at the stranger who was weeping.

He was tall, hollow-cheeked, with the kind of face that had been handsome once and had decided, somewhere along the way, to stop maintaining itself. His hands were shaking as he pulled the helmet off my suit, and when my hair came free and spilled across the cot he made a sound — a small, broken sound, like a hinge on a door that had been opened too many times.

"You did it," he said. "Elodie. You did it. You're here."

"I know," I said, because my throat hurt and it seemed like the efficient thing to say. "The field stilled. I felt it stop. Like the whole universe took a breath and decided to let me keep it."

He laughed, or tried to, and it came out as something else, and he pressed the back of his hand against his mouth to hold it in. His eyes were red-rimmed and wet and absolutely, unbearably full of something.

And I looked at him the way you look at a word in a language you almost speak. Familiar in its architecture. Foreign in its meaning.

"Why are you crying?" I asked.

It was not a cruel question. I want that on the record, wherever records go when the universe is done with them. I asked it gently, the way you'd ask someone why they're laughing at a funeral — with the understanding that grief and relief live in the same apartment and share a wall. But I watched the question land on him, and I watched it land like a knife.

He didn't answer at first. He busied himself with the med scanner, running it over my arms, my temples, and I watched the numbers climb and climb on the readout — radiation absorbed, cellular damage, the arithmetic of what I'd spent out there — and I watched him read those numbers and decide, visibly, not to say them out loud.

"Elodie," he said. "Do you know who I am?"

And here is the thing I will never be able to explain properly, not to him, not to myself, not to whatever vast patient thing at the edge of dead space is even now listening to humanity's first hello: I *did* know who he was. Ansel Montrose. Technician, last human crew of Vesper Gate, the man who'd cannibalized his own home to patch our ship, who'd taught twenty-two eight-year-olds to be passengers, who'd stayed behind at a station nobody needed because leaving would have meant admitting the silence had won.

I knew all of that the way I knew the Meridian's registry number. Facts. Inventory.

What I did not know was why his name made my chest ache.

"No," I said. "Yes. I mean — I know *of* you. I know you're Ansel. I know you stayed at Vesper Gate for eleven years and talked to the automated voices and logged the silence every day like it was a prayer. I know you gave up your bunk and your spare parts and, I'm fairly sure, your entire life for us." I paused. "I don't know why looking at you feels like reading the last page of a book first."

Something moved across his face — I want to say it was hope, but hope that has been dropped enough times learns to flinch. "That's more than I expected," he said quietly.

"Is it?"

"The price," he said, and stopped. Started again. "The signal. Answering it. It cost something. We knew it would. One of us was going to lose—" His jaw worked. "It was supposed to be a choice between two memories. Yours or mine. You found a third option. You always found a third option."

"Did I?"

"You did," he said, and his voice cracked clean through the middle of the word, and he turned away to fuss with the scanner again, and I lay there on the cot in my eight-year-old body that had walked a hull no adult could survive, and I thought: *I loved this man. I know I did. The evidence is everywhere. The evidence is the hole itself.*

* * *

The crew came to see me in shifts, because I'd ordered shifts — old habit, apparently, the command reflexes surviving in me like the last green shoots after a fire. They filed in small and solemn, twenty-two children in oversized station clothes, and each of them looked at me with an expression I recognized because I was wearing it too: the look of people checking whether someone else had lost the same thing they had.

Tomas, who had been my navigator and was now a boy who could still plot a jump in his head but couldn't remember his mother's face, sat on the edge of my cot and studied me with grave brown eyes.

"You don't remember him at all?" he asked.

"I remember that I was supposed to."

"That's the worst part," Tomas said. "I keep remembering that I used to remember. It's like being handed an empty box with *fragile* written on the side."

"Poetic," I said.

"I've had a lot of time to think. Being eight is mostly waiting."

He was quiet for a while, and then he said, "He sat outside this med-bay for the entire hull walk. Four hours. He didn't move. Priya brought him water and he didn't drink it. When you went silent for those ninety seconds near the array, he made a sound I hope I never hear again." Tomas looked at his hands. "I don't remember my adult life very well anymore. But I remember that."

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because you told me once — I think it was you, I think it was before — that a crew runs on shared memory. That when one person forgets, the others carry it for them." He shrugged, small shoulders under a too-big shirt. "So I'm carrying it. Somebody has to remember you two, and you can't, and he won't talk about it, so."

I reached out and took his hand. It was a child's hand, small and warm, and holding it felt like holding an anchor to a ship that had already drifted.

"What am I supposed to do?" I asked him. "I look at him and I feel — something. It's like a phantom limb. It hurts in a place that isn't there anymore. But there's nothing *in* it, Tomas. There's no history. There's no — I don't know what we were to each other. And he's walking around this ship looking at me like I'm a ghost, and I can't even haunt him properly."

"You could start," Tomas said, "by not calling him a stranger to his face. I saw you do it in the corridor. He went into the galley and didn't come out for an hour."

I winced. "I called him that?"

"You said, and I quote, 'Thank you, stranger.'"

"Oh no."

"He made a joke about it later. It was a terrible joke. Nobody laughed. It was the bravest thing I've ever seen."

* * *

He found me in the observation blister three days later. I'd taken to sitting there during the ship's night hours, watching the relays wake up as we passed them — one by one, the dead lights of the network flickering back to life in our wake, like a corridor of candles being lit behind us. The entity's gift, Ansel called it. The entity's *apology*, I would have said, if I'd had anyone to say it to who'd understand.

He didn't ask if the seat beside me was taken. He just sat down in it, the way you sit in a seat you've sat in a thousand times, and then caught himself doing it, and went very still.

"I'm sorry," he said. "Habit."

"It's your ship too. You cannibalized your home for it."

"That's different. Cannibalizing is easy. Sitting is hard." He looked out at the stars, at the wrong-bent light at the edge of the viewport, and I watched him gather himself the way you'd gather spilled water — carefully, knowing most of it would slip through. "I'm not going to tell you," he said.

"Tell me what?"

"What we were. What you gave up. I decided on the hull — no. Before the hull. When we were arguing about it, and you told me your memory of me was already the price paid, and you'd rather spend yourself on purpose than fade by accident." His voice was steady. It was the steadiness of a man holding a door closed against a flood. "If I tell you, I make you carry a grief for something you can't even miss. That's not a gift. That's a debt. So I'm not going to do it. I wanted you to know that. So you'd stop waiting for the other shoe."

I turned that over. Somewhere inside me, in the hollow where the picture used to hang, something ached and ached.

"That's the cruelest kind thing anyone's ever done for me," I said.

"I know." He almost smiled. "I've been practicing the speech for three days. I had a whole thing prepared about respecting your autonomy. You would have hated it."

"Did I hate your speeches?"

"Violently," he said, and then his face did the thing it did — the flinch, the drop, the man falling into the hole of his own sentence. "Did. I don't know. I'm told you did."

"Ansel." I said his name deliberately, watching it land. "Tell me one thing. Not what we were. Just — one thing. One small thing. The smallest true thing you've got."

He was quiet for a long time. Out in the dark, another relay woke up, a pinprick of gold in the black, and died behind us into light.

"When you were arguing to take the hull walk," he said finally, "you told me that in the future you remembered, I forgave myself. For something. For staying at Vesper Gate. For a lot of things." His hands were folded in his lap like a man at a funeral. "And I didn't believe you. And then you walked out that airlock to prove it, and I believed you, and now you're sitting here and you don't remember saying it, and I'm the only person in the universe who knows that you did." He looked at me. "That's my smallest true thing. You believed in a version of me that I hadn't become yet. And then you forgot him on my behalf. So I figure I owe it to you to become him."

The hollow in my chest didn't fill. Nothing could fill it; I understood that now the way you understand weather. But standing in it, listening to this stranger make a promise to a woman who no longer existed, I felt the ache change key. From grief toward something else. Something with a future tense in it.

"I don't remember loving you," I said. "But I remember that I did. And I've decided that counts for something. It counts as a down payment."

"On what?"

"I have no idea," I said. "I've never done this before. Neither, I suspect, have you — not with me, not from this side of it. So we'll be bad at it together. We'll be strangers who are bad at being strangers." I stuck out my hand, absurdly formal, a captain's handshake offered by a child's arm from a med-bay blanket. "Elodie Voss. Captain of the Meridian. I'm told I'm trustworthy. I have it on excellent authority."

He looked at my hand. He looked at me. And I watched him make a choice — watched him set down the grief he was carrying for both of us, just for a moment, just long enough — and take my hand in his.

"Ansel Montrose," he said. "I'm told I'm stubborn. I have it on terrible authority."

His hand was warm. His hand was warm and I felt the phantom limb ache all the way down to the fingers, and I did not understand it, and I held on anyway.

Behind us, in the dark, the relays kept waking up, one by one, all the way home — and in the morning, when the navigation alarm sounded, it wasn't the entity's light we found waiting at the edge of the corridor.

It was a beacon. Human-made. Old frequencies.

It was Vesper Gate, and it was calling us by name.
