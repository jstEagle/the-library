## 4. War in the Long Dark

The Meridian Gap did not look like a graveyard from the outside. That was the cruelty of it.

From the bridge of the *Corvane*, Captain Reza Voss watched the debris field resolve out of the dark in slow increments — first a glitter like frost on glass, then shapes, then shapes with edges she recognized. A cargo hauler split lengthwise along its spine, both halves still holding formation with each other as if the intervening years were a misunderstanding that could be corrected. A gunship's engine nacelle, scorched black, tumbling with the patient dignity of a thing that had forgotten it was ever attached to anything. And everywhere, everywhere, the small stuff: hull plating, coolant ice, personal effects too small to identify at this range but large enough to have been loved once.

Eleven years since the Battle of Meridian Gap. The war had moved on to a dozen other gaps and straits and shoals of vacuum, but the dead stayed where they'd been killed. Space was loyal that way.

"Convoy status," she said, because ritual was also armor.

Ensign Okonkwo answered without looking up from her board. "Fourteen barges, Captain. All nominal. Life support green across the board. Six thousand two hundred and eleven souls."

Six thousand two hundred and eleven. Reza repeated the number silently, the way her mother had taught her to repeat the names of the dead — not to memorize them, exactly, but to keep them moving through her like blood, so they wouldn't pool anywhere and rot. The barges carried colonists out of the Cassini Reach, where the front had collapsed three months ago and the Vessel-born had taken the orbital habitats without firing more than a courtesy of shots. The refugees had left everything. Houses, hydroponics, heirlooms, graves. Some of them had left people, though no one talked about that on the barges. On the barges they talked about soil pH and school schedules and whether the new colony would have real weather or simulated weather, as if the future were a place you could pre-furnish.

Reza knew better. The future was a corridor through a debris field, and you walked it with your weapons hot and your heart locked down, and sometimes the corridor closed behind you before anyone you loved was through.

"Escort spread is holding at four hundred klicks," said Lieutenant Amara Dey from tactical, her voice clipped and clean. "No contacts. No emissions. It's quiet."

"It's always quiet," Reza said. "That's what I don't trust."

Dey almost smiled. Almost. There was a version of this war where officers smiled at each other, but Reza hadn't served in it.

She settled back into the command chair and let the bridge do its work around her. The *Corvane* was an aging frigate, older than the war itself, held together by maintenance crews who treated her less like a ship than like an elderly relative with strong opinions. Her engines hummed at a frequency Reza could feel in her molars. She had commanded her for six years, and in six years the ship had never once surprised her, which was more than Reza could say for any person she'd known.

Her eyes went, as they did every few minutes, to the navigation display, where a small amber marker tracked their progress through the Gap. And near the marker, as it always was, a second notation: a coordinate, fixed, unchanging, flagged with a code she had entered herself eleven years ago and never deleted.

*VSL-771. Hull fragment, unidentified colonial registry. Recovered remains: one (1).*

Her brother's ship had died here. Not all of him had been recovered. What had been recovered had gone home in a box the size of a loaf of bread, and her mother had held the box the entire length of the funeral and afterward had never entirely put it down, in some way Reza couldn't name but could see in the way her mother's hands moved, always slightly cupped, always slightly careful, as if carrying something invisible and breakable.

Tomas Voss. Third officer aboard the patrol cutter *Kestrel*. Twenty-six years old. He had laughed like a door coming off its hinges, and he had believed — genuinely, stupidly, beautifully believed — that the war would be over inside a year, because wars were mistakes and mistakes got corrected. He had written her letters right up until the end. She still had forty-one of them. She had read them so many times she could recite passages, and there were nights when she did, alone in her quarters, like a woman praying in a language whose god had stopped listening.

*Dear Reza. Today we escorted a grain barge and nothing happened, which is my favorite kind of day. I met one of the Vessel-born once, did I tell you? At the armistice talks on Halcyon Station, before your time. They don't look like monsters, Reza. They look tired. They look like us, if we'd been tired for a very long time. I know that's not what they tell you at the Academy. But I stood three meters from one and it looked at me like it was trying to remember something, and I have thought about that look every day since—*

The letter ended there, mid-thought, because the next day he'd shipped out and the letter had gone into a courier pouch and he'd never gotten around to finishing it. Reza had spent years imagining the rest of the sentence. *Every day since* — since what? Since the armistice? Since the look? Since the war started again and remembering became treason?

She would never know. That was the arithmetic of the Meridian Gap: one unfinished sentence, weighed against six thousand two hundred and eleven souls currently under her guns, and the scales didn't tip, they just kept swinging.

* * *

They made the deep transit at third watch, running dark except for the barges' essential systems, which glowed on Reza's tactical plot like a string of lanterns someone had hung across a canyon. The escort ships fanned wide. The Gap narrowed ahead, threading between two shattered moons that had killed each other in the war's opening hours — no one remembered which side had done what; the moons had simply been in the way, the way most things in this war were simply in the way.

Reza took her tea on the bridge because leaving the bridge during transit felt like desertion, even though nothing had happened in the Gap in nine years. The tea was synthetic assam, bitter at the edges, and she drank it from the dented cup Tomas had given her at her commissioning, because grief, like the dead, stays where you put it.

"Captain." Okonkwo's voice changed register, and Reza was already setting down the cup. "Passive array just picked up something. Faint. Bearing oh-two-seven relative, deep in the northern debris sheet."

"Nature?"

"That's the thing, ma'am. It's not an emission profile we have on file. It's—" Okonkwo frowned at her repeater. "It's on the old common band."

The bridge went quiet in the particular way bridges go quiet, which is to say all the small sounds continued and all the human ones stopped. Reza set her cup down with exaggerated care.

"The old common band has been restricted for thirty years," she said. "By order of Fleet Command, any transmission detected on that band is presumed hostile. Standing orders apply."

"Yes, ma'am." Okonkwo swallowed. "Standing orders are weapons-free on confirmation. I'm asking for confirmation parameters, Captain. Because the signal strength is — it's barely above noise. It could be a ghost. Old equipment, a derelict power cell, resonance off the debris. Or it could be a trap. The Vessel-born know our rules. They know what band we're ordered to kill."

"They do," Reza agreed.

The signal pulsed again, and this time the passive array rendered it onto the bridge audio, filtered and cleaned, and it filled the bridge like weather. Not words. Not yet. A pattern of tones, rising and falling, structured — Reza's ear, trained on thirty years of signal doctrine, heard structure the way a carpenter hears a load-bearing wall — but alien in its cadence, its intervals falling in places human music didn't fall. It sounded, absurdly, like someone singing underwater. Like a lullaby played backward through a wall.

"Record it," Reza said. "Full spectrum. Don't transmit, don't answer, don't jam yet. I want the whole convoy past the narrows before we make any decisions about what this is."

Dey turned from tactical, and Reza saw the question forming on her face and answered it before it could become insubordination. "Yes, Lieutenant, I know what standing orders say. Standing orders also say I confirm before I fire, and I am confirming. Slowly. If it's a ghost, I'm not going to start a battle over static. If it's a trap, it'll still be a trap in ninety minutes."

"And if it's neither?" Okonkwo asked, very quietly, the way junior officers ask things they shouldn't.

Reza looked at the plot. Fourteen lanterns strung across a canyon. Six thousand two hundred and eleven souls. One unfinished sentence, eleven years cold, buried in the northern debris sheet somewhere within a few thousand kilometers of whatever was singing.

"If it's neither," she said, "then it's the most dangerous thing in this system, and we'll deal with that after everyone is safe. Nav, take us through. Weapons, hold fire. Nobody breathes wrong."

* * *

The narrows took four hours and cost Reza a decade of her life in installments.

Nothing fired. Nothing moved. The singing — because by the second hour the whole bridge crew had privately begun calling it that, though officially it remained *the signal* — rose and fell and rose, patient as tide. Twice it seemed to shift, subtly, in ways Reza couldn't quantify but felt in her sternum, the way you feel a chord change in a song you half-remember from childhood. Once, horribly, she caught herself humming along — not the melody, she couldn't have reproduced the melody, but the rhythm, the breathing underneath it — and she stopped so abruptly that Dey glanced over, and Reza stared at her tea until the moment passed.

At 0440 shipboard, the last barge cleared the narrows and the convoy began its long climb out of the Gap's gravity well toward open space. Reza gave the order to come about, and the *Corvane* turned her battered face back toward the debris field, toward the signal, toward the question.

"Range to source?" she asked.

"Three hundred kilometers and closing," Dey reported. "Signal's stronger now. Still no active emissions besides the broadcast itself. Whatever's transmitting, it isn't hiding and it isn't maneuvering."

"Put it on main viewer. Maximum magnification."

The forward screen bloomed with starfield, then resolved. At first Reza thought it was another wreck — the northern sheet was full of them, and her eye had long ago learned to sort wreckage into categories the way other people sorted clouds. But this shape was wrong for wreckage. Too symmetrical. Too intact.

It was a buoy. An ancient one, by its hull form — a design Reza knew only from museum schematics, from the era before the colonies and the Vessel-born had stopped sharing engineering standards, before the common band had been cut like a cable between two houses that used to be one house. Its casing was pitted with micrometeorite scarring. Its antenna was a lattice of struts taller than the *Corvane*'s keel. And bolted to its flank, legible even at this range because someone had wanted it legible, was a plate bearing two scripts: colonial standard, and the flowing, vertical script of the Vessel-born.

Both scripts read the same thing. Reza felt the words land in her chest like a dropped tool.

*THIS STATION SPEAKS FOR BOTH.*

"Captain," Okonkwo whispered. "The beacon's transponder code. It's registered. It's in the fleet database. Commissioned under—" she paused, checked again, visibly disbelieving her own console, "—commissioned under a colonial charter, ma'am. Pre-war. The commissioner of record is listed as..."

Another pause. Long enough that Reza turned.

"Listed as whom, Ensign?"

"Ishikawa," Okonkwo said. "Commissioner of record: M. Ishikawa. But ma'am — the record's flagged. Sealed by order of Fleet Command, thirty-one years ago. There's no further data. It's like the person was..." She searched for the word and found the wrong one and used it anyway. "...deleted."

Reza sat with that. Ishikawa. The name meant nothing to her, and yet the sealed flag meant everything, because Fleet Command did not seal records for clerical errors. Fleet Command sealed records the way people sealed rooms in dying houses — quietly, and hoping no one would ask what was behind the door.

And the beacon sang on, in a language with no entry in any of her databases, on a band her orders told her to burn out of the sky.

"Ma'am," Dey said carefully. "Confirmation protocols. Do we have enough to classify?"

Reza looked at the screen. At the two scripts, side by side, saying the same thing. *This station speaks for both.* She thought of Tomas, three meters from a tired Vessel-born face, trying to remember something. She thought of forty-one letters and one unfinished sentence.

"No," she said. "Log it as an unresolved contact. Full recording appended. We'll route it to Fleet signals intelligence with my recommendation for analysis." She paused. "A recommendation, Lieutenant. Not a firing solution."

"Aye, Captain." Dey's tone said she understood exactly how much rope her captain had just handed her, and exactly how far the drop was beneath it.

The *Corvane* held station in the long dark, watching the old beacon sing to no one, and Reza Voss sat in her chair with her brother's dented cup cooling beside her, and did not know — could not yet know — that the signal drifting through her bridge like weather was the first note of a message that had been waiting thirty years for someone brave enough, or broken enough, to carry it home.

Behind them, far across the system, on a station called Perihelion Rest, a reactor that had slept for a generation was beginning, very slowly, to count.
