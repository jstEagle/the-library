## 6. Grandmother's Hands

The archive was behind the water reclamation panel, which was the kind of joke that only a certain generation of engineers found funny. Silas had known about the panel for years — every station kid learned the maintenance schematics before they learned to ride a bicycle — but they had never once thought to open it, because a panel that hides water filters should contain water filters, and the universe, until recently, had been a place where things were what they said they were.

Now the universe was a place where gravity blinked and grandmothers wrote in dead men's logs, and Silas pried the panel loose with a pry bar and stood looking at a door.

Not a hatch. A *door* — hinged, wooden, actual wood gone honey-dark with age and oil, set into a frame that someone had cut into the station's structural rib by hand, the saw marks still visible under the grain like fossilized lightning. A door, on a station where every other surface was composite and ceramic. A door meant to be opened and closed and opened again, meant to feel like a room and not a compartment. Someone had wanted this to feel like a room.

The handle was brass. It was warm. Silas stood there for a long moment with their palm on it, telling themselves the warmth came from the coolant line that ran behind the rib, and knowing, in the way you know the weight of your own hand, that it didn't.

They turned it.

* * *

Paper.

The room was small — three meters by four, maybe less — and it was full of paper. Not data slates, not crystal storage, not any of the dense elegant media the colonies had perfected over two centuries of exile. Paper. Reams of it. Folders of it. Notebooks stacked spine-up in careful towers, their covers faded from indigo to the color of dusk. Loose sheets in labeled boxes. Rolled star charts tied with twine. A whole civilization's worth of thought, rendered in ink on pulp, deliberately, stubbornly analog, in an age when a single crystal could hold every library humanity had ever carried out of the Sol system.

Silas's breath fogged in the cold dry air. The room had its own climate control, its own power draw, its own scrubbers. Someone had paid for this room out of station resources for decades, quietly, the way you pay for a second life.

They found the light switch — a toggle, a physical *toggle* — and a lamp with a green glass shade came on and made the room look like the bottom of a sea.

The nearest notebook was labeled in a hand Silas knew.

Not knew — *knew*. Knew the way you know a face in a crowd before you know why. The handwriting was upright, economical, with a slight forward lean on the ascenders, and Silas had seen it a thousand times on the maintenance logs of Perihelion Rest, in the signature blocks of reports filed two and three generations before Silas was born, in the margin notes that generations of station keepers had copied out and followed without questioning because the notes were always right.

*M. ISHIKAWA*, the cover said. And beneath it, smaller: *For the one who dreams.*

Silas did not remember sitting down. They were on the floor, the notebook open on their knees, and outside the door the station hummed its ordinary hum, and somewhere below them in the reactor housing the countdown ticked through its third day — 291 hours, the readout had said that morning, and the number had felt like a stone in Silas's chest — and here, in a green-lit room full of paper, their grandmother was about to speak to them across a gap of sixty years.

*If you are reading this, the beacon woke. Which means the flicker came, which means the other side felt it too, which means we are running out of the time I pretended we had plenty of.*

*Let me tell you who I am, because the records won't. I made sure of that.*

*My name was Meridian Ishikawa, and I was born aboard a Vessel.*

Silas read it three times. The words did not rearrange themselves. The lamp hummed. Their own pulse was loud in their ears, a second, faster countdown.

*You will have been taught that the Vessel-born are not human. That they came from somewhere else, that they are the enemy that fell on us out of the dark in the war's first year. I was taught the same thing, in a colonial school, by teachers who believed it. I let them believe it about me. It was the safest thing I ever did, and it cost me everything, and I would pay it again.*

*I am what the old research called a hybrid — first generation, ship-born. My mother carried me out of the Vessel in her own body and gave me to a colonial family on Thessaly Reach because a hybrid infant in a Vessel crèche does not survive the purges, and neither does the mother. I grew up human. I learned to pass. I learned it so well that I passed myself, for a while. And then I started dreaming in the old tongue, the way all ship-born do, and I could not unlearn what the dreams knew.*

*The beacon is not a beacon. Or rather, it is, but that was the cover. I built it to be a message. One message. Addressed to both sides. Sealed so that it could only be delivered when both sides were desperate enough to listen — which I am sorry to say I calculated would take a war, and which I am sorrier to say I was right about.*

Silas's hands were shaking. They pressed them flat against the page to still them and felt the paper's texture, the faint tooth of it, the ghost of pressure where a pen had borne down. Their grandmother had held these pages. Their grandmother's hands —

They had no memory of their grandmother's hands.

That was the thing that undid them, more than the words, more than *Vessel*. They had grown up on this station. They had grown up on stories of Meridian Ishikawa, the station's founder, the engineer-saint whose notes still guided every repair. But they had no memory of her — no face, no voice, no hands — and when they reached for her the way you reach for a family story, they found a shape with the outline burned away, the way a photograph looks after a fire, everything legible except the person.

There had been a sibling. They were almost sure there had been a sibling. The name came to the edge of retrieval and slid off, smooth as wet glass, and left behind a bruise-shaped ache that Silas had learned, over the years, not to touch.

They touched it now. It hurt exactly as much as it always had, which was to say in a place that had no nerve endings and therefore hurt everywhere.

* * *

The notebooks were numbered. Silas worked through them in order, because order was the one thing they had left, and because a xenolinguist knows that a corpus is a conversation and you enter it where it begins.

The early volumes were engineering — the beacon's true architecture, layer under layer, a transmitter disguised as a lighthouse disguised as a maintenance chore. But between the schematics, in the margins and on the interleaved blank pages, ran a second text: the language.

Silas knew it.

Their whole body knew it before their training did — a lift in the chest, a warmth behind the sternum, the specific vertigo of recognition that comes when a thing you have dreamed appears before you awake. The phonology was the phonology of their nights: the ejectives like stones dropped in deep water, the long vowels that bent at the end as if listening, the click-fricatives that no human mouth should have managed and that Silas's mouth, in dreams, managed effortlessly. Here it was in ink. Here it was *written*, with a consistent orthography, with diacritics for the tonal contours Silas had never been able to fix in memory, with marginal glosses in Common.

Their grandmother had transcribed the dreams. Their grandmother had *had the same dreams*.

*The tongue has no word for enemy,* one marginal note read. *It has a word for not-yet-introduced, and a word for introduced, and a word for the grief of having been introduced and become estranged. The translators on both sides keep saying the Vessel-born have no word for peace. They're wrong. They have no word for war. They borrowed ours.*

Silas read until the lamp seemed to dim and their eyes burned and they could not have said how many hours had passed. The station's cycle lights would have shifted twice. The countdown would have dropped below 280. None of it reached them. They lived now in the green underwater light of the archive, in their grandmother's voice, which was dry and precise and occasionally, devastatingly, funny — she joked about the reactor, about the bureaucrats, about her own stubbornness — and which grew quieter and more urgent as the volumes went on.

The middle volumes were the research. The genetic work. Silas read it the way you read a diagnosis: first for the words, then again for the meaning, then a third time because the meaning kept sliding away from you like a thing alive.

*Both populations carry the founder haplotype. Both. The colonies' founding myth says we came out on the Meridian ships, refugees from Sol; the Vessel-born say they were made, grown for the Vessels, a people with no before. The genomes say otherwise. The genomes say one ship. One ship, one crew, one catastrophe that split them — some into the cold sleep of the colony fleet, some into the Vessels' care, and neither group remembering, because the ship's archive was deliberately shattered. Someone wanted the descendants to forget each other. I have spent forty years finding out who, and I will die before I finish, and that is all right. The message does not need the name. The message needs the proof.*

*And the proof is me.*

Silas put the volume down.

The room was very cold and very quiet and the lamp's green light lay over everything like algae over still water. Silas became aware, gradually, of their own body: the ache in their neck, the damp of their shirt, the taste of cold tea gone bitter on their tongue. They had been reading for — they checked — nine hours. Nine hours, and they had not eaten, and somewhere above them Okafor would be filing the report Silas had promised and not delivered, and the countdown would be at whatever the countdown was at, and none of it mattered as much as the sentence they had just read, which they read a fourth time, a fifth, pressing their thumb flat against the page as if to hold the words in place against a wind only they could feel.

*And the proof is me.*

The genome of a first-generation hybrid, carried in her body for eighty years, was the seal on the message. Not a signature — a *specimen*. The message she had built into the beacon was a genealogy, and a genealogy is only as good as its living witness, and she had been the witness, and she was sixty years dead, and the beacon was counting down, and Silas understood — with the cold, clean, horrible clarity that comes after long reading in a cold room — that the message could not be delivered without her.

Except.

*The archive can be carried,* the last volume said. *Not in crystal. In a mind. The old tongue was designed for it — that is part of what it is, part of why it sounds the way it sounds, why it takes the shape it takes in dreams. It is a mnemonic architecture. It builds itself into a dreaming mind and holds what the mind is given. I have spent the last twenty years of my life preparing the carriers.*

*Carriers,* plural.

*My children first. Then theirs. I am sorry. I am so sorry. There was no other way to keep it safe, and I told myself that a thing carried in dreams costs the dreamer nothing, and I told myself that lie for a long time, and then I watched what it took from my daughter, and I stopped telling it.*

*If you are reading this, you are the last carrier, and you have already paid, and no one will have told you what the payments were for. You will have gaps. You will have a name you cannot find. I cannot give it back to you. The archive does not return what it takes; that is not how memory works, it is not how anything works. But I can tell you what you bought with what you lost, and it is this: the truth about where we all come from, sealed in the only vault that no war has ever successfully bombed, which is the inside of a sleeping head.*

*The beacon wakes. The countdown runs. You will know what to do, because I have spent your whole life making sure you would know, and I am sorry for that too, and I am not sorry for it at all.*

*Your grandmother,*

*M. Ishikawa*

*— who was real, whatever the records say.*

Silas closed the volume and held it against their chest and sat in the green light and cried the way you cry alone in a cold room: without sound, without dignity, without any of it helping. They cried for the grandmother they could not remember. They cried for the daughter — *my daughter*, Meridian had written, and the words had gone through Silas like a blade finding a seam — the daughter whose name Silas also could not find, whose face was a burned photograph, whose existence was a bruise in the shape of a person. They cried for the child they had been, who had lost things and been told the losses were normal, who had learned to walk around the holes in their own life the way you learn to walk around furniture in the dark.

And underneath the grief, patient as sediment, something else was settling.

The dreams. The grammar. The arguments the language kept making at them, night after night, the way it had begun lately to address them directly — *you, you, carrier, carrier* — Silas had thought it was the beacon, or the war, or their own unraveling mind. It was none of those. It was their grandmother's voice, sixty years dead, speaking the tongue she had seeded into their blood, holding the message in the only vault left, waiting for the countdown to finish and the vault to be opened.

*You will know what to do.*

The lamp hummed. The station hummed. Below them, faint through the deck plating, the reactor's tick was just barely audible, a pulse under the floor, patient as a heart.

Silas wiped their face, stood, and took the last volume back down from the shelf — and stopped.

There was one more thing in the room. A box, small, set apart on its own shelf, labeled in the upright forward-leaning hand: *FOR THE DREAMER, WHEN THEY ASK THE RIGHT QUESTION.*

They had not asked a question yet. They were not sure they had ever asked a question in their life before this week.

They opened the box anyway. Inside, wrapped in cloth gone soft with age, was a child's drawing — crayon on paper, a stick figure standing on a curve of planet beneath a scribbled sun, and beside it a second, smaller figure, holding the first one's hand. Two names were written at the bottom in a child's uneven letters. One was Silas's.

The other was a name Silas had been unable to find for twenty years.

It sat there on the page in blue crayon, ordinary and small and entire, and Silas looked at it, and the bruise-shaped ache in them turned over — and the countdown below their feet ticked on toward the hour when everything their grandmother had built would burn.
