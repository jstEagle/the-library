## 1. Dead Language, Live Signal

The Kestrel Verge smelled of burnt metal and old light.

Xanthe Nakamura had learned to read the Verge the way sailors once read weather — by the taste of it on his tongue when the recycler ran dry, by the way dust drifted through the freighter's corridors in slow brown spirals, by the particular groan the *Magpie's Wren* made in her bones when a debris field pressed close. Tonight she was groaning in a minor key. Somewhere out beyond the viewport, the shattered remains of a mining consortium's flagship turned end over end through the dark, its hull blossoming with frost-flowers where atmosphere had flash-boiled two decades ago and left nothing but sculpture.

He hauled scrap for a living. That was the whole of it. The Republic called the Verge contested space; the Concord called it liberated; the dead called it nothing at all, because the dead were done with names. Xanthe threaded his aging freighter between the corpses of ships and stations and cut free whatever still had value — copper conduit, titanium plating, the occasional intact fusion cell that made a month's worth of fuel rations suddenly negotiable. It was honest work in the way that scavenging off graves is honest: technically true, spiritually complicated, and nobody asked questions if you didn't linger.

Ten years he'd been doing this. Ten years since the war ate the diplomat Emrys Nakamura whole — press releases, state funeral, a flag folded into a triangle and presented to no one, because clones don't inherit flags. Ten years since Xanthe had walked out of a growth facility on Titan Annex with someone else's face settling onto his skull like a coat that almost fit, carrying memories that weren't his and grief he had no right to.

The *Wren* was his now. That was the only thing that was.

"Course correction," he murmured to the ship, though there was no one to hear it and the autopilot did not require conversation. He talked to her anyway. A man who lives alone inside a hull learns to fill the silence or be filled by it. "Two degrees starboard. There's a shear line through the debris at marker seven."

The ship answered with a shudder of thrusters and the soft complaint of hydraulics older than either of them. Outside, the debris field rolled past like a frozen ocean — hull fragments, cargo pods split open and spilling their long-spoiled contents, a child's toy drifting in the vacuum with one arm raised as if waving goodbye forever. He'd stopped seeing these things years ago. That was the trick of survival in the Verge: you learned not to see, the same way you learned not to taste the recycled water, not to hear the reactor's heartbeat, not to remember whose hands your hands used to belong to.

His salvage scanner pinged. Routine. He glanced at the readout with the flat attention of a man reading a menu he already knew by heart.

Then he looked again.

The signal wasn't coming from the debris. It was coming from *through* it — from somewhere deep in the Drift-side dark beyond the field, past the boundary markers where charts went blank and insurance companies refused to follow. And it was structured. Not the ragged electromagnetic burble of a dying reactor, not the harmonic whine of stressed metal. Structured. Repeating. Phrased.

Xanthe set down his tea. It was cold anyway; the galley heater had been failing for three months and he'd stopped pretending he'd fix it.

He pulled the signal raw and spread it across his console like a man spreading out a hand of cards he suspected were marked.

It repeated every forty-one seconds. Three phrases, then a pause, then the three again, patient as tide. The waveform was beautiful in a way that made his chest hurt — clean intervals, deliberate cadence, a grammar of sound rather than noise. Whoever — whatever — was sending it wanted to be understood. That much was unmistakable even before translation. Meaning lived in repetition; intent lived in structure. This was language.

And he knew it.

That was the impossible part. That was the part that made his hands go cold around the mug and his breath come shallow in the recycled air. Because he knew it the way you know a song from childhood — not word by word, not consciously, but in the body, in the shape of the mouth it demanded. His lips moved without permission, shaping sounds against the silence of the cockpit: *sshen-vara, ssen-keleth, ao.* The syllables felt like river stones under his tongue, smooth and ancient and impossibly familiar.

Two hundred years extinct. Every linguist in the settled systems agreed on that. The Archive civilization had vanished — destroyed, scattered, subsumed, depending on which historian you paid — and their language had died with them, preserved only in corrupted fragments and academic arguments. Nobody spoke it. Nobody could speak it.

Except that Emrys Nakamura had studied it. Twenty-two years ago, back when the diplomat still had a name worth killing him over, he'd spent a sabbatical year buried in the restricted stacks of the Ceres Hollow archives, chasing the Archive's jump-gate mathematics through their linguistic roots. Xanthe knew this because Xanthe remembered it. Not learned — *remembered*, the memory sitting in his head like furniture left behind by a previous tenant: the smell of the archive vaults, ozone and vellum; the weight of data-slates covered in angular glyphs; the specific frustration of a mind reaching for meaning just past its grasp.

He had spent ten years trying not to use those memories. They were contraband. They were evidence. A clone who displayed too much of his original's expertise got noticed, and noticed clones got collected — by the Republic, who owned his genome; by the Concord, who'd have loved a Nakamura-shaped propaganda piece; by anyone who understood that a man who remembers being murdered might also remember why.

*Ignore it,* said ten years of discipline, calm and reasonable. *It's a ghost signal. Old relay, degraded transmission, coincidence dressed up as pattern. You're alone in the dark and the dark knows how lonely people get. Log it as interference and fly on.*

He should ignore it. He was very good at ignoring things. He had ignored the anniversary of his original's death every year for a decade. He had ignored the recruitment letters from three universities who'd found his forged credentials charming. He had ignored the way his own reflection sometimes looked at him with an expression of patient expectation, as if waiting for him to finally do something worthy of the face.

Xanthe reached for the console and opened a recording channel.

His hands were shaking. He watched them shake with a strange detachment, as though they belonged to the man in the mirror rather than to him. The frequency lock took four tries because his fingers wouldn't obey. When the recording finally caught — forty-one seconds of dead language pouring into his ship's memory like water into a cracked vessel — he sat back and listened to it eleven times in a row.

Three phrases. Pause. Three phrases. The same ones each time, he was nearly certain, though the middle phrase wavered somehow, shifted a fraction in emphasis on the seventh repetition, and something in his inherited instincts flared at that like a struck match. Stress pattern. Grammatical stress. In the Archive tongue, if the fragments in his borrowed memory were true, emphasis marked *who was speaking*. First person. Third person. The voice of a witness versus the voice of a participant.

This signal was speaking in first person.

Someone — something — was saying *I*. Saying *I* on a frequency that had been silent for two centuries, from a region of space no chart acknowledged, in a language no living throat could form.

Xanthe became aware, gradually and then all at once, of the quality of the silence in his cockpit. It had changed. For ten years the silence aboard the *Magpie's Wren* had been the silence of an empty house — inert, indifferent, the silence of a place where no one was expected. Now it felt occupied. Now it felt like the silence of a room where someone has just said your name and is waiting to see whether you answer.

He looked at the viewport. The debris field turned its slow revolutions. The dark beyond it was exactly as dark as it had been an hour ago, exactly as dark as it had been every night of ten years, and yet —

*Like someone is watching me.*

The thought arrived fully formed and unwelcome, and he examined it the way he examined any anomaly in his systems: coolly, methodically, looking for the fault. Paranoia was a occupational hazard of being what he was. He'd catalogued his own delusions before — the conviction that Republic patrol patterns had changed after his last port call (they hadn't), the certainty that a woman at Ceres Hollow market had recognized his face (she'd recognized the face, not him; everyone recognized the face). He knew how loneliness manufactured witnesses out of shadow.

But this wasn't paranoia. This was simpler and stranger than that. For the first time in ten years, Xanthe Nakamura felt *seen* — not recognized, not hunted, not mistaken for the dead man whose face he wore, but seen. Addressed. The signal repeated its three phrases into the dark, and somewhere in the architecture of those phrases was a door, and the door had his name on it, both of his names, the one on his forged papers and the one that had been carved on a memorial wall without ever having belonged to him.

"I should ignore you," he told the signal.

It repeated itself, patient as tide.

He ran the analysis suite while he waited for his heart rate to come down. Origin bearing: consistent, fixed point, deep Drift-side. Power signature: negligible — the transmitter was drawing less energy than his own galley heater, which meant either extraordinary efficiency or extraordinary patience, a broadcast designed to run for years without being noticed. Encryption: none. It wasn't hiding. It was *waiting* — whispering into the dark on a dead frequency in a dead tongue, saying *I* over and over to whoever might someday understand.

Which meant it wasn't meant for the Republic. It wasn't meant for the Concord. Neither empire had a single throat that could pronounce *ssen-keleth* correctly, and both had torn the Verge apart often enough to have found it if they'd known what to listen for.

It was meant for whoever came next. It was meant, perhaps, for a dead man's year of study locked inside a scavenger's skull.

Xanthe closed his eyes and let the memory rise, the one he kept buried deepest because it was the most dangerous: the archive vaults on Ceres Hollow, twenty-two years ago, Emrys Nakamura's hands turning a slate of glyphs toward the light. There had been a marginal note in the original's own handwriting — Xanthe could see it now, the slanted characters, the ink faded to brown — a note he'd never understood until this moment:

*They are not gone. They are waiting to be read.*

At the time, the diplomat had written it off as scholarly sentimentality. The romanticism of a man who fell in love with dead civilizations. But sitting in the dark of his scrap-hauler with a shaking cup of cold tea and a first-person voice repeating itself across two hundred years of silence, Xanthe felt the note land differently. Felt it land like a message passed hand to hand across generations, addressed to whoever could read it.

Addressed to him.

The scanner chimed — a second ping, layered under the first, and this one froze him mid-breath. Not the signal. Something else. A brief, disciplined burst from the Republic side of the Verge: a patrol net handshake, routine, distant. Except that the handshake carried a query tag, and the query tag referenced an anomalous emission event, and the emission coordinates in the query were —

His coordinates. His patch of dark. The patrol network had heard the signal too, or heard the echo of it bouncing off the debris field, and somewhere in a fleet office a duty officer was flagging it for review, and somewhere downstream of that flag a chain of command would wake up and start asking questions, and questions, in Xanthe's experience, always ended with men in grey uniforms boarding his ship and asking to see papers that proved he was nobody.

They'd heard it. Someone else had heard it.

The signal repeated. *Sshen-vara, ssen-keleth, ao.*

Xanthe sat very still for a long moment, listening to the reactor breathe, watching the debris turn. Then he did the thing that ten years of careful anonymity screamed at him not to do. He copied the recording to three separate encrypted drives — one for the ship's core, one hidden in the galley heater he'd never fixed, one sealed in a courier capsule tucked behind the life-support scrubbers where no boarding party ever bothered to look. He labeled none of them. He memorized the frequency, the bearing, the interval. Forty-one seconds. Three phrases. First person.

Then he turned the *Magpie's Wren* away from the signal, away from the Drift, and laid in a course for Ceres Hollow — because if the patrol net had flagged the emission, then the smart move, the only move, was to be somewhere else, some crowded neutral port full of alibis, before the questions started.

But as the debris field fell away behind him and the Verge opened up ahead, dark and endless and full of things no chart admitted to, Xanthe caught himself translating the middle phrase under his breath, the way a man hums a tune he can't shake. His mouth knew the shapes. His lungs knew the breath. The dead language moved through him like a current through wire, and for the first time in ten years he was not hauling scrap through contested orbits, anonymous and half-asleep.

He was being spoken to. And worse — far worse, in a way he wouldn't understand until it was far too late to turn back —

He understood.

* * *

Six hours out from Ceres Hollow, running dark through the shipping lanes with his transponder chirping a false registry, Xanthe played the recording again.

He told himself it was analysis. Verification. Due diligence. He told himself a lot of things while the three phrases poured through the cockpit speakers and his hands, steady now, traced the glyphs he remembered from a dead man's sabbatical onto a scrap of paper he would burn before docking.

*Sshen-vara.* Witness. The one who sees.
*Ssen-keleth.* The one who speaks — no. Closer. The one who *remembers aloud*.
*Ao.*

*Ao* had no clean translation. In the fragments, it appeared at the ends of testaments and at the beginnings of prayers. The scholars had argued about it for a century. Emrys Nakamura had argued about it in the margins of his own notes, and his conclusion — the one Xanthe had inherited along with his hands and his grief — was that *ao* meant something like: *I am still here. Hear me.*

Outside the viewport, the Verge scrolled past, indifferent and infinite. Behind him, the signal's source hung in the uncharted dark, whispering *I am still here, hear me* to no one, to everyone, to a clone in a rusted freighter who had spent a decade convincing the universe he was no one at all.

The comm board flickered. A new contact, small and fast, squawking a Republic customs challenge at a tramp hauler two lanes over. Routine. Distant. But Xanthe watched it with new eyes, and the silence in his cockpit had teeth now, and somewhere ahead, in the crowded dark of Ceres Hollow, men who were not scholars were already pulling files on anomalous emissions.

He burned the paper. He kept the words.

Forty-one seconds. Three phrases. First person.

*I am still here. Hear me.*

He was listening. God help him, he was listening.
