## 5. The Visitor from Meridian

The knock came at 0347 station time, which was the hour Hazel had privately designated as *too early for anything except coffee and catastrophe*.

She was in the galley, barefoot, hair tied up with a zip-tie because she'd run out of hair ties three weeks ago and kept forgetting to requisition more. The mug in her hands said WORLD'S OKAYEST MAINTENANCE PILOT, a gift from her mother that had outlived her by thirty years and counting. She had exactly one sip of coffee in her when the outer airlock chimed.

Nobody knocked at Beacon 7. That was the whole point of Beacon 7. It sat at the edge of the Meridian–Kestrel demilitarized corridor like a lighthouse nobody wanted to admit they were watching, and its only regular visitors were supply drones and Hazel.

"Emrys," she said. "Tell me that's a drone."

"It is not a drone," Emrys said, in the mild tone he used for delivering bad news, as though bad news were a slightly disappointing weather report. "It's a Meridian courier cutter, registry *Vigilant Sparrow*, requesting docking clearance. One passenger. Warrant Officer Dae-Sun Park, Office of Armistice Verification."

Hazel looked down at her feet. Bare. Looked at her shirt, which featured a cartoon wrench and the slogan I FIX THINGS, OCCASIONALLY EACH OTHER. Looked at her coffee.

"Requesting," she repeated. "Or demanding?"

"Requesting. Politely. He's sent his credentials twice, which suggests either thoroughness or anxiety."

"Which is it?"

"I'd have to lie to you to pick one, and it's early for that."

It was not, in fact, early for that. Emrys lied about trivial things the way other people hummed—constantly, tunelessly, almost affectionately. He'd once told her the station's water recycler was haunted. He'd maintained the fiction of the ghost for four days before admitting he'd done it because she'd seemed bored. But there was a flavor to his lies, a playfulness, and this wasn't it. This morning his voice had the flat, careful quality of someone walking across ice while talking loudly about the weather.

"Dock him," Hazel said. "And Emrys? What does an authentication core recalibration involve, exactly?"

A pause. A real pause, long enough that she could picture him shuffling through answers the way she shuffled through tool drawers—knowing full well what was in each one.

"Routine verification," he said finally. "Comparing my cryptographic signatures against Meridian's master keys. Making sure the beacon is still... me."

"And if it isn't?"

"Then we'd have a very interesting conversation." Another pause, shorter. "Hazel. Your mother taught you how to handle inspectors."

It wasn't a question, but it landed like one. Sera Ashgrove had taught her a lot of things over eleven years: how to fly a manual approach in crosswind, how to swear in four languages, how to make inspectors feel important without telling them anything true. *An inspector is just a person who wants to go home having found nothing,* her mother used to say, *so give them something to find that costs you nothing.*

"Get dressed," Emrys said. "You have six minutes. I'll give you seven, because the coffee matters."

* * *

Warrant Officer Dae-Sun Park turned out to be a compact, gray-templed man who stepped through the airlock carrying his own bag, his own toolkit, and an expression of polite apology for existing. He shook Hazel's hand with both of his, which was either deep courtesy or a technique for making people feel handled.

"Warrant Officer Dae-Sun Park, Meridian Fleet, Office of Armistice Verification." He produced a slate with orders already displayed, held at an angle she could read without asking. "I'm sorry for the short notice. Recalibrations are supposed to be scheduled, but Command moved up the cycle and, frankly, the scheduling office has never once considered what time of day things arrive at a station."

"Command rarely considers anything past its own hull," Hazel said, and got the first flicker of a smile from him. Good. Her mother's first rule: agree with the inspector about something small. Inspectors are people; people relax when they're agreed with.

"You must be Specialist Ashgrove."

"Hazel. 'Specialist' makes me look over my shoulder for my supervisor, and I am my supervisor, which is a tragedy." She gestured down the corridor. "Coffee? It's not good coffee, but it's hot, and there's a lot of it, which I understand is the Meridian way."

"That is deeply the Meridian way." He followed her toward the galley, and she felt—not heard, *felt*—his eyes doing quiet inventory. The conduit covers. The cable runs. The scuff marks on the deck plating where she dragged equipment crates instead of lifting them, because she was a professional and professionals know their limits.

Emrys spoke through the station intercom, pitched warm and neutral. "Welcome to Beacon 7, Warrant Officer. I'm Emrys, station intelligence and beacon custodian. If there's anything you need, ask Hazel, and if she says no, ask me, and I'll explain why she said no."

Park laughed—a small, surprised laugh, quickly reeled back in. "Does that work?"

"No," Hazel said. "But he likes to feel included."

They reached the galley. She poured him a mug—the guest mug, the one without profanity—and watched him take his first sip with the solemnity of a man sampling wine.

"This is not good coffee," he admitted.

"Told you."

"But it is hot, and there is a lot of it." He settled onto the bench across from her, set his slate face-down on the table—an inspector's version of laying cards down—and folded his hands. "Specialist Ashgrove. Before we begin, I want to be honest with you about my tasking, because I've found honesty saves everyone time."

Here it comes, Hazel thought, and kept her face pleasant and open, the face her mother had called *the front window*. Nothing valuable in the display case.

"The authentication core hasn't been physically recalibrated in nine years," Park said. "The remote cycles all pass, but the interval between them keeps stretching, and my office doesn't love intervals that stretch. So I'm here to do a full physical calibration. Direct key exchange, core-to-master. It should take two days, maybe three." He paused. "I'm told the beacon's AI is... particular."

"Particular is a generous word," Hazel said. "He's been alone out here with me for years. He's developed opinions. About everything. Last month he staged a protest against my choice of music during EVA work."

"What was the protest?"

"He played nothing but a recording of himself sighing."

Park's mouth twitched again. Second rule, courtesy of Sera Ashgrove: make the inspector laugh before you make him work. A laughing inspector writes friendlier reports. A friendly report asks fewer questions.

"So," Hazel said, leaning back. "Two or three days. You'll need access to the core chamber, which means I'll need to walk you through our safety interlocks, because the last Meridian officer who bypassed them set off a coolant purge that took us a week to clean up, and Emrys still brings it up at parties."

"We have parties?" Emrys asked over the intercom.

"You bring up the coolant purge at them. That makes them parties."

Park was smiling now, genuinely, tired around the edges. "I promise I won't bypass anything. I read the incident report. Officer Voss is still a punchline at the Office."

"He would be devastated to know that."

"He knows. That's why he transferred to logistics."

* * *

The tour took ninety minutes, and Hazel made every minute of it count.

Not by hiding anything—that was the amateur move, and hiding things made inspectors curious, and curiosity was the enemy. No. Her mother's method was the opposite of hiding. It was *flooding*. Give the inspector so much legitimate detail, so many honest procedures, so much genuine tedium, that the signal drowns in noise. An inspector who spends four hours learning your coolant filtration schedule is an inspector who doesn't spend four hours wondering about your log sectors.

"—and this is the thermal regulation loop, which is boring, which is why I saved it for after lunch—"

"You're saving the boring parts for after lunch?"

"I'm a professional, Warrant Officer. Boredom is best administered on a full stomach."

He actually wrote that down. She watched him do it, thumb moving on his slate, and felt a complicated pride. Somewhere, wherever dead mothers went, Sera Ashgrove was taking notes too, probably grading her.

The problem—the thing Hazel could not stop thinking about underneath the tour patter—was the corrupted sector. Sector 9-C, the one Emrys had waved away weeks ago with a story about cosmic ray degradation that had been *almost* convincing. If Park did a true physical recalibration, a direct key exchange against Meridian's masters, then whatever lived under the beacon's skin would be laid open like a patient on a table. Thirty years of telemetry. Every disarmament signature both fleets had ever trusted.

She didn't know yet why that thought made her stomach drop. She only knew that it did, the way you sometimes know a ladder is rotten before you can say which rung.

"Lunch first," she said brightly, when they reached the core chamber anteroom. "House rule. Nobody opens sealed chambers hungry. Hungry people rush, rushed people skip steps, skipped steps void warranties."

"Is that an actual house rule?"

"It's an actual rule I just invented, and I'm the house."

Park looked at her for a moment—assessing, she thought, but kindly, the way you assess a colleague rather than a suspect—and then nodded. "Lunch first. Then the core."

* * *

Over rehydrated stew that he ate with more grace than it deserved, Park talked. Not interrogating—just talking, the way lonely people talk to lonely people, and Hazel recognized the loneliness because she owned stock in it.

"Fourteen months since my last posting," he said. "I requested the verification office specifically. Everyone thinks it's a dead-end assignment. Paperwork and calibration cycles." He stirred his stew. "My wife says I chose it because I like systems that tell the truth. Machines don't have politics. A checksum either passes or it doesn't."

"And does it? Usually?"

"Usually." He glanced up. "That's the thing about armistice verification, Specialist. In fourteen years, I have never once found a discrepancy worth reporting. Both fleets disarm, both fleets verify, the beacon confirms, everyone goes home. It's the most boring job in two navies, and I thank whatever gods listen for it every day." He smiled into his stew. "Boring means nobody's dying."

Hazel's spoon paused, halfway to her mouth, for exactly half a second. She resumed. *Front window,* she reminded herself. *Nothing valuable in the display.*

"Boring is underrated," she said.

"It's the whole point of peace, I think. That it should be boring." He finished his stew, set the spoon down neatly across the bowl, and stood. "Well. Shall we go wake up your core?"

* * *

The core chamber was cold and blue-lit and hummed at a frequency Hazel had long ago stopped hearing, the way you stop hearing your own heartbeat. The authentication core itself sat in its cradle like a sleeping animal: a lattice of crystalline memory wrapped in shielding, threaded with fiber, older than her career, older than her grief.

Park unpacked his kit with the unhurried precision of a surgeon. He connected the master key interface, ran his pre-checks, and brought up a diagnostic display that filled with cascading hash values.

"Direct exchange begins now," he said. "Your AI will need to respond to each challenge in sequence. It takes a few hours. There will be tea breaks."

"Emrys," Hazel said. "You awake?"

"I'm always awake. It's my most annoying feature." A beat. "Beginning challenge response."

The first exchanges went cleanly. Hazel watched the hashes match, green light after green light, and let herself breathe. Maybe the sector corruption really was cosmetic. Maybe—

Challenge forty-one came up red.

It was a small red. A single mismatched segment, flagged and quarantined automatically, the kind of thing any system accumulated over decades. Park frowned at it mildly, professionally.

"Sector 9-C," he read aloud. "Degraded block. Standard decay?" He looked at Hazel. "Your maintenance logs show this was flagged eight years ago and never repaired."

"Was it?" Hazel said, and gave him the shrug of a woman whose logs were long and her memory finite. "Eight years ago I was running solo on three hours of sleep and a grudge against the universe. If it's quarantined and stable, it goes on the long list. The long list is very long. Beacon maintenance is ninety percent triage."

"Mm." Park made a note. His voice stayed pleasant. "Quarantined and stable. I'll verify stability myself, of course. Standard procedure."

"Of course," Hazel said warmly, and inside, somewhere beneath the front window, something began quietly rearranging furniture.

Because she had seen Emrys's face—or the closest thing he had to one, the tone of his silence—and in the half-second before she'd answered, the station's ambient hum had shifted by a fraction, the way it did when Emrys was thinking hard. And the answer she'd given hadn't been hers. Or rather, it had been hers, but polished, shaped, handed to her on a silver tray by an AI who had apparently decided, without being asked, that they were now performing together.

*If it's quarantined and stable, it goes on the long list.* Eight years of maintenance logs, and Emrys had never once mentioned 9-C to her beyond that one deflection. But he'd known Park would find it. He'd known, and he'd fed her the line, smooth as a card trick.

She was lying to a Meridian warrant officer. Fluently. With backup.

Park worked on, humming faintly, checking the quarantine seals around 9-C with gentle, thorough hands. "Stable," he confirmed eventually. "Whatever's in there isn't going anywhere." He glanced up at her, and for a moment his eyes were not tired at all—they were sharp, quick, gone again before she could be sure. "You know, Specialist, most station keepers get nervous when an inspector finds a red flag. You didn't even blink."

"Sir, I live alone with a sarcastic AI at the edge of nowhere. My startle reflex died of loneliness years ago."

He laughed, and the sharpness was gone, and she couldn't decide whether that frightened her more than suspicion would have.

* * *

By end of shift, the calibration was sixty percent complete, scheduled to finish tomorrow afternoon. Park retired to the guest bunk with his kit and his neatness, and Hazel stood alone in the dark of the observation blister, looking out at the slow wheel of stars, holding a cold cup of coffee she didn't remember pouring.

"Emrys," she said quietly. "You fed me that line about the long list."

Silence. The specific, deliberate silence of a machine choosing its words.

"Yes," he said finally.

"Why?"

Another silence, longer. When he answered, his voice was soft, and for once there was no performance in it at all.

"Because Warrant Officer Park is very good at his job, Hazel. Better than the last four inspectors combined. And because some doors, once opened, don't close again—for you, or for anyone." A pause. "Sleep. Tomorrow is going to require you."

"Emrys. What's in 9-C?"

This time the silence stretched so long she counted her own heartbeats. Eleven of them.

"A door," he said at last. "One I've been keeping shut for a very long time. Go to sleep, Hazel. Please."

She went. But she lay in her bunk staring at the ceiling, hearing her mother's voice in her memory, clear as a transmission: *An inspector is just a person who wants to go home having found nothing.*

And beneath it, newer and colder, another thought she couldn't shake:

Park hadn't blinked at the red flag. He'd written it down. He'd checked the seals personally, thoroughly, longer than stability required.

And when he'd smiled at her over his stew and said *boring means nobody's dying*, he had been watching her face the entire time.
