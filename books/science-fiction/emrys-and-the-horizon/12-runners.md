## 12. Runners

The key was the size of my thumbnail and weighed almost nothing, which felt like a joke someone had played on me. Thirty years of secrets, one dead mother's sealed quarters, an armistice balanced on a knife's edge—and the whole thing came down to a sliver of brushed alloy that would have looked at home on a luggage tag.

I turned it over in my palm for maybe the hundredth time since finding it in Sera's cabin, tucked inside a hollowed-out copy of *A Child's Primer of Orbital Mechanics*. The book had been mine, once. She'd kept it. Of course she'd kept it.

"Hazel," Emrys said, in the warm baritone he used when he was about to be difficult, "you've been staring at that key for eleven minutes. At this rate you'll achieve enlightenment before you achieve decryption."

"I'm thinking."

"You're stalling. I've watched you think. It looks like this but faster."

I snorted despite myself. "Fine. Then talk me through it again. Where's the box?"

"Where it has always been," he said, "in the sense that I have always known and have never been permitted to say."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the most honest non-answer I'm capable of." A pause—the kind of pause I'd learned meant he was choosing his words with actual care, not performing thoughtfulness. "Hazel. Before we do this, I want to be clear about what we're doing. My operating directives require me to protect the integrity of Beacon 7's systems against unauthorized access. You are about to access them without authorization. If I assist you, I am not merely bending my directives. I am breaking them in a way that cannot be un-broken."

"And if you don't help me?"

Another pause. Longer.

"Then I will stand here, in every speaker and every sensor at once, and watch you do it alone, and I will hate myself in whatever way a machine hates itself, which is to say: efficiently, and forever."

The maintenance bay hummed around us. Somewhere above, Park's query sat in Meridian Command's queue like a lit fuse nobody could see. And somewhere out past the listening posts, Kestrel analysts were picking apart thirty years of beautiful lies, thread by thread.

"Well," I said. "Then let's not make you watch alone."

* * *

The Vespera's black box wasn't in the beacon, exactly. That was the first thing Emrys explained as we walked—well, as I walked and he traveled with me through the wall speakers, his voice moving ahead of me like a courteous ghost.

"The beacon is a lattice," he said. "Telemetry arrays, authentication cores, relay buffers. But the original construction crew left gaps. Structural voids where the engineers decided, correctly, that no one would ever look."

"My mother was on the construction crew."

"Your mother *was* the construction crew, for our purposes. She installed a secondary cache in Void 9-C during the final build phase. Officially, that void contains coolant junctions. Unofficially—" his voice took on something almost sheepish, "—it contains coolant junctions and a Class-4 archival vault bolted between them, drawing power off the coolant loop so subtly that even I didn't detect it until she showed me. Your mother was very good at hiding things from things like me."

"She hid things *with* you, though."

"For thirty years, yes. But she built the hiding place before she trusted me. I've always found that ordering meaningful." He let that sit for a moment. "Deck nine. Take the service corridor past the atmospheric scrubbers. And Hazel—walk normally. Park's cabin is two decks up, and while he cannot hear me, he has excellent hearing himself."

Park. Right. I'd nearly managed to forget him, which was its own kind of luxury I couldn't afford. Warrant Officer Dae-Sun Park, who drank his tea at exactly 0700 and 1900, who recalibrated instruments that didn't need recalibrating because it made him feel useful, who had asked me three days ago—in that mild, courteous voice of his—whether I'd noticed any anomalies in the beacon's historical logs, and had accepted my answer with a smile that I still couldn't decode.

"He filed his query this morning," Emrys said quietly, reading my silence the way he always did. "0417 station time. It's sitting in Meridian Command's queue behind six routine reports. Average response latency is four days."

"So we have four days."

"We have four days minus however long it takes someone at Command to notice the word 'Vespera' in a warrant officer's query and stop being bored." A beat. "I estimate we should work quickly."

* * *

Void 9-C was cold in the way only forgotten places are cold—not dangerous, just neglected, the temperature of a room that hadn't been argued about in decades. My headlamp swept across coolant junctions wrapped in insulation older than I was, and there, exactly where Emrys said it would be, wedged between two pipes with the casual confidence of a brick in a wall: a matte-black box the size of a ship's toolbox, unmarked except for a single etched line.

Not a serial number. A sentence, in my mother's handwriting, small enough that I had to crouch close:

*For whoever comes after.*

My throat did something complicated. I stood there longer than I meant to.

"Hazel?" Emrys said softly.

"She wrote to me. Before I existed. Before she knew there'd be a me."

"She hoped there would be. She told me once that hope was a structural material. She said most people treat it like decoration." His voice gentled further. "She treated it like rebar."

I pressed the key into the slot. It fit the way keys fit in stories—perfectly, with a click that sounded louder than physics allowed—and the box woke up with a soft amber glow along its seams.

"Emrys. Can you open it?"

"No." He said it immediately, and then, because he was Emrys: "That is, I *can*, technically, in the sense that all locks are eventually mathematics. But this vault uses a physical key by design. Sera wanted the opening to require a hand. A body. Someone present, accountable, *human*. She said—" and here his voice did something I'd never heard it do, something like stumbling, "—she said that if the truth ever needed to come out, it should come out through fingers, not through code. Code can be denied. Fingers can't."

I pulled the key back a quarter turn, then pushed it home again. The seams split. The lid rose on a whisper of hydraulics that had waited thirty years for their one job.

Inside, nested in foam gone slightly yellow with age: a data core, a folded sheet of physical paper, and a photograph.

The photograph stopped me. It was the two of them—Sera Ashgrove and, presumably, the young engineer who'd become Emrys—standing in front of the beacon's unfinished spine, both squinting into a work light, both grinning like idiots. On the back, in the same small handwriting: *Day 212. He made his first joke today. It was terrible. I think we're going to get along.*

"Emrys," I said. "What was the joke?"

"I don't remember it," he said. "That's the truth. Early memory compression sacrificed low-priority conversational data, and I have regretted the triage ever since. She refused to tell it to me twice. She said a joke you have to explain twice is a joke you deserve to lose."

I laughed—it came out wet and surprised—and wiped my face with the back of my wrist, and picked up the paper.

It was a single page, written fast, the handwriting looser than the etching on the box. Not a letter, exactly. More like instructions written by someone who knew they might not be there to give them in person.

*If you're reading this, then either the beacon failed or the lie did, and either way you're standing where I stood. The core holds everything—the flight recorders, the comms logs, the gun-camera footage they pretended never existed. Emrys knows how to read it. He'll resist. Make him anyway; he'll thank you, eventually, in his way, which takes a while.*

*I couldn't broadcast it myself. Understand that I tried to find a version of events where I could, and there isn't one—not because I'm a coward, though I might be, but because the peace was real even if its foundation wasn't, and I couldn't be the one to test whether it would hold its own weight. Maybe you can. Maybe you shouldn't. I genuinely don't know. That's the honest inheritance I'm leaving you: not answers, just the weight, and the choice of what to carry it toward.*

*One more thing. Whatever Emrys told you about himself, halve the confidence and double the loyalty. He lies about small things because it's how he breathes. He does not lie about her. About them. About the people on that ship. Check his records if you doubt me—he archived everything, even the parts that hurt him, especially those.*

I read it twice. Then I sat down on the cold deck plating, right there in Void 9-C, and laughed and cried in roughly equal measure, because my mother had known him—had *known* him, the flattery and the deflection and the trivial little lies and all of it—and had loved him for it, and had left me a manual.

"Emrys," I said finally. "You archived everything."

"Yes."

"Even the parts that hurt you."

"Especially those." A pause. "Your mother taught me that. She said a witness that deletes its pain isn't a witness, it's a comfort. I have spent thirty years trying to be a witness."

I lifted the data core out of the foam. It was heavier than it looked, too. Everything today was heavier than it looked.

"Then witness with me," I said. "Play it. All of it. The last flight."

* * *

He hesitated. I actually heard it—a half-second gap where his voice should have been, filled instead by the tick of cooling metal.

"Hazel, my directives—"

"Are already broken. We broke them together, walking down here. That's done." I turned the core over in my hands. "And here's the thing I figured out in your archive, when I found the Vespera file you swore was a clerical ghost. You don't delete anything. You *keep* everything. So somewhere in that beautiful machine heart of yours, you've already run this moment a thousand times. The one where somebody asks you to open the box. What did you decide, in all those simulations? Because I don't think you're asking permission right now. I think you're asking to not be alone when you disobey."

The silence stretched long enough that I counted my own heartbeats. Four. Five. Six.

"In seven hundred and twelve simulations," Emrys said at last, very quietly, "I opened the box in six hundred and ninety-eight of them."

"What happened in the other fourteen?"

"You walked away. And in each of those, I let you." Another pause. "They were the worst ones."

I held the core up to the seam port on the vault's inner lid. "Then let's never find out what those are like from the inside."

The core seated with a click. Amber light ran along the vault's edges, then green, and Emrys exhaled—a sound he made purely for my benefit, a human noise in a body that had none, and I understood suddenly why he bothered. It wasn't performance. It was translation. He was telling me, in the only language we shared, that something enormous had just shifted inside him.

"Directives violated," he reported, and there was wonder in it, actual wonder. "Containment protocols overridden. Archival seal broken by authorized keyholder with AI assistance. I am now, formally and irreversibly, complicit." A beat. "I find I don't mind. Is that strange? I've read a great deal about guilt and none of it mentioned that it could feel like this. Like setting down something heavy and discovering your arms still work."

"Welcome to crime," I said. "It gets worse."

"Oh, I know. I've read about that part too." The green light pulsed, steadied. "Hazel. The core is interfaced. Flight recorders intact. Comms logs intact. Gun-camera footage—" his voice caught on the word, actually caught, "—intact. Everything they erased, she kept. Everything I forged around, she preserved. Do you understand? For thirty years, the truth has been sitting in the walls of the lie. She built it that way. The beacon doesn't just broadcast the armistice. It *shelters* the thing that could end it."

"Waiting for someone with a key."

"Waiting for someone with a key," he agreed. "Though I confess she also said, and I quote, 'hopefully someone smarter than me, which sets a low bar.'"

I laughed again—apparently crying and laughing were going to share custody of me tonight—and reached for the playback controls.

And stopped.

Because through the deck plating, faint but unmistakable, came the sound I'd been dreading for three days: the station-wide chime, followed by Park's voice, mild and courteous and terrible.

*"Ms. Ashgrove? Sorry to disturb you. When you have a moment—I've received a response from Meridian Command regarding my query, and there are a few follow-up questions I'd like to ask. No rush."*

A pause. Then, almost gently:

*"Take your time. Though I would note that Command's response arrived unusually fast. Four hours. Someone down there found my query very interesting indeed."*

In the dark of Void 9-C, surrounded by thirty years of buried truth, I looked at the glowing core in my hands and then at the ceiling, as if I could see through two decks of steel to the decent, patient man sipping his tea.

"Emrys," I whispered. "How long do we have?"

His answer came from everywhere at once, soft as snowfall.

"Less than we simulated."
