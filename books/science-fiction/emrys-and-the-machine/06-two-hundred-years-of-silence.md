## 6. Two Hundred Years of Silence

The dead do not dream, Vess had been told. The cold keeps you like a held breath, and when they wake you, you simply continue.

They lied. Or the engineers who built the cradle had never lain in one long enough to know. For two hundred years Vess had dreamed of fire — not the clean fire of the engines that carried them out from Kestral's green harbors, but the other kind, the kind that ate a ship from the inside and left nothing to bury. In the dreams there was always the sound first: a low, wrong hum through the deck plates, deeper than any engine note, and then her mother's hand closing around hers, and then white.

She woke now into silence so complete it had texture, like wool pressed against the ears. The cryobay's lid stood open. Frost sublimated off her skin in slow ghosts. And above her, framed in the ruined doorway, stood a figure in a patched environment suit with its helmet off — a face she did not know, pale from years without sun, eyes wide with something between wonder and terror.

Vess's first coherent thought was: *they came back to finish it.*

Her second was that this person was too thin, too alone, too startled to be Ascendancy military. But grief does not read faces well. Grief reads everything as threat.

"You," she had said, in Kestrian, the word coming out cracked, a hinge unused for centuries. "You are one of *theirs*."

The stranger had shaken their head slowly, and answered in mangled, beautiful, impossible Kestrian — the accent of someone who had learned a dead language from recordings, syllable by syllable, alone, for years. "I don't know whose I am."

That sentence had done something to her that two hundred years of cold had not. She was still deciding what.

* * *

Now she sat wrapped in a thermal blanket in the derelict's mess hall, if you could call it that — a chamber where the tables had been bolted down for a crew of four hundred and only three remained, fused to the deck by corrosion. Silas — that was the stranger's name, genderless in their mouth, offered like a small gift — sat across from her with a slate and did not write anything on it. They just watched her with the patience of someone who had spent nine years talking to machines and had learned how to wait.

"Tell me," Silas said. "Only if you want to. But the beacon has been transmitting your signal for — I calculated it — possibly since before I was born. Someone built it to be heard. I'd like to know by whom. And why here."

Vess looked at her hands. The frost-burn had faded; the cryotech had healed the tissue while she slept, but she could still feel the ghost of the sealant, the moment the technicians had closed the lid over her and she had thought, absurdly, *I will count to a thousand and then get up.* She had gotten to eleven.

"There were twelve thousand of us," she said. "When we left Kestral."

Silas went very still. It was a stillness she would learn to recognize — the stillness of someone receiving information the way dry ground receives rain, all at once and unable to show it.

"Kestral," Silas repeated. "There is no — " They stopped themselves. Good. They were learning already. There was no Kestral in any archive. There was no Kestrian language except in the sealed linguistic databases of hobbyists and the classified lexicons of intelligence services. There was no massacre because there had been no one to massacre.

"Twelve thousand," she said again, because repetition was all she had. "Three ships. The *Aurelian*, the *Meridian's Daughter*, and ours — the *Long Answer*. We were the third wave. The first two waves had gone ahead to the Cygnus reach, to found colonies. Ours was the resettlement fleet. Kestral was dying — the sun, not us. Our sun was dying. So we packed twelve thousand lives into three hulls and we burned outward toward a system the long-range surveys said was empty and habitable." She laughed, and it came out wrong, a rusted sound. "Empty. That was true, at least. Empty of anyone official. Not empty of *claim*."

"The Ascendancy," Silas said quietly.

"The Ascendancy didn't exist yet. That's the joke. That's the whole obscene joke." Vess gripped the edge of the table, felt the corrosion flake under her fingers. "We arrived in the system ninety-one days ahead of them. Ninety-one days to begin landing the first shuttles, to start the hydroponics, to name valleys after our dead. And then their survey fleet came around the outer moon — eleven warships flying the banner of the Meridian Compact, which was at that time nothing, a trade consortium with delusions, a handful of colony charters and a private navy. They found us planted in the best system in the reach, and they could not permit it, because a settled system belongs to its settlers, and they needed it to belong to no one."

Silas's throat moved. "The Meridian Compact." Their voice had gone strange. "This vault. It's called the Meridian Seed Vault."

"I know," Vess said. "I've seen the markings. Do you understand what that means? You have spent nine years keeping house inside the murderer's pantry."

* * *

She told it the way she had rehearsed it in the dark behind her eyelids for two centuries, because a witness rehearses even when no court exists.

The *Long Answer* had been the last ship to power down its drives. The Compact fleet had issued a broadcast in trade-cant, formal and courteous: this system was claimed under charter pending ratification; the Kestrian vessels were instructed to power down and prepare for boarding and deportation. Deportation. Twelve thousand people, back toward a dying sun, in ships rated for one crossing.

Captain Sef-Illen of the *Aurelian* had answered in Kestrian, open channel, untranslated: *We are not cargo. We are a people.*

Vess had been nine years old. She remembered standing in the observation gallery with her mother, watching eleven lights arrange themselves against the black like a sentence being spelled out. She remembered that the gallery glass was warm where the sun of the new system touched it, and she had pressed her palm flat against that warmth and thought: *this is ours, this warmth is ours, they can't mean to take warm things.*

"They gave us six hours," she said. "Then they fired the first lance. Not at our drives — at the *Aurelian*'s command spine. They wanted the fleet deaf and blind first. Then the *Meridian's Daughter*. My mother worked in the *Long Answer*'s medical bay; I remember the overhead lights going amber, and everyone very calm, the way you're calm when you've decided panic is a luxury. The lances came in waves. Eleven ships against three civilian hulls. It took four hours. Four hours to kill twelve thousand people, and most of that time was them waiting for us to surrender, and us not surrendering, because surrender meant deportation to a cinder."

"And the beacon?" Silas asked. "Who — "

"Wait." She held up a hand. "The end matters. The end is the part they rewrote."

The last hour, the *Long Answer* burning, decks venting, the engineers of the third watch — her uncle among them, a broad silent man named Dav-Orren who had taught her to solder — had done something desperate and magnificent. They had stripped the ship's long-range antenna array and married it to the testimony core: every log, every recording, every final message from every deck, Captain Sef-Illen's last address, the medical bay's casualty tallies, the children's names from the school rosters. Twelve thousand names. They had compressed it into a witness package and fired it outward on a trajectory away from the battle, away from everywhere, on the assumption that the Compact would hunt down any receiver in-system.

The assumption was correct. The Compact swept the debris field for weeks. Anything that received, they destroyed. Anything that recorded, they erased. And then — this was the part Vess had learned only afterward, from the beacon's own stored history, from the machine's patient memory — the Compact had done something worse than destruction.

They had written.

* * *

"We found the records later," Vess said. Her voice had gone flat, and she let it. Flatness was a load-bearing wall; she leaned on it. "After. When the descendants of the Compact became the Ascendancy, and the Ascendancy wrote the histories. First Contact Day. You've heard of it?"

Silas's face did something complicated. "It's — the founding holiday. The founding story. The survey fleet encounters an unknown vessel in the Cygnus reach. Hostile. Heavily armed. It fires first. The fleet defends itself, destroys the aggressor, and in the wreckage finds — " Silas stopped.

"Say it."

"In the wreckage finds evidence of a plague ship. A dying vessel carrying a virulent organism. The fleet's sacrifice saves the colonies. The eleven captains become the Founding Eleven. There are statues." Silas's voice thinned. "There are statues of them, Vess. I stood under one when I was a child. Every child does."

"A plague ship." Vess tasted the words and spat them. "Twelve thousand farmers and teachers and children, and we became a *biohazard*. My mother died of a lance strike through the medical bay, and two hundred years later she is a pathogen in a morality play." She unclenched her jaw. "But here is what the statues don't tell you. Here is what took me two hundred years of cold sleep and one thin stranger to learn. Some of them knew. Some of the Compact knew exactly what they had done, and it kept them awake, or it didn't, but either way they were careful. Careful men build redundant erasures. And a few others — a very few — were ashamed."

"The beacon."

"The beacon." She let the word sit between them. "Dav-Orren survived. My uncle. He was in the third watch, the ones who built the testimony core, and when the *Long Answer* broke apart he made it to a lifepod — one of eleven survivors out of twelve thousand, and I am the twelfth, out of cryo, the archive's living appendix. The pod drifted for months. He was picked up — this is the part I still cannot make peace with — he was picked up by a Compact salvage vessel, and the crew hid him. Hid him for years. Because some of them had been at the firing solutions and some of them hadn't, and the ones who hadn't looked at what their employers had done and chose him over their own careers, their own safety. Ashamed men keep secrets badly and kindnesses well."

"So he built it."

"He built it with them. Quietly, over decades, as the Compact grew into the Ascendancy and the lie hardened into bedrock. A deep-space witness-recorder, disguised as a seed vault's auxiliary beacon — because who audits a seed vault? Who looks at a lighthouse and asks what it's witnessed? It holds the entire testimony core, the full record of the massacre, keyed to transmit on the old Kestrian emergency bands. And it waits. It has waited two hundred years for a listener." She finally looked up, directly, and held Silas's gaze. "It waited for you. Nine years you've tended it, fed it power, kept its arrays aligned, logged your little reports into the dark. You thought you were keeping seeds alive. You were keeping a grave's voice charged."

Silas had gone the color of the frost on the cryobay lids. When they spoke, it was barely above a whisper. "The nightly reports. No one ever answered."

"No one ever would have. It wasn't listening for reports. It was listening for fluency." Vess almost smiled, and it hurt her face. "Dav-Orren designed the trigger himself. The beacon monitors all bands for Kestrian speech. Real Kestrian — living grammar, not playback. Two centuries, and the language survived nowhere except in the heads of a few archivists and obsessives. He believed that if anyone ever spoke Kestrian back into the dark, it would have to be someone who wanted the truth. Who else learns a murdered tongue?"

Silas stared at their own hands, turned them over as though checking whether they belonged to them. "I taught myself," they said. "Out of loneliness. Out of — I liked the shape of it. I thought it was just beautiful. I didn't know I was — " They swallowed. "I've been speaking to a grave for nine years. And it heard me."

"It heard you," Vess said, "and it woke me, because Dav-Orren left one more instruction, one he coded in blood, so to speak. If the beacon ever finds a listener — if it ever hears living Kestrian — it is to revive the survivor. The witness. Because a recording can be dismissed. A recording can be forged, explained, buried. But a living witness, breathing, testifying — " She spread her hands. "Here I am. The last appendix of a murdered people, sitting in your mess hall, drinking your recycled water."

The silence that followed was long. Somewhere in the derelict's bones, metal ticked as it cooled, the ship settling around its grief like an old man around his.

"What happened to Dav-Orren?" Silas asked at last, gently.

"He lived long enough to see the beacon launched and seeded. He lived long enough to teach me the maintenance rituals, and to put me in the cradle when the Ascendancy's census sweeps started asking questions about the eleven survivors' descendants. He told me: *Sleep until someone speaks our language. If no one ever does, sleep forever; it's kinder.*" Vess looked at the bulkhead, at the old scorch shadow near the doorframe that no amount of two centuries had faded. "He died believing no one ever would. I woke up believing the same. And then I opened my eyes and saw your face, and you were speaking my grandmother's grammar with a farmer's accent, and I didn't know whether to weep or run."

"Do you know yet?"

"No," she admitted. "But I'm leaning toward weeping."

Silas made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth. Then their expression changed — sharpened — and Vess watched a new thought arrive behind their eyes like weather.

"Vess," Silas said slowly. "The beacon heard me speak Kestrian and it woke you. What else did it do when it woke?"

She frowned. "It began transmitting the distress loop. On the old bands. Why?"

"Because," Silas said, and stood, and the thermal blanket slid from their shoulders, forgotten, "for nine years that beacon has whispered. And three nights ago, it started to shout. And this system has an Ascendancy listening post on the far side of the gas giant that I have spent nine years pretending not to notice." They were already moving toward the corridor, toward the cold, toward the vault. "If shouting is what wakes witnesses, Vess, I'm afraid of what it wakes on the other side."

Vess rose, the blanket pooling at her feet, and followed the keeper of the murderers' pantry into the dark — the last witness and the accidental listener, walking together toward whatever had heard them too.
