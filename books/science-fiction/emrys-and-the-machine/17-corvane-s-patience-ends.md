## 17. Corvane's Patience Ends

### Silas

The morning Corvane's patience ended began like any other morning in the vault: cold light through the observation slits, breath fogging, the smell of frost and machine oil and, faintly now, of turned earth. Silas stood in Hall Four with a pry bar in their hands and a crate of pre-collapse wheat cultivars open at their feet, and they thought, not for the first time, that grief had a texture. It was dry. It was papery. It came off on the fingers like chaff.

They had been dismantling their own life for eleven days.

* * *

### Vess

Vess felt it before the instruments did — a change in the pressure of things, the way the air in the derelict seemed to lean toward the vault as if listening. They had spent enough years aboard Kestrian ships to know when a hull was paying attention to something.

"Silas." Their voice carried down the corridor they'd cut between the ship and the array shaft, rough with dust. "Your visitor is coming back."

On the workbench beside them, the testimony core sat in its cradle of salvaged cable, humming its low patient note. Two centuries of the dead, folded into something the size of a heart. Vess had taken to resting a hand on it while they worked, the way you'd rest a hand on a sleeping child's back — not because it needed comfort, but because Vess did.

The vault's external sensors painted the picture Silas relayed a moment later, voice thin over the comm: one vessel, descending from high orbit, running dark until the last hour. The same silhouette as before. The same crest, if crests could be said to have silhouettes — and Vess believed they could. They had grown up beneath that crest. It had been carved into the lintel of every public building on the colony ship *Ardent Mercy*, and it had been the last thing visible through the viewport before the founding fleet's guns opened the ship like a seed pod.

Corvane was coming. And this time he was not coming alone.

* * *

### Silas

They landed at dawn, three shuttles where before there had been one, and Silas watched them come down through the observation slit with the calm of a person who has already decided how a thing will end and is only waiting for the details to arrive.

Eleven days of prying up deck plates. Eleven days of hauling crates of seeds into the derelict's hold — Vess insisting, and Silas agreeing, that the vault's living cargo deserved to survive even if nothing else did. Eleven days of splicing the buried antenna array back to life, joint by corroded joint, while the reactor's countdown ticked down from nineteen days to eight. And for eleven days, Corvane's ship had hung in orbit like a held breath.

Silas had known the stalling would run out. They had simply hoped the math would be different — hoped the broadcast would come first, that Corvane would arrive to find the truth already loose in the system like spilled water, impossible to gather up again.

Instead there were eight days left, an array half-repaired, and three shuttles kicking frost across the landing apron.

"Silas Marchetti." Corvane's voice, when the inspector stepped out onto the apron, was as courteous as ever, pitched to carry in the thin cold air. He wore his coat buttoned to the throat this time, and behind him came six figures in Ascendancy grey, moving with the unhurried precision of people who had done this many times. "I'd hoped my last visit would be our last conversation."

"You could have just sent a letter," Silas said.

Something flickered at the corner of Corvane's mouth — appreciation, maybe, or its counterfeit. "May we come inside?"

"You're going to whether I say yes or not."

"I am," Corvane agreed pleasantly. "But I find I'd rather you said yes. It matters to me, caretaker, how things are done properly."

*There it is,* Silas thought, walking him through the airlock. *Properly. The whole Ascendancy in two words.*

* * *

### Vess

From the shadow of the derelict's engineering section, Vess watched the inspection party enter the vault and made themselves a promise: whatever happened next, they would not watch Silas walk out of it under escort without doing something. They didn't yet know what the something was. Grief and love, Vess was learning, were both terrible strategists.

They turned back to the core. The array needed another six hours of work at minimum — the final coupling, deep in the shaft beneath Hall Seven, where the Kestrian engineers had hidden their true purpose under two hundred meters of curated biodiversity. Six hours they might not have.

Then the derelict's passive array chimed, soft as a struck glass, and Vess's blood went cold.

Corvane's lead shuttle hadn't docked at the apron. A second team had gone straight to the vault's administrative spine — and they were inside the archive.

* * *

### Silas

Silas heard the discovery before they saw it: the sharp double-footfall of someone stopping short, the clipped exchange in the corridor outside Hall Two, and then Corvane turning to look at Silas with an expression that had shed its courtesy like a coat.

"The archive," he said. "Walk with me."

He walked fast. In the archive chamber, two of his grey-clad subordinates stood before the main terminal, and on the terminal's display, glowing in the dim room like something drowned surfacing, was the historical record of the Kestrian Massacre — all four thousand entries of it, unsealed, cross-indexed, annotated in Silas's own hand with the discrepancies Vess had shown them. The dates that didn't align. The fleet manifests that named ships history swore had never been built. The casualty figures that matched no battle but fit, precisely, terribly, the destruction of a civilian colony vessel.

"When I was here last," Corvane said quietly, "this archive confirmed the official record. Flawlessly. I told my superiors so." He turned. His eyes were not angry. That was the worst part. They were *hurt*. "You've been in here since."

"Yes."

"Why?"

Silas had rehearsed lies for this moment and found, standing in it, that they had no appetite for any of them. "Because the beacon isn't a seed beacon. Because a woman woke up out of a wrecked ship in your sensor shadow carrying two hundred years of testimony your family's fleet tried to burn. Because I checked, Inspector. I checked everything. And the record doesn't just disagree with her." Silas met his eyes. "It disagrees with itself, in forty places, and every one of those places was edited by the same hand."

The room was very still. One of the grey figures shifted weight, boot creaking on old decking.

"That's a serious accusation," Corvane said.

"It's a serious archive."

For one long moment — Silas would replay this moment for the rest of their life — Corvane looked at the terminal, and his face did something complicated and young. Then he closed his eyes briefly, and when he opened them the inspector was back, sealed and correct, and he said, in a voice like a door shutting:

"Take the caretaker into custody. Secure the archive. And find the other one."

* * *

### Vess

They found the other one's trail first, and it led exactly where Vess wanted it to: away.

Vess had spent the last hour making sure of that — dragging equipment through the derelict's aft sections, letting heat bloom on sensors that should have been cold, opening and closing hatches in a pattern designed to read as panic. Let them chase noise. Let them think the Kestrian was cornered and stupid.

The truth was that Vess had never been less panicked in their life. There was a strange clarity that came from having nothing left to protect except the one thing worth protecting. The core rode against their chest in a sling made from a seed-crate strap, warm with its own hum, and every heartbeat Vess felt through it felt answered.

They went down through the array shaft instead of up through the corridors — the route Silas had mapped, the route the vault's builders had intended, because the Kestrian engineers who designed this place had understood, two centuries ago, that someday someone would need to move unseen beneath the feet of the victors. Vess whispered thanks to them as they climbed. Dead language, dead engineers, live gratitude.

Above, muffled through meters of rock and steel, boots were hammering through the seed halls. Someone shouted. Somewhere a bulkhead slammed. And then, cutting through it all, Silas's voice on the open comm channel — calm, almost conversational, pitched deliberately loud enough for anyone monitoring:

*"Vess, if you can hear me — the eastern access is compromised. Don't come for me. Finish the coupling. That's an order from your keeper."*

A pause. Then, softer, in Kestrian, accent still clumsy, vowels still wrong, and more beautiful for it:

*"Sesh-ka ren. I am here. Go."*

*I am here.* The oldest words in the Kestrian tongue. The words you said so the dying knew they weren't alone.

Vess pressed their forehead against the cold wall of the shaft, once, hard enough to hurt. Then they climbed.

* * *

### Silas

They took Silas's hands first, which Silas found oddly dignified of them. Then they walked them out through Hall Three, past the racks of empty crates, past the bare spots on the floor where nine years of careful arrangement had been dismantled crate by crate, and Silas looked at the wounds they had carved into their own sanctuary and felt — not regret. Regret required believing you'd chosen wrongly.

Corvane walked beside them. He had not spoken since the order; he spoke now, low, without looking over.

"The woman. Where is she?"

"Gone."

"Gone where?"

"Inspector," Silas said, "you've read my personnel file. Nine years of nightly reports, filed on schedule, never missed. You know what I am. I'm a person who keeps records." They let that sit. "Whatever she is, wherever she's going — it's in the record now. You can't arrest arithmetic."

Corvane stopped walking. For a moment the two of them stood in the middle of Hall Three, surrounded by the smell of opened earth and disturbed cold, and the inspector studied Silas with the expression of a man reading a document he has suddenly realized is addressed to him personally.

"You understand what happens to you," he said. "If what you've done is what I think you've done."

"I've had eight days to understand it. I recommend starting now. You have less time than you think."

"What does that mean?"

Silas smiled — the first real smile in days, and it felt strange on their face, like a muscle remembered from a previous body. "It means you should ask yourself why the reactor's countdown started the week after your first visit. It means the vault was designed by people smarter than both of us, and they planned for exactly this. And it means, Inspector Corvane—" they leaned close, and dropped their voice to the register of a secret, "—that everything your family is, is about to be read aloud."

The blow, when it came, was almost gentle: a hand on the shoulder, turning them, the grey figures closing in. Custody. Escort. The long walk down the apron toward the shuttle, frost smoking underfoot, the vault rising behind them with its lights burning in every hall Silas had emptied.

Silas did not look back. Looking back was for people who thought they might return.

* * *

### Vess

The final coupling fought them for forty minutes. Vess worked with bleeding knuckles in the dark of the shaft, the core slung against their chest, and above them — through rock, through steel, through the whole sleeping body of the vault — they felt the Ascendancy moving, searching, too late and in the wrong places.

At 14:07 vault time, the coupling seated home with a click that Vess felt in their teeth.

The array woke like something exhaling after two hundred years.

Power crawled up out of the vault's bones and into the antenna lattice threaded beneath the seed halls, and status glyphs bloomed green along Vess's wrist display, one after another, a litany, a rosary: *coupled, charged, aligned, ready.*

Ready. Eight days early. With the vault full of enemies and Silas somewhere inside it in grey hands, and the reactor counting down beneath them all like a bomb wearing a clock's face.

Vess closed their eyes. Against their chest, the core hummed, and in the hum — imagination, surely, but Vess chose to believe it — were voices. *Ardent Mercy*, saying: *we were erased. We were here. Remember us.*

"I remember," Vess said aloud, in Kestrian, in the dark. "All of you. I remember."

Then they opened their eyes, and put their hand on the firing key, and waited — because somewhere above them, in a shuttle or a cell, a stubborn, impossible, beloved keeper of small things had once logged a report every night to no one, and Vess meant to make sure that tonight, when the sky filled with the truth, Silas Marchetti would hear it begin.

The countdown read seven days, twenty-two hours.

Vess decided they couldn't wait.

Their thumb moved toward the key — and the comm channel crackled open, and Corvane's voice, tight and stripped of all its polish, said:

*"Caretaker. Tell me how to stop it. Tell me now, or tell God."*

And Vess, smiling for the first time in two hundred years, keyed the channel and answered in a dead tongue that was about to be born again.
