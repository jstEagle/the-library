## 19. The Broadcast

The cold archive smelled of frost and old paper and, beneath it all, the faint mineral breath of the vault itself — that deep geological exhalation Silas had once described to me as *the building dreaming*. I had lived inside this place for nineteen days now, long enough to know its moods, its groaning joints, the way its lights dimmed by a fraction each time the reactor's countdown ticked over another hour. Long enough to love it, in the way you come to love a dying thing.

I stood in the dark between the seed racks with the design documents spread across a workbench, my gloved fingers trembling not from the cold but from what they had found.

The plans were Kestrian. Not merely Kestrian in origin — Kestrian in *hand*. The notation system was ours, the looping script my grandmother had used for her garden ledgers, the same characters I had spent two hundred years forgetting and three weeks remembering under Silas's patient, halting tutelage. And there, in the margin of the third sheet, a signature I knew. *Elyan Vosk.* My mother's teacher. A man whose name existed nowhere in any Ascendancy record because he had never officially existed at all.

He had designed the beacon. He had designed the vault. He had buried an antenna array beneath ten thousand seed crates and then let humanity's archivists fill his machine with their precious grains and legumes, never knowing they were tending a weapon aimed at their own comfortable lies.

*The seeds were never the point,* I thought, and laughed — a small, cracked sound that echoed strangely off the frost-glazed racks. Somewhere above me, Corvane's soldiers were marching Silas Marchetti toward a holding cell, and here I was, laughing alone in the dark like a ghost who had finally remembered how to be angry.

The array ran beneath the entire eastern hall: a lattice of conductive filament woven through the foundation strata, dormant, intact, waiting. It needed only power and a signal source. Power was the problem. The reactor was the problem. The reactor was always the problem now — a wounded animal counting down its own death, thirty days collapsed into hours, its purge sequence locked behind protocols no one alive could override.

But a reactor about to detonate is also a reactor about to give up everything it has.

I pressed my palm flat against the cold floor and felt it: a vibration so low it was less sound than conviction, the reactor's death-song rising through the bedrock like a pulse. Elyan Vosk had built his antenna into the bones of this place. If the vault was going to die, it would die screaming the truth.

* * *

The derelict's transmitter bay still smelled of burnt insulation and, faintly, of home — that impossible scent memory, two centuries stale, of Kestrian ship-plastic and the particular ozone tang of our engineering decks. I had crawled back aboard through the umbilical Silas and I had rigged between the ship and the vault's maintenance airlock, moving fast, keeping below the sightlines of the Ascendancy patrol skiffs that circled outside like patient carrion birds.

Silas was in custody. That fact sat in my chest like swallowed ice.

They had gone willingly. That was the part I couldn't stop turning over. When Corvane's team breached the vault — when the alarms shrieked and the doors cycled open on armed silhouettes — Silas had stepped forward, hands raised, voice calm as a cataloguer's, and said: *The archive tampering was mine. The salvage is mine. There's no one else.* A lie so clean it took me a moment to understand it was a lie, and then another moment to understand what it was buying me.

Time. Seconds, maybe minutes. A corridor of soldiers looking at one quiet caretaker instead of searching the dark for a ghost from a massacre everyone believed was a victory.

I had wanted to scream at them for it. I had wanted to kiss them. Both impulses arrived together and neither had anywhere to go, so I had done what Kestrians do with grief too large to hold: I put it in a box, sealed the box, and got to work.

The testimony core sat cradled in the transmitter bay's last functioning cradle — a cylinder the size of a forearm, dense with two centuries of stored witness. Every voice recorded before the end. Every name. My mother's voice was in there somewhere; I had never dared play it. The core hummed against my palms like something alive, like something *waiting*, and I thought: two hundred years you have waited, and I will not make you wait one minute longer than necessary.

I jacked the core into the derelict's transmission bus. Dead. Of course dead — the ship's broadcast systems had been slagged in the attack, every antenna sheared off or fused. The core could shout all it wanted into a throat that no longer existed.

Which left only one throat in range big enough.

The buried array. Beneath the seed halls. Woven through the vault's own structure like roots under a forest floor.

And the only power source strong enough to drive it, in the time remaining, was a reactor with fifty-one minutes left on its countdown.

* * *

Forty minutes. The number glowed on my wrist display as I hauled myself down the maintenance shaft toward the array junction, and I made myself stop looking at it, because looking at it was a way of doing nothing.

The shaft was a vertical throat of rust and cable, lit by my headlamp in a swaying circle. My breath fogged. Below me, the vault's foundations opened into the space where Elyan Vosk's true work waited — a cathedral of filament and ceramic insulator, spreading out under the seed halls like the root system of some enormous sleeping tree. My headlamp swept across it and I stopped breathing.

It was beautiful. That was the unbearable thing. Our engineers had always built beautifully — the colony ships had been beautiful, the beacon had been beautiful, everything we made carried that signature care, that insistence that even a machine built to die should die elegantly. The filaments descended in slow spirals, silvered with frost, converging on a central junction node the size of a coffin. Someone had etched words into the node's casing in Kestrian script, and I dropped down beside it and brushed away the frost with my sleeve to read them.

*I speak for the ones who were not allowed to speak.*

Below that, smaller: *If you are reading this, someone kept faith.*

I knelt there longer than I could afford. Nineteen days ago I had woken from cryostasis into a ruined ship and a stranger's face, and I had hated that stranger with the whole force of my grief — assumed them a descendant of the butchers, a thief standing in our wreckage. And then that stranger had learned my language syllable by syllable, had eaten our mourning bread without flinching, had stood in front of an Ascendancy inspector and lied for the first time in nine years, and had finally stood in front of soldiers and given themselves up so that I could crawl away into the dark with the truth in my arms.

*Someone kept faith.* Yes. In the most improbable shape imaginable: a lonely keeper of seeds who talked to machines because no one else would listen.

Thirty-four minutes.

I opened the junction node. Inside, the coupling points waited, pristine under their frost — power input, signal input, ground. I connected the testimony core first, splicing its output leads to the signal input with hands that shook only a little, and when the core sensed a live circuit it woke with a soft rising tone, like a sleeper drawing the first breath after long illness. Its status light turned amber. Ready. Waiting for power.

Power meant the reactor. Power meant going up, into the heart of the vault, into the chamber where the countdown lived, and convincing a dying machine to spend its final strength on one last act instead of simply dying.

There was no override. I had searched for one for nineteen days alongside Silas, and we had found nothing, because the purge protocol was designed to be unbreakable — Ascendancy engineering, fail-safe upon fail-safe, a machine built to destroy evidence reliably. You could not tell it to stop.

But maybe you didn't need it to stop. Maybe you just needed it to hesitate. To divert. For ninety seconds, even sixty, enough current rerouted through the array to carry the core's signal out through the vault's structure and into every open channel in the system before the fire took it all.

I climbed.

* * *

Twenty-two minutes when I reached the reactor gallery, and the room itself had changed. I had been in it before, with Silas, tracing conduits, learning its geography. Now it felt like standing inside a struck bell. The whole chamber vibrated. Warning light washed the walls in slow arterial pulses, and the reactor's housing — a great ribbed column of alloy rising through the vault's spine — had begun, very softly, to sing. A high thin note underneath the vibration. Metal remembering it could fail.

The control console still accepted my credentials — Silas's credentials, actually, lent to me in the first week with a shy formality that had made me laugh for the first time since waking. *Caretaker access, full privileges,* Silas had said. *It's yours. It was always supposed to be somebody's.* I logged in and the system greeted me with the countdown, huge and indifferent:

**00:21:44 TO PURGE COMPLETION.**

Beneath it, the power routing table. The reactor fed the vault's systems through six primary buses. Five of them were already dark, shed deliberately to concentrate remaining output into containment systems that would fail anyway. One bus still lived: the one feeding the beacon.

The beacon. Silas's life's work. Nine years of nightly reports sent into silence, nine years of tending a machine whose true purpose neither of us had understood until it began whispering in a dead tongue. The beacon drew power constantly, patiently, holding its watch.

I stared at the routing controls and understood what I had to do, and my hands went still on the console.

Divert the beacon's bus. Reroute everything — all of it, the reactor's whole remaining strength — into the buried array. The beacon would go dark. Not paused. Dark, permanently, because in twenty-one minutes the reactor would take the entire vault with it, and the beacon with the vault, and the nightly ritual of a lonely keeper logging reports to no one would end forever in fire.

That beacon was the closest thing Silas Marchetti had ever had to a purpose. They had told me so on the night we ate mourning bread together, in the low voice people use for confessions: *It was mine. Nobody else wanted it. That was enough, for a long time. Being wanted by something, even a machine.*

And I was about to kill it.

I thought about my mother's voice in the core. About the names. About the children of the Kestrian colony ship who had died believing no one would ever know they had existed, because the victors wrote history and the victors wrote us out of it. I thought about Corvane's polished face, the family crest worn like a medal over an old wound they'd inflicted themselves.

Then I thought about Silas, sitting in a holding cell right now, choosing me over the only belonging they'd ever had — and choosing it *knowing* what it cost, because they had already begun dismantling their life's work crate by crate, seed hall by seed hall, and had told me, in the dark of the eastern hall with dust on their hands: *Things aren't what they're for. Things are what they do.*

The beacon had watched for nine years and heard nothing. Tonight it would hear everything.

"Forgive me," I said — to the beacon, to Silas, to the vault, to whoever keeps accounts of such things — and I rerouted the bus.

The console fought me. Fail-safes woke and demanded authorization, and I burned through them with Silas's credentials and my own stubbornness, override stacked on override, until the system asked its final question — **CONFIRM PERMANENT DIVERSION OF PRIMARY OUTPUT? THIS ACTION CANNOT BE REVERSED** — and I confirmed it with a keystroke harder than it needed to be.

Deep below my feet, the buried array drank.

I felt it happen before any display told me. The vibration in the floor changed character — gathered, focused, became directional, became *intent*. The frost on the gallery walls shivered. Somewhere beneath the seed halls, Elyan Vosk's silver roots woke after two centuries and began, at last, to conduct.

**00:18:07.**

I keyed the testimony core live.

* * *

It did not begin quietly. Nothing about it was quiet.

The core poured its charge into the array and the array seized the vault's own structure as its antenna — every strut, every conduit, every length of rail and rebar in the building becoming a singing wire — and the first thing that went out across every open channel in the system was a voice.

My mother's voice.

I hadn't known it would be hers. I hadn't dared look at the manifest. But the core played testimony in the order it was recorded, and the first recording, made in the last hour of the colony ship's life, was my mother reporting for the witness log — her voice, level and clear and terribly young, speaking formal Kestrian into a recorder while alarms she refused to acknowledge sounded behind her:

*"This is Witness Entry One, Meridian Beacon Project. I am Sefa il-Vess-Korran, recording for whoever comes after. At 0400 fleet-standard today, the vessel Ascendant Dawn and eleven ships bearing the crest of House Corvane opened fire on the Kestrian colony fleet without challenge, without parley, without warning. We are not combatants. We have never been combatants. We are farmers and teachers and engineers carrying four thousand souls to a new world, and they are killing us, and I am recording it, and if you are hearing this, then someone, somewhere, kept faith."*

My knees gave. I sat down hard on the gallery deck, in the pulsing red light, and listened to my dead mother's voice fill the vault and pour out through its bones into the sky.

The core moved on. Entry Two. Entry Three. Voices I knew and voices I didn't, names spoken slowly and clearly so they would survive transcription, the sounds of the attack itself — hull breaches, the last transmissions of ships I had slept aboard as a child — all of it streaming out through the array in a flood that no jamming band could contain, because the array wasn't broadcasting *on* a frequency so much as *through* the structure of the vault itself, a broadband scream wired into the planet's own skin. Corvane's ships could jam a band. They could not jam a building.

On my wrist display, channel indicators lit up one after another. Colony relay. Mining platform Meridian-4. The orbital transfer station. The frontier buoys. Each one flickered from standby to receiving, receiving, receiving. Somewhere out there, dockworkers and pilots and colonists were stopping mid-shift, staring at their consoles, hearing a language extinct for two hundred years describe their heroes' founding crime in the voices of its victims.

Triumph rose in me like water filling a hull — vast, lifting, terrifying. And terror came with it, inseparable, the same sensation exactly, because triumph and terror at the end of things are the same feeling wearing different clothes.

**00:11:52.**

The core reported its buffer: forty percent transmitted. The array was drinking everything the reactor could give, and the reactor was giving gloriously, spending its death like a miser suddenly generous, but the testimony was long. Two hundred years of waiting makes for a lot of words.

I did the arithmetic and didn't like it. At this rate, the fire would arrive before the last entry did. The final recordings — the ones made aboard the derelict, the engineer's log, the cryobay manifest, the names of the survivors who sealed themselves into sleep hoping someone would come — those were queued last. Those would burn unread.

Unless the signal outlived the vault.

I stared at the console, at the routing table, at the single living thread between the reactor and the array, and saw the answer lying there like a blade: the array didn't need the reactor. It needed *power*. And in eleven minutes there was going to be a very great deal of power released in this building all at once, and Elyan Vosk, meticulous, faithful, grieving Elyan Vosk, had built his antenna into the vault's load-bearing structure itself.

He had known. Some ancestor-part of him, some engineer's instinct two centuries deep, had known that the vault might die violently, and had woven his filament roots through exactly the strata that would carry the surge. The detonation wouldn't end the broadcast. The detonation would be the broadcast — one final, system-wide shout, the vault's death converting directly into signal, carrying the last of the testimony farther than any steady transmission could reach.

All I had to do was keep the core coupled. Keep the connection alive until the end, and let the fire finish the sentence.

So I stayed.

I wrapped the core's casing in thermal blanket from the emergency stores and held it against my chest like a child, and I sat in the red pulsing dark, and I listened to my mother's voice, and then to strangers' voices, and then to the engineer Elyan Vosk himself, older now, recording alone in this same vault before I was born:

*"If the beacon fails, the vault will finish the work. I have arranged it. Forgive the deception, future keeper. Seeds can be regrown. Silence cannot."*

I laughed again, wet-eyed, alone in the ringing dark. *Future keeper.* He had meant Silas. Nine years early, he had meant Silas, the stranger who kept faith without knowing what they were keeping.

**00:04:31.**

Seventy percent. Eighty. The singing in the reactor's spine had risen to a whine I felt in my teeth. Frost sublimated off the walls in ghosting veils. Outside, distantly, I heard the Ascendancy skiffs change their orbit — Corvane's people finally understanding what was happening, too late, always too late, the way they had been too late to matter two hundred years ago when they thought winning was the same as ending.

Eighty-eight percent. And then, threading through the testimony, arriving not through my ears but through the vault's own vibrating structure, a new sound.

A human voice. Live. Close.

I knew before I located the source that it was coming from the holding level — carried to me through the same bones that carried the broadcast, the vault conducting everything now, loyal to the last. Silas Marchetti's voice, hoarse, urgent, addressed to guards who were no longer listening because they were staring at their own terminals, at the impossible words scrolling across every screen in the building:

"—that's Kestrian. That's the *massacre*, don't you understand, that's the real record, it's—"

And then, cutting through, pitched to carry, pitched — I realized with a fullness in my chest that hurt more than fear — pitched for *me*:

*"Vess! Whatever happens — I hear it! Do you hear me? I hear it! It's working!"*

I pressed my forehead against the thermal-wrapped core and answered aloud, though I had no way to send it, though the words could only travel down through the floor and up through the walls and maybe, maybe, along the same conductive blood to wherever Silas was held:

"I hear you. I hear you, Silas. Stay down. Stay away from the outer walls."

Ninety-three percent. Ninety-six.

The whine became a roar. The red pulses strobed faster. On my display, the final entries queued: the engineer's last log, the cryobay manifest, four hundred and twelve names read aloud in a voice that shook and did not stop.

Ninety-nine percent.

I held the core tighter and spoke the old rite into the noise, the mourning words Silas had learned to say with me, the ones that mean *you are witnessed, you are witnessed, you are witnessed* — and the reactor reached zero, and the world turned white.

* * *

I would remember the next moments afterward in fragments, the way survivors remember everything: the sound arriving not as sound but as pressure, as a hand closing around the whole sky. Light through my closed eyelids. The core hot against my sternum even through the blanket, still transmitting, still pouring itself out as the detonation surge tore down through the vault's structure and into the array and out — *out*, farther, the final entries riding the blast wave across the system like sparks riding an explosion, the last names of my people shouted by a dying building into every ear that had one.

Then silence. Real silence, total, the first in nineteen days. No reactor song. No vibration. No heartbeat under the floor.

The vault was gone. Above me — I lay in the shattered mouth of the maintenance tunnel, half-buried, ears ringing, tasting blood and dust — above me there was no ceiling, only sky, and smoke rising in a great slow column, and stars beginning to show through the torn roof of the world.

The core was dead in my arms, its work finished, its every word delivered. I checked the wrist display with a hand that barely obeyed me. Every channel in range showed the same thing: reception logs, replay requests, the testimony echoing outward from relay to relay, unkillable now, copied beyond anyone's power to erase. You cannot burn a truth that has already been heard by ten thousand listeners.

Corvane's skiffs hung motionless in the sky like insects that had forgotten their orders.

And somewhere in the ruin, in the smoke, in the settling ash of nine years of a lonely person's devotion — Silas. Alive. I had heard their voice minutes ago through the bones of a dying building, and I refused, with everything left in me, to believe the building had taken that voice with it.

I dug myself out of the rubble and stood, and the cold air hit the blood on my face, and I started walking toward the holding level's ruins, calling the only name that had ever answered me in the dark.
