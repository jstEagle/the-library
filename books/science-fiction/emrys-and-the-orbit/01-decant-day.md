## 1. Decant Day

The first thing Bram Halloran knew was the taste of copper.

Not blood—copper in the amniotic rinse, the techs said later, a flaw in Meridian's aging filtration. But at that moment, before there were techs or explanations, it was simply the first fact of existence: *I have a mouth, and it tastes of old coins.*

The second thing was the light. White, clinical, pulsing at a frequency designed to be soothing and failing at it, because nothing about being born could be made gentle by lumens.

The third thing was someone else's life pouring into him like sediment into still water.

They had warned him about this—or rather, the original had been warned, three years ago, when he'd signed the continuity contract with Halloran Biologics' Meridian branch. The memory of signing arrived whole: the scratch of the stylus, the recruiter's smile too white for a station running on recycled air, the phrase *emergent memory integration* delivered like a warranty term. The original had laughed. Bram could feel the laugh in his own throat, ghost-itchy, belonging to a man whose face he hadn't seen yet.

Then the memories came faster, and he stopped being able to sort them from himself.

A childhood on Ceres, dust storms and ration bars shaped like bricks. A mother who sang off-key on purpose to make her children groan. Twenty-two years as a station systems auditor, which meant twenty-two years of reading other people's numbers for the lies folded inside them. A divorce conducted over six months of delayed transmissions, each message arriving more tired than the last. The smell of the hydroponics bay on Meridian, wet and green and impossibly precious. The specific weight of a data slate held in a left hand that favored the thumb joint, which ached in cold weather—

His left hand ached now. It had never ached before. It had never done anything before.

Bram opened his eyes.

* * *

The decant room was smaller than the memories said it would be. Memory lies; it pads rooms with all the times you imagined them. This one was real: four meters by three, one growth tank drained and streaked with residue, two med-techs in corporate gray watching him from behind a portable console like men watching weather they didn't trust.

"Vitals are green," said the older one, a woman with a jaw like a hatch hinge. "Neural integration at ninety-one percent. That's fast."

"That's the new line," said the younger one, not looking up. "Halloran-series integration is—" He caught himself, glanced at his colleague, and went quiet in a way that told Bram everything: *you were not supposed to hear that.*

"I'm sorry," Bram said. His voice came out raw, a reed instrument played for the first time. "Could you tell me what day it is?"

"Station day 4,112," the woman said.

He reached for the calendar in the borrowed memory and found it, absurdly, instantly—the original had kept meticulous mental dates the way some people kept knives. Day 4,084 was circled in the original's mind. The day he was supposed to wake up here, in this room, after a scheduled maintenance decant cycle. Routine. Voluntary. A way to stretch a contract another decade.

Twenty-eight days late.

"Why?" Bram asked. "I was due on 4,084."

The med-techs exchanged a look that lasted half a second and contained an entire conversation he wasn't invited to.

"There was a delay," the woman said.

"What kind of delay?"

"The kind we're not cleared to discuss." She said it without apology, which he appreciated in a distant, clinical way—the way you appreciate the honesty of a wall when you walk into it. "Mr. Halloran, your motor function will stabilize within the hour. There's clothing and a meal allotment in the locker. Your quarters are unchanged, access code intact. Transport credit has been restored to your account."

"My account." He tested the word. It tasted strange, like wearing someone else's ring. "Whose account is it, exactly? Legally. Am I him, or am I a copy of him?"

"You're Bram Halloran," she said. "That's what the paperwork says."

"And the paperwork is what's real."

She didn't answer. The younger tech had stopped pretending to work and was staring at him with something between pity and fear, and Bram understood suddenly that the fear wasn't of him. It was of the subject. Whatever had happened during those twenty-eight days, these two had been standing close enough to its edges to be frightened.

"Three weeks," Bram said quietly. "I died three weeks ago. Didn't I."

The silence stretched. The younger tech looked at the floor. The older one looked at Bram, and her hatch-hinge jaw set, and for a moment he thought she might actually say something true.

"We're not cleared," she repeated, softer now. Almost kind. "I'm sorry. I'm sorry for all of it."

* * *

They let him dress alone, which was a mercy, and released him into the corridor outside the decant wing, which was not.

Meridian Station smelled wrong. That was the first thing the body noticed, ahead of the mind—the air had a flatness to it, a staleness underneath the standard scrubber tang, like water that had been boiled too many times. The original's memory supplied the comparison unbidden: *it never smelled like this.* Eleven years on this station and the man had known its air the way a sailor knows a sea.

The corridor lights were dimmed to conservation level. Half the directory panels along the spine were dark, and the ones that lived flickered with notices:

**RESIDENTS OF RING 3: RELOCATION CONSULTATIONS SCHEDULED. ATTENDANCE MANDATORY.**

**WATER RECLAMATION: SCHEDULED MAINTENANCE. ESTIMATED DURATION: INDEFINITE.**

**MERIDIAN STATION — A HALLORAN BIOLOGICS COMMUNITY.**

*A community.* The original had worked for them long enough to know what that word meant in a corporate mouth. It meant *inventory.*

Bram walked the familiar route to his quarters on legs that remembered the route better than he did. His body knew where the handrails were worn smooth. His feet took the shortcut through the maintenance junction without consulting him. Twice he stopped just to feel it—that eerie double-exposure of self, walking through a life like a man walking through a house he'd only ever seen in photographs, except the photographs were inside his skull and they moved.

His quarters opened to his palm without hesitation. The door didn't know the difference either. That was the thing no one told you about clones, he thought, standing in the doorway of a dead man's home: the universe itself declined to distinguish you. Locks, accounts, contracts, debts—all of it flowed across the gap in the original's pulse as if consciousness were just a password, and passwords could be inherited.

The quarters were exactly as the memories left them. Dishes in the dryer rack. A jacket over the chair back. On the desk, a data slate facedown, screen dark, positioned with the deliberate casualness of something meant to be found but not stumbled upon.

Bram stood in the middle of the room for a long time.

Grief arrived, and it was the strangest grief he could imagine: mourning for a man whose every feeling he could recall from the inside. He remembered—*the original remembered*—the divorce, and the ache of it. He remembered the pride of the audit that had gotten him promoted. He remembered deciding, eight months ago, to sign up for the decant cycle, and the small vanity of it: *I'll live longer than my father did.* And now Bram was the proof that the decision had been a lie sold to a man who'd bought it gratefully, and the man was dead, and Bram was the receipt.

"They killed you," he said aloud, to the empty room, to the jacket on the chair. His voice cracked on the second word. "Or you fell, or you suffocated, or whatever story they printed—but they killed you. Something in me knows it. Something in you knew it too, didn't it? That's why the slate's face-down."

He turned it over. The screen woke to a lock prompt, and the passcode surfaced in his memory like a fish rising: the date of the original's mother's birthday, reversed, plus the number of names on a list Bram had never seen but somehow knew existed.

The slate unlocked.

Most of it was ordinary—a dead man's cluttered desktop, invoices, a half-written letter to the ex-wife that ended mid-sentence in a way that made Bram's throat close. But there was a folder in the root directory with no name, just a string of characters, and when he opened it, it contained a single text file titled **PERIHELION — DO NOT SYNC**, and forty-one lines of names, each struck through in red.

And beneath the forty-first name, in the original's handwriting converted to type, a single unfinished sentence:

*If you're reading this, then I'm already—*

The file ended there. Cut off. Not deleted—cut, like a throat.

Bram sat down slowly in the dead man's chair, wearing the dead man's body, holding the dead man's slate, and felt the disorientation of the morning curdle into something harder and cleaner. Anger. Not the hot kind. The quiet kind, the kind that audited numbers for twenty-two years and knew how to wait.

Somewhere out beyond the hull, in the dark between Meridian and everything else, there was a ship called Perihelion that official logs insisted had never flown. Forty-one people had been crossed out in red. And the corporation that owned his body's contract, his quarters, his air, had delayed his decant by twenty-eight days—for reasons no one was cleared to discuss.

The door chimed.

Bram closed the file, killed the screen, and slid the slate beneath the mattress in one motion that belonged entirely to the original's muscle memory. When he opened the door, Dr. Yusra Chen was standing in the corridor with a medical kit under one arm and an expression that had already given up pretending this was a house call.

"Bram," she said. "We need to talk about what happened to you."

"Do we?" He kept his voice level. Kept the anger folded away, neat as a ledger. "Because the med-techs weren't cleared to discuss it. Are you?"

Chen glanced down the empty corridor, then stepped inside without waiting to be asked, and the door hissed shut behind her.

"No," she said. "And if you're smart, neither are you. Because the last person who asked those questions—" she met his eyes, and hers were old and tired and afraid, "—didn't get a decant cycle. He got three weeks of silence and a closed casket. Stop digging, Bram. Please. For both of us."

Bram Halloran stood very still in the borrowed quiet of his borrowed life, and smiled the original's smile.

"Doctor," he said, "I don't know how to tell you this. But I'm already dead. You're talking to the paperwork."
