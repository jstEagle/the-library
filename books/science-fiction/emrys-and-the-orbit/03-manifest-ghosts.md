## 3. Manifest Ghosts

The station's power logs did not lie. They simply didn't speak unless you knew their language.

Bram had learned it the way the original Bram must have learned it—three nights of cross-referencing load curves against duty rosters, tracing the station's electrical heartbeat back through eleven years of archived telemetry. Meridian's grid was old, honest machinery. Every kilowatt it ever spent had been recorded, timestamped, and filed by a system too primitive to know it should lie.

And eleven years and four months ago, the grid had screamed.

It started on a Tuesday, if the colony still kept Tuesdays. A draw on Docking Bay Nine's systems that spiked to three times the station's baseline—environmental scrubbers running at full capacity, gravity plating cycled through a full restart, the cargo cranes moving for forty-one hours without pause. Forty-one hours. Bram stared at the number until the digits blurred, then looked away, then looked back, because the coincidence was too large to be a coincidence and too terrible to be anything else.

Bay Nine was sealed now. Had been sealed for a decade, according to the maintenance schedule—or rather, according to the absence of any maintenance schedule at all. The last entry simply read: *Decommissioned. Structural review pending.* Pending for eleven years. On a station that couldn't afford to let a single square meter of hull go uninspected, an entire docking bay had been left to rot like a pulled tooth.

Bram pulled the cargo manifests for the same window.

The official record showed nothing. That was the first thing you learned on Meridian: the holes in the data were the data. But cargo ships needed power, and power left fingerprints. Bram built a shadow ledger, line by line—oxygen draw, water reclamation, caloric output from the galley systems, waste processing. You could hide a ship's name. You could hide its registry, its route, its crew list. You could not hide the fact that several hundred human bodies had been breathing aboard a station for six weeks.

The numbers gave Bram a population: between three hundred and forty souls, sustained in Bay Nine's volume for thirty-nine days. Then, over a period of nine days, the draw dropped in uneven steps—ninety here, sixty there, a long plateau, a final collapse to zero. Not a departure. Departures were clean, all at once, a shuttle's worth of breath leaving the grid in a single exhalation. This was a bleed. A trickle. Something leaving in pieces, or being removed in pieces, or—

Bram closed the file. Opened it again. Made themselves look.

The original's notes, spread across the desk in the dim quarters, had a page for this. *Perihelion. Launched from Titan Yards, 2,140 souls aboard per yard records. Officially: lost with all hands, deep-space accident, year of the grid spike. Unofficially: it docked here. I've seen the fuel requisitions. Ask the engineer. Ask Sefa.*

*Ask Sefa* was underlined twice, the second stroke hard enough to tear the paper.

Bram sat with that for a long time, the recycled air humming, the station's dying lights flickering in the corridor outside like a pulse searching for a rhythm. Then they pulled up the personnel roster and found the name still on it, eleven years later, stubborn as a rivet: *Okonkwo, Sefa — Chief Systems Engineer, Meridian Station.*

The only name from the Perihelion's era still stationed. The only name the original had bothered to underline.

* * *

Sefa Okonkwo worked the night shift because the night shift was honest. Days on Meridian were full of corporate liaisons and closure committees, men and women in pressed uniforms who asked her to initial documents declaring systems functional that she had personally watched fail. Nights were just her, a diagnostic rig, and the station's groaning skeleton—problems she could actually touch.

She heard the footsteps before she saw the visitor. Nobody walked the engineering decks at 0300 except ghosts and the recently cloned, and she'd already heard about the new Halloran. Everyone had. Three weeks dead and back again, asking questions with the dead man's mouth.

"You're the engineer," the clone said, stopping at the edge of her work light. Up close the resemblance was worse than the rumors. Same heavy brow. Same way of standing like the floor might be a lie. Sefa had known Bram Halloran for nine years—drunk, obsessive, kind in the specific way of people who couldn't stop looking at things that hurt to look at. Seeing that face on someone else was like hearing a recording of your own voice.

"I'm the engineer," she said. "You're the question I've been dreading for three weeks."

"Then you know what I'm going to ask."

"Perihelion." She said it quietly, the way you'd say a name at a funeral. "Halloran came to me eleven years ago with the same fuel requisitions. I told him to drop it. He didn't. Look where that got him." She turned back to her diagnostic rig, though she'd stopped reading it. "Look where it got me, staying quiet."

"Did you crew her?"

The question landed like a dropped tool. Sefa's hands went still on the console. "Who told you that?"

"The handwriting in his notes. He wrote *ask Sefa* like it was a debt. People don't underline debts they invented." Bram stepped into the light. "You knew the ship. You knew the bay. And you've spent eleven years on the one station in the system where the evidence is buried under your own feet."

For a long moment there was only the hum of the recyclers, working harder than they should, dying on a schedule someone else had written. Sefa thought about the oath she'd taken at twenty-three, the one engineers took and corporations ignored: *the structure is the crew; the crew is the structure.* She'd kept half of it. She'd kept the structure alive and let the crew drown.

"Second shift propulsion tech," she said finally. "Perihelion, Titan to Meridian, a colony run nobody was supposed to know the destination of. We were told it was a fuel stop. Six weeks in Bay Nine while the company 're-certified' our drive." She laughed, and it came out wrong, rusted. "You know what I remember most? The smell. Fourteen hundred people breathing through a scrubber system rated for nine hundred. We complained. We filed. Nothing came back."

"The grid says fourteen hundred," Bram said. "I ran the numbers. Oxygen draw, water, waste, heat. It matches a population of about three hundred forty *remaining* aboard when she docked—already down from launch. And then it bleeds out over nine days. Ninety. Sixty. Forty-one hours of continuous crane operation." They paused. "Forty-one hours, forty-one names crossed out in red in his notes. Is that a coincidence?"

Sefa closed her eyes.

She was back in the bay, eleven years younger, watching the cranes move cargo containers that were the wrong weight. She'd been the one who signed the mass-balance sheets—she'd had to, she was the only engineer on shift—and she had felt it through the deck plates, that wrongness, containers light as luggage when they should have weighed what a human body weighs. She'd told herself: *equipment. Salvage. Personal effects.* She'd told herself for eleven years.

"Not a coincidence," she said. Her voice was steady. She was proud of that, later, and ashamed of it too. "Forty-one. That's the number that went into the void containers. I signed the mass sheets. I didn't know. I *didn't know*—" She stopped, pressed her palm flat against the console, against the station's heartbeat. "They logged them as cargo loss. Forty-one souls, logged as *cargo loss*, and the company paid out a settlement to a shell registry, and the Perihelion was re-registered and flown to a scrapyard that doesn't exist, and I kept my head down and fixed the air recyclers for eleven years."

Bram hadn't moved. "You said fourteen hundred at launch. Three hundred forty at dock. Where did the other thousand go?"

"Nobody knows." Sefa opened her eyes. "That's the part Halloran couldn't crack either. The ship left Titan heavy and arrived here light, and the gap is a thousand people with no graves and no names and no paperwork at all. The forty-one we could almost explain—badly, monstrously, but there are signatures, there are containers, there is a *trail*. The thousand before them are just—" she made a gesture, a small terrible opening of her hands, "—gone. Erased so cleanly it had to be done before the ship ever reached us."

The work light buzzed. Somewhere below them, on a deck that was supposed to be sealed, a pump cycled on that had no business cycling, and both of them heard it and both of them pretended they hadn't.

"His notes say the evidence is in the hull," Bram said. "He wrote that the hull remembers. Bay Nine—"

"Is not where you should look." Sefa stood, and now her voice had changed, dropped into something older than fear—something like the voice she used when a pressure bulkhead was about to fail and everyone needed to move *now*. "Halloran looked in the bay and it got him killed. They sealed it after, scrubbed it, but the bay was always the decoy. You want to know where the Perihelion really is?" She looked at the clone wearing her dead friend's face, and made the decision she'd been not-making for eleven years.

"Follow me. And walk like you're checking coolant lines."

She killed the work light. In the sudden dark, the station breathed around them—failing, scheduled to fail, euthanized by a company that had decided a decade of murder was cheaper than a decade of memory—and Sefa Okonkwo led the dead man's ghost down into the bones of Meridian, toward the sealed decks where the hull kept what it remembered.

She just didn't yet know that someone was watching them on the security feeds, and had already begun composing the message that would end up on Adrien Voss's desk before the shift ended.
