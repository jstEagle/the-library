## 7. What the Hull Remembers

*Sefa*

The lift to the lower decks hadn't moved in nine years. Sefa knew because she was the one who'd locked it out—her own signature still ghosted on the maintenance tag, faded to a bruise-colored smudge under the plastic. She stood before it now with a pry bar and a flashlight and a decision she couldn't take back.

"You're sure about this," Bram said beside her. Not a question. A dare, maybe. Or a prayer.

"No," Sefa said honestly. "But I've been sure about nothing else for eleven years, so."

The lockout panel came apart easier than it should have. She fed power around the dead interlock, and the lift shuddered awake like an old animal remembering its legs. The cage descended past decks she knew by heart—the hydroponics ring, the water reclamation spine—and then past decks she didn't, decks that weren't on any schematic she'd ever been given. The readout kept counting. Forty. Forty-one. Forty-two.

"Meridian doesn't go down this far," Bram said.

"Meridian goes down exactly this far," Sefa said. "We just were never told."

The doors opened onto cold. Real cold, not the managed chill of a failing station but the deep, patient cold of a place that had been switched off and left alone. Their breath bloomed white under the flashlight beams. Beyond the lift lobby, a corridor stretched into darkness, and along its walls ran a stripe of paint she recognized from cargo manifests she'd spent three nights cross-referencing: corporate gray, the exact shade used for Perihelion Company freight livery.

Bram's light found a stencil on the bulkhead, half flaked away.

**PERIHELION — DOCK 9 — RESTRICTED.**

Sefa's mouth went dry. "It never left," she said. "It's been here the whole time. Under us. We've been living on top of a graveyard and paying rent."

* * *

*Bram*

The original Bram Halloran had left a map. It wasn't a map, exactly—it was a maintenance log with certain numbers circled, a route through the ship's guts written in the margins of a work order dated three weeks before his death. Following it felt like walking through someone else's dream. Turn left at the galley bulkhead. Count seven hatches. The one that sticks, lift with your shoulder—he'd known which hatch stuck, because he'd been here. He'd stood where Bram stood now, breathing the same dead air, and he'd written instructions for a person who didn't exist yet.

*You'll be reading this after me,* the margin note said, in handwriting Bram now knew better than their own. *I'm sorry about that.*

The seventh hatch stuck. Bram lifted with their shoulder, and it gave with a groan of corroded seals, and the smell hit them—not decay, exactly; the cold had preserved everything into something worse than decay, something permanent—and beyond the hatch lay the Perihelion's passenger deck.

It looked like a ship mid-voyage. That was the first horror. Blankets folded on bunks. A child's toy—a woven thing, a doll with button eyes—resting on a pillow. Personal effects stowed in overhead nets as if the owners had only stepped out and would return any moment to finish the crossing. Eleven years of dust lay over all of it like gray snow, and beneath the dust, everything waited.

"They were told to abandon ship," Sefa said quietly behind him. Her voice had gone flat, careful, the voice of someone holding a door shut inside herself. "Emergency protocol. You leave your kit. You leave everything. You'll come back for it." She swept her light across the bunks. "Nobody came back."

"How do you know they were told?"

"Because I wrote the announcement." Her light didn't waver, but her hand did, just slightly, just enough. "Eleven years ago. I recorded the evacuation notice for Dock 9. I thought—I was told—it was a drill. A certification exercise. I sat in a booth and read a script about remaining calm and proceeding to muster stations, and I billed four hours' overtime, and forty-one people—" She stopped. Started again. "Forty-one people heard my voice telling them everything was fine."

Bram said nothing. There was nothing to say that wouldn't be a lie.

They moved deeper. The scorch patterns began at the third compartment—fanned shapes across the deck plating, radiating outward from the bulkhead seams, the unmistakable bloom of a directed incendiary charge detonated inside a sealed space. Not a fire. Fires spread chaotically; these burns were geometric, deliberate, laid out like the petals of terrible flowers. Someone had sealed each compartment and burned it from within, or vented the air and let the cold do the rest, and either way the company had walked through afterward with clipboards and called it *cargo loss*.

"Cargo loss," Bram repeated aloud, because saying it made it more impossible, not less.

"Incinerator residue, logged as agricultural waste," Sefa said. "I pulled the disposal records last night. Fourteen tons. Over six days." She crouched at the edge of a scorch fan and touched nothing. "You don't burn fourteen tons of agricultural waste in passenger compartments."

Ahead, a hatch stood sealed with a different kind of lock—corporate, magnetic, the red tab of a legal hold still clinging to its frame like a shed skin. The original's notes circled this hatch twice. Beside the second circle, three words: *THEY KEPT THEM.*

Bram pressed their palm against the metal. Cold enough to burn.

"Sefa," they said. "I need you to open this one."

* * *

*Sefa*

She could have refused. That was the thing she would think about later, in the hearings, in the months after—she could have turned around, climbed back up into the warm dying station, filed her report, taken whatever settlement they offered the way everyone always took the settlement. The lock was corporate. Breaking it was a felony under maritime law, probably several felonies, and Adrien Voss already knew her name.

She keyed the manual override anyway. Some habits outlive fear. You spend thirty years keeping ships alive and you learn that a sealed compartment is a promise somebody made to somebody else, and you open it.

The compartment beyond was small. A muster room, originally—benches along the walls, a placard for muster station C. And on the benches, arranged with a care that broke her heart precisely because it was bureaucratic rather than cruel, lay the remains.

Not scattered. Not dumped. *Processed.* Each set of remains in a recovery shroud, tagged, numbered, stacked in rows of five with the neatness of inventory. Tags dangled from each shroud, and even from the doorway Sefa could read the format: lot number, date, disposition code. **DISPOSITION: CARGO LOSS. NO FURTHER ACTION REQUIRED.**

Forty-one tags. She counted without wanting to. Her eyes did it automatically, the way they counted rivets, the way they counted colonists on the shuttle manifests lately, the way they'd started counting everything since the recyclers began failing on schedule.

Forty-one people. Someone had braided the hair of the one in row two—she could see the pattern through the shroud's worn place, a child's braid, three strands. Someone had put a wedding band in the pocket of the one in row four; the metal had printed its shape through the fabric. These weren't cargo. These were mothers, sons, someone's bad-tempered uncle, someone's first love. Every one of them had a name before they had a lot number, and the names were in Bram's hands right now, in a dead man's notes, crossed out in red by someone who had wanted them erased.

"We found them," Bram said. Their voice sounded strange—young, stunned, though they wore a dead man's memories and a dead man's face. "He found them. He stood right here."

Sefa looked at the floor and saw what Bram saw: a faint rectangle in the dust where a case had rested until very recently. Three weeks ago. The original Bram had taken the physical evidence—the logs, the recordings, whatever the case held—and hidden it somewhere only his clone would think to look. Inside his own genetic line, the archive vault note had said. Whatever that meant.

"He knew he'd die," Sefa said. "He found all this, and he knew they'd kill him for it, and instead of running he went upstairs and let himself be murdered so the evidence would have a courier." She laughed, and it came out wrong, wet and sharp. "And they cloned him anyway. They were so arrogant they couldn't even kill him properly. They grew you, and here you are, standing in his footprints."

"Here I am," Bram said.

Sefa unclipped her tool harness—the good one, the one she'd carried through every shift of her working life—and began photographing everything. Deck plates. Scorch fans. Tags. The braid. The ring's imprint. Her hands were steady now; the shaking had passed through her and left something harder on the other side. "Get the manifest pages," she said. "Every name. We match the tags to the names tonight. Forty-one people get their names back before Voss can scrub another single record."

They worked in silence for twenty minutes, two thieves in a tomb, stealing back the dead.

Then Bram's comm unit chirped—the emergency band, the one that only carried station-critical traffic—and a voice Sefa recognized with a lurch of cold dread read out a station-wide order:

*"Attention Meridian personnel. By authority of Perihelion Company Asset Recovery, lower decks are now designated active demolition zone. Seismic charges arm in ninety minutes. All personnel evacuate to upper levels immediately. This is not a drill."*

Ninety minutes. Voss wasn't waiting for the schedule anymore. He'd learned what they'd learned—or suspected it—and he was going to bury the ship, the evidence, and anyone standing inside it.

Sefa looked at Bram across the muster room, over forty-one shrouded strangers, and saw her own resolve reflected back at her.

"Ninety minutes," she said. "So we'd better decide fast: do we run—or do we make sure the whole system hears what happened down here?"

Bram was already moving toward the door.
