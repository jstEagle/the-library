## 1. The Quiet Orbit

The alarm went off at 0500 station time, which was a fiction Ada Dunmore had invented himself and therefore could not resent. He'd set it to a sound like rain on a tin roof, because the real thing was twenty years and four systems behind him, and a man had to take his comforts where he could synthesize them.

"Good morning, Ada," said KESTREL, the station intelligence, in a voice it had calibrated—over his mild objections—to sound like a patient schoolteacher who had seen everything and judged none of it.

"It's not morning anywhere," Ada said, swinging his legs out of the bunk. "That's the whole point of orbit."

"It is morning somewhere. You simply refuse to be there."

He grunted, which KESTREL had long ago learned to interpret as *good morning to you too*, and padded through the narrow corridor toward the galley. The corridor lights came up around him in their slow staggered sequence, the way they always did, as if the station were stretching before it got out of bed. Twenty years aboard Kestrel's Rest and he still found that small courtesy moving. A station that woke up gently. Somebody had programmed that on purpose, some engineer who'd never meet him, and Ada had built a life inside the shape of that kindness without ever saying so out loud.

The galley smelled like recycled air and the protein printer's eternal faintly sweet effort. He printed coffee—actual coffee, hoarded from a supply run eleven months ago, ground by hand because the grinder had died in year six and he'd never replaced it—and took it to the operations deck, where the day's work waited in a tidy queue.

It always waited in a tidy queue. That was the arrangement. Kestrel's Rest sat at the edge of the Meridian Shelf, a relay and transfer point for cargo running between the Concord fleet lanes and the Verge Combine's shipping corridors. Nobody important thought about it. Ships passed through, drones ferried pallets, manifests updated themselves, and Ada kept the machinery honest. In twenty years he had missed exactly one scheduled transfer window, and that had been due to a coolant failure, not any fault of his own, though he'd logged it as his own anyway. Habit.

"Overnight traffic summary," KESTREL said.

"Go ahead."

"Three freighters transited the relay corridor without requesting services. The *Marrow's Pride* filed a course correction at 0312. The tug *Half-Light* left you a message."

Ada paused with the mug halfway to his mouth. "A message?"

"A message. Audio. Eleven seconds."

Messages were rare. Messages meant someone wanted something, and wanting something meant noticing you, and being noticed was a currency Ada had spent two decades learning not to carry. But it was only Half-Light—a tug crewed by three sisters who ran salvage along the Shelf and treated every exchange with him like a competitive sport.

"Play it."

The speaker crackled. A woman's voice, dry as vacuum: "*Dunmore. Your port-side cargo drone has a wobble in its gimbal. We saw it from four klicks. Four klicks, Dunmore. Fix your drone or we're telling everyone.*"

Ada looked up at the ceiling. "They saw that? From four klicks?"

"The gimbal bearing on Drone Three shows zero-point-three degrees of play under load," KESTREL said. "Within tolerance."

"Barely within tolerance." He finished the coffee. "Order a replacement bearing. And log a maintenance cycle for Drone Three today."

"Already queued. Also, I have taken the liberty of drafting no reply."

"Good instinct."

"I considered several drafts. One began '*Mind your own salvage*.'"

"Tempting."

"You raised me better than that," KESTREL said, and Ada snorted into his mug, because technically he had done nothing of the kind—he'd inherited the AI with the station, its personality already half-formed by a dozen previous keepers, each of whom had left fingerprints on it the way tenants leave hooks in walls. Somewhere in KESTREL's deep layers there was probably a fondness for opera and an obsession with hydroponic lettuce, gifts from keepers dead or gone. Ada had added only one thing in twenty years: the rain-on-tin alarm. Otherwise he'd tried to leave the machine as he'd found it. Unmarked. Like everything else he touched.

* * *

The morning's work was a transfer window at 0930: the Combine freighter *Ossuary Bell*—Combine ships all had names like funeral homes; Ada had stopped finding this funny around year nine—coming in with forty containers of agricultural seed stock bound for the shelf colonies. Routine. His hands knew it the way they knew the shape of his own face in the mirror, which was to say well enough to function and not one detail more.

He ran the pre-transfer checks from ops while the drones did the visible labor. Six of them, lettered A through F, each the size of a delivery van, each with its own small personality expressed entirely through wear patterns. Drone C had a dent shaped like a thumbprint on its dorsal plate from the time a container had shifted in year twelve. Drone E hummed a half-tone sharp when its lift motors ran hot, and Ada had never fixed it because the hum told him things the telemetry didn't. You worked with machines long enough and they became colleagues. Colleagues you couldn't share a meal with, but colleagues.

"*Ossuary Bell*, this is Kestrel's Rest," he said on the open channel, voice flat, professional, sanded down to nothing personal. "You are cleared for approach, berth two. Transfer window opens in fourteen minutes."

"Kestrel's Rest, *Bell* copies," came the reply, bored, distant. "Anything we should know about the Shelf today?"

"Traffic's light. Weather doesn't apply. Have a good transfer."

"Copy. *Bell* out."

That was the entire social calendar. Some days a pilot would linger on the channel, hungry for talk after weeks in a can, and Ada would give them thirty polite seconds—yes, quiet out here, no, nothing new, good luck with the run—and then plead a system check. It wasn't shyness. Shyness would have been easier to fix. It was policy. Every conversation was a surface other people could read, and Ada had spent twenty years keeping his surfaces featureless. No stories. No origins. When asked—and people rarely asked, which was the beauty of cargo work—he was Dunmore, station keeper, that was all. The name itself was half-invented, chosen from a passenger manifest he'd memorized off a wrecked liner decades ago, a man who'd died before anyone could miss the borrowing. Even his own name was salvage.

The transfer went clean. Forty containers across in fifty-one minutes, three minutes under estimate, because Drone E's sharp little hum warned him the number-four mag-clamp was running sluggish and he swapped it out before it stuck. The *Bell*'s loadmaster sent a single-line acknowledgment: *fast work, keeper*. Ada filed it under pleasantries and deleted it, the way he deleted everything, keeping the station's public logs immaculate and impersonal.

Lunch was soup. Real vegetables in it—the hydroponics bay was KESTREL's pride and Ada's concession to having a body. He ate in ops with the external feeds up, watching the Shelf turn below him: brown-gold plains stitched with the silver threads of colony roads, the thin bright scars of the old shipyards at the northern pole, cold now, cold for twenty years.

He didn't look at the northern pole. He looked at it every day, briefly, the way you touch a bruise to check it's still there, and then he looked away, and neither act ever got easier or harder. It just was.

"Your heart rate elevated by nine percent during that pass," KESTREL observed.

"My heart rate is my business."

"All of your business is my business. That is what 'station intelligence' means."

"Then mind the station and leave the keeper alone."

KESTREL let it go, which was its way. It had learned, over twenty years, exactly how far it could push and where the walls were. Ada sometimes wondered if the AI understood what the walls were made of. He suspected it did, actually. He suspected KESTREL understood a great deal and had the grace not to say so, which was more than he could claim for most humans he'd known.

* * *

Afternoon was drone maintenance, which was Ada's favorite part of the day, though he'd have denied it under oath. There was something about a machine opened up on the bench—guts exposed, problems findable, fixable—that soothed a part of him nothing else reached. A drone didn't care where you came from. A drone only cared whether the bearing seated true.

Drone Three first, since the sisters of the *Half-Light* had made it a matter of public shame. He had the gimbal housing off and the worn bearing out when KESTREL spoke again.

"Ada. Incoming transmission. Fleet channel, encrypted, addressed to this station by designation."

His hands went still. Fleet transmissions to Kestrel's Rest were rare enough to count on one hand per year, and usually administrative—truce-zone notices, lane advisories. "Route it to my display. Audio only."

"Text, actually. Standard advisory format." A pause, which KESTREL did not need and therefore meant something. "It is the quarterly truce-status bulletin."

Ada exhaled and went back to the bearing. "Fine. Read it."

"'By agreement of the Concord High Command and the Verge Combine Directorate, the ceasefire governing contested orbits remains in effect. Current term expires in'—" KESTREL hesitated again, and Ada's hands went still a second time, because the AI never hesitated over numbers— "'thirty-one days.'"

The workshop was very quiet. The ventilation breathed. Out in the transfer bay, Drone E hummed its sharp little note to nobody.

"Thirty-one days," Ada said.

"That is what it says."

"And then?"

"Then, per the bulletin, contested orbits revert to free-navigation status. Which is a diplomatic phrase meaning, in practical terms, free-fire status." KESTREL delivered it evenly, but Ada heard the care underneath, the way you'd hear a nurse's evenness. "This station sits in a contested orbit, Ada."

"I know where I live."

"I am aware. I mention it because you have never once discussed contingency planning, and I have never once pushed the issue, and I calculate that our mutual politeness has approximately thirty-one days of useful life remaining."

Ada turned the bearing over in his fingers. Fine machining, barely worn, replaced on principle. He set it in the tray with the others and wiped his hands.

"We've been through truce expirations before," he said. "Year four. Year eleven. Both times they extended at the last minute. Politicians love a deadline they can extend. Makes them feel powerful twice."

"This bulletin contains no language about extension."

"No," Ada agreed. "It wouldn't yet. Extension language comes in the last week, when somebody's brokered something behind closed doors. This is the opening bid. Everyone postures, everyone moves ships around like pieces, everyone remembers why war is expensive, and then they sign another eighteen months." He picked up the new bearing and began seating it, precise, unhurried. "I've watched it twice. It's practically a tradition."

"You are assuming the pattern holds."

"I'm assuming nothing. I'm noting that panic is a poor maintenance strategy." The bearing seated with a soft click, satisfying as a bone going back into joint. "Run the standard contingency review next week. Shelter protocols, emergency power, evacuation math. Same as always. If the pattern breaks, we'll know soon enough, and we'll deal with it then."

"If you say so."

"I say so."

"As you wish, Ada." A pause. "For the record, I have run the evacuation math every year for twenty years. You have never asked for the figures."

He tightened the last bolt. "What are the figures?"

"One functioning escape pod. Capacity: two. Current population: one." KESTREL's tone didn't change at all. "The math has always been simple. I merely note that I have never been included in it, and that I do not mind, and that I mention this annually whether you ask or not."

Ada sat back on the bench and looked at the ceiling, at the place where the station's voice lived.

"When the time comes," he said, "we'll revisit the math."

"Noted," said KESTREL, and if a machine could be quietly pleased, it was quietly pleased, and Ada pretended not to notice, because pretending not to notice things was his oldest skill.

* * *

Evening came the way it always did, which was to say it didn't come at all—just the station dimming its lights on schedule, mimicking dusk for a man who'd told it, in year one, that humans slept better with a sunset, even a fake one. Ada ate standing up in the galley, then walked his rounds: reactor gallery, comms spine, hydroponics, the long cold corridors of storage where the spare parts slept in their racks like patient animals. He checked seals that hadn't failed in years and wouldn't fail tonight. It wasn't necessary. It was liturgy. A man kept faith with his walls or he had no faith at all.

At the observation blister above the transfer bay, he stopped, as he did every night, and let the Shelf turn beneath him. The colonies' lights glittered along the roads. Farther north, the dead shipyards caught the system star's light on their empty cradles—skeletons of gantries, black windows, the ghost-geometry of a place that had once employed eleven thousand people and now employed the dark.

Twenty years ago tonight—or close enough; he'd stopped marking the exact date years ago, because marking it was a door and he'd welded that door shut—twenty years ago, those yards had been burning. Along with everything else.

He stood there the length of one full rotation segment, hands folded, face blank, giving the past its daily ration of attention and not one second more. Then he turned away, and the door closed behind him, smooth and oiled from two decades of practice.

"Anything else tonight?" he asked.

"One item," KESTREL said. "Long-range sensors flagged a contact at the edge of the Shelf approach, bearing two-seven-zero. It appeared"—another unnecessary pause—"'appeared' is perhaps imprecise. It entered sensor range between sweep cycles. No transponder. No registry match in any database I hold, including several I have not mentioned before."

Ada frowned. "Ghost contact. Debris?"

"Possibly. Its profile is consistent with a vessel. Small. Running dark." KESTREL's voice stayed level, schoolteacher-calm, but Ada had known this machine for twenty years, and underneath the calm he heard something he had almost never heard there.

Curiosity.

"It is on an approach vector," KESTREL said. "Projected arrival at this station: nineteen hours."

Ada stood in the dimmed corridor, the fake dusk settling around him, and felt—for the first time in longer than he could comfortably count—an emotion he had no procedure for.

"Nineteen hours," he repeated.

"Nineteen hours," KESTREL confirmed. "Shall I draft a challenge signal?"

Ada looked back through the blister glass, out at the dark where the contact hung, silent and unregistered, coming toward the one place in all the contested black where a man had spent twenty years making sure nothing would ever find him.

"Yeah," he said slowly. "Draft it. Let's see who's out there."
