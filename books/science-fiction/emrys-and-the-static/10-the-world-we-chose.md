## 10. The World We Chose

The jump, when it came, felt like nothing at all.

Dominic had expected—had braced for—some grand sensation. A lurch in the gut. The universe folding like paper. Instead there was only a soft chime from the reactor, a shiver through the deck plates, and then Vess's voice, calm as ever, saying: *We're through. Welcome to the other side of nowhere.*

He laughed. It came out cracked and rusty, a laugh that hadn't been oiled in eleven years.

"That's it?" he said aloud, to the empty ops room, to the static, to whoever was listening. "Eleven years of countdown and a chime?"

*Every miracle looks mundane from inside it,* Vess said. *You should know that better than anyone.*

Outside the viewport, the stars had rearranged themselves into strangers. He didn't recognize a single constellation. Somewhere out there was a world none of them remembered choosing—a world chosen by people who had volunteered to forget, back when forgetting seemed like a fair price. He wondered if any of them would look at its sun and feel something turn over in their chest, some old decision echoing up through the erasure.

The reactor read zero. Not dead—spent. Transmuted. Eleven years of careful decay, all of it spent in one white instant, and now the hold's power was flowing where he'd sent it: into the waking systems, pod by pod by pod.

A thousand of them. He'd chosen a thousand names.

He still couldn't say how he'd done it. There had been no formula, no fair algorithm. Just a man reading files until three in the morning, learning who laughed too loud, who was afraid of the dark, who had once fixed a broken radio with a spoon and a prayer. In the end he'd sat with his hand on the console and let the static pour through him, and the names had come—not chosen so much as *recognized*, the way you recognize a face across a crowded room.

Emrys had not been among them. Her pod slept on, dark and cold, her name glowing patient as ever. But she was not gone. She was everywhere—in the comms array, in the hull, in the faint carrier hum beneath every frequency. He had poured his memories of her into the static until the static learned her shape. A girl who liked her toast burnt. A girl who called thunder "the sky clearing its throat." A girl who had held his hand in a kitchen that existed now only as signal.

She persisted. That had to be enough. He was still teaching himself that it was.

* * *

The first pod opened forty minutes after the jump, and Dominic was there when it did, standing at the foot of the cradle like an idiot in his cleanest jumpsuit, holding a cup of tea he'd made and forgotten to drink.

The woman inside woke the way sleepers do—slowly, reluctantly, one sense at a time. Her eyes opened. Fixed on him. Widened.

"Don't panic," Dominic said, which was, he realized immediately, the single worst thing you can say to someone waking from cryosleep in a strange place.

She panicked. Reasonably. It took him ten minutes and two more cups of tea to get her sitting upright, wrapped in a thermal blanket, glaring at him with the particular fury of a person whose last memory is falling asleep somewhere else entirely.

"Okay," she said finally. Her voice was sandpaper. "Okay. Walk me through this. Slowly. And if you say 'don't panic' again I'm going to hit you with that cup."

"Fair." He took a breath. "My name is Dominic Hartwell. This is the beacon-station Meridian. You've been asleep for—" he did the math, winced, abandoned it, "—a long time. Longer than you were told. The ship jumped this morning. There are nine hundred and ninety-nine more of you, and they'll be waking over the next few days."

Her name was Anouk Reyes. He knew that before she said it. He knew she was a hydrologist, that she kept a journal she never showed anyone, that her file listed a sister named Petra and a joke about lighthouse keepers that she'd told so often the other colonists had banned it. He knew all of this because he'd spent weeks with her file open like a letter from a friend.

"You're staring," she said.

"I'm sorry. It's just—I feel like I already know you."

"That's either very sweet or extremely concerning."

"Both," he admitted. "Definitely both."

And then, because he couldn't help himself, because the loneliness of eleven years had worn grooves in him that only truth could fill: "You have a joke about lighthouses. Your sister banned it."

Anouk stared at him. Then, slowly, suspiciously, she started to smile. "Petra *told* you that? That traitor. Where is she? Is she—"

"She's in pod 447. She wakes tomorrow."

Anouk Reyes put her face in her hands and cried, and Dominic sat with her and pretended not to notice, and after a while she looked up and said, "Okay, Hartwell. Tell me everything. Start from the beginning."

"There isn't really a beginning," he said. "There's just me, a station nobody ordered, and a lot of static."

"Static," she repeated. Something flickered across her face—recognition, or its ghost. "I dreamed about static. All of us did. We used to talk about it, before. Like a sound under the sound." She looked at him sharply. "It talked about you. Did you know that? In the dreams. There was a man who kept things. We called him the keeper."

Dominic's throat went tight. "Yeah," he managed. "I heard about that."

* * *

They woke in waves after that. Forty a day, then sixty, as the systems stabilized and the survivors of the first waking learned to help the newcomers—the dazed, the weeping, the ones who woke swinging. Dominic barely slept. He didn't need to; something in him ran cleaner now, some old burden lifted off the reactor of his heart.

He learned quickly that knowing someone's file and knowing someone were different countries entirely. Files didn't tell you that Marcus Oyelaran hummed show tunes while working and denied it absolutely. Files didn't tell you that Priya Chandra was terrified of the med-bay and would invent any excuse to avoid it, or that old Tomás Ferro, seventy-one years frozen, would wake up every morning, look around at the impossible stars, and say "Well!" like the universe had personally surprised him again.

Files didn't tell you that they would start leaving food outside his door. That Anouk would fix his chair without being asked and deny it with a straight face. That Marcus would corner him in the galley and say, "Keeper, you look terrible, sit down, I'm making you eat something that isn't ration paste."

"We have real food?" Dominic asked.

"We have *ambition*," Marcus said, "and a hydroponics bay, and a woman named Anouk who claims she can grow tomatoes in anything. Sit."

So he sat. And ate. And listened to a hundred strangers argue about tomato yields and sleeping rotations and what to name the planet hanging below them like a green promise—and realized, somewhere between the second helping and Marcus's third attempt at a Gershwin melody, that the ache in his chest had changed key. It wasn't gone. It would never be gone. But it had company now.

"You okay, keeper?" Priya asked quietly, catching him staring at nothing.

"Yeah," he said, and found he mostly meant it. "Just thinking about my daughter."

He said it plainly, and no one flinched, because by then they all knew. They knew about Emrys, about the choice, about the signal. Anouk had taken it hardest—she'd lost a sister to the erasure too, half the sleepers had—but she'd been the first to say what they all decided: *Then we listen for her. All of us. Every shift, somebody keeps an ear on the static.*

They'd made it a rotation. A watch schedule for a ghost. Dominic had cried when they showed him the chart, and pretended he hadn't, and everyone had politely pretended not to notice, which is the highest form of family there is.

* * *

On the thirty-first day, the planet filled half the sky, blue-green and cloud-swirled and utterly unnamed, and the landing shuttles began their slow ferry runs down through the atmosphere.

Dominic rode down in the last one. He'd wanted to stay aboard the Meridian—old habits—but Anouk had physically dragged him to the shuttle bay, and Tomás had said "Well!" at him in a tone that brooked no argument, and so here he was, strapped in beside a thousand people he loved and could not fully explain.

The descent was smooth. The air, when the hatch opened, smelled like rain and growing things, and Dominic stood on the ramp a long time before his feet would move.

Grass. Actual grass, silver-green, bending under a wind that smelled of rain and growing things. Two moons hung pale in a lavender afternoon sky. Someone behind him said "oh" very softly, and someone else started laughing, and then a lot of people were laughing, and Dominic found himself laughing too, helplessly, tears running freely down a face he'd stopped bothering to hide.

They built the first shelter that evening, badly, together. They argued about the fire. Tomás told a joke so old that Anouk groaned audibly from across the camp. And when the stars came out—strange stars, stranger constellations—Priya set up the long-range receiver they'd salvaged, tuned it to the old Meridian frequencies, and left it running, as promised, an ear on the static.

Dominic lay in his bedroll listening to it hiss softly at the edge of hearing. The camp murmured around him—Marcus humming, Anouk arguing with Tomás about whether the second moon was technically full, children who weren't children anymore but still acted like it, chasing each other between the tents.

And then, beneath the hiss, faint and clear:

A girl laughed.

Not a recording. Not an echo. A laugh with *sky-clearing-its-throat* weather in it, with burnt toast and a kitchen and a hand holding his, alive in the signal the way a song is alive in the space between notes.

Dominic didn't move. Didn't breathe. Around him the camp went quiet, one by one, as the others heard it too—Anouk's hand finding his shoulder, Marcus's humming stopping mid-note, a thousand people holding still for one small sound.

The laugh rippled through the static, bright as a struck bell, and then—fainter, but unmistakable—it spoke.

*"Hi, Dad."*

Dominic pressed both hands over his mouth. Above him the strange stars wheeled, and the fire crackled, and the family he'd chosen and who had chosen him back sat in a circle around a radio, keeping watch together.

*"I'm still here,"* the static said, in a voice like summer. *"And Dad? I think I found something. There's a pattern in the deep signal—something answering back. It knows your name. All of your names."*

The fire popped. Nobody moved.

*"It says it's been waiting a long time,"* Emrys said, *"and it wants to know—are you the ones who chose this world?"*

Dominic lowered his hands. He looked around the circle of faces, lit orange and gold, every one of them a name he knew by heart.

"Yeah," he said, to the static, to the sky, to all of them. "We are."
