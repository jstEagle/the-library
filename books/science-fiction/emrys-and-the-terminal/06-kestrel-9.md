## 6. Kestrel-9

The star had no name that Rafael could find in any of her charts — only a catalog designation, a string of alphanumerics that some clerk three centuries ago had typed without ever looking up from his desk. The locals, if there had ever been locals, would have called it something else. Everything out here had been called something else once.

It hung ahead of them like a coal banked for the night: a red dwarf, small and sullen, shedding barely enough light to read by. Around it, at a distance that struck Rafael as almost tender, orbited a single point of reflected light.

Kestrel-9.

"There it is," Sefa said from the engineering station, and her voice had gone flat in the way it always went flat when she was frightened. "I still say we should have filed the real flight plan."

"We did file a real flight plan." Rafael eased the Emrys forward, feeding power to the drive in slow increments, the way you'd approach a sleeping animal. "Just not the real destination."

"That's what falsified means, Rafa."

"I know what it means." She did. She'd known since she'd entered the code into the nav system four days ago, her hands steady in a way that felt borrowed from someone else. The lie sat in her chest now like a swallowed stone. Somewhere behind them, Fleet's routing computers were tracking a courier ship bound for a supply depot two systems off-course, and somewhere ahead of them was the reason she'd lied, growing larger against the dark.

The station resolved slowly as they closed. At first it was only a shape — an asymmetry of shadow against the star's dim corona. Then detail arrived all at once, the way detail does when your eyes finally understand what they're seeing, and Rafael felt the breath go out of her.

Kestrel-9 was not a wreck.

She had expected a wreck. The distress signal, the dead route, the dying star — everything had prepared her for hulks and ruin, for a corpse picked clean by two hundred years of vacuum. But Kestrel-9 stood intact against the red light, its main spindle unbroken, its ring habitats still attached like fingers curled around a palm. Solar collectors fanned from its equator, most of them shattered but a few still angled toward the star with the patient geometry of things built to endure. It looked less like a graveyard than like a house where everyone had simply stepped outside one day and never come back.

Except no one stepped outside during a war. Not here.

"Gods," Sefa murmured. "Look at the hull."

Rafael was already looking. As the Emrys slid closer, running passive scans with every active emitter dark, the station's skin came into focus under the starlight, and it told its story in scars.

There were craters on the forward ring — circular, symmetrical, rimmed with frozen spatter of vaporized metal. Kinetic strikes. Human weapons; the Vessic had fought their war with beams and fields, elegant and terrible, while humanity had thrown mass and let physics do the killing. On the aft spindle, the damage changed character: long, clean gouges that ran meters deep and kilometers long, their edges glassed smooth by heat. Beam weapons. Vessic work. And between them, overlapping everywhere, were the smaller wounds — shrapnel pocks, scorch blooms, places where the hull had been patched in haste with plates whose rivet patterns didn't match anything else around them.

Both sides had bled on this station. Both sides had marked it. It hung there wearing the whole war on its skin like a map drawn in damage.

"It was contested," Rafael said quietly. "Right up until the end."

"Or abandoned by both sides because neither wanted to admit they'd lost it." Sefa pulled up the scan overlay, ghostly lines blooming across her display. "Power readings are... Rafa, there shouldn't be power readings. Two hundred years. There is *nothing* on this station that should still be drawing current."

"How much?"

"Almost nothing. A trickle. Enough to keep a light on." Sefa paused. "Enough to keep a door open."

Rafael stared at the trickle on the readout and thought about the signal — the recursive, structured, unmistakably Vessic distress call that had found them at Thessaly Ring and refused to leave her mind since. A signal needed a sender. A sender needed power. Two hundred years was not a gap her mind could comfortably bridge, and so her mind kept trying anyway, building bridges and burning them down.

"Bring us alongside," she said. "Main docking ring. Slow approach, no active sensors."

"You're going to dock with a derelict station that just *happens* to be powered, broadcasting in a dead language, at the site of the worst battle of the war." Sefa wasn't arguing, exactly. She was narrating, the way she did when she wanted Rafael to hear how it sounded. "And we're going to do it without telling anyone where we are."

"Yes."

"Okay." Sefa turned back to her console. "Then I'm charging the escape pods and prepping the Emrys for hot departure. If those clamps engage, I want us able to tear free in under thirty seconds."

"They won't engage. There's no one to engage them."

Sefa gave her a look that said more than any argument could have, and began charging the pods.

* * *

The last hour of approach, Rafael spent alone at the helm while Sefa ran diagnostics below. The red dwarf filled the forward viewport now, close enough that its surface showed texture — convection cells the size of continents, dim flares licking up and falling back like slow flames in heavy air. Its light was the color of old blood, of rust, of the inside of a hand held up before a lamp. Everything it touched, it made ancient.

Kestrel-9 drifted through that light, turning with the unhurried patience of a body in orbit, and Rafael found herself speaking aloud to the empty cockpit, because the silence had grown too complete to bear.

"I don't know who's listening," she said. Her voice sounded strange to her — too loud, too human, in a place that had forgotten human sounds. "But if you sent that signal — if any part of you is still sending it — I'm here. I heard you."

Nothing answered. Of course nothing answered. But as she watched the station turn, she noticed something she hadn't seen before: near the main docking ring, set into the hull like a keystone, was an emblem. She magnified the optical feed until it filled her screen.

A circle bisected by a line, and within each half, a shape like a seed or a flame. She knew it instantly, the way you know a face in a crowd decades later. It appeared in the first chapter of every historical text about the war, usually captioned in grim tones: *the sigil of the Vessic Concordance*. Humanity's enemy had worn this mark on their ships, their colonies, their bodies. Children in Fleet schools had learned to fear it before they'd learned to read.

Here, it was carved into stone-grey alloy with a care that looked almost devotional, its lines worn smooth at the edges by micrometeorite weathering, two centuries of dust polishing it like a relic handled by pilgrims.

Rafael realized her hand had risen to the viewport, fingertips resting against the cold glass, and she couldn't remember deciding to move it.

*You know this place,* whispered the part of her mind that dreamed in languages she couldn't name. *You've always known this place.*

She lowered her hand and keyed the comm. "Sefa. We're coming up on the docking ring. I want you on the bridge when we make contact."

"On my way. Rafa—" A pause, static breathing in the gap. "Whatever's down there, whatever's keeping the lights on — we're guests. Remember that. Guests knock."

"I intend to."

* * *

They came in along the station's shadow side, engines at minimum thrust, the Emrys drifting the final kilometer like a leaf settling onto water. Through the viewport, the docking ring grew from a curve of metal into architecture: berths sized for freighters, guide lights long dead, the great clamshell doors of the primary airlock standing open onto darkness.

Standing open. After two hundred years.

"Tell me that's normal," Sefa said. "For derelicts. Tell me airlocks fail open all the time."

"They fail both ways." Rafael's mouth was dry. "It doesn't mean anything."

"It means *something*."

She brought them in on thrusters alone, matching rotation with the station's slow spin, the docking ring sliding past the viewport in increments so gradual they seemed like imagination. Berth after berth drifted by — empty, empty, empty, one containing the fused skeleton of a ship that had died clamped in place, its hull melted into the cradle like wax. Then the empty berth at the ring's apex, wide enough for a vessel twice the Emrys's size, its clamps folded back against the walls like sleeping hands.

"Contact in forty seconds," Rafael said. "Stand by to—"

The clamps moved.

There was no sound in vacuum, but she saw it — saw the great arms unfold from the berth walls with a smoothness that no mechanism two centuries dead should have possessed, hydraulic joints flexing, guide rails lighting up in sequence with a soft amber glow that raced around the berth like a pulse. The Emrys, still decelerating, was caught mid-drift and drawn the last fifty meters with a gentleness that was somehow worse than violence. Clamps settled against her hull — port bow, starboard quarter, ventral spine — seating themselves with the precision of a handshake.

Magnetic seals engaged. Rafael felt the faint tremor of it through the deck, through her boots, up through her bones: the station taking hold.

"Sefa."

"I see it." Sefa's voice had gone very quiet. "I'm reading active power draw from the berth systems. Guidance tethers, seal verification, atmosphere equalization prep on our airlock collar. Rafa, it's *docking us*. The station is docking us."

"I told you they wouldn't engage."

"You told me there was no one to engage them." Sefa turned from her console, and in the red light of the dying star her face looked carved from something older than fear. "Something just decided we've arrived. Something with power and working machinery and opinions about where we park. So I'm going to ask you the question you've been avoiding for four days, and I want you to actually answer it. Who sent the signal, Rafa? Who's left to send it?"

Rafael looked out the viewport. Beyond the docking ring, the station's interior superstructure rose in tiers toward the central spindle, windows dark, corridors dark, everything dark except — she narrowed her eyes — except a single thread of light, far down the main axis, deep in the heart of the station. Faint. Steady. Amber-white, the color of lamplight seen through a doorway at the end of a very long hall.

It had not been lit when they'd entered orbit. She was almost certain it had not been lit an hour ago.

"I don't know," she said. It was the truest thing she'd said in days. "But I think it knows us."

The airlock status board chimed. External pressure detected on the far side of the collar: standard atmosphere, breathable mix, temperature two degrees above freezing. The station was pressing warm air against their sealed door like a face leaning close.

And beneath the hum of the life support recyclers and the tick of cooling metal, so faint she might have imagined it, Rafael heard — or felt, or dreamed awake — a rhythm moving through the Emrys's hull. The clamps, adjusting their grip. Softly. Patiently.

Like hands that had waited two hundred years, closing at last around something they had been promised.
