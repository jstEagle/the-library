## 14. Fleet Hails the Emrys

The hail came on the ninth day, in the middle of a translation session, and Rafael would remember forever that it interrupted the Vessic word for *morning* — *selu* — a word Ilyen had just explained carried within it the idea of a door opening, so that every dawn was also a departure, and every departure, if you were generous with the grammar, a kind of dawn.

She had the word in her mouth. She could feel its shape, the soft closure at the back, the way Ilyen had pursed his lips to demonstrate — an old man's mouth making the shape of a young language — when the Emrys's comm array chimed the three-note priority tone that Rafael had not heard since her cadet days, and that no courier ship on a dead trade route had any business hearing.

*Fleet priority. Fleet priority. Acknowledge.*

The word *selu* died in her throat. For a moment she sat very still in the translation alcove, her hand hovering over the slate where Ilyen's annotations glowed in their patient, doubled script — Vessic on the left, her own halting Standard creeping across the right — and she listened to her own heartbeat, which had gone strange and slow, like a clock winding down.

Across the compartment, Sefa looked up from the coolant recycler she'd had open for two days, her wrench frozen mid-turn, her dark eyes finding Rafael's with an expression that needed no language at all.

*They know,* that expression said.

*They know,* Rafael's answered back.

* * *

She made herself take the call from the cockpit, alone, because that was where a captain took a hail from Fleet Command, and because some stubborn, buried part of her still believed in the choreography of rank — that if she sat in the right chair, wore the right face, the machinery of her old life might still recognize her as one of its own.

The screen resolved into the blue-grey crest of the Third Courier Command, and beneath it, into a face she had last seen eleven years and four thousand light-years ago.

"Hello, Rafael."

Captain Naomi Adeyemi had not aged the way people aged in the fleet holoportraits, all bronze and immobility. She had aged the way weather ages stone: subtly, in the deepening of lines around the mouth, in a certain settling of the shoulders, as though she had spent the decade learning to carry something heavier than rank. Her hair was cropped shorter now, iron at the temples. Her eyes — brown, quick, once the eyes that had told a frightened nineteen-year-old cadet that she had a gift and that gifts were obligations — were carefully, professionally neutral.

"Captain Adeyemi." Rafael's voice came out steady. She was almost proud of it. "This is a surprise."

"Is it?" Adeyemi tilted her head, and for a half-second the mask slipped and Rafael saw something underneath it — not anger, not even suspicion. Something more tired. More sad. "Courier vessel *Emrys*, registry 4471-K. Your filed flight plan has you running salvage survey on the Thessaly Loop, a fourteen-day circuit. Your transponder, however, places you nine days out of Thessaly Ring on a heading that terminates—" Adeyemi paused, and the pause was surgical, "—well. Let's call it a heading of interest."

Rafael kept her hands flat on the console where the camera could see them. Steady hands. She'd learned that from Adeyemi too, in a briefing room on Ceres Station, twenty years ago: *the body confesses before the mouth does, Rafael. Train the body to lie first.*

"There was a salvage lead," Rafael said. "Unregistered hulk on the old Meridian charts. I deviated to investigate. I was going to amend the flight plan at the next relay."

"The next relay is Kestrel Relay, which has been dark for sixty years." Adeyemi's tone didn't change, but the temperature of the room did. "You're not running salvage, Rafael. You're burning hard for the old front line. And you filed a false plan to do it, which is not the behavior of a woman chasing scrap metal."

The silence stretched. In the cockpit's dimness Rafael could see her own reflection ghosted over Adeyemi's face — a pale, sleep-starved woman with Vessic annotations still visible on the slate beside her, because she'd forgotten to clear it, because *selu* was still lodged in her throat like a stone.

She reached over, deliberately, and turned the slate face-down.

Adeyemi watched her do it. Said nothing.

"I don't know what you've heard, Captain," Rafael began.

"I've heard," Adeyemi said, "that a distress signal was intercepted at Thessaly Ring nine days ago. Structured. Recursive. I've heard that the courier who received it did not report it through channels, as required by standing order 114, but instead falsified her flight plan and went dark. I've heard that the signal's origin has been tentatively triangulated." Another pause. This one longer. "And I have heard, Rafael, that the signal was in Vessic."

The word landed in the cockpit like a dropped tool. Rafael felt it roll against her boot.

"Vessic is extinct," she said. "That's in the first-year curriculum. You taught the module yourself."

"I did." Adeyemi leaned forward, and the camera caught the fine grain of her skin, the exhaustion the uniform couldn't quite press flat. "I taught you that module. I remember how you sat in the third row and didn't take notes, because you'd already read the whole corpus by then, and I remember thinking: this one hears it. This one hears the language underneath the language." Her voice softened by a degree, which was somehow worse than if it had hardened. "I've thought about that a lot over the years. Where you'd end up. What you'd be for."

*What you'd be for.* Not *what you'd become*. Rafael's spine went cold, one vertebra at a time, and she thought of chapter six of her childhood — no, not a chapter, there were no chapters, there was only the training annex on Ceres and the recordings played at low volume while she slept, and the instructors who said *don't strain, just listen, let it settle* — and she thought, for the first time with full clarity, *they were planting something in me, and I grew, and I never once asked in what soil.*

"Captain," she said carefully. "Why is Fleet Command personally hailing a courier ship over a flight plan deviation?"

"Because I asked to be the one to make this call." Adeyemi sat back. Behind her, out of focus, Rafael could see the grey bulk of a command deck, and moving figures, and the cold geometry of a fleet that had never once, in Rafael's lifetime, been at peace or at war — only at readiness. "Here's what happens now, and I'm going to say it once, and then I'm going to offer you a door, and I need you to understand that the door is real, Rafael, whatever else you believe about me. Patrol vessel *Vigilant* is forty hours behind me on this channel. Her captain has orders to intercept the *Emrys*, escort her to Meridian Station, and place her crew in temporary custody pending review. That is the order. That is the machine, and the machine does not care that I'm the one talking to you."

"And the door?"

"The door is this." Adeyemi's eyes held hers through eleven years and four thousand light-years. "You turn the *Emrys* around. You resume your filed flight plan. When *Vigilant* intercepts you — and she will, in forty hours, wherever you are — you are simply a courier who got lost, who panicked, who made an error of navigation and an error of judgment and is deeply, boringly sorry. I will accept that report. I will bury it. You'll lose your priority routing for a year, maybe your bonus rating. And in exchange, whatever you've found out there—" her voice did not waver, but it thinned, stretched over something, "—whatever you think you've found, you bring it to me. Not to the arrays. Not to the relays. Not to any channel but mine. Do you understand me, Rafael? Come home quietly, and I will make sure you're the one who decides what happens next. Keep running, and you become a security incident, and security incidents get processed by people who have never once listened to a language in their lives."

Rafael looked at her for a long moment. At the face that had been the closest thing to a parent the fleet ever gave her. At the mouth that had said *you have a gift* and was now saying *give it to me.*

"You already know what I found," Rafael said quietly. "Don't you. That's why you're here and not *Vigilant*'s captain. You asked for this call because you already know what's at Kestrel-9."

Something moved behind Adeyemi's eyes — a flinch, quickly governed, but Rafael had spent her life reading the micro-expressions of people speaking languages they didn't know they were speaking, and she saw it, she catalogued it, she filed it in the growing archive of her own betrayal.

"I know what was at Kestrel-9," Adeyemi said, "thirty years ago. And I know what Fleet doctrine says about it, which is that it does not exist, and that anyone who claims otherwise is either mistaken or a threat to the armistice, and that the armistice has held for two hundred years because people like me have kept certain doors closed." She exhaled slowly. "I was there at the beginning of my career, Rafael. I helped close one of those doors myself. It's why I'm talking to you instead of letting *Vigilant* do it. Because I remember that you were a person before you were a courier, and I would like, very much, for you to remain one."

"By coming home quietly."

"By coming home *alive*." For the first time, Adeyemi's voice cracked its containment — just a hairline fracture, just enough to let something human through. "Rafael. Listen to me. Whatever you've found, whoever is out there — if you broadcast it, if you try to take it to the arrays, it will not free anyone. It will burn. Both fleets will move to contain it, and containment, in our grammar, means bodies. I have seen the war games. I've sat in the rooms where they run them. Two hundred years of cold war is held together by a story, and if you pull the story out, the whole structure comes down on the heads of everyone standing under it. Millions. Maybe billions. Is that what your language teaches you? Is that what the dead would want?"

Rafael thought of Ilyen, two hundred years old and held together by failing machines and stubborn grammar, dictating annotations in a voice like dry leaves. She thought of *selu* — morning, door, departure. She thought: *you've already decided what the dead would want, and you never asked them, because asking them would have required learning to listen.*

"Captain," she said. "Thank you for the warning. I'll consider it."

Adeyemi closed her eyes briefly, the way a person does when a door they've been holding open swings shut against their hand. When she opened them, the captain was back, and the mentor was a memory beneath her, like a language beneath a language.

"Forty hours," she said. "The *Emrys* will be intercepted wherever she is. I'd think about what you want her to be carrying when that happens." A pause. Then, softer, almost against regulation: "You were the best student I ever had, Rafael Reyes. Don't make me be right about you in the wrong way."

The screen went to the fleet crest, then to black.

* * *

Sefa was waiting outside the cockpit, arms crossed, wrench still in hand, and Rafael realized with a distant, clinical surprise that she must have heard every word — the cockpit door was thin, and Sefa had never in nine years pretended not to listen when listening mattered.

"Forty hours," Sefa said.

"Forty hours."

"Patrol vessel. Custody. Review." Sefa turned the wrench over in her hands, not looking at it. "And Adeyemi. Your Adeyemi. Offering to bury it if you hand it over."

"Yes."

Sefa nodded slowly. Then she looked up, and Rafael saw that her engineer — her stubborn, protocol-quoting, *report-it-to-Fleet-Command* engineer — had arrived somewhere new while Rafael had been talking, and had arrived there alone.

"Then we've got forty hours to figure out what we do when they board," Sefa said. "Because I've been thinking, Rafa. About what's in that archive. About what they'll do to Ilyen if they take him as an 'asset.'" Her jaw tightened around the word. "And I've decided I didn't fix your coolant recycler for two days just so Fleet could come take the whole station apart."

Rafael stared at her. "Three days ago you wanted to report the signal."

"Three days ago I didn't know what the signal *said*." Sefa pushed past her into the cockpit, already pulling up the *Emrys*'s systems schematic, already moving, because Sefa Okonkwo grieved and feared and changed her mind with her hands. "Forty hours. Talk to Ilyen. Find out if there's anything in that archive about what happens when a language is about to be taken away from the world again. Because I have a feeling—" she tapped the schematic, right over the comm array, "—that the answer is we don't let them."

Rafael stood in the corridor a moment longer, looking at the black screen where her mentor's face had been.

Then she turned and went to translate the word for *morning* — *selu*, door, departure — and to tell an old man that the world he'd waited two hundred years to reach was already on its way, and that it was bringing chains.
