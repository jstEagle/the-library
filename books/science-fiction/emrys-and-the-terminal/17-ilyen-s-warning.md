## 17. Ilyen's Warning

The hydroponics bay smelled of wet stone and old green things, and to Ilyen it smelled like the last morning of his life before the first morning of the next one — the way the seed-vaults on Vess had smelled in the hour before the evacuation sirens, when the gardeners still believed the war was a rumor told by frightened people with bad information.

He had learned, over two hundred years, to distrust mornings that smelled like hope.

He sat among the failing vines with his hands open on his knees, palms up, the posture his mother had called *receiving*. The machines around him hummed their thin, dutiful hum, filtering air he no longer deserved and circulating water through roots that had gone strange and pale in the low light. His body was a ledger of debts now: the respirator's rhythm, the nutrient drip's slow arithmetic, the coolant loop that kept his blood from cooking in its own fever. Every hour he lived cost the reactor something. He had done the calculation a hundred times. He did not say it aloud. The girl would only argue, and she argued the way water argued with stone — patiently, endlessly, wearing grooves into him until he loved the shape she left behind.

Rafael Reyes came down the ladder at the sixth bell, station-time, her boots ringing on the rungs in a rhythm he had come to know as well as his own heartbeat. She carried two cups of the terrible tea Sefa brewed from synthesized leaves, and she handed him one without asking, which was correct. In Vessic custom you did not ask an elder if he wanted warmth. You simply gave it.

"You're smiling," she said in accented but fluent Vessic. "That's either good news or you've finally lost your mind."

"Both," he said. "Sit. There is something I must teach you today, and it is not grammar."

* * *

He watched her settle cross-legged on the deck plating across from him, the cup cradled in both hands, and he felt the old archivist's ache rise in him — the ache of a man holding a library that is also a bomb. For thirty years of his long captivity he had thought of the archive as a sanctuary. It had taken him most of another century to understand what it actually was: a message, and every message is a weapon aimed at whoever is not ready to receive it.

His own people had taught him this, in the end. Not the politicians. The poets.

"When I was young," he began, "before the war, before any of it, I worked in the Hall of Recorded Voices in Ilvess-Prime. Do you know what my first task was?"

"Cataloguing?" Rafael guessed. "Restoration?"

"Listening to arguments." He smiled at her confusion. "The Hall kept recordings of every great dispute in Vessic history — the succession crises, the orbital charter debates, the water wars of the southern continents. My elders believed that a civilization is defined not by what it agrees upon but by how it argues. So I spent six years listening to people shout at each other across four centuries." He sipped the tea and grimaced, out of ritual rather than honesty; the tea never changed and neither did his grimace, and this constancy was itself a comfort. "And in six years I learned one lesson that has kept me alive longer than my entire species."

Rafael waited. She was good at waiting. That was rare in humans, he had found. Most of them listened the way they breathed — automatically, without attention. This one listened the way she translated: with her whole body leaning toward the shape of the meaning.

"The lesson is this," Ilyen said. "A truth given to only one side of a conflict does not end the conflict. It arms one hand against the other."

He let the words sit in the humid air between them. Around them the hydroponics systems ticked and sighed, and somewhere far above them, in the dark heart of the station, the reactor counted down days that were no longer plentiful.

"I don't understand," Rafael said slowly. "The evidence — the forgery, the staged attack, everything in the sealed message. If we give it to Fleet Command—"

"To your fleet," Ilyen said. "Yes. And then what happens? Your admirals read it. They verify it — or they bury it, or they dispute it, or they leak a corrupted version. And then your government faces a choice: confess, or destroy the messenger. Which do you think they will choose? You have told me yourself about the patrol vessel hunting you across the dark. You have told me about Captain Adeyemi, who loves you and who offered you amnesty for silence. Love like that is the strongest glue your species makes. They will use it to hold the lid on the truth."

"They might do the right thing."

"Might." He said the word in Vessic — *sheth*, a small sharp syllable, the sound of a pebble dropped into a dry well. "I have been alive for a very long time, Rafael Reyes, and I can tell you what *might* is worth. When the Vessic Assembly received the first intelligence that human ships had burned Colony Mereth, we gave that truth to our military alone. We trusted them to respond proportionately. We thought the truth was a lamp. But a lamp in one pair of hands is only a torch, and a torch in frightened hands is a fire." His voice roughened. "Your side received the same truth, shaped differently — that *we* had struck first. Two truths, each held by one side, each incomplete, each a weapon. Four hundred million dead between us, and every death justified by half a story."

Rafael had gone very still. He could see her translating even now, not the words but the weight of them, turning the idea over the way she turned unfamiliar syntax over, looking for the load-bearing structure.

"You're saying the message has to go to both sides," she said. "At once."

"I am saying more than that." He leaned forward, and the machines adjusted around him with soft mechanical concern, and he ignored them as he had learned to ignore pain. "Listen carefully, because this is the part I have rehearsed for two hundred years, and I may only ever say it once, and you must carry it exactly. The message cannot be delivered sequentially. Not first to one fleet, then the other. Not leaked to one government's press while the other learns secondhand. Simultaneous means simultaneous — the same words arriving in the same instant into every ear capable of hearing them. Because the moment one side possesses the full truth and the other possesses only rumor, the side with the truth holds power, and power does not confess. Power negotiates. And negotiation from asymmetry is just conquest with better manners."

"But logistically—" Rafael began.

"I am not finished." He raised one finger, and she stopped, and he loved her a little for stopping. "There is a second condition, and it is harder. The message must be whole. No omissions. No redactions for diplomatic comfort. No softened framing. If your fleet receives the forgery evidence but the testimony of the soldiers who refused the order is cut away — because it is embarrassing, because it implicates living officers — then the message becomes a blade with a handle built for one grip. Partial truth is not diluted poison. It is concentrated poison, sweetened."

"And if we can't manage it?" Her voice was quiet. "If we can't reach both fleets at once, if the timing fails, if Adeyemi's marines take the array first — what then?"

Ilyen looked at her for a long moment. Beyond the glass of the hydroponics bay, the dying star wheeled its slow red light across the station's scarred hull, and he thought about all the dawns he had watched from inside this metal shell, counting them the way other men counted money.

"Then we keep it," he said. "We keep it until we can. A truth undelivered is a grief. A truth delivered halfway is a massacre. I have chosen grief before. I can choose it again."

"That's — Ilyen, that's insane. The reactor—"

"The reactor is a clock, not a judge." He said it firmly, though his hands, he noticed, had begun to tremble, and he folded them closed to hide it. "Do not let the countdown make your decisions for you. Fear is the oldest editor in the universe. It cuts the story short and calls it mercy. Your fleet built its lie on fear. Our fleet answered with fear. Everything terrible in the last two hundred years was decided by someone in a hurry."

* * *

She did not argue after that. She sat with him through the long afternoon shift while the star light dimmed and the vines dripped and the machines kept their patient vigil over his failing body, and she asked questions — careful, precise, translator's questions — and he answered them, and somewhere in the middle of it he realized he was teaching her the way his own teachers had taught him, by circling the thing rather than striking at it, letting understanding arrive like an animal coaxed out of cover rather than flushed out by fire.

Near the end, she asked the question he had been waiting for.

"Why me?"

He had prepared many answers for this question across two centuries of waiting. The true answer was simple and terrible, and he gave it plainly, because she had earned plainness.

"Because the message was never meant to be finished by one of us." He gestured weakly at the archive, at the whole buried library of Kestrel-9, at the sealed core where the proof slept. "We wrote it knowing we would die. An extinct species delivering its own vindication — do you see the flaw? No one would believe us. The dead accusing the living! Our testimony would be dismissed as propaganda, as posthumous spite. The message needed a second voice. Someone who belonged to the other side. Someone whose loyalty could not be questioned because it *would* be questioned, loudly, and survive." He met her eyes. "Someone who learned our language not as an enemy studies a target, but the way a child learns a lullaby — without knowing why, until the reason arrives."

Her face did something complicated. He had watched human faces for a long time and still could not always parse them, but he knew grief when he saw it, and he knew recognition.

"Fleet trained me," she said. "They put Vessic material in front of me when I was a child. They were growing a translator for exactly this."

"Yes."

"And you knew? All this time — you knew what I was?"

"I hoped," Ilyen corrected gently. "Knowing is a luxury of the powerful. I hoped. And when you docked without permission and spoke to the doors in imperfect but living Vessic, I permitted myself to believe that hope had finally paid its debt." He reached out and touched her wrist, lightly, the way one touches a fragile instrument. "But hear me, Rafael. Being made into a tool does not make you one. They sharpened you for their purpose. Purposes change hands. That is the whole secret of history."

* * *

That night — or what passed for night aboard Kestrel-9, the lights dropping to ember-dim on their programmed schedule — Ilyen lay in his alcove beneath the respirator's rhythm and composed the warning again in his head, testing its structure the way an engineer tests a bridge.

He thought about the girl sleeping somewhere above him in the crew quarters of the Emrys, dreaming, perhaps, in his language. He thought about Sefa Okonkwo, who distrusted him and was right to, and whose distrust was itself a kind of integrity. He thought about the reactor, and the patrol ship surely closing across the dark, and the two fleets parked on opposite sides of an old battlefield, loaded and waiting, each certain of its own half-truth.

Two hundred years ago, he had been a young archivist carrying boxes of recordings down into the deep vaults of Ilvess-Prime while the sky burned, and an elder had pressed a data-core into his hands and said: *Take this where no one wants it, and give it to everyone.*

He had not understood then. He understood now. Everyone, or no one. Together, or never. The message was not a letter; it was a keystone, and a keystone only holds if it lands in the center of the arch.

His chest tightened — the machines responded instantly, adjusting, soothing, spending more of the reactor's dwindling patience on him — and he breathed through it, and in the breathing he made his decision final, the way one seals a document.

Tomorrow he would begin teaching her the transmission protocols. Tomorrow he would show her the array, and the timing windows, and the geometry of simultaneity. Tomorrow he would tell her the hardest part: that the message must include the confession of the Vessic fleets too — their reprisals, their atrocities, their own lies — or it would be nothing but grievance dressed as justice. Both wounds or neither. That was the price, and he had written it into the archive himself, long ago, in a steadier hand than the one he possessed now.

He closed his eyes. Somewhere below, the reactor ticked off another day. Somewhere beyond the hull, a patrol ship burned toward them with orders he could guess word for word.

And somewhere in the dark between the fleets, the space where the message would have to land — that narrow, impossible, simultaneous instant — waited, empty, like a door held open by a dead man's hand.

He slept, and dreamed of the Hall of Recorded Voices, and in the dream every shelf was full, and every recording was playing at once, and a young woman's voice was reading his words back to him in flawless Vessic — except one sentence, near the end, that she pronounced slightly wrong.

In the morning, he resolved, he would correct her grammar.

It was, after all, the only thing left that he knew how to do.
