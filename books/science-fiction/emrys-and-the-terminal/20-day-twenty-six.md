## 20. Day Twenty-Six

The number had become a kind of weather. Rafael woke to it the way she once woke to rain on the hull of a groundside posting — not hearing it so much as feeling it, a pressure behind the sternum, a gray weight on everything she touched. Fourteen days gone. Twenty-six remaining. The countdown lived in the walls of Kestrel-9 now, in the amber flicker of corridor strips running at half power, in the way Sefa had taken to speaking in short sentences, as if syllables cost wattage.

She found Ilyen where she always found him, in the archive chamber that had become his sickroom, lying beneath the arch of machines he called — when he had breath for jokes — *the cradle*. The word was beautiful and terrible at once: *vessha*, which also meant the woven basket Vessic mothers once lowered into rivers, letting the current decide whether a child would live. The machines hummed around him with the same indifference. They kept his blood oxygenated, his failing lungs inflated, his ancient heart beating in its slow, stubborn rhythm. And they drank power the way dying things drink water.

"Sit," he said, before her boots finished crossing the threshold. His eyes were closed. "You hover. Hovering is very human. I have decided it is your species' defining trait."

"You've decided that about six times."

"And each time with more evidence." He opened his eyes then, and Rafael felt the familiar lurch — the color of them, pale gold like tea held up to light, set in a face that was all planes and patience, skin gone thin and translucent over two centuries of borrowed time. "Come. Sit. Your grammar has been suffering. I heard you muttering in the corridor. You conjugated *sel* as if it were animate."

"It *is* animate. It's the verb for remembering out loud."

"*Sel* is remembering out loud, yes, but only when the memory is shared. You were alone in the corridor. Therefore you were not conjugating *sel*. You were conjugating *thel*." He lifted one hand from the cradle's edge, trembling with the effort, and sketched the shape of the distinction in the air: two spirals, one open, one closed. "Alone-remembering. A different verb. A different world. Your people never learned this because your language assumes an audience even in silence. Ours did not."

Rafael sat on the low stool beside him, the one worn smooth by two hundred years of exactly this posture — someone listening. "Then teach me the difference again."

"No." He said it gently, but firmly, the way he said most important things. "Today we do not work."

"The message—"

"The message will survive one day without me annotating it. It survived two hundred years without anyone at all." He turned his head, slowly, to look at her fully, and there was something in his gaze that made her hands go still in her lap. "Today I want to talk about power."

"Sefa rerouted the hydroponics reserves last night. We're stable until—"

"Not station power." His hand rose again, weakly, and came to rest over the cradle's control panel — the panel that governed the machines keeping him alive. "This power."

The chamber went very quiet. Somewhere above them, through layers of hull and vacuum, the red dwarf burned on, indifferent, and the reactor counted, and counted.

"Ilyen—"

"Listen to me first. You always want to speak; it is the translator's disease. Listen first." He drew a breath that the machines assisted, a long mechanical sigh threaded through his own thin whistle of air. "Sefa showed me the numbers this morning. She thought I would not understand them. But numbers are numbers, in any tongue. The reactor needs what I am taking. Every hour I breathe, the margin narrows. By day thirty-one, perhaps thirty-two, my cradle will be drawing current that containment requires. And then we will discover whether the universe prefers an old man's heartbeat or a star held in its cage."

"We can ration further. We can—"

"You cannot. Sefa has already shaved everything shaveable. She is good, your engineer. She thinks in systems the way I think in grammar — whole structures, nothing wasted." His mouth curved. "Which is why she has already done the arithmetic I am about to do aloud, and why she sent you to me instead of saying it herself. She could not say it. So she let you walk in here and see it."

Rafael looked down at her hands. They were translator's hands, long-fingered, ink-stained in the old habit even though she hadn't touched paper in years. They were shaking.

"I won't do it," she said. "I'm not going to sit here and discuss turning off your machines like we're balancing a ledger."

"You are going to listen," Ilyen said, "and then you are going to be angry, and then you are going to grieve, and then — because you are who you are — you are going to translate my meaning even when you hate it. That is what translators do. They carry what cannot be carried." He paused, gathering breath, and when he spoke again his voice had dropped into the register he used for the oldest texts, the liturgical depth, the voice of the archive itself. "*Thel sel vessha.* Do you know this phrase?"

"Remembering alone... lowered into the river."

"Close. Better than yesterday." His eyes glinted. "It is what we say at the end of a life. Not a prayer. An observation. A person becomes a basket in the current — what they carried is set down, and the river decides where it goes. My river, Rafael, is ending. I have known this longer than you have been alive. What I did not know was whether anything I carried would reach another shore. Then you came, dreaming your way into my language like a fool, like a miracle, and for twenty-six days I have been setting down my cargo into your hands, piece by piece, annotation by annotation." He stopped. Breathed. The machines breathed with him. "There is very little cargo left. But there is one thing left to carry, and it weighs more than all the rest: the message must leave this station. It must. If the reactor dies with it still aboard, then I have spent two hundred years guarding ash."

"It won't die aboard. We'll finish the translation. There's time."

"There is not. And you know it, and your engineer knows it, and the reactor knows it better than any of us." He closed his eyes again, and for a moment he looked simply, unbearably old — older than the war, older than the extinction, older than the lie that had started it all. "So. Here is my request, and I ask it as the last speaker of Vessic, which means I ask it with the full authority of a civilization: let me set down my basket early. Cut the power to the cradle. Give my hours to the reactor. Let the message live."

"No."

The word came out of her flat and hard, before thought, before grammar, before anything. Ilyen opened his eyes.

"No," Rafael said again, steadier now, and leaned forward, elbows on knees, close enough to see the fine map of veins at his temple. "You don't get to spend yourself like fuel. You're not a reserve tank. You're the last man who ever dreamed in Vessic, and when you go, the language doesn't just lose a speaker — it loses its *center of gravity*. Everything I translate after that, I translate alone. *Thel*, not *sel*. Alone-remembering, forever. You think I can carry that? You think I want to find out?"

"That is precisely why—" he began.

"Don't." Her voice cracked and she hated it, hated the human rawness of it, hated that grief made her monolingual. "Don't dress it up as duty. Don't make it a gift. I've read enough of your archive to know how your people talked about death — you didn't romanticize it, you *scheduled* it, you folded it into the household, you made it practical. Well, I'm not Vessic. I'm a fleet-raised orphan who learned your language in dreams she never asked for, and I am telling you, as the second-to-last voice your language will ever have: no. We find another way. We cut power from somewhere else. We strip the Emrys down to her bones if we have to. Sefa will figure it out. She's already figuring it out. That's why she sent me in here — not to agree with you. To keep you talking while she works."

Ilyen stared at her. And then, slowly, wondrously, he began to laugh — a thin, rusted sound, quickly strangled by his lungs, but real, the first true laugh she'd ever heard from him. The machines chirped in alarm and he waved them quiet with one finger.

"Oh," he managed. "Oh, you are *cruel*. You are cruel and correct, which is the worst combination in any language. Yes. Yes, she messaged me an hour ago — rerouting from the Emrys's auxiliary cells, buying us four days, maybe five. She asked me not to tell you until you had slept." His laugh faded into something softer, and his hand crossed the space between them and settled over hers, light as ash, warm as a hearth. "She knew you would refuse me. She said — let me render it faithfully — 'Tell the old man no. Loudly. He respects loud.'"

Rafael laughed too, wet and unwilling, and did not pull her hand away.

"But hear me," Ilyen said, and the laughter drained out of his voice, leaving only the deep still water beneath it. "Hear me fully, because I will not ask twice, and because one day — soon — you will have to answer without me beside you. I am not afraid of the river. I have been standing in it for two hundred years. What I fear is not my death but a *wasted* death — a death that takes power from the message instead of giving power to it. Promise me nothing today. But promise me you will hold both truths at once: that I want to live — oh, how I want to live, to see what you build, to hear what you dream — *and* that if the day comes when my heartbeat stands between the message and the dark, you must choose the message. Not for duty. For love. For *sel*. Because a shared memory does not die in the one who carries it onward. It dies only in the one who hoards it."

Rafael looked at their hands, his over hers, the last speaker and the almost-last, and said nothing, because he had asked her to promise nothing, and because some translations take longer than a lifetime.

Above them, faint through the deck plates, the reactor counted. Below them, in the hydroponics bay, Sefa worked by lamplight, rerouting the pulse of a ship to buy an old man time. And between those two numbers — the countdown and the heartbeat — Rafael sat in the amber half-light of a dead civilization's library, holding the hand that held everything, learning the hardest grammar she would ever study: the tense that holds love and loss in the same sentence, neither one winning, both one true.

Outside the viewport, far off across the black, a pinprick of light moved against the stars.

Too steady for debris. Too slow for a comet.

Rafael watched it for three full seconds before her stomach dropped — before she recognized the drive signature every fleet-raised child learns before they learn to read.

A patrol vessel. Coming in system.

* * *

She was halfway down the corridor before she keyed her comm. "Sefa. Tell me you're seeing the contact on long-range."

Static. Then Sefa's voice, tight and stripped of everything except function: "Been seeing it for ten minutes. Squawking a Fleet rescue code." A pause, and Rafael could hear her engineer doing the same terrible arithmetic she was. "Rafa — they're not slowing down."
