## 26. Adeyemi's Confession

The brig on the *Vigilant* was not a cell so much as a room that had been taught to pretend it wasn't one. No bars — just a door that sealed with a sound like a held breath, walls the color of unsalted porridge, a bunk bolted at an angle that suggested someone had once cared about ergonomics and then stopped. Captain Nkiru Adeyemi stood outside it for four full minutes before she keyed the lock, because four minutes was how long it took her to assemble her face into something command-shaped.

She had been assembling that face for thirty years.

Inside, Rafael Reyes sat cross-legged on the bunk with her back against the wall, and when the door opened she didn't stand, didn't straighten, didn't perform any of the small obeisances a confined officer owed a visiting captain. She just looked up with those eyes that had always made Adeyemi think of water over stone — patient, and wearing through everything.

"Captain," Rafael said. Not *ma'am*. Not *sir*. The word landed between them like a coin spun in the air, still deciding which face it would show.

"Reyes." Adeyemi stepped inside and let the door seal behind her. The room was too small for two people who had this much history; she could feel the walls taking notes. "You've been eating."

"I've been eating."

"You look thin."

"I've been eating," Rafael said again, with the faintest edge, and Adeyemi almost smiled. There she was. The cadet who'd once argued a linguistics exam question for forty minutes past the bell, not because she needed the marks but because the answer was wrong and someone had to say so.

Adeyemi dragged the single chair from beneath the fold-down desk and sat, knees nearly touching Rafael's. Close enough that the conversation could not be anything but what it was. Far enough that no one watching the internal cameras — and someone was always watching the internal cameras — could call it anything but an interrogation.

She set a slate on her knee. Didn't turn it on. It was there to be seen, not used: the prop of authority.

"Do you know why I came out here myself?" she asked. "I could have sent Commander Osei. I could have handled this from Thessaly Ring with a comm link and a signature. Instead I burned eleven days of patrol schedule to sit in a room with you."

"You wanted to see me."

"I wanted to contain you." Adeyemi let that sit. "There's a difference, and I need you to understand it before we go one word further. What you did — falsifying a flight plan, diverting a fleet courier to a restricted station, harboring a Vessic national under active security classification — none of that is a misunderstanding. None of it is defensible. If I do my job the way the manual says, you spend the rest of your life somewhere smaller than this room, and the old man spends his remaining days in a research facility being asked questions by people who will never learn to pronounce his name."

Rafael's jaw tightened, but her voice stayed level. "And if you don't do your job the way the manual says?"

Adeyemi looked at her hands. They were sixty-one years old now, and they had never once trembled in front of a subordinate, and she hated that they wanted to.

"That," she said quietly, "depends on what you think you found down there."

* * *

The silence stretched long enough that Adeyemi could hear the station through the hull — or imagined she could. Kestrel-9 hung off the *Vigilant*'s port bow like a wound that had learned to hold its shape, its scarred hull catching the red dwarf's light and giving back nothing. Her marines were aboard it now, twenty-three of them walking corridors that had been silent for two centuries, cataloguing a dead civilization's belongings as evidence. She had signed the order herself. *Secure and preserve.* As if preservation were something Fleet knew how to do.

"I'm going to tell you a story," Adeyemi said. "You're going to want to interrupt it about six times. Don't. If you interrupt me, I'll stop, and you'll spend the rest of your life wondering how it ends."

Rafael said nothing. But she shifted forward on the bunk, and Adeyemi recognized the posture — the listener's lean, the one Rafael had done in every seminar since she was nineteen years old, the one that meant *I am going to take everything you say apart later, but first I will hear all of it.*

"Thirty-two years ago I was a lieutenant commander attached to Naval Intelligence, Ninth Analysis Group. Signals, mostly. Pattern work. I was good at it — good enough that they gave me things that weren't signals anymore, things that had stopped being language and started being problems." Adeyemi turned the slate over in her hands, its dark screen reflecting her own face back at her, warped and gray. "In my third year, a courier package came across my desk from the Meridian sector. Pre-war intercepts. Vessic traffic from the months before the Kolaris Colony attack. Raw recordings, with a partial translation appended by a field analyst who died before he could finish the job."

Rafael had gone very still. Adeyemi watched her arrive at the shape of it — not the content yet, but the container, the terrible category of thing this was going to be.

"The official record says the Vessic struck Kolaris without warning. Fourteen thousand colonists. The atrocity that justified the war, the one they put in every schoolbook on both sides of the line. And the intercepts on my desk—" Adeyemi's voice caught, and she was furious at it, and she pushed through anyway, because she had carried this for three decades and if she was finally setting it down it was going to be whole. "The intercepts showed Vessic ships responding to a distress call from Kolaris. Arriving after the blast. Broadcasting rescue frequencies into wreckage. There was no attack formation, Reyes. There was no approach vector. There were ships that came to help, and arrived too late, and were blamed for it."

The room's air recycler cycled. Somewhere beyond the door, boots passed in the corridor.

"There was a second signal buried under the distress traffic," Adeyemi continued. "Human-encoded. Fleet cipher, a deep-operations key I didn't have clearance for but recognized the structure of. My analysis flagged it as anomalous. I wrote it up. I did everything right, Reyes, I want you to know that — I wrote it up exactly the way they trained us to, with citations and confidence intervals and a recommendation for immediate review." She laughed, and there was nothing in it. "Three days later my report was reclassified. A week after that, my group was dissolved and reassigned. And a major named Okwuosa — you won't find him in any record, he was retired into nothing within the year — sat across a table from me in a room very much like this one and explained my options."

"And you chose," Rafael said. Not a question. Her voice was careful, the way a person's voice is careful when they're walking on ice over deep water.

"I chose." Adeyemi set the slate down on the floor beside her chair, deliberately, the way you put down something heavy you intend never to pick up again. "I told myself it was the same choice everyone makes. That one analyst's conscience against a war footing, that the truth would surface eventually on its own, that I had a career and eventually a command and people who depended on me. I told myself the story where staying quiet was prudence instead of cowardice." She met Rafael's eyes. "Thirty-two years, Reyes. Do you know what shrapnel does? It doesn't kill you. It travels. Every year it moves a little closer to something vital. You learn to live around it. You build your whole body around avoiding the place where it sits. And then some day you're standing in a briefing room and a young officer you helped raise is looking at you across a table, and you realize the thing you've been living around all this time has a name, and the name is *Kestrel-9*, and it's orbiting a dying star with the proof sitting in it, and the girl you trained found it because I trained her too well."

* * *

For a moment neither of them moved. Then Rafael unfolded from the bunk and stood, and even confined, even stripped of rank pending tribunal, she managed to seem taller than Adeyemi — not in body but in bearing, the way a person seems tall when they are standing in a truth and you are standing beside it.

"How much do you know," Rafael said, "about what's actually in the archive?"

"I know what Command believes is in it. Propaganda materials. Cultural artifacts. Nothing operationally significant." Adeyemi's mouth twisted. "I also know that Command has wanted that station erased for thirty years, and that erasure orders don't get written for propaganda. So I know that whatever's in it frightened someone badly enough to keep the fear alive across three decades and five chiefs of staff."

"It's worse than fear." Rafael paced the three steps the cell allowed, turned, paced back. "It's the complete record, Captain. The intercepts you saw — the ones that got reclassified — they're in there. With the full translation. With testimony from Vessic survivors of Kolaris who pulled human bodies out of the rubble. With the cipher fragment matching the deep-operations key. And with an annotation, in the archivist's hand, dating the forgery decision to nine days *before* the colony was destroyed. Nine days, Captain. Someone planned Kolaris. Someone built the grief first and then manufactured the event to fit it."

Adeyemi closed her eyes. Thirty-two years of telling herself the report might have been misread, mistranslated, incomplete — the comfortable ambiguity that had let her sleep, most nights, in a bed paid for by the institution that buried it. Rafael had just taken the ambiguity away with one sentence, and Adeyemi found that what remained underneath was not surprise. It was recognition. The shrapnel, finally, all the way home.

"Ilyen," Rafael said. "The archivist. He's alive, Captain. He's been alive the whole time, waiting, keeping the record intact, correcting my grammar while his heart fails. He's the last speaker of his language and your marines walked into his home with weapons and called him an asset." Her voice cracked on the last word, and she let it. "He's dying. He was dying before we ever docked. And Command wants to interrogate him through me, selectively, and bury the rest, and I need you to tell me — looking at me, Nkiru — that you didn't come here to make me help them do that."

It was the first time in twenty years anyone aboard her ship had used her given name. Adeyemi let the sting of it pass through her and out the other side, because she had earned it, and because the alternative was to hide behind rank in the last honest conversation of her career.

"No," she said. "That's not why I came."

* * *

She stood then, because sitting felt like too much comfort for what she had to say next, and crossed to the narrow viewport — a slit of reinforced glass showing the station's wounded silhouette against the dwarf's ember-red glare.

"I can't help you," she said to the glass. "Hear me completely, because I'll only say this once. I cannot help you. If I open one door for you, the whole structure comes down on both our heads, and not cleanly — there are people above me, Reyes, people whose entire careers are built on top of Kolaris, and they have resources and reach you cannot imagine, and they will not lose gracefully. They will lose the way institutions lose: slowly, viciously, and through people like you and me."

"Then why are you here?"

"Because there's a difference between helping and hindering, and I've spent thirty years on the wrong side of that line, and I am not spending thirty more." Adeyemi turned. In the red light from the viewport her face looked older than sixty-one, older than the war itself, carved out of everything she'd refused to say. "Here is what will happen. Tomorrow morning I file my preliminary assessment. It will state that the archive's contents are consistent with Command's expectations — cultural material, low operational value — and that the prisoner Reyes is cooperative but shaken, and that the surviving Vessic is medically non-viable for extended interrogation. That assessment buys you time. Days, maybe a week, before someone senior enough to distrust my judgment sends their own analysts."

Rafael stared at her. "You're falsifying your report."

"I'm writing an inaccurate one. There's a distinction, and I've had thirty years to refine it." The bitterness in her own voice surprised her. "I'm telling you this so you understand what it costs, and so you understand what it doesn't buy. I am not giving you the array codes. I am not ordering my marines elsewhere. I am not unlocking anything, decrypting anything, or looking away from anything at any specific scheduled moment. I am simply going to be a tired old woman whose attention slips, once, in a way I will neither plan nor prevent. What you do inside that slip is yours. The consequences are yours. The tribunal, if it comes to that, is yours. I will testify to what I saw, which will be nothing, and what I did, which will be my duty, imperfectly."

"Why?" The word came out of Rafael raw. "After everything — why now? Why not thirty years ago? Why not when I was a cadet and would have followed you anywhere?"

Adeyemi walked back toward her, slowly, and stopped close enough that the words wouldn't need volume.

"Because thirty years ago I was afraid," she said. "And because this morning I stood on my bridge and watched a marine escort a dying man through a corridor in restraints, and I thought: *she learned that walk from us. We taught an entire generation to walk like that.* And because you asked me once — do you remember, second year, the ethics seminar, you were maybe twenty — you asked me what a person owes to the truth when the truth costs other people their peace. And I gave you the approved answer, and you looked at me the way you're looking at me now, and I have been answering that question wrong for thirty-two years, and I am so tired, Rafael. I am so tired of carrying it."

The recycler cycled. Out in the corridor, the ship breathed its machine breath. And Rafael Reyes — traitor, hero, student, the best mind Adeyemi had ever helped shape and the sharpest indictment of everything that shaping served — reached out and took her former captain's hand, briefly, hard, the grip of someone accepting a weight rather than offering forgiveness.

"A week," Rafael said. "Maybe less. Ilyen doesn't have a week."

"Then don't waste it talking to me." Adeyemi reclaimed her hand, squared her shoulders, and let the command-face settle back into place like a mask going on wet. At the door she paused, one finger hovering over the release, and spoke without turning around.

"There's a maintenance window tomorrow, oh-four-hundred station time. Reactor diagnostics. All nonessential personnel pull back to the *Vigilant* for radiation protocol — standard procedure, nothing anyone will question." She keyed the lock. "I'll be asleep. Captains sleep poorly, though. Light sleepers, the lot of us."

The door opened onto the corridor's white light. Behind her, she heard Rafael exhale — a long, shaking breath that sounded, to Adeyemi's ear, like a woman who had just been handed a loaded weapon and told only that the safety was off.

Adeyemi walked back toward her cabin without hurrying, nodding to the watch officer, signing the duty log, being, in every observable particular, exactly what she had always been.

And in the privacy of her own skull, where no tribunal could follow, she began — for the first time in thirty-two years — to rehearse what she would say if anyone ever finally asked her the truth.
