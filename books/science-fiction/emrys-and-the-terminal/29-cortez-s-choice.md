## 29. Cortez's Choice

The array's maintenance hatch had a hairline crack running through its seal, and Corporal Danae Cortez had been staring at it for eleven minutes.

She knew the exact number because she'd been counting her breaths to keep from thinking. Four in. Six out. The way her mother taught her when the sirens went off over Ceres Harbor and Danae was nine years old and small enough to fit inside a water reclamation duct. *Count your breaths, mija. Panic is just your body forgetting it knows how to wait.*

Eleven minutes of waiting. The crack in the seal didn't move. Neither did she.

The corridor outside the array chamber was empty at this hour — third shift, station-time, which on Kestrel-9 meant nothing real at all except that the lights dimmed to amber and the ventilation cycled louder, as if the old station were clearing its throat before speaking. Somewhere below her feet, three decks down behind two blast doors and a marine checkpoint, Ilyen was dying. She'd seen him once, through the observation glass of the medical bay they'd converted into a holding cell. A small man folded into machines, hands like dried leaves, eyes that had looked straight through the glass and straight through her as if she were a window he'd already seen everything on the other side of.

He hadn't spoken. He never spoke to any of them. Not in Standard, not in anything. He simply watched, and his watching made every marine on the station feel like the thing being examined.

Danae had signed her enlistment papers four years ago because her brother had signed his six years before that and come home in a box with a flag that cost more than their apartment. She'd signed because someone in her family had to believe the war meant something, or else Marco had died for nothing, and she could not — *could not* — carry both his death and its meaninglessness in the same body. So she'd carried the belief instead. It was heavier, but it fit where the other thing would have gone.

And now Reyes wanted her to take that belief apart bolt by bolt, starting with the lock on this hatch.

* * *

Reyes had come to her two nights ago, in the gym, which on Kestrel-9 was a repurposed cargo bay with gravity set low enough that you could do pull-ups until your shoulders screamed without your knees ever learning what the floor felt like. Danae had been hanging there upside-down from a ceiling strut, blood pooling pleasantly in her skull, when the xenolinguist walked in carrying two ration pouches and an expression like a woman who'd rehearsed something too many times.

"Corporal."

"Cortez is fine." She dropped, landed soft in the low-g, stayed crouched so they'd be closer to eye level. Reyes was fleet — a civilian contractor with fleet credentials, technically, but everyone knew what that meant: raised by the service, shaped by it, owned by it in all the ways that mattered. Danae had spent four years learning to read the difference between people who wore the uniform and people who *were* the uniform. Reyes was the second kind. Which made whatever she was about to say either very safe or very dangerous.

"I need to ask you something," Reyes said, "and I need you to understand that saying no costs you nothing."

"That's usually how the expensive questions start."

Something moved across the linguist's face — almost a smile, gone before it landed. She held out one of the ration pouches. Danae took it. It was coffee, actual coffee, the kind that came out of personal stores rather than dispensers, and that alone told her this conversation had been planned down to the gram.

"The message we're going to broadcast," Reyes said quietly. "It goes to both fleets. Simultaneously. In nine days, during the evacuation window, when the array's Fleet locks cycle for decommissioning." She paused. "Except the locks don't cycle. They're hardwired. Someone has to open them manually, from inside the array housing, and then walk away and let us do what we're going to do."

"And if I say no?"

"Then I find another way, probably a worse one, and you never have to know whether it worked."

Danae turned the coffee pouch over in her hands. Low gravity made everything float a little, even resolve.

"You're asking me to commit treason," she said. "Against the fleet that shipped my brother home in a box."

"No," Reyes said. "I'm asking you to decide who killed him."

The words hung there in the amber-lit cargo bay like something suspended in the low-g itself, refusing to fall.

Because here was the thing Danae had not said aloud to anyone, the thing she kept in the same sealed compartment where she kept the sound of her mother crying at the funeral: Marco's ship, the *Vigilant*, had been lost at Meridian Gap in the war's ninth year, ambushed by Vessic raiders out of nowhere, unprovoked, unannounced. That was the story. That was the story everyone got. But Danae had pulled the after-action file once, during a boring posting, with a lieutenant's login she wasn't supposed to have — and the file had gaps in it. Sensor logs redacted. A forty-minute hole between the last routine transmission and the distress beacon. And in the margin of the damage assessment, in some analyst's careless hand, a note she had never been able to unread:

*Origin of hostile fire inconsistent with known Vessic signatures — see attached.*

There was no attachment. There had never been an attachment. She'd requested it twice through proper channels and been denied twice, politely, and the second denial had come back faster than the first, as if someone had been waiting for the question.

She had told herself it was nothing. Bureaucratic sloppiness. Classification errors. She had told herself that for three years, the way you tell yourself a hull breach is a draft.

"I'll think about it," she said to Reyes, and the linguist nodded like a woman who understood exactly what she was asking and exactly what it would cost, and left the second coffee pouch anyway.

* * *

Now Danae stood in front of the cracked maintenance hatch with a technician's override kit in her pocket that she'd signed out under a falsified work order, and she counted her breaths, and the crack in the seal did not move.

Four in. Six out.

The array chamber beyond the hatch was a cathedral of dead intention. Kestrel-9's deep-space transmitter had been built in the decades before the war, back when this route was alive and the station's job was to sing shipping schedules and weather reports to a hundred worlds. Now it stood silent under dust covers, its antenna farm fanned out into the dark like the ribs of something enormous that had lain down here to die. The Fleet locks were new by comparison — matte-black housings bolted over the transmission controls within the last decade, ugly and deliberate, the hardware equivalent of a hand clamped over a mouth.

She'd done her reconnaissance properly. Three visits across five days, each with a legitimate maintenance pretext, each logged honestly in the duty roster because the smartest lie is the one wrapped around true paperwork. She knew the lock housings used a physical interlock — a shear pin, absurdly simple, designed to survive EMP and software compromise alike. Fleet trusted metal over code. All she had to do was get inside the housing and cut the pin, and the transmission controls would be free, and no diagnostic anywhere would flag it until someone physically tried to transmit.

Nine days. Then the evacuation window. Then Reyes and Okonkwo would come up here with whatever they carried, and either the array would speak or it wouldn't.

Her hands were steady. That surprised her. She'd expected shaking, and there was none, just the faint tremor of adrenaline banked down to coals, and beneath it a strange calm like the sea going flat before weather.

She thought about Marco again, but differently than usual. Usually she thought about him at nineteen, grinning, teaching her to cheat at cards. Tonight she thought about him in the context of the gap in the sensor log — forty minutes, forty minutes, what happened in forty minutes that someone needed erased? — and she felt the compartment in her chest where she kept the question begin, very carefully, to open.

If the note in the margin was true — if the fire that killed the *Vigilant* hadn't come from Vessic ships — then Marco hadn't died fighting the enemy. He'd died as scenery. Set dressing for a war somebody wanted, murdered by his own side so that millions could go on murdering each other with clean consciences. And every medal, every flag, every solemn speech she'd sat through with her jaw clenched and her mother's hand crushing hers — all of it built on the same foundation as the black lock housings in front of her: the certainty that the truth must never get out.

Or the note was nothing. A typo. An analyst's error. And she was about to throw away four years and her brother's memory and her mother's last working heart for a typo.

That was the choice, she realized. Not treason versus loyalty. Certainty versus doubt. And nobody — not Reyes, not Adeyemi, not God — could make it for her.

* * *

She went to see the prisoner.

Not officially. She volunteered for escort rotation, which no one else liked doing because the old man's silence unnerved people, and silence unnerved marines more than gunfire ever had. Gunfire gave you something to shoot back at.

Ilyen sat propped against the wall of the converted med bay, and up close, without the observation glass between them, she could see how precisely the machines were threaded through him — lines at his throat, his wrist, a slow pulse of light along a band at his temple like a tide coming in and giving up. His skin had the translucence of paper held up to a lamp. But his eyes were clear, and when she stopped beside his gurney instead of taking her post at the door, those eyes found her with the unhurried attention of someone who had waited two hundred years and could therefore wait out a marine's hesitation indefinitely.

"I'm not supposed to talk to you," she said.

He said nothing. Of course.

"But you understand me. Don't you. You've understood every word anyone's said in this room since they brought you here."

A flicker. Not quite a nod. Something in the geometry of his face shifted, the way a landscape shifts when cloud shadow passes over it.

Danae glanced at the door. The corridor camera covered the entryway, not the bedside; she'd checked that weeks ago, out of habit, the way she checked everything now. She lowered her voice anyway.

"There's a man," she said. "My brother. He died at Meridian Gap. Everyone says the Vessic did it." Her throat tightened around the next part. "Is it true?"

Ilyen looked at her for a long moment. Then, slowly, with a hand that trembled like a compass needle finding north, he reached up and touched the back of her wrist — just touched it, the lightest pressure, the way you'd touch something to confirm it was real — and spoke.

She didn't understand a word. The language moved through the room like water over stones, syllables she'd heard in intercepted recordings her whole career, always as noise, always as threat. From his mouth it was something else entirely. It sounded like an apology. It sounded like grief being handed over, formally, with both hands, the way you return a fallen soldier's effects.

She couldn't prove any of it. She understood nothing. And yet she stood there with tears running down her face in front of a prisoner she was guarding, because some things don't need translation — they need only a listener who has been waiting her whole life for someone to stop lying to her.

When the shift ended, she walked back through the amber corridors with her decision already made. She just hadn't admitted it to herself yet. That would take four more days.

* * *

On the sixth night, she cut the pin.

She came up alone, off-roster, during the deepest quiet the station offered. The maintenance hatch opened with a whisper of protesting seal. Inside the array chamber the dust covers rose and fell slightly with the ventilation, like sleeping animals breathing, and the black lock housings gleamed dully under her headlamp.

She worked the first housing's access panel with the override kit, fingers sure, movements rehearsed a hundred times in her bunk with a stripped training unit she'd built from salvage. The panel came away. Inside: the shear pin, bright and machined and honest, holding the transmission controls locked exactly as advertised. Fleet's whole architecture of secrecy reduced to a cylinder of hardened steel eight millimeters long.

Her hydraulic cutter fit the space perfectly. She'd measured twice.

Before she squeezed the handle, she allowed herself one last moment at the edge — the moment where she could still close the panel, sign the kit back in, request a transfer, spend the rest of her career as a woman who had almost. She thought about the tribunal that would follow if she were caught. She thought about her mother. She thought about the forty-minute gap in a sensor log, and about an old man's hand on her wrist saying something in a dead language that her bones had translated without asking her permission.

Then she thought about the fact that in nine days this station was going to become a fireball, and everything inside it — the archive, the testimony, the truth, the lie, all of it — would become light and nothing else, unless someone ordinary decided, on an ordinary night, with no one watching, to do a small illegal thing with a hand tool.

Courage, her mother had told her once, isn't the big loud kind. The big loud kind gets medals. The other kind gets you through the door when the sirens go off, and nobody ever thanks you, and that's how you know it counted.

She squeezed the cutter. The pin parted with a click so small it was almost polite.

Housing two took ninety seconds. Housing three, the master, fought her — corroded access screws, a design revision she hadn't anticipated — and for six sweating minutes she lay inside the antenna gantry with her arms above her head in the low-g while her heartbeat filled her ears like a drum corps, certain every second that boots were coming up the corridor. No boots came. The screw gave. The pin gave. The last lock on the last mouth of the last voice of a murdered people hung open to the dark.

She closed the panels. She wiped her prints out of habit, though it hardly mattered — by the time anyone investigated, the evidence would be vapor expanding at several kilometers per second. She packed her kit. She signed nothing, logged nothing, left the maintenance hatch sealed exactly as she'd found it, hairline crack and all.

At the chamber door she stopped, once, and looked back at the shrouded antenna farm — the ribs, the sleeping animal, the mouth waiting to be uncorked — and she did not let herself feel triumphant, because triumph was a luxury for afterward, if there was an afterward.

* * *

The corridor back to the marine berthing ran past the observation blister, and through it Kestrel-9's dying star filled half the sky, swollen and red, shedding itself slowly into space like a wound that refused to close. Danae stood there a moment, kit bag over her shoulder, and let herself look at it.

Somewhere out past that star, two fleets sat facing each other across a frozen line, loaded and ready, each one certain of its own story. In nine days, if Reyes and Okonkwo did their part, both stories were going to catch fire at the same instant, and whatever grew in the ashes would belong to no one Danae could predict.

Her comm buzzed. Duty roster update: she was assigned to the evacuation detail, third wave, guaranteed off-station before final day. Someone in logistics had done her a kindness, or a test.

She marked herself present for it, because a good soldier keeps her options open right up until the moment she burns them.

Then she walked on toward the berthing compartment, and she did not look back at the array chamber, and she did not look back at the star, and she did not look back at any of it — because looking back was a luxury too, and she had finally learned the difference between the things she could afford and the things she couldn't.

Behind her, in the dark of the cathedral, the unlocked array waited in silence for the voice that was coming to fill it — and in the medical bay three decks down, an old man woke from a thin sleep with the sudden, wordless certainty that somewhere above him, a door had opened.
