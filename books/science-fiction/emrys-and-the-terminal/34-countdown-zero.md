## 34. Countdown Zero

The Emrys came apart from the station's spine with a sound Sefa felt in her teeth rather than heard — the docking clamps releasing in a cascade of detonating bolts, each one a small violence, each one necessary. Through the forward viewport, Kestrel-9 fell away behind them like something being lowered into a grave: scarred hull, dark windows, and along its flank the deep-space array still glowing faintly from the heat of transmission, a filament of orange light against the red dwarf's dying glare.

*Sent,* Rafael had said, three minutes ago, her hand still resting on the broadcast console as if she could hold the message in place by touch alone. *It's out of our hands.*

Sefa hadn't answered then. She didn't answer now. Her hands were busy doing the only thing left that mattered — flying.

"Course is locked," she said, and her voice came out rougher than she expected, scraped raw by thirty-nine days of recycled air and rationed water and the particular exhaustion of knowing you were going to die somewhere and then finding out you might not. "Burn window opens in forty seconds. After that, we're riding the shockwave or we're part of it."

Rafael dropped into the seat beside her. She looked terrible. She looked luminous. There was ash-grey under her eyes and Vessic characters still flickering across the secondary display where she'd been monitoring the transmission's integrity, and when she turned to look at Sefa there was something in her face that Sefa had last seen on a girl in the Academy language labs, twenty years ago, translating poetry for extra credit because the fleet curriculum wasn't hard enough to hold her.

"It's moving at lightspeed," Rafael said quietly. "Both fleets receive it in the same second. Ilyen made me check the math four times."

"I know. I did the math too." Sefa ran the pre-burn sequence one more time, though every number was already green. "He would have wanted it checked a fifth."

That got the ghost of a smile out of Rafael, and then it collapsed into something else, something wetter, and Sefa reached over without looking away from the console and gripped her friend's wrist once, hard, the way you hold someone to a railing in a storm.

"Not yet," Sefa said. "Grieve after we're not debris. That's an engineering order."

*

The burn was ugly.

The Emrys was a courier, built for long hauls and gentle accelerations, not for outrunning the death of a station strapped to a failing reactor. Sefa pushed her past rated tolerance anyway, past the point where the frame groaned in frequencies she could feel through the deck plating, past the point where the inertial dampeners started lagging half a second behind every correction and the whole ship seemed to move through syrup. The red dwarf swelled in the viewport, huge and patient, a coal that had been burning since before either of their species learned to write.

Behind them, Kestrel-9's reactor hit the final phase of its countdown. They could see it — the station's running lights cycling faster now, amber to white to a strobing pulse, the machine equivalent of a heartbeat racing toward arrest. Somewhere inside that strobing shell were the hydroponics bays where Ilyen's body lay where they'd been forced to leave it, wrapped in the last of the station's thermal blankets, his hands folded over the annotation tablet he'd spent two centuries waiting to finish.

"We couldn't have carried him," Rafael said. It wasn't a question. It was a woman asking permission to believe something.

"No," Sefa said. "The mass budget wouldn't close. You know it wouldn't."

"He waited two hundred years. He deserved—"

"He deserved the truth delivered," Sefa cut in, gentler than she'd ever cut anything. "And he got it. He finished the sentence, Rafa. You told me yourself — he died mid-word and you completed it. That's not abandonment. That's succession."

Rafael didn't answer. On the tactical display, the countdown read **00:00:04:12** and falling.

*

The detonation, when it came, did not look like the simulations.

Simulations gave you a sphere of expanding plasma, tidy, color-coded, labeled. What actually happened was that Kestrel-9 *bloomed*. For a full second it held its shape — a silhouette of corridors and hydroponics domes and the array's great skeletal dish, all of it lit from within like a lantern — and then the light swallowed the structure entirely, and there was no more station, only a sun being born and immediately dying, a white core collapsing through blue into violet, throwing off a ring of incandescent debris that expanded outward at a fraction of lightspeed but a very real fraction indeed.

"Shockwave in ninety seconds," Sefa said. "Hold on to something."

She threw the Emrys into the gap between the blast front and the star itself, threading the needle with the kind of flying she'd learned hauling cargo through Meridian Gap's gravitational shear, except this time the shear was made of the burning corpse of the only home a dead language had left. The ship screamed. Every warning in the engineering stack went amber at once, then two of them went red, and Sefa silenced them with a flat of her palm because there was nothing to do about any of them except be elsewhere when they finished being true.

The shockwave caught them at the edge of the star's corona.

It didn't hit so much as *pick up* — the whole vessel shuddering sideways, tumbling, the viewport filling with light so complete that for a moment Sefa couldn't see the console, couldn't see her own hands, couldn't see anything except white. The Emrys rolled through turbulence made of radiation and vaporized hull fragments, and somewhere below decks something large let go with a bang that Sefa catalogued automatically — coolant line, secondary loop, survivable — and then, gradually, the light thinned, and the stars came back, and they were through.

Alive. Moving. Falling away from a point of fading brilliance where a station used to be.

Sefa realized she was laughing. Short, disbelieving, ugly laughs, the kind that come out of bodies that have been clenched for weeks. She killed the laughter with effort and checked her board again, because grief and relief both lie, and instruments don't.

"Hull intact," she reported. "Coolant's leaking but we have enough. Reactor's stable. We're alive, Rafa. We're actually—" She stopped. Turned.

Rafael was watching the viewport, where the detonation's afterglow was already dimming, folding back down into the red dwarf's ancient ember-light. Her eyes were dry. Her hands were folded in her lap, and Sefa saw that she was holding the annotation tablet — Ilyen's tablet, salvaged in the last hour, its casing scorched at one corner.

"He's light now," Rafael said. "All of him. Every word he ever spoke. It's traveling outward at lightspeed in every direction. In a hundred years it'll reach worlds that haven't even heard of us." She looked up, and now the tears came, silent, unhurried, the tears of someone who had been holding them for forty days. "He became the message, Sefa. He literally became it. Photons carrying his testimony. He'd have loved the physics of it."

"He'd have corrected your grammar while explaining it," Sefa said, and this time Rafael laughed, wet and broken and real, and Sefa let herself laugh too, and for a moment the cockpit of the battered Emrys held nothing but two women crying and laughing in equal measure while a dead star watched them with indifference older than war.

*

Then the boards lit up.

Not alarms — comms. Both channels. Simultaneously. Fleet Command's encrypted priority band and, impossibly, the open diplomatic frequency that no human ship had legally touched in two hundred years, both of them saturating with traffic at once, dozens of voices, hundreds, a flood of signal pouring in from the direction of the human battle line and, beyond it, relayed and rebroadcast, from the Vessic remnant fleets holding position at the old front.

"They received it," Rafael breathed. "Same second. Both of them. It worked, it actually—"

"Incoming hail," Sefa said. "Priority flag. Fleet Command." She hesitated, hand hovering over the accept key. "And another one. Unencrypted. Source is..." She checked twice, because the readout couldn't possibly be right. "Vessic. A Vessic warship, Rafa. They're hailing us directly. First contact in two centuries, and they're calling *us*."

Rafael stared at the two blinking indicators — the institution that raised her, and the species she'd been trained her whole life to fear, both reaching for the Emrys at once.

On the tactical display, along the entire length of the old front line, weapons systems on both sides were powering down.

Sefa looked at her friend, at the last speaker's student, at the woman who had just become the most important voice in two civilizations.

"So," she said. "Who do we answer first?"
