## 5. Two Fleets on One Horizon

The long-range scope array sits at the top of the old relay tower like a rusted crown nobody wants to wear, and I am the fool who volunteered to wear it. Three nights a week I climb the two hundred and eleven steps—yes, I counted; counting is what I do instead of sleeping—and I sit in a chair that predates my grandmother and I watch the sky for signs that the universe has remembered we exist.

For nine years, the universe obliged by forgetting us completely.

Then Mirabel answered something in the dark, and the forgetting ended, and now I would give a great deal to have the silence back.

* * *

It was the fourth watch after her transmission when I saw them. I want to be precise about this, because precision is the only inheritance my family ever passed down: my grandfather catalogued seed strains until the greenhouse failed, my mother catalogued the dead until there were no more names to enter, and I catalogue everything else. So. Fourth watch after Mirabel's answer went out on frequencies she had no business knowing. Local time 0347. I had a thermocarafe of barley tea going stale beside me and a half-finished inventory of the archive's water-damaged crates open on my slate.

The scope pinged twice. Then it did something it had never done in the six years I'd tended it.

It pinged a third time.

Triple returns meant mass. Serious mass. I recalibrated, hands steady in the way they only go steady when panic arrives, which is one of the more useless ironies of my nervous system. The readout resolved into two clusters of drive signatures, separated by maybe four degrees of arc, both decelerating hard toward Vesper Hollow.

One cluster burned clean and cold—the signature profile of human fusion torches, the kind Earth's navy used before Earth stopped signing its letters. Eleven ships. Heavy tonnage. Warship geometry, all angles and armor, nothing about them built for carrying settlers or supplies or anything that wasn't a message delivered at terminal velocity.

The other cluster burned wrong. That's the only word I have for it. The exhaust spectra didn't match anything in the colonial database, which is not surprising, because the colonial database is a lie with a table of contents. Nine ships, smaller, running hot and ragged, like engines held together by faith and salvage. Refugee ships, my brain offered helpfully. Ships that have been running for two hundred years because stopping means dying.

Two fleets. One horizon. Both pointed at us.

I sat very still for a long time and listened to the tea go cold, and I thought: *we are the coin in a game whose rules nobody told us.*

* * *

Governor Ansel received my report at 0500, which is to say I woke him, which is to say he made sure I understood, through the entire conversation, that waking him was an act of aggression I would answer for later. He listened without interrupting—that was the frightening part, Ansel always interrupting—and then he said, "Show me," and put on his coat over his sleepclothes like a man dressing for his own funeral, which, given what he did next, may have been exactly how he felt.

We stood together in the tower while the scopes refreshed their data. Ansel studied the readouts for perhaps ninety seconds. He did not ask what the second fleet was. I want to be clear about that. A man who had spent his whole career insisting our founders came from Earth, that we were a human agricultural charter gone quietly feral, looked at an alien drive signature inbound to his colony and did not ask a single question.

Which meant he already knew.

"Teo," he said, "how much of the sealed archive have you actually read?"

"Enough," I said.

"Enough to understand why those ships are coming here?"

"Enough to understand that you've known, or suspected, for years."

He turned from the scope and looked at me, and I will say this for Ansel: he never lied to your face if he could avoid it. He lied *around* your face, above it, beneath it, but direct falsehood offended some deep administrative piety in him. "I knew the founding records were inconsistent," he said. "I knew the first generation scrubbed their own history so thoroughly that even the scrubbing was scrubbed. I assumed refugees. Political embarrassment, financial crimes, something sordid and survivable." He gestured at the alien signatures crawling across my display. "I did not assume *this*."

"And now?"

"Now," he said, "it doesn't matter what's true. It matters what those two fleets believe when they arrive." He tapped the human cluster. "That is a punitive expedition. I recognize the hull classes—they're from the era when Earth still punished its colonies for existing. They'll have orders regarding whoever destroyed the original colony ships." He tapped the other cluster. "And that, apparently, is whoever the original colony ships belonged to. Or fled from. Or—" He stopped himself, jaw working. "Both of them think the other side committed a massacre. And we are sitting on the evidence."

"Are we?" I asked. "Or are we the evidence?"

Ansel gave me a look I couldn't parse then and have parsed too well since. "You sound like Eriksen," he said, which from him was roughly equivalent to *you sound like a heretic*, and he left the tower without another word, taking the stairs down at a pace that suggested he intended to outwalk the future for as long as his legs allowed.

* * *

I found Mirabel in the archive at dawn, because Mirabel was always in the archive now, sleeping in the reading room more often than her own quarters since the night she answered the signal. She had the forbidden Ashkari materials spread around her in concentric rings, like a woman building a nest out of a language.

"Two fleets," I said, no preamble. We had passed the preamble stage somewhere around the exhumation of the founding records.

She didn't gasp or drop anything. She closed her eyes for a moment, the way you do when a diagnosis confirms the symptom you'd been pretending was nothing. "How long?"

"Deceleration curves put the human ships at nineteen days. The others, twenty-three. Give or take."

"Nineteen days." She opened her eyes. "To teach an entire colony an ancestry it never knew it had."

"That's the plan? Ansel's plan?"

"It's the only plan." She gathered her pages—not scattered, I noticed, but ordered, numbered in her small precise hand. She'd been building something. Of course she had. "Teo, listen to me. If the human fleet arrives and finds a colony of humans claiming to be human, fine, except they'll want to know why we're broadcasting in Ashkari, why the archives show what they show, and eventually someone opens the sealed records and finds out the founders were Ashkari refugees wearing stolen identities—and then the punitive fleet decides we're collaborators with the enemy that murdered their ancestors' mission. If the Ashkari fleet arrives and finds humans squatting on the bones of their dead, they conclude the genocide's last chapter was written here, and they finish it." She spread her hands. "But if we are Ashkari—if Vesper Hollow stands up and says, in their own tongue, *we survived, we kept the language alive, we are what's left of you*—then the remnant fleet cannot burn us without burning themselves, and the human fleet faces a diplomatic catastrophe instead of a target."

"You're describing a lie," I said. "A colony-wide lie, taught in nineteen days, sustained under interrogation by two armed powers."

"I'm describing a translation," she said. "Of ourselves."

And here is the thing I have not told anyone, the thing I will carry in whatever pocket of me survives what's coming: for one full second, looking at her face lit by the archive lamps, I wanted to believe her. Not because the lie was clever—though God, it was clever—but because I have spent my whole life cataloguing a colony that was dying of having no story, and she was offering us one. Even a false story, it turns out, beats an empty grave.

Then I remembered Teo Vasquez, archivist, keeper of the actual records, son of the woman who entered every death into the ledger by hand, and the wanting curdled into something colder.

"The real records exist," I said. "I've read them. Sef-Ilan's people—or whoever commands that fleet—will know within minutes of landing whether our Ashkari is native or performed. And Ansel will need someone to blame when it falls apart. He'll need a falsifier. Someone whose job made falsification plausible."

Mirabel went very still. "You don't know that."

"I catalogue things, Mirabel. It's what I do. And I've been cataloguing Ansel for thirty years." I picked up my slate. "Nineteen days. I'd better start deciding what kind of liar I'm willing to be."

* * *

That evening I climbed the tower again, because ritual is just fear with a schedule, and I watched the two clusters crawl closer. Between them, in the gap of dark space they were converging on, sat Vesper Hollow: four thousand hectares of failing hydroponics and stubborn people, invisible at that distance, a dust mote between two fists.

I thought about my mother's ledger. About how she wrote each name in ink because pencil smudges, because she wanted the record to outlast the eraser. Somewhere below me, Mirabel was writing us into a fiction with the same care, the same ink, the same terrible love. Two women, two records, one truth between them, and neither willing to let go of her half.

On the nineteenth day, the human fleet would arrive first. On the twenty-third, the Ashkari. In between, we had four days to become the thing that could survive being found out by both.

I entered the new data into the log. Fleet signatures, vectors, timelines. Then, in the notes field, where I usually write things like *scope calibration drift, 0.3 degrees*, I wrote:

*Colony population: 214. Projected population at contact: unknown. The archive is ready. The archivist is not.*

Below the tower, the settlement lights came on one by one as dusk fell, ordinary lights, lights belonging to people who mostly didn't yet know that the sky above them had grown teeth. Ansel would tell them soon, or Mirabel would, or the ships themselves would, arriving as the loudest announcement in our history.

And I realized, watching the first stars come out around those two slow burns of approaching fire, that I already knew which of them would break first.

It wouldn't be Ansel. It wouldn't be Mirabel.

It would be whichever one of us the fleets asked to prove who we were—and I had three days before Ansel called me into his office and told me exactly what my role in the proof would be.
