## 10. The Last Speaker

A year ago I kept a census of the dying. Now I keep a census of the staying.

It's a shorter list. That's not a complaint; it's just arithmetic. Forty-one names as of this morning, when old Wren Halloway finally let her daughter talk her onto the transport skiff bound for the joint settlement at Meridian Basin. Forty-one souls who looked at rescue and said, politely or otherwise, no thank you.

I've started recording them again—the way they say *good morning*, which in our new language is literally "the light finds you first." The way the children argue over whether a river can be *remembered* if it dried up before anyone alive saw it. (Verdict: yes, but you have to use the long form of the verb, the one that takes four syllables and sounds like an apology to the water.)

Teo says I'm sentimental. Teo says a lot of things with the confidence of someone who was right about everything at twenty-three and will never fully recover from it.

"Sentimental," he repeated yesterday, hauling another crate of salvaged archive film into the memorial hall we're building out of the old transmitter station. "You cried at a *conjugation table*."

"It was a beautiful conjugation table."

"It was a door with words chalked on it."

"And yet." I handed him the next crate. "History will remember that door."

History, for once, seems inclined to cooperate. The joint memorial commission—three humans, three Ashkari, one exhausted mediator who has learned to sleep standing up—approved our site last month. They're calling it the Vesper Hollow Archive of Shared Grief, which is bureaucratic poetry if I've ever heard it. Sef-Ilan suggested a better name in Ashkari: *the place where we stopped counting who buried whom*. The commission is still deliberating. Bureaucracy moves slowly even when it's trying to be profound.

The fleets went home in spring. Not all at once—that would have been too clean, too much like forgiveness—but in stages, warships peeling away from orbit like leaves deciding it was time. The human punitive fleet left last, after their admirals spent six weeks reading the genocide records I broadcast and discovering that Earth's greatest shame wasn't losing two colony ships. It was erasing what happened to the people who fled before them.

There were tribunals. There are always tribunals. Ansel stood at his and told the truth for the first time in his career, which reportedly shocked the panel more than any lie could have. He's serving his sentence somewhere with better weather than Vesper Hollow ever offered. He writes me letters. I answer maybe half of them. Forgiveness, I've learned, doesn't have to be fluent to be sincere.

* * *

This morning I taught the small class—nine children, ages five through eleven, the entire future population of the valley under twelve—how to say *home* in the bridge-language.

Not the word itself. They've known the word since they were babies; half of them dream in it now, which still stops my breath when I think about it too hard. No, today's lesson was harder: how to say home *to someone who lost theirs*. How to offer shelter without implying ownership. It's a grammatical distinction we built deliberately, Sef-Ilan and I, back when the language was mostly chalk dust and stubbornness between the two of us. You cannot say "my home welcomes you" in our tongue. You can only say "this home remembers being welcomed, and passes the debt forward."

The children practiced on each other until the phrase wore smooth. Little Ada Solano—Ada, whose grandmother chose to stay specifically so her granddaughter would learn "the language Mirabel made"—raised her hand and asked why the debt always goes forward and never back.

"Because," I said, and then realized I didn't have a good answer, and then realized that was the answer. "Because backward is where the grief lives. Forward is where the grief gets to become something else."

Ada considered this with the gravity of a supreme court. Then she said, "So the language is a boat," and went back to her slate, and I had to stand very still for a moment so none of the children would see their teacher come apart over a metaphor from a seven-year-old.

A boat. Yes. Fine. We'll put it in the primer.

* * *

After lessons I walked out to the ridge where the memorial foundation is staked. Two hundred and fourteen markers eventually—one for every soul I counted in that first census, the ones who didn't live to see the fleets turn away. Some died of the slow failures that had been killing Vesper Hollow all along: bad air recyclers, thin harvests, the quiet attrition of a place running out of everything except time. A few died of the panic year, the season when Ansel's secret curdled and half the colony learned at once that Earth had forgotten them. I don't visit those graves differently than the others. Dead is dead, and grief doesn't do accounting.

But there's one marker that isn't a grave. It's a slab of local basalt with two inscriptions, one in Standard, one in Ashkari, both saying the same thing:

*Here a lie was told to prevent a war. Here the truth was told to end one. May the speakers of both be forgiven.*

Sef-Ilan helped me cut the letters. She stayed through the summer, officially as cultural liaison, unofficially because she'd grown attached to the terrible tea Wren used to brew and couldn't quite abandon the recipe. Before she left she gave me something I keep on my desk now: a data crystal containing every known recording of native Ashkari speech—poems, lullabies, court proceedings, a merchant complaining about grain prices in 2143 with such vivid irritation that I laughed out loud the first time I played it. Two centuries of a dead language, resurrected by a woman who had also refused to die when everyone expected it.

"You understand now," she'd said, pressing it into my hand, "why I answered your broken distress call instead of reporting it."

"Because you were curious?"

"Because," she said, "a voice speaking badly in a dying language is still a voice. And I had been alone with the silence for a very long time."

We write to each other now, in the bridge-language, letters that take months to cross the dark between her world and mine. Her people are rebuilding—not the empire, nothing so grand, but the language, the songs, the habit of existing. Last month she sent me a recording of her niece singing a lullaby that had been silent for two hundred years, relearned from my archives, sung slightly wrong, beautifully wrong, the way living things are always slightly wrong.

I played it for the class. Ada asked if we could learn it. We're learning it.

* * *

Evening now. I'm sitting in the transmitter station, which is no longer a transmitter station—it's a schoolroom with a very impressive antenna bolted to the roof—and I'm doing the thing I have done every night for ten years: recording.

But the census is different now. Listen:

*"Day three hundred sixty-one of the second founding. Population forty-one, plus nine children who count double, though I haven't worked out the mathematics of that yet. Today Ada Solano invented a new word for the smell of rain on hot stone, because our borrowed lexicon didn't have one and she decided that was unacceptable. The word is 'sellen.' Six people used it before dinner. This is how languages are born: one stubborn child refusing to go unnamed.*

*"The harvest came in thin but sufficient. Teo has finished cataloguing the founder archives—all of them, the true ones, the forged ones, and the ones that are somehow both. He asked me today what we should call ourselves. Not colonists, not refugees, not descendants. Us.*

*"I told him I'd get back to him.*

*"I haven't gotten back to him. But tonight, writing this, I think I know. We are the people who stayed. We are the ones who looked at two fleets and a graveyard and a lie and a truth and chose, from among all of it, this valley, these children, this language that belongs to no one because it belongs to everyone who speaks it. Inheritance, it turns out, isn't what you're given. It's what you agree to carry.*

*"End of entry. The light found us first today. Tomorrow, if we're lucky, it will find us again."*

I stop the recorder. Outside, the valley is going gold and then blue, the way it does, indifferent and gorgeous. Somewhere down the slope, Ada is teaching her brother the rain-word. Somewhere across the dark, Sef-Ilan's niece is singing. Somewhere in the archives, two hundred and fourteen names wait patiently to mean something other than loss.

Nine years I recorded endings. Now I record beginnings, and I still can't quite believe the switch, still brace sometimes for the static to come back, for the loop to decay, for someone to tell me it was all a mistake and the forgetting has resumed.

But no one comes to tell me that. Instead, tomorrow morning, nine children will climb the hill to my schoolroom, and I will teach them the words for *welcome*, and *grief*, and *forward*, and *stay*, and they will teach me words I didn't know I needed, and the language will grow one stubborn syllable at a time, the way anything alive does.

I am the last speaker of nothing. I am the first speaker of something.

That's enough. That's finally, finally enough.
