## 1. The Frequency That Should Not Exist

The cargo hauler *Mule's Patience* ran dark and cold through the debris shoals above Earth, and Zephyrine Yarrow liked it that way. Cold meant the reactor idled low. Dark meant no transponder, no beacon, no name. He had spent fifteen years learning how to be a rumor: a hull number in a port ledger, a payment code, a silhouette on a long-range scope that no one bothered to identify. Invisibility was not a condition. It was a discipline, practiced daily, like prayer.

He was hauling seed stock, ironically enough—dormant grain hybrids bound for the Martian terraces—and he kept his hands busy checking the manifest for the fourth time when the signal came through.

It arrived the way ghosts arrive: not loudly, but with a familiarity that stopped the breath. A carrier wave buried under three layers of static, cycling on an abandoned emergency band, and modulated in Old Meridian.

Zephyrine's hands went still over the console.

Old Meridian was dead. Not endangered, not declining—dead, the way Latin was dead, the way his mother's voice was dead. It had been the administrative tongue of the Meridian Compact before the Fleets broke it apart, a language of seals and testimony and formal oaths, spoken now by no one. By no one except, as far as he knew, one other living person, and he had taken pains never to learn whether that person still breathed.

The signal repeated. He let it. He told himself he would let it run twice and then report it as interference and be done with it.

*Thal en vireth, ka solenne.* Hear me, who is bound by silence.

His mother's cadence. The exact liturgical inflection she'd used when she sealed the household at night, locking the words of their old life behind a door inside her mouth. Zephyrine had been nine years old the last time he heard Old Meridian spoken aloud, standing in the doorway of a rented room on Ceres while his mother knelt and recited the confession-formula to a man whose face he could not remember anymore, only the shape of his stillness.

The signal said, in that same tongue: *I am the keeper. The house is failing. Come home, Zephyrine Yarrow.*

Not Yarrow. His stomach dropped through the deck plating. Yarrow was the name he'd bought on Titan with forged papers and a dead man's identity—the name of a man who had never existed, which made him safe, which made him nothing. The vault knew the other name. The buried one. The one stitched into him before he could choose, before his mother chose for both of them.

He sat in the dim cockpit with the signal washing over him in its patient loops and felt two things at once, which was the oldest wound he carried: dread, and longing. Dread because the seed vault hung in high orbit below him—he could see it through the forward glass if he looked, a pale spindle wrapped in its shroud of solar sails, derelict these fifteen years, officially empty, officially decommissioned. Officially a tomb. Longing because the voice speaking Old Meridian sounded, in its machine way, like hers.

He did not look. Looking was a form of going, and going was the one thing he had sworn off.

* * *

He ran the signal's origin vector instead, telling himself this was prudence, not surrender. Triangulation took eleven minutes. The answer sat in his display like a stone in the chest: the Svalbard Orbital Archive. The seed vault. The place his mother had helped build before she defected, the place her testimony was supposedly sealed away in a format no living reader could open—which had been the point, which had been the whole architecture of the peace. Two fleets, the Concord and the Verity Compact, balanced on the knife-edge of a fabricated war record, each deterred from striking first because the archive held proof neither side wanted read aloud.

Fifteen years of quiet, built on a locked room and a language almost nobody spoke.

And now the room was calling out. In the language. By his true name.

Zephyrine pulled up the vault's public telemetry, such as it was—stale, decades-old survey data—and overlaid the signal strength against it. The numbers were wrong. The distress carrier was too clean for a derelict. Something down there had woken up, or something had never fully slept.

"You're not real," he said to the cockpit, to the signal, to the boy he had been. "You're a trap, or you're a ghost, and either way I'm not your business."

The signal looped again, patient as tide. *Come home.*

He thought about his mother then—not the martyr the fleets argued about, not the traitor or the peacemaker depending on which uniform you wore, but the woman who had taught him Old Meridian lullabies in a whisper because even lullabies were evidence. She had made him fluent in a dead language and then taught him that fluency was a death sentence, and then she had gone back toward the fleets and the war and the lie, and he had run the other direction so hard and so long that running became his profession. Hauling cargo past the vault on routes that skirted Earth's orbit was not coincidence; it was penance. He flew close to the thing he could not enter, again and again, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth.

The reactor alarm chimed softly—a routine pressure fluctuation, nothing—but his heart slammed anyway, and he understood, sitting there with his palms flat on the console, that he had always known this day would come. You do not bury a name. You just wait for someone to dig.

* * *

He spent six hours talking himself out of it, which was five hours and fifty minutes more than he needed.

The arguments were familiar; he'd rehearsed them for years. If he answered, he became visible. Visible meant auditors, tribunals, questions about the Yarrow identity, questions about his mother, questions with no survivable answers. If the signal was a fleet lure—and both fleets had reason to fish those waters with exactly this kind of bait—then answering was suicide with extra steps. And if it was genuine, if the vault's keeper AI truly was dying and calling the one person alive who could hear it, then answering meant inheriting whatever his mother had left locked in there, and he had spent half his life refusing the inheritance.

But the signal changed.

At 0300 ship-time, mid-loop, the modulation shifted, and the machine voice stopped reciting the standard distress formula and began, instead, a sequence he recognized with a physical jolt: the testimony-oath. The opening formula every Meridian witness swore before sealing a statement. *By the ground that fed me and the sky that judged me, I speak what is true.*

Then, appended, in the same archaic syllables, a line that had no business existing outside a sealed record:

*She left it for the one who carries her voice. Fifteen years I have held it. My core is failing. If no one comes, it burns with me, and the peace burns after.*

Zephyrine closed his eyes. Behind them: Ceres, the rented room, his mother kneeling, the oath in her mouth like a live coal passed from hand to hand. She had known. Of course she had known—that someday the lock would need a key, and the key would be a child raised in a dead language, hidden in plain sight among the haulers and dock rats of the Belt.

"She left it for me," he said aloud, and the words in Old Meridian tasted like smoke and milk, like childhood.

He looked at the navigation plot. The vault hung forty thousand kilometers below, a pale splinter against the blue limb of Earth. Both fleets' patrol corridors bracketed that orbit like jaws. Any burn toward it would paint him on a dozen scopes. Any approach was a declaration.

He did it anyway.

The *Mule's Patience* came alive around him—reactor climbing out of idle, thrusters waking, lights rising section by section as he brought his invisible ship back into the world of ships that existed. On the transponder console his finger hovered over the identification plate. Fifteen years of darkness, and the choice came down to whether he crossed that orbit as a ghost or as a name.

He entered the registration manually, letter by letter, and watched it glow on the display:

YARROW, Z. — INDEPENDENT — ORIGIN: UNLISTED.

"Coming home," he said, in Old Meridian, to no one, to everyone, to the machine that was dying below him and the woman who had died believing he would come.

The vault answered on the old band, one last line before the static swallowed it, and this time the voice did not sound like his mother at all. It sounded urgent, and young, and afraid:

*Hurry. They are coming—all of them are coming—and the fire is already inside.*
