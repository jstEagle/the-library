## 4. Seeds in Broken Ground

The vault burned for eleven hours.

Zephyrine watched it from the *Pale Meridian*'s observation blister, his cargo hauler dwarfed between the warships of two fleets that had spent fifteen years pretending the other did not exist. The station's hull glowed cherry-red along its spine, and every few minutes a section would vent in a silent bloom of frost and fire, seeds that had slept since before the wars boiling away into vacuum. Somewhere inside that light was his mother's voice. Somewhere inside it was the version of himself he had buried at nineteen, when he signed on to a freighter under a name that belonged to no one.

But the vault was not dying alone.

Across the orbit, ships from both fleets ran with their holds open. He could see them on the tactical overlay—Corvane hulls and Meridian hulls, matched gun-for-gun at a distance neither side dared close, while their shuttles crossed the gap like antibodies toward a wound. Every packet of data Zephyrine had broadcast in those final minutes had been caught, copied, argued over, archived. His mother's testimony. His confession appended beneath it in his own flat, unlovely voice. The seed catalog itself, terabytes of provenance and genome maps, scattered across forty-one vessels so thoroughly that no tribunal, no admiral, no act of arson could ever gather it all back into one place where it might be quietly destroyed.

He had learned that trick from her. Ilsa Venn had understood it too: a truth kept in one place is a hostage. A truth kept everywhere is a foundation.

* * *

The summons came on the third day, when the fires had guttered to embers and the vault was a cold sculpture of slag turning slowly above the Pacific.

*The Joint Tribunal of Corvane and Meridian requests the presence of the custodian.*

Not *the traitor*. Not *the defector's son*. Custodian. The word had traveled through both fleets faster than any order he'd ever seen move, and it arrived at his airlock wearing the neutral grammar of a document no single faction had dared amend.

They convened aboard the Corvane flagship *Ardent Measure*, in a hall designed for ceremonies of surrender. Zephyrine walked the length of it alone—no counsel, because no lawyer in either fleet would touch him, and no escort, because both fleets had independently decided that harming him now would look like destroying evidence. He felt their eyes the way he had once felt the weight of sealed orders: hundreds of people reading him, deciding what he was.

At the center of the hall stood a single table, and behind it, three chairs. Two were occupied by admirals—one from each fleet, their dress uniforms a study in opposed geometries. The third chair held Ilsa Venn, who had surrendered her auditor's commission two days ago in a letter that had reportedly caused several senior officers to require medical attention. She wore civilian gray now, and she looked at Zephyrine with an expression he recognized from mirrors: someone standing at the edge of a life they can't go back to.

"Zephyrine Yarrow," said the Corvane admiral, whose name was Ostrava and whose voice sounded like a hull under stress. "You are aware that your broadcast constituted—" She stopped. Consulted nothing. Started again. "You are aware of what you did."

"I am," he said. "I did it deliberately."

"You ended fifteen years of peace."

"No," Zephyrine said, and heard his mother's cadence come up through him like water finding an old channel. "I ended fifteen years of a lie holding the peace together. Those aren't the same thing. A peace built on my mother's forged record was a dam with her name carved into it. It was always going to break. The only question was whether anyone would be standing downstream with shovels when it did."

The Meridian admiral—a narrow, patient woman named Kestrel-Sao—leaned forward. "And you believe you are holding a shovel."

"I believe I'm holding the only complete account of how we got here," Zephyrine said. "Your archivists have fragments. My ship has the master sequence. Forty-one other vessels hold copies none of us can recall. Destroy me and you destroy a man, not a record. That was the point."

Silence, of the particular quality that precedes either verdict or negotiation. He had sat through enough fleet tribunals secondhand—in the stories his mother told badly, late, after drink—to know the difference.

It was Ilsa Venn who spoke next, and she did not address the admirals. She addressed the hall.

"The testimony is verified," she said. "I've spent nine days doing nothing else. Every claim in Halcyon Yarrow's account cross-checks against fleet records, against signal logs, against the vault's own immutable timestamps. The war that nearly happened was manufactured. The defector who prevented it never existed as described. And the peace we've lived inside since was purchased with a fiction that two governments agreed, separately and secretly, never to examine." She let that settle. "We are not here to judge Zephyrine Yarrow. We are here because he is the last person alive who understands the whole shape of what was done—and the first person willing to say it out loud."

* * *

They gave him a room aboard the *Ardent Measure* that night—not a cell, though it locked from the outside, and not quarters, though it had a bunk and a window. He slept four hours and woke before dawn-cycle to find Ilsa Venn waiting in the corridor, two cups of something hot and terrible from the officers' galley.

"They're going to offer you a title," she said. "Some committee. Custodian-General of the Reconciled Record, or some such. They'll want you to be official, which means they want you to be theirs."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then you'll be unofficial, which means you'll be everyone's." She handed him a cup. "I've read your mother's testimony eleven times now. You know what struck me most? Not the forgery. The footnote. Page ninety. Where she writes that someday someone would have to tell the truth, and that whoever did it should be someone with nothing left to lose." She looked at him over the rim of her cup. "She meant you. She engineered your exile so you'd be free to do exactly this. Did you know?"

"I suspected," Zephyrine said. "Knowing and suspecting turn out to be different countries. I've been living in the wrong one for fifteen years."

Below them, Earth turned, blue and indifferent, the burned vault a scar in high orbit catching the sunrise. Down there, in the broken ground of the old world, the seeds in the catalog still waited—the real ones, the genetic inheritance of a planet that had almost been spent as ammunition. Both fleets wanted them. Both fleets now knew the other wanted them. And for the first time in fifteen years, that knowledge was not a secret to be fought over but a fact to be negotiated around.

"They'll offer you the title tomorrow," Ilsa said. "What will you tell them?"

Zephyrine looked at his reflection in the dark glass—no longer the invisible man, no longer the buried son, something new and unarmored and afraid of it. He thought of his mother's voice in the vault, steady even as the reactor screamed. He thought of the seeds scattering across forty-one ships like dandelion spores on a wind no one controlled.

"I'll tell them the truth," he said. "I'm told it works."

* * *

They convened again at noon, and Admiral Ostrava rose, and offered him the title exactly as Ilsa had predicted, down to the word *custodian*. Zephyrine stood, and the whole hall leaned toward him like grass toward weather.

"I accept the work," he said, "but not the office. The record belongs to no fleet. It will be published entire—to both fleets, to the colonies, to anyone with an antenna and the patience to listen. I will sign my name to it. All of it. Including the parts that shame me." He paused, and found that his hands were steady. "My mother bought your peace with a lie. I intend to keep it with the truth. Whether you help me is no longer the interesting question."

Admiral Kestrel-Sao smiled—thinly, dangerously, the smile of a woman recalculating a war she had expected to fight. "And if we refuse?"

"Then refuse," Zephyrine said. "Broadcast anyway. I already gave the keys to forty-one ships." He let the silence hold for exactly as long as his mother would have. "Shall we begin?"

Outside the viewport, the sun cleared the curve of the Earth, and the ash of the vault turned briefly to gold, and somewhere below, in soil that had waited fifteen years for an honest season, the first of the seeds began, invisibly, to remember how to grow.
