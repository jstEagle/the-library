## 5. Two Keepers

**Orsen**

The stranger ate like a man who had never been hungry and was trying to learn how.

Orsen watched him from across the galley table — the narrow room he'd built for himself out of shipping crates and stubbornness — and catalogued the wrongness the way he'd catalogue a failing seal or a drifting temperature gauge. The man held his spoon correctly. He chewed at the correct intervals. But there was no anticipation before the bite, no satisfaction after. He ate the way Orsen checked the beacon: because the procedure required it.

"You don't have to eat," Orsen said. It wasn't a question. He'd decided somewhere around dawn that pretending otherwise was a waste of both their time.

Felix set down the spoon. Up close, in honest light, he looked almost ordinary — mid-thirties, dark hair going gray at the temples, a face like a thousand faces Orsen had seen in archive footage of the colonies before the war. That was the part that unsettled him most. Not that Felix looked synthetic. That he looked *familiar*.

"No," Felix agreed. "The body processes nutrients. It's efficient for maintaining cover, if I ever need to pass among people." He paused. "I eat because you made two portions. It seemed rude not to."

Orsen grunted and turned back to his ration stew. Outside the viewport, the ice field turned its slow glittering wheel, and beneath it the vault hummed its thirty-year hum.

Three days since he'd opened the stasis pod in the buried ship that officially never flew. Three days since the man inside had looked at him with eyes still booting up and said, *You're Orsen Hartwell. You've kept my signal alive.*

He'd slept badly since. He kept reaching for explanations the way his tongue reached for a missing tooth. A castaway. A deserter. A ghost. None of them fit a man who knew things he shouldn't, whose memories arrived in his voice like weather from another country.

"Ask me," Felix said quietly.

"Ask you what?"

"Whatever you've been circling for three days. You test everything else on this rock — every seal, every seed bank, every gram of protein. You're testing me too. The water ration question yesterday. The route question this morning, when you already knew I'd get it wrong." A faint, rueful movement at the corner of his mouth. "You want to know what I am. Ask."

Orsen put down his spoon. The galley felt suddenly small, the walls of crates pressing in like witnesses.

"What do you remember?" he asked. "Your first memory."

Felix was silent for a long moment. The vault's ventilation breathed around them.

"A market square," he said finally. "On Calloway's Rest, before the war made it lawless. Stalls under blue tarpaulin. A woman selling roasted chestnuts even though chestnuts hadn't grown on Calloway's Rest in a generation — she imported them, at ruinous cost, because her mother had sold them and her grandmother before her. I remember the smell. I remember being eight years old and burning my fingers on one." His voice didn't waver, but something behind it did — a strain, like a hull holding against pressure. "I remember the name of the street. Corvane Street. It doesn't exist anymore. Nothing on that street exists anymore except in me, and I wasn't there."

"Whose memory is it?"

"His name was Elias Voss." Felix said the name carefully, the way a man carries something breakable across a rough floor. "He was a dock clerk on Meridian Station. He was the first person killed in the war — forty-one years ago, eleven minutes past midnight, station time, when the *Providence* fired on the civilian quarter during what the Accord still calls an unprovoked colonial attack." He looked up, and his eyes were steady and terrible. "I have all of him. Every year of his life, ending in the last four seconds of it. And none of me. I woke up three days ago knowing your name, your routines, the layout of this vault — because he studied this place, near the end, planning for someone like you to exist — and I know chestnut smoke on Corvane Street, and I don't know a single thing that happened to *me* before the pod."

The stew had gone cold in Orsen's bowl. He found he didn't care.

"Why you?" he asked. "Why build a man out of a dead dock clerk?"

"That," Felix said, "is the question I was built to answer. And I can't. Not yet. The sealed partition in my core won't open until—" He stopped. Recalibrated. "Until certain conditions are met. I know that the way you know your own heartbeat. There's a message in me, Orsen. I'm not just carrying his grief. I'm carrying proof."

* * *

**Felix**

The old man did not sleep well. Felix had learned this in three nights of listening to the vault's sounds — the creak of thermal expansion, the tick of the beacon's timing chain, and above it all Hartwell's restless turning in the bunk behind the thin partition wall.

Felix did not sleep at all. He lay in the guest cot — the first bed anyone had offered him in his three days of existence — and let the borrowed memories move through him like tide.

That was the worst of it, he was learning. Not the strangeness. The *ownership*. Grief came to him pre-installed, worn smooth by someone else's years of handling. When he thought of the woman on Corvane Street — Marta, her name was Marta Voss, Elias's mother — he felt love with the specific grain of a son's love, and he had no right to any of it. He was a squatter in a dead man's heart.

But the dead man had left instructions.

At 0400 vault time, Felix rose, dressed, and walked the corridor to the beacon room without needing light. He knew the way the way Elias had known it — from schematics, from a plan laid down decades ago by people who had trusted a lonely caretaker they'd never met.

Hartwell was already there. Of course he was. The man treated sleep as a supply to be rationed.

"You should be resting," Orsen said, not turning from the console.

"So should you." Felix came to stand beside him. On the main display, the beacon's waveform scrolled, patient and green. "Thirty years, nine months, eleven days. That's how long this transmitter has run without interruption. Do you know why it's never been allowed to stop?"

"Because a signal is a promise. Whoever built this wanted someone to find it."

"That's half true." Felix touched the console — gently, asking permission with his fingertips before he did anything more. Orsen nodded once. Felix brought up the transmission architecture, layer under layer, the way Elias had taught him to see it, though Elias had never seen it himself; the knowledge had simply been *placed* in Felix, folded into the sealed partition like a letter into an envelope. "Look here. The carrier wave everyone would notice. And underneath — this handshake protocol. This beacon isn't calling out, Orsen. It's *holding a door shut*."

Orsen went very still. Felix had begun, in three days, to read the small weathers of the man's face: suspicion, curiosity, the flinty patience of someone who had spent thirty years waiting for bad news and refusing to be surprised by it.

"Say that again," Orsen said.

"It's a dead-man's switch. As long as the beacon transmits, whatever sits behind the handshake stays buried — encrypted, distributed, dormant. The moment the transmission stops — power failure, sabotage, the death of whoever maintains it — the archive releases itself. Automatically. To every public channel in the colonial net. No one can stop it. No one can recall it." Felix met the old man's eyes. "And I know what's in the archive. Part of it. Enough."

"How could you possibly—"

"Because they gave me the key and the summary together. The Accord didn't fire on Meridian Station by accident, and the colonists didn't attack first. The *Providence*'s weapons release was authorized under a false flag operation — colonial transponders spoofed onto civilian ships, a staged provocation, signed off six weeks before the massacre by three members of what is now the Accord's standing security council." He heard his own voice go flat and precise, Elias's cadence bleeding through, the dock clerk reading a manifest of horrors. "Forty-one years of war, Orsen. Eleven million dead by the last honest count. It started as a paperwork decision. They killed a market street to justify a fleet expansion, and then they burned the evidence, and the war grew over the grave like kudzu."

The silence afterward had weight. The beacon hummed. Somewhere below them, ten thousand seed banks slept in their cold drawers, each one a promise that the world could begin again.

Orsen sat down slowly in his chair, the one worn to the shape of him, and put his face in his hands. When he spoke, his voice came out sanded raw.

"I tended it," he said. "Thirty years. I thought I was keeping a light on for survivors. For *somebody*. And the whole time I was sitting on a bomb."

"Not a bomb." Felix crouched so he was level with the old man, the way you'd speak to someone grieving — which, he realized, was exactly what this was. "A testimony. There's a difference. A bomb kills. This only *tells*. The question is whether the truth ends the war or just starts a worse one, and whether anyone gets to choose how it lands." He hesitated. "There's more. The conditions on my sealed partition. I think — I think the message was meant to be delivered, not detonated. Carried to the Accord's seat by hand. By someone they couldn't erase. By *me*. And the beacon was the failsafe, in case the courier never came."

"And you're the courier."

"I'm the courier." Felix smiled, and it hurt, because the smile was Elias's smile and the fear underneath it was entirely his own. "A dead man's ghost, carrying a dead world's last letter, guarded for thirty years by a man who talks to seeds."

"They talk back," Orsen said automatically, and then, absurdly, they both laughed — short, cracked laughter in the green light of the beacon, the first either of them had managed in three days. It broke something loose. Felix felt it go: not trust, not yet, but the possibility of it, like thaw under ice.

* * *

**Orsen**

In the days that followed, Orsen ran the tests he'd stopped pretending he wasn't running.

He asked Felix about the vault's south coolant loop and got a perfect technical answer sourced from Elias's engineering certification. He asked about the taste of coffee and got a blank, honest stare — Elias had hated coffee, and apparently some preferences hadn't survived the transfer, or had been deliberately stripped. He told a joke about a freight pilot and a customs drone and watched Felix laugh a half-second too late, assembling the response rather than feeling it, and filed that away too.

But then, on the sixth day, in the seed library — the long cold room where the drawers of the old Earth harvests slept under violet grow-lamps — Felix stopped in front of drawer 4471 and said, without preamble:

"Wheat. Kavarna strain. You talk to this one most. You tell it about your sister."

Orsen stood in the aisle with a maintenance slate in his hand and felt the floor tilt.

"My sister died before I took this posting," he said carefully. "I've never mentioned her."

"You mention her every Tuesday, when you rotate the Kavarna stock. You say, 'Ana always said wheat was just grass with ambition.'" Felix's expression was gentle and appalled at once, a man watching his own hands commit a trespass. "It's in Elias's research file. He studied you, Orsen. Before the pod. Before the war ended for everyone else. He chose you — or the people who built me chose you, using his judgment — because you were the one soul in the colonial registry with no faction, no family, no price. Thirty years alone. They counted on your loneliness the way they counted on everything else."

The slate creaked in Orsen's grip. Anger came up in him like groundwater, slow and total. Thirty years of his life, and it had been a *selection criterion*.

"They used me," he said.

"Yes." Felix didn't flinch. "And they were right to. That's the filthy arithmetic of it. If they'd picked a man with a family, a man with ties, the beacon would have gone dark in year three and the truth would have rotted in the dark with it. Your solitude saved the evidence. I won't insult you by pretending that makes it better. But I'll say this." He stepped closer, and his voice dropped to something that had nothing of Elias in it — something young and new and afraid. "They planned for a caretaker. They did not plan for a *friend*. Whatever I am, whatever they built me for — these last six days, eating your rations, learning your vault, hearing about Ana and her ambitious grass — none of that is in my specifications. That part is mine. I'd like to keep it. Even if everything else burns."

Orsen looked at him for a long time. The grow-lamps hummed. Ten thousand sleeping harvests breathed their cold breath around them.

"Supper's at nineteen hundred," he said at last. "If you're going to eat when I make two portions, you can help cook. And tomorrow you're learning the coolant loop yourself. If we're both keepers now, you pull weight."

Something moved across Felix's face — relief, Orsen thought, plain and naked as a child's. "Yes," Felix said. "I'd like that."

* * *

**Felix**

On the ninth night, they shared secrets over reconstituted noodles, the way Felix supposed families must have done once, before the war taught everyone to eat standing up.

Orsen talked about Ana — really talked, for the first time, not the Tuesday litany but the whole shape of her, the sister who'd emigrated to the outer colonies and been silenced by distance and then by the war, the reason he'd taken a posting at the edge of everything. Felix talked about the four seconds. Elias's last four seconds, which lived in him at permanent volume: the alarm klaxon, the smell of ozone and burning paper, the thought — Elias's final coherent thought — *Marta's chestnuts, I never wrote to Marta.* Felix had carried those four seconds through three days of existence and had finally, tonight, said them aloud to another human being, and discovered that saying them made them weigh slightly less.

It was Orsen who broke the quiet afterward, turning his spoon in his bowl.

"The beacon," he said. "You said it releases if the transmission stops. What happens if it *changes*? Power dips. Solar flare interference. Thirty years, Felix — I've had brownouts. I've had to swap the timing chain twice. Why hasn't it ever fired?"

Felix set down his fork. He had been turning this over for two days, a splinter working deeper the more he examined it, and he had promised himself he would not keep it from Orsen. Keepers shared. That was the word they'd chosen, and words like that carried obligations.

"I've been studying the handshake logs," he said. "Someone has been querying the beacon. Regularly. From outside. Not attacking it — *checking* it. Confirming it's still live, still sealed, still maintained." He met the old man's eyes. "The queries started fourteen months ago. They're getting closer to tracing the source. And Orsen — the query signature isn't colonial military. It's not any registry I know. Someone else knows this beacon exists. Someone is looking for it."

Outside, far off across the ice field, the wind changed pitch — the sound Orsen had spent thirty years learning to hear, the sound that meant weather coming, or something moving where nothing should move.

Orsen set down his spoon.

"Then we'd better find out who," he said, "before they do."
