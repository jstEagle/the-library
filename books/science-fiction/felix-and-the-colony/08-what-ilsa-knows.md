## 8. What Ilsa Knows

The Kestrel's galley smelled like recycled air and burnt coffee, and Ilsa Reyes sat in it the way she sat everywhere — like a woman keeping watch over her own body from somewhere slightly behind her eyes.

Dov had gone to sleep in the engineering alcove. Sefa was running inventory with the methodical patience of someone who'd once counted bandages in a refugee camp and never stopped counting anything since. Ilsa had the ship to herself for four hours, and she spent them the way she always did on nights before something broke open: reading.

Not contracts. Not salvage manifests. Letters.

They lived in a sealed partition of her personal slate, encrypted twice, backed up in three places, and never — not once in eleven years — shown to another living soul. Forty-one documents. Her brother's handwriting rendered in cold digital ink, because the paper originals had burned with everything else on Ceres Station when she was fourteen.

*Dear Ilsa,* the first one began. *By the time you read this I'll be famous or dead, and knowing our luck, probably neither.*

Tomas had written like he talked: fast, funny, convinced the universe owed him a punchline and determined to collect. He'd been nineteen when he signed onto the Meridian's crew — a colony ship, a fresh start, a paycheck that would finally get their mother off the station and somewhere with real gravity and real soil. Ilsa remembered him practicing his acceptance speech in the galley mirror of their cramped quarters, grinning at himself, at her, at the whole rotten world.

*I'm not supposed to tell you where we're going,* letter three said. *Which is funny, because I'm not entirely sure anyone's supposed to know. The manifest lists us as a training cruise. Training for what, Tom? you ask. Good question. Ask the men in gray who come aboard every few weeks and don't log in.*

She'd been too young, then, to understand what she was reading. She'd thought it was adventure. Her brother, going somewhere secret and important, sending letters home through channels so convoluted they took months to arrive — routed through three relay stations and a cousin of a cousin on Ganymede, because official mail, he wrote, "has a way of getting read by people whose job titles are classified."

The last letter was dated eleven days before the Meridian ceased to exist.

*Ilsa. Something's wrong here. They moved up the timeline. Captain's furious — says we're not ready, says the cargo isn't what we were told. There are passengers aboard who aren't on any list I can find. One of the techs swears Deck Four doesn't exist on the schematics but she's walked it. If anything happens —*

It ended there. No signature flourish, no joke. Just *If anything happens —* and then nothing, forever, because three days later the Colonial Accord announced that the civilian transport Meridian had suffered a reactor failure during an unregistered deep-space exercise, all hands lost, condolences to the families, please direct inquiries to the Office of Public Information.

Except there were no families to console. That was the part Ilsa hadn't understood until years later, until she was old enough and angry enough to dig. Every crew member of the Meridian had been scrubbed. Not killed — erased. Service records dissolved. Birth registrations flagged and folded into closed archives. Their mother had gone to the records office on Ceres with Tomas's birth certificate in her hand, and the clerk had looked at it, looked at her, and said, with genuine bureaucratic gentleness, *I'm sorry, ma'am. There's no such person.*

Her mother died two years after that, of the kind of slow collapse that grief builds in the body when it has nowhere else to go. Ilsa had stood at the recycler with the birth certificate burning in the incinerator slot and made herself a promise with the specific, load-bearing weight of a structural beam: *Someone erased my brother. I will find out who, and why, and I will make it cost them.*

That promise had built her life. It had taught her to fly, to fight, to read a room, to lie beautifully. It had put her in command of a salvage ship crewed by other people the war had chewed up and spat out — Dov, whose station settlement had been "relocated" by Accord order and never re-established; Sefa, who'd learned medicine on bodies the news feeds said didn't exist. The Kestrel wasn't just a livelihood. It was a search pattern. Eleven years of chasing anomalies across the outer colonies, following the faint magnetic scars of things the Accord wanted forgotten.

And now the trail had led here. To a seed vault on a frozen rock, tended by one old man, broadcasting a signal that no beacon should be able to broadcast — because buried inside its carrier wave, wrapped in forty-year-old encryption protocols that only a government archive would still recognize, was a data structure that made Dov's instruments sing like struck glass.

*Structured payload,* Dov had said, eyes wide over his console. *Cap, this isn't telemetry. This is a message. A big one. Somebody built this signal to carry something, and whatever it is, it's been sitting under our feet the whole war.*

Under her feet. Under her brother's grave.

Ilsa set down the slate and pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes until she saw constellations that weren't there.

* * *

The buyer's name was Aldous Wren. At least, that was the name on the contract.

She'd met him eight months ago, in a private lounge above a shipping exchange on Tycho, the kind of place where the gravity was tuned to feel expensive and the drinks came without prices. Wren was silver-templed and soft-spoken, with the unhurried manner of a man who had never once needed to raise his voice because institutions raised it for him. He'd offered her a contract with numbers that made her breath catch: track a signal anomaly originating from an uncharted seed vault in the Halvard system. Confirm its source. Retrieve any physical assets associated with it.

"Salvage," she'd said. "You want me to salvage a weather station."

"I want you to confirm whether a piece of obsolete equipment is still functioning." Wren had smiled the way ice forms — slowly, and all at once. "If it is, there may be a bonus involved. For discretion."

She should have walked away then. She told herself later that she should have walked away then. But the numbers had been enormous, and the signal coordinates had made her heart stop — because she'd seen that region of space before, in a declassified fragment of a declassified fragment, a shipping corridor adjacent to the last known position of a ship called Meridian.

So she'd taken the contract, and she'd done what she always did with employers: she'd investigated them harder than they investigated her.

Wren's identity was flawless. That was the first tell. Flawless identities cost more than flawed ones, and only one kind of organization could afford perfection. She'd traced his payment routing through six shells and found the seventh shell was funded out of a discretionary line item in the Colonial Accord's Office of Strategic Continuity — a budget designation that appeared nowhere in public documents and whose name, when she'd finally pried it out of a drunk logistics officer on Calloway's Rest, made the man go pale and change the subject.

Strategic Continuity. The euphemism was almost beautiful. The office that kept the story straight. The office that kept the dead dead and the records blank.

She'd known, for months now, that she was working for the people who had erased her brother. She'd told herself it was cover — get close, learn what they knew, turn it against them. And maybe that was even true. But she'd also known, in the honest hour of the night, that she'd have taken the contract from the devil himself if the coordinates were right.

Because the coordinates were right. The signal was coming from the last place Tomas had been alive.

* * *

Now she sat in the dark of the Kestrel's galley with her brother's letters glowing on her slate, and she turned the new pieces over in her mind like stones pulled from a riverbed, still wet, still warm from somewhere deep.

The old man. Hartwell. Thirty years alone in a vault full of seeds nobody had come to collect, tending a beacon nobody had asked about. She'd watched him through the observation feed during their approach — watched the careful choreography of his routines, the way he checked the antenna array twice, the way he paused at the greenhouse dome each evening like a man saying goodnight to someone.

She'd played the naive caretaker game with him for six hours today, and he'd played it well. Better than well — he'd played it with the fluency of long practice, thirty years of being the harmless old keeper, the footnote, the man the galaxy forgot. His hands had shaken when the Kestrel landed, but his voice hadn't. He'd given her the tour: seed banks, hydroponics, the beacon tower standing against the violet sky like a needle sewn through the horizon. He'd answered every question with exactly the right amount of detail.

That was how she'd known. Innocent men under-answered. Guilty men over-explained. But men hiding something precious did something else entirely — they answered correctly, uniformly, like students reciting a lesson they'd rehearsed until it wore smooth. Orsen Hartwell hadn't been lying to her. He'd been performing a script he'd performed so long it had become his face.

And underneath the script, in the spaces between his answers, she'd caught the flickers. The half-second delay before he mentioned the lower levels. The way his eyes had gone, once, involuntarily, toward the eastern ridge — toward the crater country, where Dov's passive array had picked up a metallic return too large for any piece of vault equipment. The way he'd said the word *alone* — *"it's just been me alone out here"* — with a small hesitation before it, like a man stepping over a tripwire he'd laid himself.

There was someone else on this rock. Or something.

And the signal — the signal that Wren wanted confirmed, the signal wrapped in Accord-grade encryption older than the war — was coming from the old man's beacon. Which meant either Orsen Hartwell was an Accord asset running a thirty-year deep-cover operation, which the evidence argued against, because Accord assets didn't live alone on ice eating ration-grown greens and talking to seed banks —

Or he was a keeper. A warden. A man entrusted with something so dangerous that the Accord had left him undisturbed for three decades, hoping the ice and the silence would bury it deeper than any archive could.

Something like a ship that officially never flew. Something like a crew that officially never existed. Something like a passenger manifest with names scrubbed so thoroughly that even the erasure had been erased.

Her brother was on that ship. She felt it the way you feel a storm front in your teeth — not knowledge, but certainty, arriving ahead of any proof. Tomas had written about unlisted passengers, about a deck that didn't exist on the schematics. And now a signal carrying government-grade secrets pulsed out of the ground near the Meridian's last coordinates, guarded by a man who flinched at nothing except questions about his lower levels.

Orsen Hartwell was sitting on the truth of her brother's fate. Maybe literally — maybe the answers were in his archives, in his beacon logs, in whatever slept beneath the eastern ridge. Thirty years of truth, and he'd spent them watering lettuce and mending antennas while the families of the erased — the ones who remembered, the ones like her mother who'd died being told their children had never existed — went on grieving a hole shaped like a person.

The anger came up through her like pressure through a drill casing. She let it. She'd learned long ago that her anger was the truest instrument she owned — more reliable than Dov's sensors, sharper than Sefa's diagnostics. Grief was fuel that burned out. Anger refined itself. Anger, properly contained, could run a ship for years.

But contained. Always contained. Because the moment she walked into that vault tomorrow and let the old man see what was in her eyes, the performance would end — his and hers both — and whatever came next would be negotiation or violence, and she'd spent eleven years making sure that when that moment came, she'd be the one holding the leverage.

She opened the contract file. Wren's terms scrolled past: confirm the signal, retrieve assets, *discretion*. And beneath the terms, in the boilerplate she'd read a hundred times looking for traps, the clause she'd memorized on the first night:

*In the event the Contractor determines the Asset to be compromised, Contractor is authorized to take all necessary measures to preserve operational security, including termination of local personnel.*

Local personnel. One old man, alone for thirty years, who watered his plants and said goodnight to his greenhouse and had never hurt a living soul.

Wren didn't want the beacon confirmed. Wren wanted to know if the grave was still sealed — and if it wasn't, he wanted it resealed, permanently, with the caretaker inside it.

Ilsa closed the file. In the viewport above the galley table, the vault's lights glimmered on the night side of the planet, tiny and stubborn, a candle someone had refused to let go out. Somewhere down there, under the ice, her brother's ship waited with its secret deck and its unlisted passengers and its eleven-day-old final letter.

Tomorrow she would land again. Tomorrow she would smile at the old man and ask her careful questions, and he would give her his smooth rehearsed answers, and two liars would circle each other in a building full of sleeping seeds.

But tomorrow she would also do something new. Tomorrow she would stop pretending she didn't know what the beacon was carrying — because a man who'd kept a secret for thirty years deserved to hear it spoken aloud by someone who understood what the secret cost. And she needed him to understand something else, too: that she wasn't Wren's knife. That whatever deal they struck, it would be struck between the two people the secret actually belonged to — the keeper and the sister — and not the office that had made them both orphans of the same lie.

She touched the slate's edge, dimming her brother's last unfinished sentence.

*If anything happens—*

Something had happened, Tomas. It took forty years, but I'm here now, and I'm not leaving without the rest of the sentence.

On the flight deck, the approach alarm chimed softly: scheduled landing window, T-minus nine hours. Ilsa rose, rolled her shoulders, and went to wake her crew.

They had a vault to crack, an old man to convince, and a buyer to betray — in that order, if she was lucky, and in no order at all if she wasn't.
