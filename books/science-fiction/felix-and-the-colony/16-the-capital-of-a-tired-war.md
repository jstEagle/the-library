## 16. The Capital of a Tired War

The drive died three hours out from Meridian Station, and after that the *Kestrel* simply fell — a slow, graceful fall, the way a stone falls into deep water. Dov spent those hours in the engine room swearing in two languages while Sefa ran coolant through systems that had no business still being warm. Ilsa stood at the helm with her arms crossed, watching the capital grow in the viewport like a bruise spreading across the dark.

Orsen watched it too.

He had never seen a city from orbit. He had never seen a city at all, not really — the vault's archives held images, of course, decades-old survey footage and propaganda reels, but an image of a city was like an image of rain. It told you nothing about what it did to you when it landed on your skin.

Corvane spread below them across the northern hemisphere of Accord Prime: a continent of light. Not the clean, orderly grids of the archive footage — those were forty years out of date — but something organic and scarred, districts grown over other districts, light bleeding into light, and threaded through it all, unmistakable even from this height, the long dark seams where whole neighborhoods had been demolished for war industry and never rebuilt. The city looked like a body that had learned to keep working around its wounds.

"Vashti's flag is up," Ilsa said. "Diplomatic courier, priority channel. They're giving us a lane."

"They're giving us a cage," Dov called from the corridor, wiping his hands on a rag that had given up being gray sometime last year. "A diplomatic lane is just a corridor they can close at both ends."

"It's also the only way in," Ilsa said. "So we take the cage."

Orsen said nothing. He had learned, over the weeks aboard the *Kestrel*, when his silence was worth more than his words. But he felt Felix at his shoulder — not physically; Felix stood by the aft viewport, perfectly still, the way he always did when he was thinking hard enough that motion seemed wasteful — and he felt the weight of what all of them were carrying. The message. The truth. Forty years of it, folded small inside one synthetic mind.

*We are bringing a match to a house made of paper,* Orsen thought, and could not decide whether he meant Corvane or the colonies entire.

* * *

They came down through cloud the color of old pewter, and Orsen pressed his forehead to the viewport like a child because there was no one aboard left to judge him for it.

He had expected grandeur. The Accord's seat of power — every broadcast from the vault's intercepted feeds had shown him spires and banners, the great Assembly Hall with its ring of forty pillars, one for each original colony. What he saw instead, as the *Kestrel* limped down the designated approach vector, was a city holding itself together with visible effort.

The spaceport sprawled beneath them, and beyond it the transit yards, and beyond those — this was the thing that undid him — the lines.

Ration lines. He knew them instantly, though he'd never seen one, because they looked exactly like what they were: queues of people arranged with the weary geometry of habit, snaking through covered walkways and around barricades stamped with the Accord's sunburst seal. Whole city blocks organized around waiting. He thought of his own stores in the vault — the careful shelves, the rotation schedule he'd kept for thirty years, the way he'd rationed protein packs down to the gram and called it discipline. Here, an entire civilization had been doing the same arithmetic for four decades, except their ledger was millions of lives, and there was no resupply ship coming.

"There," Sefa said quietly, pointing as they descended past a plaza. "Look."

A memorial wall. Orsen had read about them — every colony world maintained one, the feeds said, though the vault's receivers had long since stopped distinguishing news from noise. This one stretched the length of the plaza and then some, a sweep of dark stone carved with names, and before it, in the gray morning light, people. Hundreds of them. Some standing, some kneeling, some simply sitting on the cold ground as if they had run out of anything else to do. A woman in a transit worker's coat pressed her hand flat against a name. A boy who couldn't have been more than ten stood at attention before a section of the wall with the rigid posture of someone who had been taught it, drilled in it, raised inside it.

*A generation raised on grief,* Orsen thought. *Raised on it the way I was raised on silence.*

He had spent thirty years believing his solitude was the loneliest thing in the colonies. Looking down at that wall, he understood, with a vertigo that reached all the way into his chest, that he had been alone the way a lighthouse is alone — and that these people had been alone the way a drowning fleet is alone, each of them isolated inside a grief shared by everyone around them, which is its own particular kind of isolation, the kind that has nowhere to go.

Felix appeared beside him at the viewport. Orsen hadn't heard him move. He rarely did.

"The wall has more names than the official casualty figures," Felix said softly. "By roughly eleven percent. The difference is the erased — people removed from records the way my template was." His voice was level, but Orsen had learned to hear the strain underneath it, the sound of a consciousness carrying memories that were not his own and grieving them as if they were. "I know some of these names, Orsen. I remember dying under some of them."

Orsen turned to look at him. Felix's borrowed face — young, unremarkable, the face of someone designed to pass unnoticed — reflected faintly in the glass over the shrinking city.

"When we land," Orsen said, "people will look at you. They'll see a passenger. Just a man."

"For now." Felix's eyes stayed on the memorial wall until it slid out of view. "Vashti says the arrest order hasn't gone public. Says we have maybe a day before it does, maybe less. After that, every camera in Corvane will be looking for me." A pause. "It's strange. I spent forty years in a stasis pod wanting to be seen. Now I'm counting the hours before I'm invisible again."

* * *

The landing was rougher than any of them pretended to be calm about. The *Kestrel*'s maneuvering thrusters coughed twice on final approach, and Ilsa brought her down on a diplomatic pad near the Assembly quarter with a controlled drop that rattled Orsen's teeth and drew a sound from Dov that was either a prayer or a curse.

When the ramp lowered, Corvane's air hit Orsen like a physical thing.

It wasn't bad air, exactly. It was just *full* — full of exhaust and cooking food and wet stone and ten million lives compressed into a space his lungs had no framework for processing. After thirty years of filtered vault air, scrubbed and silent and utterly empty, the sheer density of it made him dizzy. He gripped the ramp rail and breathed and tried to look like a man who belonged on a diplomatic courier instead of a man who talked to seed banks.

Vashti Okonkwo waited at the foot of the ramp.

She looked worse than she had in the transmission — thinner, grayer, wearing civilian clothes that fit her like a disguise rather than a choice. Her eyes swept the boarding party once, cataloguing: Ilsa, armed and watchful. Dov, already scanning the pad perimeter. Sefa with a medical kit slung like a sidearm. Orsen, an old man in a patched coat who had walked out of another century. And Felix, standing slightly behind him, deliberately unremarkable.

"You made good time," Vashti said. "For a dead ship."

"We made *some* time," Ilsa corrected. "Where's safe?"

"Nowhere is safe. There's a place that's useful." Vashti started walking, and they fell in around her, a loose formation none of them had discussed. "Safehouse in the Tenth District. Council security is stretched thin — there were riots in the shipping wards last week over grain allotments. That buys us movement, but it also means the streets are volatile."

"Riots," Orsen repeated. The word felt foreign in his mouth.

Vashti glanced back at him, and something in her expression shifted — the intelligence officer's mask slipping just far enough to show the tired woman underneath. "You've been off-world thirty years, Mr. Hartwell. You should see what you're walking into before you decide to walk into it. Take the long route with me. All of you."

* * *

They took the long route.

Vashti led them through the transit tunnels first — vast, echoing corridors built for maglev trains that ran at half capacity now, their tracks prioritized for freight. The few civilians in the tunnels moved with their heads down, and Orsen noticed that nearly all of them carried bags, bundles, containers. Everyone was carrying something. Everyone was keeping their possessions close, as if the city itself might take them.

Then up, into the streets, and Orsen stopped understanding the city and started simply surviving it.

The Tenth District was a canyon of habitation blocks, most of them older than the war, their facades patched with solar film and structural bracing and, in one case, what looked like the hull plating of a decommissioned patrol craft bolted over a collapsed section like a bandage. Between the blocks, the ration lines he'd seen from orbit resolved into human scale: families with children too quiet for their age, elderly people folding and unfolding paper slips that recorded their allotments, a man arguing with a distribution clerk in a voice worn smooth from repetition rather than anger.

Nobody shouted. That was the thing that frightened Orsen most. In the archive footage, unrest always looked like fire and broken glass. Here it looked like endurance — a whole district of people who had passed through anger years or decades ago and come out the other side into something quieter and much more dangerous.

"They're not angry anymore," Orsen said. He hadn't meant to say it aloud.

"No," Vashti agreed. "Angry would be easier. Angry means they still think someone might listen." She nodded toward a corner where a municipal screen cycled through the day's announcements: grain allotment reductions, a call for volunteer labor in the orbital yards, and then — Orsen's breath caught — the faces of the war dead, a rolling memorial feed, dozens of names per minute, running continuously, endlessly, like a heartbeat the city couldn't stop. "That feed has run without interruption for thirty-one years. Every name, on a loop. It takes nine days to cycle completely." She said it flatly, but her jaw was tight. "I grew up under it. Everyone my age did. We learned our letters off the names of the dead."

Sefa made a low sound. Dov had stopped walking entirely, staring up at the screen, and Orsen realized with a jolt that the engineer — who joked in two languages and feared nothing mechanical — had gone pale.

"My parents' names are on that loop," Dov said finally. "Colony worlds' casualty lists get fed into the central archive. I used to watch for them when I was a kid. Set alarms so I wouldn't miss their minute." He shook himself, started walking again, faster than before. "Let's keep moving."

No one spoke for three blocks.

* * *

They crossed a plaza where the memorial wall began, and here Vashti stopped, whether by design or exhaustion Orsen couldn't tell.

Up close, the wall was worse than it had been from the air. The stone was dark and dense, and the names cut into it were small — deliberately small, so that the wall could hold them all, so that reading any single name required stepping close, touching the surface, getting personal with the dead. Flowers sat in niches. Photographs, laminated against the weather, showed faces: young faces, mostly. Faces of people who had died before Orsen had ever heard the beacon's signal fail to answer anyone.

An old woman was cleaning a section of the wall with a cloth, working methodically, name by name. She had probably been doing it for years. She would probably do it for the rest of her life.

Orsen stood before the wall and felt the full weight of what rode in Felix's memory settle onto his shoulders.

This was what the message was *for*. Not for the council behind their closed doors, not for the tribunals that might or might not come. For this. For the woman with the cloth. For Dov's alarm clock set to his parents' minute. For the boy standing at attention. For nine days of names on a loop, for ration lines measured in generations, for a war sustained not by hatred — he could see that now, see it everywhere in the tired faces around him — but by grief, which is far more durable and far less honest.

And the message said: *it didn't have to be this way. It was chosen. Someone chose this for you.*

Orsen thought of the vault — his beautiful, silent vault, where grief had been a single room he lived in alone. Here, grief was the architecture. It was the walls and the streets and the screens and the schools. You could not walk anywhere in Corvane without walking through it.

He felt small. Utterly, terrifyingly small — one old man with dirt under his fingernails and thirty years of talking to seeds, standing in front of a monument to millions. What did he know of this? What right did he have?

But underneath the smallness, steady as the beacon's pulse, was the other thing. The necessary thing. Because the message was real, and he was one of only a handful of living people who knew it, and the woman with the cloth deserved to know why she cleaned. The boy at attention deserved to know what he was standing guard over. Dov deserved his parents' true story.

Small and necessary. Both at once. He supposed that was what it meant to carry something.

Felix stood beside him at the wall. He had found a name — Orsen saw him find it, saw him stop — and Felix laid his palm against the stone the way the woman with the cloth did, gently, as if the stone were skin.

"This one," Felix said quietly. "Elin Marchetti. She was nineteen. She was the first to run toward the breach instead of away from it." His voice caught — actually caught, a synthetic throat producing a very human hitch. "Her name isn't in my inherited memories. I checked. She's just... a name on a wall to me. And I needed her to be more than that, so I—" He stopped. Started again. "I don't know how to hold all of this, Orsen. Millions of names. How does anyone?"

"They don't," Orsen said. "Not alone."

Felix looked at him then, and something passed between them that neither of them had words for — the caretaker and the kept, the keeper and the kept, the borders between those words worn thin over the long voyage.

"That's the message," Orsen went on, surprising himself with the clarity of it. "That's what you're carrying, isn't it? Not just proof. The victims' own plea. Truth over vengeance. Because vengeance makes more walls, and truth—" He gestured at the plaza, the lines, the loop of names cycling overhead. "Truth is the only thing that lets any of this stop."

"Then we'd better deliver it," Felix said.

* * *

The safehouse was a former consulate apartment in the Tenth District, high enough to see the Assembly Hall's ring of pillars lit against the evening sky. Vashti secured the door, swept the rooms for listening devices with a device of her own, and only then let her shoulders drop — a centimeter, no more.

"Tomorrow," she said, "I take the evidence to the two council members I still trust. If they'll move, we get an emergency session within seventy-two hours. If they won't—"

"If they won't?" Ilsa asked.

Vashti's silence answered for her.

Orsen stood at the window, looking out at the city as night settled over it. The ration lines had dispersed. The memorial plaza glowed softly under its lights, and even from here he could see figures moving along the wall — night visitors, the ones who came when grief was too heavy to wait for daylight. Above it all, the memorial feed cycled on, names pouring down the sides of buildings like rain.

Nine days for a complete cycle. Thirty-one years running. And somewhere in that endless river of names, Elin Marchetti flowed past every nine days, nineteen years old forever, running toward the breach.

Orsen pressed his hand briefly against the cold window glass, the way Felix had pressed his against the wall.

Then, behind him, Vashti's comm unit chimed — an encrypted priority tone, and by the way her face changed as she read it, by the way her hand slowly lowered the device, Orsen knew before she spoke that whatever margin they'd been counting on had just closed.

"They've moved the session forward," Vashti said. "Closed hearing. Tomorrow morning." She looked up, and her eyes found Felix in the dim room. "And there's an arrest warrant attached. Unregistered synthetic property. They're not waiting anymore."
