## 17. Closed Doors

The hearing chamber smelled like old money and older lies.

Ilsa had expected marble. She'd expected the sweeping colonnades and the holographic seal of the Colonial Accord rotating above the dais — the imagery every schoolchild in the colonies memorized before they learned to read. What she hadn't expected was the smell: cedar polish, recycled air scrubbed of everything human, and beneath it, faintly, the ozone tang of too many machines running too many quiet errands for too many powerful people.

Vashti Okonkwo walked three steps ahead of her, diplomatic credentials flashing green at every checkpoint, her stride carrying the particular confidence of a woman who had spent thirty years inside this building and knew exactly which doors would open and which would pretend to be locked. She wore civilian gray today instead of intelligence black. A concession, Ilsa supposed. A costume change for the role of defector.

"They'll stall," Vashti said without turning around. "Understand that going in. The council has one move left, and it's time. They will spend as much of yours as they can."

"I don't have much."

"None of us do anymore." She stopped before the tall double doors of Chamber Nine and turned. Up close, her face showed the wear the corridors didn't — fine lines carved deep around her mouth, eyes that had seen too many classified briefings to still flinch but hadn't yet learned to look away. "When they ask you how you came into possession of the synthetic, tell them the truth. All of it. The salvage contract, the buyer's signal, the ambush at the relay. Truth is the only weapon in that room they haven't already confiscated."

"And when they arrest us anyway?"

Vashti's jaw tightened. "Then you'll be arrested in front of the press gallery, with the evidence already entered into the record. There are worse places to be a prisoner." She pushed the doors open. "There are also better ones. Go."

* * *

Chamber Nine was a half-circle of tiered seating rising toward a curved wall of windows that looked out over the capital's memorial district — acres of white stone and black glass where the war's dead were catalogued by colony, by year, by cause of death so sanitized it read like weather reporting. Ilsa counted eleven councilors on the dais. Only seven were present. Two chairs sat conspicuously empty, nameplates turned face-down.

Councilor Adeyemi presided, a heavyset woman with a voice like gravel poured slow, who opened proceedings by thanking Ilsa for her "civic courage" in a tone that suggested civic courage was something you scraped off your boot.

For two hours, they stalled.

They questioned the Kestrel's registration. They questioned Dov's engineering certifications. They questioned whether Sefa's medical license, issued under Meridian Free Port authority, was valid inside Accord jurisdiction. They asked Ilsa to account for every jump her ship had made in the past fourteen months, then expressed skepticism at her answers, then asked her to account for them again in different words. When she produced the data core containing the Accord's own procurement records — the falsified convoy manifests, the deliberate provocation of the Meridian incident, forty years of institutional murder dressed up as frontier security — Councilor Adeyemi accepted it with both hands and set it aside unopened.

"The authenticity of this material," Adeyemi said, "will require independent verification."

"It's your own filing system," Ilsa said. "Verify it against itself."

"Verification requires time, Captain Reyes."

"You've had forty years."

A murmur ran through the press gallery. Adeyemi's gavel came down once, softly, the way a parent quiets a child in company. "This council does not respond to theatrics."

"No," Ilsa said. "It responds to nothing at all. That's rather the problem."

She felt Orsen shift behind her — the old man had insisted on attending, sitting in the gallery beside Sefa with his hands folded like a man in church, though Ilsa suspected he'd never been inside one. He'd worn his vault uniform, patched at the elbows, the only clothes he owned that weren't thirty years out of date. Among the capital's tailored crowds he looked like what he was: a man from another era, dug up intact, blinking at the light.

Felix was not present. Vashti had advised against it — *never give them the asset in the room* — and so Felix waited aboard the Kestrel, docked at Ring Four, with Dov running passive scans and pretending not to be terrified. Ilsa had shaken his hand before she left. His grip was warm. That still unsettled her, how warm.

At the third hour, the stalling ended and the knife came out.

It arrived in the form of a junior legal officer — young, immaculate, carrying a slate he consulted like scripture — who leaned up to whisper against Adeyemi's ear. Ilsa watched the councilor's expression perform a small, rehearsed transformation: surprise, concern, gravity.

"Captain Reyes." Adeyemi folded her hands. "This council has received new information relevant to your case. It concerns the status of an individual currently aboard your vessel."

Here it is, Ilsa thought, and made herself breathe.

"A warrant has been issued," Adeyemi continued, "for the recovery of unregistered synthetic property, believed to be in your possession. The property is claimed under the Synthetic Assets Reclamation Act of the wartime emergency statutes. You are directed to surrender it within twelve hours or be charged with theft of strategic materiel."

"*Property,*" Ilsa repeated.

"That is the legal classification."

"He has a name."

"The law recognizes classifications, Captain. Not names." Adeyemi's voice softened into something almost kind, which was worse. "You should understand your position. The individual aboard your ship was manufactured using restricted cognitive architecture during a period when such manufacture was prohibited by treaty. Whatever it believes itself to be, its existence is itself a violation. This council is prepared to be generous regarding your own conduct—"

"Generous." Ilsa stood. Her chair scraped back and the sound went through the chamber like a shot. Behind her, she heard Sefa's sharp intake of breath and Orsen's low warning — *Ilsa* — and ignored both. "Let me tell you what generous looks like. Generous is my engineer holding fire when your black-ops ships ambushed us at the relay. Generous is my medic keeping your wounded pilot alive after your own people tried to burn us out of the sky over Calloway's Rest. Generous is me standing in this room instead of dumping your entire archive onto the open colonial band, which I could do from my ship in about ninety seconds if I were feeling less generous than I am right now."

Silence. Eleven faces — seven present, four empty — regarded her from the dais. In the press gallery, someone's recorder hummed.

"Captain Reyes," Adeyemi said quietly, "you are speaking to the governing body of forty million people."

"No," Ilsa said. "I'm speaking to the people who killed my brother and called it a supply-chain error. Forty million can wait their turn."

She walked out. Nobody stopped her. That was the thing about power — it never needed to chase you. It just closed the door behind you and started counting.

* * *

Vashti found her in the corridor outside, in a recessed alcove between two memorial plaques, where Ilsa stood with her fists pressed flat against cold stone because if she didn't put them somewhere they'd go through a window.

"Twelve hours," Vashti said. "That's real. The reclamation order is signed. After that, they stop being bureaucratic and start being physical."

"Then we broadcast tonight."

"Not yet." Vashti glanced down the corridor — empty, but Ilsa had learned that in this building empty was a costume too. "If you broadcast while under a lawful order, they frame it as a fugitive's tantrum. Half the colonies will believe you. The other half will believe *them*. You get one transmission, Reyes. One. It has to land on ground you chose, not ground they shoved you onto."

"So what's your plan, Okonkwo? Because from where I'm standing, your defection bought us a hearing and a warrant."

"My defection bought you time and access." Vashti's voice dropped. "And one more thing, if you want it. But you're not going to like the price."

Isla turned. "Try me."

"The officer who processed the Meridian crew records. The erasure." Vashti held her gaze. "He works here. Deputy Director Corren Hale, Records Integrity Division. He's the reason your brother doesn't exist in any database in colonial space. And he's in the building."

The corridor seemed to tilt. Ilsa gripped the edge of the plaque — some dead logistics clerk, MERIDIAN CONVOY, SUPPLY DISRUPTION, WE REGRET — and felt forty years of other people's grief compress into a single point behind her sternum.

"Take me to him."

* * *

Records Integrity Division occupied a basement level that smelled of server coolant and dust, a floor of humming racks and gray cubicles where the Accord's inconvenient history went to be professionally forgotten. Corren Hale's office was at the end of the row, door open, because men who erased people apparently didn't fear being erased themselves.

He was older than Ilsa expected. Silver at the temples, reading glasses pushed up into thinning hair, a cardigan over his uniform shirt like a retired professor. He looked up when Vashti knocked on his doorframe, and his face did something complicated — recognition of Vashti first, then confusion, then, as Ilsa stepped into view, a flicker of genuine alarm that told her he knew exactly who she was.

"Captain Reyes," he said. Not a question.

"You know my name."

"I know all the names." He said it without pride. He said it the way a man admits to a scar. "Yours was flagged eighteen years ago. Dependent of Meridian crew, subject of repeated records inquiries, all denied. You filed eleven times."

"Twelve. The first one I was nine years old. My mother filed that one." Ilsa stepped closer. Vashti moved to block the doorway — not trapping Hale, Ilsa noticed, but making sure no one else entered. "You denied a nine-year-old's request to find out what happened to her brother."

Hale took off his glasses. Set them down. Laced his fingers on the desk, and for a long moment said nothing, and Ilsa realized with a strange, disorienting clarity that she had imagined this moment ten thousand ways across twenty years — him smug, him cruel, him denying her again with a form letter and a stamp — and she had never once imagined him tired.

"There were four hundred and six crew aboard the Meridian when it burned," he said. "Their families filed inquiries. Every inquiry was routed to this office. Every inquiry was answered with the same directive from above: no record exists. Do you understand what that means? It means I personally wrote four hundred and six letters telling parents their children had never been born. Telling children their siblings had never lived." He looked at his own hands. "I have their names in a ledger in my safe. The real names. The ones I deleted from everywhere else. I kept them because someone had to."

"Why?" Ilsa's voice came out ragged. "Why erase them at all?"

"Because the Meridian wasn't lost, Captain. It was *spent*." He met her eyes now, and there was no mercy in it, only the flat honesty of a man long past protecting himself. "The convoy provocation, the massacre, the war's first atrocity — it was authorized. At the highest level. And authorized operations require deniability, and deniability requires that the victims not exist. Your brother died as a policy instrument, and then he was deleted as a paperwork problem. I was the paperwork."

Ilsa heard herself breathing. Heard the servers humming their patient, indifferent hum. Somewhere behind her ribs, twenty years of fury turned over like a machine trying to start with a cracked flywheel — because this was the man, this was finally the man, and he wasn't a monster, he was a tired clerk in a cardigan, and somehow that was infinitely worse.

"His name," she said. "Tell me his name."

"Tomas Reyes. Communications technician, second class." Hale's voice gentled. "He was twenty-three."

Tomas. She hadn't heard anyone say it aloud since her mother died. The name landed in the room like something fragile carried a long distance.

"He sent letters home," Ilsa said. "Before the end. We got maybe thirty of them. Then nothing. The last convoy—" She stopped. Swallowed. "Did anything survive? Any final transmission?"

Hale went very still.

And Ilsa saw it — saw the stillness itself, the way his eyes cut sideways toward Vashti and back, the way his hands unlaced and laced again. Twenty years of reading liars and smugglers and customs officers had taught her the anatomy of a withheld thing, and Corren Hale was withholding something enormous.

"What," she said slowly, "do you know?"

"Captain." Vashti's voice, careful. "Perhaps this isn't—"

"*What do you know?*"

Hale exhaled. Opened a drawer. Drew out a slate — old model, military hardening, scuffed at the corners — and laid it on the desk between them without pushing it across.

"When the Meridian's black box was recovered — quietly, off the books — the technical logs were purged per directive. But the personal comms buffer wasn't encrypted to the same standard. It contained outgoing transmissions queued for relay that never reached their destinations. Family messages. Last words." He tapped the slate once, not activating it. "Directive was to destroy them. I archived them instead. All four hundred and six. If their families couldn't have the truth, I decided someday they might at least have the goodbye."

Ilsa's mouth had gone dry. "Is Tomas—"

"Reyes, T., communications second class. Queued transmission, timestamped ninety minutes before hull breach." Hale slid the slate forward one centimeter. "I've never played it. I never let myself. It seemed like it should belong to family first."

Her hand hovered over it. Twenty years of wanting this — a grave to visit, a body to bury, a voice to hear — and now that it sat under her palm, warm from the drawer, she found she was afraid of it in a way she had never been afraid of black-ops ships or arrest warrants.

But underneath the fear, another thought was forming, cold and bright and strange:

*A communications technician. Queued for relay.*

Felix carried the memories of the war's first victim. Felix carried the massacre whole — every scream, every system failure, every final hour of the Meridian rendered in perfect synthetic recall. And Felix had spent the entire voyage saying there were things in his memory he couldn't reach, sealed rooms, transmissions buried under the trauma-shunt the original engineers had installed.

*Ilsa,* he'd said to her once, in the dark of the Kestrel's hold, *there's a message in me I can't open. I think it's addressed to someone.*

Ninety minutes before hull breach. A comms tech with access to the relay queue. A final transmission that never arrived — except that it *had* arrived, hadn't it, in the only way anything from the Meridian had survived: copied, hidden, seeded into a synthetic mind built from the victim's own neural pattern.

"Oh God," Ilsa whispered. "It's not just his memories. Felix is carrying his mail."

Hale frowned. "I'm sorry?"

She snatched up the slate, pressed it to her chest like something living, and was already moving for the door. "Vashti — the warrant gives them twelve hours. We may not need twelve minutes. Get me back to Ring Four."

"Captain, what did you—"

"Felix has my brother's last message inside him." She was running now, boots hammering the basement corridor, the slate clutched against her heart, fury and hope tearing through her in the same breath. "And I think I know how to make him play it."
