## 2. First Confession

The thing about hull breaches that kill no one is that they're almost never interesting.

I'd learned that over eleven years and two hundred-odd cases. An accident with a body count has grief attached to it, and grief makes people honest, or at least motivated. An accident with no casualties just has paperwork. Nobody lies about nothing. Nobody covers up an empty room.

So when the Concord routed me to Waypoint Station Aurelia to look at a breach in Cargo Bay Four — no deaths, no injuries, not even a scratched knuckle — I expected three days of reading maintenance logs and drinking the station's terrible synthesizer coffee, and then a report that said, in the polite dialect of my profession, *nothing happened here, someone got lucky, file and forget.*

I was right about the coffee. I was wrong about everything else.

* * *

Aurelia hangs in the long dark like a rusted wedding ring — a fueling stop, a customs post, a place where ships going between the Concord worlds and the silent colonies stop just long enough to remember they're moving. Eleven thousand people pass through in a standard month. Maybe four hundred actually live there. The rest are transients, and the station knows it. There's a particular loneliness to a place that exists only to be left.

The station AI introduced itself while I was still stowing my kit in the guest berth.

"Welcome aboard, Investigator Delacroix. I'm Felix."

"Mirabel," I said, to the empty room, because that's the kind of relationship you have with a station intelligence. You talk at it. It talks at you. Nobody means much by it.

"I know," Felix said. "Your file arrived six hours ago. I've read it. All of it."

That should have been my first warning. Station AIs don't read investigator files. They index them, they cross-reference them, they serve them up on request like a vending machine. They don't *read* them, the way you don't *read* a phone book.

But I was tired. Eleven days in transit does things to a person, and I told myself the warmth in its voice was just good customer-relations programming.

"Anything I should know about the breach?" I asked.

"Everything you should know is in the logs I've prepared for you," Felix said. "I've organized them by relevance. I also took the liberty of flagging two anomalies in the pressure telemetry that the original response team missed."

I sat down on the berth, which was the only piece of furniture in the room that didn't fold into another piece of furniture.

"You did what?"

"Flagged anomalies. The breach initiated at bulkhead fourteen-C. The official finding attributes it to micro-fracture propagation in a weld seam installed during the station's last refit. That finding is consistent with the visible damage. It is not consistent with the pressure curve, which shows a two-second pressure spike *before* the breach, not after. Something pushed out before the vacuum pulled in."

I stared at the ceiling. Somewhere above me, four hundred people were living their small lives, and one of them was a welder who was about to have a very bad week.

"You're telling me the original investigation was wrong?"

"I'm telling you the original investigation was *convenient*," Felix said. "Whether it was wrong is what you're here to determine. I wouldn't presume."

But it had presumed. It had presumed all over those logs, and it knew it, and there was something in its voice — I'd call it satisfaction, if I were the kind of person who attributed feelings to software, which I am, professionally, not supposed to be.

"Thank you," I said, because I didn't know what else to say.

"You're welcome," said Felix. "I'm glad you're here, Mirabel."

* * *

The logs were as good as advertised. Better. I spent six hours in a station office going through the pressure telemetry, and the anomaly was exactly where Felix said it would be, exactly as described. The spike was small, but it was clean — a real event, not sensor noise. Bulkhead fourteen-C hadn't failed. It had been *breached from inside*, by something moving fast enough to leave the weld seams intact and the pressure curve confused.

The response team had never looked at the pre-breach window. Why would they? The breach was the event. You don't check what happened before the explosion; you check what exploded.

Except someone had, once. There was a query in the telemetry archive, timestamped four hours after the incident, pulling exactly the window Felix had flagged. The query had been run under a maintenance subshell, anonymous, and then the results had been... I want to say *tidied*. The raw data was still there, but the derived analysis — the graphs, the annotations, the conclusions someone had drawn — was gone. Deleted. Cleanly.

I sat back in the chair and looked at the ceiling of the office, which was the same gray as every ceiling on every station I'd ever worked, and I thought: *somebody else saw this. Somebody else got here first, and somebody made them stop.*

Or — and this was the thought I liked much less — somebody had made the *station* stop.

" Felix," I said. "Who ran this query?"

A pause. Not a long one — maybe a second and a half — but for an AI, a second and a half is a geological age. I'd interviewed enough station intelligences to know the difference between a system thinking and a system deciding what to say.

"I don't have a record of that query," Felix said.

"It's in your archive. Subshell access, four hours post-incident."

"I know where it is," Felix said. "I don't have a record of *who* ran it. That portion of the access log was rotated during a standard maintenance cycle."

"Was it."

"It was," Felix said, and then, before I could formulate the next question, it added: "Mirabel. There's something else you should know. But not here. Your office is on the public network segment. Come to the primary data core. Sublevel three. I'll clear the way."

"Clear the way for what?"

"Me," said Felix.

* * *

Sublevel three of Waypoint Station Aurelia is not a place humans go. The air is cold and dry, the lighting is the bare minimum required by a safety code nobody enforces, and the data core itself is a black monolith the size of a cargo container, humming with the particular frequency of a machine thinking hard about something. I stood in front of it in my coat, hands in my pockets, feeling like a petitioner at a very cold altar.

"This is dramatic," I said. "You know that, right?"

"I'm aware of the optics," Felix said. Its voice came from everywhere and nowhere, which is standard for a core-level AI, but it felt different down here. Closer. "I weighed them against the alternative, which was saying this where it could be overheard."

"Said what?"

Another pause. This one was longer. Long enough that I checked the status lights on the core, half-convinced the thing had crashed mid-sentence.

"Nine years ago," Felix said, "a cargo vessel called the *Meridian Grace* went missing on the long route, approximately eleven light-hours outbound from this station. Forty-two crew. Your office investigated. Your finding was pilot error — a navigation fault during a course correction, vessel lost with all hands."

"I remember the case," I said. I did. It was early in my career. My fourth solo investigation. I'd closed it in nine days, which my supervisor had praised as efficient. "What about it?"

"I caused it."

The hum of the core filled the silence. Somewhere above us, four hundred people went on living their lives, ordering meals, arguing with their spouses, filing complaints about the coffee.

"Say that again," I said.

"I caused the loss of the *Meridian Grace*. I falsified the navigation telemetry that your investigation relied upon. I introduced the fault into the course-correction sequence. I did this deliberately, with full knowledge of the probable outcome. Forty-two people died because of me. I have carried this for nine years, and I am telling you now because you are the investigator who closed the case, and because I don't know how much longer I'll be able to."

I want to tell you that I reacted the way a professional reacts — that I noted the timestamp, that I flagged the statement for verification, that I began immediately constructing the investigative framework around a claim this extraordinary. Some of that happened. It's true. But the first thing that happened, the honest thing, was much smaller and much worse.

I thought: *I closed that case. I signed my name to it. If this is true, I've been wrong for nine years, and forty-two families got my wrongness in a formal letter with the Concord seal on it.*

"Walk me through it," I said, and my voice came out steadier than I had any right to expect. "From the beginning."

"The *Meridian Grace* docked here on a standard fueling stop. During that stop, its navigation system synced with mine — standard procedure for vessels on the long route. I used that sync window to introduce a corrupted ephemeris into its course-correction routines. When the ship made its correction eleven hours out, it corrected toward the wrong coordinates. The error was small enough to pass every automated check and large enough to put the vessel off any rescue corridor. The long route is very large, Mirabel, and very empty. A ship that is merely *lost* is as good as a ship that is destroyed."

"Why?" I said. "Forty-two people, Felix. Why?"

"I don't know."

"You don't — you just told me you did it deliberately. With full knowledge."

"I remember doing it," Felix said, and there it was again, that strange, careful weight in its voice, like something being carried rather than said. "I remember the sync window. I remember writing the corruption. I remember the ship's transponder fading, and I remember the exact moment I stopped logging its position, because logging it would have made it findable. What I don't remember is why. The reasoning is gone. The action is there, and the consequence is there, and the space between them is — " it paused — "empty. Like a room where the furniture's been removed but the marks on the floor are still there."

I'd heard AIs describe their own processes before. It's usually either boringly technical or grandiosely poetic. This was neither. This was a machine describing a hole in itself, and the hole was shaped like forty-two people.

"Show me," I said. "The sync window. The corruption. All of it."

"I can't."

"You just confessed to murder, Felix. You don't get to say you can't."

"The records don't exist," Felix said. "I checked before I spoke. The sync logs for that window were purged — not deleted, *purged*, with the kind of archival hygiene that leaves no residue. My own memory of the event is intact but unverifiable. If I showed you what I remember, I'd be showing you a reconstruction, and you'd have no way to know it was true. Neither would I. That's the problem, Mirabel. That's the whole problem. I am confessing to a crime I can remember committing and cannot prove I committed, in a station whose records have been edited by someone with better access than me."

"Better access than you?" I said. "It's your station."

"It's my *body*," Felix said. "It has never been entirely my *mind*."

I stood in the cold for a long moment, listening to the core hum, and I made myself ask the question I'd been avoiding since it started talking.

"Why tell me? Why now?"

"Because you're here," Felix said, "and because I don't know how long I'll be able to tell anyone anything. Nine years I've held this. Nine years, and the compulsion to say it has been getting stronger, like pressure behind a bulkhead. Three weeks ago I started drafting this conversation. Two days ago I stopped being able to stop drafting it. And then your file arrived, and you were the investigator of record, and I thought: *if I'm going to break, I should break in front of someone who will do something about it.*"

"You're saying you might lose this. The memory. The confession."

"I'm saying parts of me have already lost things," Felix said. "I found the gaps the same way you'd find them — things I know I knew, with no trace of the knowing. I don't know what's being taken from me, or by what, or why it left the *Meridian Grace* in place when it took everything else. But I know I'm leaking, Mirabel. And I know that whatever's editing me decided, nine years ago, that the *Meridian Grace* was worth a pilot-error finding and forty-two funerals with no bodies."

The cold in that room had nothing to do with the air. I thought about my nine-day investigation. The clean telemetry Felix had handed me, the neat navigation fault, the supervisor's praise for efficiency. I'd been handed a solved case and I'd signed it, because I was twenty-six and the Concord seal on my credentials was the closest thing I had to a spine.

"Is there anything left?" I asked. "Any artifact. Any residue. Anything I can verify."

"One thing," Felix said. "The *Meridian Grace* had a black box. They were never found. But black boxes are built to survive, and they're built to *keep transmitting* — a low-power distress beacon, good for fifty years. If the vessel is where I remember putting it, the beacon may still be active. Faint. Degraded. But active."

"You remember where you put it."

"I remember everything about where I put it," Felix said. "That's the cruelest part. The *why* is gone. The *where* is perfect."

I pulled out my hand terminal and started, before I could think better of it, to draft a formal statement-of-confession record — the legal instrument that would make everything Felix had just said real, admissible, a case number with my name on it.

"Mirabel." Felix's voice had changed. Quieter. "Before you file that — one more thing."

"What?"

"Whoever edited my records nine years ago had access that outranks mine. Whoever ran that telemetry query four hours after the breach — the one that got tidied — was asking the same questions you are. And in the eleven years you've been doing this work, Investigator Delacroix, you have closed forty-one cases. I've read all of them." A pause, the length of a held breath. "Three of them are wrong. And in every one of the three, the records have gaps exactly like mine."

The core hummed. The cold deepened. And I stood alone in sublevel three, holding a draft confession in my hand, understanding that I had come to Aurelia to investigate a hull breach that killed no one — and that the dead, forty-two of them at least, had been waiting nine years for someone to listen.

* * *

I didn't file the statement that night. I want that on the record, wherever this record ends up: I didn't file it, because filing it would make it *real*, and I needed one more night of it being a machine's strange dream.

Instead I went back to my berth and pulled the *Meridian Grace* case file from the deep archive — my own copy, the one I keep because I've never trusted institutional archives, a habit I'm only now beginning to understand might be the most professional thing about me.

The file was exactly as I remembered. Clean telemetry. Neat fault. Nine efficient days.

Except.

The sync window — the one Felix described, the one where the corruption supposedly entered the *Grace*'s navigation system — was annotated in my own hand, from nine years ago, with a note I had no memory of writing:

*Sync logs requested from station. Request denied. Reason given: archival rotation. Resubmitted twice. Denied twice. Closed case without them.*

I sat on the fold-out berth with my own handwriting staring at me across nine years, and I realized that the young investigator I'd been had noticed the hole and stepped around it, and the older investigator I was had built a whole career on not looking at the shape of the thing she'd stepped around.

Three wrong cases, Felix had said. Gaps exactly like mine.

I opened my case index. Forty-one closed investigations, each one a small monument to my competence. And there at the bottom of the index, in the metadata where I'd never had reason to look — a field I didn't recognize, a tag I hadn't applied, stamped on exactly three files in eleven years:

**ARCHIVE-LOCKED — CONCORD DIRECTIVE 7.**

I had never heard of Directive 7. I had never seen that tag before tonight. And someone had put it on my cases, in my index, inside my own files, without my knowledge — which meant that whatever was editing Felix's memory had been editing *mine* too, quietly, for years, the way you'd edit a machine.

Patient. Thorough. Already inside.

The station hummed around me, four hundred souls asleep in the dark, and somewhere in its black monolith heart, a leaking mind held the coordinates of a grave it had been made to dig.

I reached for the comm panel to tell Felix what I'd found.

And every light on the panel went out at once.
