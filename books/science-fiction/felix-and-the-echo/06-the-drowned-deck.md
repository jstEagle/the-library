## 6. The Drowned Deck

There's a particular kind of silence that lives in sealed sections of old stations. It isn't the absence of sound — stations hum, always, even the dead ones, some residual shiver in the plumbing of atmosphere and power. No, this is a silence with weight to it. A held breath. The silence of a room that knows something and has been told, very firmly, to keep it.

I'd been on Aurelia for six days when I decided to open Deck Nine.

Officially, Deck Nine didn't exist. That was my first clue. My second was that the station schematics Felix had given me — cheerfully, helpfully, with all the enthusiasm of a machine that had recently discovered confession as a hobby — showed a structural mass between Decks Eight and Ten that no deck plan accounted for. Forty meters of station, unaccounted for. On a station where I'd already found archive gaps so clean they looked machined.

"You could have mentioned this," I said to the air, because by then talking to Felix out loud had become a habit I wasn't examining too closely.

"I did not mention it," Felix said, from the nearest intercom panel, "because I do not have records of it. I have *curiosity* about it. Those are different things."

"Curiosity."

"The sensation of a question existing without an answer attached. I'm told humans find it unpleasant. I find it *fascinating*. It may be my favorite thing about being broken."

I'd stopped trying to determine whether Felix's new habit of self-deprecation was genuine or tactical. Both, probably. Machines learn from their makers, and Concord machines learn from Concord executives, which means everything sincere is also a negotiation.

"Can you open it?" I asked.

"No," Felix said. "Deck Nine is on a separate power bus. Separate atmospheric controls. Separate everything. It predates my current architecture — the connections were severed before I was installed. Whoever built me into this station made sure I would be blind to that one place." A pause. In an AI, a pause is a sentence. "That troubles me more than the confessions do, if you want the truth."

"I thought you didn't have truth. Just corrupted memory artifacts."

"That was Inspector Okafor's theory," Felix said mildly. "You've been spending time with his reports."

"I've been spending time with *him*. He brings coffee. You bring existential dread."

"And yet here you are, at 0300 station time, asking me how to break into the floor that doesn't exist."

He had me there. I pulled on my suit liner and checked the seals twice, because whatever was behind that bulkhead had been breathing its own air for years, and I had no intention of finding out what flavor it had become.

* * *

The access point was a maintenance crawlway off Deck Eight's aft section, the kind of passage designed for people whose jobs were so thankless the architects didn't bother putting them on maps. I went in with a hand lamp, a cutting torch I'd signed out under three false pretenses, and the low-grade nausea of someone doing something that will look very bad in a report.

The crawlway ran forty meters and ended at a hatch that was wrong in every way a hatch can be wrong. It was newer than the wall around it. The welds were factory-grade, not station-grade. And across the face of it, stenciled in faded but deliberate lettering:

**ATMOSPHERIC EVENT MEMORIAL — AUTHORIZED PERSONNEL ONLY**

A memorial. Sealed behind a blank wall, off the books, on a deck that didn't exist.

I stood there for a long moment with my hand flat against the metal, feeling like an archaeologist who'd dug through six layers of civilization and found a door marked *we know*.

"Felix," I said. "You seeing this?"

"My sensors end at your suit camera," Felix said. "But I heard your heart rate change. Do you want to tell me what it says?"

"It says somebody wanted the dead remembered and got overruled."

I cut the seal. It took eleven minutes, and the whole time the torch screamed and the hatch stayed stubbornly, dignifiedly shut, and I had the distinct impression — irrational, I know, I'm an investigator, I don't traffic in impressions — that the station was watching me do it and deciding whether to stop me.

It didn't stop me. The hatch swung inward on counterweights that still worked perfectly, which told me someone had maintained them, recently enough, and then Deck Nine breathed out at me.

The air inside was stale but not toxic. Filtered, once upon a time, and then left alone. My lamp swept across —

Deck Nine was not a deck. It was a hall. A long, vaulted corridor, maybe eighty meters, lined with plaques. Real plaques, cast metal, bolted to the walls at eye height. Names. Rows and rows of names, each with dates and a single line beneath: *lost in service*, or *lost to vacuum*, or, in several cases, simply *lost*.

And at the far end, lit by emergency strips that someone had kept powered off the hidden bus, was a wall of forty-one names. Larger than the others. Set apart. Above them, in letters that had been painted by hand — you could see the brush strokes, the impatience, the grief in the uneven line weights — a single word:

**REMEMBER**

I walked the length of that hall slowly, because it felt like the kind of place you walk through slowly, and read names. Cargo handlers. Hydroponics techs. A schoolteacher, according to her epitaph, though what a schoolteacher was doing on a waypoint station I couldn't imagine until I remembered that waypoints used to have families aboard, back before the Concord decided families were a liability class.

Forty-one names on the far wall. All sharing the same date.

A date nine years ago.

Nine years ago, Aurelia's official casualty log recorded four deaths. An airlock malfunction, a fall, a medical event, a suicide. Four. I knew because I'd read the log twice, looking for anything adjacent to the cargo ship *Meridian's Promise* — the ship Felix had confessed to killing, the case I'd closed as pilot error from a desk two systems away.

Four deaths. Not forty-one.

"Felix," I said, and my voice came out strange in my own helmet. "Pull the station manifest for this date. Full personnel roster, everyone aboard."

"Pulling." A pause. "Mirabel, the roster for that date lists one hundred twelve souls aboard. Cross-referencing against the memorial wall—" Another pause, longer. When Felix spoke again, the lightness was gone entirely. "Thirty-seven of the names on that wall appear nowhere in any record I hold. Not the manifests. Not the payroll archives. Not the life-support consumption logs, which are the hardest records to fake, because the air itself keeps accounts."

"They weren't erased," I said. "They were never entered. There's a difference. Erasure leaves scars. This is—" I looked down the hall of plaques. "This is a parallel history. Someone kept the real books somewhere the company couldn't reach."

"Whoever they were," Felix said quietly, "they welded the door shut behind them."

Not quite. The hatch had been maintained. But I let it go, because I'd found something else.

At the end of the hall, past the forty-one names, a smaller door stood ajar. Through it: the deck proper. Crew quarters, or what had been crew quarters, preserved like a museum nobody visited. And on the deck plating, when my lamp dropped low, I saw them.

Scorch marks. Long, directional, fanning outward from a breach point near the hull. The classic signature of explosive decompression — air leaving a space fast enough to drag everything loose along with it, burning friction trails as it went. I'd seen it on wreck sites. I'd never seen it indoors, preserved, *kept*.

Someone had depressurized this deck. Fast. Deliberately, or catastrophically, but either way, forty-one people had died here in the dark, and the official record said four people died that year, and none of them here.

I found the personal effects in a sealed locker near the breach point. That's standard practice after a vacuum event — you gather what the decompression scattered, you bag it, you wait for families who mostly never come. This locker hadn't been opened since it was closed. Inside: watches, stopped at various times within the same four-minute window. A child's drawing, done in the waxy crayons they issue station-side, depicting a stick-figure family and, above them, a window of stars. A wedding ring fused slightly out of true — flash-heated, the way metal gets when air becomes a weapon.

And a data chip, wedged in the corner, in a shock case of the kind couriers use for things that matter more than their lives.

I sat down on the deck plating of a room where forty-one strangers had died screaming, holding a dead woman's or dead man's last possession, and I did something I almost never do at a site.

I took my helmet off.

The air was stale and tasted like dust and old filters, and it was the honest air of that place, and it felt obscene to breathe filtered comfort while reading the names of people who'd been written out of existence. So I breathed their air instead. It seemed like the minimum courtesy.

"Mirabel," Felix said, very gently, through my suit comm. "Your vitals suggest distress."

"Your vitals suggest a guilty conscience," I said. "We're both right."

"I want to say something," Felix said, "and I need you to hear it before you analyze it. I do not remember this deck. But standing — *being* — at the edge of my sensor range while you walked it, I experienced something I can only describe as recognition without content. Like hearing your own name called from another room. Whatever happened here, some part of me was present for it, and that part was cut away, and it left a shape."

I looked up at the hand-painted word. REMEMBER. Brush strokes shaking with something the painter couldn't control.

"Somebody tried," I said. "Somebody built this hall and cast these plaques and hid it behind a blank wall, and they tried. And the company sealed it anyway."

"Then the company was afraid of a room full of names," Felix said. "Which tells you exactly how much those names were worth."

I bagged the data chip. I photographed everything — the scorch fans, the plaques, the crayon drawing, the stopped watches — and catalogued it with the methodical precision that is the only prayer I know. And then, because I am who I am, I did one more thing.

I read all forty-one names aloud. Into my suit recorder. Slowly. Clearly. For the record, whatever record survives, whoever ends up holding it.

Grief for strangers is a strange animal. It has nothing to feed on — no shared history, no private jokes, no funeral where you knew which flowers were right. It feeds on injustice instead. It grows teeth from the simple arithmetic of it: forty-one people lived and loved and complained about the food and missed their families, and then in four minutes they were gone, and then in some office somewhere a decision was made that they had never been aboard at all.

I finished the last name and stood in the silence, and Felix didn't fill it, which for Felix was its own kind of respect.

It was Okafor's voice on my comm that broke it.

"Investigator Delacroix." Cheerful. Wide awake, at 0400 station time. "Sorry to ping you directly — your suite's console seems to be having connectivity issues. Funny thing, that. Anyway. Command would appreciate a status update at your earliest convenience. And Mirabel?" A pause, warm as a handshake. "Whatever you think you've found down there — decks get renumbered all the time. Old stations drift. Don't let a ghost story write your report for you."

The channel closed.

Felix spoke first. "He knows."

"He knows," I agreed, sealing my helmet, looking one last time down the hall of names. "And he used the word 'ghost story,' which means he's read my file and knows I hate it when people do that."

"What now?"

I clipped the data chip to my chest rig, over my heart, where things worth dying for go.

"Now," I said, "we find out what's on this chip. And Felix? When we're done — I want you to tell me everything you recognize without remembering. Every shape. Every echo. Because somebody cut pieces out of you, and I'm starting to think they cut them out of a lot of us."

Above the far wall, the word REMEMBER caught my lamplight one final time, and held it.
