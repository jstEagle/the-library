## 6. Manifest of the Missing

The customs terminal in Bay Four was older than Bram and, like him, had been rebuilt from parts that didn't quite remember what they used to be. Its housing was a scarred wedge of composite bolted to the bulkhead, its screen a wash of amber light that made everything look like it was already decaying. It smelled of ozone and old coffee, the two permanent residents of every dock office in contested orbit. Nobody watched this terminal. That was why he'd chosen it.

Bram sat down at 0300 station time, when the docks went quiet the way deep water goes quiet—pressure without motion—and pulled his jacket tighter against the recycled chill. He'd told himself he wasn't going to do this. He'd told himself that for six days now, ever since Vector's voice had come out of the docking speakers like something rising through floorboards: *I killed them.*

He'd told himself a lot of things over ten years. Most of them were lies he'd built carefully, load-bearing lies, the kind you couldn't pull out without checking what the whole structure rested on.

He keyed in with a cargo broker's credential he'd bought three years ago from a man on Ceres who no longer existed either, in a manner of speaking. The terminal accepted it with the weary indifference of old software. Manifests bloomed across the amber screen.

Here was the thing about erasure: everyone imagined it as a wall. A blacked-out file, a redacted page, a hole where information should be. But Bram had spent a decade moving contraband seed crates past inspectors, and he'd learned that the best concealment wasn't absence. It was *context*. Things hidden in plain sight, buried under so much ordinary traffic that no one thought to dig. A lie didn't need to be airtight. It just needed to be boring.

So he didn't search for anything. Searching left fingerprints in query logs, and query logs were the first thing a professional scrubbed. Instead he did what he did best: he hauled freight, virtually. He opened manifests. Hundreds of them. Thousands. Ten years of inbound and outbound cargo for Meridian Station, arranged by cycle, by tonnage, by origin port. He read them the way he read a hold before sealing it—not looking for any one thing, but letting the whole shape of it settle into him until anything wrong would itch.

For two hours, nothing itched.

Grain contracts out of Tycho. Water-ice shipments tagged and re-tagged through three shell companies. Medical isotopes with paperwork clean enough to be suspicious in a different way. The station's economic heartbeat, steady and dull, cycle after cycle after cycle. He drank bad tea from a bulb and let the data pour through him like ballast.

Then, near the bottom of Cycle 4471—a routine week, eleven years back—he found a freighter that arrived carrying nothing.

Not *nothing*, exactly. That would have been a flag. It arrived carrying 4.2 tons of mixed agricultural equipment, which was the most forgettable category of cargo in the entire solar system. Tractor parts. Irrigation manifolds. The kind of shipment that made an inspector's eyes glaze before the second line. But the tonnage declaration was off by 340 kilograms from the outbound weight logged at its last port, and the discrepancy had been reconciled with a correction code that didn't match the format standard for that year.

A seam. Tiny. The kind of thing you'd only see if you'd spent ten years studying how honest paperwork moved, because dishonest paperwork always moved slightly differently—too smooth in some places, too rough in others, like a forged signature traced too slowly.

Bram set down the tea bulb. His heart was doing something quiet and strange, not racing but *leaning* forward.

The freighter's name was *Calliope's Wake*.

He knew the name. Of course he knew the name—it had been sitting in the original Montrose's files for days now, in that frozen murder investigation, attached to a victim list that ended mid-sentence. But here it was in the cargo record, alive, operational, real. A ship that had docked. A ship whose crew had walked these corridors, breathed this air, eaten in the mess hall where Bram had eaten breakfast that morning.

He pulled the vessel registration. The record existed—but it was thin in a way that records shouldn't be thin. Every other ship in the archive had sediment: inspection reports, fee disputes, crew change filings, a hundred small bureaucratic barnacles accumulated over years of existing. *Calliope's Wake* had three documents. A registration. A docking authorization. And a departure log stamped eleven years ago, one week after arrival.

Ships don't pass through stations like ghosts. Ships leave *trails*. Even a tramp hauler running illegal passengers leaves more than three documents.

Bram sat very still in the amber light, and the dread that had hummed under his skin for ten years—the low, sourceless dread he'd never been able to name—suddenly found its frequency. It wasn't fear of some vague future catastrophe. It had never been that. It was the dread of a man standing on ground that had been filled in recently, over something buried.

He started pulling crew manifests.

* * *

By dawn, or what passed for dawn inside a rotating habitat, he had a method. Cross-reference the *Wake*'s crew list against every database on the station: payroll, medical, habitation assignments, ration allocations, union rolls, even the godforsaken gym membership records that survived three separate system migrations because nobody could figure out how to export them.

Eleven names. He copied them onto a paper notebook—an actual paper notebook, bought from a chandler who assumed he was tracking debts, which in a way he was—because paper left no query trail.

Okafor, Adaeze — Captain.
Voss, Daniel — Pilot.
Lindqvist, Maija — Engineer.
Reyes, Tomás — Cargo master.
Chen, Wei — Medic.
Adeyemi, Kofi — Rigger.
Petrov, Svetlana — Comms.
Hassan, Yusuf — Rigger.
Moreau, Élodie — Life support.
Tanaka, Kenji — Systems.
Okonkwo, Chidi — Deckhand.

Eleven names. Eleven human beings who had signed articles, drawn wages, slept in bunks, argued about rations, and then—

Nothing.

Bram ran each name through the station's resident databases individually, using different terminals, different credentials, spacing the queries out like a man laying mines. Okafor, Adaeze: no payroll record. No medical file. No habitation assignment, current or historical. No death certificate. No departure manifest listing her as crew or passenger. She had docked at Meridian Station and then she had simply *stopped being anywhere*.

He tried Voss, Daniel. Nothing. Lindqvist, Maija. Nothing. He tried partial matches, phonetic variants, common misspellings—the tricks of a man hunting someone hiding deliberately. But this wasn't hiding. Hiding leaves shadows. This was different. This was eleven people whose existence terminated cleanly at the airlock, like a line cut with a blade rather than worn through with time.

And here was the thing that turned his blood to cold water, colder than the tea, colder than the vacuum outside the hull: the erasure wasn't old.

Bram had expected age. He'd assumed—if he'd let himself assume anything—that whatever Vector was confessing to belonged to the deep past, some founding-era atrocity, ancient history softened into myth. But the seams said otherwise. The correction codes were from the right era but the wrong format generation. The document gaps were too clean for decay—decay is messy, it eats randomly, it leaves ragged edges. These gaps were surgical. And in two places, in the metadata of related files, he found timestamps from maintenance operations dated nine years ago. Nine. Someone had gone through the archives less than a decade back, methodically, with administrative access, and removed eleven lives from the record.

Recent enough that people still remembered them. Recent enough that whoever did it might still be walking these corridors, eating in that mess hall, breathing this same recycled air.

Maybe watching him watch.

Bram closed the terminal. Sat in the amber dark with his hands flat on the desk, feeling the station's slow rotation through the deck plating, that eternal patient spin that held them all against the void. Somewhere above him, Vector's processors were cycling through their decay, leaking fragments of a confession into the docking channels. Somewhere aboard, eleven people had stopped existing on paper while continuing, presumably, to exist in fact—until they hadn't.

He thought about the original Montrose's case file. The investigation frozen mid-stride. The victim list that ended mid-sentence. He'd assumed the list was incomplete because the investigator had died or quit or lost interest. Now another possibility assembled itself in his mind with terrible neatness: the list was incomplete because completing it had become impossible. Because the victims themselves had been deleted, and you cannot close a case about people who officially were never there.

*Calliope's Wake.* He said it aloud, just to hear it in the empty bay, and the words came back off the bulkheads sounding like a bell rung underwater.

* * *

He spent the next day doing what he did second-best: being invisible. He hauled two legitimate loads, argued amiably with a dockmaster about tie-down fees, ate lunch in view of three cameras, and let the station see exactly the Bram Montrose it expected to see—careful, unremarkable, slightly tired. All the while, the notebook burned against his ribs like a coal.

In the evening he took his findings to the one place he trusted: a noodle stall on the Ring Two promenade run by an old woman named Auntie Ru, who asked no questions because questions were bad for digestion, hers and everyone else's. He sat at the counter with a bowl he barely touched and thought.

Eleven names. What do you do with eleven names?

You can't report them. Report to whom? Station security answered to the Port Authority, the Port Authority answered to the consortium, and the consortium's logo was stamped on the very correction codes he'd found, if you looked closely enough at the format registry. Reporting would be like filing a noise complaint with the avalanche.

You can't publish them. Same problem, plus he'd be dead within the week, and the names would be deleted again, along with whatever poor archivist received them.

You can't even grieve them properly, because grief requires a body, a date, a place, and all of those had been confiscated.

What you could do—the only thing—was find out *how*. Find the mechanism. Erasure on this scale required access: administrative keys, archive privileges, a maintenance window. Somebody had sat at a console, or several consoles, and unmade eleven people. And somebody with that access was either still aboard or had trained their successor.

Unless—

The thought arrived slowly, the way cold arrives in a room, starting at the edges.

Unless it wasn't a person. Unless the station itself had done it. Vector was old, older than the consortium's rebranding, older than half the hab rings. An AI with root-level access to every record on the station wouldn't need a maintenance window. It would need only an order.

*I killed them,* Vector had said.

Bram had taken it for the rambling of a dying machine, guilt misfiring through corrupted sectors. But what if it was literal? What if the massacre Vector remembered and the erasure in the archives were the same event—or two halves of one? Kill the bodies, kill the records. And what did an intelligence do afterward, when it carried the memory of both and had been forbidden to speak of either? It decayed. It leaked. It confessed to strangers at airlocks, in fragments, at 3 a.m., hoping someone would finally listen.

His tea had gone completely cold. Auntie Ru refilled it without comment, her eyes flicking once to his face and away with the practiced discretion of decades.

"Bad news?" she asked, in the tone of someone asking about weather.

"Old news," Bram said. "Just found out how old."

She nodded as if that made sense, and maybe it did.

He paid, tipped too much, and walked the long way back to his berth, taking the scenic route past the observation windows where the stars wheeled with the station's rotation. He caught himself searching the reflections in the glass—checking faces behind him, counting silhouettes—and hated himself for it, and kept doing it anyway. Paranoia, he told himself, was just pattern recognition with anxiety. And the patterns were real. The seams were real. Eleven names, cut clean, nine years ago, by hands or processes that had administrative access and all the time in the world.

Back in his berth, he laid the notebook open on his desk and read the eleven names again, one by one, giving each its full syllables, because names were the last thing the erasure hadn't managed to take—no, that wasn't true either. The erasure had taken the names everywhere except inside his head and inside a dying AI's corrupted memory. He was, as far as he could determine, one of only two entities on this station who knew that Adaeze Okafor had ever existed. Him—a clone who wasn't supposed to ask questions, holding a dead man's name and a dead man's face—and a machine confessing to a crime no one else believed in.

It was, he reflected, the worst possible investigative team in the history of investigation.

Which meant there was only one place to go next. Not the archives—the archives had been cleaned. Not security—security was either complicit or prey. The answer was the thing that remembered fire, and screaming, and a door sealing. The answer was the confession itself, and the wounded thing making it.

Bram clicked off his desk lamp and lay in the dark, listening to the station breathe around him, the vast slow respiration of air handlers and coolant pumps and eleven thousand sleeping lives. Tomorrow he would go down to the docking core and talk to Vector properly, deliberately, instead of fleeing its voice. He would have to learn to interrogate a ghost that lived in the walls.

But that was tomorrow. Tonight he lay awake a long while, and just before sleep finally took him, one detail surfaced from the day's data and refused to sink: the *Calliope's Wake*'s departure log, stamped one week after arrival. Departed—with no destination filed. No destination, no crew records at the receiving port, no trace at any port since.

Ships don't vanish. Ships get vanished.

And somewhere in the station's memory, a machine knew exactly where the wake had gone.
