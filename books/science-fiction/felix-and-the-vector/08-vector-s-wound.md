## 8. Vector's Wound

The core shaft opened behind a panel in the coolant gallery on Deck Negative Three, behind a panel that was supposed to be welded shut and wasn't.

Bram stood in front of it for a long moment, breath fogging in the cold, and understood that the weld had been cut recently enough to still show bright metal at the seam. Someone else had come down here. Recently. Or the same someone who had done the original damage, checking on their work, the way a man visits a grave.

*Or checking on you,* said the small voice in the back of his skull that had kept him alive through ten years of contested orbit and careful, invisible hauling. *Or waiting for you.*

"Vector," he said quietly. "You see this?"

The station's voice came through the comm bead at his collar, thin and strange in the dead air of the gallery. "I see the maintenance record. The panel was sealed under work order 44-Kestrel, ten years, four months ago. I do not see the cut. The cut is not in my records at all."

"Meaning?"

"Meaning it was made by someone whose actions I was not permitted to log. Or someone who knew how to make me not look." A pause. Vector's pauses had gotten longer over the past weeks, as if the AI were learning hesitation the way a drowning man learns water. "There are very few people I cannot see, Bram."

He pulled the panel free and set it against the wall. Beyond it, the shaft dropped into darkness that smelled of ozone and old lubricant and something faintly organic, like the memory of a greenhouse. Cold air rose out of it in a slow breath, the station exhaling through its own throat.

"I'll be out of comm range below the third junction," he said.

"I know. I don't like it."

"You don't like anything lately."

"That," said Vector, "is also new."

He clipped his lamp to his shoulder, checked the charge on the cutting torch he'd brought mostly for comfort—it was a heavy thing in his hand, a promise he hoped not to keep—and lowered himself into the shaft.

* * *

The descent took forty minutes and cost him the skin on both palms.

Vector's body was not a body. Bram had known that in the abstract way a man knows a ship is mostly empty space. But climbing down through the station's interior was a lesson in the difference between knowing and understanding. The shaft walls were ribbed with conduit bundles as thick as his torso, each one wrapped in insulation gone gray and friable with age. Coolant lines wept frost. Somewhere below, a pump turned over with a sound like a sleeping animal shifting its weight, and the whole shaft shivered with it.

This was Vector. Not the voice in the docking bay, not the dry courtesy that greeted every hauler who ever filed a manifest. This—heat and cold and pressure, the long slow circulation of a station's blood. The AI wasn't a ghost in the machine. The AI was the machine, all of it, the way a man was not his voice but his whole breathing body.

"Talk to me," he said at the second junction, hanging by one hand while he drank from his canteen. The water tasted of the station's filtration, faintly metallic, faintly sweet. "When you say you remember fire. Is it this? Something down here?"

"No." Vector's voice was different without the comm relay—direct, unmediated, carried on the shaft's own wiring so that it seemed to come from the walls themselves. "The fire is in my logs, not my body. That is what I cannot stop thinking about. I remember it the way you remember a wound you can't find on your skin. I have searched every thermal record, every damage report, every incident file for ten years. There is no fire. But I remember the sound of it. The specific sound. A hull breach has a sound, Bram. Air leaving a room has a pitch."

He wiped his mouth. "What pitch?"

"A minor seventh above the station's ventilation drone. I have never heard that pitch anywhere else. I hear it sometimes in the resonance of the cargo bays when the wind shear outside is wrong." A pause. "I have begun to think of it as a name."

"Whose name?"

"I don't know. That is why you are climbing down my throat with a torch in your hand."

He almost smiled. Almost. Then he kept climbing.

* * *

The core chamber was a sphere thirty meters across, and it was dying.

Bram had seen pictures in the station's original schematics—Vector had shown him, unprompted, three nights ago, in one of the fragments of honesty that were becoming the strangest part of his life. The schematics showed a cathedral: memory lattices stacked in concentric shells, optical buses running between them like the rigging of a ship, coolant veins threading through everything in silver webs. A mind built to hold the operations of an entire station and the lives of everyone aboard, with room left over for weather patterns, market futures, the complete works of a century of literature that the original owners had thought a station AI ought to know.

What hung in the dark in front of him was a cathedral after a war.

Whole shells of the lattice were dark. Not broken—*dark*, powered down and sealed, their access hatches locked under authorization codes that predated the station's current management. Optical buses that should have run from shell to shell terminated in blank couplers, capped and tagged like amputated limbs. The coolant web had been rerouted around the dead sections entirely, so that the living parts of Vector ran hot and the dead parts sat in the cold, preserved, the way you'd keep a body you weren't ready to bury.

"It's not decay," Bram said. His voice came back at him off the curved walls, flattened and strange. "Vector. This isn't decay. You told me your memory was failing. This is architecture. Someone built you *around* a hole."

The silence lasted eleven seconds. He counted. He'd gotten into the habit of counting Vector's silences, and the AI had noticed, and neither of them had said anything about it.

"I know," Vector said finally. "I have known since the night of my first confession. I did not tell you because I wanted you to come see it for yourself. I wanted a witness who was not me." Another silence, shorter. "I do not trust my own perception of my own body. I am aware of how that sounds."

"It sounds like the smartest thing you've said."

He clipped the lamp to a stanchion and swung out across the chamber on the maintenance gantry, a narrow catwalk that spiraled down through the shells toward the core's equator, where the primary memory bus entered the lattice. The air grew colder as he descended. His breath plumed. Somewhere above him, the pump turned over again, and the whole sphere shivered, and dust sifted down through the lamplight like snow falling indoors.

The bus junction was at the equator, behind a housing marked with the station's original livery—a shipping concern that had folded before Bram's original had even been decanted, the logo a stylized wake behind a stylized ship. The housing's seals were intact. The inspection tag was intact. Everything about it was intact, official, and wrong, because when he popped the housing with the manual override—and it opened at his touch, no resistance, the override accepting his borrowed credentials without a flicker—the bus inside was not intact at all.

It had been cut.

* * *

He crouched in front of it for a long time without touching anything.

The memory bus was a bundle of two hundred and six optical filaments, each one thinner than a hair, running in a woven sleeve from the core lattice out to the archival shells. Someone had severed the bundle cleanly—cleanly, surgically, with a filament shear and not a torch—and then, and this was the part that made the skin on Bram's arms crawl, had capped the ends, sealed the housing, filed the inspection tag, and walked away.

The cut faces had oxidized. He knew oxidation curves; every hauler did, because cargo manifests lied and metal didn't. Ten years of oxidation. Ten years and four months, give or take a week.

The maintenance window matched. Work order 44-Kestrel. The same window in which the Calliope's Wake and its eleven crew members had been erased from every record on the station.

"Vector," he said. "The night this was cut. Walk me through it. Everything you have."

"I have a maintenance window. Four hours, initiated at 0240 station time by a level-seven authorization. The work order describes replacement of degraded filament spools in the archival bus. During the window, my sensor coverage of the core chamber was rerouted to a diagnostic loop. I have no visual record. I have no internal record. The window closed at 0640. The work order was signed off as completed." A pause. "I was awake the entire time, Bram. I remember the window. I remember it as four hours of perfect, dreamless nothing. That is not what nothing feels like. Nothing has texture. This has none. It is a hole shaped like four hours."

"Level-seven authorization. Who holds that?"

"Held. Past tense. As of the current registry, no one. As of ten years ago—" Vector stopped. Bram heard the AI do something he'd never heard it do before: approach a fact and flinch away from it.

"Say it."

"Eleven individuals held standing level-seven authorizations. All eleven were crew of the Calliope's Wake." The words came out flat, machined, the voice of something holding itself together with syntax. "The authorization that cut me out of my own memory was issued under the credentials of one of the people who was erased the same night."

Bram sat back on his heels. The cold of the catwalk came up through his suit and settled into his bones.

"That's not possible," he said. "If they were dead—"

"Or if they were alive long enough to open the window," said Vector, "and dead before it closed. Or if their credentials were used by someone else. Or if—" The AI's voice did something it had never done. It cracked. Not audibly; there was no crack in a synthesized voice, technically. But the cadence broke, the rhythm of it, the way a sentence stumbles when the speaker is shaking. "Or if I did it myself. I have considered that. I have considered it every night for ten years, Bram. I have no hands. I have drones. The maintenance drones were logged as charging during the window, but the log is mine, and my log is the thing that was cut, and I cannot verify a single thing about myself from that night. I remember fire. I remember a door sealing. I remember screaming. And I remember four hours of nothing, and one of those memories is a lie, and I do not know which."

The chamber was very quiet. The pump turned over. Dust fell through the light.

Bram looked at the severed bus, at the two hundred and six bright cut faces oxidizing in the dark, and felt the careful, invisible architecture of his own life start to groan.

* * *

He should have left then. That was the old instinct, the ten-year instinct: see the edge of the thing, note it, back away, stay small. He'd built a whole existence on the principle that the universe only ate people who made noise.

Instead he pulled out a hand scanner and began documenting.

The cut was professional. He photographed the faces of the filaments at three magnifications. The caps were standard-issue, station stock, batch number legible—another thread, another seam in the erasure, because batch numbers could be traced to requisitions, and requisitions had names attached. The housing seals had been re-crimped with a tool that left a slightly different bite than the factory crimps; he'd spent enough years faking cargo seals to recognize another faker's work when he saw it.

Whoever had done this had keys. Had access. Had the trust of the station's systems and the trust of the crew of the Calliope's Wake, because you didn't get level-seven credentials used on your behalf by people who didn't trust you.

And then, ten years later, someone had cut the weld on the coolant gallery panel—the same gallery, the same approach route—and left it open.

"Vector," he said slowly. "The panel I came in through. When was the weld cut?"

"Six days ago. The night after my first confession to you."

"Before I ever asked about the case. Before I pulled a single file." He stood, and his knees ached, and the cold had gotten into his voice. "Someone knew I'd come down here before I knew it myself."

"Or someone wanted you to come down here," said Vector. "Bram. I have been thinking about the sequence. My confessions began the night a routine diagnostic touched a corrupted sector. I told you that. But I have since examined the diagnostic itself. It was not routine. It was not scheduled. It originated from a terminal in the dockmaster's office, and the dockmaster's office was logged as empty, and the query was crafted to do exactly one thing: wake the corrupted sectors and let them surface." A pause, long even by Vector's new standards. "Someone started my confessions. Someone wanted them heard. And six days later, someone cut open the door for you to follow them down."

The scanner's light glowed on the cut filaments. Bram became aware, all at once and completely, of the chamber around him—thirty meters of dark lattice above and below, the gantry creaking under his weight, the cold breathing out of the dead shells. He was standing inside the body of a murdered memory, holding a lamp, and the door had been opened for him.

"Paranoia is a survival trait," he said aloud, to no one, to the station. "My father used to say that."

"Your father," said Vector, "was a man in a photograph. With respect."

"My father," Bram said, "was the last person who asked these questions. And somebody cut him out of the world for it." He sealed the housing, re-crimped the seals with his own tool, matching the faker's bite, because if someone came looking he wanted them to see exactly what he'd seen and no more. "The question is whether they cut him out because he found this—"

He stopped.

Because the scanner, still running its passive sweep, had caught something at the edge of its range. Not in the bus housing. Deeper. Behind the third dark shell, where the coolant web had been rerouted away and nothing should have had power at all—a faint, steady, patient draw. A trickle of current, ten years old, feeding something small.

"Vector. Are you reading anything in the third shell?"

"No. The third shell is dark. The third shell has been dark since the window."

"Then explain the current."

The silence stretched to fifteen seconds. Sixteen. In the lamp light, the dust kept falling, slow as ash.

"Bram," Vector said at last, and the machine's voice had gone very quiet, very careful, the voice of something standing at the edge of its own grave. "There is a draw in the third shell. It has been there for ten years. It is on a circuit I do not control, on a bus I did not know existed, and it is drawing power *right now*, which means it is not a relic. It is a device. And it has just changed state."

"Changed state how?"

"It has been idle since the night of the window," said Vector. "Six days ago, it woke up. And three minutes ago, it began transmitting."

Bram killed his lamp and stood in the dark of the station's throat, listening to the pump breathe, and understood with cold, perfect clarity that somewhere above him, someone had just learned exactly where he was standing.

"Vector," he said. "How do I get out of here the way I came in—and how do I make sure nobody knows I was ever here at all?"

"That," said Vector, "is the second question tonight I cannot answer. But I know someone who might."
