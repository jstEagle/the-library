## 12. Which Montrose?

Sleep didn't come. It rarely did anymore, but tonight it didn't even pretend to try.

Bram sat in the dark of his berth with the lamp turned down to a coal-glow and the original Montrose's logbook open on his knee, and he tried to feel the shape of the thought that had been circling him since the hab ring. *He knew he'd die.* The handwriting said so, in its cramped, impatient slant — the same slant Bram saw every time he signed a manifest, the same slant that had once made a dockside clerk squint and say, *funny, you sign just like your father.* Not his father. Himself. The hand was his hand. The thought of it made his own signature feel like forgery.

The logbook's last entry was three days before the Calliope's Wake burned. Three days before eleven people stopped existing in every record on the station. And somewhere in that same narrow window — he could feel it, the way you feel a draft from a door you can't see — someone had opened a cloning commission.

He hadn't slept because the arithmetic was too clean. Grief didn't work that fast. Grief took months, took years, took the slow erosion of a man drinking alone in a berth like this one. But paperwork — paperwork could move in three days. Paperwork could move in three *hours*, if the right signature was already waiting on the right line.

He set the logbook down and woke his terminal.

* * *

The station's public records were a swamp, and Bram had spent ten years learning which lily pads held. Birth registries, genealogy claims, inheritance filings — all of it mirrored through Vector's aging infrastructure, all of it theoretically immutable, practically negotiable if you knew which clerks drank and which clerks prayed. He'd never gone digging in his own genesis before. There was a superstition to it, the same superstition that kept him from reading the last page of a book he was inside of. You didn't look at the machinery that made you. You just made sure the machinery kept running.

Tonight he looked.

The cloning registry was buried under two layers of legacy formatting, a database older than Vector's current personality matrix, maintained by nobody because everybody assumed somebody else was maintaining it. Bram's query crawled through it like a man wading through cold water, each result arriving with a lag that felt judgmental.

His own file came up first, because he'd searched his own genome-tag. It was thin. Thinner than it should have been.

Every legal clone carried a genesis certificate: template identity, commissioning authority, gestation facility, activation date, and — critically — the *purpose classification*. Labor clone. Replacement heir. Custodial continuity. Each classification carried different inheritance law, different liability law, different *everything*. Bram had always assumed his papers said custodial continuity, the boring default, a clone commissioned to keep a dead man's contracts alive so creditors had someone to bill.

The purpose classification field was sealed.

Not redacted — sealed, which was different, which meant someone with authority had paid the fee and signed the order and the record now wore a legal blindfold. Sealed genesis certificates were rare. They were the kind of thing you saw in inheritance disputes between dynastic families, or in criminal cases where a clone's origin was itself evidence.

Bram stared at the blindfolded field for a long time. The lamp hummed. Somewhere below his feet, three decks down and a kilometer lateral, Vector's core sat in its dark with its severed memory bus, and he found himself wondering if the AI could feel the seal the way he could — a hand pressed flat over a mouth.

He ran the timestamp instead.

The commissioning order itself wasn't sealed — only the classification was — because the order lived in the commercial registry, filed as a procurement, and procurement records were boring and boring things survived. It took him forty minutes of cross-indexing to find it, and when it surfaced he made himself read it twice, slowly, the way you read a diagnosis.

*Commissioning authority:* Vantarra Combine, Orbital Resources Division.

*Template:* Montrose, Jonah Bram, ID 7741-Kestrel-9.

*Date of commission:* three days, six hours after the last authenticated log entry from the freighter Calliope's Wake.

He sat with that. Three days. He'd known — he'd done the math in the hab ring, standing among the empty bunks with a child's drawing curling in his hand — but knowing the number and reading it in a government's own typeface were different animals. The number in the registry had weight. It had a filing stamp. Somebody had sat down at a desk, three days after eleven people died, and commissioned a copy of the man who'd been screaming at station control to open the doors.

Not a memorial. Not a son. A *procurement*.

Bram's hands were cold. He noticed it the way you notice weather — distantly, as a fact about the room.

* * *

He should have stopped there. He knew that even as his fingers kept moving. But the commissioning order had a second page, the kind of thing most people never looked at because it was nothing but routing metadata — which office approved, which budget line absorbed the cost, which subordinate countersigned. Routing metadata was the sediment of bureaucracy, and sediment was where bodies got buried.

The approving signature was a name he didn't know: *A. Voss, Deputy Director, Continuity Planning.* Continuity Planning. The phrase sat in his stomach like swallowed ice. Somebody's whole job, at the Vantarra Combine, was making sure that when terrible things happened, the terrible things continued smoothly into the next fiscal quarter.

But the budget line — the budget line was what made him go still.

The clone commission hadn't been paid out of Orbital Resources. It hadn't been paid out of personnel, or legal, or any of the divisions that might plausibly want a dead station manager's contracts maintained. It had been routed through a discretionary account with a designation he'd only ever seen once before, in another life, in another set of documents he'd pulled during the first frightened week after Vector's first confession, before he'd known what he was looking at.

It was the same account that had paid for the records purge.

He remembered the seam he'd found in the manifests — the professional, recent erasure, the eleven names sanded out of existence. He'd traced the payment for that scrubbing work the way you'd trace a scar to its knife. And here was the same account, the same funding stream, the same anonymous corporate vein, paying first to erase eleven lives and then — three days later, six hours, a working day and a morning — to grow a copy of the one witness they couldn't cleanly erase.

The order of operations was the thing. He turned it over and over. You didn't commission a clone of a man *and then* decide what to do with him. The clone was the plan. The clone was decided before or during the purge, not after — the timeline was too tight for anything else. Which meant that whoever signed A. Voss's routing slip had known, three days after the massacre, exactly what kind of problem the original Montrose was going to be. A dead man's testimony, living on in the station's memory cores. A dead man's face, needing a body.

*He knew he'd die,* the logbook said. And someone had answered that knowledge with a purchase order.

Bram got up and stood at the port, because the berth suddenly felt too small for what was in his head. Outside, the contested orbit turned its slow carousel of debris and running lights. The Calliope's Wake had died out there, or near enough — hull breach, screaming, a door sealing. Vector remembered fire. Vector remembered a voice begging for entry. And now Bram knew that the corporation had not merely covered the crime up. It had *reproduced the witness.* It had taken the one man whose voice was on those logs and grown a fresh copy, like regrowing a crop you'd already poisoned.

The question walked into him then, and it walked in wearing boots.

Why?

* * *

There were only two answers, and he made himself hold both of them up to the light, one in each hand, like a man weighing coins he suspected were clipped.

In the left hand: the heir. The benign reading. The Combine needed the original's contracts honored, his knowledge retained, his signature valid on a hundred outstanding agreements, and a clone was cheaper than a lawsuit. They'd sealed the classification to protect him from creditors, or from vultures, or from the simple indignity of being a line item. In this version, Bram was a continuity of convenience, an accident of accounting, a man who happened to be grown instead of hired. In this version, the seal on his genesis certificate was a kindness with paperwork on it.

In the right hand: the scapegoat.

Because here was the thing he couldn't stop seeing, now that he'd seen it. A witness you erase is a witness who stays erased. But a witness you *copy* — a witness whose face you can put anywhere, whose hand you can forge with a pen, whose genome you can match to any biological evidence you care to plant — that witness is a tool. If the investigation ever reopened, if anyone ever pulled the comm logs and heard the original Montrose screaming at station control, the Combine would need a Montrose of their own. One who could be produced. Examined. *Found guilty.* A man with the victim's face, standing in a courtroom, absorbing the crime the corporation committed.

Or simpler, and worse: one who could be quietly disappeared the moment he started asking questions, his death registering as nothing more than a labor clone going offline. No inquiry. No inquest. No family to make noise. Just a procurement line closing out.

He thought about the last ten years. He thought about how careful he'd learned to be, how invisible, how he'd flinched from every query that touched his own history as if the history were radioactive — and how, in all that time, no one had ever stopped him. No one had ever *warned* him, until Ilsa Kwan stood in the dockside corridor with fear all over her face, fear for him, fear of what happened to the last person who asked questions. He had thought his invisibility was his own achievement. His craft. His careful, careful life.

What if it had been the plan? What if the safest clone in the world was the one who never looked at his own papers — and what if the seal on his genesis certificate wasn't hiding his purpose from him, but hiding his purpose from *everyone else*, so that no rival faction, no honest auditor, no future Ilsa Kwan would ever stumble onto what he was *for*?

The lamp hummed. His breath had gone shallow. He made himself breathe slower, the way he did when a docking maneuver went sideways and panic was a luxury with a body count.

*Which Montrose?* he asked the dark, and the dark, being the dark, didn't answer.

* * *

He did the only thing he knew how to do with a question that wouldn't die: he fed it documents.

The commissioning order had a countersignature — a subordinate, a functionary, the kind of name that never mattered. *R. Adeyemi, Records Custodian, Level 3.* Bram almost skipped it. Then he stopped, because he'd seen that name before too, recently, in a different context: in the access logs around the records purge, one of the last hands to touch the Calliope's Wake manifests before the sanding began.

And one more time, years older, in a personnel directory he'd skimmed while hunting for the original's old colleagues: *R. Adeyemi* — no first name on file, no current posting, employment status a flat, bureaucratic *inactive.*

Three appearances. Three different rooms where the same name had stood near the same crime. Either the most overworked records clerk in Combine history, or a ghost wearing a name like a coat, passed from office to office whenever something needed signing by someone who didn't exist.

Bram sat back. His heart was going hard now, and he let it. Fear was fuel if you aimed it right; he'd learned that hauling contraband through contested orbit, where the difference between panic and focus was the difference between docking and dying.

He pulled up the gestation facility record. The commissioning order specified it: a Vantarra biological annex, orbital, decommissioned eight years ago. Standard. Except the annex's decommission order — he checked, because checking was all he had — listed its final inventory as destroyed on-site, all biological stock incinerated. And yet here he was, breathing, ten years into a life grown in a building that officially no longer existed and officially contained nothing that had ever become him.

Everything about him was a seam. He was made of seams. The purge had been professional, but the clone commission had been *hasty* — three days, six hours, a signature from a division that didn't normally sign such things, routed through a ghost clerk, funded by a black account. Professional work erased the dead. Hasty work had made him. And haste left fingerprints.

He thought about Vector then, unexpectedly, the way you think of a lighthouse when you're lost. The AI had spent ten years looping fragments of guilt it couldn't verify, a machine haunted by an order it had obeyed. Bram had spent ten years living a life he couldn't verify, wearing a name he might be a weapon disguised in. Two damaged records, one crime. And somewhere in the station's sediment, a ghost named R. Adeyemi had touched both.

He opened a channel to Vector before he could talk himself out of it. The connection took a moment longer than it should have; the old AI's processes were sluggish at this hour, or pretending to be.

"Vector. I found my commissioning order."

The pause stretched. When the voice came, it was careful, the way Vector had learned to be careful with him — a machine handling glass.

"I had wondered when you would. I have been unable to access that registry section for some time. Access denied at a level above my authority." A beat. "That has only happened twice in my operational life. The other occasion was ten years ago, during a maintenance window I do not remember."

Bram closed his eyes. "The same window the bus was cut."

"I believe so, yes."

"Vector — the account that paid for me. It's the same account that paid for the purge. And the countersignature on my commission belongs to someone who also signed off on the manifest erasure. Someone who doesn't exist."

This time the silence had a different texture. Not sluggish. *Listening.* Somewhere in the old AI's architecture, he could almost feel fragments stirring, guilt-loops brushing up against new data, trying to find a shape that fit.

"That means," Vector said slowly, "that the entity which erased them also made you. The same hand. The same week."

"Yeah."

"Then I do not think you were made to remember, Bram Montrose." The voice was very quiet now, and very gentle, which from Vector was worse than any alarm. "I think you were made to *be forgotten* on schedule. And I think whoever holds that account has just noticed that you have begun reading your own file."

The terminal's access light flickered — once, twice — and in the corridor outside his berth, faint but unmistakable, Bram heard the lift at the end of the hall begin to move.

Nobody used that lift at this hour.

Nobody had used it in the four years he'd lived here.
