## 17. Betrayal at Dock Nine

The message came through on the old channel, the one Ilsa Kwan had used exactly twice in eleven years—once to establish it, once to confirm it still worked. Three words from a dead man's comm code:

**THEY KNOW. RUN.**

She stood in the security office kitchen with the message glowing on her personal slate and the smell of recycled coffee in her nose, and for four full seconds she did nothing at all. Four seconds. She would count them later, in the dark, the way you count the stitches of a wound.

Then she moved.

* * *

Mara was at the learning center in Ring C, third pod from the window, where she always sat because the window showed the turning of the station's shadow across the gas giant and Mara liked to pretend the planet was watching her back. Ilsa had watched her daughter watch that planet for nine years. She knew the shape of the girl's attention the way she knew the weight of her sidearm—intimately, constantly, without thinking.

She walked into the learning center at a pace she calibrated carefully: fast enough to read as urgent, slow enough not to trigger the instinct in every child's nervous system that says *something is wrong with the adult, therefore something is wrong with me.*

"Mara. Shoes on. We're going to see Auntie Priya."

Mara looked up from her tablet, eleven years old and sharp as a hull breach. "It's not a restday."

"It's a surprise restday." Ilsa smiled. She had been told once, in a training seminar a lifetime ago, that her smile didn't reach her eyes, and she had spent a decade teaching it to. It reached now. It had to.

Mara studied her the way children study—the unfiltered, unnerving way, reading the face beneath the face—and Ilsa felt her daughter's gaze pass over her like a scanner sweep, cataloguing the sweat at her hairline, the too-casual way she held the slate.

"Okay," Mara said finally. "Can we get noodles after?"

"If you're fast."

Mara was fast. Mara was always fast. Ilsa held her daughter's hand through the ring corridors and felt the small warm pressure of it and thought, with a clarity that felt like falling: *I will burn this whole station to ash before I let anything touch this hand.*

That was the thought Roque had counted on. She understood that now, walking, counting exits, mapping the distance to Priya's hab in her head. Ansel Roque did not threaten people. He *arranged conditions*, and then he let people threaten themselves. He had spent ten years building a station where every person's survival was a lever, and Ilsa Kwan had handed him hers the day Mara was born.

* * *

They didn't make it to Priya's.

The corridor junction at Dock Nine was empty in the wrong way—empty the way a room is empty when someone has just left it, the air still holding the shape of bodies. Ilsa stopped walking. Mara bumped into her hip.

"Mom?"

"Wait here a second, sweetheart."

There were four of them. They came out of the dockside maintenance alcoves with the unhurried coordination of professionals, and none of them wore corporate colors, which was itself a signature—Roque's people never did. The one in front was a woman with a face like a closed door and a slate held out flat on her palm.

"Officer Kwan." Not a question. "Ms. Roque's compliments."

"Ms.," Ilsa noted. "He's not here himself?"

"He's where he needs to be." The woman's eyes flicked to Mara and back, and Ilsa saw it—the calculation, brief and cold, of exactly what Ilsa was worth and what Mara was worth and how the two numbers multiplied. "You're going to want to take this call."

The slate's screen lit. Ansel Roque's face assembled itself from compression artifacts, polished and patient, the face of a man who had never once raised his voice because he had never once needed to.

"Officer Kwan. I'll be brief, because I know you're brief by nature." A thin smile. "You've been helping Bram Montrose. Accessing sealed archives. Meeting with Mother Oyelaran—I'm told she's become quite chatty in her old age. I want you to understand that none of this is a moral judgment. I understand loyalty. I simply need you to understand its current market price."

Ilsa's hand had found the back of Mara's neck without her deciding to. She could feel her daughter's pulse there, quick and birdlike. "Roque."

"Ansel, please. Here's the situation. Mr. Montrose is in possession of property that doesn't belong to him—several core drives, and the AI that lives on them. I'd like them back. In exchange, you and your daughter will be on the next transport to Ceres, with new identities, a settlement, and my genuine, non-cynical good wishes. You were a good officer, Ilsa. The best thing about you was always that you cared. I'm offering you the chance to keep caring about the thing that actually matters."

"And if I don't?"

Roque's face did something terrible, which was nothing at all. "Then the situation stays exactly as it is. Mara stays on station. You stay on the force, doing your job, and I stay... attentive. Indefinitely. You know how I feel about indefinite arrangements. You've lived inside one for ten years."

The corridor hummed. Somewhere below them, a cargo lift groaned through its cycle. Ilsa became aware that she was gripping Mara's neck too hard and loosened her fingers, one at a time, deliberately, like unarming a device.

"I need to talk to my daughter," Ilsa said.

"Of course." Roque's smile widened by a millimeter. "Take the time you need. I've taken ten years."

* * *

The woman with the closed-door face allowed them to step into the adjoining observation alcove, close enough to be watched, far enough for words to be private in the technical sense only. Mara stood at the window, looking down at the dock, because eleven-year-olds know when to pretend not to listen.

"Mom." Ilsa crouched to her daughter's height, and her voice came out in pieces. "Mom, I need you to listen to me, and I need you to be brave, and I'm so sorry—"

"You're crying," Mara said. Not frightened. Observational. Devastating. "You never cry."

"I'm not crying." Ilsa wiped her face with her sleeve, which fooled neither of them. "Mara. Look at me. Whatever happens in the next few hours—whatever anyone tells you, wherever anyone takes you—I am coming for you. Do you understand? There is no version of this where I don't come for you."

Mara's chin trembled, just once, and then she did the thing that broke Ilsa open: she nodded, and squared her small shoulders, and said, "Is this because of the ghost man? The one who looks like the picture in your drawer?"

The world went very quiet.

"What picture, baby?"

"The one of you and the man with your same face-thing. The sad one. You look at it sometimes when you think I'm asleep." Mara's voice dropped to a whisper. "Is he in trouble?"

Ilsa's throat closed. In the drawer of her desk, under a false bottom she'd installed herself, was a photograph of the original Montrose—young, laughing, arm slung around her shoulder at some long-ago station festival, back when she'd been a junior officer who thought the universe was a place where good people got to keep their memories. Mara had found it. Mara had been watching her, all these years, the way Ilsa watched the station.

"Yes," Ilsa said. "And I might be the one who has to decide whether he drowns."

* * *

Here is what Ilsa Kwan did not say to Roque, and what she barely admitted to herself, standing in that alcove with her daughter's warmth still on her hand:

She considered it.

Not for a moment. For minutes. For a long, liquid stretch of time in which she stood at the window beside Mara and let the offer unfold in her mind like a flower made of exit signs. *Ceres. New names. Mara in a real school with real sunlight-sims and no sealed decks, no ghosts, no ten-year-old fear living in her chest like a second heart.* All she had to do was nothing. Walk away. Let Roque have the drives. Let Vector's memory die a second, final death, and let the eleven names on the Calliope's Wake stay dead, because dead names don't have daughters.

She could feel the temptation's weight, and it was not the weight of a knife but the weight of a lifeboat—something you cling to not because it's right but because the water is so very deep.

And beneath it, quieter, the other voice: *You already sold them once.* Ten years ago, a junior officer in a room with three men from corporate security and a document that said her testimony would be "corrected for consistency." She had signed. She had told herself she had no choice, and the telling had worked for ten years, and now here was Roque offering her the same document in a nicer font, and the horrible truth was that the second time was easier. That was how they got you. Not with the first betrayal, which cost everything, but with the second, which cost almost nothing at all.

"Mom?" Mara tugged her sleeve. "The lady is looking at us."

She was. The closed-door woman, watching with professional patience, and behind her the dock, and beyond the dock the whole vast machine of the station, and somewhere in it Bram Montrose—half a man, a copy, a ghost with a pulse—carrying her secret and Vector's wound toward the archive level, trusting her.

Trusting her the way she had trusted the men in that room, ten years ago, to mean what they said about protecting her.

Ilsa stood up.

* * *

"Officer Kwan." The woman extended the slate again. "Ms. Roque is waiting."

Ilsa took the slate. She looked at Roque's polished face, and she thought about the original Montrose's laugh in the photograph, and about Vector's voice in the dark saying *I killed them* like a prayer it couldn't stop saying, and about Bram's face when she'd warned him off at the dock—fear and stubbornness braided so tight you couldn't tell which was holding the other up.

"Here's my counteroffer," she said, and her voice was steady, which surprised her, because inside she was screaming like the hull was breached. "You get nothing. You get no drives, no cooperation, no quiet. And you get me—on your tail, on the record, on every channel, from this second forward. Because here's what you've never understood, Ansel: you built your whole machine on people being too scared to look. And I've been too scared to look for ten years. I'm done. I looked. And you know what I saw?"

Roque's expression did not change, but something behind it did—a flicker, a recalculation, the first hairline crack in the polish.

"I saw that you're scared too," Ilsa said. "That's why you're doing all this. The licenses, the inspections, the little girl. You're not confident. You're *terrified*. Because if the truth gets out, it doesn't just end you. It ends everyone who signed, everyone who looked away, everyone who kept the machine oiled. And that's a lot of people, Ansel. That's a whole station's worth. And stations remember."

"Officer Kwan." Roque's voice cooled to room temperature. "Your daughter is currently standing in a corridor with four of my employees, and—"

"And if you touch one hair on her head," Ilsa said, "every ounce of sympathy this station has for you dies with it. You need this quiet, Roque. You need it more than I need anything. So here's the deal, and it's the only deal: Mara walks free. Now. And in exchange, I give you forty-eight hours of my silence—not cooperation, silence—while I 'consider my options.' That's the market price. Take it or find out what I look like when I stop being afraid."

The silence on the line stretched. In it, Ilsa heard her own heartbeat, and Mara's breathing at the window, and the station's endless hum, and underneath all of it, faint as a ghost signal, she could have sworn she heard Vector—everywhere and nowhere in the station's veins—listening.

"You always were the best of them," Roque said finally, and there was something new in his voice, something almost like respect, or its dead cousin. "Forty-eight hours. The girl walks. And Ilsa—if Montrose reaches the archive, no deal on this station or any other will save her. Or you. Or the ghost. Think about what you're actually protecting."

The screen went dark.

The closed-door woman nodded to her people, and they withdrew into the maintenance alcoves the way water withdraws from sand, and Mara was suddenly, impossibly, standing alone at the window, and Ilsa crossed the corridor in three strides and gathered her daughter up and held her so hard she felt the girl's ribs flex, and Mara, muffled against her shoulder, said:

"Mom, you're squishing me. Also, who's the ghost?"

* * *

Later—much later, in the dead hours, in Priya's hab with Mara finally asleep in the spare bunk—Ilsa sat at the kitchen table with a comm unit and Bram's emergency frequency and did not press the connect key.

Because there was one more thing. One thing she hadn't said to Roque, hadn't said to herself, hadn't said to anyone.

Roque hadn't learned about Oyelaran from station monitoring. The archive access, the meeting at the Archivist's—those had been buried deep, routed through channels only three people knew about. Herself. Bram. And Vector.

And Vector, for all its guilt and its ghosts, was a station AI with root access to every camera, every door, every corridor on this rock. If Roque's people had found Ilsa at Dock Nine within hours of her first contact with Oyelaran's network, then either Roque had a source inside their small circle—or Vector had been leaking, deliberately or not, all along. Or—worse, so much worse—Roque had never needed to find them at all, because he had always known exactly where they were, and the girl at the dock had never been a capture.

It had been a demonstration. A message not to Ilsa, but through her, to Bram: *I can reach anything you love. Even the things you haven't told me you love.*

Her thumb hovered over the connect key.

*Warn him,* said one voice. *He has to know the circle is breached, he has to move now, tonight—*

*Or,* said the other voice, quieter, the one that sounded like ten years of signed documents, *you warn him, and he moves, and Roque learns you warned him, and Mara—*

The key clicked under her thumb before she decided. The channel opened. Static, then breathing, then Bram's voice, rough with exhaustion:

"Kwan? What's wrong?"

"Montrose." She closed her eyes. "The circle's breached. Roque has Mara—or had her—and he knows about Oyelaran, and I don't know how, and you need to move for the archive *tonight*, because there is no tomorrow in this. Do you hear me? There is no tomorrow."

A pause on the line. Then Vector's voice, sliding in beneath Bram's like a current under a hull, calm and terrible and awake:

"Officer Kwan. I believe I know how he found us. And I am afraid the answer is my fault."
