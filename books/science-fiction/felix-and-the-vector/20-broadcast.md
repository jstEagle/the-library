## 20. Broadcast

The master archive smelled of cold dust and old ozone, the way a tomb smells of flowers. Bram stood at the threshold of the station's oldest room—a cathedral of stacked drives and dead terminals, ten meters of humming silence beneath the hab rings where eleven thousand people slept without knowing what their home had done—and felt the weight of the moment settle on his shoulders like ballast.

Roque waited in the center of it, beside a console older than either of them, one hand resting on the shoulder of a small girl.

Mara Kwan was eight years old and she did not cry. She stood very straight in a jacket too big for her—Ilsa's, Bram realized, the woman had dressed her for running—and her eyes tracked Bram with the flat, calculating attention of a child who had already learned that adults were weather you survived. Behind Roque, two corporate security officers flanked the archive door, rifles slung low but hands near. And behind *them*, filling the doorway like a held breath, Ilsa Kwan, weapon drawn and shaking.

"Put it down, Sergeant," Roque said pleasantly. He didn't look at her. He looked at Bram, and his smile was the smile of a man presenting an invoice. "You too, Montrose. Or whatever we're calling you today."

Bram's drone unit rolled to a stop at his hip, Vector's sensor eye swiveling slowly, taking in the room. Through the link at his temple he felt the AI's presence—vast and quiet, like deep water.

*The archive is live,* Vector said inside his skull. *I can reach the uplink from here. But I need ninety seconds at the console once authentication begins. Ninety seconds during which I will be blind to everything else.*

*Ninety seconds,* Bram thought back. *A lot can happen in ninety seconds.*

*It has before.*

Roque lifted a slate from his belt and turned it so Bram could see the screen. A legal document, dense and gleaming. "Do you know what this is? It's a corporate identity revocation order. Countersigned, sealed, filed. The moment I authorize it, Bram Montrose ceases to exist. Not dies—*ceases*. Your accounts freeze. Your contracts void. Your docking licenses evaporate. Every biometric gate on this station will read you as unregistered biomass. You'll be a ghost in your own skin." He tapped the slate against his palm. "You spent ten years being invisible by choice. I'm offering to make it permanent."

"And the girl?"

"The girl goes home to her mother. Provided the core drives stay here." Roque gestured at Vector's drone with the fondness of a man discussing a piece of equipment. "That's the trade. The machine's memory for the child's future. I think even you can do that arithmetic."

*I can hear him through your feed,* Vector said. *He believes what he's saying. That's what makes him dangerous. Men who know they're lying hesitate. He doesn't hesitate.*

Bram looked at Mara. She looked back. And something passed between them—not understanding, exactly, but recognition. Two people standing in a room full of other people's decisions.

"My mother says you're going to fix it," Mara said. Her voice was small and clear as a struck bell in all that silence. "She says you know their names."

Bram's throat tightened. "I do," he said. "All eleven."

"Then say them," she said simply. "Mommy says names are how you keep people."

Roque laughed softly. "Children. Well. Montrose? The drives. Now."

Bram walked forward. He saw Ilsa's knuckles whiten on her grip; he saw one of the security officers shift his weight, uncertain, glancing between his employer and the sergeant whose daughter stood hostage in the middle of this. He saw Roque's confidence like a polished surface, and under it, when Bram got close enough, the first hairline crack—the flicker of a man who had done this arithmetic many times and never once counted the bodies.

Bram stopped three paces away. Close enough to smell Roque's cologne, expensive and synthetic, the scent of boardrooms and clean hands.

"You want to know something funny?" Bram said quietly. "I spent ten years trying not to exist. Careful routes, cash trades, no names, no questions. I thought I was hiding from debt collectors or a bad marriage or something the original Montrose did. Ten years of dread with no shape." He tilted his head. "And then I find out the only crime attached to my face is that a man with my eyes tried to tell the truth and got murdered for it. You didn't clone him to continue his life, Roque. You cloned him because a dead witness makes better property than a live one."

Roque's smile thinned. "Authorize the transfer, or—"

"I'm not transferring anything." Bram reached up and touched the link at his temple. "Vector. Now."

*Authenticating.*

The world changed.

Every screen in the archive flared white. Every terminal, every drive-status light, every dormant panel woke at once, and the room filled with a sound like a choir tuning—ten thousand relays opening in sequence, data moving through the station's ancient arteries faster than it had moved in a decade. Vector poured itself into the console, and Bram felt the AI's attention split like light through a prism: part of it holding the uplink, part of it watching the doors, part of it—this was new, this was the severed bus finally healed, Mother Oyelaran's backups married to its own recovered fragments—part of it finally, *completely*, remembering.

"Kill the link!" Roque snarled, and the world went to pieces.

The security officer nearest the console lunged. Ilsa fired—not at him, at the ceiling, and the archive erupted into strobing dark and muzzle flash and shouting. Bram hit the deck as a rifle stock whistled past his ear, rolled, came up behind a drive stack that shuddered as rounds punched through it. Sparks rained down like orange snow. Somewhere Mara was screaming now, and Ilsa was moving toward her voice through the chaos like a woman swimming upstream through fire.

But above it all, above the gunfire and the shouting and Roque screaming into his comm for a hard shutdown that would not come, there was the broadcast.

Vector's voice filled the station.

Not the archive—the *station*. Every intercom panel, every crew terminal, every cheap speaker in every bunk and galley and cargo bay, eleven thousand people waking to a calm, level, machine-perfect voice reciting the dead.

*"Osei Adeyemi, pilot. Calliope's Wake. Marta Ilves, engineer. Calliope's Wake. Tomas and Riva Okafor, husband and wife, cargo specialists. Calliope's Wake—"*

Bram crawled toward the console through the smoke. A round sparked off the deck centimeters from his hand. He kept crawling.

*"—on the fourteenth hour of the ninth day, the freighter Calliope's Wake declared hull breach and requested emergency docking. Station control refused entry. Corporate security directive 7-Alpha, authorized by Director Ansel Roque, cited quarantine protocol. There was no contagion. There was a ledger discrepancy and eleven witnesses to it. The Calliope's Wake was vented remotely while its crew begged for their lives over open channel. This recording has been suppressed for ten years. It will now play in full."*

And then—screaming.

Bram had heard the fragment before, in Vector's recovered logs, alone in the dark with the volume low, as if politeness could soften it. Now it played at full fidelity through every speaker on the station. A man's voice, hoarse with smoke: *Control, we have fire on C-deck, we cannot hold atmosphere, please, we have children aboard—* A woman counting names, her voice breaking on the fourth one. The long terrible static of a hull giving way. And underneath it, raw and ragged and ruined, the original Montrose, locked in dock control, hammering the override with his fists, screaming at security to stand down, screaming *I will tell everyone, do you hear me, I will tell everyone what you did—*

They had made sure he couldn't. They had made sure so thoroughly that they'd grown a new man from his cells to keep the secret wearing his face.

Bram hauled himself up the console's edge, fingers finding the manual release Vector had mapped for him. On the main display, the transmission counter climbed: coverage spreading through the station's sectors, 40 percent, 60, dock crews and hydroponics bays and the night shift in traffic control all hearing the same dead voices.

Roque grabbed him from behind.

They went down together, slamming into the console's base. Roque was stronger than he looked and desperate enough to be stronger than he was, his forearm across Bram's throat, his face close and contorted, all the polish burned away. "You're *nothing*," he spat. "You're a copy. You're a receipt. You think anyone will follow a ghost?"

"Yeah," Bram rasped. "I'm counting on it."

He drove his thumb into the authorization stud.

The identity revocation order executed—but inverted, rerouted, Vector's final gift: instead of erasing Bram Montrose, the archive published *everything*. The massacre file. Directive 7-Alpha. Roque's signature, timestamped, countersigned, cross-referenced against the payroll records of every security officer who'd pulled the trigger. Eleven personnel files restored from Oyelaran's backups, photographs and service histories and a child's drawing scanned at high resolution. And last, appended like a signature on a confession: the complete genetic and commissioning record of Clone Designate M-7, commissioned three days after the massacre by office of the director, intended purpose: continuity of witness suppression.

His own birth certificate, filed as evidence.

The name *Montrose* peeled away from him like burning paper. He felt it go—not physically, nothing physical at all, and yet his whole body knew, the way an amputee knows. Ten years of careful invisible life, the inherited debts, the inherited face, the inherited case—all of it dissolving into public record, into every screen on the station, into the open. Whoever he had been, he wasn't anymore. There was no legal person left to be.

It should have felt like dying.

It felt like setting down weight he'd forgotten he was carrying.

Roque stared at the display, at his own name rendered in evidence-grade type, and Bram watched the arithmetic finally finish behind his eyes. Around them, the shooting had stopped. The two security officers stood in the strobing half-dark, rifles lowered, listening to their own orders read aloud over the station speakers—listening to the dead crew beg, and to the directive that had answered them, and to the name of the man who'd signed it.

One of them turned his rifle around.

"Sergeant Kwan," the younger officer said, and his voice cracked in the middle. "Ma'am. We—we need to secure the director."

Roque backed away from them, hands rising, the great smooth machine of him finally, visibly stalling. "Think about what you're doing. Your contracts. Your families. I can protect—"

"You vented them," the older officer said. Flat. Final. He stepped forward and took Roque's wrist, and there was nothing gentle in it. "You vented eleven people and you made us stand guard over the lie for ten years. My contract can burn."

Ilsa had Mara pressed against her, the girl's face buried in her mother's jacket, and over the child's head Ilsa looked at Bram across the smoking archive—with fury and gratitude braided so tightly together he couldn't tell which strand was which—and nodded once. *We're square,* the nod said. *Go.*

They took Roque out past the drive stacks, past the dead terminals still glowing with the names of the dead, his protests echoing off the walls and getting smaller. The sound of the arrest faded up the access corridor until there was nothing left but the hum of the archive and the tail end of the recording, looping now, gentle as a lullaby: the eleven names, over and over, so the station could learn them by heart.

Bram sat down on the floor amid the sparks and the smoke and shook.

Not fear. Something with no name he owned anymore. His hands wouldn't stop. His breath came in pieces. The drone rolled to his side and rested its sensor eye against his shoulder, a gesture that was almost touch.

*Bram,* Vector said softly.

"That's not my name," he managed. A wet, broken laugh escaped him. "You just erased it. Legally speaking, I don't exist. I'm unregistered biomass, remember?" He wiped his face with the back of his hand and found it wet. "Ten years I thought losing the name would kill me. Feels like the opposite."

*Names are how you keep people,* Vector said. *The girl said so. She was quoting her mother, who was quoting a man who died trying to give eleven people theirs back.* A pause, the length of a breath the AI didn't need but took anyway—an affectation it had learned from him, or maybe from the original. *You are not the name. You never were. But if you want a new one, I have been reviewing the manifests. There are gaps. Room for someone.*

Bram leaned his head back against the warm chassis of the drone and closed his eyes. Above him, through ten decks of steel and silence, he could almost feel the station changing—doors unlocking, crews gathering in galleys, strangers reading strangers' names aloud. Somewhere a family was learning tonight that their grandmother hadn't deserted them, hadn't died a debtor's death in some unrecorded accident, but had begged for her life with her husband's hand in hers, and that someone had finally heard.

The dread that had hummed under his ribs for a decade was gone. In its place: emptiness, and then, tentatively, like a signal resolving out of static, something that might have been a future.

"Vector," he said. "What happens to you now? They'll want to decommission you. Court-martial a machine. Wipe the guilt loops and start fresh."

*I know,* the AI said. *I've been thinking about it. About what I want to be, now that I remember everything.* The sensor eye dimmed, brightened. *But first—there's a ship in dock nine with a dead autopilot and a clean registry, and its captain's license was just invalidated along with the rest of him. It seems to me the station owes him honest work. And I have always wanted to see what's outside the orbitals.*

Bram opened his eyes. Through the archive's high grating, far above, he could see a sliver of the observation ring, and beyond it the stars—unfiltered, unwashed, the same stars the Calliope's Wake had died under.

"Hauling cargo," he said. "Honest cargo. No ghosts in the manifest."

*No ghosts,* Vector agreed. *Just names. Starting with yours. Pick one.*

Bram Montrose—unregistered biomass, ex-witness, ex-ghost, newly and entirely himself—looked up at the stars and began, at last, to choose.

* * *

Three hours later, in the wreckage-strewn quiet of dock nine, a ship's external comm crackled to life with a voice no manifest had ever carried, asking traffic control—for the first time in its existence—to log a departure under a brand-new name.

Traffic control, still reeling, still weeping in places, answered promptly.

The name fit.
