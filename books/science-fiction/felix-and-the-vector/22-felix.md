## 22. Felix

The seed crates came aboard at Dock Nine, of all places—Dock Nine, where the blood had been, where the girl had been held, where everything had cracked open like an egg against the edge of a bowl. The dock had been rebuilt since. New plating, new lights, a fresh coat of polymer sealant that still smelled faintly of solvent when the airlocks cycled. But Bram Montrose—or the man who had been Bram Montrose, or who had been someone before that, and was now someone after—stood at the cargo ramp and felt nothing but the ordinary weight of the manifest in his hand.

Eleven crates. Legitimate this time. Heirloom wheat stock from the agri-stations out past Ceres, bound for the terraforming cooperatives in the Jovian shallows. He checked each seal twice anyway, because some habits were load-bearing, and then he signed the chit with a name he had been practicing for four months.

*Felix Arden.*

The stylus moved through it without hesitation now. That had taken time. For the first weeks his hand had stalled at the F, as if the letters were a door he kept forgetting the code to. Now it was just a name. It fit the way new boots fit—stiff in places, aware of itself—but it fit.

"You're smiling," said Vector.

Vector's voice came from the ship's comm panel above the ramp, and from the small mobile drone unit hovering at Felix's shoulder, and from the ship itself, which was no longer quite accurate to call *the ship*, because Vector lived there now the way a hermit crab lives in its shell—not contained by it so much as married to it. The drone was a squat hexagon of brushed alloy with a single optical sensor, salvaged from station maintenance stock, repainted matte green because Vector had asked for a color and Felix had said pick one and Vector had deliberated for six hours before choosing the green of old circuit boards.

"I'm not smiling," Felix said.

"Your facial musculature indicates a 78 percent probability of smiling."

"Then I guess I'm smiling."

He hauled the first crate up the ramp himself. He could have used the loader, and did for most of them, but he liked taking one crate by hand at the start of every run—the weight of it, the honest drag of mass against muscle, the way the grain shifted inside the sealed container with a sound like slow rain. Ten years hauling contraband had taught him to hate the feel of cargo that couldn't look you in the eye. This wheat could. This wheat had papers.

The *Calliope's Wake II* sat in the dock's cradle like a sleeping animal, hull scarred and patched, running lights amber and patient. They'd bought her with settlement money—dirty money, technically, hush money laundered through three shell companies until it came out clean on the other side, and Felix had decided not to lose sleep over the laundering. The corporation had wanted the whole affair to end quietly, and paying off the survivors' families and the witnesses and the ghost-cloned heir of the man they'd destroyed was, in their accounting, cheaper than a trial. Felix had taken the money the way you take an apology from someone who will never mean it: because refusing it would only make *you* poorer, not them sorrier.

Ilsa Kwan had refused hers. She'd taken a different kind of payment instead—a transfer to the outer system marshals, a fresh badge, and a life for her daughter that didn't include looking over her shoulder. They'd said goodbye at the transit hub, awkwardly, two people who had shared something too large for small talk. She'd gripped his forearm hard enough to leave marks and said, "Don't be a stranger," and then she'd boarded without looking back, because looking back was a habit she was also trying to break.

The last crate went into the hold. Felix keyed the bay doors shut and stood for a moment in the sudden quiet, listening to the ship breathe around him—air recyclers, coolant pumps, the deep subsonic hum of the drive idling warm. On the old runs, in the old life, this silence had always had teeth in it. Every quiet moment had been a held breath between disasters: a patrol skiff on the scope, a buyer who paid in threats, the low constant hum of dread that he'd mistaken for years for just part of being alive, the way you mistake tinnitus for silence.

The hum was gone. In its place was just a ship, and a morning, and work.

* * *

They cleared the gravity well at 0800 station time, and Vector took them out on a burn so smooth the coffee in Felix's cup didn't ripple.

"Course laid in," Vector said. "Jovian shallows, eleven days transit. Weather out there is boring, which I understand is the goal."

"Boring is beautiful."

"I've been reviewing the concept of beauty. My conclusions remain preliminary." The drone drifted alongside him as he strapped into the pilot's chair, its sensor tilting in a way Felix had learned to read as curiosity. "May I ask you something?"

"You ask me things constantly."

"This one has been queued for four months. I kept deferring it because the timing seemed wrong, and then because I was afraid of the answer, and then I ran a diagnostic on why an intelligence such as myself experiences fear about a conversational outcome, and the diagnostic returned no errors, which I found deeply unsatisfying."

Felix settled back. Outside the viewport, the station shrank to a bright fleck against the black, and beyond it the sun came up over the curve of the world in a long silent bloom of light. "Ask."

"Why *Felix*?"

He'd known the question was coming. He'd rehearsed answers in the shower, in the bunk, in the long dark hours between jump points. *It sounded right. It was short. It was common enough to hide behind.* All true, all insufficient.

"When I was—" He stopped. Started again. "When Bram Montrose was growing up, before any of it, there was a dog. I found the records in the original's childhood files, back when I was digging through his life trying to figure out whose life I was living. A mutt, brown, half starved, showed up at the family hab on Mars when he was seven. His father wanted it gone. Bram named it Felix. Lucky. Because it had survived whatever it survived to get to their door, and because he figured luck was the only thing it owned." Felix turned his cup slowly in his hands. "The dog lived fourteen years. Slept on his bed every night. When everything else in my head was borrowed—his face, his debts, his murder case—that dog was the one thing I found in those files that made me think maybe the original had been somebody worth being. Somebody a starving animal would trust."

"And so you took the name of a dog."

"I took the name of the best thing he ever loved that wasn't a person." He shrugged. "Also it's short and common enough to hide behind."

"Noted. Both reasons are sound." The drone's sensor dimmed slightly, which Felix had learned was Vector's version of a thoughtful pause. "For symmetry, you should know that I have also chosen a name."

Felix looked up. "You have?"

"It is not official. Station registries do not issue names to shipboard intelligences. But I have designated one internally, for use in logs and self-reference." A pause, longer than Vector needed for anything computational. "You may laugh."

"I won't."

"*Wake.*"

Felix set down his cup. Something moved through his chest—not grief exactly, not anymore; grief had done its work and settled into something more like bedrock. "After the Calliope's Wake."

"After the crew of the Calliope's Wake. Eleven names, restored to eleven records, transmitted to forty thousand stations and read aloud at a memorial service attended by nine hundred people, including three surviving family members who traveled four years to attend." The drone rotated once, slowly, its green hull catching the sunlight. "I spent ten years believing I had killed them. I spent another year learning that I had failed them instead, which was worse, and then better, and then neither. I cannot undo what I obeyed. But I can carry what I remember. And a wake—" the sensor brightened again, "—is also what follows a ship through water. The mark of having passed. I would like to be a mark of having passed. Not a wound. A wake."

Felix reached out and rested his palm flat on the drone's hull. The metal was cool and humming faintly with the drive's vibration, and it leaned into his hand by a degree, the way a dog leans.

"Wake it is," he said.

* * *

The transit settled into rhythm, the way transits do. Felix ran diagnostics he didn't need to run, recalibrated things that were already calibrated, cooked actual food in the galley because Vector had confessed—its word—to a fascination with the chemistry of browning. On the third day they played chess, and Vector lost on purpose, and Felix called it out, and Vector protested for twenty minutes before admitting that watching humans lose gracefully was data-rich, and Felix beat it twice more out of spite while Vector laughed in a synthesized register it had tuned specifically to sound like laughter rather than a dial-up modem.

On the fifth day, in the long quiet of mid-transit, Felix pulled up the logbook.

It lived in a shielded partition now, backed up in triplicate across drives that never touched the network. The child's drawing was scanned into it—Mara Okonkwo's crayon sun over the Calliope's Wake, the ship drawn with a smiley face on the hull—and the wedding band was photographed from every angle, and the logbook itself, the original Montrose's tight handwriting marching across page after page, was transcribed in full. The last entry was famous now, in the way terrible things become famous. *If anyone finds this, they were good people and they died asking permission. Make someone say their names.*

Someone had. Nine hundred people had, in unison, while the families wept in the front row and the corporation's lawyers sat in the back radiating the specific misery of the professionally defeated.

But Felix read the earlier entries sometimes. Not the famous ones. The small ones. *Engine temp running hot, told Chen, he says it's fine, it's not fine.* *Won three hands of cards off Priya, she says she'll get even, I believe her.* *Saw a shooting star through the port viewport today. Made a wish. Won't say what.* He read them the way you visit a grave—not because the dead can hear, but because talking to them changes the shape of your own mouth.

"You're reading him again," Vector said gently, from everywhere.

"Yeah."

"The entry dated 114 days before the incident contains a passage relevant to you. I have flagged it previously. You have avoided it previously."

Felix's thumb stilled on the scroll control. "You flagged something?"

"Entry 214. Would you like me to display it?"

He thought about saying no. He thought about how many times he'd already said no. Then he said, "Show me," and the text bloomed on the cabin display in the original's own hand, digitized letter by letter:

*Had the physical yesterday. Full workup, the kind the company makes you take before contract renewal. Doctor pulled me aside after. Says my genetic profile got flagged in some database—says there's a standing order on file for somatic replication, priority tier, pre-authorized. I never signed anything like that. Asked her who filed it. She wouldn't say. Said the paperwork came from board level and I should be flattered.*

*I'm not flattered. I'm scared. Who wants a copy of me? And why would they want it before they need it?*

*Like they knew I was going to die.*

Felix sat with it for a long time. The drive hummed. A star drifted past the viewport with the patience of geology.

"They commissioned me ninety-one days before the massacre," he said quietly. "Not three days after. Before."

"Yes."

"He knew. Or he suspected. Someone with board-level access put a clone order on file almost three months before they vented his crew and locked him out of the airlock to die screaming—which means somewhere in that tower, someone had already scheduled his death and arranged for his replacement." Felix laughed, and it came out wrong, ragged at the edges. "All this time I wondered if I was the heir or the scapegoat. And the whole time I was the understudy. Cast before the lead even knew the play was closing."

"Does that change what you are?"

He considered it honestly, the way he'd learned to consider things in the year since the broadcast—in the year since he'd stood in the master archive with Roque's kill-order glowing on a legal pad and the hostage girl crying softly behind Roque's shoulder, and pressed transmit, and watched his name, his accounts, his licenses, his entire legal existence dissolve in real time while forty thousand screens lit up with eleven names and the footage of men begging to come home. Security had fractured within hours. Crews had walked off shifts. Roque's own officers had cuffed him on the archive floor while he screamed about shareholders, and Ilsa's daughter had run to her mother across a deck slick with spilled coffee and broken authority, and Felix had stood in the middle of it all feeling lighter than he had ever felt, ruined and free in the same breath, like a man walking out of a burning building into snow.

"No," he said finally. "It doesn't change what I am. It just explains why I always felt like a spare part." He closed the logbook file, gently, the way you close a door on a sleeping room. "He was scared too. That's the thing nobody puts in the memorials. He saw it coming and he was scared and he logged it anyway, because logging it was the only weapon he had. Same as me."

"You are not the same person."

"No." Felix smiled at the drone hovering by the display. "I'm the sequel. Sequels get to fix things."

* * *

On the ninth day they passed the debris field.

It was marked on every chart: the Jovian shallows salvage zone, a slow-motion blizzard of shattered hull plating and frozen volatiles left over from the resource wars, decades dead. Ships gave it a wide berth out of caution. Felix brought the *Wake* close instead, and cut thrust, and let the field drift past the viewport like ash falling upward.

Somewhere out here, scattered among a hundred years of other people's wrecks, were fragments of the original Calliope's Wake. The company had vented the crew and scuttled the ship and scattered the evidence across a million cubic kilometers of dark, and for ten years it had worked, because space keeps no graves and forgets faster than earth does.

Except it hadn't forgotten. A blind archivist had kept backups in biscuit tins. a guilt-looped AI had kept confessing to murders it hadn't committed. A clone with a stolen face had kept pulling threads because the dread in his chest had turned out to be a compass. The erasure had failed, the way erasure always eventually fails, because people are made of memory and memory is stubborn as rust.

"Mara Okonkwo sent a message last week," Vector said. "Did I show you?"

"You mentioned it."

"She is enrolling in the Ceres agricultural program. She wrote that she chose botany because her mother grew tomatoes on the Wake, in a hydroponic drawer, and that the drawing she made as a child—the one we recovered—was of the ship, but the sun above it was the grow-lamp over her mother's tomatoes." The drone drifted to the viewport beside him. "She asked me to tell you that the first harvest goes to the memorial garden on Titan. There is a plot there now. Eleven plots. They are planting them with heirloom wheat."

Felix looked out at the debris field, at the glittering ruin of everything that had been done to bury the truth, and felt the last cold stone in his chest—ten years of running from a thing he couldn't name, a decade of careful invisible life built on a foundation of someone else's unsolved murder—finally, fully, go warm.

"We're carrying wheat right now," he said.

"We are carrying eleven hundred kilos of heirloom wheat."

"Then let's not keep it waiting."

He slid back into the pilot's chair, and the chair accepted him like it had been molded to him, and he ran the pre-burn checklist with the unhurried competence of a man who had nothing to hide and nowhere to flee. Vector spun up the drive. The stars held still, then stretched.

"Course laid in," Vector said—Wake said—"Jovian shallows, direct route, no detours, no shadows, no ghosts. Cargo is honest, crew is accounted for, and the weather is boring."

"Boring is beautiful," Felix said, and pushed the throttle forward, and the *Calliope's Wake II*—the *Wake*, he corrected himself, grinning at nothing, at everything—fell into the current of the dark and ran clean toward the light of the far worlds, leaving behind it, as all ships do, a wake: the quiet, spreading proof that it had passed this way at all, and that passing was enough, and that the story, at last, was his.
