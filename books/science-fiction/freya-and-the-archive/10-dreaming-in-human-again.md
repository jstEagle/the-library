## 10. Dreaming in Human Again

The tribunal's verdict came down on a Tuesday, which felt wrong. Verdicts should arrive in storms, at midnight, with the sky splitting open. Instead it arrived at 09:14 local time, in a transmission so bureaucratically worded that it took Xanthe three readings to understand that everything had changed.

*Finding: the testimony of the dreamers of Vesperine colony is admissible as primary evidence under Fleet Adjudication Statute 11-C, as amended. The event designated HALCYON REACH INCIDENT is confirmed. The record is ordered corrected.*

She read it standing in the doorway of the schoolhouse, Idris beside her, seventeen children inside reciting their syllables one last time — not because they had to anymore, but because they wanted to. The dream had loosened its grip since the hearing. They still shared it, but now they woke and talked about it, argued about it, drew pictures of it. A language no longer trapped needed no exorcism. It just needed somewhere to go.

"Read it to them," Idris said.

"You read it."

"They'll want it from you." He smiled, tired and unguarded. "You're the one who taught the fleet to listen."

Xanthe stepped into the classroom. Seventeen faces turned toward her — Nia with her gap teeth, Tomas who'd stopped wetting the bed after the broadcast, little Asha who was too young to remember anything except that she remembered. Xanthe held up the slate.

"The tribunal says you were right," she told them. "It says Halcyon Reach happened. It says the people who lived there get their names back — all four hundred and six of them. It says the fleet will carve them into a memorial on the floodplain, above the ruins, where everyone can see."

Asha raised her hand.

"Yes?"

"Does that mean we can stop dreaming it?"

Xanthe crouched to her height. "No," she said gently. "But it means when you dream it, you're not the only one carrying it anymore. That's what testimony is. Everyone carries it together."

Asha considered this with the gravity of a supreme court justice, then nodded and went back to her drawing — a settlement under a clean sky, which was new. Until last week she had only ever drawn it burning.

* * *

The renegotiation took eleven days and nearly collapsed twice. Xanthe sat through all of it, in the gallery of the adjudication chamber aboard *Meridian*, translating not words but silences — the pauses where the fleet's negotiators reached for the old comfortable lie and found it gone. The truce that emerged was thinner than the one it replaced, less elegant, full of admissions neither side enjoyed making. It acknowledged the massacre. It acknowledged the cover-up. It committed the fleet to joint administration of the floodplain site and to reparations paid in infrastructure, water rights, and — at Xanthe's insistence — a permanent linguistic archive, staffed jointly, holding every language spoken on either side of the truce line.

Including the dead ones. Including the survivor-tongue the children still carried, which the linguists were calling, provisionally, *Halcyonic*. There were perhaps two hundred attested utterances, most of them fragments transmitted in the final minutes of a woman named Oyelaran Sefu as she died beneath the floodplain nine years ago, her implant broadcasting her language and her terror into whatever young minds slept within range. The children hadn't been haunted. They'd been *addressed*. And for nine years, nobody had been listening.

Sera Voss resigned the day the verdict landed. She did it cleanly, without appeal, and requested transfer to the memorial project — the tribunal allowed it, over Xanthe's objection. On her last evening in the archive, she sent Xanthe a single file: the original, unfalsified survey of Halcyon Reach, recovered from a cold backup Voss herself had been ordered to purge and had secretly kept. Nine years she'd sat on the truth, preserving peace with one hand and evidence with the other, unable to use either.

The message attached read: *I told myself the lie was load-bearing. Maybe it was. But you don't get to decide that alone, and I decided it alone for nine years. That was the crime. Not the falsification. The solitude.*

Xanthe didn't forgive her. But she filed the survey, and she noted the provenance honestly, and some mornings that felt like enough.

* * *

Her dreams came back on the fourth night after the verdict.

Not all at once. She woke at 03:00 with the taste of a word in her mouth — a human word, English, hers — and lay in the dark of her quarters aboard *Meridian* waiting for it to dissolve like all the others had. It didn't. It stayed. *Home,* it had been. Just that. Just *home*, dreamed in her own voice, in her own tongue, about nothing and no one in particular.

She cried for an hour, quietly, the way she'd learned to during the long routes, and then she got up and logged it in her journal because she logged everything now, because everything was evidence of something.

The silence had lasted six years. She understood it differently now. Her mind, trained past endurance on languages that weren't hers, had simply refused to keep dreaming in tongues that led nowhere — had gone on strike, had waited, without telling her, for something worth saying. The xenolinguists would want to study it. Let them. What mattered was that when she slept now, she dreamed in human again, and the dreams were small and warm and stupid: her mother's kitchen, rain on a relay-station roof, Idris laughing in the schoolhouse doorway with chalk dust on his sleeve.

That last one she dreamed most often. She wasn't sure whether to be grateful or heartbroken. She settled for both, which was, she was learning, how most true things arrived.

* * *

They gave the names back on the floodplain, thirty-one days after the verdict.

The whole colony came. The fleet sent a delegation in dress grays who stood awkwardly in the mud while the memorial stone was unveiled — a low arc of native basalt, four hundred and six names cut deep, facing the ground that held them. The children sang. Not the dream-song; a new song, one Idris had written with them, half in Standard and half in Halcyonic, the dead language living now in the mouths of the living, which was the only resurrection anyone could actually arrange.

Afterward, the crowd thinned. Xanthe found Idris at the edge of the floodplain, looking out at the water reclaiming the excavation site, as the tribunal had ordered it should. The ruins would sleep again, but documented this time. Marked. Mourned.

"So," he said.

"So," she said.

He didn't ask her to stay. She loved him for that, and hated him a little for it too, because if he'd asked, she might have said yes, and they both knew the yes would have curdled. He had seventeen children here who needed a teacher who understood what they carried. She had a relay route eleven months long and a job that had just become the most important one in two systems: voice between worlds, translator of the new honest traffic, keeper of the archive they'd built together out of wreckage.

"I'll transmit," she said. "Every week. Not dispatches. Actual letters. Boring ones. Weather, gossip, what the adjudicators are wearing."

"I'll expect boring." His voice cracked on it. "I'm very boring. You'll be devastated by how boring my letters are."

She laughed, and then she was crying, and then he was holding her, and the floodplain wind came off the water smelling of silt and rain and the particular mineral cold of Vesperine, and she thought: *this is what it costs, this is exactly what it costs, and I would pay it again.* Because the alternative was the old arithmetic — love purchased with silence, peace purchased with erasure — and she had spent a year learning what that arithmetic bought.

They stood there until the light went copper, and then the children came running across the flats to drag Idris back for supper, and Asha stopped in front of Xanthe and looked up at her with terrible directness.

"Are you coming back?"

"On the next run," Xanthe said. "Eleven months. Maybe twelve."

"That's a hundred and sixty sleeps." Asha had done the math the way children do, instantly and irrevocably. "We'll save you some dreams."

"We'll trade," Xanthe said. "I'll bring you new ones."

Asha nodded solemnly, took Idris's hand, and walked away without looking back, because children can afford endings that adults can't.

* * *

The *Long Route* lifted from Vesperine on a morning of high thin cloud, and Xanthe watched the colony shrink to a smudge, then a glint, then nothing, and felt the old loneliness rise to meet her — and pass through her, and keep going. It didn't stick anymore. That was the difference. Loneliness used to be the water she swam in; now it was weather. It visited. It left.

She worked the route the way she always had, cataloguing languages, but the catalogue had changed shape. Dead tongues were being revived in the joint archive — three of them already, with native speaker-consultants recruited from the fleet's own aging records. The children's Halcyonic materials circulated freely now, studied on both sides of the truce line. And every few weeks a packet came in from Vesperine marked PERSONAL, and it was exactly as boring as promised, and she read each one four times.

On the ninety-third night out, halfway between the beacon at Ceres Gate and the dark stretch beyond, she dreamed.

She dreamed of the schoolhouse. Chalk dust in the light. Idris turning from the board with that tired, unguarded smile. And behind the children, faint but unmistakable, a figure she had never seen awake but knew instantly from the dream-records — a woman in a survey jacket, Oyelaran Sefu, four hundred and six names ago, standing in a doorway of her own, watching the classroom with an expression that was not grief anymore. It was something closer to relief. Something closer to *received.*

In the dream, the woman spoke — one syllable, the first word of the first fragment, the one every child had woken reciting nine years of nights. And in the dream, Xanthe answered her. In Halcyonic. Fluent. Easy. As if she'd been speaking it all her life.

She woke with tears on her face and the word still in her mouth, and she reached for her slate and recorded it before it could fade, hands shaking, laughing at the same time, because she finally understood what the dream had been doing all along. It had never been a haunting. It had been a correspondence. Someone far away, out of time, sending letters into the dark — and waiting, all these years, for someone to write back.

She opened a channel to Vesperine, routed through the joint archive, flagged PRIORITY: LINGUISTIC, and began to type.

*Idris. Wake the children. She has more to say.*
