## 2. Grammar of the Dark

The signal arrived on a Tuesday, which meant nothing out here, where Tuesdays were a courtesy Beatrix paid to a planet he hadn't stood on in six years.

He was three hours into the night shift—his shift, his only shift, since Kestrel-9's roster consisted of one name and an empty chair kept for tradition's sake—when the beacon's traffic pattern shifted. He noticed it the way you notice a change in breathing in the next room: not a sound, exactly, but an absence where rhythm used to be.

Kestrel-9 was a relay station bolted to a nickel-iron rock at the edge of the Perseus Reach, and its beacon had been transmitting for ninety-one years without anyone on Earth reading more than a summary of what it heard. The commission file called it *Deep Listening Array 7*, though the original builders' notes referred to it, in one marginal annotation Beatrix had found and loved, as "the ear we left behind." It listened. It forwarded. Nobody home.

Beatrix Almeida was the nobody. He had accepted the posting the way people accept monasteries: not because they believe God is listening, but because the silence is honest about itself.

He pulled up the traffic log and watched the anomaly unspool across his screen in amber columns.

For ninety-one years, the beacon's incoming traffic had been noise with manners—pulsar ticks, hydrogen hiss, the long slow gossip of magnetars, all of it catalogued by classification trees older than Beatrix's parents. Predictable chaos. The universe shouting and never once saying anything.

This was different. This had *structure*.

He ran it through the standard parsers first, out of discipline. Entropy analysis: structured. Repetition interval: present, but nested—repetitions inside repetitions, like a sentence that contained its own echo. Distribution curve: wrong. Beautifully, unmistakably wrong. Natural signals decayed along predictable slopes; this one held its shape the way a held note holds its shape, deliberately, against entropy's whole argument.

Beatrix sat back in his chair. The station hummed around him, that sub-audible drone he'd stopped hearing years ago and only noticed when it stuttered during power cycling—the sound, he sometimes thought, of being alive inside a machine that did not care whether he was.

"Okay," he said aloud, to no one, because saying things aloud was another courtesy he paid to keep himself human. "Okay. Let's look at you."

* * *

He worked until the station's false dawn cycle washed the observation port from black to a bruised grey-blue, and somewhere in those hours the dread arrived—not suddenly, but the way cold water arrives in a boot: gradually, then completely.

It happened when he stopped analyzing the signal as data and started reading it as language, because that was what he was, underneath the maintenance certifications and the reactor-safety renewals: a xenolinguist, trained at a university that had since renamed two departments and forgotten him. And languages have textures. They have *grain*. He'd spent his career learning to feel the grain of a grammar the way a carpenter feels wood—where it wanted to bend, where it would split.

This signal had a grain he knew.

Not resembled. Not recalled. *Knew.*

The nesting repetitions weren't nested the way human syntax nests—no center-embedding spirals, no left-branching ladders. Instead the clauses folded inward like origami, each statement containing its own precondition, meaning arriving not linearly but *all at once*, the way—

The way his dreams arrived.

Beatrix's hands went still on the console.

He dreamed every night. He had told himself for six years that this was normal—isolation did that, the mind entertaining itself with borrowed material—and he had told the station medical AI, once, in a moment of weakness, that his dreams had become unusually *linguistic*. The AI had prescribed better sleep hygiene and asked if he'd considered a hobby. He had not mentioned it again.

But he knew those dreams. He knew them the way you know a house you grew up in: the way the corridors turned, the weight of the doors. In the dreams there was always a grammar unfolding around him, patient and vast, folding statements inward like paper, meaning arriving whole, and in the dreams he *understood it*, understood it effortlessly, the way you understand your own name, and the understanding felt less like learning and more like remembering something that had been written in him before he was born.

And now here it was. On his screen. In the beacon's traffic. Folded inward like paper.

"No," he said.

His voice sounded small in the operations bay. He said it again, louder, and the word bounced off the bulkheads and came back to him thinner.

He was a scientist. He knew about apophenia, about pareidolia, about the mind's genius for finding faces in wallpaper and messages in static. Six years alone could do this. Isolation primed pattern-matching; loneliness was a solvent that dissolved the membrane between perception and projection. There were case studies. He had read them, smugly, in another life, in seminar rooms that smelled of coffee and certainty.

But apophenia didn't work like this. Apophenia produced approximations—a face that was *sort of* a face, a message that was *sort of* a message. What glowed on his screen was not approximate. He ran the comparison himself, hand-transcribing forty seconds of the new signal into phonetic notation, then transcribing from memory the opening structure of last night's dream—which he had begun keeping logged in a private file months ago, telling himself it was linguistic curiosity, knowing even then that it was closer to a man checking his own skin for marks.

The match was not statistical. Statistics allowed for coincidence. This was structural identity, feature by feature: the inward folding, the preconditions nested inside assertions, the strange absence of any tense system—as if everything the language said was happening *now*, had always been happening now, would always be happening now.

A language with no past. A language with no future. Only an enormous, patient present tense.

Beatrix looked at his hands. They were steady, which frightened him more than shaking would have. Shaking would have meant his body understood the situation.

"Work and nightmare," he murmured. "Same water table."

* * *

He slept badly, which was to say he slept exactly as he always did, but now he woke from the dream already reaching for the terminal, and the transcription he made at 0340 station time matched the incoming signal's next segment before it even finished broadcasting. The signal, he realized with a vertigo that sat down heavily in his chest, was *predictable to him*. His dreaming mind was running ahead of the transmission, the way a fluent speaker anticipates the end of a sentence.

He was not receiving the language. He was *conversant* in it.

That word—he chose it carefully, sitting in the galley at 0500 with tea going cold beside him—was the one that broke something open. You could be exposed to a signal. You could be *affected* by one, the way sailors are affected by sirens in old stories. But conversant implied history. Conversant implied that some part of him had been studying for years, attending a class he couldn't remember enrolling in, taught by a teacher who only came when he was unconscious.

He thought about the beacon. Ninety-one years of listening. Ninety-one years of forwarding everything it heard to Earth, where the summaries piled up unread in archives, and meanwhile the beacon itself sat thirty meters from his sleeping quarters, pouring its collected sky into the station's systems, into the air handlers, into the resonant bones of the hull he slept inside like a marrow.

Had it been teaching him? Was that what a beacon did, when the thing it heard decided to answer back?

Or—and this thought arrived colder, quieter, wearing the reasonable face of scientific hypothesis—had the signal always carried this second layer, this folded grammar, and ninety-one years of listeners had simply lacked the instrument? And the instrument, in this case, was him?

He didn't know which possibility he preferred. One made him a student. The other made him a key.

* * *

At 0715, showered, shaved, dressed in the clean uniform of a man performing normalcy for an audience of zero, Beatrix sat down to write the report.

He drafted it four times.

The first draft was pure science: methodology, spectral analysis, entropy figures, structural notation. He deleted it because reading it cold, he sounded insane—a lone relay technician claiming first contact based on a feeling that his dreams matched the data. No committee on Earth would forward that past the first junior analyst, and worse, they might flag the report's author. Psychological review requests traveled fast outsystem. He'd seen a hydroponics tech on Ceres lose her certification over less.

The second draft minimized. He framed the discovery as a routine reclassification—an unusual natural source, possibly a new pulsar subclass with anomalous modulation. Honest enough to pass review, dishonest enough to bury the one fact that mattered. He deleted that too, his cursor hovering over the send field, because if he was right—if this was what he believed it was—then burying it made him complicit in whatever came next, and whatever came next was already, apparently, coming *for him*.

The third draft he wrote honestly, and it was the hardest: measured, precise, every claim hedged where honesty required hedging and firm where the data permitted firmness. He described the structural analysis. He included the notation samples. Then, in a section he titled *Observer Correlation*, he laid out the dream correspondence in flat clinical prose, as if describing someone else's symptoms, someone whose file he'd been assigned, someone he could remain professionally detached from.

*The undersigned acknowledges the low prior probability of subjective corroboration and recommends independent verification via archived signal comparisons dating to the array's commissioning era.*

There. That was the escape hatch he built into the report for himself: check the old archives. If the folded grammar appeared in decades-old traffic, then it wasn't his madness inventing patterns—it was a pattern that had been waiting, patiently, for someone who could finally hear it. And if it didn't appear in the old traffic, if it was new—

Then it had started recently. Recently meaning: since he arrived. Since he began sleeping thirty meters from the ear.

Either result terrified him. He sent the report anyway, priority-tagged to the Xenolinguistics Directorate on Earth, cc'd to the Deep Listening oversight board, and then he sat in the sudden silence of having done the brave thing and felt nothing but the station humming.

Transmission lag to Earth was eleven days each way. He knew this. He had known it for six years; it was the fundamental fact of his exile, the reason his posting required a temperament suited to talking to people who were always eleven days dead when they answered. Whatever Earth said back, it would say twenty-two days from now, about a version of him that no longer existed, because the version of him that existed now was changing daily, and the direction of the change was a question he was afraid to finish asking.

* * *

The automated rejection arrived nine hours later.

Later, Beatrix would understand that this was impossible—that no human reviewer on Earth could have read, assessed, and rejected a priority-tagged xenolinguistics report in nine hours, that the latency alone exceeded the round-trip to the nearest relay hub, that the reply must have originated somewhere much, much closer—but that night, exhausted, wrung out, he read the message header and felt only the familiar small death of bureaucratic dismissal.

**AUTOMATED TRIAGE NOTICE — XENOLINGUISTICS DIRECTORATE**

**RE: SUBMISSION AL-2291-K9 ("Structural Anomaly, DLA-7 Traffic")**

**STATUS: REJECTED — INSUFFICIENT PRIORITY CLASSIFICATION**

**DETAIL: Submission does not meet criteria for directorate review. Non-verified observer. Single-source data. Subjective correlation flagged (see Appendix: Observer Mental Fitness Disclosure, Form 44-C).**

**NOTE: The Directorate reminds all field personnel that submissions involving self-reported cognitive anomalies require concurrent psychological evaluation through certified channels PRIOR to resubmission. Your report has been retained. No further action will be taken.**

**This is an automated message. Do not reply.**

Beatrix read it twice. Three times. The words arranged themselves into a shape he recognized from his career's graveyard: the polite machinery that existed to ensure nothing unusual ever reached a human being. *Non-verified observer.* Of course. He maintained a beacon; he wasn't credentialed anymore; his university appointment had lapsed into emeritus-in-name-only years ago. *Single-source data.* There was no one else. There had never been anyone else. That was the entire architecture of his life.

*Subjective correlation flagged.*

They had flagged the dreams. An automated filter, keyword-scanning his honest clinical prose, had extracted the phrase *observer reports recurrent dream-state linguistic activity* and stamped the report with the modern equivalent of *madman's letter, file under H for hysteria.*

He should have lied on the fourth draft. He almost laughed. Twenty-two days of careful ethical deliberation, and the outcome was identical to what a coward would have produced in ten minutes: nothing. Worse than nothing—a flag. Somewhere in a database, Beatrix Almeida now existed as a mental-fitness concern attached to a deep-space asset. If anything went wrong out here, that flag would surface first. *Unstable isolated technician, known psychological disclosures.* He had handed them the narrative they'd use to erase him.

He got up. He walked the length of the operations bay, twice, three times, his boots loud on the deck plating, until the pacing burned off the first white heat of anger and left behind the thing underneath, which was fear, which had been underneath all along.

Because here was the thought he could no longer defer, the thought that had been waiting politely at the edge of his attention all day the way the signal waited at the edge of the spectrum:

The rejection had come in nine hours.

Eleven-day lag. Nine-hour reply. He stood at the observation port with his breath fogging the cold glass and did the arithmetic again, hoping he'd made an error, knowing he hadn't. The nearest routing hub with autonomous triage authority was the Ceres gateway—fourteen hours' lag, minimum, and Ceres's filters wouldn't touch xenolinguistic priority tags anyway. The reply had come from somewhere close. Somewhere in-system. Somewhere that had been watching his submission the moment it left the antenna.

Or—or the other explanation, the one that fit the numbers even better, the one he set down gently on the table of his mind like something fragile and venomous:

Something had read the report *before it was sent*. Something that lived in the station's systems, in the beacon's long memory, in the folded grammar of a language that had no future tense because it had never needed one—because everything, always, was already happening now.

The beacon's status light pulsed green in the dark beyond the glass, steady as a heartbeat, steady as it had pulsed for ninety-one years, and Beatrix Almeida stood alone at the edge of human space and understood, with the calm of a man reading his own diagnosis, that he had just filed a report to two audiences: one on Earth that would never read it, and one much nearer, that had answered within the hour.

*Do not reply,* the automated message said.

Too late, he thought. I think I already did. I think I've been replying for years—in my sleep, in a tongue I'm only now learning to spell.

Behind him, the console chimed softly. New traffic on the primary band.

The signal had changed again. And this time, when he opened the parse window with hands that were still steady, still terribly steady, the first segment resolved into notation he recognized instantly, because he had transcribed it that very morning from the edge of a dream:

A greeting. Patient, folded, addressed—to his rising horror and something dangerously adjacent to relief—by name.
