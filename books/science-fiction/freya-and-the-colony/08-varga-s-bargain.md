## 8. Varga's Bargain

Commander Ilse Varga had been aboard the *Persephone* for eleven days, and in that time she had learned three things about the children of the *Meridian*: they did not sleep when expected, they did not cry when frightened, and they watched her with an attention that made her skin crawl.

She stood in the observation bay of the holding deck, hands clasped behind her back, and watched Wren through the glass. The child sat cross-legged on the floor of the compartment, drawing in the condensation of the viewport with one small finger. Spirals. Branching lines. A script that looked, to Varga's untrained eye, like frost forming in fast-forward.

"Status," she said, without turning.

Lieutenant Okonkwo shifted at her shoulder. "Subject Wren refused breakfast again. The others are the same. They eat a little, then stop. Medical can't find anything wrong with them. Metabolisms are—" he hesitated. "Strange. They're running hot, Commander. Like furnaces."

"They're not furnaces, Lieutenant. They're translators." She had read Beatrix Almeida's reports—all of them, including the rejected ones Earth's linguistics division had bounced back with automated courtesy. The man had been screaming into the void for years, and the void, it turned out, had been screaming back.

"And the translator on the station?" Okonkwo asked.

"Almeida." She said the name like she was weighing it. "He's cooperative. Too cooperative. That bothers me more than the children do."

"Ma'am?"

Varga finally turned from the glass. Wren had stopped drawing. The child was looking directly at her through two panes of smartglass and forty meters of vacuum-purged air, and Varga would have sworn on her commission that Wren could see her—*all* of her—the ambition, the orders sealed in her private cipher, the exact shape of the sentence she was going to speak to Almeida in the morning.

"Get some sleep, Lieutenant," she said. "Tomorrow we close the deal."

* * *

Beatrix knew the *Persephone* had arrived before the station's proximity klaxon sounded, because the dreams had changed.

For years they had been the same dream: a vast dark syntax unfolding like a flower made of grammar, petals of meaning he could almost read, almost—always almost—grasp. But the night the corporate ship slid into Kestrel-9's shadow, the dream had grown a second voice. A cold one. A counting one. He'd woken with the taste of iron in his mouth and a word sitting behind his teeth that he did not know and understood perfectly: *negotiation*.

He was in the airlock bay when the boarding party arrived, dressed in his cleanest jumpsuit, hair combed, three days of sleep debt hidden behind a face he'd practiced into neutrality. The hatch cycled. Six marines in corporate grey fanned out with the bored precision of people who had done this a hundred times, and then she walked through—Commander Ilse Varga, tall, silver at the temples, eyes like audit software.

"Dr. Almeida." She extended a hand. "Commander Varga, Halcyon Reach Development Group, operating under Earth Commerce Charter 114."

"Nobody ordered a charter here," Beatrix said, shaking her hand. Her grip was exactly calibrated—firm, brief, forgettable. "This station predates the Reach. My commission comes from the Outer Sciences Directorate."

"Which was absorbed by the Reach eleven years ago." She smiled without warmth. "So in a very real sense, Doctor, I'm your new employer. I've read your reports. All of them."

"Earth rejected those reports."

"Earth rejects everything that doesn't fit in a quarterly briefing. I'm not Earth." She walked past him into the station's spine, her marines falling in behind her, and Beatrix understood that the tour had already begun whether he gave it or not. "You found a grammar in the beacon traffic. You documented your own linguistic drift with scientific rigor while requesting psychiatric evaluation in the same filing. Do you know how rare that is? Most people in your position would have gone quietly mad. You went *methodically* mad. I find that admirable."

"I'd find it admirable if you'd led with a reason for being here."

She stopped at the junction where the corridor split—habitation ring to the left, beacon core to the right—and turned to him. "Here's my reason, Doctor. Halcyon Reach is prepared to offer you passage home. First-class berth on the *Persephone*, full medical workup, treatment for your condition—whatever it turns out to be. A real lab. Colleagues. Coffee that wasn't grown in a hydroponic tray older than you are." Her voice softened by exactly one degree, a machine simulating warmth. "You've been alone out here for nine years. I'm offering you your life back."

The words landed harder than he wanted them to. He thought of his quarters, the narrow bunk, the wall where he'd pinned printouts of dream-syntax like a madman's wallpaper. He thought of Earth, which he had stopped believing in the way you stop believing in a country you left as a young man.

"And in exchange," he said.

"You hand over the translation keys. Your decoding frameworks, your corpus, your dream transcriptions. Everything. And you sign a standard exclusivity agreement."

"And the children?"

"The children are Halcyon assets now. Salvage rights on the *Meridian* are ironclad. They'll be well cared for." She said it smoothly, and Beatrix heard the seam in the sentence—the place where *well cared for* had been spliced over whatever she actually intended.

"They're sixty years old," he said. "They were adults when the *Meridian* went missing. They have memories of Earth, of families—"

"Who are, at generous estimate, dead or very old. Doctor." Varga stepped closer, and for a moment the corporate polish thinned and he saw the thing underneath, the cold engine of her. "You've been out here so long you've forgotten how the universe works. Value is the only morality that scales. Those children are the most valuable biological objects humanity has ever recovered. I am *not* going to leave them on a station maintained by one lonely man with a deteriorating brain. Whatever you think of me, that part is true."

"The keys aren't mine to sell," Beatrix said quietly. "That's the thing you don't understand. I didn't invent the translation. It was given to me. Or done to me. I still don't know which. And the ones who gave it—" he tapped his temple, "—they're not party to your charter."

Varga studied him for a long moment. Then she smiled again, and this one reached her eyes, which was worse.

"I had hoped you'd be reasonable," she said. "But I budgeted for unreasonable. You have until 0600 station time to reconsider. After that, I proceed under salvage and security protocols, and you'll find my interpretation of those protocols very creative."

* * *

He tried to warn the children. That was the part that shamed him later—not the refusal, which had cost him nothing but a berth home, but the failure afterward.

The holding deck's security had been upgraded in the six hours since Varga's ultimatum. Beatrix stood outside the compartment with a maintenance override he'd had since the station's commissioning and which the marines had, in their arrogance, never thought to revoke, and the door sighed open, and four small faces turned toward him in the dim light.

Wren was on her feet immediately. "You came to say goodbye," she said. In English. Her English was getting better by the day, assembled from the station's entertainment archives with eerie speed, but she still spoke it with a strange formality, like a diplomat's ghost.

"I came to tell you they're going to take you," Beatrix said. "I refused their offer. They're taking you anyway."

"We know." Wren glanced at the others—Maren, who had been the *Meridian*'s botanist and was now maybe eight; the twins, Sefi and Dov, who held hands when they slept. "The ship's engines changed this morning. We heard it through the hull. Ships that are leaving sound different from ships that are staying." She said it simply, without accusation, and that simplicity cut him worse than anger would have.

"Can you refuse to go?" he asked. "Make them—"

"Commander Varga has marines," Wren said. "And we are small." She tilted her head, and in the low light her eyes did the thing that unsettled everyone—went old, went deep, went somewhere sixty years downstream. "You could come with us."

"I could—"

"You could come with us and keep translating. That's what you're for. That's what you're *afraid* you're for." She stepped closer. "The beacon called you for nine years and you answered every night in your sleep. Don't pretend the offer frightens you more than the loneliness did."

Beatrix opened his mouth. Closed it. Because she was right, and because somewhere beneath his fear there was a terrible relief at hearing it said aloud.

The deck lights went red.

* * *

They came at 0600 exactly, because Varga was a woman who honored her own deadlines the way other people honored gods.

Beatrix was escorted—there was no other word for it—to the observation gallery overlooking the docking collar, two marines at his shoulders, not quite restraining him. Below, through the armaglass, he watched them load the children onto the *Persephone*.

It was done gently. That was the horror of it. No chains, no sedation. Varga had understood something Beatrix had not: that the children would not resist, because resistance required a strategy, and they were four small bodies carrying sixty years of memory with nowhere to put it. Maren went first, holding a marine's hand like a niece at a wedding. The twins followed, walking in perfect step. Wren went last.

At the collar, she stopped. Turned. Found him through the armaglass with the unerring accuracy she always had, and raised one small hand—not waving. Drawing. Her finger traced a shape in the air, quick and precise, and Beatrix's dream-grammar rose in him unbidden and read it before his conscious mind could object:

*We are not lost. We are carried. Follow the carried things.*

Then the collar sealed, and she was gone.

* * *

Varga found him in the beacon core two hours later, because of course she did. He was running diagnostics he didn't need to run, hands moving over the ancient console because his hands needed occupation or they would shake.

"You're angry," she said from the doorway.

"I'm not angry. I'm impressed. It's worse." He didn't turn around. "You took children, Commander."

"I took *assets* that you were keeping in a room with a broken recycler." She came to stand beside him at the console, and her reflection in the dark display looked at his reflection, and neither of them blinked. "Here's what happens now, and I'd like you to actually listen, because this is the last time I'll be generous. The *Persephone* returns to Reach space. The children will be studied by people with resources you cannot imagine. Your translation keys, frankly, matter less than I let you think—my linguists will reconstruct your framework from the children's speech inside a year. The offer was courtesy. A courtesy you declined."

"Then why are you here?"

"Because of the beacon." She laid one finger on the console, on the live signal trace pulsing there like a slow heartbeat. "Because your reports say the signal is an *invitation*, Doctor. An invitation that's been broadcasting for decades, that infected you through your sleep, that turned a forty-year-lost crew into children who speak it natively. And because my orders—my *actual* orders, the ones in my private cipher—say that if the source of that signal is reachable, Halcyon Reach reaches it. And if it can't be reached, it gets put down."

Beatrix turned slowly. "Put down."

"The beacon. The relay. Whatever's on the other end." Varga's face was perfectly calm, and that was how he knew she'd already made peace with it. "You think I came out here for translation keys? I came out here because something in the dark has been calling to my species for thirty years, and my board wants to know if it's a market or a threat. I was hoping for a market." She pushed off from the console. "You should come aboard, Doctor. Genuinely. Not for the keys—for your own safety. Because in about six hours, this station's beacon is going to stop being a scientific curiosity and start being a target, and I'd rather you weren't standing next to it."

She left. Her boots rang down the corridor, evenly spaced, unhurried.

Beatrix stood alone in the beacon core with the signal pulsing on the screen and the shape of Wren's last gesture burning behind his eyes.

*We are not lost. We are carried.*

He looked at the maintenance bay manifest on his secondary display. Skiff Two: fuel at seventy-one percent, life support rated for one, range—his thumb moved over the numbers—range enough to reach the dead-space relay, if he didn't stop, if he didn't sleep, if he didn't turn back.

The signal pulsed. And for the first time in nine years, Beatrix Almeida answered it while awake, aloud, in a voice that cracked on the first syllable and steadied on the second.

"I'm coming," he said. "Wait for me."

The beacon's rhythm did not change. But somewhere at the edge of dead space, something old and patient and afraid turned its attention toward a lonely man in a forgotten station—and began, very gently, to make the way easier.
