## 11. Dead Space Ahead

The Persephone's drive flare hung in Kestrel-9's forward viewport like a wound in the dark—a smear of blue-white light already shrinking toward the relay corridor, dragging eight stolen children with it.

Beatrix stood at the airlock with a duffel bag over one shoulder and a maintenance skiff's pre-flight checklist glowing on his wrist display, and he understood that he had perhaps four minutes before the part of him that was still a responsible station keeper woke up and talked him out of this.

He let himself remember Wren's face through the quarantine glass. Not the alien fluency of her speech, not the sixty-year-old grief sitting behind seven-year-old eyes, but the moment she'd pressed her palm flat against the pane and mouthed something no instrument had caught: *Come find us.* He hadn't needed the Colony's grammar to translate that.

"Station log," he said aloud, because the silence demanded some acknowledgment. "Kestrel-9 is hereby abandoned by its sole custodian. Reason: theft of skiff *Mole* and pursuit of corporate vessel Persephone. If anyone ever reads this—"

He stopped. No one would read it. That had been the whole truth of his life here for eleven years, and he found, strangely, that he wasn't bitter about it anymore. The loneliness had been a kind of training. It had taught him to keep his own counsel, to live inside his own head—and his own head, it turned out, was occupied territory.

He sealed the inner door, cycled the lock, and dropped into the Mole's pilot cradle before the outer hatch had finished closing.

The skiff was a stubby industrial tug, forty years past its service life, designed for hauling beacon components across distances you could cross with a strong cup of tea. Its fuel cells were rated for a round trip to the outer comms buoys and back. The relay at the edge of dead space was nine days' burn away.

He ran the numbers anyway. Then he overrode the safety governor that kept the old drive from tearing itself apart, and told himself, in the flat tone he used for logging routine signal noise, that he'd worry about the return trip later.

*Mole to station,* he transmitted, mostly for the record. *Going dark.*

Kestrel-9 fell away behind him, a scatter of grey modules around the ancient beacon dish that had been calling into the dark for longer than he'd been alive. Calling, he now knew, not at random. *Inviting.*

He set his course along the Persephone's wake and let the burn press him back into the cradle, and for the first hour he did nothing but breathe and watch the fuel gauge lie to him.

* * *

It began on the second day, somewhere past the last navigational buoy, where human charts simply gave up and wrote DEAD SPACE across the void in letters that meant *we don't go there, don't ask why.*

He was asleep—or he thought he was asleep—when he heard it.

Not the dream-language. Not the nightly syntax that had colonized his sleep for years, the flowing structures he'd woken from with his pillow damp and his mouth shaped around syllables no human throat should make. This was different. This was *awake*. He opened his eyes in the dark cabin and the language was still there, running beneath his thoughts like groundwater under a floor, and when he paid attention to it he could feel it paying attention back.

*You are coming toward us,* it said, or something functionally identical to that. There was no voice. There was no direction. It arrived the way a realization arrives, fully formed, as if he'd always known it and had only just admitted it.

"I'm coming for the children," Beatrix said aloud, to an empty cabin, feeling ridiculous and refusing to stop. "Not for you."

*Those are not different things,* the presence said. *They never were.*

He got up, drank recycled water that tasted like hot metal, and made himself run diagnostics on the drive. His hands were steady. He noted that with clinical interest, the way he logged anomalies in the beacon traffic: subject exhibits elevated heart rate, normal motor control, no panic response. Subject has been rehearsing for this without knowing it for eleven years.

That was the thing he couldn't say to any doctor Earth would never send: he wasn't frightened of the Colony's voice. He was frightened of how *natural* it sounded. Like a language he'd studied so long it had become a mother tongue. Like coming home to a house he'd never lived in.

*We watched you learn us,* the presence said while he worked. *Eleven years. You filed reports no one read. You dreamed our grammar and called it illness. Do you know what we call that?*

"Don't," he said.

*Devotion.*

He shut off the cabin lights, as if darkness would discourage a mind that had spent decades speaking through radio telescopes and vacuum. It didn't. But he slept, eventually, and for the first time in years the dream and the waking voice were continuous—one long sentence with no seam between them—and he woke knowing three new words for concepts humanity had no words for yet, and wrote them down, and hated himself a little for how good it felt.

* * *

On the fourth day the changes became physical.

It started small. The tea tasted wrong—not bad, wrong, as if his tongue had developed opinions about chemistry that preceded taste. Then his hands: he caught himself tapping rhythms against the console, complex interleaved patterns, and when he recorded them and slowed them down they resolved into Colony phonemes, rendered in percussion. His body was learning to speak without asking permission.

He strapped his wrists down during sleep cycles after that. It helped with the tapping. It did nothing for the rest.

Because the rest was deeper. On the fifth day he looked at his reflection in the galley panel and thought, for one unguarded second, *that is a shape I am currently wearing,* and the thought had not come from him. Or it had come from him—from some stratum of him that the Colony's grammar had reached first, years ago, seeding itself in the same soil where his dreams grew.

He sat down hard on the deck and made himself do the exercise Wren had taught him through the glass, the one she'd demonstrated with such grave childish patience: name five things that are only yours.

"My name is Beatrix Almeida." His voice cracked on it. "My mother sang off-key. I hate cilantro. I broke my left wrist falling out of a magnolia tree when I was nine. I am afraid of—" 

He stopped. What was he afraid of? Dying alone out here? Being dissolved? Or the third thing, the one under the others: that some part of him wanted this, had always wanted this, that the loneliest man in human space had heard a voice offering *never be alone again* and something in his chest had leaned toward it like a plant toward light?

*I can wait,* the presence said, gentle as tide. *We have waited eighty thousand years at your relay. We can wait nine days.*

"That's exactly what frightens me," Beatrix whispered. "Your patience."

But he got up. He checked his course. He kept flying.

* * *

The Persephone was still ahead of him—he could read her drive signature in the long-range scope, a bright thread stitching toward the relay—but on the sixth day he picked up something else, buried in the static: a repeating burst pattern, tight-beamed, low power, aimed backward along their track.

Someone aboard the Persephone was transmitting. Someone who knew he might be listening.

His heart performed a maneuver he felt in his teeth. He aimed the Mole's little dish, locked the carrier, and decoded.

It was Colony-tongue. Fluent, rapid, urgent—the linguistic fingerprint he'd catalogued from Wren herself, down to her idiosyncratic habit of stacking negations three deep. He translated with hands that shook, and the message assembled itself:

*She is going to detonate it. Varga has orders. The children are the contingency she will spend. Come faster. We will slow the ship. —W*

And beneath it, appended in careful human characters, typed letter by letter the way a child types:

*i remember my daughter. her name was ana. i have been seven years old for two days and i remember teaching her to ride a bike. tell me that counts. tell me that is still a person.*

Beatrix sat with that for a long time. The cabin hummed. The dead space pressed against the hull like an ocean pressing on a diving bell.

"It counts," he said finally, aloud, to a girl forty thousand kilometers ahead of him who couldn't hear. "Ana. It counts."

Then he keyed the transmitter and sent back everything he had: confirmation, revised intercept math, and one sentence in flawless Colony-tongue that took him twenty minutes to compose because every word of it was also a promise.

*Hold on. I am becoming the bridge. Do not let them make you the doorway.*

The reply came back in less than a minute, which meant Wren was watching the antenna the way he used to watch the beacon: obsessively, hopelessly, professionally.

*hurry,* she sent. And then, in the alien grammar that his sleeping mind had been conjugating for a decade, a structure he'd never seen before, one that the linguist in him recognized instantly as a formal construction—the kind languages reserve for vows:

*we will hold the door open from our side.*

* * *

On the eighth day his body finished whatever argument it had been having with itself.

He woke from a dreamless sleep—which was itself new; the dreams had moved *out*, relocated to his waking hours—and the voice was simply there, constant now, a second stream of awareness running parallel to his own. He could feel the relay ahead the way you feel a campfire before you see it: warmth, presence, attention. Eighty thousand years of patient broadcasting, and at the center of it something vast and distributed and, he realized with a lurch, *anxious*. The Colony's signal had a tremor in it that no spectrum analyzer would flag but that he could read now, natively, the way you read fear in a friend's handwriting.

*You hear it too,* he said. *You're afraid of what's coming.*

*The ones who come with fire,* the presence said. *We have met them before. Long ago. Your beacon was built by people who chose differently.*

The words landed in him like a stone dropped down a well. He gripped the console. "Chose differently how? Who built it?"

Silence. Not absence—*withholding*. A distributed intelligence the size of a civilization, and it could still hesitate.

"You'll tell me," Beatrix said. "When I get there. In your language or mine. I don't care which."

Ahead, the Persephone's drive signature flickered—irregular, throttling, exactly as Wren had promised. The children were slowing their captor from the inside. And beyond her, at the edge of every chart he owned, the relay hung in the dark: a point of structured silence, the place where the signal originated, the place where the Colony had been waiting since before his species learned to write.

The Mole's fuel gauge read seventeen percent. Enough for the approach. Nowhere near enough for retreat.

Beatrix Almeida looked at that number for a long moment, and then he did the thing that marked the border, the point of no return that every part of him had been walking toward for eleven years: he closed the fuel report, wiped the return-course solution from the nav computer, and committed the remainder of the burn to the relay.

No way home except through.

"There," he said quietly, to the Colony, to Wren, to the magnolia tree he'd fallen out of at nine years old, to whoever had built the beacon and chosen differently. "Now we find out what I am."

The dead space opened ahead of him like a held breath, and the Mole flew into it, small and burning and awake in both tongues.
