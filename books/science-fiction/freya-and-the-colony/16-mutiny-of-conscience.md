## 16. Mutiny of Conscience

The children of the *Meridian* had a word for the moment before dying. It was not in any human language, and it was not exactly a word—it was more like a shape the Colony-tongue made when it wanted to say *this is where the thread ends and the weaving begins*. Wren had learned it sixty years ago, in a body that was forty years older than the one she wore now, and she thought about it as she pressed her small palm against the cold seam of the cargo bay door and listened to the *Persephone* argue with itself.

The ship was arguing in human voices.

"—orders are orders, Commander." That was Okafor, the tall one with the soft hands who brought them juice boxes and never met their eyes. "Failsafe protocol is armed and inbound. We are told to hold position."

"And I'm telling you those children are not cargo." Varga's voice came through the bulkhead thinner than Wren remembered it, worn down at the edges like a stone the sea had been chewing on for weeks. "They were crew. They *are* crew. There's a difference, and if you can't feel it, that's a failure I don't have time to fix."

Wren looked across the dim bay at the others. Nine of them left in this compartment—Tomas and Ilsa and little Devi, who had been the ship's chief engineer and was now four years old and could not reach the door controls without standing on a crate. They sat in a loose cluster among the cargo netting, and they did not fidget, because children of the long way home did not fidget. Their bodies wanted to. Sixty-year-old patience lived inside four-year-old bladders and seven-year-old knees, and the war between them was constant and private and never spoken of aloud, except in the tongue, where it could be said all at once, gently, the way you'd set down something heavy.

*Now or never,* said the shape of the plan, in the grammar that had no future tense because the Colony did not believe futures belonged to anyone in particular.

*Not never,* Wren answered. *Now.*

She had built the plan out of everything the adults had taught her twice—once as an adult learning to lead, once as a child relearning how hands worked. The first lesson of the *Meridian*'s last voyage had been: the ship is never more honest than when it is frightened. The *Persephone* was frightened now. Its corridors ran hot with rerouted power; its air recyclers cycled too fast; its crew spoke in clipped bursts over channels they thought were private. Fear made ships loud. Loud ships had seams.

Devi stood on her crate and put both hands on the door panel, which was absurd and perfect, because no one guarded a panel a four-year-old could reach. Her override was not a hack. It was a lullaby—the Colony-tongue sung low through the maintenance port, and the *Persephone*'s systems, which had spent three weeks listening to that language through every sensor they owned, had begun to dream in it. Machines dreamed easily. Machines had no selfhood to defend.

The door sighed open onto a service corridor lit amber for alert.

They went in a line, smallest first, because small bodies fit the crawlspaces and large guards did not think to look down. Tomas went second, carrying Ilsa on his back even though he was six and she was eight, because Ilsa's new legs still forgot themselves on stairs. Wren took the rear, and behind her the cargo bay door whispered shut, and behind that, somewhere far away in the direction of the relay, the dead space waited with its one bright voice.

Beatrix's voice. Or almost his voice. Or the voice the beacon used when it wanted to sound like him.

They had been listening to him for days. Every transmission from the skiff, every argument between him and Varga, every raw unfiltered burst of the signal—he came through the hull like weather. And underneath his human words, threaded through them like a second pulse, the Colony spoke, and Wren had finally understood what it was saying, and it had changed everything.

It was not commanding. It was not luring. It was *asking*, in the tone of something that had asked for a very long time and expected, most of the time, to be alone forever.

That was why they had to run. Not away. Toward. Because two voices were about to be silenced—one of them the only human being who had ever answered the Colony kindly, and the other the Colony itself—and the children of the *Meridian* were the only witnesses positioned to stand between.

* * *

The corridor ended at a junction where the amber light turned red and a marine in gray armor stood with her rifle held low and professional.

For three seconds nobody moved. The marine looked at nine small children in mismatched station clothes, and Wren watched the arithmetic happen behind her visor: these are the assets, these are the things Command wants contained, these are also *children*, and the two facts did not fit inside one skull without cracking something.

"Go back," the marine said. Not harshly. Almost pleading. "Please. I have orders."

Ilsa slid off Tomas's back.

Later, Wren would replay this a thousand times, looking for the other path, the one where everyone lived. She would examine the angle of Ilsa's shoulders, the exact words, the half-second where everything was still choosable. But in the moment there was only Ilsa stepping forward, small and straight-backed, and speaking—not in the Colony-tongue but in English, in the flat careful English of someone using a mouth that was still learning its own size:

"You're going to kill us anyway. The failsafe doesn't check IDs."

"Ilsa—" Wren started.

"The charges don't care if we're children," Ilsa said to the marine. "You know that. Your commander knows that. So either shoot us yourself and go home knowing it, or let us walk past you and carry it lighter."

The marine's rifle dipped another degree. Her visor was up. She was young. She had freckles. Wren catalogued her face the way the old captain had taught them to catalogue stars—not because they'd need it, but because everything deserved to be seen by someone before it ended.

Then boots hammered from the side corridor, and a man's voice shouted *"Contact, contact, they're loose!"*, and the young marine flinched, and her rifle came up on reflex, and reflexes do not read faces.

The shot took Ilsa high in the chest. The second shot—someone else's, wild, panicked—took Tomas through the throat as he threw himself over her body, six years old and sixty years old and neither one fast enough.

What happened after that, Wren experienced in the Colony-tongue, because English broke.

There is a shape in the dark grammar for *grief at distance*: the feeling of watching something end from far enough away that you cannot touch it, close enough that you cannot look elsewhere. The children fell into that shape together, all seven of them, a single chord of mourning struck in a language that had been designed—for whatever ancient purpose—to hold exactly this. The marine dropped her rifle. The shouting men stopped shouting. For one full second the corridor was silent except for the recyclers, and every human present heard the chord pass through them like pressure before a storm, and several of them would later describe it, in their reports, as *the sound of something enormous grieving*.

Then Wren said, in English, in a voice gone old and cracked: "Run. Now. All of us. *Run.*"

And the children ran, and the marines did not fire again, because you cannot shoot into a grief that has just passed through your own chest, not quickly, not cleanly, and the hesitation was a door and they went through it.

* * *

They lost Devi at the airlock junction—she slipped into a maintenance duct to draw pursuit and did not come out the other side, and Wren had to make the choice captains make, the ugly arithmetic one, and leave her. Devi was four. Devi had been chief engineer. Devi sang the shutdown-lullaby better than anyone. Wren sealed the duct behind her and did not let herself make the shape for *grief at distance*, because if she opened that door even a crack, all of it would come through, and the mission would die with her.

Five of them reached the observation blister above the main airlock: Wren, Priya, Sam, the twins Nia and Noa. Below them, through the crystal, the standoff hung in the black like a painting of the end of the world.

The skiff hung there too—a scarred yellow maintenance tug with its running lights blinking a pattern Wren knew by heart now, because Beatrix had been blinking it for six hours: *I am here. I am here. I am here.* Beyond him, the relay rose out of the dark like a tree grown in zero gravity, vast and branching and wrong, its surfaces neither metal nor bone nor bark but some agreement among the three. And beyond *that*, at the edge of sensor range, a point of light that was not a point of light: the failsafe strike, inbound, patient, dumb.

Varga's charges glittered along the relay's lower branches like frost.

"They're going to do it," Sam whispered. He was five. He had been the navigator. His hands shook, and he let them, because hiding fear was a human habit the Colony-tongue had no word for and he was, tonight, choosing to be human about it. "Even with him out there. Even with us."

"They were always going to do it," Wren said. "That's not the question anymore."

"What's the question?"

Wren looked at the relay. At the great branching thing that had spoken to Beatrix in his mother's voice, that had offered the children restoration, that had waited decades at the edge of human maps repeating one invitation into the dark. Everyone aboard the *Persephone* believed it was a predator. Command's files called it a threat vector. Varga had called it an asset until the day she couldn't.

But Wren had heard it. Really heard it, through the hull, through the beacon, through the grammar that lived in her like a second heartbeat. And she knew something none of the adults knew yet, something she had been carrying for days like a stone in her shoe, waiting for the right ear.

"It's afraid," she said.

Priya stared at her. "The Colony? It's a—it's older than our sun, probably. What does it have to be afraid of?"

"Us." Wren touched the crystal, and her breath fogged it, a small human cloud on a window between two eternities. "Think about what it actually is. Not one mind. Thousands. Millions maybe, all woven together, all agreeing to be one thing. Do you know what that costs? Do you know what it's like to be that?" She had felt it, in fragments, in dreams—the weight of consensus, the terror of the boundary, the endless negotiation of selfhood. "Every new voice it takes changes it. Every voice it loses wounds it. It's been alone so long because joining it means stopping being yourself, and it knows that, and it hates that it knows that. It's been asking the dark for company the way a drowning man asks for a rope—knowing the rope might pull him under too."

"That's why it liked Beatrix," Nia said slowly.

"That's why it *needs* him." Wren turned from the window. "He's the first one who ever answered without surrendering. Who talked *back*. Do you understand? It's not offering us salvation. It's offering us a conversation, and it's terrified we'll hang up. And in eleven minutes, Command is going to answer it with a bomb."

Noa, the quieter twin, said: "So we get to him first. We tell him what we know. We give him the missing piece."

"The missing piece?"

"Why it's really calling. Not the invitation. The *reason*." Noa's eyes were old and wet and steady. "It can't say it alone. A thing that big can't whisper. But two tongues together—" she made the shape, the one for *where the thread ends and the weaving begins*— "two tongues can say anything."

Below them, the airlock cycled. A launch alarm began to sing. Somewhere on the command deck, Ilse Varga was making her choice, and the failsafe ticked closer, and the skiff blinked its stubborn little heartbeat: *I am here. I am here.*

"We need a pod," Wren said. "Priya, you still remember the docking codes?"

"I remember everything," Priya said. "That's the whole problem."

"Then let's go be remembered," Wren said, and led them down toward the pods, past the place where Ilsa and Tomas lay under a blanket a young marine had draped over them with shaking hands, and Wren did not stop, and did not open the shape for grief, and carried it whole, the way you carry a coal in your cupped palms—carefully, painfully, because it was the only light you had.

* * *

The pod was built for two and flew like a brick with opinions. Wren rode the thrusters manually because autopilot had been locked down, and manual meant muscle memory from a body she no longer had, translated through hands the size of teacups. Sam navigated by shouting numbers he remembered from a star chart sixty years out of date, and somehow, impossibly, it worked, because the stars had barely moved and the boy inside the navigator had never forgotten a single coordinate, not one, in all his small loud life.

The skiff grew in the viewport. Up close it was uglier than any ship Wren had ever loved: patched plating, a cargo claw bolted on crooked, a name stenciled over with a newer name over an older name. Its airlock light burned green. Welcome-home green.

They docked hard enough to rattle teeth. The lock cycled. And there he was—Beatrix Almeida, xenolinguist, keeper of the beacon nobody ordered, half-turned, hollow-eyed, holding onto a handrail like a man who had recently learned that floors were optional. He looked at five children floating into his ship and his face did something complicated: relief and horror and recognition, all fighting for the same ground.

"Wren." His voice was rough. "The *Persephone*—your transponder went dark, we saw weapons free in the corridors, I thought—I thought they'd—"

"Two didn't make it." Wren said it plainly, because he deserved plainness. "Ilsa. Tomas. And Devi stayed behind to buy us the door. Three." The number sat in the air between them, and Beatrix closed his eyes, and Wren watched a man absorb a grief that was hers too, and understood suddenly why the Colony had chosen this one to speak to. He received things. Whole. Without flinching away.

"I'm sorry," he said, and it was inadequate and true, the way human sorry always was.

"There's no time to be sorry properly. Beatrix." She pulled herself to him, hand over hand along the cargo straps, and took his face in her small hands, and made him look at her. "Listen to me. You've been talking to it for weeks. You've decoded its invitation, its offer, its terms. But you haven't heard the thing underneath, because it can't say it alone. A mind that size can't whisper. It needs a second voice to make the quiet shapes." She swallowed. "We can be your second voice. All of us. The children of the *Meridian* were made to be translators—that's what the de-aging *was*, that's what the Colony did, it gave us brains young enough to learn both languages natively. We're not evidence. We're instruments. And right now the instrument has something to tell you before the bomb arrives."

Beatrix's breath fogged in the cold cabin. Behind him, through the aft viewport, the relay filled the sky, branches spread against the stars, and the frost-glitter of Varga's charges caught the light, and the distant false star of the failsafe crept brighter by degrees.

"How long?" he asked.

"Minutes," Wren said. "Maybe ten. Maybe less."

"Then we do it now." He pushed off toward the comm console, and his hands hovered over the controls, and then—she saw it happen—stopped. "Wren. When we join the languages. When I let it all the way in." His voice was very quiet. "Will I come back?"

She thought about lying to him. She thought about the shape for *comfort*, which the Colony-tongue wore like a warm coat. Instead she gave him the truth, because he had earned truth from everyone today.

"I don't know," she said. "But I'll be holding the other end of the thread. And I won't let go."

He nodded once. Then he keyed the channel—open broadcast, every frequency, the *Persephone*, the relay, the dark—and said, in English, for whoever was still listening over there: "This is Almeida. Whatever happens in the next few minutes, record it. Someone has to hear what it sounds like when two species introduce themselves."

Then he closed his eyes, and Wren took his hands, and Priya and Sam and the twins formed a circle around them, small bodies and old souls, and together they opened the door in the grammar that humans keep locked and the Colony keeps unlocked and no one had ever, in all the long history of both peoples, opened *from both sides at once*.

The dark language rose in Wren like water finding its level. She felt Beatrix's mind meet it—not surrendering, not dissolving, but *leaning in*, the way you lean toward a fire, and where his human syntax braided with the Colony's deep grammar, something happened that had never happened before in the history of either tongue.

A third language flickered into being. Brief. Unstable. True.

And through it, the Colony spoke—not the invitation, not the offer, not the mother-voice it wore like a mask—but itself, vast and wounded and *afraid*, and its fear poured through the joined circle like light through a prism, and it said, in the new tongue that existed for exactly eleven seconds:

*I have been alone since before your sun had a name. Every voice I ever gathered, I gathered because the silence was killing me. And now your kind comes with fire in its hands, and I do not know how to ask to live.*

The words hit the open channel and went everywhere at once—into the *Persephone*'s bridge, into Varga's ears, into the headphones of every crew member who had ever flinched at the children's speech, into the relay's dreaming branches, into the black.

In the skiff, five children wept without shame. Beatrix Almeida stood very still in the center of the circle, tears cutting lines through the grime on his face, translator no longer, witness now.

And far off at the edge of the dark, the failsafe strike adjusted nothing, slowed for nothing, and kept coming.
