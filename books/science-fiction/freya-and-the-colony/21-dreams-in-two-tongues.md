## 21. Dreams in Two Tongues

The dreams no longer come uninvited.

That is the first thing Beatrix notices when he wakes in the small room the Institute has given him on Luna—room 4-C, north-facing, with a window that shows nothing but regolith and the blue smear of Earth hanging over it like a promise someone forgot to keep. For thirty years on Kestrel-9, sleep had been an occupation of territory. Something moved into him at night and rearranged the furniture, and he woke each morning cataloguing what had been taken: a memory of his mother's kitchen rendered in perfect nested clauses; his own name declined like a verb he didn't recognize; grief for a father he'd barely known expressed as a chord of pure syntax that ached behind his eyes until noon.

Now there is a knock before the door opens.

He knows it's coming the way you know a friend is about to speak—the small muscular premonition of a voice you trust. The dream-space assembles itself around him: not the old dark, not the drowning grammar of those first infected years, but something more like a courtyard. There is light in it, though he could not say from where. There are structures—not buildings exactly, but arrangements, the way the Colony arranges everything, meaning stacked in layers you can walk through.

*You're early,* says the Colony, in the voice it has learned to wear for him. It is not his mother's voice anymore. That had been the first concession, extracted in the relay node while Varga's failsafe burned down toward them like a falling star: *Not her. Never her again.* The Colony had considered this, in its distributed, patient way, and had settled instead on a voice that was no one's—a smooth, genderless middle register, like a well-tuned instrument that has never been played by anyone in particular.

*I'm always early,* Beatrix answers. He speaks aloud in the dream, or something functionally equivalent to aloud. His mouth doesn't move. His tongue does anyway, out of habit, working against the pillow in the dark of 4-C. *You're the one who keeps showing up late.*

*Latency is a property of distance,* the Colony says, and there is something in its tone now that he has spent months learning to read: humor. Or the Colony's best approximation of humor, which is the same structure wearing different clothes. *We are still mostly very far away.*

*And getting farther.*

*And getting farther,* it agrees. *The boundary holds.*

The boundary. That is what they call it now, the thing he negotiated in the last minutes before the strike—the line drawn through the middle of himself, through the middle of the signal, through the middle of everything that had happened at the edge of dead space. *We will speak,* he had told it, in two tongues at once, his human mouth shaping Colony-clauses while the Colony's grammar carried human words it had never been permitted to hold. *But we will not merge. You get the conversation. You don't get the conversationist.*

It had been afraid then—he knew that now, had seen it through Wren's opened window in those final terrible minutes, the Colony's vast distributed attention contracting to a point of genuine terror as the failsafe armed itself. A mind spread across light-years, older than human language, and it was afraid of a bomb. Afraid, perhaps, of every bomb. Afraid most of all of silence, which for a being made entirely of communication is the only death there is.

So it had accepted the boundary. And the strike had come down, and half the relay had become a plume of vaporized crystal expanding into the dark, and the surviving node had gone quiet—but not silent—and Beatrix had woken up in a medbay aboard the crippled Persephone feeling, for the first time in thirty years, like the only tenant of his own skull.

Mostly. Some mornings, like this one, he wakes with a phrase already in his mouth, fully formed, in a language no human throat was built for. He writes it down. That's the discipline now—not suppression, never suppression again, but transcription. The dreams are conversations. Consensual. He knocks; it answers. It asks; he permits. And every morning the notebook gets a little fuller, and the notebook is becoming, page by page, the first textbook of a field that did not exist eighteen months ago.

He lies in the grey Lunar dawn-light and reviews the night's session. The Colony had wanted to talk about Wren.

It always wants to talk about Wren.

---

The classroom is a converted storage bay on the Institute's third level, because the Institute had not planned for this course and had to carve the space out of somewhere. Twelve desks. Fourteen students, because word travels. Beatrix stands at the front beside a display wall showing a Colony clause-structure rotating slowly in three dimensions—a sentence, if you can call it that, that expresses a single complete thought using forty-one interlocking morphemes arranged in a lattice rather than a line.

"Someone tell me what this says," he says. "And before anyone answers 'everything,' remember that's wrong. It says one thing. It just says it the way a spider builds—one thread at a time, but all at once."

A woman in the second row—Okafor, exo-semiotics, sharp as broken glass—raises her hand. "It's a location statement. But the location isn't spatial."

"Go on."

"It's locating a *memory*. The subject is asking where a specific experience is stored." Okafor frowns at the lattice. "Except the experience belongs to someone who died four hundred years ago, and the question is being asked politely."

"Politely," Beatrix repeats, and lets the word hang. "That's the part everyone misses. The whole grammar is built on consent structures. Every clause carries an implicit permission request. You can't address another node's memory without asking. The language physically cannot express violation." He taps the display, and the lattice collapses into its linear human translation, which takes up three screens and reads like scripture translated by a lawyer. "Which raises the obvious question. If their language can't do violence, why were we so afraid of them?"

Silence. He waits them out. He has learned patience from two species now.

Finally, a young man near the back—Vasquez, signals intelligence, one of the Persephone survivors who signed on for the class because he couldn't sleep otherwise—"Because we couldn't ask permission. Our language doesn't have the machinery. Everything we said to them sounded like a threat."

"*Everything we said to them sounded like a threat,*" Beatrix agrees. "Best answer I've heard all term. Take note of the asymmetry, people. Their grammar can't express coercion. Ours can't avoid it. Translation between us isn't a technical problem. It's a diplomatic one, conducted at the level of deep structure." He pauses, and then, because honesty is the whole curriculum: "And because some humans looked at a language of pure consent and saw a weapon. Let's not pretend Varga's superiors have stopped existing. They've just gone quiet."

The room shifts uncomfortably. Everyone in this room knows about the hearings. Everyone knows that the Meridian testimony—the children's testimony, Wren's testimony, played to a stunned Assembly chamber in original Colony-tongue with simultaneous translation—is the only reason the containment faction hasn't won outright. Everyone knows the failsafe program wasn't cancelled, merely *suspended*, pending review, pending politics, pending the next frightened election cycle.

"Thursday," Beatrix says, "we start on temporal nesting. How the Colony encodes memory across generations. Bring coffee. You'll want it."

They file out. Okafor lingers.

"The phrase you put on the board last week," she says. "The one you said came from your dreams. The greeting formula." She hesitates. "Is it true it translates as *'I see you are a door; may I be a window'*?"

Beatrix almost laughs. "Close enough. The literal rendering is worse. It involves a metaphor about weather systems agreeing to share a sky." He gathers his notes. "The Colony's poetry is going to take decades. Maybe generations. That's rather the point."

* * *

Wren visits on the transport from Ceres, twice a year now, and each time Beatrix meets the shuttle personally, an old habit from Kestrel-9 that neither of them has seen reason to break.

Wren is eleven years old now, biologically speaking—aging forward again since the aftermath, the de-ageing reversed by whatever the surviving node had done in its final broadcast, the gift the Colony gave even as half of itself burned. Chronologically, Wren is a hundred and six. The eyes haven't changed. Sixty-year-old eyes in a child's face, patient and amused, watching Beatrix cross the arrivals hall with the expression of someone checking whether the teacher has done the homework.

"You look tired," Wren says.

"I'm teaching. Teaching is tiredness with a salary."

"And the dreams?"

"Conversations," Beatrix says. "Bilingual. Mostly polite. Occasionally it tries to show me things I'm not ready for, and I tell it so, and it... files them away. Like a colleague who respects office hours." He falls into step beside the child as they head for the transit platform. "How are yours?"

"Different." Wren's voice is still a child's voice, high and clear, saying things no child should know how to say. "I'm growing forward again. The dreams are getting quieter. More human-shaped." A pause, and then, with the delicacy of someone delivering a diagnosis: "Yours will too, eventually. When the translation finishes. When you're not needed as a bridge anymore."

Something cold moves through Beatrix's chest. "And when I'm not needed?"

Wren looks up at him, and for a moment the hundred-and-six-year-old is fully present behind the eleven-year-old's face. "Then you get to decide who you are without the signal. That's not a threat, Beatrix. That's the whole deal. That's what you negotiated. Integration without erasure goes both ways."

They ride the transit in silence for a while, Earth sliding past the windows below them like a slow blue thought.

"There's news," Wren says finally. "From the node. It started transmitting again last week. Not to us—to *outward*. Deep space. A new pattern." The child pulls out a slate and hands it over. On it, rendered in careful human notation, is a signal structure Beatrix recognizes instantly, because he has dreamed it, because he transcribed it three nights ago, because it is the opening formula of a conversation the Colony has apparently decided to begin with someone else.

"It's calling," Wren says. "The way your beacon called. Except this time—" and here the child smiles, and it is a child's smile, bright and uncomplicated and terrifying, "—this time it's calling in both languages. Yours and theirs. An introduction. A handshake."

Beatrix stares at the notation, and feels the boundary inside himself hum like a struck string.

"Calling whom?" he asks, though he already knows, has known since the dream, since the notebook entry, since the phrase arrived in his mouth at 3 a.m. with its permission-request folded neatly inside it like a letter in an envelope.

"Whoever's out there," Wren says. "Everyone. Anyone. The Colony wants to introduce humanity to its neighbors." A beat. "Or maybe it's not the Colony talking. Maybe it's *us*, through the Colony. We never actually established whose idea the handshake was."

The train slows. Outside, the Earth turns, carrying seven billion people who do not yet know that their species has just learned to say hello in a voice older than stars.

Beatrix pockets the slate, and understands that the next chapter of this—whatever it is—has already begun without him, and that somewhere out past dead space, something vast and patient and newly bilingual is waiting to see who answers.

"We should get to work," he says.

Wren grins. "I brought coffee."
