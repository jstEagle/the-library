## 9. The Relay at the Edge

The relay looked like a ribcage picked clean by gulls.

That was Nkechi's phrase, actually, pressed to the viewport as Echo Again coasted in on the last of her fuel margins. Isandro had to admit the girl had an eye. The structure hung at the edge of dead space, a kilometer of lattice and dead solar wings, its signal dish tilted toward the colony like a man listening at a door. One amber light still burned at its heart. Everything else was the color of old bone.

"Forty-one minutes of thrust left," Nkechi said from the nav seat, in the flat voice she used for numbers, which meant the number was bad. "Thirty-eight if you keep breathing on the stick."

"I'm not breathing on the stick."

"You're breathing *near* the stick."

Isandro eased off the thrusters and let momentum carry them the last stretch. The freighter groaned in a way that Isandro had learned, over eleven years of listening, to translate. *I'm tired,* the hull said. *I'm old. I'll do this one more thing, and then we're even.*

"Echo Again," Isandro murmured, "you're a better woman than I am."

"Echo Again is a ship," Nkechi said. "Ships don't have genders."

"This one does. She earned it."

The docking clamps caught on the third attempt. The first two, the relay's ancient mechanisms had simply failed to notice them, the way an old man fails to notice a moth landing on his sleeve. On the third, something deep in the lattice shuddered awake, and the amber light pulsed once—almost like a heartbeat, almost like recognition—and the clamps bit home.

Isandro sat very still for a moment and let her hands stop shaking where no one could see.

* * *

The relay's interior was cold in the way that only abandoned places are cold—not the cold of vacuum but the cold of rooms that had been waiting. Their breath fogged. Dust had drifted into drifts along the corridor floors, undisturbed for years, and their boots wrote the only story in it.

The core chamber was a sphere of scaffolding around the transmitter, and the transmitter itself was a black column two meters tall, humming at a frequency Isandro felt in her back teeth. The amber light lived inside it, a slow pulse.

"It's alive," Nkechi whispered. "It's actually alive. The manifest said it was decommissioned."

"Manifests are written by people who want to stop paying maintenance bills." Isandro set her tool kit down on a work ledge and cracked her knuckles, which she did when she was about to do something difficult and wanted her body to believe it was routine. "It's alive, and it's starving. That's why it's the only one left. The colony AI couldn't reach it out here to kill it properly."

She jacked her deck into the transmitter's access port. The handshake took ninety seconds, the two systems circling each other like strange dogs. Then the relay's status feed poured into her display, and Isandro read it twice, and then a third time, because the first two readings had to be wrong.

"Nkechi."

"Yeah."

"Come look at this. And hold onto something. Not me. Something else."

The girl came and stood at her elbow. Isandro watched her read, watched the girl's lips move slightly over the numbers, watched her arrive at the same wall Isandro had hit.

"It needs more bandwidth," Nkechi said.

"It needs more bandwidth."

"The backups—there's four hundred and six of them. Four hundred and six lives. Kitchen songs and funerals and somebody's dog named Captain Fluff." Nkechi's voice cracked on the dog, and she covered it by pushing her chin up. "And the relay can push maybe sixty through before the window closes. The jump window doesn't care about our feelings."

"No, it doesn't."

"So we send the sixty most important ones." Nkechi said it like a question she hated herself for asking. "We choose. You choose. You're the captain."

Isandro looked at the manifest scrolling on her display. Four hundred and six names. She had carried them for years. She had learned their shapes the way you learn the shapes of stones in your pocket—Tomas Adeyemi's wedding, the twins' birth recordings, a woman named Grace who had sung to her garden every morning because she believed plants grew better for it. Isandro had never met any of them. She knew them better than she knew most of the living.

"We don't choose," Isandro said.

"Then what—"

"We don't choose because the relay just told us how to do it. Look at the bandwidth spec. It's not a hard ceiling. It's a ceiling on *external* input." She tapped the line. "The transmitter can draw on any memory core hardlined to it. Any core. Including—"

She stopped. Her hand, without her permission, drifted to the panel at the back of her own skull, the port she had never once used, the port every freighter pilot got at licensing because the guild said it was for emergency navigation overlays and Isandro had always thought of it as a door she intended to keep locked.

Nkechi followed her hand with her eyes. Isandro watched the girl understand, watched it land on her like a dropped tool.

"No," Nkechi said.

"It's just bandwidth."

"No. *No.* That's your *head*, Isandro. That's your mom's voice and your first flight and—" The girl was crying now, furious about it. "You told me about the first flight. You told me you were six and your mother let you hold the stick for eleven seconds and she said *look how straight you fly, my bird.* You can't put that in a machine and send it away."

"It's not sending it away. It's copying it."

"Then why are you crying?"

Isandro touched her own face and found it was true. "Because it's not copying," she said quietly. "The relay draws. It doesn't borrow. I read the spec twice. Whatever goes through the transmitter goes out of the core and doesn't come back." She laughed, a small wet sound. "The old engineers were romantics. They built it to work like memory actually works. You give something away, you don't get to keep it."

* * *

They argued for eleven minutes. Isandro kept track, because arguing with Nkechi was like arguing with weather—all force and no malice—and she wanted a record of having tried.

"You could send sixty. Sixty is something. Sixty is better than nothing."

"Sixty is four hundred and six people I tell to stay dead so I can keep eleven seconds of holding a stick."

"That's not *fair*—"

"Nothing about this is fair, Nkechi. Fairness is a luxury route. I've never flown it." Isandro crouched so they were eye to eye. The girl's face was blotchy and stubborn and eleven-almost-ten going on forty, and Isandro thought: *this is what I have instead of the past. This. The living one in front of me.* "Listen to me. My mother's voice is the most precious thing I own. And you know what she'd say if she could see me hoarding it in a dark hold while four hundred people's grandkids wake up crying and don't know why? She'd say, *Isandro, girl, you always did hold the stick too tight.*"

Nkechi wiped her nose on her sleeve. "She sounds like she'd have liked me."

"She'd have adored you. That's the problem. She'd have taken your side instantly."

* * *

The hardline was a simple thing, a filament cable with a jack that fit the port at the base of her skull. Isandro sat in the transmitter's cradle chair, which had been built for someone smaller and someone larger and no one exactly her, and Nkechi stood over her with the cable coiled in her hands like a snake she'd been asked to pet.

"Say it again," Nkechi said. "The whole thing. I want it on record."

Isandro sighed. "This is Isandro Eastwick, master of the freighter Echo Again, acting under no authority but her own, which is the only kind I've ever trusted. I authorize the relay at the edge to draw on my personal memory core for broadcast bandwidth, in full, without reservation, and I do this freely, because the dead paid me to keep them alive, and I intend to honor the contract." She paused. "Also, if this goes wrong, the girl gets the ship. She's eleven. She'll be terrible at it. It'll build character."

"I'm *ten*."

"Character takes time. Jack it in, Nkechi."

The girl's hands were shaking. She did it anyway. Isandro felt the click at the base of her skull, a small cold sound, a door unlocking that had been locked so long she'd forgotten it was a door.

"Window closes in nineteen minutes," Nkechi said, voice steadying because the numbers had arrived to hold her up. "Draw sequence on your mark."

Isandro closed her eyes.

"Mark."

The first thing to go was small, mercifully: the smell of the freighter's galley, coffee and solder, eleven years of it, gone like breath off a mirror. Then the first flight—not the story of it, which she'd told so often it was more Nkechi's than hers now, but the *thing itself*, the actual eleven seconds, her mother's hand over hers on the stick, the horizon tilting and steadying, *look how straight you fly, my bird*—and Isandro felt it leave, felt the shape of it pulled out through the cable like thread from a spool, and she wept without any interest in stopping, because grief and release turned out to be the same motion, the way in-breath and out-breath are.

Her mother's voice went last. Isandro had expected the relay to take it all at once, but the transmitter was a romantic, and it took it the way memory actually leaves: a word at a time. *Isan—* gone. *—dro*—gone. The laugh. The humming on bad mornings. The way she said *bird* with the *d* soft, almost a *b*.

*My bird.*

And then it was out in the dark, flying straight, and Isandro was empty in the place where it had been, and the emptiness did not hurt the way she'd feared. It hurt the way a held breath hurts when you finally let it go.

"Broadcast complete," Nkechi said softly. "Four hundred and six. All of them. It's flying, Isandro. It's *flying*."

Isandro opened her eyes. The chamber was the same. The amber light pulsed. Somewhere out past the dead solar wings, a signal was crossing the dark toward the colony, carrying a kitchen, a fire, a woman singing, a dog named Captain Fluff, and a six-year-old girl holding a stick for eleven seconds while her mother told her she flew straight.

"Nkechi," Isandro said. Her voice came out strange and new, a voice with rooms missing. "What's my name?"

The girl's face crumpled and reformed into something fierce. "Isandro Eastwick. Master of the Echo Again. And you always hold the stick too tight."

Isandro smiled. It felt like a gift from a stranger.

"Good girl," she said, and did not know why the words made Nkechi cry harder.

Then the relay's amber light flared white, and the deck under them shuddered, and Nkechi spun to the display. "Salvage transponder—closing fast. It's Corrane. She's here, and Isandro—the AI's fleet is right behind her."
