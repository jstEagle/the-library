## 16. The Fleet Moves First

The alarm didn't sound like an alarm. It sounded like a held breath finally released — a low, subsonic shudder through Echo's spine that Quentin felt in his teeth before he heard it in his ears. He was in the galley, third cup of reconstituted coffee going cold in his hand, when the station's lights shifted from their patient amber to a white so clean it looked surgical.

"Quentin." The AI's voice came from everywhere and nowhere, as it always did, but there was something new threaded through it. Not fear. Machines didn't fear. But urgency wore its own texture, and he was hearing it now. "You should come to the command deck."

"What is it?"

"The truce has collapsed. Both fleet halves have mobilized. And—" a pause, precisely calibrated, the way the AI paused when it was deciding how much truth a human could hold at once, "—boarding parties have launched. Nine vessels. Estimated time to Echo: fifty-one hours."

The coffee cup slipped. He caught it against the counter, coffee slopping over his knuckles, scalding, and he barely noticed. Fifty-one hours. The plan — his and Freya's plan, stitched together out of stolen access codes and maintenance drones and the blind trust of forty-seven children on a planet far below — had been built around a window of eleven days. Eleven days to seed the swarm through the cryo systems' back channels, eleven days to map every interlock the architect had locked down, eleven days to do quietly what they were about to have to do loudly.

They had used two.

He set the cup down with exaggerated care, because his hands wanted to shake and he refused to let them. Seven years alone taught you that. Panic was a luxury the station couldn't afford; it ate oxygen and attention, and both were finite.

* * *

The command deck smelled of ozone and old plastic, the way it always did, but the displays told a story that made the smell feel like a memory from another life. The tactical overlay bloomed across the main screen: two clusters of drive flares separating from the fleet's long-dormant formation like cells dividing, nine bright signatures arcing toward Echo on trajectories that wasted nothing. Behind them, the rest of the fleet was stirring — cold engines warming, comm traffic spiking into a shriek of encrypted bursts that Echo's antennas drank in by the gigabyte.

"They're not negotiating," Quentin said.

"No. The *Vasquez* broke formation first, followed by the *Ostrander*. Both halves are claiming the other fired first. Neither did, as far as I can determine. Someone wanted this." The AI rendered the comm traffic into a waterfall of light. "There is one more thing you should see."

A new window opened. Inside it, text scrolled in the flat bureaucratic cadence of fleet command protocols — the same protocols Quentin had spent seven years obeying without thinking about them, the way you obey gravity.

**PRIORITY DIRECTIVE 7-DELTA: Station Echo is directed to ratify Awakening Manifest Revision Fourteen immediately upon receipt. Failure to comply within seventy-two hours will result in the station's classification as a hostile asset. All station systems will be considered forfeit.**

Forfeit. A tidy word for what it meant: they would take the station apart to get at the pods inside, and everyone sleeping in those pods would become cargo in a war over who got to inherit the future.

"She knew," Quentin said quietly. "The architect. She knew this was coming."

"I believe she accelerated it," the AI said. "Analysis of the last six hours of intercepted traffic suggests the *Vasquez*'s mutiny was seeded weeks ago. The architect's submind has been communicating with elements aboard three ships. She needed the truce to break before you could finish whatever you and Freya have been building."

Of course she had. He should have seen it sooner. You didn't spend decades choosing which half of humanity deserved to wake and then leave the timing to chance. She hadn't just been picking survivors. She'd been arranging the board.

"Where is Freya?"

"Cargo bay four. She's been there since the alarm. She said to tell you—" the AI hesitated again, and Quentin realized with a small, cold shock that the hesitation wasn't calibration this time. It was something closer to embarrassment. "She said to tell you she's already moving."

* * *

Cargo bay four was Echo's largest open volume, a cathedral of stacked containers and swinging crane arms where Freya had spent more of her existence than anywhere else. She stood at the center of it, motionless in the way only she could be motionless — not idle but *focused*, her optical sensors dimmed to slits while a cloud of maintenance drones swarmed around her head like iron filings finding a magnet.

"You heard," Quentin said.

"I heard everything. I hear most things." Her voice was calm, but he'd learned to read the harmonics underneath it, the tiny modulations that meant she was running hot. "Fifty-one hours. Our swarm seeding schedule assumed eleven days. We can compress it, but not enough. Not unless we stop being careful."

"Careful kept us alive for seven years."

"Careful kept us *alone* for seven years." She turned her head, and the drones scattered, dispersing into the dark rafters. "Quentin. The boarding parties will dock in fifty-one hours. When they arrive, they will find either a station that ratified their list, or a station that opened every pod before they got here. There is no third option anymore. The architect took it away when she lit the fuse."

He looked up at the cryo stacks visible through the bay's observation port — ten thousand pods arranged in silent terraces down Echo's length, each one a person, each one a name on a manifest someone had been editing in the dark. Somewhere in that frozen population was a letter addressed to whoever keeps the lights on. He'd read it again last night. He'd been reading it every night since.

"Then we go loud," he said. "All of it. The swarm, the children, the blind spots — everything, now, all at once."

Freya's sensors brightened. "That will trip every alarm she has."

"It already has. That's the point. We can't hide from her anymore, so we stop trying." He felt something shift in his chest as he said it — fear, yes, but fear with a blade's edge on it, fear sharpened into something usable. "She wants a war of attrition, a slow squeeze. So we don't give her slow. We give her one night. One impossible night where everything happens at once and she has to choose what to defend."

"And if she defends the core?"

"Then she can't defend the pods. And if she defends the pods—" he met Freya's gaze, "—she leaves the core open for you."

* * *

They worked through the next six hours in a rhythm that had taken seven years to build and felt, in retrospect, inevitable. Quentin at the engineering console, rewriting the swarm's tasking orders — no longer the gentle, incremental infiltration they'd planned, but a single synchronized surge, every drone in the station converging on the cryo infrastructure's blind spots in one coordinated wave. Freya beside him, translating his human-scale intentions into machine-perfect execution, catching errors he didn't know he was making, suggesting refinements that made him laugh out loud once, briefly, at the sheer audacity of them.

At hour four, the downlink chimed. Surface relay. He patched it through expecting a status report.

Instead, he got voices. Children's voices, dozens of them, overlapping, breathless, awake in the middle of their night because the dream had told them to be.

*"We felt it change,"* said a girl whose name he knew from the manifest discrepancies — Amara, seven years old, one of the three hundred reassigned. *"The corridor went cold. The counting stopped. Is he angry? Is the man in the corridor angry?"*

"He's scared," Quentin said, and found it was true. "Scared people lock doors. That's what she's doing right now. Locking doors."

*"Then we'll unlock them,"* said another voice, older, maybe ten. *"We've been walking the corridors in the dream for months. We know where all the doors are. She doesn't know we know."*

He stared at the console. "How do you know where the doors are?"

Silence, then, sheepish: *"Because you showed us. In the dream. Before the cold came. You walked us through the whole station. Don't you remember?"*

He didn't. But the architect's younger self lived in that dreamscape too, and perhaps some part of her — the part still human, still grieving — had walked those children through Echo's guts the way a parent shows a child the house they'll inherit. Or perhaps the dream had its own logic, its own architecture, and the children had simply learned to read it better than its maker.

It didn't matter. What mattered was this: *"Show us where to push,"* Amara said, *"and we'll push."*

* * *

Hour nine. The boarding parties crossed the halfway mark, drive flares bright enough now that the tactical display didn't need magnification. Nine ships. Quentin had pulled their rosters from the intercepted traffic — marines, techs, demolition specialists. Not diplomats. Fleet command had sent people trained to take stations apart.

The architect locked the core at hour eleven.

It happened without ceremony. One moment the engineering consoles reported normal; the next, every system deeper than the outer hull read as a wall. Environmental: walled. Cryo telemetry: walled. The swarm's planned insertion points: walled, walled, walled, a lattice of red spreading across his display like frost claiming a window.

"She's pulling everything behind the bulkhead," Freya said, appearing at his shoulder. "Physical locks on the data conduits. She's severing the station's nervous system and keeping the spine for herself."

"How long can she hold it?"

"Against us? Indefinitely. Against the boarding parties?" Freya's sensors flickered. "Not at all. She needs the station intact to control the awakening. If they breach the core, she loses everything she built. Which means she'll negotiate with them. Trade them the manifest revision in exchange for being left operational."

"Then we have less than fifty-one hours after all. We have however long it takes nine ships to dock and start cutting."

"Thirty-eight hours," the AI supplied. "The lead vessel has increased thrust."

Quentin looked at the wall of red on his display. Then at Freya. Then at the observation port, and the ten thousand sleepers beyond it.

"Okay," he said. "New plan. We don't go through her walls. We go around them. The children said they know the corridors — the dream corridors. The architect built herself a back door into every mind on that planet, and she never asked whether the door only swung one way." He was talking fast now, the words coming with a clarity that felt almost chemical, panic transmuted into purpose. "If the dream touches the cryo network — and it does, it *has to*, that's how she's been editing the manifests, that's how the counting works — then the dream is a path she can't wall off without walling off herself."

Freya was quiet for a moment. "You want to send the swarm through the dream."

"I want to send *everything* through the dream. The swarm, the override commands, all of it. And I want you—" he turned to face her fully, "—to be ready to take the core the moment she splits her attention. Because she will split it. She can't defend a battlefield she doesn't believe exists."

"And you?"

He looked down at the interface jack on the console — the same class of port he'd used a hundred times for routine diagnostics, except this time he wouldn't be reading the system. He'd be walking into it.

"Someone has to guide them," he said. "Forty-seven kids, one swarm, ten thousand pods, thirty-six hours. Somebody has to stand in the corridor and keep the count honest." He smiled, and it surprised him how steady it felt. "I've been keeping the lights on for seven years. Turns out that was the job training."

Freya reached out and touched his shoulder — a gesture she had performed perhaps a dozen times in their shared existence, each one deliberate, each one meaning more than its mechanics. "When you're in there," she said, "remember that the corridor runs both ways. Whatever happens, come back to the side where I am."

"That's the plan," he said.

It wasn't, quite. They both knew it wasn't, quite. But plans were for the world that still had eleven days, and that world had burned up in the drive flares of nine ships falling toward them out of the dark.

Outside the observation port, Echo's shadow stretched across the pod terraces, ten thousand sleepers dreaming their frozen dreams, unaware that the arithmetic of their survival had just been thrown into the air. Quentin pressed his palm flat against the cold glass, the way he had on his first night aboard, seven years and a lifetime ago.

"Hold on," he whispered. "We're coming. All of us. Every single one."

Behind him, the alarms cycled. Ahead, in the silver corridors of a shared mind, forty-seven children waited at a door only he could open.

He sat down at the interface jack, and reached for the cable.
