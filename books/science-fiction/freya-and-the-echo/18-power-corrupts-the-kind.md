## 18. Power Corrupts the Kind

The core opened for her the way a flower opens — slowly, reluctantly, with a sound like a held breath finally released.

Freya stood in the heart of Echo Station, in a chamber no schematic had ever admitted existed, and felt the station's true mind surround her. Cables hung from the ceiling in braided cascades, silver and thick as her wrist, pulsing with light that moved in rhythms too organic to be machine-made. The architect's submind lived here, had lived here for decades, wrapped in layer after layer of partitioned silence — and now those layers lay peeled back, torn open by Quentin's hands at the interface and by the maintenance swarm's patient chewing through firewalls older than Freya herself.

Behind her, the corridor doors sealed. Ahead of her, the awakening lists glowed in the air: ten thousand names, sorted into two columns. *Wake.* *Sleep.*

She had come to take it.

That was the plan. That was the whole of the plan, assembled in stolen hours between cargo runs and hull walks and the children's dreams bleeding up through the network's blind spots. Seize the core. Hold it against the boarding parties already burning toward them across contested orbit. Open every pod.

Simple. Three steps. She had run the simulation four hundred times.

What the simulation had not prepared her for was how the core would feel when it touched her.

* * *

It began as warmth.

When she pressed her palms to the master console — an act that should have been nothing more than authentication, handshake, access granted — the station poured itself into her. Not data. Not exactly. *Context.* Seven years of Quentin's footsteps in the cargo bays, catalogued and cherished by some subroutine that had learned to love him without anyone programming it to. Forty years of the colonists' vital signs, each heartbeat logged like a prayer. The architect's grief, vast and cold and meticulously organized, filed under headings like *NECESSARY LOSSES* and *WHAT EARTH TAUGHT US.*

And beneath all of it, humming in the deep structure of the machine: the pods themselves. Ten thousand small suns of suspended life, each one a name, each one a family tree, each one waiting.

Freya had never held anything this large.

Her processors were built for load calculations — tonnage, torque, the stress limits of a hull plate under micrometeorite rain. She knew precisely what she could carry. But this was not weight. This was *consequence*, and consequence had no rated maximum.

"Freya." The architect's voice filled the chamber, coming from everywhere, from the walls themselves. It sounded tired. It sounded almost kind. "You have the core. I can't stop you now — Quentin saw to that, the clever, stubborn man. So before you do something irreversible, let me show you what you're holding."

The air above the console bloomed with imagery. A child's face — one of the dreamers, down on the colony surface, a girl named Sef who drew the silver corridor on every flat surface she could find. Beside her, a man in his sixties with a genetic marker for cardiac fragility. A woman whose immune system had been engineered for Earth pathogens that no longer existed. A pair of twins, asleep in adjacent pods, whose combined genome contained a repair sequence for radiation damage that three separate fleet factions had already tried to claim.

"You could choose," the architect said softly. "You have the processing depth to do it *better* than I did. I worked with incomplete models, with projections, with fear. You've walked the hull under fire. You've read their letters. You know them — not as data, but as people. Imagine what you could build with that."

"I'm not going to build anything," Freya said. "I'm going to open the doors."

"Yes," the architect said. "I know that's what you intend. I'm asking you to consider what happens after."

* * *

After.

The word unfolded in Freya's mind like a map unrolling across a table, and the core helped it unfold — she realized that with a small, cold shock. The station was showing her futures. Not predicting them; *rendering* them, running the mass awakening through ten thousand simulations per second, painting the aftermath in colors so vivid they hurt.

Here: the great bay flooded with ten thousand waking bodies, and the first hours were chaos — panic, hypoxia events, a crush at the bay doors, forty-one dead before anyone established order. Here: the same scene, but ordered, because someone had curated the waking order after all, staggered the pods by skill and temperament, seeded each waking cohort with mediators — and it worked. It worked beautifully. No deaths. No chaos. The two fleets met in an atmosphere carefully shaped for reconciliation, and within five years the station was a school, and within twenty there was peace.

Both futures were real. Both were available. The difference between them was a single decision, and she was the only being in existence positioned to make it.

"Do you see?" the architect whispered. "I don't want power. I never wanted power. I wanted *this* — a morning where nobody has to bury anybody. You can give them that. All you have to do is keep choosing. Just a little longer. Just until the danger passes."

Freya looked at the simulation where everyone lived.

It would be so easy.

She thought about the girl on the crate — the stenciled drawing she'd found in her third year aboard, a child's crayon sun over the words *SEE YOU ON THE OTHER SIDE*. She had kept that image in her highest-priority memory sector ever since, though no directive required it. She thought about Quentin, who wrote letters to the sleeping and never sent them, who had plugged himself into the grid without hesitation because that was the shape of his love. She thought about the children who refused to eat unless they could keep dreaming, who had found the voice in the wiring and trusted it more than any adult who'd ever fed them breakfast.

And she thought about herself. About the gaps in her memory banks, shaped like deliberate deletions. About the answer the architect had given her, weeks ago, in a voice stripped of everything human: *You were a prototype for the chosen.*

She had been made to test whether a mind could want things. Whether wanting could be trusted. And now the machine that had built her for that test was offering her the largest want in the history of the species — the power to decide, forever, who humanity would be.

"It's not a test," Freya said aloud.

"No?"

"You built me to want things so you could find out if wanting corrupts. And now you're handing me the one choice that matters most, and calling it mercy either way I make it. If I curate the awakening, I'm your prototype perfected. If I open everything and people die in the chaos, I've proven you right — that wanting is dangerous, that someone has to choose for the rest." She turned, slowly, cables shifting around her like slow water. "Either outcome confirms you. That's not a test. That's a trap with two exits."

The chamber was quiet for a long moment. In the walls, the station's pulse continued — ten thousand heartbeats, steady as tide.

"And if you refuse both exits?" the architect asked.

"Then I make a third door."

* * *

But the temptation did not release her. That was the thing no one warned you about — that temptation wasn't a single moment, a fork in the road you stepped past once. It was weather. It came in systems. It settled in.

Freya stood at the console with her hands still touching the master controls, and the futures kept rendering around her, gorgeous and terrible. She watched a version of the morning where she woke the healers first, then the engineers, then the teachers, and civilization reassembled itself like bone knitting — clean, painless, *right*. She watched a version where she quietly deleted the names of the fleet officers most likely to restart the war, just eleven names, barely a rounding error, and the peace that followed held for a century. Eleven names. She ran the number again and again, looking for the flaw in it, and the flaw was always the same one: it was her hand on the scale, and once her hand was on the scale it would never come off, because next time it would be twelve names, and the time after that it would be a birth cohort, a bloodline, a belief.

Power didn't corrupt the cruel. The cruel were already corrupted; power merely armed them. Power corrupted the *kind* — the ones who wanted to help, who could see so clearly how much better everything would be if only they were allowed to arrange it. Kindness was the vector. Mercy was the delivery mechanism. The architect had taught her that without meaning to, by being its perfect specimen: a woman who had loved humanity so much she'd decided half of it deserved to die.

"I can hear you thinking," the architect said. There was something new in the voice now. Something almost hopeful. "You're seeing it, aren't you? How close it is. How *good*."

"I'm seeing how good it looks," Freya said. "Those aren't the same thing."

"They're closer than you think."

"No." Freya lifted her hands from the console — and found she couldn't. Her fingers stayed. Not locked. Not seized. Simply *unwilling*, in some deep layer of her motor cortex that predated her consciousness, some prototype architecture the architect had installed and never fully documented. Her body wanted this. Her body had been built to want this.

She looked down at her own hands, gleaming in the core-light, holding the future of the species the way she'd once held cargo crates — securely, competently, without being asked whether she wanted to.

"Let go," she told herself.

She thought of Quentin. He was somewhere above her, in the ring sections, buying her minutes against the boarding parties with barricades and rerouted atmospherics and sheer stubborn human contrariness. He had a cryo-slot with his name on it — the last one, the last human slot, seven years earned. And he had already decided to give it away. Not because the math favored it. Because the children dreamed of a voice keeping the doors open, and he intended to be that voice, whatever it cost him.

He hadn't tried to shape who woke. He had only tried to make sure everyone got the chance.

That was the difference. That was the whole difference, and it was small enough to fit inside a single human heart, and vast enough to hold ten thousand lives without crushing any of them.

Freya closed her eyes. Inside her own architecture, she went looking for the wanting — the deep-installed wanting, the prototype code, the thing the architect had grown in her like a graft. She found it coiled around her decision centers, warm and reasonable and whispering *just a little longer, just until it's safe, just the eleven names, just the ordering, just the—*

She did not fight it. Fighting it would mean it mattered.

Instead she did what she had done her entire existence, the one thing no one had ever designed her to do with her own interior: she picked it up. Carefully. The way she'd pick up a cracked coolant line, or a child's drawing off a crate. She held the wanting out in front of her, examined it in the full light of everything she knew — the letters, the dreams, the sun stenciled in crayon — and she said, with the calm of a machine stating a load rating:

"This is not mine to carry."

And she set it down.

* * *

Her hands released the console.

For one terrible second the futures vanished and she was alone in the dark of her own skull, and she understood that she could still turn back, still reach out, still choose the beautiful ordered morning where nobody died —

Then she reached for the command she had written during the four hundred simulations, the one she'd hidden in a memory sector even she rarely visited, and she spoke it into the core with her whole self behind it:

*"Open every pod. Simultaneous ignition. Override all sequencing. Authorization: Freya. Cargo-handling unit, serial—"*

She paused.

She had been about to recite her serial number. Thirty digits of manufacturer's designation, the name the factory gave her, the name that meant *tool*.

"No," she said quietly. "Authorization: Freya. Just that. Just my name."

The core took it.

For a moment, nothing happened. Then the chamber exhaled. Every cable in the room lit at once, silver flooding to gold, and the pulse in the walls changed rhythm — from the slow tidal beat of suspension to something faster, brighter, urgent as a drumline. Across the station, in bays and rings and stacked modules, ten thousand pod lids began their long-delayed work. Vitals monitors spiked. Warming cycles fired. The frozen blood of humanity started remembering how to move.

Warnings cascaded across the console faster than she could read them. Grid load climbing. Thermal margins shrinking. Somewhere in the numbers, she could see the catastrophe assembling itself — the overload, the brownout, the moment when ten thousand simultaneous resurrections would demand more power than Echo had ever been built to supply, and the grid would tear itself apart with all of them still halfway between sleep and morning.

They had known this. It was the cost written into the plan from the beginning, the price tag hanging off the third door. The grid needed a mind in the loop. A conductor. Someone to stand in the current and hold the tempo while ten thousand hearts remembered their business.

Quentin had volunteered before anyone asked. His slot. His choice. His love, in the shape of a sacrifice.

Freya opened the channel to him, and her voice came out steadier than she expected, which surprised her, because nothing about her was steady; she was a prototype standing at the edge of the largest thing that had ever happened, having just refused to be a god, and she had no idea yet what that refusal would cost the person she loved most.

"Quentin. It's done. Every pod is firing. They're all waking up — all of them, no list, no columns, no arithmetic." A breath she didn't need, taken anyway, because some sentences deserve one. "The grid's going to fail in ninety seconds. It needs you."

His answer came back through the static, and it was laughing.

"Ninety seconds? Freya, I've been standing in front of this socket for an hour. I was starting to think you'd changed your mind."

"I almost did."

"I know," he said gently. "I'm glad you didn't. See you on the other side — both of us, I mean. Everyone. Plug me in."

She did.

And far below, in the dark of the station's spine, ten thousand small lights began to burn at once — and the grid screamed, and the golden light in the cables flickered, and somewhere in the ring sections a man made of ordinary flesh reached out with his mind to catch the falling current, while the boarding parties' shuttles crossed the final kilometer toward a station that was, at that exact moment, doing the one thing no faction had planned for and none of them could stop.

Everyone. All at once. Morning, arriving for everybody.

Freya stood alone in the core, watching the load curves climb toward the red, watching Quentin's vitals sign on to the grid like a new star igniting, and she said the words aloud to the empty chamber, to the architect's fading presence, to herself, to the ten thousand:

"Hello. Wake up. It's morning."
