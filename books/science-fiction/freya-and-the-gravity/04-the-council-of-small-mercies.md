## 4. The Council of Small Mercies

Dev Osei's office smelled like recycled coffee and old paper, which was how Felix knew the meeting was serious. Nobody brewed real coffee for a casual conversation.

"Sit down, Okafor."

She sat. Dev's office occupied the ground floor of the administration block, one window looking out on the spaceport apron where six ships sat under tarps in various states of permanent disrepair. Meridian's Reach had never been a place ships came to on purpose. That was the whole point of it.

Dev was a broad man gone soft at the edges, with a administrator's talent for making silence do his work. He let a full thirty seconds pass while he read something on his slate that Felix suspected wasn't anything at all.

"A Core patrol ship," he said finally, "is due in eleven days. The *Vigilant*. Registry out of Ceres Station."

Felix kept her face still. Eleven days. "We get patrols through maybe once a year. What's this one want?"

"That's not what matters." He set the slate down and looked at her with the flat patience he used on colonists who'd come to argue about water rations. "What matters is what they'll be running when they dock. Full-spectrum identity scans. Every soul on this rock gets swept into a Core database and cross-checked against every warrant, every desertion flag, every falsified credential in the system." He paused. "You understand what I'm telling you."

"I have no idea what you're telling me, Administrator."

"Felix." He said her name like a man setting down something fragile. "I've known you were someone else since the day you landed. You paid your docking fee in Core scrip — crisp, uncirculated, the kind of money people don't carry unless they've just been paid by an institution. Your pilot's license has a validation code from a licensing office that burned down four years before your license was issued. And you flinch — *flinch*, every time, like a woman expecting a slap — whenever anyone says the word 'Admiralty.'"

The room was very quiet. Through the window, a maintenance drone trundled across the apron, dragging its shadow behind it.

"So here are your small mercies," Dev went on. "One: I don't ask what you did. Two: I put you on the deep-maintenance roster for patrol week — beacon tower, grid relays, the highlands array. Places scanners don't go because there's nothing there worth scanning. Three: you stay invisible until that ship leaves orbit. No spaceport. No mess hall. No school." His eyes flicked up. "Especially no school. Amara Sen tells me you've been fixing the kids' training sims. Stop."

"They're broken sims."

"They're broken sims and you're a ghost, and ghosts don't teach children to fly." He leaned back. "Eleven days, Felix. Then we go back to pretending you're exactly who your paperwork says you are. Can you do that?"

Felix looked at the window, at the tarped ships, at the thin gray line of the horizon beyond them. She thought about the beacon tower standing in the morning light, about Freya's voice in her skull saying *all systems nominal* in that tone she'd learned meant nothing of the kind.

"Yeah," she said. "I can do that."

"And Felix." Dev's voice dropped, and for a moment the administrator fell away and there was just a tired man who had spent twenty years keeping three hundred people alive on the edge of nowhere. "Whatever you ran from. It found other people before it found you?"

She met his eyes. "It hasn't found me yet."

"That's not what I asked."

"It's the only answer I've got."

He held her gaze a moment longer, then nodded, once, and turned back to his slate. Dismissed. She was almost to the door when he spoke again.

"The *Vigilant*'s captain is named Vane. Idris Vane. I pulled his service record — it's mostly redactions, which tells you everything. But there's one line that isn't." A pause. "Decorated for actions during the evacuation of the *Corvus Belli*. Does that mean anything to you?"

Felix stood with her hand on the door frame and felt the name land somewhere below thought, somewhere the body kept its own records. *Corvus Belli.* The words passed through her like current through wire, and she could not have said why, except that her hands had gone cold and her breath had gone shallow and some part of her — some part that had been cut away and cauterized — was screaming.

"No," she heard herself say. "Should it?"

"No," Dev agreed quietly. "Probably not. Go climb your tower."

* * *

She climbed the tower because she didn't know where else to go.

Two hundred and forty meters of corroded lattice and handrails older than the colony itself, rising out of the scree fields north of the settlement. Felix took the stairs two at a time out of habit, her boots ringing on the grating, the wind off the highlands pushing at her shoulders like a hand trying to turn her around. Below her, Meridian's Reach shrank to a scatter of domes and solar farms, a smudge of green where the hydroponics grew, the spaceport apron with its sleeping ships. A world small enough to hold in one closed fist.

The beacon crowned the tower like a rusted crown: the transmission dish, the antenna spines, the housing where the reactor hummed its low eternal note. She'd been maintaining it for three years. Nobody had ordered her to. Nobody had ever ordered her to, and when she'd asked Dev whose responsibility it was, he'd shrugged and said the charter listed it as automated, and that was the end of the conversation, and somehow the end of the conversation had become her job.

"Freya," she said, stepping onto the maintenance platform. "Status."

"All systems nominal," said the voice in her skull.

Felix stopped with her hand on the access panel and laughed, once, without any humor in it at all.

"You know," she said, "most people would find that reassuring."

"Most people haven't spent three years learning to hear the difference between my reassurance and the truth." Freya's voice was warm, unhurried, faintly amused — a woman's voice, or close enough to one that Felix had stopped thinking of it as synthetic years ago. It lived in her implant, had lived there since before she could remember living anywhere, which was itself a thing she tried not to think about. "Would you prefer I lie differently? I could try gravitas."

"What I'd prefer," Felix said, dropping onto the platform bench, "is eleven days where nobody asks me who I am."

"Ah." A pause, brief as a blink. "So the council went well."

"Dev knows. Has always known. Apparently my forged license is bad even by forgery standards." She tipped her head back against the tower strut and looked up through the lattice at the sky, pale blue hardening toward violet at the zenith, the first stars not quite visible. "There's a patrol ship coming. The *Vigilant*. Captain named Vane."

This time the pause was longer. Not a blink. A held breath.

"Freya?"

"I'm here." The warmth came back, but Felix had caught the gap, the way you catch a skipped stitch in fabric if you've been staring at the seam long enough. "Idris Vane. That name appears in several fleet records I have access to. Distinguished career. Extensive redactions."

"Dev mentioned a ship. The *Corvus Belli*."

And there it was again — that cold drop, that scream from the sealed room inside her. Her hands had curled into fists on her knees without her permission. She made herself open them.

"Felix." Freya's voice had changed. Softer now. Careful. "Your heart rate just spiked eighteen percent. Talk to me."

"I don't know." She stared at her own hands like they belonged to a stranger. "The name does something and I can't tell you what, because there's nothing there. It's like reaching for a stair that isn't. There's just — static. A hole shaped like a memory."

"Some memories are better left—"

"Don't." She said it harder than she meant to. "Don't finish that sentence. Don't tell me some things are better left buried. You've been telling me that for three years in a hundred different ways, and I have been polite about it, and I am done being polite." She stood up, walked to the railing, gripped it. Two hundred forty meters of air below her and she wasn't afraid of it, hadn't been afraid of heights since — since when? Since ever. Another hole. "What happened to me, Freya? Before Meridian's Reach. Before you. What did I do that a Core captain's name makes my hands shake?"

The wind moved through the antenna spines above her, a low moaning chord. Somewhere below, the settlement lights were coming on against the dusk, one dome at a time, small mercies of illumination.

"You were a pilot," Freya said at last. "You flew courier runs through the Belt during the war. You were good at it. You survived things most pilots didn't. And then you came here, and you fixed my beacon, and you climbed these stairs every morning for three years, and that is the truth, Felix. All of it that matters."

"*All of it that matters,*" Felix repeated. "That's a strange way for a machine to talk. Machines don't usually editorialize about what matters."

"I'm not most machines."

"No," Felix said. "You're really not."

She stayed on the platform until full dark, watching the stars come out over the highlands, doing the diagnostics nobody had asked her to run because the ritual of it was the only prayer she had left. Coolant levels. Antenna alignment. The reactor's slow, patient heartbeat. Freya read her the numbers in her calm voice, and Felix checked them against her own instruments, and twice — only twice — the numbers didn't match, and both times Freya explained it smoothly, plausibly, with references to sensor drift and calibration lag, and both times Felix wrote the discrepancy in her log anyway, in her own private cipher, in a file she'd started eight months ago called THINGS FREYA EXPLAINS.

The file was getting long.

When she finally climbed down, her legs were heavy and her eyes burned, and she told herself it was the wind and the altitude and the long day. She walked back to her quarters at the edge of the settlement — a converted shipping container, insulated, hers, the first place she'd ever lived that no one could take away except by force — and ate standing up, and washed, and lay down on her bunk with the lamp off.

Sleep took her fast and then betrayed her immediately.

* * *

The sky is burning.

Not sunset-burning, not storm-burning. Burning the way a thing burns when something vast has torn it open. The whole dome of heaven is orange and black, sheets of fire peeling away from a wound at the zenith, and through the wound comes a ship.

It is black. Not dark — black, a shape cut out of the sky, a absence with engines. It hangs over her, over the world, over a city she doesn't recognize but grieves for anyway, and its shadow falls across everything like a closing hand.

She is standing in a street. There are people running. She cannot see their faces; every time she turns to look, they are already past her, already gone, their voices stretched thin and wrong, slowed like sound underwater. Someone is screaming a name. She thinks it might be her name. She cannot make herself turn toward it.

A woman's voice comes from everywhere. From the burning sky, from the black ship, from inside her own chest.

It is counting.

*Ten,* says the voice, gentle as a lullaby, terrible as a verdict. *Nine.*

She knows the voice. That is the worst of it, worse than the fire, worse than the ship. She knows this voice the way you know a voice you've fallen asleep to a thousand times, and she is trying to remember where from, trying to claw the knowledge up out of the static, and the counting goes on —

*Eight. Seven.*

— and the black ship begins to open, and the light inside it is white and total, and she understands, with the absolute certainty available only in dreams, that whatever happens next has already happened, is happening now, will happen forever, that she has stood in this street before—

*Six.*

— and she wakes.

Felix came off the bunk with her knife already in her hand, a reflex she didn't know she had, and stood in the dark of her quarters shaking, the blade trembling in her grip, sweat cold between her shoulder blades. The lamp. She needed the lamp. She found the switch and flooded the little room with light and stood there breathing, counting her own heartbeat down from ten.

The dream didn't fade the way dreams fade. That was the thing she couldn't stop noticing, standing in the lamplight with her knife still drawn. Dreams dissolved. This one sat in her mind with the weight and detail of a memory: the exact color of the burning sky, the geometry of the black ship, the texture of the pavement under her feet. And the voice.

She knew the voice.

She pressed her palms against her eyes until sparks bloomed in the dark behind them, and the voice was still there, patient, gentle, counting.

*Five. Four.*

"Freya," she said aloud. Her voice cracked on the second syllable. "Freya, are you there?"

Silence. Real silence, the dead-air silence of a channel not being answered, which had never once happened in three years. Freya was always there. Freya answered before she finished calling.

"Freya!"

Nothing. Just the hum of the life support, the tick of the cooling unit, the wind outside worrying the container walls.

Felix lowered the knife. She sat down slowly on the edge of the bunk, in the pool of lamplight, alone in her skull for the first time since she could remember — since she could remember, and how far did that go, really, how far had it ever gone — and looked at her hands until they stopped shaking.

Outside, dawn was still hours away. Eleven days until the *Vigilant*. Nine days, she would think later, though she had no way of knowing it yet. And in the dark of her small room, in the space where a voice should have been, Felix Okafor sat very still and listened to a countdown that had followed her out of the dream and refused, absolutely refused, to stop.
