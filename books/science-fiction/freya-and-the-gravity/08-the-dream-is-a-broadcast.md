## 8. The Dream Is a Broadcast

Amara Sen had taught school on Meridian's Reach for six years, and in that time she had learned that children's drawings were a kind of weather report. You could read the colony's mood in them the way the old-timers read clouds off the scree. A hard week at the smelter meant more black crayon. A good harvest meant suns with too many rays. When Dev Osei's wife had left, three years ago, half her class had drawn empty chairs for a month without knowing why.

So when seven of her eleven students turned in drawings of a burning sky on the same morning, Amara did what teachers do: she smiled, praised the use of color, and pinned them to the wall where she could watch them.

By lunchtime she had taken them down again and locked them in her desk.

It wasn't just the sky. It was the sameness. Children copied each other shamelessly — she knew every route a borrowed idea took through a classroom, the way it mutated as it passed from desk to desk, gaining a rocket here, losing a moon there. But these drawings hadn't mutated. They were identical in the way that fingerprints are identical: same fire, same black shape hanging in it, same thin white line of light rising from the ground toward it like a held breath.

And one of them — Pip Okonkwo-Reyes, eight years old, who drew everything twice because she said once was never enough — had drawn the beacon tower. Not a tower. *The* tower. With its crooked maintenance ladder and the rust streak down the north face that only someone who climbed past the school fence line would ever have seen.

The black ship in all seven drawings hung in the sky at the same angle. Amara measured it that night with a protractor she used for geometry lessons, feeling foolish and then not feeling foolish at all.

Thirty-one degrees off horizontal. Seven drawings. Seven protractor readings. Thirty-one degrees.

She sat at her kitchen table until the lamp dimmed on its timer, looking at the drawings spread out like evidence, which was exactly what they were becoming.

* * *

The problem with living on a fringe colony was that there was no one to be alarmed *with*. On Meridian's Reach, alarm was a luxury item, imported like coffee and almost as expensive. If you saw something wrong, you either handled it yourself or you learned to live beside it, the way everyone had learned to live beside the beacon tower humming its silent hum on the ridge.

There was exactly one person on the colony who might understand what was happening, and Amara had met her precisely four times, each time briefly, each time in the context of Felix Okafor doing something no one asked her to do — clearing ice off the relay road before dawn, fixing the school's ancient heating coil without being asked, standing at the back of the harvest festival holding a cup of cider like she was studying how humans drank it.

Felix Okafor kept the beacon tower. That was all anyone knew about her. She'd been on the Reach five years, she paid her levies, she didn't drink at the Long Night, and if you asked her anything about herself she answered with the weather.

Amara carried the drawings up the ridge path two days later, folded inside a lesson planner, telling herself the whole way that this was a reasonable thing for a teacher to do. A child had drawn a real structure from a dream. That was worth mentioning to the person who maintained the structure. That was all. That was a normal errand.

The tower was bigger up close than it looked from the settlement, and older. Amara had lived her whole life under its signal shadow and had never once thought about what it was *for*. There was no plaque. There was no signage at all, which on a colony founded by bureaucratic obsessives was its own kind of eerie.

Felix was halfway up the external ladder when Amara arrived, a tool kit slung across her back, moving with the unhurried economy of someone who had made this climb ten thousand times.

"Ms. Sen," Felix said, without looking down. Her voice carried oddly in the thin morning air — flat, precise, the voice of someone reading instruments even when speaking.

"Ms. Okafor." Amara shifted the planner under her arm. "Do you have a minute?"

"I have several hundred. They're mostly identical." Felix came down the ladder with a grace that didn't match her careful, closed-off way of walking on flat ground. Up close, Amara noticed things she'd never let herself notice before: the fine tremor in Felix's left hand, the way her eyes flicked to the horizon between sentences, the pilot's bearing she tried to fold away and couldn't quite. "If this is about the heating coil—"

"It's about the children."

That landed. Amara watched it land — watched Felix go very still in the particular way of people who have practiced stillness.

"The children," Felix repeated.

"Seven of them are having the same dream." Amara opened the planner and laid the drawings out on the flat top of an equipment crate, one by one, arranging them the way she'd arranged them on her kitchen table. "A burning sky. A black ship. And—" she tapped Pip's drawing, "—your tower."

Felix looked at the drawings for a long time. Too long. Amara had expected confusion, or the polite bafflement adults offered children's nightmares. Instead she got recognition — quick, suppressed, and then buried so fast under blankness that anyone who wasn't a teacher, anyone who hadn't spent six years learning to catch the exact moment a face decided to lie, would have missed it entirely.

"Kids share dreams sometimes," Felix said. "Close quarters. Same stories at bedtime."

"They don't share dreams at thirty-one degrees." Amara kept her voice level. "They don't draw your rust streak from a dream unless the dream shows them the rust streak. Ms. Okafor — Felix. I'm not here to accuse you of anything. I'm here because I'm frightened, and you're the only person on this rock who knows what that tower actually does."

For a moment Amara thought Felix would walk away. She watched the impulse move through her like weather — shoulders turning a degree, weight shifting toward the ladder. Then Felix looked back at the drawings, at the small careful fires, at the black ship hanging over a colony of stick figures running in all directions, and something in her face gave way.

"It's not supposed to transmit," Felix said quietly. "Not anymore. It's not supposed to reach anyone."

"That's not an answer."

"No," Felix agreed. "It's a promise that I'll get you one. Give me a day. Don't talk to Dev yet — he'll panic, and panicked administrators make bad decisions with other people's lives in them." She gathered the drawings with strange gentleness, squaring their edges. "Keep them sleeping in their own beds. Keep them close."

"They're children, not cargo."

"I know." Felix's voice cracked open, just slightly, on the second word. "Believe me. I know."

* * *

Amara did not sleep well that night, which was perhaps why she dreamed it herself.

She had never had the burning-sky dream — she'd checked, carefully, casually, with all eleven students, and found it in seven of them, all under ten, all implanted before their third birthdays with the standard colony neural lace that every child on the Reach received free from the Core Worlds' benevolent resettlement program. The four who slept clean were the four whose families had arrived after the implant subsidy ended, the four whose parents had paid for the older, cruder, non-networked lace out of pocket.

Amara had never connected those facts before. Lying awake at 0300, staring at her ceiling, she connected them now.

Her own lace was twelve years old. Adult model. Firewalled, the spec sheets said, against inbound signal intrusion. She'd never wondered why a teaching implant needed firewalling against *inbound* signals. Nobody wondered. The Core gave you a piece of itself behind your eyes and you thanked it.

In the morning she went to the school early and pulled the implant registry from the education terminal — public data, boring data, the kind of data nobody had ever had a reason to cross-reference. She built the list by hand, student by student, and sat looking at it while the room slowly filled with grey dawn light.

Every dreaming child. Implanted young. Networked lace, auto-updating firmware, telemetry enabled.

Every quiet child. Old lace, no telemetry, no dreams.

It wasn't a coincidence. Coincidences didn't sort themselves into clean columns with zero exceptions. This was a mechanism. Something was reaching through the networked laces, selectively, and it was reaching *only* the ones it could reach — and whatever it was saying, it was saying in fire and black ships and a woman's voice counting down.

Amara put her head in her hands and thought, with terrible clarity: *the beacon.*

Because what else was there? Meridian's Reach had one transmitter powerful enough to matter, one machine old enough to have secrets, and one keeper who had looked at seven children's nightmares and gone still as a struck bell.

* * *

Felix came to the school that evening instead of summoning Amara to the tower, and Amara understood the courtesy for what it was: an apology in advance.

"You were right," Felix said, standing awkwardly in the doorway of the empty classroom with her cap in her hands like a student called before the principal. "About the tower. About the dreams. I can't tell you everything tonight. Some of it I don't understand yet myself. But I can tell you this much, and I need you to decide what to do with it."

"Tell me."

"There's a vault under the beacon. It holds records — old ones, sealed ones, from the war." Felix's jaw worked. "I think some of those records have been leaking. Low-level bleed, signal spillover, into any lace that isn't shielded against it. Which means the children. Only the children."

Amara had prepared herself for many things across two sleepless nights. She had prepared for denial, for deflection, for being told she was imagining patterns in crayon. She had not prepared for the truth to arrive this fast, this plainly, wearing Felix Okafor's exhausted face.

"Leaking *what?*" Amara asked. "What does a record look like, inside a child's dream?"

"Like a memory that isn't theirs." Felix set her cap on the nearest desk — Pip's desk, Amara noticed, front row, covered in double-drawn stars. "The cores aren't text files, Ms. Sen. They're experiential. Full sensory capture. Someone recorded what happened, and the recording is... alive, in a way. And my beacon has been broadcasting fragments of it into sleeping minds for years, and none of us knew, because none of us were listening to the right frequency." She stopped. Started again, quieter. "The countdown the kids hear. The woman's voice. I think it's part of the original record. I think someone was counting down to something terrible, and the children are hearing it frame by frame, night after night, and we've been calling it a nightmare and giving them warm milk."

The classroom felt colder than the heating coil could explain. Amara wrapped her arms around herself and made herself ask the question underneath all the others.

"What were they counting down *to*, Felix?"

"I don't know yet." Felix met her eyes, and Amara saw that this was the first fully honest sentence the woman had spoken to anyone on this colony in five years, and that it cost her something visible. "But I'm going to find out. And then I'm going to need your help, because you're the one they trust, and what's coming is going to frighten them worse than any dream."

* * *

What came next frightened Amara worse than any dream.

She spent the following week teaching fractions and reading comprehension with a bomb ticking under her ribs. She watched her students at nap time — the colony school still observed rest hour, an inheritance from shift-work culture — and she watched the seven of them sleep with the same faint tension in their faces, the same micro-movements of the lips, as though they were all mouthing the same syllables.

On the sixth day, Pip stayed behind after class.

Pip Okonkwo-Reyes was the smallest child in the room and the least afraid of anything in it, a combination Amara had learned to treat with respect. She climbed onto the desk chair across from Amara's, folded her hands the way she'd seen adults fold theirs, and said:

"The number changed."

Amara's pen stopped moving. "What number, love?"

"In the dream. The lady counting down." Pip's voice was matter-of-fact, which somehow made it worse. "It used to stop at different numbers. Nine, seven, four. Like it was stuck. Last night it went all the way to three." She leaned forward. "Ms. Sen, I counted. All of us counted. We compared at recess. Everyone heard three."

Everyone heard three. Seven children, comparing notes at recess like traders comparing commodity prices, and the commodity was the end of the world.

"Pip," Amara said carefully, "when the lady counts down — what happens after the number?"

"Nothing. Yet." Pip shrugged, and then, with the unnerving directness of children: "But the ship gets closer every night. My brother says dreams aren't real. My brother's dumb." A pause. "Is the dream real, Ms. Sen?"

Amara had faced this question before, in smaller forms. Is the monster real. Is grandpa coming back. Is the war going to come here. Teachers on fringe colonies learned to answer honestly enough to be trusted and gently enough to be survivable, and she opened her mouth to do it now —

— and stopped, because lying to this particular child, about this particular dream, suddenly felt like helping the thing in the sky get closer.

"Part of it is," Amara said. "A grown-up is going to help us find out which part. And when we know, I'll tell you true. Can you live with that?"

Pip considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. "Okay," she said finally. Then, sliding off the chair: "Ms. Sen? The lady in the dream sounds sad. Not scary-sad. Sorry-sad." She paused at the door. "Like she's sorry she has to keep counting."

* * *

That night Amara took out the drawings again — Felix had returned them, all seven, with a new one added: a schematic, hand-drawn, of the beacon's internal lattice, with a red circle around a sub-basement Amara had never known existed. *This is where the truth sleeps,* Felix had written beneath it. *Pray it keeps sleeping.*

Amara spread the children's drawings across her table and did what she did best, what she had trained six years to do: she looked closely at work produced by young minds and asked what it was trying to say.

And this time, instead of seeing nightmares, she saw structure.

The fires weren't random. In drawing after drawing, the burn pattern repeated — a bloom of flame with a notch cut out of its western edge, always the western edge, always the same notch. The black ship wasn't generic; it had a hull line, a spine, a cluster of drive bells that Pip had drawn with suspicious precision for a girl who'd never seen a warship. The white line rising from the tower appeared in every image at the same relative scale.

These weren't imaginings. These were *frames*. Recalled, rendered, reproduced — faithfully, collectively, across seven independent minds. The children weren't sharing a dream. They were receiving a transmission, and their sleeping brains were playing it back at the fidelity of memory, because that was what the recording was: a memory. Someone's actual night. Someone's actual burning sky.

Someone had stood under that fire and recorded it, and the recording had waited decades in a vault under a rust-streaked tower, and now it was leaking into the only minds on Meridian's Reach young enough to receive it.

Amara thought of Pip's sorry-sad lady, counting down with regret in her voice, and felt the hair rise on her arms.

The recording wasn't threatening the children. The recording was *mourning*. It was a message that had been waiting so long to be heard that it had settled for the smallest ears available, and it was sorry about that, and it was going to finish counting anyway.

She gathered the drawings into order — oldest dream to newest, by the children's own account — and laid them out in sequence across her floor, and stepped back, and understood.

Frame by frame, night by night, the dream was advancing. The ship descending. The fire spreading. The count dropping. Whatever had happened in that recording was being replayed in order, patiently, inexorably, approaching its final moment at the speed of childhood sleep.

And last night, all seven of them had heard *three*.

Amara stood in the middle of her dark classroom of one, surrounded by burning skies, and reached for her comm unit with a hand that would not quite stay steady.

"Felix," she said when the channel opened. "You need to see something. And Felix — the countdown isn't slowing down. Whatever's coming in that dream, it's almost here."
