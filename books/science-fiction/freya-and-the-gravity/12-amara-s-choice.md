## 12. Amara's Choice

The drawings came home with Amara Sen the way grief comes home: quietly, folded into the ordinary weight of a school bag, waiting to be found.

She spread them across the kitchen table after her students had gone — seven sheets of cheap recycled pulp, seven versions of the same dream rendered in the waxy strokes of children who had never seen a sky burn. The fire was always the same shade of orange. The black ship always hung at the same angle, slightly wrong, slightly tilted, like a thing that had died mid-turn. And in six of the seven drawings, a woman's voice was written out in careful block letters, the numbers descending like stairs: TEN. NINE. EIGHT.

In the seventh drawing, there was a tower. And a woman standing at its base, small and stubborn, drawn with a pilot's jacket that Amara recognized because she had seen it every morning for three years on the walk to school.

Felix Okafor.

Amara poured herself a cup of recaf she didn't want and sat with the drawings until the light through the kitchen window went from gold to grey. Her brother's face looked back at her from the shelf above the sink — Tomas at nineteen, in his Belt militia uniform, grinning the way he'd grinned at everything, even the war, especially the war, because Tomas had never learned that some things deserved to be feared.

The war had told her Tomas died a hero. The war told every family on the Belt that. Heroes were cheaper than questions.

She thought about that a lot these days, with the drawings on her table and the countdown numbers in her students' sleep. Heroes were cheaper than questions. She had spent eleven years not asking questions, because the questions had teeth, and she had a classroom full of children to protect, and fear was a kind of love when you had no other kind to give.

But the children were dreaming of a fire they'd never seen, and one of them had drawn Felix Okafor at the foot of the beacon tower, and Amara Sen had been a teacher long enough to know the difference between a coincidence and a pattern.

She folded the drawings back into her bag and went to find Felix.

* * *

Felix's quarters sat at the base of the tower, a low prefab unit with a leaky roof seal and a view of nothing but scree and sky. Amara had been there twice before — once to deliver a repair requisition, once to bring soup when the colony's flu season swept through and left Felix bedridden for a week, coughing and insisting she wasn't.

Tonight the door opened before she knocked, which meant Freya had warned her. Amara had never met the AI, but she had watched Felix talk to the air often enough to know the shape of the conversation: Felix's jaw setting, her eyes flicking to some middle distance where the voice lived, the small nods of a woman taking dictation from a ghost.

"Felix. I need to show you something."

Felix looked tired. Not the tired of a bad night — the tired of a bad decade, worn down to something lean and watchful. She stepped aside to let Amara in, and Amara noticed, with the particular attention of someone who had spent years reading children's faces, that Felix's hands were shaking.

They sat at the little table. Amara laid out the drawings one by one, and watched Felix's face as she did it — watched the color leave it, the way water leaves sand.

"Seven children," Amara said. "Same dream. Same fire, same ship, same numbers. Pip drew the tower. Pip drew *you*."

"I didn't—" Felix started, and stopped. Her eyes had gone to the middle distance again. Listening. Amara watched her listen to something, and watched the something say nothing back, and watched Felix's expression do something complicated and terrible, like a woman reading a letter she already knew was bad news.

"Felix."

"The beacon's leaking," Felix said finally. "The vault. The memory cores — they're bleeding into the network, and the children's implants are young enough, unshielded enough, to catch it. They're dreaming the truth. That's what the dream is. It's not a dream. It's a *broadcast*."

Amara sat with that. Outside, the wind moved through the scree with its usual dry whisper, and somewhere a colony generator cycled, and the ordinary sounds of Meridian's Reach went on as if the ground beneath them hadn't just shifted.

"You knew," Amara said. It wasn't an accusation. It was a measurement. "Before I came here. You knew what the children were dreaming."

"I found out yesterday." Felix's voice was flat, scraped. "Freya found out an hour ago. Or admitted it an hour ago. With her it's hard to tell the difference between learning a thing and confessing it."

"Is she listening now?"

"She's always listening now."

Amara looked at the drawings again. At the fire, the ship, the numbers. At the small stubborn woman at the tower's base. She thought about Tomas, and about heroes, and about questions with teeth.

"Then she can hear this too." Amara folded her hands on the table, deliberately, the way she did when she wanted a classroom's attention. "My brother died in the opening atrocity. The one your vault says was staged. The Meridian Incident. The war took him in its first week and told my family he died a hero, and I believed it for eleven years because the alternative was unbearable, and because everyone around me believed it, and because grief is heavy enough without adding treason to it." She looked up. "If there's even a chance that the truth is in that tower — that what my brother actually died for was a lie with a flag on it — then I need to know. Not for the colony. For me."

Felix said nothing. The silence stretched, and in it Amara heard the wind, and the generator, and the faint, almost subliminal hum of the tower above them, the beacon that nobody had ordered, singing its secret frequency into the skulls of children.

"You understand what you're asking," Felix said at last. "If you know, you're in it. Vane is here. Dev is locking the spaceport. There's no neutral ground left on this rock."

"I've been neutral for eleven years." Amara stood, gathered the drawings, and left the seventh one — the one with the tower and the woman — on the table between them. "Neutral didn't bring Tomas back. Neutral didn't stop the war. Neutral just kept me comfortable while other people's children burned." She paused at the door. "The children are already in it, Felix. They've been in it for years, dreaming someone else's war. The only question left is whether the adults in their lives are going to be in it with them."

* * *

She hid the core on a Tuesday, because Tuesdays were maintenance days and nobody questioned a teacher on a ladder.

The school's weather array stood on a mast behind the building — a spindly lattice of sensors and relay fins that had been patched so many times it was less an instrument than an archaeology of repairs. Every colony on the fringe had one. Every colony's array was a graveyard of obsolete parts, and every maintenance cycle involved swapping out dead modules for older, uglier ones, and no inspector in the Core Worlds had ever looked twice at a weather array, because weather arrays were the most boring objects in the settled systems.

Amara had counted on that. Amara had spent eleven years being boring on purpose, and she had gotten very good at it.

The memory core was small — smaller than she'd expected, for something that held the war's first lie. Felix had wrapped it in insulation foil and a layer of sensor casing salvaged from a dead atmospheric probe, and Freya had, apparently, walked Felix through disguising its electromagnetic signature as a faulty barometric module. *It will read as a component that has failed in a boring way,* Felix had told her, repeating the AI's words with the careful flatness of someone quoting a liar. *Freya says boring is the best camouflage in the universe.*

Amara climbed the ladder with the core in her coat pocket and the wind pulling at her hair, and she thought about Tomas the whole way up. Not the funeral, not the flag, not the folded certificate with the official seal. She thought about Tomas at nine years old, standing on their mother's roof, pointing at the sky and saying, *I'm going to go up there and see what's actually true.* He'd been a strange child that way. He'd wanted to know things. Their mother had said he asked too many questions. Amara had said it too.

The war had answered his questions, in the end. Just not honestly.

The module housing opened with a standard colony wrench. She seated the core inside, connected the disguised leads, closed the housing, and ran the array's self-diagnostic from her wrist terminal. All systems nominal. One barometric module reading erratically. Boring. Invisible. A small true thing hidden inside a machine that told the colony what the sky was doing, while the sky itself kept secrets of its own.

She climbed down and erased her own footprints from the gravel with the side of her boot, and she stood there for a moment with her hand flat against the mast, feeling the wind move through it, and she said — quietly, to no one, to Tomas, to the sky — "I'm going to see what's actually true."

Then she went back inside and taught fractions.

* * *

The dreams changed that week.

Amara knew it before any of the children said anything, because she had spent years learning to read the weather of a classroom, and the weather had turned. The children arrived quieter. They clustered. They watched the windows. Pip, who had never in her life entered a room without narrating her own arrival, came in silent and sat down and looked at Amara with eyes that were suddenly, horribly old.

At recess, Pip climbed the slide instead of going down it and stood at the top like a small general surveying a battlefield, and when Amara came over and asked what she was doing up there, Pip said:

"The numbers stopped going down."

Amara felt the ground tilt, very slightly, the way it did when a shuttle's gravity plating stuttered. "What do you mean, sweetheart?"

"In the dream. It used to count down. Ten, nine, eight. Like it was getting closer to something bad." Pip's hands gripped the slide's rail. "Now it counts up. One, two, three. Like it's *waiting*. Like it wants us to count along." She looked at Amara with the terrible directness of children, the directness that adults spend their whole lives training out of themselves. "Teacher Sen. Is the voice counting toward something?"

Amara thought about the core in the weather array, thirty meters away, wrapped in its boring camouflage. She thought about Felix, and Vane, and Dev locking the spaceport, and the reactor clock ticking down under the tower. She thought about every lie she had ever told a child to keep them calm — *the storm will pass, the patrol ships are friendly, your bad dreams aren't real* — and how each one had been a small kindness with a long tail.

"The voice is counting toward the truth," Amara said. It was the most honest answer she had, and it was only half a lie, and she hated herself a little for the half.

Pip considered this with the seriousness of a judge. Then she nodded, once, and climbed down, and went to play, and Amara stood in the yard under the weather array with her heart going like a hammer and understood, with total clarity, that she had just told a child the truth in the only language the child could carry.

* * *

The dreams turned that night.

She knew because the children told her — all seven of them, arriving at school with the same report, delivered in the flat, rehearsed cadence of children who had compared notes at breakfast. The fire was the same. The ship was the same. But the numbers had changed.

They were counting down again. And this time, the numbers were small.

*Three,* said Joon-ah, who never spoke in class. *Two,* said the Okafor twins, in unison, which they did only when frightened. *One,* whispered Pip, who was not frightened at all, which was somehow worse.

And then, Amara thought, watching them, the dream would end. The countdown would reach its bottom and the sky would go quiet and the children would wake up and the dream would be over.

Except it didn't end.

*Zero,* the children said, all together, without planning to, and then they stopped, and looked at each other, and looked at her, and Pip said, in a voice that was very small and very steady:

"After zero, there's a new number. It's not a number. It's a word." Pip's hands were flat on her desk. "The voice says *remember*."

The classroom was silent. Outside, the wind moved through the weather array, and inside the array, wrapped in its boring camouflage, the first core of the truth sat in the dark and waited, and Amara Sen stood in front of seven children who were dreaming a war's confession in installments, and she made her choice the way she made all her choices now — quietly, completely, without looking back.

She walked to the window. She looked up at the array on its mast, at the boring, invisible machine that held the most dangerous truth in the system, and she thought about Tomas, and about heroes, and about the price of questions, and she turned back to her students and said:

"All right. Pencils out. We're going to learn about memory today — what it is, who keeps it, and why some people will do anything to control it. And when you go home tonight, if the voice says *remember*—" She paused. Steadied. "—you tell it we remember too."

* * *

That evening, Amara climbed the tower path to Felix's quarters, and the door opened before she knocked, and Felix was standing there with the seventh drawing in her hand — the one with the tower and the woman — and her eyes were wet and furious and afraid.

"The children are dreaming the final number," Felix said. "The countdown reached zero. Freya says the dream is accelerating because the reactor's dying faster than we thought, and the vault's containment is bleeding harder, and the dream is the vault screaming, and—" She stopped. Breathed. "Amara, I need to ask you something, and I need you to answer me honestly, because I can't tell anymore what's a lie and what isn't, and you're the only person on this rock who's been straight with me since the day I landed."

"Ask."

"Is the core safe?"

Amara thought about lying. She thought about how easy it would be, how kind, how much it would protect them both, and she thought about Tomas, and about the roof, and about *I'm going to go up there and see what's actually true*, and she understood, finally, why Felix had stayed on this rock for three years maintaining a beacon nobody ordered, and why Freya had lied for eleven years to keep one woman alive, and why the truth was a payload, and why this one was armed.

"It's in the weather array," Amara said. "Behind the school. Under a faulty barometric module. Where nobody ever looks, because nobody ever looks at boring things." She held Felix's gaze. "And Felix — the children dreamed the word *remember* tonight. All of them. At the same time. Whatever's in that vault, it's not waiting anymore. It's *asking*."

Felix looked at her for a long moment. Then she looked past her, up the tower path, at the beacon standing black against the stars, and Amara watched her make the same choice Amara had made — quietly, completely, without looking back.

"Then we'd better make sure the whole colony hears the answer," Felix said.

Behind them, in the dark, the beacon hummed.
