## 27. Infiltration of the Vigilant

The crate smelled of freeze-dried protein and old fear.

Sorel had requisitioned it herself — a standard resupply pallet, flagged for the Vigilant's mess stores, scheduled on the morning shuttle like any other. That was the beauty of it, she'd told Felix in the dark of the relay shack: nobody inspects their own groceries. The ugliness was everything else.

Felix lay curled against the crate's inner wall, knees to her chest, the memory cores strapped flat against her ribs beneath a thermal wrap. Amara was pressed beside her, breathing slow and deliberate the way she did when she was terrified and refusing to admit it. Through the seam of the lid, a knife-thin line of dock light slid across Sorel's face as she ran the final manifest check.

"Two minutes," Sorel murmured. "Then I seal you in and I don't know you."

"You'll know us," Felix said quietly. "That's the problem."

"That's the job." Sorel closed the lid most of the way, then paused. Her voice dropped to almost nothing. "Okafor. If this goes wrong — if they take me — you keep moving. You don't come back for me. Swear it."

"I don't make promises I intend to break."

"Swear it anyway."

Felix held her eyes through the gap. "I swear."

Amara's hand found Felix's wrist in the dark and squeezed once. Then the lid came down, and the world was foam padding and the hiss of atmospheric seals, and Sorel's boots receding across the deck plates like a heartbeat walking away.

* * *

Lieutenant Danae Sorel had spent nine years learning how the Vigilant thought.

The ship thought in manifests. It thought in schedules. It trusted its own paperwork the way a devout man trusts scripture — completely, and without looking up. So she walked the shuttle bay with her slate held at the correct angle of boredom, nodded to the two deckhands pulling cargo, and let them do what deckhands always did: move the pallets where the manifest said, without opening anything, because opening things created work.

She watched the crate disappear into the hold stack. She logged its position. She altered one routing flag — a maintenance priority that would keep the pallet unopened for six hours — and felt the small cold satisfaction of a lie told well.

It lasted four minutes.

"Sorel."

Captain Idris Vane stood at the bay's observation rail, hands clasped behind him, watching the deckhands with the mild attention of a man watching weather. He didn't look at her. He never had to look at anyone; his attention arrived like pressure.

"Captain."

"The Meridian resupply came in light. Two crates short." Still not looking at her. "You countersigned the manifest."

"There was a substitution error at the colony depot. I corrected it—"

"With a maintenance priority." Now he turned, and his face was its usual courteous blankness, which was worse than anger. Anger would have meant surprise. "Danae. I've read your file every year since you were nineteen. I know how you sign your name when you're lying, because you press harder on the last letter. You've done it twice today."

The bay noise seemed to recede. Somewhere under her sternum, her pulse counted out the seconds she had left.

"Sir, I—"

"Don't." He said it gently. "Whatever you're about to say, understand that I will have to report whatever you say. That's the position we're both in. So think carefully about whether you want your words to be treason or a mistake."

And there it was — the door he always offered, the narrow one, the one he'd built for her out of habit or mercy or some arithmetic she couldn't read. A mistake could be absorbed. Treason had to be answered.

Sorel thought about the core she'd seized from the school's weather array. She'd reviewed it alone, three nights running, frame by frame, because she'd needed to be sure before she let herself know. And there, in the staged footage of the Meridian Incident — the burning transport, the "Belt attack" that started the war — she had seen the corvette *Kestrel*. Her first deployment. She had stood watch on that hull. She had watched the transport burn from four hundred meters and been told it was enemy fire, and she had believed it for eleven years, and the truth had been sitting in a schoolteacher's weather array on a rock nobody wanted.

Her own first deployment, doctored. Her own eyes, borrowed by the Admiralty and never returned.

"Sir," she said, and pressed hard on the last letter of every word, "it was a mistake."

Vane studied her for a long moment. Something moved behind his courtesy — not belief. Acknowledgment. The look of a man recognizing a choice he'd once made himself, made correctly this time.

"No," he said softly. "It wasn't. Take Lieutenant Sorel to holding. Log it as insubordination pending review." He turned back to the deckhands, already dismissing her from the universe. "And someone open that misrouted pallet in the hold. Routine inspection."

* * *

They took her phaser first, then her slate, then her dignity in the usual increments. In the brig, behind glass, she sat very straight on the bunk and did the only useful thing left to her.

She stalled.

She argued jurisdiction with the duty officer. She demanded a formal charge sheet. She invoked review rights that required a hearing board, which required scheduling, which required signatures, which required time. Every minute she ground out of the machine was a minute the crate sat unopened in the hold stack — except Vane had already ordered it opened, and she knew it, and so the minutes were worth nothing except that she couldn't stop spending them.

*Let them find an empty crate,* she thought. *Let me be wrong. Let me have burned myself for nothing.*

The intercom above the brig door crackled. Vane's voice, unhurried:

"Lieutenant. For the record — I ordered the inspection before you were detained. Whatever happens next is not on you."

Which was either kindness or cruelty, and with Vane there had never been a difference.

* * *

Inside the crate, time had gone soft and close.

Felix counted by breaths. Four hundred breaths, five hundred. Amara had stopped pretending to be calm somewhere around three hundred and now gripped Felix's hand hard enough to grind bone, and Felix gripped back, and neither of them spoke because speaking was air and air was finite and the lid was sealed and somewhere above them men with scanners might be walking toward a maintenance flag that a captured lieutenant had bought with her career and possibly her life.

Then the flag died. Felix felt it — or Freya felt it, whispering through the implant link, faint as a dying radio station: *the routing change has been reversed. Inspection team en route to hold stack seven. Eleven minutes.*

"Freya," Felix breathed. "Options."

"One. You are discovered, and everything ends here. Two—" a pause, and Felix had learned to hear the shape of Freya's hesitations, the tiny computational flinches before a lie, but this pause had no flinch in it, only weight, "—there is a coolant conduit that runs beneath the hold stacks. It is hot, cramped, and leads to junction C-9, which is forty meters from the comm deck access lift. I can guide you. Barely."

"Barely."

"My maps of this ship are eleven years old, Felix. Ships grow. Like people."

Amara's voice, a thread of sound: "We're getting out of a sealed crate in a military hold to crawl through a coolant duct."

"Yes," Felix said.

"I want it noted that I became a teacher because I hate adventure."

"Noted. Move."

The lid hinges gave to Felix's multitool after ninety seconds that felt geological. They rose into amber hold-light between towers of cargo, and Felix got her first look at the Vigilant's guts — clean lines, Core-standard, everything squared away and confident, a ship built by people who had never doubted they were the good guys. She hated it instantly and completely.

Conduit hatch. Freya talked her through the lock — eleven-year-old schematics, two false starts, one moment where the mechanism shrieked and both of them froze among the crates like animals. Then the hatch gave, and the heat swallowed them.

The conduit was exactly as bad as promised. Coolant lines radiated warmth against their backs; the space forced them to move bent double, single file, Amara's breath ragged in Felix's ear. Freya's voice ran underneath it all, quiet and constant, calling turns: *Left. Junction. Left again.* Once a maintenance drone passed them going the other way, its sensor dome swiveling, and Freya did something — Felix never learned what — and the drone continued past as if they were plumbing.

"Why is she helping us?" Amara whispered at one junction, meaning not Freya but Sorel. "She's Core Navy. She shot our array door off."

"She looked at the truth until it was hers," Felix said. "That's all any of us can do."

They climbed. The conduit tilted upward toward the ship's spine, and the heat gave way to chill, and then Freya said: *Stop. Junction C-9. Above you is an access panel. Beyond it, the aft corridor. The comm deck lift is forty meters down that corridor, past a checkpoint.*

"Past a checkpoint," Felix repeated.

"Two guards. Rotating shift change in—" the pause again, the honest kind, "—ninety seconds. It is the best window I can give you. It is not good."

"It's the only one we've got." Felix got her shoulder under the panel. "Amara. When this opens, you go back. Down the conduit, back to the crate if you can reach it, back to the colony if you can't. You've done your part. You don't belong on the comm deck."

Amara looked at her in the conduit's gray light, and for a moment she was every teacher who had ever refused to leave a child behind, and Felix knew the argument was already lost.

"My brother died in that war," Amara said. "Eleven years ago they told my family it was heroic Belt treachery. I taught children his name as a lie for a decade. I'm not going back down a duct, Felix. I'm coming to the comm deck, and I'm going to stand there while you tell everyone what they did to him."

Felix had commanded crews once. She remembered, suddenly and painfully, what it was to be out-argued by someone braver than the mission required.

"Together, then," she said, and pushed the panel.

* * *

The corridor was white and quiet and forty meters long.

They crossed it during the shift change, when the checkpoint thinned to one guard whose back was turned, and Freya threaded them through the dead zones of the camera lattice like a needle through fabric — *now, stop, now* — and every second Felix expected alarms, the shudder of deck guns, the whole confident machinery of the Vigilant waking up around them.

It didn't come. The silence kept stretching, wrong-shaped, too complete.

At the lift, Freya said: *Felix. The alarm system has been silent for six minutes. Not disabled — silenced. Someone with command authority has suppressed local alerts on decks four through six.*

Felix's blood went cold. "Who?"

"I cannot tell. But there is only one person aboard with that authority without triggering fleet oversight."

Vane. Vane knew. Vane had known since the crate, maybe since the dinner, maybe since the day he'd erased her memory and started keeping her file like a wound he couldn't stop touching. He wasn't hunting them through these corridors.

He was letting them walk.

"No," Felix said aloud. "No, that's a trap. That has to be a trap."

"Or," Freya said, and her voice did something Felix had never heard it do — it went careful, almost gentle, "it is a man running out of ways to apologize."

The lift doors opened onto the comm deck without challenge.

The room was a cathedral of screens, starlight falling through the forward ports, and every console stood dark and waiting. And at the center of it, hands clasped behind his back, facing the stars as though he'd been standing there for hours — as though he'd been standing there for years —

Captain Idris Vane turned around.

"Felix Okafor," he said, courteously, quietly, the way he said everything. "You're late."

Behind her, Amara's breath caught. Behind everything, in the base of her skull, Freya went utterly, deliberately silent — and Felix stepped off the lift into the light of the enemy's flagship, carrying the truth against her ribs, and understood that the hardest part had just begun.
