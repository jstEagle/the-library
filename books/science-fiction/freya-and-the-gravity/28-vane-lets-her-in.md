## 28. Vane Lets Her In

Idris Vane had spent thirty-one years learning to read a ship the way other men read faces. The *Vigilant* spoke to him in pressures and harmonics — the hum of the grav plating, the tick of coolant cycling through the spine, the particular silence of a crew that had stopped believing in its captain without yet finding the courage to say so.

Tonight the ship was telling him something he already knew.

He stood on the comm deck at 0300 station time, alone except for the deck's sleeping systems, and watched the colony turn beneath him like a coin someone was deciding whether to spend. Meridian's Reach had no right to be beautiful. It was a scab of a world, rust and scrub grass, a single settlement strung along a river valley like beads on a wire. But from orbit, in the blue hour before its dawn, it looked like something worth keeping.

That was the problem with this whole assignment. Everything worth keeping kept getting in the way.

His wrist display pulsed. Sector Four — the resupply bay, where a shipment of atmospheric processors had arrived two days late with paperwork too clean to be honest. He'd flagged it himself, six hours ago, and then done nothing about it. A captain who ordered an inspection would find three stowaways in a cargo crate. A captain who wanted to find them would have ordered it hours ago.

He hadn't ordered it.

*You're letting her walk into your ship,* he thought, and could not make the thought feel like a warning. It felt more like a door opening after years of pushing on a wall.

The lift chimed. Soft, almost apologetic. Vane didn't turn.

"Captain." Lieutenant Sorel's voice, from behind him. Steady. Too steady — the steadiness of someone who'd rehearsed. "Perimeter report. The beacon tower went active twenty minutes ago. Power draw off the colony grid. Whatever they're doing down there, they're doing it now."

"I know."

"Sir, if Okafor is planning a broadcast—"

"She is." He turned then. Sorel stood at parade rest in the doorway, and there was something new in her face, something she hadn't managed to sand smooth in time. She'd been reviewing the seized core for three days. He'd known she would. He'd also known what was in it — the doctored footage of the Kestrel Verge deployment, her first war, her first dead — and he had let her review it anyway, because some part of him had been arranging his own defeat for months and calling it procedure.

"You've seen the core," he said.

It wasn't a question, but Sorel treated it as one. "Yes, sir."

"And?"

"And I've filed no report." Her jaw worked. "I'm prepared to be court-martialed for that, Captain. I'd just like to know which side of it I'm standing on when it happens."

Vane looked at her for a long moment. Twenty-six years old, maybe. Good hands, good instincts, the kind of officer the Admiralty grew in vats and called loyalty. Except she wasn't loyal anymore, and they both knew it, and the knowing sat between them like a third person.

"The crate in Bay Four," he said. "Atmospheric processors. Late shipment, clean manifest."

Sorel blinked. "Sir?"

"It hasn't been inspected. I don't intend to inspect it." He let that land. "If anyone asks, you reported your suspicions and I overruled you. That will protect you later, and I'll need someone protected later."

Understanding moved through her face slowly, like weather. "You're letting them board."

"I'm opening a door," Vane said. "What walks through it is out of my hands. That's rather the point."

* * *

He dismissed her — gently, he hoped; he was out of practice with gentleness — and returned to the comm deck's central console. The array controls glowed amber in the dark, waiting for authorization codes only he carried. On the main display, the beacon tower's power signature climbed in slow increments, patient as a tide.

Eleven years ago, he had stood in a white room above the Meridian shipyards and held a neural lance against the temple of a woman strapped to a chair, and he had cut eleven minutes out of her life with the care of a surgeon and the conscience of a tool. He remembered every second of it. That was the joke the universe had played on him: they'd erased her memory and left his intact. Someone in the Admiralty had decided the erasing officer should carry the record, as insurance. As a leash.

He'd told himself for years that the leash ran both ways.

Felix Okafor had been a courier. That was all she'd been to the operation — a pilot with clean biometrics and no family, trusted because she was nobody. She'd carried a memory core from the shipyards to a relay at the system's edge, and she'd never known what she carried, and when the Meridian Incident broke and the war began, the Admiralty had realized their courier had flown through the proof like weather through an open window. So they'd sent him to take the window out of her head.

She'd fought them. Of course she had. Not with her body — four orderlies saw to that — but with her eyes, tracking him across the room the whole time, filing his face somewhere the lance couldn't reach. He'd told himself she wouldn't remember him. He'd been wrong. People remember the hand that hurts them even when they lose everything else. It's the last thing to go.

And then, years later, the trail had gone cold, and the Admiralty had closed the file, and Vane had not. Because he'd read the full record by then — not the summary, the record — and he knew what those eleven minutes contained. He knew the Meridian Incident was theater. He knew the burning liner, the dead civilians, the outrage that had lit the Core Worlds' righteous fire — all of it staged, timed, signed. He knew because he'd helped bury it, and a man who buries things learns to dig in his sleep.

For nine years he had followed her. Quietly. Ostensibly hunting a fugitive pilot, actually shadowing one, arriving always a step behind or a step ahead, closing doors before she reached them and hating himself for it, because every door he closed kept her alive and kept the truth buried, and he could no longer tell which outcome he was protecting.

Now here she came anyway. Through a cargo crate, past a lieutenant who'd chosen her side, up through his ship toward the one room he could still give her.

*Make it mean something,* he thought. *Whatever else you've failed to do. Make this one thing mean something.*

* * *

The deck lights flickered once — Freya reaching, testing the locks, finding them open and not trusting the gift. Vane almost smiled. Even the ghost in the tower suspected a trap. Fair enough. He'd earned the suspicion.

He poured two cups of tea from the deck's ancient dispenser, because the ritual steadied his hands, and set one on the console beside the array controls. Then he sat down in the operator's chair, put his authorization tablet face-up on the armrest, and waited.

She came at 0341.

The comm deck hatch cycled, and Felix Okafor stepped through with a sidearm she didn't raise and eyes that found him instantly and did not leave. Eleven years since the white room, and she looked older than her file, thinner than her file, and exactly as awake. Behind her, half in the corridor shadows, a girl — one of the dream-children, the schoolteacher's charge, twelve years old and holding a flashlight like a weapon. Amara Sen's kid. Pip.

Vane rose slowly and left the chair empty between them.

"Felix," he said.

"Don't." Her voice was low and rough. "Don't say my name like we're friends. You don't get the name."

"No," he agreed. "I don't." He gestured at the console. "The array is yours. Full broadcast authority. My codes are on the tablet. Freya will need them inside ninety seconds to chain the network before the reactor cycle shifts."

Felix didn't move. "This is a trick."

"Yes," he said. "It's the opposite of a trick, which looks identical from where you're standing. I understand that. Take the codes anyway."

"Why?" The word cracked out of her harder than she meant it, and he watched her hate that. "Why now? Why ever? You erased me. You hunted me. You put Sorel on the school—" He flinched; he couldn't help it. "You knew about that?"

"I knew Sorel was off the leash. I didn't stop it fast enough. That's on the list too." He took a breath. "There's a list, Felix. It's long. This doesn't shorten it. Nothing shortens it. But the array is on it, at the bottom, underlined twice, and I'm asking you to take it before I lose the nerve that took me nine years to grow."

Pip's flashlight beam wavered between them. The girl said nothing. Smart child. The whole colony had learned to let adults finish their failures.

Felix crossed the deck in five strides, snatched the tablet, and held it like it might bite. "Freya," she said aloud, to the air, to the implant, to whatever remained of the voice in the tower. "Confirm the codes."

A pause. Then, faintly, through the deck speakers — the AI had already threaded the ship's systems, of course she had — a voice Vane recognized from eleven years of intercepted logs: dry, warm, and guarded.

*"Codes accepted. Oh, Captain. You kept your maintenance schedules current. All these years, and you kept the Vigilant's arrays tuned. Did you know you were doing that?"*

"I hoped," Vane said.

*"Hoped what?"*

"That someday they'd be used for something true."

Silence on the line. Somewhere below, the beacon's reactor groaned through another cycle. On the main display, the chaining sequence began to build itself — beacon to relay to the *Vigilant*'s deep-space array, three nodes becoming one throat.

Felix stood at the console with the tablet in her hand and finally looked at him straight on. "In the white room," she said. "Was it you? The hand?"

"Yes."

"Did you hesitate?"

"No." He made himself hold her gaze. "I want to tell you I hesitated. I didn't. That's the man you're taking amends from. Weigh it accordingly."

Something moved behind her eyes — not forgiveness, nothing so generous. Recognition, maybe. The look of a woman confirming the shape of a wound she'd carried blind for a decade.

"Then why," she said quietly, "are you doing this?"

Vane walked to the viewport. Below, the colony's lights were out — Freya's blackout, the whole settlement dark except the beacon tower burning like a struck match. They were all down there in the dark, the council that betrayed her and the children who dreamed her truth and the teacher hiding cores in weather arrays, and they had answered an ultimatum with a single word, and the word had broken something loose in him that nine years of duty hadn't touched.

"Because I spent nine years making sure you'd never say it," he said. "And they're going to say it tonight whether I live or die, and I would rather be the door than the wall. That's all. There's no cleaner version. Make them remember me wrong, Felix. Make them remember me however gets them through this. Just make them remember."

Behind him, the console sang a rising tone — the chain complete, the array hot, ninety percent of a truth waiting on a threshold.

Felix set the tablet down and put her hand on the transmit key.

"Freya," she said. "On my mark."

Vane stood at the viewport with his back to the weapon he'd spent a decade aiming at her, and listened to the countdown begin.

* * *

The transmission tore out of the *Vigilant* like light leaving a dying star, and every screen in the system woke to the Meridian Incident — unedited, unsigned, undeniable.

Vane watched the fleet channels flood. Watched orders countermand orders. Watched, on the tactical plot, the Core battle group at the system's edge go from advance formation to confused drift, forty ships suddenly unsure whose war they were in.

Then the reactor alarm sounded, thin and urgent, and Freya's voice came through the deck speakers stretched thin as wire:

*"Ninety percent. Felix — the reactor won't hold. Ninety percent is where it ends unless—"*

The sentence stopped. Not cut off. Held, the way a breath is held.

Felix looked up from the console, and Vane saw her understand, all at once, what the *unless* was going to cost — and he saw her reach for the answer before the voice could finish asking.

On the dark colony below, the beacon tower burned alone against the night, and the last ten percent of the truth hung in the balance like a held note.
