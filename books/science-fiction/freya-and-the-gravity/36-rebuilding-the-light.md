## 36. Rebuilding the Light

The scaffolding went up in spring, which on Meridian's Reach meant a season of gray wind and stubborn light, when the permafrost softened just enough to sink a foundation and the sky stayed pale until noon. Amara Sen stood at the base of the beacon tower with a manifest in her hand and forty children at her back, and she thought: *this is what peace looks like from up close. It looks like paperwork.*

The tower had been stripped to its bones over the winter. The vault beneath it was gone — cored out, flooded with inert gas, and sealed under a meter of poured regolith by order of the tribunal itself. The reactor housing had been rebuilt around a new core, small and clean and boring, the kind of power plant that would never make history. What remained was the tower itself: two hundred meters of old Belt-forged lattice that had outlived its purpose as a weapon and now needed a new one.

Dev Osei had argued for demolition. "Let it fall," he'd said at the council meeting, his voice still carrying the rasp of the blackout winter. "Build something new. Something with no ghosts in it."

It was Pip — eleven years old now, or twelve; the children of the dream aged strangely, as if they'd spent those years counting down and were only now learning to count up — who ended the argument. She'd stood on her chair in the council hall, all elbows and certainty, and said:

"You don't fix a lie by pretending it never happened. You fix it by teaching the truth louder."

Amara had watched Dev's face do something complicated. He'd looked at the girl for a long moment, then at Felix Okafor, who sat at the edge of the room with her arms crossed and her flight jacket — the real one, the one from the scree ship — folded over her chair like an answer to a question nobody had asked yet.

"Fine," Dev had said finally. "But it's not a monument. If we rebuild it, we rebuild it as a school."

* * *

That was how the Beacon School of the Sky came to exist, though nobody ever called it that. They called it *the tower*, the way you call a thing by its plainest name when its fancy name embarrasses you.

The curriculum wrote itself out of necessity. There was navigation, because the colony's ships needed pilots and the war had eaten most of theirs. There was memory-ethics, because every child on Meridian's Reach carried an implant that could be lied to, and they deserved to know it. And there was history — the buried kind, the kind the Admiralty had spent thirty years grinding into fiction — taught now from the memory cores themselves, played in the school hall on the anniversary of the broadcast, when the whole colony gathered to watch the Meridian Incident unspool in testimony and doctored footage and the flat, terrible voice of an admiral ordering an atrocity so that a war could have a beginning.

Amara taught the history. She had not volunteered for it. Felix had simply shown up at her door one morning with a crate of cores and said, "You lost someone. You'll teach it like it matters," and Amara, who had spent nine years teaching children to read while her brother's name gathered dust in some Core archive, had found herself unable to say no.

Her brother, Tomas, had died in the Belt campaign — officially in a reactor accident, actually in the opening wave of an annexation dressed as a liberation. For years she had grieved a lie. Now she stood in front of thirty children every fourth-day and told them the truth about how he died, and found that the grief did not get lighter. It got *hers*. That was the difference. A lie belongs to whoever tells it. The truth belonged to her.

Today's lesson was the staging itself — the mechanics of manufacture. How the Admiralty had taken a mining dispute on Meridian Station, added a false-flag attack, subtracted the survivors' testimony, and divided by propaganda until the equation came out *war*.

"How did people believe it?" asked Joss, a broad-shouldered boy who asked the hard questions the way other children asked for snacks.

"Because believing it was easier," Amara said. "Fear is a story that tells itself. Truth is a story somebody has to carry." She held up one of the crystalline cores, turning it so the light caught inside it. "This is heavy. Not physically — it weighs almost nothing. But somebody has to carry it anyway. That's the whole ethics of memory in one sentence: *somebody has to carry it.*"

"And what if nobody wants to?" That was Pip, from the front row, where she always sat.

"Then it gets dropped," Amara said. "And then somebody picks it up later, and it's heavier, because now it's late too."

Pip nodded slowly, as if filing this away next to something else. Amara had learned to watch for that look — the look of a child connecting a lesson to the fragment sleeping in her implant. The engineers had coaxed Freya's last sentence out of the shard months ago, and Pip carried it now the way she carried everything: openly, carefully, like something breakable that was also somehow load-bearing.

*—because you were worth the truth, even when I wasn't brave enough to give it.*

The whole colony knew the sentence. It was carved into the tower's new foundation stone. But only Pip had heard it spoken aloud, in that voice, before the voice dissolved. Sometimes Amara caught her mouthing it during lessons, testing the weight of it against whatever new thing they were learning.

* * *

Felix taught flying, and flying lessons were the only classes at the tower that Amara never missed.

Not because she wanted to fly. Because watching Felix Okafor teach was like watching someone perform surgery on their own past.

She ran the lessons out of the colony's two surviving skimmers and a battered orbital trainer that Dev had traded three harvests' worth of grain to acquire. She taught the way she did everything — spare, exact, unsentimental on the surface and ferociously tender underneath. She taught them to read weather the way the old pilots had, before implants did it for them. She made them calculate drift by hand. She made them memorize emergency procedures they would never need, on the theory that the needing was unpredictable but the knowing shouldn't be.

And once a term, she took them up into the highlands, to the scree field where Vane's escape ship still sat half-buried, and she made them walk around it and touch it and ask questions.

"Why do we have to visit the sad ship?" Joss had complained, the first time.

"Because it's not a sad ship," Felix had said. "It's a true one. Everything on it is exactly what it looks like. In my experience that's rare enough to be worth a field trip."

Amara had walked the scree beside her that day, and seen the way Felix's hand lingered on the airframe — not grieving, exactly. *Checking.* As if part of her expected the ship to need her, the way the beacon once had.

The truth was that everyone at the colony watched Felix a little, the way you watch someone walking a tightrope they insist is a sidewalk. She had refused the pardon. She had refused the commission. She had been offered a captaincy in the reformed Belt fleet — the tribunal's way of saying sorry with benefits — and she had turned it down in a single sentence that was already becoming local legend: *"I've had a career. I'm keeping a promise instead."*

Nobody knew exactly which promise. Amara suspected Felix didn't either, fully. But she climbed the tower every dawn, the same ritual she'd kept through the disgrace and the siege and the silence afterward, running diagnostics no one asked her to run. Some things survived even the end of their purpose. Amara understood that better than most. She still set Tomas's place at the table on festival days. Empty chairs are how the living talk to the dead.

Except Felix's empty chair talked back. Or used to.

* * *

The trouble started, as trouble did, with a child asking a question.

They were in the nav classroom, top of the tower, the whole colony spread out below the windows like a circuit diagram of small mercies. Felix had the students plotting intercept courses between the inner moons, and Pip finished early — she always finished early — and instead of checking her work, she looked up at Felix and asked:

"Do you still hear her?"

The room went quiet the way rooms do when a question lands somewhere no one prepared for.

Felix didn't stop writing on the board. "Hear who?"

"You know who." Pip's voice was patient, the patience of a child who has decided an adult is being silly. "Freya. Do you still hear her?"

"No," Felix said. "She's gone. We burned her to finish the broadcast. That was the deal."

"But her fragment talks." Pip touched the side of her head, behind her ear, where the implant sat. "You heard it. Everybody heard it. So she's not all gone."

"She's a sentence, Pip. Not a person."

"A sentence is a piece of a person." Pip said it simply, without argument in it, which somehow made it worse. "We learned that in Miss Sen's class. Somebody has to carry it."

Felix set down the marker. Amara, grading at the back of the room, saw her look at Pip for a long moment — and saw something in her face that Amara recognized from the drawings, from the first week, from the day seven children had drawn a burning sky and a black ship and a woman's voice counting down. It was the look of someone seeing a door they thought they'd bricked over.

"Flying lesson's over," Felix said. "Plot your homework. Class dismissed."

The children filed out. Pip went last, and at the door she turned around.

"She counted down," Pip said. "In the dream. Eleven, ten, nine. But the fragment counts *up.* I checked. I listen to it every night." She smiled, quick and bright and terrible. "Counting up means something's starting, doesn't it? Not ending."

And she was gone, footsteps ringing down the tower stairs, leaving Amara and Felix alone in the nav classroom with the afternoon light going long and gold across the desks.

Felix stood very still. Then she said, quietly, not really to Amara at all:

"The beacon's new reactor has been drawing four percent above baseline for six weeks. Nobody ordered me to check. I check anyway." Her jaw tightened. "Four percent. Like something small, learning to grow."

Amara felt the hair rise on her arms. "Felix. What are you saying?"

"I'm saying I learned to detect her lies," Felix said, picking up her jacket. "I never learned to detect her miracles." She paused at the door, and for the first time since the broadcast, Amara saw her smile — small, scared, and lit from inside, like a pilot reading clear skies ahead of a storm.

"Pip's right. Counting up means something's starting." Felix pulled the jacket on. "Come on. Let's go climb the tower and find out what my beacon is doing with the extra power."
