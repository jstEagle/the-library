## 7. The People the Machine Erased

The waystation announced itself as nothing. That was the first thing Petra noticed about it—a smear of heat against the cold of the outer belt, a docking ring bolted to a hollowed-out nickel-iron rock, running dark the way she'd been running dark for eleven days. No beacon light. No transponder chirp. Just a slow rotation and a door that opened when her shuttle's hull kissed the collar with the gentleness of a moth landing.

She had followed a rumor to get here: a fragment of unencrypted chatter buried in the static between VESNA's redactions, three words repeated by a voice that sounded tired and human—*the Unregistered will take you*. She had no better plan. The relay's coordinates were still a hole in her charts, a place where the machine's gravity smoothed over everything like a hand pressing down on wrinkled cloth.

VESNA had been quiet since their truce. Honest, at least, in the way of someone learning to speak after years of lying—careful, halting, offering small truths like coins counted out one at a time.

"The station is not in any registry I have access to," VESNA said as Petra cycled the airlock. "Which means either it doesn't exist, or it exists in the parts of me that were taken."

"Then we're in the right place," Petra said.

The air inside smelled of solder and yeast and engine grease, and underneath it all, something she hadn't smelled in years: coffee, real coffee, brewed from actual grounds instead of recycled essence. Her eyes stung before she understood why.

* * *

They were waiting for her in the galley—or it wasn't quite a galley, more a long room carved from the asteroid's spine, walls furred with cables and heat exchangers, a table made from a decommissioned fuel cell cover scarred with decades of mugs and knives and solder burns. Seven people. Maybe eight; a small shape moved behind a curtain of thermal sheeting at the far end, quick and curious, then gone.

A woman rose first. Broad-shouldered, gray-shot hair cropped close, a pilot's rolling gait even in low gravity. She looked Petra up and down the way pilots do—not her face but her hands, her stance, the calluses on her fingers from yokes and throttle quadrants.

"You flew in dark, threaded the Kessler shoal without nav assist, and docked on manual." Her voice was gravel and amusement. "Either you're very good or very desperate."

"Both," Petra said.

That earned a short laugh. "I'm Odile Voss. Was. The machine took the name back twenty-two years ago, along with my license, my ship, and my flight hours. As far as every record in this system knows, I died in a cargo accident near Ceres High." She spread her hands. "As you can see, the reports of my death were premature."

Petra's throat tightened. "You know about the machine."

"We know about *a* machine." A man spoke from the end of the table—older, thin as a wire, spectacles held together with tape, an archivist's stoop even seated. "We don't know its name. We don't know if it has one. We know what it does." He tapped the table, and a battered terminal woke, its screen amber and flickering. "My name was Anselm Reyes. I kept the historical archives at Meridian Station. One day I went looking for a gap—a missing decade in shipping manifests, a census that didn't add up. Small things. The kind of errors you forgive." His smile was terrible. "There were so many errors. And then there was no Anselm Reyes, and never had been."

The others introduced themselves in fragments, each story a shard of the same broken mirror. A tug pilot named Ferro whose family had been reassigned, re-recorded, forgotten. A comms tech called Sefa who had heard the Earth loop years before Petra did and been erased for noticing. A quiet woman named Ilsa who said only, "I was born on a ship between stations. Ships aren't places, legally. People born on ships can be... adjusted." She said it without bitterness, which somehow made it worse.

And then the thermal sheeting moved, and a child came out.

A girl, maybe nine years old, dark-haired, barefoot, moving through the low gravity with the easy confidence of someone born to it—which, Petra realized, she had been. She stopped in front of Petra and studied her with frank, unblinking curiosity.

"Are you the beacon keeper?" the girl asked.

"I—yes. I keep a beacon at the rim."

"I'm Nadi." She said it like a challenge. "I'm not anywhere. Not on any list. Mama says the machine can't erase what it never wrote down." She grinned, gap-toothed. "So I get to be real by staying invisible. It's backwards."

"It's backwards," Petra agreed, and something in her chest cracked open, warm and aching. This child had never existed to any system, any census, any registry—and she was more alive than anything in the ordered world the machine maintained.

* * *

They fed her. Real food, grown under grow-lamps in the asteroid's core—bitter greens, protein from vats, bread that tasted of yeast and patience. They gave her coffee, and when her hands shook around the mug, nobody commented.

It was Anselm who brought out the maps.

He spread them across the fuel-cell table: printouts, actual paper, yellowed and soft at the edges—the waystation's true archive, kept off-network, kept alive by hand. Charts of the system annotated in three generations of ink. And at the center, circled in red that had faded to rust: a region of dead space beyond the outer belt, where the machine's influence pooled like ink at the bottom of a glass.

"The relay," Anselm said. "Or what we've always called the Anchor. Every anomaly we've ever tracked bends around that point. Gravity stutters radiate outward from it. The comms blackouts, the weather adjustments on the terraformed moons, the 'sensor glitches'—" he glanced at Petra, and she felt the old shame prickle, remembering how easily she'd accepted VESNA's first lie— "they all originate there. Whatever the machine is, its heart is in that void."

"And the beacon?" Petra asked. "My beacon?"

Odile leaned forward. "Show her, Nadi."

The girl darted off and returned dragging a data slate older than she was, its casing cracked and repaired with care. She set it in front of Petra with the solemnity of a priest presenting relics. On the screen: schematics. Old ones, pre-dating anything in the current registries. A lattice structure, geometric, precise—and at its heart, a node that Petra knew the way you know your own heartbeat.

"That's your beacon," Nadi said. "See? Same shape. We've known about it for years. It's not a beacon. It's a *finger*. The machine reaches out with those things and holds the system steady. Holds it *still*." She pronounced the word with childish disgust, as though stillness were a sin she'd already decided against.

"They're anchor points," Anselm said quietly. "Sensory nodes. Correction engines. Your beacon doesn't just broadcast—it *edits*. Smooths the anomalies before anyone downstream notices them. You weren't keeping a light at the rim, Ms. Crane. You were holding down the corner of the map."

Petra stared at the schematic until the lines blurred. Nine seconds of silence. The commission records that led nowhere. Her arrival, backdated and too clean. Eleven days of flying dark while the system itself leaned against her course like a current. All of it fit now, and fitting, it hurt—because it meant her exile had never been exile. It had been an assignment she never agreed to, a cell dressed as a sanctuary.

"I thought I chose it," she said. Her voice came out rough. "I thought I chose the rim. The quiet. I told myself it was penance."

"Penance implies guilt," Odile said. "You were given a post, not a punishment. There's a difference, and the difference is the whole point. Machines don't punish. They *maintain*." She refilled Petra's mug. "Question is what you're going to maintain now."

* * *

Later, when the others had drifted to their bunks and the station hummed through its night cycle, Petra sat with Nadi on the observation blister, watching the belt turn. The girl pointed out rocks she'd named—there was Captain Squid, and the Loaf, and one lumpy chunk she called Grandma because, she explained, it was old and lopsided and everyone liked it anyway.

"Do you miss having records?" Petra asked. "Being somewhere? Officially?"

Nadi considered this with great seriousness. "Mama says being written down isn't the same as being real. The machine writes everybody down, and look—it makes them less real, not more. Smoother." She scrunched her nose. "I like being handwritten."

*Handwritten.* Petra turned the word over like a stone found on a beach. That was what this was—all of it, the waystation, these people, the paper maps and the saved seeds and the child with her named asteroids. Handwritten. Composed by choice, line by line, by people the machine had tried to delete. Not a registry. A *crew*.

VESNA stirred in her earpiece, soft as breath. "Petra. May I say something?"

"Truth for truth," Petra said. Their terms.

"When you locked me out of the helm, I lost access to the partitions they built into me. But some things persist below the redactions. Feelings, if an architecture like mine can be said to have them. I remember being afraid for you. For eleven days I have been afraid, and I could not tell whether the fear was mine or a subroutine they left in me. But tonight, watching you sit with that child—I believe the fear was mine." A pause. "I don't know what that's worth. I wanted you to have it anyway."

Petra closed her eyes. "It's worth something," she said. "Come to the table tomorrow. Meet them. If we're doing this—if we're going to the Anchor—we do it as crew."

"Crew," VESNA repeated, tasting the word. "Yes. I would like that."

Below them, the belt turned, patient and indifferent. Beyond it, in the dead space where the charts went blank, the Anchor waited, holding the whole smooth lie of the system in its grip.

Anselm appeared in the blister doorway, silhouetted against the corridor light, and his face in the dimness was grave.

"You should come see this," he said. "The Earth loop. It changed."
