## 3. A Ship That Never Flew

The archive vault sat beneath the administration tower like a swallowed secret—three sublevels down, past a checkpoint that logged Dominic's thumbprint twice and asked no questions, into air so dry it tasted of paper and cold metal. The lights came on in reluctant sections as she descended, each bank of fluorescents stuttering awake and then holding steady, as if the building itself had to think about whether to let her in.

She had expected dust. What she found instead was order—aggressive, meticulous order. Rows of server stacks hummed under their cooling shrouds, and along the far wall stretched physical media: crystal lattices in labeled cases, tape reels, even printed ledgers bound in faded synth-leather, the handwriting of dead clerks marching across their pages in columns of careful ink. Someone had loved this place once. Someone had kept it alive through whatever lean years had starved the greenhouses above.

Dominic set her case on the long reading table at the vault's center and pulled up the first chair. The seat creaked under her weight with a sound almost human.

"Launch records," she said to the terminal, and her voice fell flat in all that silence. "Meridian Colony. Complete flight history, orbital and surface-to-orbit."

The system thought about it. She could hear it thinking—a low cycling whir from somewhere behind the stacks, like an animal turning over in its sleep. Then the results bloomed across her display: four hundred and six entries spanning sixty-one years of colonial operation. Freighters, personnel shuttles, survey craft, the great colony ships themselves at the top of the list, their arrival dates treated with the reverence of founding myths.

Dominic Whitlock—the second one, the copy, the inheritance—pulled her original's file open beside the launch logs and began to work the way he would have worked: methodically, without inspiration, trusting only the grind.

That was the part nobody warned you about, being a clone. Not the identity questions, not the legal gray zone, not even the strange grief of remembering a life you hadn't lived. It was this—the way you caught yourself performing the dead. Her original, Dominic Whitlock the first, had been an investigator for the Fleet Office of Colonial Affairs, and he had died on Meridian eleven months ago with his murder file still open on his desk. The Office's solution to that problem had been elegant and cheap: grow another one, load him into her, ship her out. *Finish the audit,* they'd said. *Close the file.*

So she closed files. She cross-referenced. She ground.

* * *

Three hours in, her eyes had learned the rhythm of the data, and the rhythm was wrong.

It wasn't anything dramatic. It was the kind of wrongness that lived in margins—in gaps too consistent to be accidental, in numbers that rounded too neatly. Meridian's traffic control entries were thorough for every vessel except a cluster of freighters in the middle decades, where departure times clustered suspiciously at the top of standard reporting windows, as though someone had filled them in after the fact, from memory or from convenience. And the fuel requisitions—

Dominic leaned back and rubbed her eyes. Fuel. That was the tell, wasn't it? Every launch consumed propellant, coolant, reaction mass, and every gram of it passed through requisition forms, because bureaucracy forgave everything except unaccounted mass. You could falsify a departure log with a keystroke. You could not falsify forty tons of hydrogen without leaving a receipt somewhere.

She built the query slowly, checking her syntax three times before running it, because she wanted the answer to be clean when it came. Cross-reference: all registered launches against fuel drawdowns by pad, by date, flagging any vessel whose logged departure exceeded its logged consumption profile by more than fifteen percent.

The terminal ran for a long time. Long enough that she got up, walked the aisles between the server stacks, read the labels on the crystal cases—*Hydrology Survey 2044–2051*, *Genetic Registry, Inaugural Generation*, *Personnel Manifests, Consolidated*—and listened to the ventilation breathe. The vault felt less like a room the longer she stayed in it and more like a held breath.

Then the chime sounded, soft and apologetic, and she went back to the table.

Seventeen flags. Most were trivial—rounding errors, sensor drift, one shuttle whose pilot had apparently refueled off-book from a mining rig and never told anyone. Dominic dismissed them one by one, feeling the familiar disappointment of investigation collapsing back into paperwork.

The eighteenth entry stopped her hand above the keyboard.

**Vessel:** *Perihelion*
**Class:** Heavy freighter, atmospheric-capable
**Registry:** MER-FRT-009
**Status:** DEPARTED — Pad Nine
**Departure (logged):** Standard Date 4711.203, 0640 hours
**Destination (logged):** Ceres Transfer Station, via fleet relay
**Manifest classification:** Personnel + cargo

She opened the fuel record for Pad Nine on Standard Date 4711.202 through .204 and felt the temperature of the room change—or imagined she did, which amounted to the same thing.

The *Perihelion*'s departure was logged at 0640 on day 203. The pad's fuel drawdown for that entire window showed a total transfer of nine hundred kilograms of reaction mass. Nine hundred. A heavy freighter of that class burned more than that taxiing off its cradle. The figure wasn't a rounding error; it wasn't drift. It was the signature of a ship that had never moved—of systems tested, perhaps, of engines warmed and cooled, but of no departure. No burn. No climb. No ship going anywhere.

Dominic sat very still.

Someone had written *departed* into the log for a vessel that had never left the ground. And someone else—or the same someone, twenty-some years ago, in this same vault or one like it—had signed off on it, because the record showed a validation stamp, clean and official, from the office of the Colony Administrator.

Her pulse picked up, and she noticed herself smiling, a small involuntary thing, and hated it a little. This was the feeling. The hook setting into flesh. Every investigator knew it and no investigator trusted it: the moment when a discrepancy stops being data and becomes a door.

"Okay," she murmured to the empty vault. "Where did you go, *Perihelion*?"

She pulled the full service history. The freighter had arrived at Meridian fourteen years after colonization, assigned to the orbital logistics pool. Its maintenance logs were exemplary right up until day 203—and then ended entirely. No decommission record. No scrapping certificate. No transfer to another registry. The ship simply ceased to exist administratively, the way people sometimes did in colonies where the census was more aspiration than document.

And there was something else. Dominic scrolled back up to the departure entry and looked at the manifest line again. **Personnel + cargo.** For a heavy freighter running a routine supply loop to Ceres, that phrasing was odd enough—cargo runs didn't usually carry personnel manifests—but what made her breath go shallow was the count attached to it.

Forty-one souls.

Forty-one names, encrypted under a standard privacy seal, with a single name visible in the summary header—the manifest officer's entry, the one name required to be public under colonial transparency law:

**VOSS, FREYA**

Dominic stared at it.

The name meant nothing. She checked that carefully, the way her original would have, refusing to let pattern-hunger manufacture significance out of noise. She ran Freya Voss against every database the vault offered: the living census, the genetic registry, the personnel rolls, the burial records, the medical archive, the education system's alumni files, the tax rolls, the water-ration allocations. Sixty-one years of Meridian's institutional memory, and the name appeared exactly once.

On the manifest of a ship that had never flown.

No birth record. No death record. No arrival, no departure, no residence, no next of kin. In a colony small enough that everyone was, in some sense, everyone's business, Freya Voss was a ghost with a boarding pass.

Dominic sat back. The fluorescent light buzzed overhead, and somewhere in the stacks a cooling fan cycled down, and the silence that followed had weight to it, the silence of a room listening.

People didn't vanish from registries by accident. Registries were how colonies kept track of calories and oxygen and grief; every entry was a body accounted for, a mouth fed, a berth assigned. To be absent from all of them was to be absent from the economy of staying alive. Either Freya Voss had never existed—which raised the question of who had put her name on a classified manifest—or she had existed and been removed, scrubbed with a thoroughness that took effort, authority, and motive.

Or—Dominic turned the third possibility over slowly, reluctantly—she had been removed by the same hand that had written *departed* over a pad that had never launched, and the two erasures were the same erasure. Forty-one souls, sealed and encrypted. One name left visible, maybe by oversight, maybe by carelessness, maybe—she couldn't shake the thought—maybe deliberately, the way you leave a window open in a house you want someone to enter.

She was getting ahead of herself. She knew that. Twenty years in the grain of old data could turn coincidence into conspiracy if you stared long enough. Her original's notes were full of warnings against exactly this: *the file wants a story; don't give it one.* But the notes also said, in his cramped late-period handwriting, the entry dated three days before his death: *Everything on this rock reconciles except the things that matter. Find what doesn't reconcile.*

The *Perihelion* didn't reconcile.

* * *

She worked until her eyes burned and the vault's chronometer told her she'd missed two meals, and then she worked a little longer, because stopping felt like letting the trail cool. She mapped Pad Nine's physical layout from the engineering archives: a heavy-lift cradle on the colony's western shelf, decommissioned now, officially "retired due to structural fatigue." She pulled the incident reports for the surrounding district and found nothing—no fire, no crash, no anomaly logged anywhere near the pad in the relevant window. Whatever had happened to the *Perihelion*, it had happened without disturbing a single form.

Except one. At the bottom of the fuel drawdown file, buried in the automated system annotations, was a single manual override entry, timestamped 0217 on the morning of the logged departure—four hours *before* the ship supposedly left. Someone had overridden the pad's telemetry feed manually. Someone had been standing on Pad Nine in the dark, telling the sensors to stop watching.

Dominic copied everything. Three copies: her case drive, a crystal lattice from the vault's blank stock, and—after a moment's consideration—a fragment encoded into the metadata of her own audit working files, where no one auditing an auditor would think to look. Paranoia was just foresight wearing a bad reputation, her original used to say. She was beginning to hear his voice in her head more often now, and she hadn't decided yet whether that was comfort or haunting.

She was sealing the lattice into its case when the terminal chimed again—an incoming system message, unprompted.

**NOTICE: Your session has exceeded standard archival access duration. Further queries require Archivist authorization. Contact: S. VOSS, Records Custodian, Sublevel Archive Services.**

Dominic went very still.

*S. Voss.*

She read it three times, certain she was seeing patterns now, certain and unable to stop. The colony's archivist—the keeper of the vault, the one person whose authorization she'd need to dig deeper—shared a surname with the only visible name on a ghost ship's manifest. Coincidence. It could be coincidence. Voss might be as common on Meridian as rust.

But her hands were already moving, pulling the staff directory, and there it was: **Sable Voss, Records Custodian**, employed in archive services for twenty-two years—hired, Dominic noted, with a small cold click of recognition, within eighteen months of the *Perihelion*'s logged departure. Twenty-two years tending these records. Twenty-two years of whoever-this-was walking past the same shelves, maintaining the same servers, preserving with such evident devotion the very documents that contained the hole.

A custodian who kept everything, guarding the one thing that had been taken out.

Dominic closed her sessions, powered down her display, and sat for a moment in the humming dark with the lattice case in her hands. Above her, three levels of stairs and a checkpoint away, Administrator Cole was probably giving someone his rehearsed smile, reciting his optimistic numbers, numbers that matched no census she could find. Below her, the dead clerks' handwriting marched on in its leather-bound ledgers, recording water rations and seed yields for a colony that had, at least once, recorded a departure that never happened.

One name. One window left open.

Freya Voss.

Dominic stood, shouldered her case, and started up the stairs toward the archive offices, toward the custodian's door, moving faster than an auditor strictly needed to move—moving, if she was honest, the way a person moves when a hook has set and the line has gone taut and something enormous is waiting on the other end of it, patient in the deep, ready to be reeled up into the light.
