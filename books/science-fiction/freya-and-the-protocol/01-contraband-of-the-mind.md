## 1. Contraband of the Mind

The trick to smuggling memories, Paloma Vane had learned over eleven years and four hundred runs, was to make them boring.

Nobody inspected boredom. Customs officers on the long route between the Consensus and the Freeholds were paid by the container, not by the curiosity, and a man who arrived looking interesting got unpacked. So Paloma cultivated dullness the way other men cultivated gardens: patiently, with attention to soil and season. He wore a coat the color of wet cardboard. He filed his manifests early, in tidy fonts, with rounding errors small enough to be honest but large enough to be human. He yawned at the right moments.

Today he was carrying seed patents.

That was the beauty of it—seed patents were genuinely, catastrophically dull. Four hundred pages of drought-tolerance alleles and germination protocols, owned by a Freehold agronomy collective, licensed to an orbital hydroponics consortium, argued about by lawyers on both sides of the cold war who billed by the hour and therefore hoped it would never end. The Consensus wanted the seeds. The Freeholds wanted the royalties. Everyone wanted the paperwork. Nobody, in eleven years of the long route, had ever once opened the appendix.

Paloma opened the appendix.

Not physically—the cache was sealed under three layers of manifest law—but in the way that mattered, in the quiet hour before docking when the freighter *Cormorant* hung in the customs queue like a moth waiting for a porch light. His rig sat in the hold's shadowed corner, a smuggler's lattice of memory-sleeves and checksum ghosts, and inside it, wrapped in the seed patents' digital bulk like a pearl inside a very tedious oyster, was a woman's wedding day.

Her name was Amara Osei. She was dying—that was the whole reason for all of this—and she had hired him through three cutouts and a dead-drop because she wanted her wedding given to her granddaughter on Earth, in the Freeholds, where orbital memory-transfers were classified as contraband of the mind. Forty years of cold war, and the treaties had settled everything except what a grandmother was allowed to leave behind.

Paloma ran the final pass himself. He always did. Automation was for people who could afford to lose a shipment, and he couldn't afford to lose anything, least of all this. The wedding bloomed across his rig's display in fragments as he verified the weave: a chapel with real wood pews, scandalously expensive; a groom crying before the bride even appeared, which the officiant pretended not to notice; Amara's own hands, young and steady, holding a bouquet of something orange and stubborn that had no business surviving the Freehold dust but did anyway, because that was apparently how the family did things.

He checked the emotional watermark twice. If the granddaughter played this back and felt even a flicker of the wrong feeling—if grief bled into joy where it didn't belong—the whole gift would curdle. Memory was perishable cargo. It bruised.

"Beautiful," he murmured to the empty hold, and meant it, and hated that meaning it had become part of the job.

* * *

Customs Station Kestrel-9 hung at the midpoint of the long route, a spinning ring of gray corridors and gray coffee staffed by gray people who had all, at some point, decided that vigilance was someone else's line item. Paloma had been through Kestrel-9 sixty-one times. He knew which inspector read manifests properly (Officer Deng, Tuesdays), which one skimmed (everyone else), and which one had once held him for nine hours out of sheer professional spite (Inspector Voss, who had since been promoted, which told you everything about both institutions).

Today was a Tuesday. Today, Officer Deng was reading.

"Seed patents," Deng said, scrolling. "Agronomy collective. Hydroponics consortium." He looked up with the expression of a man who had personally witnessed every crime humanity was capable of and found most of them administrative. "Vane. You're still doing this."

"I'm still doing this."

"Eleven years. Same coat."

"It's a good coat."

Deng grunted and scrolled deeper, and Paloma stood in the pleasant, practiced calm of a man whose heart rate was lying to everyone including itself. This was the part he was good at—the standing, the waiting, the being nobody. Other smugglers relied on nerve or bribery or spectacular violence. Paloma relied on the radical proposition that the universe mostly ignored things it found unremarkable. He had made himself so unremarkable that he sometimes worried he'd done it too well, that somewhere along the long route he had become a rumor to himself.

"Appendix," Deng said.

Paloma's pulse did not move. "What about it?"

"You always have an appendix error. Point-zero-three percent, every run. It's like a signature." Deng tapped the screen. "It's there again."

"Honest mistakes repeat themselves. That's what makes them honest."

"That's either very wise or very stupid."

"My mother said the same thing."

Deng stared at him for a long moment, then stamped the manifest. "Clear. Go be boring somewhere else."

Paloma thanked him warmly, sincerely, and walked away with a dead woman's wedding day tucked into a shipment of seeds, feeling absolutely nothing, which was the skill, which was the whole terrible skill.

* * *

The *Cormorant* made its slow way down-route toward the Freehold side, and Paloma did what he always did between stations: he ate alone, he maintained his rig, and he wrote letters he never sent.

The letters were a habit from early days, back when he'd still imagined he might someday have someone to send them to. They lived in a folder called MAINTENANCE LOGS, encrypted, because sentiment was the one contraband that could get you killed on both sides of the border. The Consensus would call it inefficiency. The Freeholds would call it orbital contamination. Paloma called it Tuesday night, and he wrote:

*M. — Today I moved a wedding past two governments. The bride is dying and the groom has been dead six years and the granddaughter doesn't know any of it is coming. I keep thinking about the orange flowers. You'd have liked her. You'd have liked the job, maybe, if the job hadn't eaten the parts of me you liked.*

He deleted it, as always. Then he restored it from backup, as also always. He was, whatever else could be said of him, a careful archivist.

The truth he'd stopped saying out loud, even to himself, was simple: he was the best at staying invisible, and invisibility was a tax collected continuously against everything human. He knew four hundred clients intimately and had friends among none of them. He carried the tenderest cargo in the system—first kisses, deathbed reconciliations, the sound of children laughing before they learned the family dialect of silence—and he delivered each one sealed, untouched, anonymous. A courier of intimacy who could not afford intimacy. There was a joke in there. He'd stopped looking for it around year seven.

His comm chimed. Waystation traffic advisory, routine, the kind of thing that arrived forty times a day: *Unscheduled contact, bearing 114 mark 22, transponder dark, trajectory consistent with inbound docking request. All vessels advise clearance.*

Paloma almost deleted it. Then he read it again.

Transponder dark was common enough—smugglers, refugees, the occasional prospector running silent to dodge tolls. But the bearing was wrong. Bearing 114 mark 22 wasn't the shipping lanes, wasn't the mining belts, wasn't anywhere anyone came *from*. It was deep space. The long empty. Nobody docked from the long empty, because nobody was out there. The cryosleep fleets had been grounded for a generation; the deep routes had gone fallow; the star maps beyond the system's edge were forty years out of date and gathering dust in two governments' archives, each convinced the other was the only threat left worth mapping.

And yet something was coming in. Something old, by the drift of its approach—decelerating for weeks, probably, unseen, patient. Something that had started home a long time ago.

Paloma watched the advisory refresh. The waystation's traffic net flagged the contact, queried it, got nothing, flagged it again with the digital equivalent of clearing its throat. Then, after a long minute, the contact answered—not with a transponder code but with a voice transmission, audio only, degraded by distance into static and ghost tones.

The traffic net routed it to every station on the long route automatically. Paloma heard it in his quiet hold, alone, at the edge of nowhere, and the hair on his arms rose like the orange flowers refusing to die.

The voice was a woman's, exhausted, fraying at the edges, repeating on a loop:

*"This is the freighter Meridian, registry Consensus Deep Range, requesting emergency docking. Our crew complement is twelve. Our reactor is stable. Our position—" a crackle, a breath, "—our position is home. We are coming home. Please tell us what year it is. Please. Tell my daughter—"*

The loop restarted. *This is the freighter Meridian—*

Paloma sat very still. Meridian. He knew the name the way everyone in the system knew it—a ship lost twenty-two years ago, a cautionary tale told in both capitals, a tragedy the Consensus mourned publicly and the Freeholds privately suspected had been something else entirely. Twelve crew. Declared lost with all hands in the deep range. Closed file. Buried file.

Coming home.

He looked at his rig, at the wedding sleeping inside the seed patents, at the folder of unsent letters, at the whole careful architecture of his invisibility—and felt, for the first time in eleven years, the distinct sensation of being seen. Not by customs. Not by a government. By history, maybe, or by whatever it was that had spent two decades in the dark and was now decelerating toward a waystation full of bored inspectors and gray coffee, asking the only question that mattered.

*Tell us what year it is.*

Paloma reached for the comm, hesitated, and then did the most dangerous thing he had done in his entire career.

He answered.
