## 12. The Company Man

The ship came down through Freya's Landing's perpetual ash-haze at 0400 local, and Xanthe knew it before the dock alarms finished cycling. They knew it the way a body knows weather — the pressure drop in the ears, the particular bass note of a military-grade drive bleeding off heat it had no business carrying in a civilian chassis.

Meridian-class courier. Corporate registry. *Their* registry.

Xanthe had been awake anyway. Sleep had become a negotiation they kept losing, and the last three nights they'd spent in the records annex instead, cross-referencing the water reclamation schematics against the original habitat blueprints, building a case file that had no court to go home to. The tea in their flask had gone cold hours ago. They drank it anyway, watching the descent burn bloom orange against the grey sky like a wound opening in the clouds.

Sefa found them at the annex door, still in her dock jacket, hair flattened on one side where she'd clearly slept sitting up. She looked at the sky, then at Xanthe, and her face did something complicated.

"That's not a supply ship."

"No."

"That's not even a *legal* ship. That registry prefix—" She stopped. "You know who it is."

"I know what it is." Xanthe set down the flask. "It's a decision arriving before I've made it. That's how they work. They never give you time to choose. They just present the choice as already made and wait to see if you're obedient enough to agree."

"Who's *they*, Xanthe?" Sefa's voice was quiet, and underneath the quiet was steel. "Who did you work for? Really?"

Xanthe looked at the burning sky and told her the truth, because the hour was too ugly for anything else.

"Meridian Assurance Consortium. You'd know them by their retail name — Meridian Life & Property. The company that holds the settlement bonds on Freya's Landing. The company that paid out your widow's claim. The company that—" Xanthe exhaled. "The company whose falsified paperwork I've spent three weeks documenting."

"And they sent a courier at four in the morning to collect one disgraced investigator?"

"No." Xanthe picked up their case slate, the one with everything on it — the fracture signatures, the forged actuarial tables, the dead-letter archive receipt buried under nine layers of misdirection. "They sent a courier to collect one disgraced investigator *and everything they've touched*. The investigator is incidental. The records are the liability."

Sefa stepped into the doorway, blocking it with her body — an old habit, Xanthe realized, a dockworker's reflex, standing between cargo and harm. "Then they don't get them."

"Sefa—"

"You heard me." Her jaw was set. "This colony has buried thirty-one people on paperwork that called it weather. I'm not handing the only honest ledger those people ever had to the people who wrote the lies. You can arrest me for obstructing a corporate retrieval if you want. Get in line. There's a fleet commander ahead of you."

Despite everything — the cold tea, the sleepless nights, the orange wound in the sky — Xanthe felt something in their chest turn over. It took them a moment to name it, because it had been so long since they'd felt it that it arrived unlabeled, like cargo with the manifest burned off.

Someone was standing between them and the fire.

"Nobody's arresting anybody," Xanthe said. "Yet. But we need to move the archives before that ship finishes deorbit checks. And I need to meet this retrieval agent on the dock, alone, before they decide to meet us somewhere worse."

"You're going to *talk* to them?"

"I'm going to *stall* them." Xanthe pulled on their coat, the good one, the one with the corporate lapel pin they'd never removed because removing it would have been a statement and keeping it had always just been a habit — until now, when it was going to be a prop. "There's a difference, and it's going to matter in about twenty minutes."

* * *

The courier settled onto Pad Three with the delicacy of a predator that has never needed to hurry, and Xanthe stood at the base of the ramp in the pre-dawn cold and watched the airlock cycle, and felt the past come down the stairs two steps at a time.

Dax Corrigan looked exactly the way ten years had promised he would. Broader through the shoulders, greyer at the temples, the same unhurried walk, the same half-smile that had once made Xanthe laugh across a claims office desk in a life that belonged to someone else. He wore retrieval-grade armor under a civilian greatcoat, which was its own kind of message: *I came prepared to be your friend, but I budgeted for the alternative.*

"Ravensworth." He stopped at conversational distance, hands visible, palms out — Corrigan's old trick, making compliance look like courtesy. "You look terrible."

"You look expensive." Xanthe kept their voice level. "Meridian doesn't field retrieval assets for claims under nine figures. What am I worth now, Dax?"

"You were never worth anything, Xanthe. That was always your problem." He glanced past them at the dark colony, the dead streetlamps, the habitat towers with their patchwork of emergency sealant glowing faintly in the ash-light. "You never understood that the file isn't about you. It's never about the investigator. You're a vector. You're what the liability travels along." He smiled again, softer. "That's why they sent me. I know how you think. I trained half of it into you."

"You trained me to find the truth and then file it somewhere it couldn't hurt anyone."

"I trained you to *survive*." Something flickered behind the half-smile — not quite regret, but the shape of it, the negative space. "And you were so good at it, Xanthe. Twenty years. Two hundred closed claims. Not one of them ever came back on us. Do you know how rare that is? Do you know what you *were*?"

"A good actuary of ruin."

The old phrase landed. Xanthe watched it land — watched Corrigan's face do the small involuntary arithmetic of a man hearing words he'd said in another decade come back to him in this one, in this place, wearing this meaning.

"You've been reading the old case files," he said quietly.

"I've been reading *everything*."

"Then you know why I'm here." Corrigan turned, slowly, taking in the colony the way an assessor takes in a write-off. "Meridian holds the settlement bond on Freya's Landing. Meridian holds the reinsurance on the habitat's structural integrity. Meridian holds the war-risk portfolio for the Fleet's third squadron." He said it flatly, a man reciting a balance sheet. "And Meridian held the war-risk portfolio for the *other* side, two hundred years ago, when there was another side, and holds the continuity instruments on both governments' founding archives today. We have been the only counterparty on both ends of this conflict since before either government had a name." He looked back at Xanthe. "The war doesn't end, Xanthe. Wars with a single insurer don't end. They *renew*."

The cold went through Xanthe then, deeper than the dawn air. They had suspected it — had felt the shape of it in the numbers, the way the sabotage patterns echoed across decades like a signature practiced for generations. But suspicion was a hypothesis. Corrigan had just handed them the theorem.

"Both sides," Xanthe said. "You underwrote both sides."

"We *stabilized* both sides. There's a difference, and the difference is that one of them is legal." Corrigan's voice hardened by a degree, the warmth draining out of it like heat from a breached hull. "Two centuries of ceasefire, two centuries of managed tension, two centuries of premiums paid by governments that would have collapsed into each other or apart without us. Every orphan pension, every reconstruction bond, every fleet deployment contract — underwritten. You want to know what happens when a child walks into an archive with a message that proves the whole architecture was built on a falsified act of war?" He shook his head slowly. "Every policy voids. Every pension defaults. Every government on both sides discovers, simultaneously, that its entire financial foundation was an insurance product. You think that's liberation? That's not liberation, Xanthe. That's *collapse*. Billions of people holding instruments that become wallpaper overnight."

"So the deaths here—" Xanthe kept their voice steady through sheer professional discipline, the discipline they'd spent twenty years building and now despised. "The thirty-one. The widow's husband. The pressure anomaly. The water reclamation. That's not sabotage in the sense I documented. That's *loss prevention*."

"That's actuarial necessity." Corrigan said it without flinching, and that was the worst part — the absolute absence of flinch. "Freya's Landing was scheduled for managed decommission in fourteen years. Your records, your fracture signatures, your beautiful forensic case — it accelerates a process that was always coming, and it does so in a way that creates discoverable evidence of *intent*. Intent is the one thing we cannot underwrite." He stepped closer. "Give me the archive, Xanthe. The case slate, the dead-letter receipt, everything. Come home with me. Full reinstatement — not the disgrace-and-reassign deal they offered you, the real thing. Senior investigator, Meridian Special Claims. You'd have authority to reopen any file in the consortium's history." He paused, and the half-smile returned, and for one moment it looked almost like the face of the man who had bought Xanthe a drink after their first closed claim and said, *we keep the world running by keeping its stories straight.* "You could spend the rest of your career un-falsifying things. Isn't that what you've been doing out here? Doing it with a badge instead of a fugitive's coat."

It was a good offer. That was what Xanthe held onto as the silence stretched — that it was a *good* offer, calibrated by someone who knew them, aimed at the exact fracture line where Xanthe's twenty-year career had broken. A lesser temptation would have been money. This was meaning. This was *come back and be what you were, but honest this time.*

"You're offering me the chance to spend my life correcting the record," Xanthe said slowly, "inside an organization whose entire function is to make sure the record never gets corrected."

"I'm offering you the only room where the record can be touched at all."

"And the child?"

The words came out before Xanthe had decided to say them, and the moment they did, they knew it was a mistake — and watched Corrigan know it too.

The half-smile didn't change. That was the terrible thing. It didn't change at all.

"What child?" Corrane said — Corrigan — pleasantly, and then, because he had never been cruel in ways that were wasted: "The signal source on the derelict station? The one the Fleet flagged as a sanitization target six days ago? The one whose existence is, itself, an uninsurable liability?" He tilted his head. "You said *the child*, Xanthe. I said *signal source*. You might want to think about what you just told me."

The ash-haze was turning grey-gold at the edges. First light. Somewhere across the settlement, a shift whistle would be sounding, and Sefa would be moving the archives through the mine shafts, and a child who had never touched another human being until three weeks ago was learning what it meant to have people who would stand between them and the fire.

"I told you nothing," Xanthe said. "You already knew. You always know. That's what the consortium *is* — it's knowing things before the people they happen to have finished finding out."

"Then we understand each other." Corrigan straightened his greatcoat, and the courtesy drained out of his posture like a tide going out, leaving the retrieval-grade armor showing through. "Here's how this proceeds, Ravensworth. You have until local noon to surrender the archive. At noon, my team executes a standard evidence-recovery warrant — it's valid in three jurisdictions, including the one you're standing in — and we search this settlement top to bottom. And if, in the course of that search, we locate an undocumented minor of unknown provenance carrying consortium-classified materials—" He let it hang. "Then the child stops being a Fleet problem and becomes a Meridian problem. And Xanthe, I need you to hear me on this, because I'm saying it as the last friend you have on this rock: the Fleet *sanitizes*. We *preserve*. Whatever Meridian does with that child, it will at least be alive afterward. Can you say the same for Commander Voss?"

"They're the same threat wearing different logos."

"No." For the first time, something real moved through Corrigan's face — something tired, something old, something that might once have been the man who bought the drink. "The Fleet is a threat. Meridian is a *fact*. You can fight a threat, Xanthe. You can't fight a fact. The consortium insured both sides of this war for two hundred years because facts are the only thing that survives them." He turned toward the ramp, then paused, not looking back. "You were the best I ever trained. Come noon, I'd rather not find out how good."

The airlock swallowed him.

Xanthe stood alone on Pad Three as the dawn came up sickly through the ash, and did the arithmetic they were built for. Four hours until noon. A courier with a retrieval team — six, maybe eight operators, corporate-grade. A fleet in high orbit with a sanitization order. A colony of four hundred people with water reclamation held together by Sefa's hands and prayer. A child in the mine shafts. An archive that could end a war or detonate one.

And somewhere in the middle of it, the last friend they had from the old life, standing on the wrong side of a line Xanthe had crossed three weeks ago in a widow's kitchen and never crossed back.

Xanthe keyed their comm. "Sefa. It's me. Change of schedule — we don't have until the fleet arrives anymore."

Static. Then her voice, rough with sleep and something harder underneath: "How long?"

"Noon."

A pause, long enough for Xanthe to hear the colony waking up behind it — the shift whistle, the habitat pumps, the small stubborn sounds of four hundred people refusing to be a write-off.

"Then we'd better get to work," Sefa said. "And Xanthe — whoever he is, whatever he was to you—"

"He's what I would have become," Xanthe said. "That's the whole horror of it. Come on. We've got four hours to make sure the truth is somewhere he can't file it."

Across the settlement, in the dark of the mine shafts, a child who carried a war in their memory was waking to the sound of footsteps — and for the first time in their life, they weren't afraid of them.
