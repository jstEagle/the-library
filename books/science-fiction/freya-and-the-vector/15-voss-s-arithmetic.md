## 15. Voss's Arithmetic

The *Meridian's Sword* hung at the edge of the Freya system like a held breath.

Commander Halvard Voss stood at the viewport of his ready room and did the arithmetic he had done every morning for eleven years. It was simple arithmetic. It was the only arithmetic that mattered.

One fleet: forty-one warships, crewed by ninety-three thousand people. One armistice line, drawn in blood two centuries ago and maintained since by nothing but habit and mutual exhaustion. One truce, expiring in thirty-nine days unless both governments found a reason to extend it — and neither government had found one in twenty years of negotiations that Voss privately thought of as the Long Funeral.

Against that: one colony of four hundred souls on a rock nobody had wanted until it was too late to want anything else. One derelict station. One child, if the intercepts were accurate, though Voss had learned to distrust the word *child* when intelligence officers used it. Children grew into witnesses. Witnesses became martyrs. Martyrs became wars.

He turned from the viewport. The arithmetic held. It always held.

"Status," he said, not turning around, because Lieutenant Okafor had been standing at attention behind him for the better part of a minute and good officers learned to wait.

"Patrol elements are in position along the armistice corridor, Commander. The *Kestrel* and *Anvil* have completed their sweep of the outer system. No anomalies." A pause, precisely measured. "No anomalies we've been authorized to look for."

Voss allowed himself the ghost of a smile. Okafor was one of those rare subordinates who could insubordinate with perfect courtesy. He'd promoted her twice for it.

"You have something."

"We intercepted a burst transmission from the surface of Freya's Landing, Commander. Low power, tightly beamed at the old relay buoy. The content is fragmentary, but the linguistic section flagged it." She hesitated. "They flagged it as Meridian."

The word landed in the ready room like a dropped tool. Meridian. The language of the founding generation, the language of the war's first declarations, dead for two hundred years except in the archives where the treaties slept. Nobody spoke it. Nobody was supposed to speak it. Its extinction had been, in its way, one of the peace's quiet achievements — a whole vocabulary for grievance, buried.

"Source?"

"Unknown, sir. But the transmission originated within fifty kilometers of the Vesper quarantine zone."

Voss closed his eyes. Behind them, unbidden, came his grandfather's voice — not the real one, he'd been dead before Voss was born, but the recorded one, the one every child of fleet families heard at the memorials. *We do not fight because we hate. We fight because forgetting is worse.* Fleet families said things like that the way other families said grace. Voss had stopped believing it around age nineteen and had spent the following thirty years building a life on top of the wreckage of that disbelief, the way you build a house on a fault line: carefully, and without ever digging deep.

"Get me everything on Vesper," he said. "And open a channel to our contact on the surface. The corporate one."

"Corrigan, sir?"

"He has people down there. I don't. That asymmetry is about to become inconvenient for everyone."

* * *

The trouble with being the man who kept the peace, Voss reflected later, over the cold tea he never finished, was that keeping a peace required exactly the same skills as waging a war. You needed targets. You needed contingencies. You needed, above all, a clear understanding of what you were willing to destroy in order to preserve the thing you'd named yourself the guardian of.

His father had guarded nothing. His father had been a dockworker on Thessaly Station when the Second Exchange happened — the exchange of ordnance, not words — and Halvard Voss had grown up in the pressurized aftermath, in the corridors of a station that smelled for years of scorched polymer, doing his homework by emergency lighting while the fleet recruiters went door to door offering orphans a purpose. Purpose. That was the word they'd used. Not revenge; the recruiters were careful about that. *Purpose.*

He had believed in it for a long time. Then he'd sat in enough debriefing rooms watching survivors of both sides describe the same battle in mutually incompatible languages, and he'd understood something that reshaped him the way pressure reshapes carbon: the war had no true account. Both governments maintained official histories that contradicted each other on every material fact, and both histories were lies, and the lies were load-bearing. Remove them and the whole structure — the armistice, the trade corridors, the demilitarized zones, the ninety-three thousand careers, the fragile daily peace that let millions of people on both sides wake up and assume there would be a tomorrow — came down.

That was the arithmetic. Not ships and tonnage. Certainty versus uncertainty. A known evil against an unknown worse.

Because here was what the idealists never understood: the truth was not a cure. The truth was a solvent. And you did not pour solvent on a load-bearing wall to see what was underneath, not when people were sleeping in the rooms above.

He had convinced himself of this so thoroughly, over so many years, that the conviction had worn smooth as river stone. He no longer experienced it as a belief. He experienced it as gravity.

And now a dead language was whispering out of a quarantine zone that officially contained nothing, and somewhere on that dying colony below, someone was listening.

* * *

The briefing room smelled of recycled air and recycled arguments. Voss stood at the head of the table and looked at the faces of his senior staff — Okafor; Commander Reyes of intelligence, whose reports were masterpieces of saying nothing at length; Captain Idris of the marine detachment, who had the particular stillness of a man who had done things he'd stopped describing even to himself.

"Vesper Station," Voss said, and put the file up on the display. It was thin. That thinness was itself information. "Officially decommissioned two hundred and six years ago. Officially stripped and abandoned. Unofficially—" he let the silence do work "—it was a quarantine facility during the last year of active hostilities. Civilian population. War orphans, primarily. Records sealed under the Armistice Accords, Article Nine."

"The children's station," Idris said quietly.

Voss nodded once. Everyone in this room knew the rumor. Nobody in this room had ever said it aloud in an official setting, which was how the fleet handled most of its history: by mutual, disciplined silence.

"Recent intelligence suggests the station is not abandoned. There may be a survivor. There may be records. There is almost certainly a transmitter, because something just sent a burst in Meridian toward the surface of Freya's Landing." He let that sit. "I don't need to explain to anyone at this table why that cannot be permitted to continue."

Reyes shifted. "Sir, if there are records — if they're authentic — suppressing them after discovery creates its own risk. If this reaches either government through another channel—"

"It won't."

"With respect, Commander, you can't know that."

"I can't know anything," Voss said. "That is the entire point of my job. I operate in the space between what I know and what I can prove, and I keep the peace inside that space. It is a small space. It gets smaller every year. But it is the only thing standing between ninety-three thousand of our people and a war that both governments will prosecute with weapons two generations more advanced than the ones that burned Thessaly." He met Reyes's eyes. "You think the truth sets people free. I've read the casualty projections for what happens when two hundred years of official history collapses in a single news cycle. There is no version of that scenario, Commander, that ends with fewer graves than the status quo. None. I've had my analysts run it eleven ways. The best case is a decade of border incidents. The worst case doesn't bear description."

"And the colony?" Okafor asked. Her voice was level, but Voss heard the question underneath it. "Four hundred civilians on Freya's Landing. Some of them may already know what's on that station. What happens to them?"

Voss looked at the display, at the thin file, at the redacted decades.

"They're insured," he said. "Which means they're documented, which means they're manageable. The corporation has people on the ground. We'll coordinate." He paused, and then, because some part of him still insisted on honesty even now — especially now — he added: "If it comes to sanitization, we'll do it clean. Evacuation pretext. Humanitarian inspection. Nobody dies who doesn't force the issue."

Idris said nothing. Okafor wrote something down. Reyes looked at the table.

Voss dismissed them, and stood alone in the briefing room for a long moment, looking at the thin file, and felt the familiar weight settle onto his shoulders — the weight he had carried so long he'd mistaken it for character.

This was mercy. He genuinely believed that. That was the thing no one understood about men like him: they were not monsters. Monsters were easy. Monsters wanted to burn things. Voss wanted to prevent fires, and if preventing a fire meant demolishing a house before the flames reached it — well. Firefighters did that. They called it a controlled demolition, and they mourned what was lost, and they saved the block.

He was saving the block. He told himself this every morning with his arithmetic, and every morning the telling worked, and the stone of his conviction wore a little smoother.

* * *

Corrigan's channel opened at 0300 ship time, which told Voss the man had been waiting up. Insurance enforcers kept strange hours; they lived, Voss suspected, in the gaps between other people's disasters.

"Commander." Corrigan's face resolved on the screen — neat, gray-eyed, wearing the smile of a man who had already calculated your price. "I understand we have a shared interest."

"We have a shared problem. Interests imply choice."

"A distinction without a difference, in my experience." Corrigan folded his hands. "You want the station quiet. My employer wants its liability exposure managed. The colony wants, as far as I can tell, to keep breathing. These goals are compatible."

"For now."

"For exactly as long as we both need them to be." Corrigan tilted his head. "There's an investigator on the surface. Ravensworth. Sent to document the habitat failures — the ones my employer's actuaries have been so eager to classify as natural." Something flickered across his face, quickly mastered. "Ravensworth has become inconvenient. I'm given to understand they've been asking questions about Vesper."

"Then retrieve them."

"I intend to. But retrieval requires access, and access requires that local authorities not interfere, and there's a dockmaster down there — Sefa Okonkwo-Varga — who has developed an unfortunate attachment to the investigator, and an even more unfortunate competence with the colony's systems." Corrigan smiled again, thinner. "I'm telling you this, Commander, because I want us aligned. When I move, I'd prefer your patrol elements to be... otherwise engaged. A training exercise, perhaps. Something in the outer system."

Voss studied the man's face and felt the cold, familiar pleasure of recognizing a fellow professional. Corrigan was what Voss might have become if the recruiters had come to a different door. Both of them custodians of a lie. Both of them paid to keep it load-bearing. The only difference was that Voss still told himself stories about mercy, and Corrigan had evidently retired from storytelling altogether.

"You'll have your window," Voss said. "Forty-eight hours, starting tomorrow. After that, my marines go in under humanitarian inspection protocols, and whatever happens on that station afterward happens under fleet authority. Not yours."

"Understood." Corrigan paused, and for a moment the professional mask slipped, and Voss saw underneath it something tired and old and frightened. "Commander, do you ever wonder what's actually in those records? On Vesper?"

"No," Voss said.

It was a lie, and Corrigan knew it was a lie, and both of them let it stand, because the lie was also load-bearing.

The channel closed. Voss sat in the dark of his ready room and did the arithmetic one more time, slower, the way a gambler counts a losing hand.

Ninety-three thousand lives, weighed against four hundred. Two centuries of peace, weighed against one child's testimony. A known order, weighed against an unknown justice.

The numbers didn't balance. They never balanced. That was the secret of arithmetic at scale: you didn't solve it, you chose which losses to carry forward, and you carried them, and you slept — or didn't — and you got up the next morning and counted again.

Thirty-nine days until the truce expired. Forty-eight hours until Corrigan moved. One dead language whispering in the dark.

Voss keyed the intercom. "Lieutenant Okafor. Schedule the training exercise. Outer system, full complement, beginning tomorrow at 0600." He paused. "And pull the Article Nine files. All of them. I want to know exactly what we're pretending not to remember."

In the silence after the channel died, he almost — almost — admitted to himself why he wanted those files. Not to suppress them. To check. To run the numbers one final time, and confirm that the house was still worth saving, and the solvent still too dangerous to pour.

Somewhere below him, on a colony built to be forgotten, a woman with ink-stained fingers and a child who had never touched another human being were about to make that confirmation impossible.

But Voss didn't know that yet. He went to sleep believing in his arithmetic, and it was the last night he would ever believe in it cleanly.
