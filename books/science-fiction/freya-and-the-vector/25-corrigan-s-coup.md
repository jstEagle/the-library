## 25. Corrigan's Coup

The storm came in off the ice fields at dusk, and Dax Corrigan came in with it.

Xanthe saw the shuttle first — a corporate long-range skiff, matte black, running without lights, sliding down through the snow like a knife through wet paper. They were standing in the doorway of the records office with a mug of Sefa's terrible chicane tea going cold in their hand, and for three full seconds they simply watched it descend and thought, with the detached professionalism that had once been their whole personality: *that's a nine-million-credit airframe, and no one flies one of those to a dying colony by accident.*

Then they set the mug down and ran.

The transmitter sat on the ridge above the settlement, a skeletal tower of pre-war alloy that had been relaying weather data and personal messages to the inner colonies since before Xanthe was born. It was the only voice Freya's Landing had. And tonight it was supposed to be rehearsing its most important sentence in two hundred years.

"Sefa." Xanthe hit the comm on their wrist as they took the switchback path two steps at a time. "Sefa, Corrigan's shuttle just landed. He's moving toward the tower."

Static. Then her voice, breathless, from somewhere near the generator shed: "I'm five minutes out. Don't do anything stupid."

"I never do anything stupid," Xanthe said. "I do things that are merely indefensible."

They crested the ridge and stopped.

Corrigan stood in the snow beneath the transmitter array with six enforcers fanned out around him in a formation Xanthe recognized from a hundred claim disputes gone wrong — the corporate wedge, designed to look like security and function like a cage. Snow gathered on his shoulders. He wasn't wearing a coat over his suit, because Dax Corrigan had never once in his life dressed for the weather instead of the meeting.

"Ravensworth." His voice carried easily across the flat white space. Pleasant. Almost warm. That was always the worst part of him — he sounded like a man about to offer you a drink. "You've made this difficult. I want you to know I respect that. In another life we'd have been colleagues of the year."

"Corrigan." Xanthe walked forward slowly, hands visible, boots crunching. "You're a long way from your jurisdiction."

"I'm exactly where my jurisdiction ends and my responsibility begins." He gestured up at the tower with one gloved hand. "This asset is insured under Meridian Consolidated policy 4471-C. As senior claims enforcer, I'm exercising standard protective seizure. Structural hazard. Imminent collapse risk. You understand how the paperwork works."

"The paperwork doesn't work anymore, Dax. You read the same archives I did. You know what's going out over this tower in thirty-six hours."

Something moved behind his eyes — not doubt. Calculation. "I know that a message gets broadcast, and two governments that have spent two centuries keeping a war profitable and polite suddenly have to explain themselves to four billion people. I know that markets convulse. I know that armistice fleets start asking who armed them, and insurers start asking who paid for both sides, and somewhere in that avalanche, a lot of very comfortable people decide that the adjuster who lit the fuse should share the blast radius." He smiled, thin and genuine, which was somehow worse. "I'm not here to threaten you, Xanthe. I'm here to contain you. There's a difference. Threats are for people who might still be reasoned with."

"And me?"

"You're past reasoning. You're in the stage where you think you're right." He tilted his head. "It's a good look on you, honestly. Terrible for your career."

Behind Corrigan, one of the enforcers shifted, and Xanthe saw what they'd been positioned to hide: a second figure being walked up the tower's access stair, hands bound in front, coatless in the wind.

Grey hair whipping loose from its braid. Shoulders squared like she was arguing with the storm itself.

The world went narrow and red at the edges.

"Let her go," Xanthe said, and heard their own voice drop into a register they didn't use — low, flat, the voice from the widow's kitchen in chapter three of their own ruin, the voice that had stopped being an investigator's voice somewhere around the time they started investigating for the dead.

"Dockmaster Sefa Okonkwo is not a hostage," Corrigan said smoothly. "She's a material witness to unauthorized use of insured infrastructure. She'll be released when the transmission equipment is verified destroyed and the record is clean. You have my word."

"Your word is a forgery with good posture."

"Yes," Corrigan agreed pleasantly. "It is. But it's the only currency on this ridge, so let's spend it. Here's the arrangement. The message dies tonight. All copies. Every archive node, every personal slate, every backup the colonists are hiding in their water filters — yes, we know about the water filters, Ravensworth, we wrote the manual on hiding things in infrastructure. In exchange, the colony keeps its transmitter, keeps its dockmaster, and keeps its insurance coverage, which as of this morning has been quietly reclassified as *under review*. You know what that means. You've written that letter. Everyone in this settlement loses everything, slowly, in the dark, in winter. Or—" he spread his hands, snowflakes landing on black leather, "—you give me the message, I burn it, and everyone goes back to dying honestly of old age and bad engineering. Which, frankly, is more than they were promised."

Xanthe looked at Sefa on the stairs. She'd stopped struggling. She was looking directly at Xanthe, and even at forty meters through falling snow, Xanthe could read her expression perfectly, because they'd spent months learning to read it: *Don't you dare.*

"She'll freeze before you finish your speech," Xanthe said.

"She'll freeze slower if you keep talking." Corrigan checked a chronometer on his wrist with theatrical patience. "You have until the storm front closes the valley. After that, my people complete the seizure, the tower suffers a catastrophic structural failure — ice load, tragic, the fleet will confirm it during their humanitarian inspection, Commander Voss is very cooperative about these things — and Dockmaster Okonkwo's death goes in the file as collateral. Actuarially speaking, it barely registers. One life against—"

"One life," Xanthe said, "against the truth. Yes. I've read that policy. So has everyone on this ridge. We filed it under *things the war taught us*."

Sefa's POV:

The bindings were cheap polymer, the kind that softened under sustained friction heat, and Sefa Okonkwo had spent thirty years working cargo rigging in temperatures that ate metal, so she knew exactly how much friction heat her wrists could generate without anyone noticing. She also knew the young enforcer holding her elbow was nervous — his grip kept tightening and loosening like a man testing a rope he didn't trust — and nervous men watched faces, not hands.

But she wasn't going anywhere yet. Not until she understood the shape of the thing.

From the top of the access stair she could see the whole tableau below: Corrigan monologuing in the snow, Xanthe standing there in that coat with the burned-off sleeve they refused to replace, and beyond them both the lights of the settlement strung along the valley floor like someone had spilled a jewelry box across the ice. Her lights. Her people. Two hundred and eleven souls, minus the ones the falsified records had already stolen, plus one child from a dead station who was currently, thank every god Sefa didn't believe in, hidden in the mine shafts with half the colony's children because Sefa had insisted after the water plant burned.

Corrigan thought he was taking a hostage. Corrigan thought he was negotiating with an ex-investigator and a dockworker.

Corrigan had never once, in all his polished career, negotiated with a *dock*.

Because here was the thing about docks: everything came through them. Everything. Ore, food, fuel, letters, lies, enforcers' shuttles. A dockmaster knew the weight of every crate and the registry of every ship and the maintenance history of every seal, and Dax Corrigan's beautiful black skiff had come down through a storm on landing struts that Sefa had watched being serviced at Port Calloway four years ago, on a work order she'd signed off as a transit favor, and those struts had a fatigue rating that this kind of descent ate into like rust.

She hadn't planned any of this. But plans were for people with time, and Sefa had wrists, a memory, and a temper.

Below, Xanthe was talking now — really talking, buying minutes, and Sefa felt something complicated and fierce move through her chest, because she'd known this person when they arrived as a folded-up paper ghost of a human being, all edges and disclaimers, and now here they were standing between a corporate army and a message, stalling like a professional, and if Sefa got out of this she was absolutely going to tell them they'd learned it from watching her.

The nervous enforcer's grip tightened again. Above them, the transmitter's warning beacon swept its slow red circle, painting everyone in pulses of light like a lighthouse having a nightmare.

Sefa began, gently, patiently, to work her wrists apart.

Xanthe's POV:

"You're stalling," Corrigan said.

"I'm negotiating. There's a difference. Threats are for people who might still be reasoned with."

For a moment — one flickering, dangerous moment — Corrigan laughed. Actually laughed, head tipped back, snow catching in the collar of his immaculate suit. "Oh, that's mine. You stole my line. That's genuinely upsetting, Xanthe, I said that to you in the deposition on Calloway Station, I remember because you flinched." He wiped his eyes. "You've gotten better at this. It's almost a shame."

"Here's my counteroffer." Xanthe stepped closer, close enough to speak without shouting, close enough that the enforcers tensed. "You take your people and your skiff and you leave. Because here's what you haven't calculated, Dax, and it's going to embarrass you: the message isn't a file anymore. It's not in a vault you can seize or a tower you can topple. Last night we distributed it to every personal archive in this settlement. Two hundred and eleven copies, encrypted, redundant, mirrored into the mining network's deep storage where your forensic teams would need a year and a permit to find them. You can't burn it. You can only make burning it look exactly like what it is — which is a corporation murdering a colony's future to protect a balance sheet. And tomorrow morning, when Voss's marines land for their inspection, the first thing they'll find is a colony full of witnesses who all received the same document at the same timestamp from the same seized transmitter. Your transmitter seizure becomes our evidence."

Corrigan went very still.

That was the tell. Xanthe had spent fifteen years reading people across conference tables, and stillness was always the tell — the pause where the smooth machinery caught on something true.

"That's a bluff," he said.

"It's a distribution log with checksums. You taught me how to authenticate those. Chapter twelve of your own enforcement manual — *a forgery survives scrutiny only until it meets the original*. I authenticated your company's own records flawlessly last week, Dax. Do you really want to test whether I can authenticate mine?"

He stared at Xanthe for a long moment. Snow accumulated on both of them equally; the storm didn't care whose side you were on. And then Corrigan sighed, and the sigh was the most frightening sound of the night, because it was the sigh of a man closing one ledger and opening another.

"No," he said. "I don't. Which is why I'm not going to test it. I'm going to skip it entirely."

He raised two fingers.

The enforcers moved — not toward Xanthe, but toward the tower's base, toward the junction housings, and Xanthe saw the cutting tools come out and understood, with a lurch, that the negotiation had never been the point. The negotiation was the clock. While Xanthe talked, his people had been wiring the array's structural joints with shaped charges, and the whole elegant conversation had been Corrigan giving his demolition team time to finish.

"—and the dockmaster?" Xanthe called, voice cracking on the last word despite everything.

"Collateral," Corrigan said, already turning away. "Ice load. Tragic. The fleet will confirm—"

The world filled with light and noise.

Not the charges. Something else — a shriek of stressed metal from the tower's upper section, a strobing of the beacon, and then Sefa's voice, amplified through the transmitter's own emergency PA, ragged and furious and *alive*:

"ATTENTION ALL PERSONNEL. THIS IS DOCKMASTER OKONKWO. THE TOWER'S GUY-WIRE ANCHOR THREE IS BURNING LOOSE AND THIS STRUCTURE IS COMING DOWN IN APPROXIMATELY NINETY SECONDS, SO IF ANYONE STANDING UNDER IT WANTS TO KEEP THEIR INSURANCE VALID, I'D MOVE."

Chaos. Beautiful, total chaos. The enforcers scattered from the tower's shadow. Corrigan spun, shouting coordinates, and above them all Sefa came over the top rail of the access platform with her bindings cut — cut, Xanthe realized later, on the guy-wire anchor housing itself, using the housing's own heated joint, because of course she had, because Sefa Okonkwo could defeat a hostage situation with maintenance knowledge — and threw herself down the far-side ladder as the first charge blew the eastern leg.

The tower groaned. Leaned. Held, barely, listing like a drunk leaning on a wall, one leg shattered, beacon still sweeping its stubborn red circle.

And in the strobing light, Xanthe saw Corrigan's face clearly for the first time all night — stripped of charm, stripped of the pleasant warmth, showing the thing underneath, which was fear. Not of the tower. Of the arithmetic. His plan required the destruction to look like an accident, and accidents required witnesses to die, and his witnesses were currently climbing down a ladder unassisted while his demolition charges fired out of sequence against a structure that was refusing, stubbornly, dockmaster-grade-refusing, to fall cleanly.

"Seal the perimeter!" Corrigan roared. "Nobody leaves this ridge! Nobody—"

Headlights swept the switchback road. Then more headlights. Then the deep, grinding growl of a cargo hauler that had no business being out in a storm, followed by figures on foot — dozens of them, colonists in thermal gear pouring up the road with hand-lamps and pry bars and the particular unhurried fury of people who had finally run out of things left to lose.

Old Marta Vasquez from the hydroponics co-op, carrying a wrench the size of a forearm. The Tam brothers, who ran the ore lift. Half the night shift from the docks, still in their work harnesses. They fanned out across the ridge in a wide, silent crescent, and they didn't attack, didn't shout. They just *stood there*, between the enforcers and the road, between the enforcers and the settlement, an unarmed wall of witnesses in orange thermal weave.

Corrigan looked at them. Looked at his six enforcers. Looked at the leaning tower, the escaped hostage, the spreading crowd, and Xanthe watched him do the actuarial math in real time — watched him arrive, visibly, at a number he didn't like.

"This isn't over," he said quietly. Only to Xanthe. Only for Xanthe. "The truce expires in thirty-six hours. When it does, Voss bombards Vesper, the message dies with the station, and every copy in your little mining tunnels becomes a historical curiosity. I don't need your tower, Ravensworth. I just need the clock." He backed toward his skiff, enforcers converging on him. "Enjoy the win. Check the expiration date."

The skiff lifted in a scream of snow-thrusters and vanished south into the storm.

The colonists surged forward — to the tower, to Sefa, to the smoking wreckage of the eastern leg. Xanthe stood in the middle of it, heart slamming, and let Sefa reach them first, grey hair wild, wrists raw, gripping Xanthe's shoulders hard enough to bruise.

"Ninety seconds," she said. "I gave them ninety seconds because that's what the anchor housing needed to cool. You're welcome."

"You cut your own bindings on a live demolition site."

"I cut my bindings on a *maintained* demolition site. There's a difference." She was shaking. Xanthe could feel it through her grip, and pretended not to, because that was the gift she'd given them months ago — treating people like persons, not forms — and it was time to return it. "Tower's salvageable. Eastern leg's scrap but the array head is intact. We can be transmitting by tomorrow night if the storm breaks."

Xanthe nodded. Then, because the thought wouldn't stay down: "Sefa. What he said. About the clock."

"I heard him."

"Thirty-six hours. If the truce expires before we broadcast—"

"Then we broadcast faster." She turned to face the crowd, the leaning tower, the storm, and raised her voice over the wind with the effortless authority of a woman who had loaded ten thousand ships. "Everybody listen up! Tower's damaged, not dead! I need riggers on the east anchorage, welders on the junction housings, and somebody get Iri on the comm and tell that child—" her voice caught, just slightly, just once, "—tell that child the tower held. Tell them we held."

A cheer went up, ragged and defiant, swallowed almost immediately by the wind.

And high above them, the beacon swept its slow red circle — damaged, listing, alive — painting the snow in pulses of light like a heartbeat that refused, on principle, to stop.
