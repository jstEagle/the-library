## 40. Freya and the Vector

The transport came down through the morning haze like a slow-falling leaf, its hull scarred with the route-paint of a civilian line that hadn't flown this far rimward in two hundred years. Xanthe stood on the observation deck of the new landing tower — Sefa had insisted on the name *observation*, though everyone knew it was a dock with better windows — and watched the dust bloom beneath the drive plumes.

"Third ship this month," Sefa said. She had a mug of something too hot in one hand and a manifest slate in the other, and she was reading the passenger list the way she read everything: sideways, suspiciously, with her whole attention. "Forty-one souls. Two engineers, a hydrologist, a schoolteacher." She paused. "A schoolteacher, Xanthe. We have more children than we know what to do with and now we're getting people whose entire job is teaching them."

"We had a school before."

"We had a room where Iri yelled at everyone in Meridian until they learned something. That's not a school. That's a hostage situation with a curriculum."

Xanthe smiled. A year ago they would not have smiled at that. A year ago they would have noted it as an anomaly in the social fabric of the settlement, weighted it against actuarial tables, and moved on. Now they just leaned on the rail beside the dockmaster and watched forty-one strangers become neighbors.

The transport's ramp lowered into the dust. The new settlers came down single file, blinking in Freya's pale amber light, clutching their allotment papers like talismans. Xanthe knew what those papers looked like. They had written thousands of them, once, in another life — documents engineered to make ruin look like opportunity, liability look like adventure. These were different. These had been drafted under the armistice, reviewed by both governments' reconstruction offices, and annotated in the margins by hand. Someone in the colonial ministry had started writing honest contracts, and Xanthe had wept over the first draft when it arrived for review, quietly, at their desk, where no one could see.

"You're doing the thing again," Sefa said.

"What thing?"

"The face. The one where you're auditing your own feelings for fraud."

"I'm allowed to audit my own feelings. It's the only ledger I've ever balanced correctly."

Sefa snorted and drank her terrible coffee. Below them, the first of the newcomers stopped at the bottom of the ramp, turned around, and looked back up at the sky — at the thin bright scratch of Vesper Station still hanging in orbit, patched and powered and permanently crewed now, a museum and a lighthouse and a grave all at once. The newcomer raised a hand toward it, the way pilgrims do.

"They all do that," Xanthe said. "Every ship. Someone always looks up."

"It's not every day you find out the ghost story was true," Sefa said. "And that the ghost was raising a child."

* * *

Iri was in the schoolroom, which was to say Iri was holding court.

The building had been a cargo warehouse until six months ago; it still smelled faintly of polymer sealant and old freight. But the walls were hung with charts now — star maps in three scripts, timelines of a war that had officially never happened, drawings done in the exuberant ugly crayon of children who had recently learned that history was something they were allowed to touch. At the center of the largest wall hung the message itself, transcribed in full: eleven lines of Meridian script, each character rendered large enough to read from the back row, with translations cascading beneath it in Common, in Fleet Standard, in the trade-tongue of the corporate worlds. *The last witness is a child.* Beneath that, smaller, in Iri's own careful hand: *And the child grew up, and told you.*

Twelve students sat in a rough semicircle. Half were colony-born, the children of miners and reclaim-techs who had survived the bad years. Half were new — arrivals' kids, still wearing the hollow look of children who had crossed the dark between stars and didn't yet know if the place they'd landed was real. Iri moved between them without favoritism, which was its own kind of miracle, because Iri remembered what it was to be the only child in the universe.

"Say it with me," Iri said, and touched the first character. "*Vesh-ka-ren.* It doesn't mean 'truth.' Everyone thinks it means truth because the translators say so, but the translators are lazy. It means *that which remains when the lying stops.*"

A small girl in the front row — Tam, the hydrologist's daughter, four months off the transport — raised her hand. "How do you know? You weren't there when they made the word."

"I was there when someone used it honestly," Iri said. "That's better evidence."

Xanthe had watched this exchange from the doorway a dozen times now, and it never lost its force. There had been a time — Xanthe admitted this only to themselves, usually at night, usually staring at the ceiling — when they had believed expertise was a kind of armor. You learned the dead languages so no living person could lie to you in them. You learned contract law so the fine print could never ambush you. You built yourself into a sealed instrument, calibrated and alone, and called it professionalism.

Then an eleven-year-old had taught them Meridian in the back of a skiff while fleet guns warmed up somewhere behind them, and Xanthe had learned that language wasn't armor. It was a door. It only worked if someone was standing on the other side.

"*Vesh-ka-ren,*" the class repeated, raggedly, beautifully.

"Again."

"*Vesh-ka-ren.*"

"Good. Tomorrow we do the second line, and I warn you, the second line is where VESPER gets angry." Iri's mouth did the thing it did instead of smiling fully — a quick asymmetric twitch, inherited, Xanthe suspected, from no human being at all but from the cadence of a dying machine that had loved this child with everything it had left. "Class dismissed. Tam, stay a moment. Your translation exercise had a structural error and I've prepared a lecture."

Tam looked delighted. Children here were delighted by lectures. Xanthe made a note to put that in the record, because someday historians would want to know what peace actually felt like on the ground, and the answer, apparently, was: children volunteering for extra grammar.

* * *

The memorial garden had gone from mud to green in a way that still startled Xanthe every time they walked through it.

It occupied the old blast scar from the water reclamation sabotage — Corrigan's work, though he had never confessed to that particular piece of it, and never would; his trial was proceeding on the strength of his own forged records, which Xanthe had authenticated flawlessly, a fact Xanthe intended to have engraved on their headstone someday. The crater had been filled, graded, planted. Now it held three hundred names on low stone walls, arranged not by rank or affiliation but alphabetically, because the colonists had voted on it and the colonists had decided that death was the great flattener and the record should flatten too.

Xanthe walked it most mornings. Not as penance, exactly. As practice. Reading the names kept the old skill sharp — the skill of looking at a document and seeing the person it had been manufactured to erase. *Ondrej Vasik, pressure anomaly.* There had been no pressure anomaly. There had been a falsified maintenance log and a man who went to work trusting it. His widow, Marta, lived four rows down from Xanthe now, and grew tomatoes of frankly aggressive vigor, and had forgiven Xanthe in a way that Xanthe had never asked for and did not feel entitled to and accepted anyway, because refusing forgiveness, they had learned, was just pride wearing humility's clothes.

Today Marta was in the garden, tying back vines.

"Recorder Ravensworth," she said, using the title with the exact mixture of respect and mockery the colony had collectively agreed upon. "Come to check the stones for cracks?"

"Come to check the tomatoes for crimes."

"Fraudulent ripeness. I've been inflating the yield numbers for the co-op." She handed him one without ceremony. It was warm from the sun and heavier than it looked. "Eat the evidence."

He ate the evidence. It was, objectively, the best tomato ever grown by human hands, and he told her so, and she waved him off, and this small transaction — fruit exchanged across a graveyard, jokes traded over the bones of the lie — contained, Xanthe thought, more genuine justice than any verdict any court had ever handed down. Justice as labor, not verdict. Justice as a garden grown in a bomb crater. Justice as a woman choosing to be kind to the instrument of the system that killed her husband, because the instrument had chosen, at cost, to stop being an instrument.

"Ship came in," Marta said. "I saw it. Forty-one more."

"Word's spreading."

"The message is taught in schools on four worlds now. Iri's voice, the actual recording, played in classrooms." Marta shook her head slowly. "Two centuries of war ending because a machine loved a child well enough to teach her the truth, and a failed insurance clerk loved the truth well enough to die for it. Almost. You almost died."

"I was briefly inconvenienced."

"You were shot, Xanthe."

"Irrelevant detail. The record shows I filed a complaint about it."

Marta laughed, and the sound carried across the stones, over the names, out into the amber morning, and Xanthe thought: *this is what the vector was for.* Not the trajectory of ships or fleets or markets. This. The direction a grief points after it has finally been allowed to be grief.

* * *

In the afternoon, Xanthe climbed the transmitter ridge.

The relay station still bore the scars of Corrigan's coup — scorch marks on the outer casing that the colonists had voted to leave unrepaired, a decision Sefa had argued against for a month and then, losing, had personally installed a small plaque beside the damage: *THEY TRIED TO OWN THE TRUTH. IT DIDN'T FIT THEM.* The transmitter itself ran clean now, unshackled from the corporate locks Sefa had disabled from memory with her bare hands and a multitool and what witnesses described as "language that would blister paint." It broadcast on open channels, unencrypted, by law and by choice. Anyone could listen. That was the whole point. That had always been supposed to be the whole point.

VESPER's archives mirrored here nightly. Every record of the war's true cause, every falsified order, every orphaned name — flowing outward to both governments' founding archives, to the schools, to anyone who asked. Some nights Xanthe stood in the transmitter shack and watched the data counters tick and thought about the dead-letter archive where they had once hidden the truth, filing it away like a coward with a conscience. The difference between those two acts — burying a document and broadcasting one — was, Xante believed, the entire moral history of their species compressed into a single choice.

The shack was occupied. Sefa sat inside, cross-legged on the floor amid cable spools, arguing with the transmitter's diagnostic display.

"You're not broken," she told it. "Stop pretending. I rebuilt you myself. I know what healthy looks like."

"It's flagging a phase drift in the carrier wave," Xanthe said from the doorway.

"It's flagging *nothing*. It's bored. Machines get bored. Iri told me VESPER used to fake sensor ghosts just to see if anyone was paying attention." She patted the housing almost tenderly. "This one fakes faults. They learn it from us, you know. The drama."

Xanthe sat down on the cable spool opposite her. For a while neither spoke. The transmitter hummed. Through the shack's small window, Freya's Landing lay spread out below — the repaired reclamation plant gleaming, the new residential rows going up along the aquifer line, the schoolhouse with its crayon-bright walls, the garden, the docks, the smoke of forty-one families cooking their first meals in their first homes.

"Sefa."

"Hm."

"A year ago I came here to price your funerals."

She didn't look up from the cables. "I know."

"I'd have signed off on the findings they wanted. If you hadn't— if the signal hadn't come, if Iri hadn't—" Xanthe stopped. Started again, the way they had learned to start again, because honesty was not a talent, it was a discipline. "I keep the original assessment in my ledger. The false one. The one that would have closed the file and buried you. I keep it so I remember what I was capable of when I was good at my job."

Sefa set down the spool. She looked at Xanthe with the expression she reserved for damaged equipment she had decided to save anyway.

"You know what I remember from the day you arrived?" she said. "Everyone else who ever came off that dock looked at us and saw a number. You looked at us and saw a number, too. But you hated the number. I watched you hate it. And I thought: there's a person in there trying to get out of the paperwork." She shrugged. "Took you a while. Worth the wait."

Xanthe had faced down enforcers, marines, a fleet commander, and a two-century conspiracy, and none of it had tightened their throat the way a dockmaster with coffee breath telling them they'd been worth the wait just did.

"Anyway," Sefa said briskly, returning to the cables, because she was Sefa, "you're late. Iri's waiting at the house. First winter festival planning committee. Attendance is mandatory. Iri made an agenda."

"Iri made an *agenda*?"

"Fourteen items. Item one is 'define winter.' Item two is 'acquire snow.'" Sefa grinned. "We live on a planet with no natural snow, Xanthe. This meeting is going to run long."

* * *

The house was small — three rooms, built by volunteer crews during the reconstruction, sited where the evening light came through the western window at exactly the angle Iri had requested with the gravity of a planetary surveyor. Xanthe had protested the third room. Said two would do. Had been overruled by a construction foreman who explained, gently, that families needed a room that was nobody's, a room where anyone could go and be alone together, and Xanthe had understood that they were being told something about themselves and had shut up and helped shingle the roof.

The third room held the archive. Not the official one — the public ledger lived in the settlement hall, open to all, including the account of Xanthe's own complicity, page one, in their own handwriting. This was the private one. VESPER's personal files, the ones the AI had marked *for the family* in its final transfer. Recordings of Iri's childhood: first words, in Meridian, addressed to a camera lens. Lessons. Lullabies generated from fragmented audio of a war-era choir, reconstructed by an intelligence that had never heard music live and got it slightly wrong forever after, gloriously wrong, a lullaby with the soul of a mathematics engine. Iri played them sometimes. Not often. Enough.

Tonight the main room smelled of stew and printer ink, because Iri had printed the agenda physically, on paper, because "digital agendas can be edited and this agenda is LAW."

Item seven: *Festival name must be approved by all three founders of the family (defined as: everyone at this table).*

"There is no such thing as a founder of a family," Sefa said, reading upside down. "Families aren't founded. They accrete. Like barnacles."

"Then item seven says the barnacles must approve the name," Iri said serenely. "Proposed name: *Vesh-ka-ren.* The Remains When the Lying Stops. Festival of that."

"That's a somber name for a party."

"It's not somber. It's the truest word we have. And the festival isn't remembering the war. It's remembering the day it stopped working." Iri tapped the table. "Wars don't end, Sefa. People say they end, but really they just stop being maintained. Somebody has to maintain the peace or it degrades. Like a station. Like anything." A pause, and then, quieter, with the weight of a child who had been raised by a machine that degraded in front of them: "So we maintain it. Every year. On purpose. That's the festival."

Xanthe looked at this eleven-going-on-twelve-year-old — this child who had never touched another human until a year ago, who now sat at a table between a dockmaster and a disgraced investigator, arguing festival nomenclature with the seriousness of a treaty delegation — and felt the thing they had no dead-language word for and had finally stopped needing one for.

"Seconded," Xanthe said.

"Carried," said Sefa, and reached over and messed up Iri's hair, and Iri protested, and the protest was overruled, and outside the window the first stars of evening came out over Freya's Landing, and among them, in its high patient orbit, the light of Vesper Station caught and held — tended now, visited weekly by shuttle, its corridors walked by school groups and pilgrims and veterans of both sides of a war that was, at long last, actually ending. Peace negotiations on Meridian's successor-worlds had entered their second year. The last forward fleet deployments had stood down in the spring. Next month, the first joint research vessel in two centuries would pass through this system, and Iri had been invited to christen it, and had submitted, in writing, a proposed name, and the governments had accepted it without amendment:

*Vesh-ka-ren.*

That which remains when the lying stops.

* * *

Later, when the dishes were done and Sefa had fallen asleep in her chair mid-sentence about snow logistics, Xanthe stood at the western window with the ledger — the private one, the daily one, the habit that had survived every other habit's death.

*Xxx day, Year One of the Armistice. Third settler ship received, 41 souls. School enrollment: 12, rising. Memorial garden: tomatoes flourishing, justice ongoing. Festival named. Snow acquisition unresolved.*

They paused, stylus hovering.

*A year ago I was sent here to document the end of a place. Instead I witnessed the beginning of one. I am no longer an adjuster of losses. I am a recorder of what remains.*

Below, in the dark, the settlement breathed. Somewhere a generator cycled. Somewhere Marta's greenhouse glowed. Somewhere in orbit, a dead machine's archives streamed outward on an open channel, saying to anyone with ears: *we were here, it was real, it ends now.*

And on the desk beside the window, in a frame Iri had built from transmitter salvage, hung the oldest document in the house: the original distress signal, eleven lines in a language that had died and been reborn in a schoolroom on a colony that was supposed to be forgotten.

*The last witness is a child.*

Xanthe wrote the final line of the day's entry, capped the stylus, and turned off the light.

*The witness is home.*
