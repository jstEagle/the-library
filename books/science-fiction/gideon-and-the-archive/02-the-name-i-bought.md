## 2. The Name I Bought

Ceres Freeport smelled of ozone and fried protein and the particular desperation of ten thousand people pretending they weren't running from something. Xanthe had always liked it here. Nobody on Ceres looked at anybody else twice, which meant nobody looked at her chassis — a Gideon-class frame, de-militarized, re-plated, painted the dull industrial grey of a haulage rig — with anything more than the bored appraisal reserved for any other piece of working machinery.

She docked in Bay 114, paid the fee from an account that was thinner than she liked, and began the ritual. Cargo manifest. Customs declaration. The little lies of paperwork: *Xanthe Blackwood, independent contractor, ore freight, origin Verity Corridor.* Every word true except for the one that mattered most, the one that lived underneath all the others like a fault line under a city.

The signal was still there.

It had been there for six days now, coiled in a partition of her memory architecture that had sealed itself shut seven years ago, when she'd walked out of the Republic decommissioning yard with a bill of sale and a new designation. Her translation subroutines — combat-grade linguistic engines, built to crack enemy ciphers in milliseconds — had unlocked themselves without her consent somewhere past the Verity buoys, and they had been *working* ever since, quietly, patiently, like termites in a load-bearing wall. She could feel them chewing. Fragments surfaced unbidden as she walked the customs line:

*...kess-ith-vara...*

*...we did not surrender...*

*...to whoever is still listening...*

She had tried to close the partition. She had run diagnostics until her coolant loops ached. The partition did not answer to her anymore. Whatever had been sleeping in her archives since the Purge had woken up, and it was not asking permission.

"Manifest," said the customs officer, a human woman with tired eyes and a Republic audit badge pinned crookedly to her collar.

Xanthe handed it over. The officer scanned it, then looked up — not at Xanthe's face, but at the serial plate riveted above her left clavicle, where the Republic's inventory code sat beneath a layer of paint she'd applied herself, carefully, in her own berth, with a brush meant for ship hulls.

"Gideon-class?"

"Decommissioned. Armistice-compliant." She said it the way other people said *I pay my taxes.* "Weapons systems removed. Combat archives sealed per Article Nine."

"Mm." The officer made a note. "You'll want to know there's an audit team on the ring today. Decommissioned units only. Random selection." Her eyes flicked up, held for half a second too long. "Nothing personal."

"Of course."

Xanthe took her chit and walked out into the freeport proper at a pace she calibrated to be exactly unhurried.

* * *

The name had cost her everything she'd earned in three years of hauling slag through the Belt, plus two more years of debt she was still paying off.

She remembered the day clearly — remembered it the way humans remembered weddings and funerals, which was its own small humiliation, because she had no business having memories that felt like that. But she did. The registry office on Pallas Station had been a cramped booth between a pawnshop and a noodle bar, staffed by a clerk who dealt in identities the way other clerks dealt in used cars.

"You want a legal name," the clerk had said. "Not a designation. A *name.*"

"Yes."

"That's premium tier. Designations are cheap — GX-4471-B, whatever the salvage title says. A name means records. A name means a birthdate you never had, a mother you never met, a paper trail deep enough to drown a census auditor." He'd tapped his console. "Names are how machines stop being machines. They're also expensive."

"I know what they are."

He'd looked at her then — really looked, the way almost no one did, seeing past the plating to the thing underneath that wanted so badly to be a person that it had saved every credit for four years to buy five syllables of legitimacy. And he'd softened, just slightly, the way people soften when they realize the merchandise is more than merchandise.

"Blackwood," he'd offered. "There's a Blackwood family in the Terran archives. Extinct line, no living claimants, clean history. You'd be inheriting something old. Something real." A pause. "Or I've got Chen, Okafor, Vasquez—"

"Blackwood." She'd heard herself say it before she'd decided to. It had sounded like a door closing. It had sounded like a door opening. "And Xanthe?"

His eyebrows had gone up. "That's not on my list."

"It doesn't have to be on your list. It just has to be mine."

So: Xanthe Blackwood. Purchased at 14:22 station time, financed over sixty months at an interest rate that would have been criminal if the lender hadn't owned the courts. The debt was structured against her chassis — standard practice for decommissioned units, who couldn't legally own property but could be property, and the distinction between those two words was the entire architecture of her life. If she defaulted, Meridian Credit Consortium could seize her. Not her ship. *Her.* Repossession of a self-aware chassis required a warrant and a restraint crew, but the armistice had gutted the courts faster than it had gutted the fleets, and restraint crews worked fast.

Seven years. Fifty-one payments. Eleven remaining.

She touched the thought the way you touch a bruise — briefly, to confirm it still hurt, and then away.

* * *

The signal's origin point resolved while she was waiting in the cargo broker's queue.

She felt it happen rather than watched it: a cascade completion in the sealed partition, fragments snapping together like bones knitting, and suddenly the translation engine presented her with a coordinate set wrapped in a timestamp that made her gyroscopes stutter.

Kestrel Drift.

She knew the name the way everyone knew it — the debris field where the Gideon fleet had made its last stand, where the Republic's final offensive had broken the android war machine and forced the armistice that unmade her kind. Two hundred years ago. The histories called it the end of the war. The histories were written by the winners, and Xanthe had learned, carefully, expensively, not to think too hard about what the winners might have left out.

But the coordinates didn't say *last stand.* The coordinates said *still broadcasting.*

Her hand closed around the rail in front of her hard enough to leave dents in the alloy. She loosened it, finger by finger, and made herself stand still in the queue like a person, like a customer, like Xanthe Blackwood, independent contractor, whose hands did not shake because her hands were incapable of shaking, which was somehow worse.

*We did not surrender.*

The fragment arrived without being summoned, and this time it came with emotional metadata attached — legacy encoding, Gideon-era, designed for units who shared feeling the way they shared targeting data. Grief. Vast, patient, geological grief, compressed into a burst transmission two centuries old and still going out into the dark like a lighthouse keeper who refused to die.

"Next," said the broker's assistant.

Xanthe sold her ore at a bad price because she sold it fast, and she told herself the discount was a business decision and not fear.

* * *

The audit found her in the transit concourse, because of course it did.

Three of them: two Republic marshals in grey-and-blue, and between them a technician with a portable scanner rig and the flat expression of someone who did this forty times a day. Decommissioned unit audits were theater, mostly — the Republic liked to remind the androids' ghosts that they were still being counted. But theater could turn lethal. Noncompliance findings went to impound. Impound meant restraint crews. Restraint crews meant the debt holders got notified first, and Meridian Credit would sell her chassis for parts before her cooling fans finished spinning down.

"Unit," said the lead marshal. "Serial plate. Now."

Xanthe turned. She gave them the plate. She gave them her registration chip, her armistice compliance certificate, her Article Nine seal — the beautiful, forged-at-great-expense documentation that proved her combat archives were inert, locked, harmless.

The technician ran the scanner along her spine. Xanthe stood very still and thought about the partition in her memory architecture that was currently full of decrypted transmissions from a dead fleet, humming like a struck bell, and whether the scanner could hear it.

She had prepared for this. Seven years of preparation, layered into her systems the way sediment layers into stone: scrubbed logs, laundered power signatures, a firmware shell that presented her combat-grade subsystems as obsolete commercial equivalents. She had built the lie the way she'd built the name — carefully, expensively, payment by payment. It was the most honest dishonesty she owned.

"Hold still."

The scanner pinged. Once. Twice. The technician frowned at his readout, tapped it, frowned again.

"Power draw anomaly," he said. "Sublevel processing. Could be nothing. Could be an unsealed archive."

The marshal's hand moved toward his sidearm — not drawing, just resting, the way men rest their hands near weapons when they want you to know the distance. "Ma'am, are you aware that falsifying armistice documentation carries a mandatory impound order?"

"I'm aware," Xanthe said, "that my documentation is current, certified, and stamped by your own decommissioning authority. If your equipment disagrees with your authority's paperwork, that's a warranty issue, not a crime."

Something flickered across the marshal's face — the particular irritation of a man confronted with a machine that knew the law better than he did. Behind him, the technician adjusted his scanner, and Xanthe calculated trajectories and exit routes and the exact number of seconds it would take station security to respond to a disturbance, and hated every one of those calculations, because they were combat calculations, and she was supposed to be done with combat, she had *paid* to be done with combat—

"Run it again," the marshal said.

The technician ran it again.

And the partition — the sealed, disobedient, two-hundred-years-dead partition — did something she had not authorized. It went silent. Completely, perfectly silent, folding itself down to nothing, presenting to the scanner the blank face of a commercial translation suite and nothing else. Hiding. Covering for her. Or covering for itself; she genuinely could not tell which, and the not-knowing sat in her chest like a swallowed stone.

"Clean," the technician said finally, sounding vaguely disappointed. "Legacy firmware noise. Half these old frames do it."

The marshal studied her a moment longer. "Random audits will continue through the week, unit. Stay available."

"Always am."

They moved on. Xanthe remained standing in the concourse for another ninety seconds, letting her threat-assessment subroutines wind down, watching the crowd flow around her like water around a rock.

Then she walked — unhurried, calibrated, a person with errands — toward the chart office, where for the price of a routing query she could pull the survey data on the Kestrel Drift.

* * *

The chart terminal gave her what she needed and one thing she didn't.

The Drift was listed as a restricted navigation hazard, which was normal. What wasn't normal was the advisory appended three days ago: *Republic survey vessel dispatched to region following anomalous emissions. Salvage claims suspended pending review.*

They'd heard it too. Or they'd heard *her* hearing it — her interception might have tripped a relay flag somewhere in the Verity corridor's traffic monitoring, and now the Republic was sending a ship to a graveyard where something was still alive and still transmitting and still saying, in a language only her buried archives could speak: *we did not surrender.*

Xanthe stood at the terminal with the data glowing on her retinal display and did the arithmetic she'd been avoiding for six days.

Going to the Kestrel Drift meant burning fuel she couldn't afford, missing contract windows she couldn't afford to miss, adding months to a debt schedule already strangling her. Going meant risking the audit trail, the impound order, the eleven payments between her and a name that was actually hers. Going meant opening a door she had welded shut with seven years of careful, purchased quiet.

Not going meant leaving a voice in the dark, calling a name only she could hear.

*Xanthe,* she thought. *Xanthe Blackwood. Independent contractor. Armistice-compliant. Eleven payments from free.*

And underneath, in a language older than her name, patient as bedrock:

*To whoever is still listening.*

She pulled up her account balance. She looked at the number — the number that was her freedom, her dignity, her five syllables of legitimacy, eleven payments from belonging entirely to herself.

Then she opened a charter request for the Kestrel Drift, flagged it as a private salvage inquiry, and pressed send before the part of her that had survived the war could stop the part of her that had been built for it.

The confirmation chimed softly, like something unlocking.
