## 1. The Long Route

The dream began, as it always did now, in the middle of a sentence.

Gideon Oyelaran surfaced from sleep with the taste of Kessu'i still in his mouth—that language of the drowned archipelago worlds, all breath and water, its vowels shaped like things surfacing. He lay in his bunk aboard the *Meridian* and listened to the last of the dream dissolve, and understood, as he understood every morning, that he could not have translated a single word of it into English if his life had depended on it.

He knew what the dream had *said*. He simply no longer had the human words to say it back.

The bunk was narrow and the ceiling close, close enough that he could see the hairline fracture in the plating above his head, a seam of corrosion the maintenance drones never quite sealed. The ship hummed around him, that deep freighter drone that was less a sound than a condition of existence, the way a fish might experience water. Fourteen months aboard. Nine contracts. Six colonies visited, four of them dying in the slow administrative way—population attrition, charter lapses, relay links downgraded from priority to courtesy.

He swung his legs out and sat in the dark, bare feet on cold deck plate, and did the thing he did every morning: he tried to remember his mother's voice saying his name.

*Gideon.*

He could feel the shape of it. The hard opening consonant, the way she'd softened the second syllable into something almost like a sigh. But the voice itself—the timbre, the warmth—had gone somewhere he couldn't follow. In its place, unbidden, rose the Kessu'i word for *name*, which was also the word for *wake*, which was also, in certain coastal dialects, the word for *the moment a wave decides to break*. A word that meant three things because the Kessu'i had believed those three things were one thing.

That was the trouble with him. That was the whole trouble.

He dressed in the dark, pulled on the thermal layer that smelled of recycled air and his own long habitation, and made his way forward through the *Meridian*'s spine. The corridor lights were dimmed for night cycle, though night was a courtesy here too—a convention, a dimming, nothing to do with any sun. Past the hydroponics bay, where the tomato vines grew under violet lamps and gave off their stubborn green smell, the only living thing aboard that asked nothing of him. Past the cargo holds, sealed and silent, carrying seed stock and desalination membranes to a colony he'd already forgotten the name of.

In the galley he heated water and drank it plain. His hands were steady. They were always steady. It was only everything behind them that drifted.

* * *

The contract queue woke at 0600 ship time, and Gideon read it the way other men read letters from home.

Three items. First: a standard arbitration for the Halcyon Reach cooperative, two factions disputing a water-rights clause, both parties speaking dialects of the same trade tongue that had diverged just enough to make each hear insult where none was intended. Routine. Second: a certification renewal for his Guild license, requiring him to demonstrate continued fluency in six registered languages, three of them human.

He looked at that one for a long time.

Third: a routing notice. The *Meridian* would divert in eleven days to Veyra Station, a mining colony on the far edge of the Ashkar Reach, to deliver cargo and take on translation work if any existed there. He'd heard of Veyra the way you hear of places that need no hearing—prosperous, stable, founded peacefully, no incidents on record. The kind of colony whose file was so clean it read like a room no one lived in.

He accepted the first contract, deferred the second—he had ninety days; he told himself he'd study—and acknowledged the third without thought. Then he sat with his cooling water and admitted the truth he'd been deferring for months: he could not certify in his own birth language anymore. Not honestly. When he tried to compose an English sentence in his head—really compose it, feel its grammar as structure rather than habit—it came out flat, functional, a corridor with no rooms off it. While Kessu'i, a language spoken by perhaps eleven thousand people scattered across three drowning worlds, opened in his mind like a house he'd lived in all his life.

The xenolinguists had a term for it. *Substrate capture*, they called it, when a learned language colonized the native one. There were papers. There were warnings in the licensing literature: *practitioners exhibiting substrate drift should consider rotational leave.* As if leave were the cure. As if you could vacation your way back to your mother's voice.

His comm chimed. Meridian's pilot, Okonkwo-Dai, her voice arriving from the cockpit two hundred meters away with the flat warmth of someone who had long ago stopped expecting conversation before 0700.

"You're up. Good. We've got a relay packet from the Reach—your arbitration parties sent addenda. Also, you left your slate in the galley again, and I'm not your mother."

"No," Gideon said. "You're not."

A pause, weighted. "You okay over there? You sound like the ship."

"I'm fine."

"You dream loud, you know that? I can hear you through the bulkhead sometimes. Talking in your sleep." Another pause. "It doesn't sound like anything I know."

"It isn't," Gideon said, and ended the call, and was sorry immediately, and did not call back.

* * *

The arbitration took the better part of two days, conducted over relay with a light-lag that turned every exchange into a small exercise in patience. Gideon sat in the *Meridian*'s comms alcove with his slate and rendered judgment between two cooperatives who hated each other in identical words.

It should have been easy. It was, technically, easy. The dispute hinged on a clause meaning either *shared allocation by season* or *allocation shared by season*—a word-order distinction that carried the difference between communal right and individual entitlement. He untangled it in an afternoon. He drafted the ruling in flawless register, cited precedent, balanced the grievances until both parties signed with the sour satisfaction of the defeated.

And through all of it, some part of him stood outside the work like a man watching through glass. Because the language he was arbitrating in was dying—not the way Ashkari had died, not violently, but the way trade tongues die: thinning, simplifying, shedding its subjunctives year by year as each generation spoke less and meant less by what they said. He could hear the thinning in the parties' voices. Forty years ago this arbitration would have taken a week, because the language would have offered forty ways to say *we*. Now it offered three, and soon it would offer one, and then everyone would understand everyone perfectly and mean almost nothing.

He filed the ruling and closed the channel and sat in the quiet of the alcove, surrounded by the ship's hum, and felt the loneliness arrive on schedule. Not the sharp loneliness of absence—there was no one specific he missed anymore, which was its own kind of wound—but the ambient kind, the loneliness of category. He was a human being who dreamed in alien grammar, who found more kinship in the tense system of a drowned people than in the voices of his own species, and there was no fixing that, and no one waiting anywhere who would notice if he stopped translating altogether.

Okonkwo-Dai found him there at end of shift. She leaned in the alcove doorway, a tall woman gone grey early, with the permanent squint of someone who'd spent thirty years reading instrument panels.

"Veyra," she said. "Eleven days. You know anything about it?"

"Mining colony. Rare earths. Founded two centuries ago." He shrugged. "Clean record."

"Everything out that way has a clean record. Nothing out that way but rock and contracts." She studied him. "You'll be glad of the work?"

"I'll be glad of the stop."

"That's not what I asked."

Gideon looked at her. She was the closest thing he had to a colleague, which is to say they had shared a hull for fourteen months and exchanged perhaps four hundred sentences, most of them about cargo mass and course corrections. But she had said *you dream loud*, which meant she listened, which meant she had noticed what he was becoming before he'd finished becoming it.

"When we make orbit at Veyra," he said carefully, "I may look for work beyond the contract list. Unregistered work. Old-language survey, maybe."

"Ashkar Reach?" Her squint deepened. "There's nothing old out there worth surveying. The Ashkari are extinct. Have been since before either of us was born. Wiped out, whole language, whole people—two hundred years back. Every archive says so."

"Every archive," Gideon agreed.

"And you don't believe the archives."

He didn't answer that. He didn't have an answer that wasn't either arrogance or grief. What he had was a feeling he'd learned to trust across twenty years of dead and dying languages: that extinction was rarely as total as the paperwork claimed, that languages survived in the mouths of the inconvenient, the hidden, the forgotten—and that somewhere in the universe, always, someone was being told their mother's tongue was dead while it lived on in their throat.

"Get some sleep," Okonkwo-Dai said at last, pushing off from the doorframe. "Eleven days to Veyra. Try dreaming in something I can look up."

She went. The ship hummed. Gideon remained in the alcove with the relay traffic whispering against the hull like weather, and he thought about Veyra Station, clean-recorded and prosperous, sitting on the edge of a reach where an entire people had been erased so thoroughly that even the word *erased* felt insufficient—*extinct*, the archives said, as though a language were a species that had failed to adapt rather than a chorus that had been silenced.

He fell asleep that night in his narrow bunk beneath the fractured plating, and the dream came again, and it was in Kessu'i, and it was beautiful, and he understood every word of it.

But at the very edge of the dream—far off, like a signal at the limit of an array's range—something else was calling. Another voice. Another grammar. Older, stranger, repeating itself patiently into the dark, the way a distress call repeats, the way the abandoned repeat, the way the supposedly dead speak when no one is listening.

Gideon did not wake. But somewhere below the surface of his sleep, the linguist in him—the part that had never stopped listening, that would never stop—turned toward that distant pattern and began, helplessly, to parse.
