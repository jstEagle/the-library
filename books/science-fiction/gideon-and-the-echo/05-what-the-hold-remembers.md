## 5. What the Hold Remembers

Chief Engineer Adaeze Okafor had a theory about memory, and the theory was this: memory was like plumbing. You didn't think about it until it backed up.

Hers was backing up spectacularly.

She stood in the Erebus's engineering bay at 0400 station time, holding a torque wrench she did not remember picking up, staring at a coolant coupling she had apparently already fixed. The work was done. It was good work—her work, unmistakably hers, right down to the slightly over-torqued third bolt that she always did because her hands had learned the pattern before her pride caught up. But she had no memory of doing it. The last thing she clearly remembered was going to sleep four hours ago, and before that—

Before that was the problem. Before that was a smear.

"Okay," she said to the empty bay, in the tone she used on recalcitrant reactors. "Let's try that again."

She closed her eyes and walked backward through her own evening. Dinner in the mess. She could see it: reconstituted jollof rice, Voss complaining about it, Kwan laughing so hard he knocked over his water. Except—the jollof rice had been three weeks ago. Last night's dinner had been noodles, and Voss hadn't been there, because Voss was still in medical being monitored for a heart condition he wouldn't develop for another nine years.

That was the thing nobody on this ship could stop tripping over. The tense of it all. Everyone aboard the Erebus was living in a grammatical error.

Her comm chimed. She flinched, then laughed at herself—a forty-one-year-old woman, or possibly a thirty-two-year-old woman, startled by a doorbell.

"Okafor."

"It's Ines." The investigator's voice, dry as vacuum-packed crackers. "You said I could come by when I couldn't sleep. It's—" A pause. "I'm aware of how that sounds."

"It sounds like you can't sleep," Okafor said, "and you remembered I offered, which puts you ahead of me. Come down. Mind the third step; it's not broken yet, but it's ambitious."

* * *

Ines Halloran arrived looking like a woman who had been arguing with paperwork and losing. She carried two cups of tea from the station vendors, which was either bribery or kindness, and Okafor had decided weeks ago that with Halloran it was both.

"You look terrible," Okafor said, accepting her cup.

"Thank you. I've been cultivating it." Ines settled onto an upturned cargo crate, perching with the careful posture of someone who had spent twelve years sitting in places that weren't chairs. "How are the memories?"

"Out of order." Okafor leaned against the drive housing, feeling its warmth through her coveralls. The drive was cold, technically—it wasn't running—but it held heat the way certain people held grudges. "It's not like forgetting. Forgetting would be a mercy. This is more like... somebody shuffled my deck. I grieved my mother's death this morning. Properly grieved it, ugly crying, the whole production. Then I checked the date and realized she's alive, back on Lagos Station, and according to my service record I haven't even gotten the call yet." She shrugged. "So now I'm grieving something that hasn't happened, and dreading the relief I'll feel when it doesn't."

Ines was quiet for a moment. "Does anyone else have it that bad?"

"We all have it different. That's what's interesting. Or horrifying. Interesting-horrifying." Okafor ticked off fingers. "Captain remembers his daughter—his daughter, Ines, a whole person, with a name and a laugh and a scar from falling off a bike—and the man hasn't conceived her yet. Kwan keeps remembering conversations we haven't had. Half the crew dreams in second person. And me, I get..." She hesitated.

"Get what?"

"I get the hold." Okafor turned to look at the great sealed bulkhead at the aft end of engineering, forty tons of nothing where forty tons of ore should have been. "I remember the hold. Not what was in it—I remember the hold *remembering*. Which is insane. Holds don't remember. Holds are big dumb boxes."

"What does a box remembering feel like?"

Okafor opened her mouth to say *like nothing*, and then stopped, because that wasn't true, and hadn't been true for some time, and she was tired of lying to herself in her own head, which was supposed to be the one rent-free apartment she controlled.

"It feels like hunger," she said. "A patient kind. Like the hold is a stomach and something in it is still digesting."

* * *

The memories came for her properly at 0630, while she was alone, which was how they preferred to operate.

She was running diagnostics on the jump array when the deck seemed to tilt—not physically, the grav plating was fine—but inside, the way a dream tilts when it changes its mind. And then she was there.

*Deep space. No beacon, no chart line, nothing but the long dark between systems where honest ships don't go. The Erebus drifting on thrusters around a derelict—or not a derelict, not exactly, because derelicts are dead and this thing was *waiting*, the way a trap waits, the way a word waits on the tip of your tongue.*

*It hung in the void like a knot in black silk. Not large. Maybe two meters, maybe less, its surface neither metal nor stone nor anything her eyes wanted to file. The salvage crew had found it by accident, a mass reading where no mass should be, and Captain Reyes—who was younger then, or older, the tenses were useless—had ordered it aboard against the advice of everyone who would later pretend they'd agreed.*

*"Careful," someone said, and it might have been her.*

*They'd brought it into the hold. Forty tons of displacement registered on every sensor, though the object itself weighed almost nothing. And the moment the outer doors closed behind it, Okafor remembered—remembered *now*, standing at her console six months after the fact that hadn't happened yet—the hold went quiet in a new way. Not empty-quiet. Attentive-quiet.*

*Then they jumped.*

*And the jump was wrong. She knew jumps the way she knew her own heartbeat, and this one was wrong in her bones before the instruments screamed. Space didn't tear so much as* fold *—the ship passing through its own wake, through its own timeline, a needle driven back through the fabric it had just sewn. She saw the Erebus from outside the Erebus. She saw the crew as a constellation of moments, all of them lit at once: birth, death, dinner, grief, the launch, the return, everything stacked like plates.*

*And the thing in the hold drank.*

*That was the only word. It drank the timeline the way a drain drinks water—all of it, theirs, the years they'd lived and the years they'd been scheduled to live, pulled backward through them, leaving them younger and wrong and full of holes. Thirty people aging in reverse while their futures poured out of them into a hungry knot of nothing.*

*She felt it take her own. Felt twenty years of tomorrow lift out of her chest like heat off a stove. And in the last instant before the memory ended—instant, decade, she couldn't say—she understood the artifact completely, the way you understand a fall once you're already falling:*

*It didn't store memory. Storage was for keeping. This kept nothing. It devoured, and what it devoured was gone, and the folding of time was just the sound of it chewing.*

Okafor came back to herself kneeling on the deck, both palms flat against the drive housing, her breath coming in ragged pulls. Her nose was bleeding. She wiped it with the back of her wrist and looked at the blood without surprise.

"Huh," she said, to the empty bay. "So that's what happened."

* * *

Ines found her there an hour later, still sitting on the deck, drinking tea she'd made and half-forgotten twice.

"You look worse than I do," Ines said. "I'm almost jealous."

"Ines." Okafor's voice came out steady, which surprised her. "Sit down. I need to tell you what's in the hold's space, and I need you to not write it down, because if you write it down, it becomes evidence, and evidence is the one thing those two fleets out there will use to burn us all."

Ines sat. She didn't reach for her tablet. Okafor noted that, filed it away in whatever drawer of her mind was still organized, and told her. All of it. The derelict-that-waited. The fold. The drinking.

When she finished, Ines was very still. "The Echo," she said quietly. "Both fleets are calling it the Echo."

"They'd know that name how?"

"That's what I intend to find out." Ines stared at the sealed bulkhead, and Okafor watched her do the arithmetic—the same arithmetic Okafor had done, arriving at the same terrible sum. If the artifact ate memory, then every record, every log, every testimony aboard this ship was suspect. Not forged. *Digested.* The truth wasn't hidden. The truth had been eaten, and the two fleets blockading Meridian were circling each other over the crumbs.

"There's something else," Okafor said. "Something I probably shouldn't tell you, because telling you means you carry it, and I've seen what carrying does."

"Tell me anyway."

Okafor took a breath. "The fold isn't finished. The drive's still resonating—I can feel it in the housing, a harmonic that shouldn't exist. Whatever tore open out there is still stitched to this ship. And here's the part that's going to keep you up nights: the resonance is getting stronger as the next window approaches. Almost like it's *hungry* again." She met Ines's eyes. "Almost like it remembers us."

Ines stood slowly, setting down her untouched tea. "I have to go make some phone calls I'll regret."

"Story of my life lately," Okafor said. "Only mine haven't happened yet."
