## 4. Records That Agree Too Well

The Meridian Station archive was housed in a tower on Ceres that had once been a grain silo, back when Ceres still grew things. Now it held something Paloma considered far more perishable: the past.

He came in through the public reading room, where the light was the color of weak tea and the air smelled of ozone and old polymer. He'd dressed down for it — dockworker's jacket, forged transit chit, the face of a man with nothing to lose but an afternoon. In his pocket, wrapped in a scrap of thermal cloth, sat the canister he'd copied from the boy's hands. The original stayed hidden in his smuggling cache, triple-sealed, cold as a heart that had stopped. The copy was degraded — smuggler's duplication always was, a little blur at the edges of every memory, like a photograph left too long in the sun — but it was enough. Enough to know what he was looking for.

Meridian Station. Decommissioned peacefully, forty years ago. Population relocated without incident. That was what every record said. That was what *he* half-remembered believing.

He took a terminal near the back wall, under a window that looked out onto the black curve of the belt, and began to search.

* * *

The first record was a decommissioning order, stamped with the seal of the Outer Systems Authority. Clean typography. Standard boilerplate. *Meridian Station, population 11,402, scheduled for orderly closure following economic review. All residents granted relocation assistance. No incidents reported.*

Paloma read it twice. Then he pulled the second document: the relocation completion report, filed eleven months later. *All 11,402 residents successfully resettled across four colonies. Satisfaction surveys attached. No incidents reported.*

Then the third: the structural demolition permit. The fourth: the final energy audit showing the station's reactors powered down in sequence, gently, like a body going to sleep. The fifth: a news digest from the period, three paragraphs long, noting the closure as a footnote between a grain futures report and an advertisement for lunar real estate.

He ran them against each other. Dates aligned. Names aligned. Figures matched to the decimal — 11,402 appearing in every document like a refrain, never once rounded, never once disputed, never once accompanied by so much as a single dissenting voice, a single appeal, a single complaint filed by a single resident who hadn't wanted to leave their home.

Eleven thousand four hundred and two people, and not one of them had ever said a word about it again. Not one memoir. Not one lawsuit. Not one obituary mentioning *born on Meridian Station*. Not one grandchild's genealogy query in any public forum across forty years.

Paloma had spent twenty years moving other people's memories through customs. He knew what authentic records felt like. They were messy. They contradicted themselves. A man's birthdate shifted between two filings; a widow spelled her own name wrong on a claim form; a shipping manifest disagreed with its own cargo list because someone had been tired, or bribed, or human. Authenticity had a texture, and the texture was friction.

These documents had no texture at all. They slid against each other like oiled glass. Every source agreed with every other source the way mirrors agree with mirrors — perfectly, infinitely, and with nothing behind any of them.

He sat back in the creaking chair and felt the vertigo open beneath him, slow and vast, like a crack spreading through ice underfoot.

*It wasn't just the massacre they erased,* he thought. *They erased the erasure. They went back and sanded down the grain of the world.*

And then, quieter, in a voice that didn't feel like his own: *How much of me did they sand down too?*

His documented past sat in the archive like everyone else's — birth registration, school records, work permits, all of it smooth, all of it consistent, all of it agreeing with itself a little too well. He pulled his own file and read it like a stranger's. *Paloma Yarrow, born Tharsis Habitat, mother listed as Vess Yarrow, father unrecorded.* He searched for Vess Yarrow and found her death certificate, dated thirty years ago, cause of respiratory failure, next of kin notified: none. No hospital admission record preceding it. No burial permit. Just a certificate, floating alone in the archive, agreeing with nothing because there was nothing else there for it to agree with.

A single thread with no weave around it. He stared at it for a long time.

* * *

"You've been on that terminal for six hours."

The voice came from behind him, dry as paper. Paloma turned. An archivist stood between the stacks — a narrow woman of indeterminate age, gray-streaked hair cut short, wearing the dust-colored smock of the permanent staff. Her name tag read SEF in faded block letters. She held a data-slate against her chest the way a doorkeeper holds a bar.

"Just doing some family research," Paloma said.

"Family research." She looked at his terminal, and though she was too far away to read it, he had the distinct sensation that she could. "Six hours of family research on Meridian Station."

"The station my mother came from."

"Your mother came from Tharsis Habitat," Sef said. "According to your file. Which you've also been reading." She crossed the reading room without hurrying and stopped beside his chair, close enough that he could smell the archive on her — dust and coolant and something faintly floral, preservative chemicals maybe, the smell of things being kept from rotting. "You want to know what I do all day, smuggler?"

The word landed like a needle. Paloma kept his face still. "I don't know what you mean."

"I mean I know what comes into this building and what goes out. I've worked here nineteen years. And in nineteen years I have learned one thing better than anything else in this whole rotten system." She set the slate down on his terminal's edge, deliberate as a woman laying down a card. "I have learned which questions get answered fast, and which ones make the lights flicker."

"The lights flicker?"

"When someone asks about Meridian Station, somewhere in this building, a maintenance request gets filed within the hour. Climate recalibration. Stack rotation. Something harmless. And the person who asked finds their access quietly reviewed, their transit history quietly examined, their life quietly weighed." She looked at him with eyes the color of dishwater, and there was no fear in them, only a terrible tired patience. "Yours is already filed. Request number 88-4417. You have maybe two hours before someone reads it and decides what you are."

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because you're the fourth person in nineteen years to ask." She picked up her slate again. "The other three don't work here anymore. Two of them don't exist anymore — I checked, after. Their files dissolved. Smooth as everything else." She paused at the end of the stack aisle, half-turned, a gray figure in gray light. "Whatever you think happened out there, whatever you found, whatever proof you're carrying in that jacket pocket — the record isn't missing, friend. The record was *written*. There's a difference between a hole in a wall and a wall that was built over a hole. This is the second kind. And nobody tears down walls here. They only build them higher."

"Then help me look behind it," Paloma said. "You've been here nineteen years. You must have—"

"I must have nothing." Her voice didn't rise, but something in it hardened, like water going to ice. "I file documents. I keep the lights on in a building full of beautiful, perfect lies, and I go home, and I sleep, because the alternative is to stop sleeping forever. You want my advice? Stop asking. Take your family research and go home. Some doors are load-bearing."

She walked away into the stacks, and the tea-colored light seemed to close behind her like water.

* * *

But Paloma had spent his whole life around sealed things, and he knew a secret about seals: they were always strongest exactly where someone most needed them to be.

He didn't leave. He waited out the two hours in the reading room's washroom, listening to the ventilation, watching the door, while somewhere above him request 88-4417 made its slow way through whatever machinery decided what people were. Then, when the shift changed and the reading room emptied to three snoring drifters and a student crying softly over exam files, he went back to the terminal and did the thing he should have done first.

He stopped searching for Meridian Station.

Instead he searched for the gaps. He queried the archive for documents *adjacent* to the story — supply manifests for the relocation fleet, port logs at the four destination colonies, medical intake records for eleven thousand refugees. If the story was true, the edges would be ragged. Real events shed debris the way comets shed tails.

The port logs agreed perfectly. The medical intakes agreed perfectly. Eleven thousand four hundred and two people had arrived at four colonies, been processed, been housed, been absorbed — and then, in every single record, on every single world, they simply stopped appearing. No marriages. No property purchases. No school enrollments for their children. Eleven thousand lives that arrived like rain and left no puddles.

Except one.

Deep in a colony medical database, buried under forty years of routine entries, Paloma found a single anomaly: a vaccination record. Batch notation, date, location — a clinic on Halcyon Reach, eleven months after the decommissioning. Patient ID redacted. Age: nine years old. Origin line, typed in a clerk's hurried hand before some standardization protocol had smoothed the rest of the system flat:

*Refugee — Meridian Stn — witness statement pending.*

Witness statement pending.

Not *relocated resident*. Not *no incidents reported*. *Witness.* A word that belonged to a different story entirely, a story with blood in it, and some tired clerk had let it slip into the record like a thread pulled loose from a hem — and for forty years, no one had pulled it back.

Paloma copied the record to a sliver of smuggler's storage no bigger than a fingernail, sealed it against his skin beneath the thermal cloth, and killed the terminal.

When he looked up, Sef was standing at the end of the aisle again. He hadn't heard her come. She was holding out something small and dark — a physical key, old-style, brass-colored, warm from her pocket.

"Sub-basement three," she said quietly. "Nineteen years ago I processed the physical media transfer from the Meridian incident team. Before the digital consolidation. Before the smoothing." Her dishwater eyes held his. "I told myself I filed it all. I told myself that for nineteen years. I'm done telling myself things."

She pressed the key into his palm and closed his fingers over it.

"They'll have flagged your exit by now. There's a freight lift on the north side the cameras don't love. And Paloma—" she used his name, though he'd never given it to her, and smiled without any warmth at all, "—whatever's down there, once you see it, you're either going to wish you hadn't, or you're going to wish you'd seen it sooner. With people like us, it's always both."

Above them, faint through the tower's bones, Paloma heard the great transmission loop of Earth turn over another cycle — the same voices, the same music, the same goodbye, repeating toward silence. Somewhere in that loop, he knew now, was a version of the world that had never existed, broadcast so long and so beautifully that it had replaced the real one.

He closed his hand around the brass key and went to find the hole behind the wall.
