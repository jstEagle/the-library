## 5. The Beacon's First Voice

The beacon hung at the end of its tether like a lantern someone had forgotten to blow out.

Paloma approached it the way he approached everything he loved: slowly, with his hands visible. Old habit from the smuggling trade — show the cargo you mean no harm — though this was no cargo, and it had never once harmed him. It was a spire of dull alloy and solar skin, forty meters of patient machinery bolted to a rock that had never been named, orbiting in the long shadow of a gravity well nobody bothered to chart. Officially, it did not exist. Unofficially, it had been singing to him for eleven years.

He docked his skiff at the maintenance collar and felt the familiar shudder as the clamps took hold. Inside the skiff, the boy slept in the false-bottomed crate where Paloma had hidden him through customs, curled around the canister like it was a warm animal. He hadn't woken for the transit. Children slept like the dead, or like people who had already died once and saw no reason to fear a second time.

Paloma sealed the skiff behind him and drifted up the beacon's spine, hand over hand, into the dark.

* * *

The interior was cold in the way of places built to outlast their builders. His breath fogged. The emergency lights came on in sequence as he passed, amber and forgiving, each one flickering awake like an old dog hearing footsteps. He knew every meter of this shaft. He had replaced the coolant lines himself, soldered the antenna couplings, re-wound the power bus with wire he'd bought under three different names. Eleven years of small devotions. He had told himself it was sentiment. A smuggler needed one honest thing in his life, even if it was a machine that answered to no registry, no owner, no flag.

He reached the core chamber and clipped his boots to the deck. The main console woke at his presence, the way it always did, screens blooming soft green. Maintenance interface. Diagnostics. All the humble tools he'd used a thousand times.

But tonight the console was already awake when he arrived.

He stood very still. The green light lay across his gloves, across the scar on his knuckles where a cargo clamp had caught him in '61. On the central screen, a single line of text pulsed, patient as a heartbeat:

**BIOMETRIC MATCH: 99.7%. CONFIRM IDENTITY?**

"I didn't touch anything," he said aloud, and heard how thin his voice sounded in all that cold air.

The cursor blinked. Waiting.

His hands hovered over the panel without his permission. He thought about the boy in the crate below, sleeping with a massacre cupped in his palms. He thought about the archives, all those records agreeing too perfectly, Sef's face going gray as she said *stop asking, Paloma, please, just stop asking.* He thought about how the beacon had never once asked him for credentials in eleven years, and how tonight — the night after he'd held a canister containing his own childhood face — it wanted confirmation.

As if something inside it had been waiting for the rest of him to catch up.

He pressed his palm to the reader.

* * *

The chamber changed around him.

Not physically — the walls stayed walls, the cold stayed cold — but the light shifted, green deepening to a color he had no name for, and every screen in the room filled at once with text that scrolled too fast to read, then stopped, then resolved into a single window. An audio file. Timestamped forty-one years ago.

The date meant nothing to him. That was the first vertigo. He was fifty-three years old; forty-one years ago he would have been twelve, a station brat somewhere in the inner system, and yet the file header carried his own name. Not *Yarrow*. Another name — a longer one, older, with syllables that tasted like a language he'd half-forgotten his grandmother speaking. But the metadata field labeled AUTHOR read, in plain characters:

**PALOMA YARROW.**

"That's not possible," he said. "I'm not— I haven't been—"

The file played anyway. Machines are patient with grief.

*"If you're hearing this,"* said a voice, *"then you finally put your hand on the glass."*

It was his voice. Not the voice he heard in recordings now — roughened by decades of recycled air and cheap liquor and lies told smoothly — but his voice underneath all that, the one he heard in his own skull. Every rasp and hesitation exactly where he kept them.

*"I know you won't believe me. I wouldn't have. I'm going to say it plainly and let you hate it: I am you. Or I was, before they took the years out of me. Before I paid a surgeon in unmarked credits to cut Meridian Station out of my head and leave a smooth wall where it used to be. You've been living in front of that wall your whole life, Paloma. You just never knocked on it."*

Paloma's knees found the deck. He didn't remember sitting down.

*"You built this beacon,"* the voice continued. *"Six months after Meridian. After the airlock. After what you saw and what you did and what was done in front of you while you stood there holding a wrench like it could save anyone. You built it because the record was going to be erased — because it was already being erased, the bodies burned, the manifests rewritten, the survivors scattered and edited. Everyone who remembered was made not to remember. Except us."*

A pause in the recording. He could hear breathing on the other end of forty-one years. His own breathing, younger, ragged.

*"They left me alive because a dead witness becomes a martyr, and martyrs get investigated. An edited witness becomes nothing. So I became nothing. I became a smuggler with a clean face and a fake past, and I maintained this beacon for decades, waiting."*

*"Waiting for what?" Paloma whispered at the console. "Waiting for what?"*

*"For the loop,"* the voice said, as if it heard him. *"Earth's transmission loop dies soon. You've seen the decay curves; you maintain the relays, you know what's coming. When it goes, every archive in the system goes with it, and the last copies of the official story die in the dark — and so does any chance of correction. Unless something outside the loop is already carrying the truth. This beacon is outside the loop. It always has been. That's why no registry claims it. That's why you could never find its commissioning records. There aren't any. I erased them myself. A dead man's switch doesn't file paperwork."*

The cold of the deck climbed through his suit into his bones. He let it. It gave him something true to hold.

*"And then there's the boy."*

Paloma looked up.

*"You've met him by now. You'll know him the way I knew him — instantly, horribly, completely. He's you. The version of you that lived through Meridian at twelve years old, thrown forward through whatever the Cassiopeia fell into, aged backward, carrying the canister we sealed with our own hands. Don't ask me how the physics works. I watched the same impossible thing happen that you did, and I've had forty more years to fail to understand it. What matters is this: he is the only other copy of the truth in existence. The canister is the first. He is the second. And you—"*

The voice stopped. In the silence, Paloma realized he was crying, and that he couldn't remember starting.

*"You are the third. Whatever they left in you, whatever smooth wall they built — underneath it, you remember. I made sure of that before the surgery. I buried the memory deeper than their tools could reach, wired into the oldest part of you, the part that knows how to keep your hands steady when the air goes bad. You won't be able to see it. But you'll feel it. That hairline crack of unease you've carried your whole life — the sense that you're a stranger in your own history? That's not damage, Paloma. That's the load-bearing beam."*

*"Protect him,"* the voice said, and now it was quieter, almost gentle, the way Paloma spoke to frightened clients in the dark of a cargo hold. *"Protect the boy until the loop ends. Feed him, hide him, teach him to be invisible — you're good at that, you've had practice. When the blackout comes, take him here. Broadcast the canister through this beacon on every frequency that will still carry. Let the colonies hear what happened at Meridian before the silence closes over it. They'll call it a ghost story. Doesn't matter. Truth doesn't need to be believed to survive. It only needs to be transmitted."*

*"One more thing."* The breath again, longer this time. *"There's a cost. There's always a cost. The beacon runs on identity — literal identity, biometric signature, the full cryptographic weight of who you are. To broadcast at that power, outside the loop, far enough to reach the outer settlements... it will burn your records as fuel. Every registry entry, every biometric key, every document that says Paloma Yarrow exists. By the time the signal clears the belt, there will be no record anywhere that you were ever born."*

A sound that might have been a laugh, worn down to its threads.

*"I know. I've had four decades to think about whether I could do it. I still don't know. Maybe you're braver than I was. Maybe you're the same coward, one step further down the road. Either way — I chose this for you before you could choose it yourself, and I'm sorry. That's the inheritance. Nobody asks to inherit anything. You just find yourself holding it."*

*"The boy is asleep in your skiff right now. Go check on him. He gets scared when he wakes up in strange places. I remember."*

The recording ended. The screens dimmed to embers.

* * *

For a long time Paloma knelt on the cold deck of a machine he had built and could not remember building, in a body that had been edited by surgeons he could not remember hiring, listening to the hum of a dead man's switch that had been waiting for him the way a trap waits — patiently, lovingly, since before he knew there was anything to be caught.

*Chosen before he knew.* The phrase turned over in him like a stone in a current. Every year of his life rearranged itself around it. The loneliness that had fit him like a tailored coat — of course. The comfort with false names. The way customs scanners had always seemed to slide off him. The hairline crack. He had spent fifty-three years thinking he was a man with a hole in him, and he was a man with a wall in him, and someone had built the wall out of mercy or cowardice or both, and now the wall had a voice, and the voice was his.

He thought about refusing. He actually stood there and considered it, honestly, the way you consider jumping when you stand too near a ledge. He could walk away. He could seal the beacon, fly the boy somewhere far, raise him on forged papers in some backwater habitat, and let the loop die with the lie intact. The universe would not notice. The universe had never noticed Paloma Yarrow doing anything.

But he could hear the boy's breathing through two hulls and forty meters of shaft. Small, even, trusting nothing, trusting him anyway.

"Damn you," he said to the empty chamber, to the buried self, to the twelve-year-old with the wrench. "Damn you for deciding."

The console glowed faintly, neither agreeing nor disagreeing.

He unclipped his boots and went down the spine toward the skiff, hand over hand, faster than he'd come up. Through the crate's seam he could see the boy's face, slack with sleep, one hand still curled around the canister. Even unconscious, he guarded it. Even unconscious, he was the last witness.

Paloma crouched beside the crate and watched him breathe, and felt the strangest thing settle over him — not resolve, not yet, but its precursor: the recognition that he had been preparing for this moment his entire life without knowing it, the way a harbor prepares for a ship it was dredged to receive.

The boy's eyes opened. Gray, clear, forty years young. They found Paloma's face and did not flinch.

"You sound like me," the boy said.

"Yeah," Paloma said. His throat was tight. "I know."

Above them, faint through the hull, the beacon's carrier tone shifted — a new harmonic threading into the old one, a frequency Paloma had never heard it make in eleven years of tending. Almost like a throat clearing. Almost like a voice remembering how to speak.

And far away, past the curve of the planet, Earth's great transmission loop ticked into its final cycle, and began, everywhere at once, to count down.
