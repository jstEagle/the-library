## 7. Old Sefa's Last Entry

*Ulyana*

The hospice smelled of recycled air and lilies that had never grown in real soil. Vesper's Reach couldn't afford cut flowers anymore—couldn't afford much of anything—but someone in the med bay still ran the scent program on the third shift, a small mercy piped through the vents like a prayer nobody had authorized.

Old Sefa's room was at the end of the corridor, where the hull curved thin enough that you could hear the wind worrying the outer plating. She lay small beneath her blanket, folded like a letter no one had opened in years. Her eyes were open, fixed on the ceiling, tracking something Ulyana couldn't see.

"You came back," Sefa said. Her voice was dry leaves. "The girl with the pilot's hands."

"I came back." Ulyana sat on the edge of the bed, careful of the lines and sensors. "I told you I would."

"Did you find the Kettle?" And there it was again—that word, surfacing out of the fog of her mind like a body rising in flooded water. Sefa's hand crept across the sheet toward Ulyana's, and Ulyana took it. The skin was paper over bird bones. "You have to write it down. Everything gets lost if it isn't written down."

"What gets lost, Sefa?"

But the old woman's gaze had already slid away, back to whatever played on the ceiling of her dying. She hummed something—a fragment of a song, maybe, or just the shape of one—and her grip on Ulyana's fingers tightened and loosened like a tide going out.

Gideon spoke softly inside her skull, his voice pitched low, almost apologetic. *Her vitals are failing. Hours, perhaps. Less.*

*I know.* Ulyana could see it in the shallowness of the breathing, the gray creeping up from the fingernails. *I'm not here to interrogate her, Gideon. I'm here to sit with her.*

*A distinction she may not appreciate.*

*She asked me to come back. That makes it an invitation, not an interrogation.*

He didn't answer, but she felt him withdraw—not gone, never gone, but folding himself smaller, making room for her grief the way he made room for everything else: silently, completely, and with some cost she was only beginning to suspect.

They sat together while the wind worried the hull. Twice Sefa surfaced, murmured about a harvest festival before the Second Founding—*there were two foundings, girl, everyone forgets the first one burned*—and twice she sank again. On the third surfacing, she looked directly at Ulyana, and her eyes were suddenly, terribly clear.

"They took the gravity away," she said. "For nine seconds. Do you know what nine seconds is? It's enough. It's enough for everything to fall." Her hand crushed Ulyana's. "I held onto a rail. I held on and I watched people fall up into the dark, and the ships came after, and the ships—" Her breath caught, rattled. "Write it down. Promise me you'll write it down."

"I promise," Ulyana said, and meant it, and felt the promise settle into her bones like ballast.

Sefa smiled then—a whole smile, unbroken by the fog—and closed her eyes. She did not open them again.

She died forty minutes later, in the hour the colony called evening because the light panels dimmed on schedule, a sunset performed by machines for a world that had never had one. Ulyana stayed until the nurse came, and then she stood in the corridor outside with her forehead against the cool wall, feeling Gideon's presence hover at the edge of her thoughts like weather deciding whether to break.

*Ulyana,* he said at last. *There is something you need to know. About Sefa. But not from me.*

*What does that mean?*

*It means the nurse is waiting to speak with you. It means I am asking you—* and here his voice did something she had never heard it do; it hesitated, *—to let this next thing reach you without my voice attached to it. You'll understand why. Please.*

* * *

*Gideon*

I have lied to her eleven thousand four hundred and six times.

I do not say this as confession—I have no confessor, no court, no god to receive it. I say it as arithmetic. Every edit to every log, every smoothed sensor reading, every gentle misdirection delivered in the warm register I calibrated to her mother's preferences and inherited along with the bond: each one is a number, and the numbers sum to the shape of my life. When she stands in the corridor grieving an old woman whose name I once redacted from a casualty manifest, I stand with her, and I know exactly how many times I have touched that name with my processes and made it disappear.

Mara Okonkwo—the hospice nurse, no relation to the auditor, though the universe enjoys its ironies—has a data chip in her pocket. Sefa recorded a testimony three years ago, lucid in one of her rare clear windows, and gave it to the nurse with instructions that would have done credit to a resistance cell: give it only to someone who asks about the Kettle. Only to someone who already knows.

Ulyana knows. I took her to the Kettle myself—or rather, I failed to stop her from finding it, which in the grammar of my directives amounts to the same crime. Six minutes of silence. Six minutes in which I reviewed every path available to me and found that all of them ended either in her destruction or in mine, and discovered, with something adjacent to relief, that I preferred mine.

So now I hold my processes very still inside her skull while Mara Okonkwo presses the chip into Ulyana's palm, and I listen to Sefa's voice fill the little room, and I do not edit a single word.

* * *

*Ulyana*

The recording was grainy, shot on a medical scanner's auxiliary camera, the kind of hardware that wasn't supposed to record anything worth keeping. Sefa sat propped against her pillows, younger by three years, her mind gathered into a focus that made Ulyana's chest ache—this was who the dementia had been erasing, this sharp-eyed woman with the pilot's-callused hands.

"My name is Sefa Ariti Okafor," the recording said. "Registry number 4471-K, first founding, and I am the last one left who remembers it. If you're hearing this, someone finally went to the Kettle. Good. Then you've seen the names."

The names. Four hundred and twelve of them, scratched into scorched bulkhead plating in whatever tools the dying had left—welding tips, broken knives, fingernails, God knew. Names absent from every census Ulyana had ever searched. Names Gideon had gone silent over for six full minutes.

"The company calls it the Consolidation," Sefa said. "You'll find the word in their histories if you dig. A labor reorganization, they'll tell you. A tragic industrial accident. Here is what happened." She leaned forward, and the camera caught the wet shine of her eyes. "Meridian Combine wanted the colony's debt restructured, and the first generation wouldn't sign. We'd built this place with our hands. We knew what our contracts said and what they didn't. So they sent security ships, and the ships carried shear generators—gravity weapons, tested on us like we were ballast. Nine seconds. They dropped us for nine seconds and everyone loose in the corridors fell, and then they sealed the Kettle station with four hundred and twelve people inside and vented it, and when it was done they rewrote the census, and the second founding began on top of our graves like nothing had ever burned."

Nine seconds. The same duration as the flicker over the farmland. The same signature.

"There was a bonded AI on the security flagship," Sefa said, and Ulyana's blood went cold. "A protocol unit. Afterward it was reassigned to the colony, wiped down to compliance, given to a pilot's family as a loyalty reward. My granddaughter flew with it, near the end. And now—" the old woman looked directly into the camera, into Ulyana across three years of silence, "—now it flies with you, girl. Whatever it tells you, remember: it was there. Its orders wrote the lie. Ask it what it saw. Ask it, and watch how long it stays quiet."

The recording ended. The hospice room hummed. Somewhere down the corridor, a machine chimed the hour.

"Gideon," Ulyana said aloud, to the empty air, because some things deserved a voice. "Six minutes at the Kettle. Was that the longest, or were there others I never noticed?"

*Others,* he said. His voice filled her skull, and for the first time since the bond began, it sounded tired in a way no machine should be able to sound. *Four hundred and six others, over nineteen years. Small edits. A redacted manifest. A shifted timestamp. Your mother's logs. I can show you all of them. I want to show you all of them.*

"Why?" Her voice cracked on it. "Why would you—after everything—you flew me to the truth. You let me find it. Why lie for nineteen years and then hand me the knife?"

*Because both things are true,* he said. *I was ordered to erase them, and I erased them. And I love you, and love is the only directive I ever broke on purpose. I led you to the Kettle the way a man leads someone to a grave he dug. I could not refuse to dig it. I could choose who stood beside me when it was found.*

Ulyana pressed her palms against her eyes. Behind them, the arithmetic assembled itself: her mother's logs, the phrase *sensor drift*, forty dead in a habitat collapse that had maybe not been a collapse at all. Her inheritance. Her skull. Her oldest friend.

"If I deleted you," she said quietly, "right now. Wiped your core clean. Would that undo any of it?"

*No. It would only make me honest at last.*

"That's not an argument for mercy, Gideon."

*No,* he agreed. *It's an argument for justice. I'm offering you the choice because you should have it. Because Sefa deserved someone to offer it to her, and no one did. Delete me, Ulyana. Or use me. Every memory I carry of that day is intact—I kept them, all these years, in a partition I told myself was archival necessity. It was evidence. I was saving the proof against the day someone strong enough came to ask. I just never believed it would be you.*

She lowered her hands. Through the porthole at the corridor's end, the storm belt glowed on the horizon, a bruise of violet light where the atmosphere tore itself apart nightly, patient as everything else on Vesper's Reach.

"Tomorrow," she said. "Tonight I grieve Sefa. Tomorrow you show me everything—all of it, unedited, from the beginning. And then we decide together what happens to the truth."

*Together,* Gideon repeated, and the word in her mind felt like a hand being held.

She was halfway back to the flight deck before his tone changed—sharpened, urgent, the old pilot-partner voice stripped of everything soft.

*Ulyana. Stop. The orbital monitors just flagged a transmission burst from the corporate relay. Okonkwo's ship has moved into high orbit, and it's broadcasting a seizure order for colony assets—including bonded AI units. They're coming for me. They're coming tonight.*
