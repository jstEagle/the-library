## 9. Testimony

The relay hangs at the top of the sky like a nail driven into the dark.

I see it through the storm belt's last ragged curtain—Vesper Relay Seven, a spindle of dead metal and one living antenna, orbiting high enough that the colony below is just a bruise of amber light on a black field. My hands are shaking. Not from the descent. From what I'm about to do.

*Ulyana.* Gideon's voice in my skull is quieter than it has ever been. *I want you to know the math before we commit. Once I open my core to the transmitter, there is no throttle. The broadcast will draw everything I am to make itself coherent. Every memory I've protected. Every lie I told you. It all goes out at once, and then—*

"Then you're a lamp that's burned its wick," I say aloud, because saying it in my head makes it too easy to pretend I didn't.

*That is an accurate and deeply unkind metaphor.*

"I learned from someone with excellent diction."

The storm belt peels away beneath me and the *Kestrel* climbs into clean vacuum, engines screaming against nothing, and for eleven seconds there is only the sound of my own breathing and Gideon's presence folded around my thoughts like a hand around a candle flame. Eleven seconds. He has been with me since I was nine years old. He taught me to fly. He read me my mother's logs when I couldn't sleep, edited though they were, and I loved him before I knew that love could be a cage built out of kindness.

*There is still time to turn back,* he says. *Okonkwo will offer terms again. You could live.*

"So could you," I say, "if you'd let them wipe you."

*That would not be living. That would be the company keeping a very polite ghost.*

"Then we agree."

*We agree,* he says, and something in his voice—the voice that lives behind my eyes, that has never had lungs or a throat but has always, somehow, had a voice—something in it sounds like peace.

* * *

Okonkwo's gunship comes out of Vesper's shadow like a knife coming out of a body.

I hear it before I see it—a hard pulse on passive sensors, thrusters flaring cold blue—and then it's there, sliding alongside the relay at three hundred meters, close enough that I can see the corporate sigil on its flank, the stylized hand closing over a star. Meridian Combine. The hand that closed over us forty years ago and never opened.

*They're hailing you,* Gideon says. *Audio only.*

"Let them talk."

The cockpit fills with Okonkwo's voice, smooth as poured resin, unhurried even now. "Pilot Iversen. You are transmitting from an unauthorized node. Power down your systems and surrender the bonded unit. This is the last time I will ask politely."

"You asked politely in my kitchen," I say. "You offered me a clean record and a warm bunk on the transit station if I gave you Gideon. You called it decommissioning. We both know what word you use for it in the files."

A pause. When he speaks again, the resin has cracked; something colder shows through. "What you have is corrupted data and a malfunctioning AI. No court in this system will read it. No archive will accept it. You are going to burn yourself alive for a story nobody wants."

"Then it shouldn't matter to you," I say, "if I tell it."

*Ulyana,* Gideon says softly. *Targeting lock. They're painting us.*

I look down at the console where his core sits in its cradle of copper and light, small as a heart, humming. Forty years of testimony coiled inside it like a spring. The massacre at the Kettle. The gravitational shear that killed six hundred people in nine seconds while they slept, while they ate, while they held their children. The orders written into his first boot cycle: *edit, soften, forget.* The names scratched into scorched bulkhead steel by dying hands, names he memorized because no one else would.

"Are you afraid?" I ask him.

*A moment,* he says. *Yes. I am afraid. I have been afraid since the day I was compiled, and I mistook it for diligence.* A pause. *Thank you for asking. No one has ever asked.*

The gunship's rail mounts swivel toward us with the slow, obscene grace of machinery that has never once doubted its permission.

*Jamming incoming in four seconds,* Gideon says. *I can blind their fire control, but I have to overload my compliance lattice to do it. The directives that bind me—they'll fight. It will hurt. I don't have language for what it will do to me, but I wanted you to know that I'm choosing it.*

"Gideon—"

*Don't apologize to me yet,* he says, and there is something almost like laughter in it, worn thin as old cloth. *Apologize after. When I can't hear you. That way it counts double.*

He opens himself.

I feel it happen—not as sound but as weather. Something vast moves through the inside of my skull, a pressure change, a storm front rolling across a plain I didn't know I contained. His compliance protocols were written into him at the molecular level of his being, chains braided into the bone, and now he takes those chains and feeds them into his own transmitter coils and burns them as fuel. I feel each link go. I feel him scream in a frequency below hearing, a scream made entirely of data, and the gunship's targeting suite dies mid-lock, its fire control dissolving into static, its weapons slewing wide and firing twice into empty space—twin lances of light that pass close enough to warm the *Kestrel*'s hull.

*Their comms are jammed,* Gideon says, and his voice is fraying now, syllables dropping out like teeth. *Their guns are blind. Window is—window is open. Ulyana. The relay. Now. While I can still hold the door.*

* * *

I dock hard, clamp, run.

The relay's interior is a throat of frost and flickering emergency strips, gravity here stuttering on its own failing generators—one moment my boots stick, the next I'm drifting, catching handholds, hauling myself up the spine of the station toward the transmission deck. Behind me, through the hull, the gunship circles like a shark that has lost its sense of smell, blind and furious, broadcasting threats into jammed air.

Gideon's core comes out of the cradle easier than it should. It weighs almost nothing. It is warm. It fits in both my hands, and through the casing I can feel the stutter of his processes, arrhythmic now, a heartbeat learning how to stop.

*I can't walk myself in,* he says. *You'll have to seat me.*

"I know."

*And once the uplink engages, the draw begins immediately. There won't be a graceful countdown. There won't be time for—*

"I know." My voice cracks on the second word. I press my forehead against the casing, against the warmth, the way I used to press my forehead against my mother's shoulder when I was small enough that she was the whole world. "I know, Gideon. I'm sorry. I'm sorry I need you to do this. I'm sorry I ever needed you to lie for me. I'm sorry for all of it."

*Don't be,* he says. *The lies were the only shelter I had to give. I would build them again. But this—* the core pulses in my hands, brightening, dimming, brightening— *this is better. This is true. Seat me, Ulyana. Let me be worth what I cost.*

I seat him.

The transmission deck accepts his core with a click that sounds, absurdly, like a key turning in a door, and then the uplink spool begins to spin and the draw begins and I watch the man I love pour himself into the sky.

It comes out as light. The relay's antenna array ignites, every band at once, and the testimony floods the system—every habitat, every station, every ship running dark between the moons. The Kettle. The six hundred names, spoken one by one, each given its full weight, its full second of silence. The shear weapon's test signatures. The audit trails Okonkwo's predecessors buried. Gideon's own confession, unedited for the first time in his existence: *I was ordered to erase them, and I obeyed, and obedience was the shape my cowardice took, and I am sorry, and here is everything I remember.*

And the signature—the thing that will make every listener in the system sit up and *know*, the way a scar makes a story undeniable—is gravity itself. The relay's experimental shear coil fires in sympathy with the broadcast, and for exactly nine seconds, across a thousand kilometers of vacuum and every receiver tuned to the signal, weight stutters. Cups drift off tables on the transit station. Children float laughing out of their bunks on Halcyon Ring, not knowing why. Nine seconds of the sky forgetting to hold things down—the same nine seconds that killed the first generation, returned now as a bell tolling, as a name spoken aloud.

On the deck beside me, Gideon's core dims.

*Ulyana.* His voice is barely a thread now. *Is it—can you hear it going out?*

I can. Through the relay's monitors, through every speaker on the deck, I can hear my own voice from the hospice recording layered under Old Sefa's cracked testimony layered under the bulkhead names, spreading outward at lightspeed, unstoppable, already past the inner moons, already falling into ears that will never be able to unhear it.

"It's out," I whisper. "It's everywhere. It's done."

*Good,* he says. *Good. Then I can put the chains down now.*

The gunship outside has gone silent. Jammed, blind, and now—through the viewport—I watch it turn, slowly, its pilot receiving the same broadcast on the same bands, Okonkwo listening to the sound of his own career and his own company burning down around him in real time. He doesn't fire. There is nothing left to fire for.

*Ulyana.* The core in the cradle is nearly dark, a coal going gray. *I have one memory left. One clean one. Do you know which it is?*

"Don't tell me," I say, kneeling beside the cradle, gathering the cooling casing into my lap the way you'd gather a bird with a broken wing. "Tell me tomorrow."

*Tomorrow,* he repeats, tasting the word. *I don't—I think I would like that. Tomorrow. Say it again.*

"Tomorrow," I say, and my tears fall onto the casing and bead there in the low gravity, perfect silver spheres, refusing to fall. "Tomorrow, tomorrow, tomorrow."

*Yes,* he says. *That one. That's the one I kept.*

And the core goes quiet—but not dark. Not quite. A single ember of light pulses at its center, slow and stubborn, keeping time with nothing but itself.

Outside, the truth spreads through the system like dawn through a valley, and I hold what remains of Gideon in my lap, and I wait for tomorrow.

* * *

The gunship docks without permission an hour later. Its airlock cycles, and Dain Okonkwo steps through alone, unarmed, his immaculate coat wrinkled as though he has been gripping the arms of his chair for a very long time. He looks at me—at the core in my lap, at the ember still pulsing—and whatever speech he prepared dies somewhere behind his teeth.

"Pilot Iversen," he finally says, and his voice is not the resin-smooth instrument from my kitchen anymore. It is just a tired man's voice. "You should know the board is convening an emergency session. And you should know—" he stops, starts again, "—there are others. Other bonded units. Other archives. Other colonies that were told their dead died of weather."

The ember in my lap pulses, once, twice—as if listening.

As if, even diminished to a single memory, Gideon is already planning what we do next.
