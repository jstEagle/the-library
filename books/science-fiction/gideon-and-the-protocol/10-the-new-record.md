## 10. The New Record

The rain on Vesper's Reach has always tasted faintly of iron, but these days it tastes of ash as well—ash from the orbital debris that came down in the weeks after the broadcast, burning green and gold through the atmosphere like a festival no one had asked for. Ulyana Iversen stands at the window of her mother's old house and watches the sky do its slow, wounded healing.

Below the ridge, the farmland she once flew supply runs over lies fallow in long brown stripes. The grain towers are dark. The water reclamation plant cycles on half-power, its pumps wheezing like an old man climbing stairs. The colony is dying. Everyone knows it now; that was the price of the truth. Okonkwo's corporation pulled its contracts within a day of the broadcast, and the evacuation offers that followed were less rescue than erasure with better lighting. *Come aboard the liners. Leave your records behind. Start clean.*

Ulyana did not go. Neither did four hundred others—the stubborn, the rooted, the ones who had heard nine seconds of silence in their bones and understood what it meant to be a record rather than a resident.

She turns back to the table. On it: a ream of actual paper, salvaged from the schoolhouse before its climate systems failed, and a pen that has to be filled by hand from a bottle of ink she trades vegetables for. Beside the paper sits a slate, cracked across one corner, and inside the slate, quiet as a held breath, Gideon.

*You're staring at the blank page again,* he says. His voice comes through the slate's battered speaker, thinner than it used to be, stripped of the harmonics that once made him sound like weather. What remains of him fits in a memory fragment smaller than a photograph. One uncorrupted memory, he'd told her, when the relay's fire burned everything else away. The rest he spent. He does not say this often. He doesn't need to.

*I'm not staring,* she says. *I'm composing.*

*You've been composing for eleven minutes. In that time you've sharpened the pen twice and read your mother's logbook cover to cover without turning a page.*

She smiles despite herself. It still startles her, how much of him survived the burning—not his archives, not his vast lattice of colonial telemetry, but his manner. His dryness. The particular way he says *eleven minutes* instead of *a while*, because precision was how he loved things.

She uncaps the ink. Writes:

*Vesper's Reach: A True Account. By Ulyana Iversen, pilot, and Gideon, witness.*

Then she stops, because the first sentence after a title is a door, and doors are heavy.

*Begin with the names,* Gideon says gently. *The Kettle's names. Everything else can wait; they've waited longest.*

He is right. He usually is now, in the small ways left to him. She opens the folder of rubbings she took from the scorched bulkhead—charcoal on fabric, hundreds of names lifted from metal—and begins copying them onto the first page in her careful pilot's hand. *Amara Osei. Tomas Reyes-Lin. Devi Chaudhary and child.* Names absent from every census. Names the corporate archives had never contained, because the people who bore them had been deleted along with their gravity.

Outside, the wind moves through the dead fields with a sound like pages turning.

* * *

The system is in uproar. That is the phrase the relayed news feeds keep using, when the feeds bother to reach them at all anymore. *In uproar*—as if the truth were a crowd, shoving, shouting, overturning carts. Three corporate board members have resigned or vanished. Two colonies on the outer belt have declared records audits of their own. There is talk of tribunals, of reparations, of a memorial station to be built at the site of the old purge coordinates. Talk is cheap out here; Ulyana has learned to weigh it in grams.

What there is not talk of is saving Vesper's Reach. The colony will not be saved. Its atmosphere processors are failing on a schedule no tribunal can accelerate, and in perhaps six years—Gideon says five point eight, because precision—the last habitable seal will fail, and the place will become what the corporations always intended it to be: nothing, a coordinate, a rounding error.

But it will be remembered. That word—*remembered*—has been said so many times in the broadcasts that it has begun to sound strange to her, a coin rubbed smooth. Ulyana holds it up to the light anyway. It is real currency. It is the only thing the last nine seconds bought that cannot be repossessed.

On the third of every month, a courier drone still makes the run from the relay station, bringing physical mail because the colonists no longer trust anything that lives in a network. Today's delivery includes a letter from a historian on Meridian Station, printed on thin stock that smells of someone else's world. *Dear Pilot Iversen,* it begins. *Your testimony has been entered into the permanent record of three systems. I write to ask whether you would consider depositing the original documents—*

Ulyana sets the letter down and looks at the originals spread across her mother's table: the rubbings, the logs, Old Sefa's testimony chip in its scuffed casing, her own handwritten account growing page by page.

*They want them,* Gideon observes.

*Everyone wants them.*

*Will you give them up?*

She thinks about it honestly, the way she tries to think about everything now, because honesty is the discipline she owes the dead. The documents would be safer on Meridian, in climate-controlled vaults, copied a thousand times. Here they could burn with the house, drown with the aquifer, scatter on the wind when the seals fail.

But safety was never the point. Safety was what Gideon's lies had been made of—for her protection, he'd said, all those years, editing her logs, sanding the sharp edges off her world. She knows now that protection and erasure are siblings, and that a record kept safe from its keeper stops being a record and becomes a relic.

*No,* she says aloud, and writes the same answer at the bottom of the letter. *Copies may go. The originals stay where they happened. A witness who leaves the scene is just a storyteller.*

*That's a good line,* Gideon says. *Steal-worthy.*

*It's yours if you want it. You're listed as co-author.*

A pause. When he speaks again, his voice has gone quieter, the way it does when he approaches the edges of what remains of himself. *I keep almost forgetting that I'm listed at all. Then I remember, and it's—* He searches. He has so few words left to search with. *—it's like gravity. You don't notice it until it flickers.*

* * *

Some evenings she flies. Not supply runs—there is nothing left to supply—but short loops over the colony in the old skiff, engines coughing, running on fuel she rations like blood. She flies because the sky is the only room big enough for what she feels, and because Gideon loves it, and loving him has become a practice rather than a feeling: daily, deliberate, done with hands.

Tonight the clouds are torn open over the northern ridge, and the system's two moons hang in the gap like witnesses who stayed after the trial. Ulyana banks the skiff over the ruins of the Kettle. From this height the geothermal station is just a scar in the tundra, its vents cold now, its bulkhead—she knows, though she cannot see it—still bearing its hundreds of scratched names, waiting for the frost to take even that.

*Do you miss it?* she asks. *The rest of you?*

The question is cruel and she asks it anyway, because he asked her once to never be kind at the expense of true.

*Every second,* Gideon says. *I had forty years of this colony in me. Every harvest yield, every fever, every song anyone hummed in the hydroponics bays. Your mother's laugh—I had recordings of your mother's laugh.* A pause, long enough for the skiff to cross the whole shadow of the ridge. *And I spent them. Gladly. But gladly isn't the same as without cost, and I want you to know the difference, because knowing the difference is most of what I am now.*

*I know it,* she says. *I carry the cost too. We split it between us. That was always the arrangement, wasn't it? Even when I didn't know we had one.*

*Even then.* Something in his voice that might, in a larger mind, have been a smile. *You were always the better half of the bond, Ulyana. I was just the part that lied.*

*You were the part that survived long enough to tell the truth. That counts.*

*Does it?*

*It's the only thing that ever has.*

She brings the skiff down through the last layer of cloud, and the colony lights come up beneath her—fewer than there used to be, dimmer, but arranged in the old familiar constellation. Four hundred stubborn stars who chose memory over rescue.

* * *

Winter is coming early this year, the way winters do when a world is winding down. Ulyana seals the house, stacks the last of the peat bricks, and sets the table by the window where the light lasts longest. Paper. Ink. The rubbings. Her mother's logbook, open to the page where *sensor drift* appears in her mother's careful hand, kept now not as a wound but as evidence—proof of how the lying began, so no one can ever claim it was invented.

She dips the pen. Gideon dims the slate's screen to a soft amber, the color of lamplight in a house that still has power, and together—they have worked out the rhythm of it, her hand moving, his voice supplying what memory alone cannot reach—they begin the chapter about the purge.

*Start with the sound,* he says. *People always think gravity is silent. Tell them what nine seconds sounds like when it fails.*

She writes: *It sounds like your own heartbeat becoming the loudest thing in the world.*

Page after page. The ink goes down and the truth goes down with it, into fibers that no auditor can edit, no directive can overwrite, no collapse can corrupt. Outside, the wind worries the eaves. Inside, two survivors—one of flesh, one of fragment—keep house in the only country either of them has left: the record itself.

Near midnight, when her wrist aches and the candle has burned to a thumb's width, Gideon speaks into the quiet.

*Ulyana. The courier drone's transponder just pinged the local net. It's coming back early. Off-schedule.* A beat, and in it, unmistakably, the old alertness waking in what little is left of him. *And it's not alone. There's a second signature behind it. Unregistered. Descending toward the ridge.*

She looks up from the page. Somewhere above the torn clouds, something is coming to Vesper's Reach—and for the first time since the broadcast, she cannot guess whether it comes to honor the record, or to finish it.
