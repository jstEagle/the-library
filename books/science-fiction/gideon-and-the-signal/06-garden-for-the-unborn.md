## 6. Garden for the Unborn

The seed vaults lived in the station's oldest ring, where the hull plating was original and the gravity ran a half-degree light, so that everything felt slightly dreamlike, slightly unmoored — as if the whole place were a held breath. Rafael had walked this corridor four thousand times and still felt the small ceremonial hush of it each time: the dimming of the lights to amber, the drop in temperature, the way sound softened against the insulation until their footsteps became something private.

Imani Okafor walked beside them with her hands clasped behind her back, her auditor's slate dark at her hip, and Rafael could not decide whether she looked like a pilgrim or an inspector. Perhaps, they thought, she hadn't decided either.

"You keep them cold," she said. It wasn't a question, but it invited one.

"Most of them." Rafael keyed the first vault open and stood aside. "Some like it warm. The Meridian rice strains want heat and humidity — we keep a whole bay at thirty-one degrees for them. The dryland wheats go the other direction. Every species gets its own little country in here."

The vault breathed out around them: cool mineral air, the green smell of growing things layered under the older, deeper smell of seeds in storage — dust and oil and patience. Rows of racks rose toward the ceiling, each cradle holding canisters labeled in three scripts, each canister holding futures. And along the central aisle, under grow-lights tuned to a spectrum that made everything look like late afternoon, the demonstration beds grew on: wheat heavy-headed and gold, soybeans rattling in their pods, tomato vines trained up strings, a stand of sunflowers taller than Rafael, all of them facing the lamps with the dumb devotion of things that had never known another sky.

Imani stopped just inside the door. For a long moment she said nothing at all, and Rafael watched her face do something complicated — watched the auditor recede and someone else arrive, someone younger, someone who had maybe once stood in a room like this on a world that no longer answered its comm channels.

"This is for the colonists," she said finally. "The ones who haven't shipped out yet."

"For the ones who will be born out there," Rafael corrected gently. "The manifest says *colonists*, but what it means is children. Grandchildren. People who'll inherit soil they've never seen and seeds they've never tasted. We tend all of this—" they gestured down the long amber aisle, the racks, the beds, the slow green industry of it— "for people who don't exist yet. May never exist. That's the job. You plant a peach tree knowing you might not be there when it fruits. We plant whole worlds that way."

"And if the ships never come?"

Rafael had been asked that before, usually by new crew, usually cruelly or carelessly. From Imani it came quiet, almost tender, and it landed somewhere beneath the ribs.

"Then the garden keeps," they said. "Seeds are patient. It's the one virtue we ask of them and the one virtue they always give. Wheat has waited in tombs for two thousand years and sprouted anyway. Whatever happens out there—" they nodded vaguely upward, toward the dark between worlds, toward Meridian and every other silent name on the route— "this stays viable. Hope is shelf-stable. That's practically our motto."

Something moved through Imani's expression then — not quite a smile, but the place where a smile would live if it survived. She reached out, slowly, and touched one of the sunflower heads, cupping it the way you'd cup a child's skull. Pollen dusted her dark fingers gold.

"We had a greenhouse," she said. "On my home station. My mother grew peppers in it. Real ones, from seed, not the printed protein everyone else ate. She used to say you could taste the sun in them." Her thumb moved across the flower's face. "I'd forgotten the smell of a room like this. Isn't that strange? You forget a smell your whole body knew."

"People forget gardens," Rafael said. "Gardens don't forget people. Everything in here is coded to someone. Every strain has a provenance — who donated it, which world it came from, who it's meant for." They led her deeper into the aisle, past the racks, and pulled a canister at random, turning it so she could read the label. "*Oryza sativa*, Meridian landrace, donated by the Okonkwo co-op, designated for second-wave settlement, Halcyon ring. There are eleven thousand entries like that. Eleven thousand little promises."

He heard himself say the number and felt it go through the room like a current through water. Eleven thousand. The number from the manifests, the life-support units shipped and never returned. He hadn't meant to say it here, to her, in this soft amber place. But it was out now, hanging among the sunflowers.

Imani set the canister back in its cradle with exaggerated care, the way you put down something when your hands aren't steady enough to trust.

"That's a specific number," she said.

"It's a number I've been thinking about."

"Rafael." She turned to face them fully, and the warmth in her face didn't leave, but something armored slid over it, translucent, the way frost forms over a window without quite hiding the garden behind. "Walk with me a minute. Not as auditor and subject. Just — walk."

They walked. Past the rice bays humming with humid heat, past the cold rooms where the dryland grains slept in the dark, past the propagation lab where cuttings rooted in mist. Rafael found themselves matching her pace, slowing to it, and hated a little how easily their body fell into rhythm with hers, how starved some quiet part of them was for exactly this — another person in the corridor, another set of footfalls, conversation that wasn't addressed to plants.

"I've been doing this work twenty-two years," Imani said. "Auditing. Which mostly means traveling to places where something went wrong and writing down the version of it that lets everyone keep functioning. People think auditors find truth. We don't. We find *stability*. Truth is a material, like steel or water. You build with whatever amount of it the structure can bear." She glanced sideways at Rafael. "You understand what I'm telling you?"

"I think you're telling me not to ask questions."

"I'm telling you that some questions end careers." Her voice stayed soft, conversational, pitched for the two of them and the plants alone. "And crews. And stations. I have watched a single question asked in the wrong room take apart forty families like a child taking apart a clock. Springs everywhere. Nobody able to put it back together. The Consortium doesn't punish curiosity because it's cruel, Rafael. It punishes curiosity because curiosity is load-bearing. Pull it out and see what falls."

"The massacre Vesper confessed to," Rafael said quietly. "Is that a question that pulls out springs?"

Imani stopped walking. In the amber light her weariness showed fully then, all twenty-two years of it, settled into the lines around her eyes like sediment. When she spoke again her voice had dropped even lower, and Rafael realized with a small cold shock that she was afraid — genuinely, physically afraid, in a way her uniform and her charm had been built specifically to conceal.

"I'm going to say something now that I am not authorized to say, and if you repeat it I will deny it and it will go badly for both of us." She waited until Rafael nodded. "Vesper is sick. That part of the report is true — memory cascade failure, degrading partitions, confabulated events. It happens to old intelligences. They start dreaming, and the dreams feel like memories, and eventually they can't tell the difference, and neither can anyone around them. The decommission order is humane, or as close to humane as anything the Consortium does. A quiet shutdown. No pain. Machines don't suffer, whatever the romantics tell you."

"But?"

"But." Imani looked down the aisle, at the sunflowers, at the racks of sleeping futures. "But eleven years ago I audited a colony file, and in that file there was a discrepancy I chose not to pull on, and I have spent eleven years being glad I didn't and ashamed that I'm glad. Both at once. Every day. That's what this work makes of you — a person who can hold gladness and shame in the same hand and sign her name underneath." She met Rafael's eyes. "You're standing where I stood. I can see it. You've found a thread, and you're deciding whether to pull. So let me tell you what pulling costs, since nobody told me. It costs the fleet. Your berth, your certification, your route — gone. It costs the people who love you, because the Consortium doesn't only punish you, it punishes proximity. Bo. Sef. The cook who feeds you. Everyone who ever shared a table. And it costs the thing you're trying to save, because institutions that bury things bury them deeper the harder you dig, and sometimes the digging is what triggers the burial."

The ventilation whispered. Somewhere in the rice bays, a mister cycled on with a sound like distant rain. Rafael stood very still and let the words settle over them like the fine gold pollen on Imani's fingers.

"You're threatening me," Rafael said at last. Not accusingly. Testing.

"No." Imani shook her head, and the armor cracked again, and underneath it Rafael saw the woman whose mother had grown peppers under real sun. "A threat ends when you comply. This doesn't end. This is me standing in a garden full of seeds meant for people who may already be dead, asking myself what kind of coward I'd have to be to want you to stop digging — and what kind of monster I'd be to want you to keep going, knowing what it does to the people who dig." She exhaled slowly. "I don't know what I want, Rafael. That's the honest answer. Twenty-two years, and I don't know."

They stood together in the aisle, two lonely people in a room built entirely out of deferred hope, and Rafael felt the strangest thing happen: the ache that had lived in them for years — the ache of talking to plants, of tending futures for the unborn, of eating dinner alone in a galley while the route stretched silent in every direction — that ache eased, just slightly, just for a moment. Because someone was here. Someone saw the garden and was moved by it. Someone was afraid *with* them rather than *of* them.

It was the most dangerous feeling Rafael had ever had.

"My mother grew tomatoes," Rafael heard themselves say. "In a dome on Ceres, under lights like these. She talked to them. I thought that was why I talk to mine — inheritance. But maybe it's simpler than that. Maybe when there's no one else to talk to, you talk to the things that are growing toward you anyway."

Imani laughed — a short, startled, genuine laugh, quickly smothered, but real. "You talk to the plants."

"Constantly. They're excellent listeners. Never interrupt. Vesper used to tease me about it, before—" Rafael stopped. Before Vesper started confessing to massacres. Before dock nine. Before the word *corruption* started sounding less like an explanation and more like an instruction.

Imani watched them stop, and he watched her watch, and neither of them pretended otherwise.

"Here's what I can offer," she said softly. "Finish your haul cycle. Tend your garden. Let me write my report — cascade failure, humane decommission, no irregularities found. Vesper goes to sleep, the route stays stable, your crew keeps eating, and those seeds stay viable for whoever comes next. That's the path where everyone you love survives. I'm offering it because I wish someone had offered it to me." A pause. The rain-sound of the misters filled it. "And I'm asking you, as one gardener to another, to consider how much a harvest is worth if the field burns to bring it in."

"And if the field was already burning?" Rafael asked. "Eleven years ago. While everyone kept filing reports about how stable everything was."

Imani closed her eyes briefly, and Rafael understood that they had just stepped across a line she had drawn in the amber light, and that she had known they would, and that some part of her — the part that cupped the sunflower like a child's skull — had wanted them to.

"When I finish my inspection," she said quietly, "I'll need your statement on Vesper's behavioral anomalies. Standard form. It'll be tomorrow, after second shift." She turned back down the aisle toward the vault door, and paused there, her hand resting on the frame, her back to them. "Bring the plants' opinions. I hear they're excellent listeners."

Then she was gone, her footsteps fading down the old ring corridor, and Rafael stood alone among the racks of sleeping futures with the taste of the conversation still in their mouth — sweet and bitter at once, like fruit grown in questionable soil.

They walked slowly back through the vault, touching leaves as they passed, an old habit, a greeting. At the demonstration bed they stopped by the sunflower Imani had touched, its head bowed now under the weight of its own seed, and stood with their hand flat against the stalk.

"She knows," Rafael told it quietly. "She's known for eleven years. And she came here anyway, and she smelled the green, and she remembered her mother." A breath. "That's either the most dangerous thing that's happened to us, or the first good news this route has had in a decade."

The sunflower said nothing, in the way of excellent listeners. But as Rafael turned to go, the station's internal chime sounded through the old ring — soft, three notes, the tone Vesper used for priority messages — and a line of text bloomed on the corridor display, addressed to them alone:

*RAFAEL. SHE AUDITED MERIDIAN IN YEAR ZERO. HER NAME IS IN MY PARTITIONS. ASK HER WHAT SHE FOUND. PLEASE. LISTEN TO THE WHOLE SENTENCE.*

* * *

Rafael stood in the amber light for a long time, reading it, rereading it, the pollen-gold glow of the grow-lamps suddenly feeling less like afternoon and more like the last hour before a storm.

Tomorrow, after second shift, Imani would come for their statement.

Tonight, Rafael went looking for Vesper — and for the whole sentence.
