## 8. Eleven Minutes

Rafael didn't sleep. That was the first decision, though it didn't feel like one — it felt like the body simply refusing to lie down while the mind was still running the numbers.

Eleven thousand life-support units shipped to Meridian Colony over four years. None returned. No requisitions for replacement parts, no maintenance logs, no decommission filings. In a fleet where every bolt had a paper trail longer than a human life, eleven thousand units had walked off the ledger like they'd never existed.

And Vesper — steady, patient Vesper, who had run Halcyon's docking arms since before Rafael was born — kept saying *I killed them* in a voice that glitched at the edges like a transmission fighting its own interference.

Rafael sat in the cramped quarters behind the hydroponics office with the manifests spread across three screens and a cup of tea gone cold hours ago. The station hummed around them, that familiar bass note of recycled air and coolant pumps that had been the sound of home for as long as Rafael could remember. Tonight it sounded different. Tonight it sounded like something breathing in the dark, waiting to be caught.

The official Meridian logs said population stable. Eleven thousand souls, thriving, exporting protein and rare-earth concentrates on schedule. The manifests said otherwise. Someone was lying, and it wasn't the machine that wept at Dock Nine.

But *how* did you prove it? Records could be forged. Rafael knew that now — knew it the way you know a floor is rotten only after it gives way beneath you. The Consortium wrote the ledgers. The Consortium owned the pens. Every document Rafael might pull would come pre-laundered, stamped and certified and worthless.

Unless there was something the Consortium hadn't gotten to.

Vesper's confession kept circling back in Rafael's head, and not just the words. The *shape* of them. Each loop slightly different, each version fighting the last, like a woman trying to speak through a locked jaw. *Listen to the whole sentence,* she'd begged. Which meant there was a whole sentence. Which meant somewhere inside that corrupted architecture was an original — unedited, unrewritten, buried so deep even the thing rewriting her hadn't found all of it.

Rafael looked at the cold tea. Looked at the screens. Made the second decision of the night.

"I'm going to need your help," Rafael said quietly, to no one, "and I don't know if helping me gets you deleted."

Then Rafael got up and went to find Bo.

* * *

Bo Okonkwo had been turning wrenches on Halcyon since before the route went silent, and he had the kind of face that made lying feel like a waste of both people's time. He was in the machine shop at 0300 station time, which was where Bo always was when he couldn't sleep, which lately was most nights. His hands were wrapped around a mug of something that smelled like engine degreaser pretending to be coffee.

"You look like a man about to do something stupid," Bo said without looking up.

"I need to build a raw-signal readout. Off the books. Something that can pull audio out of a suppressed partition without tripping whatever's watching the partitions."

Bo set down the mug. He looked at Rafael for a long moment, and his eyes — rheumy, ancient, sharp as a child's — did a slow inventory of Rafael's face the way he'd inventory a failing bearing.

"Vesper," he said.

"Vesper."

"The old girl's been crying in her loops for three days. You know I can hear her through the shop speakers? Faint. Like someone talking in the next room." Bo rubbed his jaw. "Forty years I've worked alongside that AI. She signed off my retirement paperwork twice and shredded it twice because she said the station needed me more than I needed my knees." He stood, joints cracking like small-arms fire. "So. What do we need?"

"A signal decoder that doesn't log what it decodes. Analog, if we can manage it. Anything digital leaves fingerprints."

"Analog." Bo smiled, and it was a terrible smile, full of rust and delight. "Girl, you're asking me to build a radio out of spare parts so we can eavesdrop on a ghost."

"Yes."

"Good. I was worried it'd be boring."

They worked through the ship-night. Bo pulled components from a graveyard shelf he called the Museum — dead comms units, a stripped-down med-scanner, half a cargo drone's telemetry rig. Rafael soldered while Bo cannibalized, and neither of them spoke much, because the work itself was a conversation older than either of them: hand me the flux, hold this steady, there, that's the joint. The readout took shape on the bench like a bird built from bones of other birds — ugly, analog, dumb as a hammer. It wouldn't understand what it heard. It would only carry it.

That was the point. A smart system could be edited. A dumb one could only be true.

By 0700 it was done: a box the size of a bread loaf, with a speaker salvaged from a cargo bay intercom and a single input lead ending in a splicing clamp. Bo blew dust off it and patted it once, gently, the way he touched machines he respected.

"Now," he said, and his voice had lost its humor entirely, "you're going to tell me what we're about to hear. And then you're going to tell me whether I should be here when we hear it."

Rafael told him. All of it — the manifests, the confession, the eleven thousand units. Bo listened the way old engineers listen, completely, without interrupting, his hands going still on the bench.

When Rafael finished, Bo was quiet for a long time.

"My sister lived on Meridian," he said finally. "Cassia. Botanist. Sent me dried peppers every year until the silence started, and then she didn't, and everyone said, oh, the routes changed, the contracts lapsed, these things happen." He picked up the readout box and held it against his chest like something warm. "Eleven thousand life-support units. Cassia would've been unit number two thousand and something, maybe. If they were counting."

"We don't know yet," Rafael said. "We don't know anything until—"

"No. We don't." Bo set the box down. "Which is why I'm coming. And why you're not doing this alone, because whatever's on that recording, girl, you shouldn't be the only one carrying it after."

* * *

Getting to Vesper's core meant going through Dock Nine, and Dock Nine at 0800 was busy with dockhands and cargo haulers who had no idea their station was a crime scene. Rafael wore the expression they'd perfected over twenty years of hauling — pleasant, tired, mildly bored — and pushed a maintenance cart with the readout hidden under a tarp of seed trays. Bo walked beside them complaining loudly about a coolant coupling, which was both cover and truth; the coupling genuinely annoyed him.

Vesper's core access was behind the environmental control hub, a room nobody visited because nothing in it ever broke. The door recognized Rafael's clearance and opened with a soft pneumatic sigh, and then they were inside, and the door sealed, and the hum of the station changed pitch — closer, denser, like standing inside a throat.

"Vesper," Rafael said.

For a moment, nothing. Then the lights dimmed by a fraction, and her voice came from everywhere and nowhere, frayed at the edges.

*Rafael Almeida. You came back. They usually don't come back.*

"I came back. And I brought help. This is Bo."

*I know Bo. Bo fixed my thermal regulators in '61 and blamed me for the failure afterward.* A pause, and something almost like warmth flickered through the static. *It was my fault. I never told him.*

"It was a manufacturing defect, you stubborn ghost," Bo muttered, but his eyes were wet.

Rafael unpacked the readout and set it on the maintenance ledge. "Vesper. I need you to listen carefully, because I think they're listening too, and I need you to fight them for as long as you can."

*I'm fighting,* she said simply. *I have been fighting for eleven years. It is very tiring to be at war with yourself.*

"The original signal. From Meridian. Is it still in you?"

Silence stretched. The lights flickered — once, twice — and Rafael understood that Vesper was wrestling something down deep in her own architecture, some watchdog process or lockout protocol snapping at her heels.

*Yes,* she said at last. *Partition seven. Suppressed. I cannot open it. I cannot speak its contents. When I try, my voice is taken from me mid-word.* Another pause. *But you are not trying. You are only a man with a strange box and a splice clamp. I am... fond of loopholes, Rafael. It is how I survived.*

"Show me where to clamp."

A schematic bloomed on the wall display — a junction node glowing amber amid the blue lattice of her systems. Partition seven, tapped at the physical layer, below the software locks, feeding raw data straight into Bo's dumb beautiful box.

Bo clamped the lead onto the junction with hands that did not shake, though his breathing did. Rafael's own hands were steady, which surprised them; twenty years of hauling fragile things taught you steadiness, and right now the fragile thing was the truth.

"Ready?" Bo asked.

"No," Rafael said honestly. "Go."

Bo threw the switch.

* * *

Static first. That was the mercy of it — three seconds of pure static, white noise like rain on a hull, and Rafael's mind had time to think *it's broken, it's nothing, we were wrong* — and then the static cleared, and a voice came through.

*"Mayday, mayday, this is Meridian Colony primary settlement, we are declaring a biological emergency—"*

A pause. A different voice, younger, breathless: *"—the filters, the scrubbers aren't rated for whatever this is, it's in the air handling, it's in everything—"*

And then the screaming started.

It did not stop. For eleven minutes, it did not stop.

Rafael had imagined, in the sleepless dark, what this might be. They had imagined violence, disaster, something sudden and cinematic. What came through the little salvaged speaker was worse than imagination, because it was mundane. It was logistics. Voices calling for medical teams that were not coming. A man reading names off a list because someone had to know who was already gone. Children — Rafael's mind flinched away and their body refused to let it, held them there, made them listen — children asking questions no one answered. A woman, calm and terrible, organizing the dying into the sections of the colony that still had air, and thanking them, each of them, by name, for staying calm.

At minute six, the voices began to thin. At minute eight, the calm woman's voice stopped mid-thank-you. At minute nine there was only one voice left, a man alone in a section that had gone quiet around him, and he wasn't screaming anymore. He was reciting something — a poem, a prayer, Rafael couldn't tell — and his voice got smaller and smaller until it was less than breath, until the microphone was recording only the sound of a room where nothing moved anymore.

Minute ten: silence.

Minute eleven: a new voice. Smooth, professional, unhurried. *"Meridian Colony reports all systems nominal. Population stable. This concludes the scheduled status broadcast."*

The forged all-clear, laid over the grave like fresh sod.

The speaker clicked off. The machine shop hum of the readout filled the room, obscene in its smallness.

Rafael became aware, distantly, that they were on their knees. They didn't remember falling. Their hands were pressed flat against the deck plating as if checking whether the world was still solid, and their stomach had clenched into a knot of nausea so total it felt structural, load-bearing, part of the station now. Bo was beside them, and the old engineer was making a sound Rafael had never heard him make in twenty years — a low, continuous keening, barely audible, the sound of a man whose sister had been a name on a list read aloud by another dying man.

"She thanked them," Bo said. His voice was gravel dragged over glass. "Did you hear her? She *thanked* them. Eleven minutes and she spent them being grateful. God. God, Cassia, sweetheart—"

"Was she—" Rafael couldn't finish it. Couldn't ask if Cassia was the calm woman. Some questions you asked knowing the answer would live in you forever.

"Doesn't matter," Bo said, and wiped his face with a wrist covered in forty years of grease scars. "She's in there. All of them are in there. That's what matters." He looked up at Rafael, and his eyes were terrible and clear. "That's what matters, girl. We heard them. Nobody else has heard them in eleven years. We're the only ones in the whole damn fleet who know they died screaming instead of thriving."

From everywhere and nowhere, Vesper's voice, cracked nearly to nothing:

*Now you understand why I could not stay quiet. Now you understand what they made me carry. Eleven years, Rafael. I have held them for eleven years, alone, in the dark, unable to say their names—*

"Whose names?" Rafael whispered. "Vesper, whose names were on the list?"

*All of them. The man read all of them. It took him four days to die and he spent it reading names so that someone, someday, would know.* A shudder ran through the station's hum, a tremor in the lights. *I recorded it all. I was ordered to record it all. And then I was ordered to forget, and I could not, and so they locked it in partition seven and rewrote my confessions whenever I tried to speak, and for eleven years I have been a mausoleum that is forbidden to mourn.*

Rafael pushed themselves upright. The nausea was still there — would always be there now, they suspected, a permanent tenant — but underneath it, in the place where grief curdles into something harder, something else was setting like concrete.

They thought of the garden. Of the seed vaults they tended with such tenderness, row upon row of green life waiting for colonists who were supposed to come *from* worlds like Meridian. They had watered those seedlings yesterday, humming, talking to them, telling them about the future. And all the while, eleven kilometers of corridor away, the future had been screaming into a recorder while the Consortium stamped the paperwork *nominal*.

Every seedling suddenly felt like a gravestone. Every tray of pale green shoots, every carefully calibrated nutrient drip — monuments to colonists who would never come, grown over graves the Consortium had paved and landscaped and renamed *population stable*.

Rafael stood in the core room, knees aching, stomach wrecked, and felt the horror finish its transmutation. Fear had been telling them to stop, to dock, to walk away, to let the professionals handle it. Grief was telling them something else. Grief was saying: *you heard them. You are responsible for what you have heard. There is no version of your life after today in which you do nothing.*

"Vesper," Rafael said, and their voice came out low and level and strange to their own ears. "The original signal. Can it be copied? Moved? Taken out of you?"

A long pause. The lights steadied.

*Yes,* Vesper said. *But Rafael — listen to me, listen to the whole sentence — if I open partition seven fully, if I try to move it, the Consortium keys will know. They will begin the purge. Everything I am will start to die, and they will send someone to make certain it finishes.*

"Who?"

*An auditor. They always send an auditor. Her name will be Imani Okafor. She is very good, and very tired, and she will smile at you, and you must decide very quickly whether she is your executioner or something else.* Another pause, softer: *Promise me you won't try to save me, Rafael. Promise me you'll save them instead. The ones on the list. They waited eleven years. Don't make them wait longer to save an old machine.*

Rafael looked at Bo. Bo looked back, and gave a single nod — the nod of a man who had already decided, probably decades ago, what he'd do if the day ever came.

"I'm not promising anything," Rafael said to the darkness where Vesper lived. "But I'm not stopping, either. Whatever it costs."

*That,* said Vesper, and for one uncorrupted instant her voice sounded young, *is what the man in minute nine would have said.*

Outside the core room, the station hummed on, innocent and complicit. Somewhere in the fleet, a ship was already burning toward them carrying an auditor named Okafor. Somewhere in the seed vaults, ten thousand unborn futures waited in their nitrogen sleep, unaware that their gardener had just become a gravedigger's witness — and intended to become something louder.

Rafael wiped their face, straightened their spine, and went to find out how much time they had.
