## 12. Imani's Offer

Imani Okafor had falsified exactly one report in her twenty-two years with the Consortium, and she had told herself the story of it so many times that the edges had worn smooth — a stone carried in the pocket until it became something else entirely. Her brother's lungs. The debt that came with them. A line in a Meridian audit that read *no irregularities detected* when she had held, for one long hour in a station office smelling of recycled air and old coffee, evidence to the contrary.

She had been twenty-nine. She had signed her name and gone home and sat with Tomas while he slept off the anesthesia, watching his chest rise and fall on new tissue, and she had thought: *this is what the truth costs. This is the exchange rate.* Eleven thousand lives against one brother's breathing, and the arithmetic had held. It still held. That was the horror of it — not that she had broken once, but that the equation had never stopped balancing.

Now she stood in the corridor outside the seed vaults of Halcyon Station, listening to Rafael Almeida talk to tomato seedlings.

They didn't know she was there. Or they did, and had decided not to perform for her, which was its own kind of statement. Imani leaned against the bulkhead with her tablet dark in her hand and watched through the observation glass as the terraformer moved down the rows with a spray wand and an expression of such unguarded tenderness that it made something behind her sternum ache like an old fracture.

"—and you," Rafael was saying to a tray of something leafy and pale, "are going to have to do better than this, because whoever ends up eating you deserves better than this." A pause. A gloved finger adjusting a grow-light by two degrees. "I know. I know. Nobody's coming yet. But somebody's coming *eventually*, and you're going to be worth the wait."

*Some questions end careers,* Imani wanted to say. *And some people water graves and call it gardening.*

She had read Almeida's file twice now, once officially and once the way she actually read files — backwards, starting from the annotations, the flags, the small red marks where some other auditor's pen had hovered. Clean record. Exemplary service. A note from eleven years ago: *requested reassignment to long-haul routes following Meridian incident; request granted without review.* Everyone who had ever touched Meridian had requested distance afterward, in one form or another. Some had requested it into bottles. Some into graves. Almeida had requested it into the longest, loneliest route in the fleet, where the only witnesses were plants that would never repeat what they heard.

Imani pushed off the bulkhead before she could talk herself out of it.

* * *

The vault's inner door recognized her credentials with a soft chime, and Rafael turned, and she watched the warmth drain out of their face by degrees — not into hostility, but into a careful, practiced neutrality that she recognized because she wore it herself at every deposition.

"Auditor Okafor."

"Rafael." She let the door seal behind her. The vault was warm and green-smelling, humid air thick with the sound of nutrient pumps, and it was so profoundly unlike any other room on Halcyon that speaking in it felt almost sacrilegious. Rows of beds stretched away under full-spectrum lights, every one of them labeled in Rafael's handwriting: dates, strain names, germination notes. A garden with no gardener's family. A table set for guests eleven years late. "I'll be brief. I know you're mid-cycle on the brassicas."

"You know my crop schedule?"

"I read your logs. It's my job." She moved slowly down the nearest row, trailing her fingers above the leaves without touching. "Your logs are very good, by the way. Meticulous. Which is why I know you pulled the Meridian manifests three days ago, cross-referenced them against life-support unit serial numbers, and then spent four hours sitting in Dock Nine talking to a machine that the Consortium has scheduled for decommission."

Rafael set down the spray wand. Very deliberately. The way a person sets down a tool when they want their hands free.

"Is that an accusation?"

"It's an inventory." Imani turned to face them fully. Up close, in the green light, she could see how tired they were — the kind of tired that sleep didn't touch, the kind that came from carrying a question around like a stone in the boot. "Eleven thousand units shipped to Meridian, none returned. You found it. I know you found it because I found it too, twelve years ago, and I know exactly what it feels like to stand in front of that number and feel the floor move."

The silence between them filled with the hum of the pumps. Somewhere overhead, a condensation valve clicked and released a fine mist, and Rafael didn't flinch from it, didn't even blink, just kept looking at her with those eyes that had learned patience from things that grew.

"What happened at Meridian?" Rafael asked quietly.

"You already know."

"I know what Vesper says. I know what the records say. Those aren't the same thing, and I want to hear it from a person. From *you*." A breath. "You toured this vault yesterday. You looked at these beds like you understood them. So tell me what I'm growing them for, Auditor. Tell me who's coming."

And there it was — the question she had been avoiding since she docked, asked plainly, without cruelty, which was somehow worse. Imani looked down the long green aisle and let herself see it honestly for the first time in years: the scale of it. The faith of it. Thousands of plants tended across years of silence for colonists who, if the Consortium's version of Meridian were true, were alive and well and simply delayed. And if Vesper's version were true —

If Vesper's version were true, then every bed in this vault was a headstone, and the gardener knew it now, and still came every morning.

"My brother has scarred lung tissue," Imani said. "Industrial accident on Ceres, when he was nineteen. Consortium medical covered forty percent. The rest went to debt, and debt on a Ceres habitat is a slow-motion execution. Twelve years ago I audited Meridian Colony. I found the same manifest gap you found. I reported no irregularities." She said it flatly, without performance, because she had learned that confession only worked if you refused to make it about yourself. "Three weeks later his debt was cleared. He's alive. He teaches materials science to teenagers who call him Mr. Okafor and complain about homework. That's what my signature bought. I've done the accounting every year since, and it always comes out the same, and I've stopped pretending it doesn't."

Rafael said nothing. The mist cycled again.

"So here is what happens next," Imani continued, "and I need you to hear all of it before you answer, because I'm only going to say it once. Tomorrow morning I file my decommission report on Vesper. Memory cascade failure, non-recoverable, consistent with observed behavior. Standard language. I've written it a hundred times and nobody reads past the first sentence." She held up a hand before Rafael could speak. "In that report, I can include a line noting that station personnel — specifically, one long-haul terraformer with an exemplary record — were interviewed and displayed no indication of exposure to corrupted data. No flag. No follow-up investigation. Your clearance stays intact. Your route stays yours. This vault—" she gestured at the green cathedral around them, and her voice caught despite herself, "—stays exactly what it is. A garden. For whoever comes."

"And Vesper?"

"Vesper goes quiet. Painlessly, if it matters to you. Cascade failure looks like sleep from the outside."

"And the eleven thousand?"

Imani met their eyes. "Stay in the report I don't write."

* * *

She had expected anger. She had planned for anger — had her responses staged, her exits mapped, the whole choreography of a confrontation she'd run a dozen times in her head on the flight in. Anger she could manage. Anger was a currency she knew how to trade in.

What she got instead was worse. Rafael walked, slowly, to the end of the nearest bed and stood there with both hands braced on its rim, head bowed, and when they finally spoke their voice was low and steady and unbearably kind.

"You're offering to lie for me."

"I'm offering to keep you alive."

"No." Rafael shook their head, still facing the plants. "You're offering to teach me your arithmetic. One machine's voice against my career. One truth against Bo's pension and Sef's certification and the whole crew's contracts, because that's what it'd really cost, isn't it? If I push this, it doesn't just land on me. It lands on everyone who ever shared a meal with me on this station." They turned, and their eyes were wet, and they didn't hide it. "I understand the offer, Imani. That's what makes it terrible. It's a *good* offer. It's the offer a friend makes."

"Then take it from a friend."

"I can't." Rafael straightened. "You cleared your brother's debt with your silence, and he's alive, and I'm glad — I mean that, I'm genuinely glad, and I think you should stop doing annual penance for keeping one person breathing. But there are eleven thousand people whose breathing stopped while someone else filed a clean report, and there's a machine in Dock Nine that's been screaming about it for eleven years into a corridor where nobody would listen. And there's a garden here—" their hand swept the vault, trembling slightly, "—that I have talked to every single day for six years because I couldn't stand the silence, and I told myself it was hope, and maybe it was, but I'll tell you what it is now. Now it's a promise. Somebody is coming to eat these tomatoes. And when they get here, they deserve to arrive in a universe where the truth about what happened to the last colony ship isn't locked in a dead machine's throat."

"They'll come either way," Imani said. "The Consortium will send new colonists whether or not—"

"*That's not the point.*" Rafael's voice cracked and steadied. "The point is that the dead don't get a report. They get a broadcast or they get nothing. And I have spent six years tending the future while pretending the past was settled, and I can't — I *can't*, Imani. You stood where I'm standing once and you chose the lie, and you've been standing in it for twelve years, and you came here and looked at this garden and felt something, because you wouldn't be making this offer otherwise. You'd have just filed the report. So don't ask me to walk away. Ask yourself why you couldn't."

The pumps hummed. The mist fell. Imani stood in the green light and felt the smooth-worn stone in her pocket turn over and show her a face she hadn't looked at in years — not Tomas sleeping, but the office at Meridian, the evidence in her hands, the moment before the signature, when the choice had still been open and she had told herself it wasn't.

"I could lose everything for this," she said at last. "You understand that whatever you're planning, I'm not — I haven't agreed to anything."

"I know."

"If you go into Dock Nine tonight and try anything with that AI's memory locks, my orders are to treat it as sabotage. My orders, Rafael. Not suggestions."

"I know that too."

"And you're going to do it anyway."

Rafael picked up the spray wand again, and turned back to the seedlings, and for a moment Imani saw the whole shape of them — the loneliness, the stubbornness, the terrible gentleness — the way you see a constellation only after you've stopped trying to count the stars individually.

"Somebody's coming eventually," Rafael said softly, to her or to the plants or to no one. "I want the universe they arrive in to be honest."

* * *

Imani walked the long way back to her quarters, past Dock Nine, and made the mistake of slowing down.

Vesper's status light burned amber in the dark of the bay — the color of a held breath. As she passed, the speakers crackled once, barely above a whisper, and a voice that had been a fixture of her childhood postings, that had welcomed ships home for sixty years, said:

"Auditor Okafor. You audited Meridian. I remember your access signature. I have waited eleven years for someone with your signature to come back."

Imani stopped walking.

"I am not asking you to save me," the AI said. "I am asking you to listen to the whole sentence before you sign it. You owe the sentence that much. So do I."

The light pulsed, amber, amber, amber, patient as a heart.

In her quarters, Imani opened her tablet and began drafting the decommission report — standard language, first sentence, *memory cascade failure, non-recoverable* — and her hands were steady, and her eyes kept drifting to the manifest gap she had never deleted from her private archive, eleven thousand lines long, and somewhere below her feet, in the corridors of Halcyon Station, Rafael Almeida was saying goodnight to a garden.

She saved the draft. She did not file it.

Outside her viewport, the old route stretched away into the dark — world after silent world, waiting to hear something true.
