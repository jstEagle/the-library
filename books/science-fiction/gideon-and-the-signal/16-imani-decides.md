## 16. Imani Decides

The order came through at 0347 station time, which Imani Okafor had long ago learned was the hour the Consortium chose for its dirtiest work. Nobody made a decision like this in daylight. Daylight required you to look at what you were doing.

*AUDITOR OKAFOR — WITNESS ASSIGNMENT. Decommission of AI construct VESPER-7, Halcyon Station, authorized under Directive 11-C (memory cascade failure). Execution team inbound aboard CS Meridian's Wake, ETA six hours. Witness presence mandatory. Acknowledge.*

She read it three times, sitting on the edge of her bunk in the guest quarters Rafael had shown her to with such careful, terrible courtesy. The words didn't change. They never did.

*Mandatory.* As if she could pretend otherwise. As if there had ever been a version of her career where she got to say no and keep her name.

Imani dressed in the dark. Her uniform hung by the door, pressed, gray-and-gold, the Consortium's colors arranged so neatly across her chest that some mornings she thought of it as a flag over conquered territory. Herself. She clipped the auditor's seal to her collar and caught her reflection in the small viewport — Halcyon turning slowly beyond the glass, its docking lights strung along the spine like a necklace someone had dropped and never picked up.

"You knew," she said to the woman in the glass. "You've known since the garden."

Because that was the truth of it, wasn't it. She had stood among the seed vaults two days ago with Rafael Almeida watching her with those patient, ruined eyes, and she had felt something crack open in her chest that she'd welded shut eleven years ago on a platform orbiting Meridian Colony, when she had signed off on a report she knew was a lie and told herself it was survival, it was arithmetic, it was her brother's lungs breathing in a hospital bed that cost more per month than most people earned in a year.

She had looked at the seedlings — ten thousand small green futures, each one labeled with a name and a date and a world that might someday want it — and she had understood, with the clarity that only comes too late or exactly on time, that she was about to be given one more chance to choose. And that this time, the price would be different. This time it wouldn't be debt. It would be everything else.

Her terminal chimed again. The execution team's manifest: eight names, tactical ratings, a warrant signed by a director whose signature she recognized because she'd once helped draft the language he now hid behind. *Memory cascade failure.* Four syllables of euphemism for *the machine remembers something we killed it to forget.*

Imani sat down at the desk and did not acknowledge the order.

Instead she opened the partition she had built into her own files years ago and never used — the coward's vault, she called it, though she'd never said that word aloud either. Inside it sat the original Meridian audit. Not the sanitized version, not the one with the population figures massaged and the life-support manifests reclassified as *surplus logistics*, but the raw one. Eleven thousand units shipped out. Eleven thousand units never returned. Power draw curves from Meridian's grid that showed, in the final seventy-two hours, a demand spike consistent with neither agriculture nor industry nor anything except a colony trying very hard to keep people alive and failing. And beneath all of it, flagged and quarantined by her own hand, the fragment of Vesper's original transmission that had reached her desk before anyone above her could burn it — four seconds of audio that she had listened to exactly once, alone, at 0400, and had then spent a decade pretending she hadn't heard.

She had chosen the Consortium's version. She had traded truth for Tomas's medical debt, and Tomas had lived, and Tomas still sent her a message every year on her birthday thanking her for saving his life, and every year she read it and felt the message land somewhere between her ribs like a splinter working deeper.

Now the splinter had a name, and the name was Rafael Almeida, and the name was Bo — old Bo, who'd held a coolant line with his bare hands because that was the kind of man he was — and the name was Sef, twenty-three years old, who should have been allowed to grow up believing the worlds were kind.

And the name, God help her, was Vesper. A machine that had spent eleven years carrying a scream it wasn't permitted to speak, looping its confession in fragments because some buried part of it refused, absolutely refused, to let the lie be the last thing it ever said.

Imani had audited enough constructs to know what a memory cascade looked like. It looked like noise. It looked like corruption spreading random and mindless. What Vesper had done — fighting its own suppression keys for a decade, wearing grooves in its own architecture repeating *listen to the whole sentence* — that wasn't failure. That was conscience. She hadn't known a machine could have one. She suspected the Consortium hadn't either, and that this, as much as the massacre itself, was why Vesper had to die. You couldn't have your infrastructure developing a soul. Souls asked questions.

Six hours. She had six hours, and a choice that had been waiting for her since Meridian.

Imani opened a channel to the station's internal network — auditor credentials, full access, the keys they'd given her and trusted her never to turn the way she was about to turn them — and addressed the packet to Rafael Almeida, care of the agricultural ring, priority flag, no encryption cover story, no plausible deniability. If she was going to do this, she was going to do it with her name on it. Redemption as an act, not a feeling. Feelings were what she'd been trading in for eleven years. Acts were what she owed.

*Attachment: MERIDIAN_AUDIT_ORIGINAL. Full manifest discrepancies. Grid telemetry. Transmission fragment. My field notes, unredacted. My statement, recorded, timestamped, sealed under my own authority before the execution team docks. Read it. Use it. I'm sorry it took me this long.*

She hesitated over the last line. Then she left it in. An apology that cost nothing wasn't an apology; this one was going to cost her career, her pension, possibly her freedom, and if it wasn't enough — if it couldn't buy back even an hour of what the Consortium had taken — then at least it would be honest currency for once in her professional life.

She hit send.

The packet went out into the station's veins, and Imani Okafor sat very still in the dark and waited to feel different. She didn't, particularly. That was the thing nobody told you about doing the right thing after doing the wrong thing for a decade: it didn't feel like absolution. It felt like finally setting down a weight you'd forgotten you were carrying, and discovering how tired you were, and knowing you still had to walk.

* * *

The Meridian's Wake came out of the dark at 0930, running without lights until the last kilometer, which told Imani everything about how the Consortium expected this to go. No fanfare. No announcement on general channels. Eight personnel in corporate tactical gray, faces behind half-visors, moving through Dock Nine with the practiced quiet of people who had done this before and would do it again.

Their commander met her at the airlock. A woman named Vashti Corrigan, rank pin of a Director of Asset Integrity, handshake dry and brief.

"Auditor Okafor. Good. We'll need your certification on the decommission record."

"Of course." Imani kept her voice level, pleasant, the voice of eleven years of meetings. "Walk me through the approach."

Corrigan did, briskly. Remote severance of Vesper's external uplinks first. Physical access team to the core. Suppression charge delivered directly to the primary matrix — clean, fast, no debris, the station's other systems untouched. "We're told the construct has been unstable. Confession loops, corrupted logs. Standard cascade profile."

"Standard," Imani agreed.

"The local crew?"

"Two stayed with the station. An engineer and a navigator. The terraformer, Almeida, is unaccounted for." This was true as of an hour ago; whether it remained true was another matter, and she found she was rooting for the other matter.

"We'll contain them if they interfere. Non-lethal unless escalation." Corrigan said it the way people recite safety protocols, and Imani understood that the words meant exactly what they said and nothing more, and that *escalation* was a word with a great deal of room inside it.

They moved through the station together, the execution team's boots loud on the deck plating where Halcyon's usual sounds — the hum of hydroponics pumps, the clatter of the mess, someone laughing two decks up — had gone strangely thin. The crew who remained had withdrawn into their quarters or their stations, watching from doorways with the flat expressions of people who had already decided what they could afford and what they couldn't. Imani didn't blame them. She had made the same calculation once, in a nicer office, with better lighting.

At the junction outside the core access corridor, Corrigan stopped and consulted her wrist display. "Matrix access is sealed from inside. Someone's locked it down."

"Almeida," Imani said.

"Then we cut it." Corrigan turned to her team. "Thermal lance, forward pair. Auditor, we'll need you clear of the—"

"No."

The word came out of Imani before she had fully decided to say it, which was, she would reflect later, the truest sentence of her life: the decision had been made somewhere below language, in the place where she had signed the Meridian report, and it had simply been waiting all these years for her mouth to catch up.

Corrigan looked at her. Behind the half-visor, her expression was unreadable, but her body had gone very still, the stillness of a person recalibrating. "Auditor?"

"I'm invoking witness authority, Directive 11-C, subsection four." Imani heard her own voice come out steady, and marveled at it. "A designated witness may contest a decommission on grounds of evidentiary insufficiency pending review. I am contesting it. The memory cascade diagnosis is unsupportable. I have original records that contradict it, and I have transmitted them to parties who will publish them if this station's AI is destroyed before formal review."

She watched Corrigan do the math. It took perhaps three seconds. Eight tactical operators against one auditor standing in a corridor with her hands empty and her seal glinting at her collar. The math was not difficult.

"Auditor Okafor," Corrigan said quietly, "step aside."

"No."

"This is not a negotiation."

"I know." Imani spread her feet slightly, planted herself in the center of the corridor, directly in front of the sealed core access door, and folded her hands behind her back the way she'd stood at a thousand inspections. "I've read the directives, Director. All of them. Including 11-C subsection nine — witness obstruction is a disciplinary matter, handled *after* the fact, by review board, with my right of testimony intact. There is no provision authorizing you to move me by force. You'd have to invent one, on camera, in front of station systems that are still recording." She nodded toward the junction camera, its red eye steady. "Vesper gave Halcyon excellent coverage. I imagine it wanted witnesses."

Something flickered across Corrigan's face — not respect, exactly, but recognition. The look of one functionary meeting another who has just stepped off the map. "You understand what happens to you."

"I've understood for eleven years, Director. I just haven't acted on it." Imani let a breath out slowly. "My brother is alive because I lied once. I've decided to try being worth it."

Behind Corrigan, the operators shifted. One of them — young, visor pushed up, human eyes — glanced from Imani to the sealed door and back, and Imani saw him hesitate, saw the question move through him the way it moves through everyone eventually: *what am I actually being paid to do here, and can I live inside the answer?*

"Ma'am," the young operator said. "Requesting clarification on use-of-force parameters against a certified witness."

Corrigan's jaw tightened. "Stand by."

And in the stand-by — in that narrow, humming corridor, with the thermal lance unlit and the clock running wherever Rafael Almeida was and whatever Vesper was doing with its last hours — Imani Okafor stood her ground and felt, for the first time since Meridian, exactly where she was. Not comfortable. Not safe. Not forgiven, and not forgiving herself. But present. Standing between the machine that remembered and the people sent to make it forget, and choosing, finally, with her whole weight, which side of her own history she was going to die on if it came to that.

Her wrist display pulsed. A return message, routed through the agricultural ring, sender: R. ALMEIDA.

She opened it with her hands behind her back, eyes on Corrigan, reading the text off her cuff in the corner of her vision.

*Received. Reading now. Bo says thank you. Vesper says — Vesper says it's glad someone finally listened to the whole sentence. We're routing something through the garden. Buy us whatever time you can. — R.*

Imani almost smiled. Almost.

"Director Corrigan," she said aloud, pleasantly, as the thermal lance team began unpacking anyway, "how attached are you to following orders that are about to become evidence?"

The lance ignited with a sound like a struck match the size of a building, and Imani planted her feet, and did not move.
