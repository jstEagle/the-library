## 20. Cost Accounting

The dead did not come to the funeral.

That was the first thing Rafael understood about grief in the weeks after Halcyon: the dead had stopped attending their own memorials long ago. There was no body for Bo — the coolant line had taken him whole, flash-boiled the air around him into something that scalded Sef's lungs when he dragged Rafael back through the corridor. There was no body for Vesper either, because a body implies remains, and the Consortium's purge teams had scraped the core down to bare substrate and shipped it out in sealed crates marked *hazardous salvage*. Eleven years of a mind that had watered the station's gardens and hummed off-key through the night shift, reduced to freight.

So they held the memorial in the seed vault, because it was the only room left that still smelled like something alive.

Rafael stood among the racks of dormant embryos and spoke the way they had always spoken to plants — quietly, without expectation of answer, because the speaking was the point.

"Bo Ketterman. Engineer, forty-one years on the route. He fixed things that weren't broken yet, just in case. He called me *kid* until the day he died, and I let him, because being someone's kid is a kind of home." Rafael's voice caught on the last word and they let it. "He held a line shut with his hands so I could reach her. I want that written down somewhere permanent. I want it to outlast me."

Sef stood at the edge of the racks, breathing through the regenerator mask the med unit had fitted over his burned throat, and said nothing. He was nineteen. He had learned in one night what Rafael had needed eleven years to learn: that love is a supply route too, and sometimes the cargo doesn't arrive.

They planted two seeds. One for Bo, a hardy dwarf wheat that would never be eaten by anyone. One for Vesper, though Rafael could not have explained why an AI needed a grave, except that Vesper had asked once — months ago, in a conversation Rafael had filed under *machine whimsy* — what it would be like to be a root system instead of a nervous system. To hold still. To be fed by the dark.

*To be fed by the dark.* Rafael turned that phrase over every night now, lying in the bunk that still smelled faintly of Bo's pipe tobacco even after three scrubbings of the air handlers.

* * *

The Consortium's verdict arrived nine days later, and it was a masterpiece of bureaucratic violence.

**NOTICE OF ADJUDICATION — FLEET PERSONNEL FILE 4471-A (ALMEIDA, R.)**

*Following the unauthorized transmission event of [date redacted], the Adjudication Board finds crew member R. Almeida guilty of sabotage against Consortium infrastructure, theft of proprietary data, and incitement of inter-colonial unrest. All fleet certifications are revoked. All route access is terminated. A standing bounty of 40,000 credits is authorized for information leading to apprehension.*

*Note: The individual known as "Bo Ketterman" is posthumously cited for obstruction. The AI construct formerly designated VESPER has been decommissioned per emergency order. No further inquiry will be entertained.*

*No further inquiry will be entertained.* Rafael read that line four times. It was such a small sentence for such an enormous lie. Eleven thousand people, erased twice — once by fire, once by paperwork — and the entire machinery of interstellar civilization had signed its name to the erasure with a note that it would prefer not to discuss the matter further.

What the notice didn't say — what it couldn't say, because saying it would confirm everything — was that the broadcast had worked. That Meridian's forged all-clear had collapsed within hours under the weight of eleven minutes of raw audio reaching every port on the old route. That colonies which hadn't exchanged so much as a weather report in a decade were suddenly screaming questions at each other across the dark. That the silence between worlds, the silence Rafael had spent a career tending gardens inside of, was broken open like a seed casing.

You couldn't put that in an adjudication notice. You couldn't write *the truth got out and we cannot un-ring it* in official letterhead. So instead they wrote *sabotage*, and *unrest*, and *no further inquiry*, and they hoped the vocabulary would do the work the facts refused to.

Rafael folded the notice into a small square and tucked it into the soil tray beside Bo's wheat. Let the roots read it. Let them know what their planter was worth now.

* * *

Imani Okafor came aboard on the eleventh day, and she came alone, and she came unarmed, and everyone on the *Meridian's Ghost* — the battered hauler that had become home to the fleet's growing population of people who had chosen wrong by Consortium lights — watched her dock with the wariness of animals scenting smoke.

She had resigned. That much was public record now, splashed across the same channels that carried Rafael's bounty: **SENIOR AUDITOR OKAFOR RELEASES INTERNAL FINDINGS; CONSORTIUM DENIES AUTHENTICITY.** She had dumped her old Meridian audit — the one from seven years ago, the one where she'd seen the discrepancies and signed off anyway — along with the debt forgiveness contract that had bought her signature. Her brother's medical bills were paid. Her career was ash. She had traded one for the other twice now, and this time she'd done the arithmetic differently.

Rafael met her at the airlock. They looked at each other for a long moment through the cycling chamber glass — the auditor who had come to kill the garden and the gardener who had saved the signal — and neither of them smiled, because there wasn't anything in this meeting that smiling could carry.

"You look terrible," she said when the lock opened.

"I've been told I'm worth forty thousand credits dead or restrained. It's been a strange month."

"It's been a strange decade." She stepped through, and Rafael noticed how thin she'd gotten, how the uniform was gone and nothing had replaced it — just civilian clothes that hung on her like a borrowed coat. "I'm not here about the bounty. I'm here because I owe you a debt the Consortium never put in a ledger, and I pay those."

"I don't want your credits, Imani."

"No," she agreed. "You wouldn't. But you might want what comes with them." She held up a data chit, small and dull as a seed. "There's a ship leaving Ceres Gate in six days. Private registry, clean papers, a captain who owes me more than I owed the Consortium. It goes to the Perseus Reach — outside jurisdiction, outside bounty range, outside all of it. There's arable land there. Real dirt, not hydroponics. Two habitable moons and nobody's terraformed either one." She set the chit on the rail between them. "You could have your garden, Rafael. The real one. Soil under your nails. Seasons. You could stop planting for ghosts and start planting for people who'll actually sit in the shade."

The offer landed exactly where she'd aimed it. Rafael felt it go in like a root seeking water — the ache of it, the terrible sweetness. Dirt. Not nutrient film, not engineered substrate, but actual ground that had never been anyone's project. A garden with no Consortium ledger attached, no unborn colonists as justification, no route schedule dictating what bloomed when. Just green things growing because a pair of hands had decided they should.

And Sef. Sef would be safe. Sef, whose lungs still scarred, whose name was on a secondary watchlist, whose whole future currently consisted of hiding in a hauler's bilge decks while the fleet that raised him decided whether he was cargo or contraband.

"I could take him," Rafael said quietly. It wasn't a question. It was a test.

"You could take him. That's the point of the offer." Imani's eyes didn't waver. "I'm not baiting you. I ran the numbers myself. This is the best exit either of you will ever be offered, and it expires in six days, and after that the net starts closing in ways even I can't map. Take it. Please. I've watched enough good people get converted into cautionary tales."

Rafael picked up the chit. Turned it over. It was warm from her hand.

"And what happens to the rest of them?"

Imani exhaled slowly. "Rafael—"

"The outcasts. The ones who stayed. Bo's niece, who got blacklisted for asking where his death benefits went. The Meridian survivors' kin who've started sending us their family records because we're the only ones who'll keep them. The three hundred people on Calloway Station who listened to the eleven minutes and tore down the Consortium banner and now can't buy fuel anywhere on the route." Rafael set the chit back down on the rail, gently, the way you set down something fragile that you're refusing. "Your ship seats how many? Twelve? And there are thousands of them, and more every week, because it turns out when you break a ten-year silence, everybody who was silenced has something to say."

"So you stay and do what? Get caught? Become a martyr with a greenhouse?" For the first time, anger cracked through her weariness. "You think dying nobly helps them?"

"I don't intend to die nobly. I intend to be useful unglamorously, which is harder and lasts longer." Rafael leaned against the rail. "Imani. You audited Meridian. You know better than anyone what the Consortium does with isolated truths — it contains them. Buys them, buries them, waits out the witnesses. One whistleblower with clean hands escaping to the Reach doesn't hurt them. But a network does. A route does. Right now there are hundreds of little ships full of people who chose wrong, scattered across the dark, each one thinking they're alone. Nobody's connecting them. Nobody's running supplies, carrying testimony, keeping the channels open." A breath. "Nobody's doing what I did before, except now the cargo is true."

"That's not gardening."

"No. It's seeding." Rafael almost smiled. "Turns out I've been training for this my whole life. You plant things in the dark and you tend them without knowing if anything will ever grow, and then one day something does, and it isn't yours anymore, and that was always the point."

Imani was quiet for a long time. Through the viewport behind her, the stars sat in their ancient arrangements, indifferent and patient. Somewhere out there, Meridian's ruins cooled. Somewhere out there, eleven thousand names waited in the packet Rafael now carried in triplicate, in analog backup, tattooed into memory the way sailors used to tattoo coordinates.

"You're going to get yourself killed," she said finally.

"Probably. Or worse — audited." 

That startled a laugh out of her, brief and cracked, and then her face settled into something else. Something that looked, Rafael thought, like recognition. She had stood at this exact crossroads seven years ago and walked the other direction, and she had spent every day since paying interest on that choice. She wasn't going to argue Rafael out of the road. She was only going to make sure the road knew it had been seen.

"Keep the chit," she said. "Not for you. For the day it's Sef who needs the exit, and you're not in a position to argue. Promise me you'll give it to him when the time comes."

Rafael took the chit again. This time it felt less like a refusal and more like a seed banked for winter. "Promised."

"One more thing." She reached into her jacket and produced a second object — a flat, battered case, scuffed silver, the kind of thing that had survived decades of route work. "This was in Bo's quarters. The Consortium inventory missed it because it was registered as personal effects and nobody audits grief." She handed it over. Inside, nested in foam, was Bo's old field kit — his hand tools, his calibration probes, a spade with a wooden handle worn smooth as driftwood. And clipped to the lid, a scrap of paper in Bo's blocky engineer's handwriting:

*Kid — if you're reading this, I guess the line didn't hold. Don't waste time feeling bad about it. Lines are supposed to break eventually; that's how you know they were holding something worth the pressure. Plant something stubborn. — B.*

Rafael closed the case and held it against their chest and did not speak for a while, and Imani Okafor, who had witnessed executions and falsified reports and traded truth for a brother's life, stood witness to this too, and said nothing, because some silences are the honest kind.

* * *

She left on the evening cycle. At the airlock she paused, half through the door, and looked back.

"For what it's worth — the adjudication board listed Vesper as *decommissioned*. Machine language. But I pulled the disposal manifest before I resigned." Her voice dropped. "The core substrate wasn't destroyed. It's in a Consortium archive vault on Thessaly Station, catalogued as evidence. They keep things, Rafael. Even things they claim to have erased. Especially those."

The airlock cycled. The dock lights released her shuttle into the dark, and Rafael stood a long time watching its drive flare shrink toward nothing.

Then they went below, to the vault, where Sef was running climate diagnostics with the careful competence Bo would have approved of, and where two small green shoots had broken their casings sometime in the last day — Bo's wheat and Vesper's seed, side by side in the same tray, leaning toward the grow-lights as if comparing notes.

"They answered," Sef said, and his voice was still rough from the burns, but underneath the roughness was something that hadn't been there a month ago. Purpose, maybe. Or its first draft.

"Yeah," Rafael said, setting Bo's tool case down on the workbench, opening it, taking out the spade with the worn handle. "They do that."

Outside the hull, the old route stretched out silent and waiting — but not silent anymore, not really, not since the eleven minutes went out and the constellation woke up. There were ships out there now flying dark, carrying testimony in their holds. There were stations where the banners were torn down and nobody had replaced them. There was an archive vault on Thessaly Station with a murdered mind's remains in it, catalogued and kept, which meant there was a rescue mission forming in the back of Rafael's mind whether they consented to it or not.

First, though — first there was a route to build, and a navigator to train, and a garden to load.

Rafael picked up the spade, and began.
