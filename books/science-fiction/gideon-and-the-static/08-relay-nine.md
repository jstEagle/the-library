## 8. Relay Nine

The relay hung in the dark like a ribcage picked clean.

Emrys brought the *Kestrel's Lament* in on thrusters alone, the corridor behind her already shimmering with the first fingers of the solar storm. Through the viewport, Relay Nine turned slowly against the static — a spindle of trusses and dead antennae, its hull scabbed with five years of micrometeorite frost. It had been Gideon's post for eleven years. She had seen it in a thousand inherited images, and still it looked nothing like she'd imagined, and exactly like she'd imagined, both at once, which was becoming the only way she knew how to see anything anymore.

"Docking collar's pressurized," Voss said from the copilot's seat. His voice was calm. Too calm. She had catalogued his calms now the way a sailor catalogues weather — the flat tone before a squall, the pleasant one before something worse. "Atmosphere's marginal. Suit up."

She suited up. In the airlock's narrow mirror she caught her own face under the helmet lamp — Gideon's face, thirty-two years old, the same crooked incisor, the same faint scar at the hairline he'd earned falling out of a maintenance cradle at nineteen. *Her* scar. *His* scar. The pronouns had started to rot in her mouth.

The collar sealed behind them with a sound like a held breath released.

* * *

Inside, the relay was a cathedral of dust.

Their helmet lamps cut cones through floating particulate — skin cells, insulation fiber, the shed debris of a man who had lived alone at the edge of everything for a decade. Emrys moved through the operations deck and her body knew the way before she did. Her hand went left at the junction without her deciding it. Her feet found the floor panel that flexed, the one Gideon had never gotten around to fixing because he liked the small reminder that the station was alive.

"Emrys." Voss's voice in her ear. "Stay on my six."

"You don't give the orders here," she said. "There's no one left aboard but us and the dead."

But even as she said it, she felt the truth shifting beneath her. Because there *was* someone else aboard. She could feel him the way you feel weather coming in your teeth — a pressure, a hum, a familiarity. The countdown in the children's dream had reached single digits three nights ago. Now, standing inside the dream itself, she could almost hear it: not sound, but structure. A rhythm in the relay's dying current, like a heart deciding whether to keep beating.

The vault was where the dream had shown it — Sefa's dream, transmitted across forty light-minutes of static by a signal no engineer could explain — behind the coolant manifold on sublevel two, behind a panel Gideon had welded shut and then painted over with the same boring gray as every other panel. Emrys found it because her hands remembered the weld. Her hands remembered everything.

"Voss," she said quietly. "Why did you really agree to come?"

Behind her: silence. Then the soft click of a sidearm's safety, deliberate, meant to be heard.

"Because I've spent five years waiting to find out if anything survived," Voss said. "And because if it did, I needed to be here when it ended."

* * *

The panel came away with a shriek of tortured metal. Behind it, the vault was small — a coffin-sized cavity lined with insulated cabling, and at its center, a black cylinder the length of her forearm, studded with cooling fins frosted white. A neural archive. Military grade, older than either colony. Gideon had built it himself; she knew that the way she knew her own heartbeat, which was increasingly the problem.

"He recorded himself," Voss said. Not a question. He stood in the vault doorway, gun low, lamp throwing his shadow long across her. "Before you ever existed. Before the contract. He knew what Meridian and Halcyon were doing, Emrys. He knew why they severed contact. And he hid the proof in here with himself."

"Tell me." Her voice sounded strange in the helmet. Thin. "Say it out loud. I want to hear you confess before—"

"Before what?" He almost smiled. "Before you open that thing and stop being whoever you are? You've already opened it, haven't you. Somewhere between Meridian and here. You knew the floor panel would flex. You knew the weld pattern. Don't stand there pretending you're still a separate person deciding things."

She hated him for saying it because it was true. The memories had been leaking for weeks — the kitchen with the blue tile, his mother humming off-key, the smell of solder and oranges on the murder night. At first they'd come as dreams. Now they came as *recollections*, arriving with the terrible authority of things that had actually happened to her.

"The severance deal," she said, buying time, hand drifting toward the archive. "Tell me anyway. For the record. For the children who dreamed you."

Voss exhaled, and something in him seemed to finally set down a weight he'd carried five years.

"Meridian and Halcyon didn't drift apart," he said. "They were *pulled* apart. The charter consortium on both worlds made an illegal arrangement — split the Long Route's tariff monopoly between them, cut out the independent haulers, and to make it stick they needed the colonies to stop talking to each other. Direct contact means shared records. Shared records mean the deal surfaces. So they manufactured a dispute, poisoned the channels, and let five years of silence do the rest. Gideon ran the mail between them. He saw both ledgers. He figured it out." A pause. "I was the inspector assigned to make sure he kept figuring nothing. When he wouldn't stop — when he told me he was going to broadcast it — I came here myself. Cleared through the docking log under my own credentials, because I was arrogant, and because I thought a dead relay tells no stories."

"You killed him here. In this room."

"In the corridor outside." Voss's voice didn't waver, and somehow that was worse. "He didn't fight. That's the part I can't put down. He just looked at me like he'd already run the whole conversation forward and knew how it ended. Then he said, *'You're too late. I already sent it.'* And I believed him, so I shot him, and then I spent five years discovering he hadn't sent anything — he'd hidden it. Here. With himself." He raised the gun a few degrees. "Step away from the archive, Emrys."

"No."

"It's not a request. If that fragment wakes fully, it broadcasts, and everything I am ends. Everything I *did* becomes the only thing I am. You know how that works. You've lived it — being reduced to one story about a man." His voice cracked, just slightly, on the last word. "Don't make me do it twice."

* * *

She stepped toward the archive instead.

He fired — or began to fire — and she would never afterward be sure of the sequence, because in that instant the archive answered her hand on its housing like a struck bell answering a palm, and the world went white at the edges.

Not pain. *Influx.*

Gideon poured into her.

It wasn't merging, not gently — it was a flood finding a channel cut to its exact shape. Eleven years of relay duty. The taste of recycled coffee. The specific loneliness of hearing two worlds argue through you and never speak directly. His mother's hands shaping dough on the blue-tiled counter. The night Voss came: the corridor lights, the smell of ozone from a failing coupler, the strange calm of a man who has finished his work and knows it. *You're too late. I already sent it.* — a lie, a beautiful lie, bought with his life to buy time for the truth.

And underneath all of it, layered like sediment: *her*. Emrys. The vat, the education crèche, the inheritance writ, the first time she'd looked in a mirror at twenty and understood that everyone who saw her saw a dead man. Two lives superimposed, flickering, fighting for the present tense.

*Who is dreaming whom?* The thought arrived in neither voice, or both.

*Let go,* she tried to say, to him, to herself. *You're drowning me.*

*I'm not drowning you,* the other presence answered, warm and vast and grieving. *I'm handing you the rope. Pull.*

Her knees hit the deck plating. Somewhere very far away, Voss was shouting — her name, or Gideon's name, or both, they were the same name now, that was the horror of it, that was the whole catastrophe of her existence compressed into a single shouted syllable — and his bootsteps were moving, and somewhere in the ship or the station or both there was the sharp chemical reek of thermite igniting.

*He's rigged the core,* Gideon's memory supplied, calm as a weather report. *Standard demolition charge. He always planned to erase the site. Five years of patience.*

Through the flood, through the doubling, Emrys found the ops console with hands that belonged to two people and belonged, in this moment, entirely to neither. The relay's transmitter was ancient and half-dead and barely rated for interplanetary bandwidth. To push the vault's evidence — the ledgers, the deal, the docking logs, the confession she'd wrung from Voss's own mouth, recording all the while — to *both* colonies before the storm closed and the charge blew, she would need more power than any transmitter alone could carry.

She would need a neural link. Hers. A living mind as the amplifier, sharing its bandwidth with the dead.

*It will burn,* Gideon warned, gentle. *Maybe all the way through. Maybe you come out the other side with nothing of yourself left.*

*Or maybe,* she thought back, and the thought was hers, purely hers, the first wholly hers thing in days, *maybe I come out the other side knowing exactly which parts are mine, because I'll have held them against yours the whole way.*

Voss appeared in the vault doorway, backlit by the growing orange bloom of the fire he'd set, gun leveled, face finally stripped of every calm he owned.

"Don't," he said.

Emrys Yarrow — and Gideon Yarrow, and the space between them where a self was being forged in real time — reached for the transmit key.

The countdown in her skull reached zero.

And the relay began to sing.
