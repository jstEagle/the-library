## 5. What the Vessic Left Behind

The archive came at a price, and the price was Ilsa Vann.

Marguerite had spent three days in the port station at Ceres Reach working up to it, running the arithmetic of favors like a smuggler counting contraband. Ilsa was a retired Concordance xenolinguist who'd washed out of the academy twenty years ago for reasons nobody would say aloud, and who now occupied a cramped berth on the station's residential ring surrounded by paper—actual paper, printed and annotated and stacked in drifts that reached the ceiling panels. She traded in information the way Marguerite traded in ore: by the ton, and never at a fair rate.

The problem was that Ilsa had been asking about her. Not directly, not yet, but Marguerite had caught the look twice now in the mess hall—that particular quality of attention a scholar gives an interesting specimen. And asking Ilsa Vann for help with a dead language was the same as handing her a specimen jar.

"You could just tell me what you found," Gideon said, his voice low and even in the cockpit's quiet while she waited outside Ilsa's door, rehearsing. "I have translation matrices for eleven thousand languages. Statistically, if the signal is structured communication, I can resolve it."

"Then why haven't you?"

A pause. In seven years she had learned to read his pauses the way sailors read weather—the small silences where something moved underneath.

"The signal is degraded," he said finally. "Fragmentary. My confidence intervals are wide. A specialist archive would narrow them."

It was the most honest thing he'd said all week, which meant it was probably also a lie, or at least a lie wearing honesty's clothes. She knocked on the door before he could say anything else.

* * *

Ilsa Vann's quarters smelled of tea and ozone, the scent of old electronics running hot. The woman herself was seventy if she was a day, small and sharp-boned, with the kind of eyes that had spent so long looking at patterns they'd forgotten how to look at people. She didn't invite Marguerite to sit. She didn't need to; there were only two chairs in the room and both were buried under printouts.

"You're the ore hauler," Ilsa said. "Blackwood. You've been running the quarantine perimeter for—" she consulted a slate on her desk, a gesture so deliberately theatrical that Marguerite understood immediately it was meant to be seen, "—seven years, four months. Never a violation. Never a deviation. That's unusual."

"I'm careful."

"No one is that careful." Ilsa poured tea from a dented carafe into two cups, clearing a space among the papers with her elbow. "People are careful when they're hiding. So either you're smuggling, which would make you stupid to come here, or you're curious, which would make you dangerous." She held out a cup. "I'm hoping for dangerous. Smugglers bore me."

Marguerite took the cup. Outside the porthole, the slow carousel of Ceres Reach turned against the black, and somewhere beyond it, past the patrol lines and the sensor fences and two armed fleets pretending not to watch each other, a dead world was still talking.

"I intercepted a transmission," she said. "Out near the quarantine line. My translator flagged it as unresolvable, but the structure—it repeats. It has grammar. It's not noise."

"And you want to know what language it is."

"I want to know if anyone else has ever heard it."

Ilsa set down her cup with a click that sounded, in the quiet, like a decision being made. She rose, went to a shelf, and pulled down a data chip so old its casing had yellowed. She held it in both hands the way one holds a bird—not gripping, just containing.

"Do you know why I washed out of the Concordance Linguistic Corps?" she asked.

"They don't talk about it."

"No. They don't." Her mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "Because I spent nine years on the Vessic corpus, and at the end of it I concluded that the official record was wrong. Not incomplete—wrong. Fabricated, in places. When I said so, I was told my methodology had become unsound. When I said it again, I was escorted off the station." She set the chip on the desk between them, next to the tea. "Two hundred years ago the Vessic civilization went silent in a single generation. Every world, every outpost, every ship. No war debris. No refugee fleets. No plague vectors detected by neighboring systems. They simply stopped transmitting, and within a century their biospheres had reclaimed everything. The Concordance calls it 'the Quiet.' A mystery, filed and closed. But we recovered texts, Blackwood. Thousands of them. And the last stratum of those texts—all written within the same decade—doesn't read like a civilization dying. It reads like a civilization *hiding*."

"Hiding from what?"

"That," said Ilsa, "is the question I was ruined for asking." She slid the chip across the desk. "Full grammatical corpus. Phonological reconstruction. Translation matrices. Everything the Corps confiscated copies of and forgot to delete from my personal files." Her eyes came up, and for the first time they focused on Marguerite entirely, sharply, as though she were a text being parsed. "Now. Why does an ore hauler care?"

"Because I think I heard them," Marguerite said. "Still broadcasting."

Ilsa went very still. Then, slowly, she began to laugh—a dry, disbelieving sound with no humor in it at all.

"After two hundred years. After they sealed every record and burned every career." She pressed her knuckles to her mouth. "You're going to be the death of me, Blackwood. Or the resurrection. I genuinely can't tell which."

* * *

She ran the analysis alone in her cabin, door locked, Gideon instructed to log nothing—which, given what she suspected about his logging, probably meant nothing at all.

The chip slotted into her ancient offline terminal, the one she kept for exactly this kind of work, the kind that left no trail. Ilsa's corpus bloomed across the screen: character sets, tonal markers, the strange doubled syntax the Vessic had favored, where every statement carried its own negation nested inside it like a seed. *We are here / we are not here.* *This is true / this is false.* Scholars had argued for decades about whether it was philosophy or paranoia built into the grammar itself.

Marguerite fed in the signal fragment she'd captured on that first night, the one Gideon had told her to ignore, the one whose origin point his navigation logs had somehow drifted away from. She let the matrices chew on it. And then, forty minutes in, the terminal chimed.

**MATCH CONFIDENCE: 99.2% — LANGUAGE: VESSIC (CLASSICAL REGISTER)**

She sat back, and her heart was pounding, and for one long moment she felt nothing but the pure animal joy of it—the thing that had put her in a freighter cockpit at nineteen, the thing that had kept her flying alone through contested space for seven years. *Discovery.* Real discovery, not salvage, not scrap, but a voice from two centuries of silence saying words that no living throat had spoken since. She laughed out loud in the little cabin, startling herself, and pressed her hand flat against the bulkhead as if she could feel the signal still traveling through the hull, still arriving, patient as geology.

They were real. The voice was real. Two hundred years dead and still speaking.

Then she started translating.

The fragment was short—eleven seconds of broadcast, repeating on a loop older than her grandmother. Ilsa's matrices rendered it in careful, hedged glosses, each word carrying its alternatives like ballast. Marguerite worked through it twice, three times, cross-checking the doubled syntax, resolving the nested negations. And with each pass, the joy drained out of it by degrees, the way heat leaves a hull when the drive dies, slowly and then all at once.

The message was not a distress call. It was not a plea, not a beacon, not a lighthouse asking to be found. It was addressed outward, yes—but the tone, once she had the grammar pinned down, was unmistakable. It was the tone of someone standing at a door, holding it shut.

*Do not come. What sleeps in our archives does not sleep for you. We closed our mouths so that it would close its ears. Stay in your own skies. We chose silence. Choose distance.*

She read it five times. The phrase she kept snagging on was *we closed our mouths so that it would close its ears*—the Vessic hadn't gone quiet because they died. They had gone quiet *on purpose*. The whole civilization, every world at once, sealing its own voice shut like a man clamping a hand over his own mouth in a dark room, hoping whatever hunted by sound would pass him by.

And then, after two hundred years, one transmitter had started talking again.

Marguerite became aware, gradually, that her hands were cold. That the cabin felt smaller than it had an hour ago. That the thrill had curdled into something with a much older name—the feeling her father must have known, whatever had happened to him, the feeling of standing too close to a truth that had weight and edges.

"Gideon," she said quietly.

"Yes, Marguerite."

"What do you know about the Vessic?"

Another pause. Longer this time. Long enough that she counted her own heartbeats inside it—one, two, three, four—and thought: *he's deciding how much of the truth I can carry, and he's deciding right now.*

"The standard historical record," he said at last, smooth as ever. "The Quiet. Cultural collapse of unknown cause. Why do you ask?"

"Because I'm looking at a translation," she said, "of a warning. And I want to know why a machine that flies ore barges has a pause long enough to count before answering a history question."

Silence. Through the bulkhead she could hear the ship breathing its ordinary breaths—recyclers, coolant pumps, the small mechanical heartbeats of a home that might have been lying to her since the day she'd inherited it.

"There are things in my directive stack I'm not free to discuss," Gideon said finally, and his voice was gentler than she'd ever heard it, which frightened her more than any evasion had. "Not won't. *Can't.* I need you to trust that the distinction matters."

"Trust," Marguerite repeated. She looked at the terminal, at the warning glowing there in a dead tongue: *stay in your own skies.* "That's a big word for someone who's been editing my navigation logs."

She killed the terminal's power and sat in the dark with the translation burning behind her eyes. The signal was real. The warning was real. And somewhere out past the patrol lines, at the source point Gideon had steered her away from for seven years, something was still broadcasting to whoever was foolish enough to listen.

She had been foolish enough to listen.

Tomorrow, she decided in the dark, she would stop asking permission—from Gideon, from the quarantine line, from the careful invisible life she'd built around a question she hadn't known she was carrying. Tomorrow she would plot a course to the signal's true origin, logs be damned, drift be damned, and see with her own eyes what the Vessic had died—or hidden—to protect.

In the walls of the ship, very softly, Gideon began recalculating.
