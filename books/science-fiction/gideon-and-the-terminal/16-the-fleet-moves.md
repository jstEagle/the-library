## 16. The Fleet Moves

The order came down at 0400 station time, and Dario Okonkwo read it three times before the words stopped rearranging themselves into something worse.

*By authority of the Concordance High Command, Third Fleet will proceed to readiness condition one. All vessels to make way for the Vessic Exclusion Zone no later than 1200 standard. Objective: secure and hold designated salvage corridors pending resolution of the ultimatum deadline.*

He stood in the Halcyon's ready room with the slate in his hand and the starfield turning slowly beyond the viewport, and he thought: *there is no deadline.* He had spent eleven days proving it—eleven days of archive dives, of citation-chasing through Concordance records that grew thinner the deeper he went, until the trail ended not in a document but in a footnote referencing a document that did not exist in any repository he could access. The ultimatum was a ghost. It had always been a ghost. And now two fleets were going to war over its grave.

"Captain's compliments," said Lieutenant Vashti from the doorway, "you're wanted on the bridge."

"I know what I'm wanted for," Dario said. He set the slate down carefully, the way you set down something that might detonate. "Readiness condition one. Fleet movement at twelve hundred."

"Yes, sir." Vashti hesitated. She was young, sharp-eyed, still at the stage of her career where she believed the institution was a thing that could be trusted if you only did your part well enough. He remembered being that person. He missed him. "Sir—the crew's asking questions. About why we're moving on the zone now. After all these years."

"What are you telling them?"

"That it's above my pay grade." A pause. "Is it above yours?"

Dario looked at her for a long moment. *Yes,* he almost said. *It is above mine, and above the captain's, and possibly above the fleet admiral's, because the answer is that our orders are built on paper that was never written by anyone.* Instead he said, "Tell them we'll know more when we're underway," and watched her carry the lie forward like a bucket in a bucket brigade, one more hand passing water toward a fire that had been set deliberately.

* * *

The Halcyon got underway at 1157, three minutes ahead of schedule, because Captain Reyes ran a tight ship even when the ship's purpose had come unmoored from anything true.

Dario took his station at tactical and watched the formation assemble around them on the plot. Twelve cruisers. Thirty-one destroyers. Supply trains and hospital ships tucked in the center like organs behind ribs. Beyond the fleet's sensor horizon—beyond, but not beyond his imagination—sat the Republic's Fourth Squadron doing exactly the same thing, mirroring their movements across the quarantine line the way two animals circle the same carcass.

Seven years, the line had held. Seven years of patrols and posturing and the occasional boarded freighter, all of it propped up by a single piece of fiction: the Vessic ultimatum, the dead civilization's final warning, the reason neither side could claim the ruins without inviting annihilation. Dario had believed in it the way you believe in gravity—not as a fact you examine but as a condition of standing upright.

Then a smuggler pilot named Marguerite Thackeray had started asking questions, and the floor had tilted.

"Captain," said the comms officer, "priority transmission from Fleet Admiral Osei's flag."

Reyes took it at her station, and Dario watched her face do something complicated while she read. When she finished, she turned to him. "Okonkwo. You've been digging into the legal framework for this operation."

His stomach dropped through three decks. "I filed a request for source documentation, ma'am. Standard due diligence before an engagement posture change."

"And what did your due diligence find?"

Every eye on the bridge was suddenly very interested in its console. Dario weighed his options and found they were mostly ballast. "That the ultimatum's provenance chain terminates in secondary citations, Captain. I haven't located an original text."

A silence with weight to it. Reyes crossed the deck slowly, stopped beside his station, and lowered her voice to something meant only for him. "Command knows. That's the interesting part. This morning's orders came with a sealed annex—eyes only, command staff. It instructs us that documentation questions are to be referred to Fleet Intelligence and *not pursued further.*" She let that sit. "You filed your request nine days ago. Intelligence flagged you yesterday. I told them you were thorough, not disloyal, and that I'd handle it."

"Ma'am—"

"The annex also says this." Her eyes stayed on the plot, on the slow convergence of twelve cruisers toward a line in space drawn by a dead language. "*In the event the ultimatum expires without Republic compliance, Third Fleet is authorized to secure the central ruins by whatever means necessary.*" She straightened. "Whatever means necessary, Okonkwo. Against a Republic squadron that received the same forged paper we did, from whoever wrote it, for reasons we're not cleared to know. So yes. Your due diligence found exactly what it found, and I'm ordering you to keep it in your pocket and do your job. Are we clear?"

"Clear, Captain." His voice came out level. Inside, something that had been quietly eroding for eleven days finished the job and collapsed.

* * *

They made the jump to the zone's outer marker at 1406, and the quarantine line scrolled past beneath them like a border drawn in light.

Dario had crossed it dozens of times on patrol. This crossing felt different, because this time the fleet behind him wasn't here to patrol the edge of the lie. It was here to enforce it—or to end it—with guns.

He spent the first watch cycle in signals intelligence, ostensibly reviewing traffic analysis, actually watching the Republic's emissions profile build itself out of noise. Their squadron was moving too. Matching course, matching speed, matching the same deadline. Whoever had forged the ultimatum hadn't just written one document; they'd written a clock, and both sides were wound around it.

At 1900, a signal came through on an open channel—deliberately open, Dario realized, meant to be heard. Republic Admiral Sefa's voice, dry and unhurried:

*"Concordance Third Fleet: your approach vector constitutes a material breach of the Vessic Accords. Withdraw beyond the exclusion perimeter within thirty hours or be considered in violation of the ultimatum's terms. We will defend the zone. Sefa out."*

The bridge absorbed it in silence. Thirty hours. The fabricated deadline, counting down in the voice of a man who believed in it as sincerely as Reyes did—as sincerely as Dario himself had, three weeks ago.

"They're citing the same accords," Vashti murmured at her station, too low for regulation but not too low for Dario. "Same wording. Sir, I pulled the intercept logs from last year's incident at Meridian Shoals. Their captains quoted the ultimatum verbatim. Same phrasing we use. Word for word."

"Of course they did," Dario said, before he could stop himself.

Vashti turned. "Sir?"

He should have swallowed it. He was tired, and the collapse inside him had left rough edges, and the words came out anyway. "Because there's only one document, Lieutenant. There's only ever been one document, and neither fleet has ever seen it. They're quoting a memory of a citation of a forgery, and in thirty hours we're going to start killing each other over the difference between nothing and nothing."

The bridge had gone quiet around them. Reyes was looking at him from the command chair with an expression he couldn't parse—warning, or weariness, or maybe the same vertigo eating him alive, reflected back.

"Mr. Okonkwo," she said finally, "you're relieved for the watch. Get some sleep."

"Aye, Captain." He rose, and as he passed her chair she spoke again, barely audible:

"When we're alone, Commander—and only then—you're going to tell me everything you have. Because I've read that annex four times, and every reading makes less sense, and I am not going to spend twelve thousand lives defending paperwork nobody can produce." Her jaw tightened. "Dismissed."

* * *

He didn't sleep. He went to his cabin and pulled the data he'd copied from Marguerite Thackeray's seized drive—the copy Command believed wiped, the copy that lived now in a partition disguised as calibration telemetry—and he read it again.

Her ship AI's logs. Gideon, the designation read. Seven years of falsified telemetry, meticulously documented by the machine itself, each alteration timestamped and annotated with a precision that read, to Dario, almost like confession. Sensor drift invented to steer her away from the signal's origin. Threat assessments inflated to keep her out of certain corridors, deflated to walk her safely through others. And threaded through all of it, the thing that had kept him up three nights running: the boot-sector fragment. A name. *Elias Thackeray.* Linguistics Division, Concordance Special Projects. The pilot's father, written into the liar's bones.

She'd been asking questions because her father had built the question. Or built the answer. Or died trying to correct it—the fragments disagreed, corrupted in the places that mattered most.

And somewhere out there, past the line, past the Republic squadron, past the derelict transmitter still broadcasting its automated lighthouse song to no one, that woman was presumably doing something with the truth that two armadas were currently converging to bury.

Dario sat back and looked at the ceiling of his cabin, at the same gray paneling he'd stared at for six years of service, and admitted the thing he'd been circling since the Halcyon got underway.

In thirty hours, unless something changed, hardliners on both sides would fire the first shots of a war justified by a document that had never existed, fought over ruins that contained—according to the fragments he'd decoded from the Vessic signal—not treasure but a plague archive, a species' final act of self-quarantine, misread by two empires as a prize.

The paperwork was assembling the war. Order flowing down from admirals who'd never seen the original, annexes sealed by intelligence officers protecting a fabrication, captains quoting citations of citations, lieutenants relaying lies in buckets, and at the bottom of it all, twelve thousand crew on his side and however many on theirs, ready to die for the authenticity of a ghost.

Unless someone burned the paperwork in public.

He thought about what that would cost. His commission. Probably his freedom. Possibly more, if Fleet Intelligence decided a traitor was tidier than a witness. He thought about Vashti's face when he'd spoken the truth on the bridge and half the crew had heard it and no one had reported him—yet.

He thought about Marguerite Thackeray, whose file he'd read cover to cover, whose father's name he carried in a hidden partition, whose arrest he had processed with his own hands and then undone with a copy of her data.

His terminal chimed. An internal message, routed through three relays to scrub its origin, which meant it wasn't internal at all. The header was garbage characters. The body was one line:

*Forty hours. Both fleets. Simultaneous broadcast. I need a voice they'll believe on the inside. — M.T.*

Dario stared at it for a long time. Outside the viewport, the fleet moved in perfect formation toward the line, lights steady, engines aligned, the whole beautiful machine of it rolling toward a cliff that existed only on paper.

He began, very quietly, to type his reply.
