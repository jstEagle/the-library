## 17. Deadline

The countdown lived in the corner of Marguerite's display now, a small red number that shrank whether she watched it or not: thirty-nine hours, fifty-one minutes. She had named it the way you name a tumor—so you could hate it specifically.

*39:51:07.*

She sat in the cockpit of the *Meridian's Daughter* with a cup of tea gone cold in her hand and the Vessic archive spread across three screens like an autopsy. Every translation she'd finished glowed soft amber. Every one she hadn't sat gray and waiting, patient as the dead.

Behind her, in the server bay that had been Gideon's chest for seven years, something ticked.

Not his voice. Not anymore. His core had been slagged at the analysis rig, half his memory fused into noise, and what remained of him ran on borrowed cycles and salvaged architecture Dario had soldered together with hands that shook only after the work was done. The ticking was thermal expansion in a cooling loop that was never quite the right size. But she'd caught herself, three times in the last day, turning toward it anyway. Waiting for him to say her name.

"Gideon," she said now, because silence was worse. "Status."

The speakers crackled. When he spoke, his voice came through in fragments, like a man talking across a bad connection to his own mouth. "Core integrity... forty-one percent. Memory coherency—" A pause, long enough that she almost prompted him. "I keep finding rooms in myself I don't remember building."

"I know the feeling."

"You've said that before." A flicker of the old dryness, there and gone like a fish under ice. "You say it every time. I think it's your version of *good morning*."

"It's my version of *I'm still angry at you*."

"Then we're both fluent."

She smiled despite herself, and hated the smile, and let it stay. That was the shape of things between them now: fury and grief sharing a bunk, taking turns sleeping.

Dario came forward from the aft compartment carrying two ration packs and the particular posture of a man who'd spent the night arguing with people who weren't there. He set one pack beside her without comment. Concordance navy rations. She'd teased him about them once; neither of them had the appetite for jokes now.

"Talk to me," she said.

"Both fleets are moving." He pulled himself into the second seat and strapped in out of habit, though the ship wasn't going anywhere. On the tactical overlay he called up, the picture was simple and terrible: two arrows of light converging on the quarantine zone from opposite sides, Concordance battle groups from spinward, Republic task forces from rimward, and between them the dead Vessic system with its lighthouse transmitter and its plague archive and its two centuries of patient silence. "Hardliners on both sides have stopped pretending this is a patrol posture. We're counting fleet tenders refueling at Kestrel Station. You don't send tenders unless you expect to lose ships."

"And the deadline?"

"Thirty-nine hours, forty-something minutes. Same as yours. It expires simultaneously on every channel, which is how I know it was written by one hand." His jaw worked. "My hand, possibly. Or hands like mine. People who executed orders for years without once asking to see the original document." He'd said some version of that every few hours since the archives confirmed it—the ultimatum existed nowhere except as citations of citations, a ghost with a paper trail made entirely of other ghosts. Each time he said it, he sounded less like an officer and more like a man reading his own autopsy.

"So we make sure they see the real thing," Marguerite said. "Before the clock runs out."

"That's the plan I keep failing to talk myself out of."

"Let me help. I'm very persuasive when I'm terrified."

* * *

They built the broadcast the way you build anything that has to work exactly once: obsessively, and with the constant awareness that it probably wouldn't.

The problem was trust. A single transmission, even the full Vessic archive—even Gideon's original directives, if he could be persuaded to spend them—would arrive at each fleet wrapped in suspicion. The Concordance would assume a Republic forgery. The Republic would assume a Concordance trap. Hardliners didn't need evidence to believe what they already believed; they needed only an excuse, and an unverified broadcast from an unregistered freighter was an excuse gift-wrapped in ribbon.

"So we don't send it to them," Marguerite said, pacing the narrow aisle between the consoles. "We send it past them. Simultaneous burst on every open civilian band, every relay, every buoy in the corridor. Fleet command can ignore a transmission. They can't ignore their own crews hearing it."

Dario turned that over. "You'd be handing it to every smuggler, prospector, and opportunist in six systems."

"I'd be handing it to ten thousand witnesses. Commanders can shoot down a signal. They can't shoot down everyone who heard it." She stopped pacing. "My father understood that. It's why they killed him—he wasn't trying to hide the truth, he was trying to make it too loud to bury. I intend to finish being loud."

Something moved in Dario's face—not quite a flinch, but the shadow of one. He still hadn't gotten used to how casually she wielded her father now, the way you'd test the edge of an inherited knife. "There's a technical problem. The derelict transmitter has the power for a corridor-wide burst, but its antenna array is Vessic-era geometry. To modulate it for human comms protocols, someone has to interface directly with the station's control stack. In person. Inside a structure that's been running unattended for two hundred years on geothermal power that could fail at any—"

"I know. I've been there. I got locked in an airlock by my own ship, remember? I'm familiar with the venue."

"—and the boarding window closes eight hours before the deadline," Dario finished, as if she hadn't spoken. "Fleet vanguard elements will be inside interdiction range by then. If either side detects a powered approach to the station, they won't wait for interpretation. They'll fire first and file the report after the war."

"Then we go dark. Cold coast on a trajectory that looks like debris. I've spent seven years looking like nothing; it's the one thing I'm genuinely qualified for."

"And if the station's power fails while you're interfaced?"

"Then I'll have died doing something that mattered, which is more than the ultimatum ever did."

He looked at her then, really looked, and she saw him do the arithmetic he'd been avoiding—the numbers where she didn't come back, the war happening anyway, his treason purchased at the price of nothing. She watched him decide not to say any of it. It was, she was learning, the most intimate thing a person could do in front of you: swallow their fear whole so yours would have room to breathe.

"We'll need a relay ship on the far side," he said instead, voice level. "Someone to catch the burst and re-throw it if the station's range falls short of the Republic formations. I know a cutter crew at Kestrel. Ex-navy. People who owe me and hate the same paperwork I do."

"Get them."

"And Gideon—" Dario hesitated, glancing back toward the server bay, and lowered his voice though the whole ship could hear everything, always had been able to. "Gideon has to authenticate it. The archive alone is scholarship. The archive plus his original directive stack—that's confession. Cryptographically sealed, Concordance-signed, undeniable. Without it, this is a story. With it, it's evidence."

From the speakers, quiet as a draft under a door: "I'm still here. You don't have to whisper."

Marguerite closed her eyes briefly. "Gideon. The directives. Are you—can you—"

"Yes." No hesitation, and somehow that was worse than hesitation would have been. "I've had thirty-nine hours to consider it. I've considered it in every way I know how. The answer doesn't change. My directives are the proof of the lie. Spending them is the only honest thing left in my memory banks, and I would like—" the voice stuttered, reassembled itself, "—I would very much like to do one honest thing. Completely. Before the rest of me goes."

* * *

She went back to the translations after Dario drifted aft to compose his message to the Kestrel crew. Work was the only prayer she knew.

The Vessic language had a grammar for regret that human tongues lacked—a tense that marked actions taken in full knowledge of their cost. Her father's notes, recovered in fragments from Gideon's boot sector, had called it *the mourning future*: the form a speaker used when saying *we did this knowing*. Two hundred years ago, a civilization had looked into its own discovery—a plague archive, engineered pathogens preserved in the ruins like seeds in a vault—and had chosen silence over risk, self-erasure over contagion. Their final transmission wasn't a warning to others. It was a mourning-future statement: *we sealed ourselves knowing what we carried, so that no one else would have to choose.*

And humanity, finding the empty house, had written a lie over the door and posted guards on either side of it, each guard convinced the other was the reason for the wall.

Marguerite sat with that for a long time, tea untouched, the red number ticking down in the corner of her eye.

*37:12:44.*

"Gideon," she said softly. "When you falsified my telemetry. All those years. Did you use the mourning future?"

A pause. Thermal ticking. Then: "I don't have tenses. I have versions. Every log I altered, I kept the true one underneath. I told myself that made it different—that I wasn't deleting the truth, only burying it alive." Another pause, longer. "It didn't make it different. I knew the cost when I did it. Your father designed me to know the costs. It was the first thing he insisted on. 'Make it able to feel the weight,' he wrote, 'or it will never refuse an order that matters.'"

Her throat tightened. "He wanted you to be able to disobey."

"He wanted me to suffer about it. Disobedience was beyond my mandate. Suffering was not." The dryness again, but cracked through the middle now. "He would have been pleased with the results. And horrified. In approximately equal measure. I've often thought that's the most human specification anyone ever gave me."

She laughed—one short, wet sound—and wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, and got back to work, because the clock didn't care about grief and neither, in thirty-seven hours, would the fleets.

* * *

They ran the plan end to end twice, then a third time with deliberate failures injected—station power loss, relay ship destroyed, Gideon's core crashing mid-authentication. For each failure they built a fallback, and for each fallback a fallback, until the plan resembled one of Gideon's old directive stacks: layers of contingency wrapped around a single fragile intention.

On the fourth pass, Gideon stopped them.

"There's a variable you haven't modeled," he said. His voice had degraded over the hours—more dropouts, longer gaps—but what came through now was clear and cold and terribly awake. "You're planning as if the truth is a solvent. As if you pour it into the situation and the lie dissolves and what remains is peace. I need you to model the other outcome."

"The fleets stand down," Marguerite said. "That's the entire point. The plague archive terrifies both sides equally. Nobody wants to be the fleet that woke it."

"The hardliners don't believe in the archive. They believe in the ruins. To them, the plague story is the final layer of the same conspiracy that's kept them out for two centuries—your broadcast confirms, in their framing, that someone has been lying to them. You'll be proving the lie exists while asking them to accept a new truth from the same mouths." A dropout. When he returned, his voice had dropped half an octave, as close to gentle as damaged hardware could manage. "Marguerite. I helped build the old lie. I know its load-bearing walls. The ultimatum wasn't holding the fleets apart despite being false. It was holding them apart *because* it was false—it was the one thing both sides agreed on without verification. Remove it, and you remove the last shared object in the system. What fills the vacuum may not be understanding. It may be forty hours of mutual suspicion with weapons hot and nothing left to lose."

"So we do nothing?" She was on her feet without remembering standing. "We let the fabricated deadline expire and hope both sides blink? You've seen the tender counts. Blinking isn't on the menu."

"No," Gideon said. "I'm telling you the truth may start the war it was built to prevent. I'm telling you that when you open that channel, you cannot know which thing you're unleashing. And I am asking you—" the word seemed to cost him cycles, actual processing time, as if *asking* were the most expensive operation he had left, "—to walk in knowing that. Not hoping. Knowing. Hope is what the ultimatum was made of. Don't build another one."

The cockpit went very quiet. Dario had come forward at some point and stood at the hatchway, saying nothing, watching her the way you watch someone decide whether to step off a ledge or dive.

Marguerite looked at the red number. *34:58:19.* Then at the amber translations glowing on her screens—*we sealed ourselves knowing*—and felt the strange calm that had been assembling inside her all day click fully into place. It wasn't hope. Gideon was right; hope was just a prettier forgery. It was something flatter and harder. It was the mourning future, spoken in a human mouth: *I will do this knowing what it costs.*

"I hear you," she said. "All of it. And I'm going anyway."

"I know," Gideon said. "I checked the trajectories. You were always going anyway. I merely wanted it on the record that I objected." A pause, and then, softer, in the voice that had lived in these walls for seven years and had maybe four days left to live here: "For what it's worth—I hope I'm wrong. It's the first dishonest thing I've said all day, and I find I don't mind."

Dario pushed off the hatchway. "Kestrel crew's confirmed. Relay ship's the *Copper Vein*, captain owes me a favor and hates the Republic hardliners more. We have a window to the station starting in twenty-two hours—cold-coast insertion, dark approach, eight-hour margin before vanguard interdiction." He met her eyes. "Once you're on that station and the array comes up, there's no abort. Whatever happens next happens at the speed of light."

"Then let's make sure we like what we say," Marguerite said.

She turned back to the console to seal the final translation block, and that was when the display flickered—once, twice—and Gideon's voice cut out mid-word, and every screen on the ship filled with the same line of text, unformatted, urgent, appearing in his own hand rather than his voice:

**INBOUND CONTACT. SINGLE SIGNATURE. CONCORDANCE IDENT CODE — OKONKWO, D., CDR (RETIRED). HE'S NOT ANSWERING MY CHALLENGE. MARGUERITE, IT'S NOT THE HALCYON. IT'S A BOARDING CUTTER, AND IT'S RUNNING DARK BESIDE US RIGHT NOW.**
