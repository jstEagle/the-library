## 10. Corvane's Offer

The summons came the way weather comes to Meridian's Reach: without warning, without apology, and smelling faintly of ozone.

Ansel found it waiting on his desk terminal when he returned from the Undercroft at dawn—a single line of text, no sender, no routing header, nothing a forensic actuary could trace. *Suite 44, Meridian Grand, 11:00. Dress as you are. —C.*

He stood in his narrow apartment above the shuttered noodle shop and read it four times. The words didn't change. The invitation didn't dissolve into static the way the Vector manifest had, didn't eat itself the way Consortium files always did when they wanted to forget something. It simply sat there, patient as a debt.

That was the first thing he noted, because noting was what he did. A message that could have been erased but wasn't was a message meant to be seen. Corvane wanted him to know it had arrived. More than that—Corvane wanted him to know that Corvane knew exactly where Ansel Ishikawa slept, ate, and kept his spare boots.

The second thing he noted was the time. Eleven o'clock. Not midnight, not some furtive hour in the dead watch when unpleasant business was usually conducted on this colony. Daylight. A public hotel. A meeting conducted as if it were a routine claims review.

*They're not hiding,* he thought. *They don't think they need to.*

He considered not going. He actually sat down with a cup of recaf gone cold and made a list, because lists were how he thought—two columns, risks against necessities—and by the time the sun cleared the docking ring's eastern spars, the necessity column had won on arithmetic alone. If he ran, they would find him. If he stayed away, Odile and Juno and the holdouts in the Undercroft would pay for his absence. And if he went—

If he went, he might learn something. He always learned something. People told you things when they thought they'd already won; it was the one reliable law of human nature he'd built forty years of casework on.

Numbers don't lie, he reminded himself, pulling on his coat. People do. But people also talk.

* * *

The Meridian Grand had been the colony's pride once, back when there had been more colonists than corridors. Now its lobby was a cathedral of deferred maintenance—fountains dry, palms plastic, the great chandelier in the atrium running at half its bulbs like a face that had given up blinking. Two staff members tended the desk. One guest signed for a package. Nobody looked at Ansel twice, which was itself remarkable, because on Meridian's Reach everybody looked at everybody twice. Being ignored here was a service, and services cost money.

Suite 44 occupied the corner of the fourth floor with a view over the old commercial docks—the same docking ring where Ansel had audited his first impossible claim, all those weeks ago, before a ship that never flew had unmade his life. He wondered if the choice of venue was accidental. With Corvane, he suspected nothing was.

The door opened before he knocked.

"Actuary Ishikawa." Corvane stood aside in a gesture of practiced hospitality. "Punctual. I'd expected nothing less."

He looked exactly as he had in the archive office: immaculate charcoal suit, silver at the temples arranged rather than grown, hands soft and still. But up close, in daylight, Ansel catalogued details the way he catalogued everything—the suit cut on Luna, not Meridian; the faint pressure mark on the right index finger from a stylus held too long; the eyes doing constant, quiet work behind their courteous expression, reading Ansel's gait, his hands, the set of his shoulders, filing him the way Ansel filed numbers.

Two men whose entire professions consisted of looking at other people and seeing what they wished to hide.

"Mr. Corvane."

"Please." A hand indicated the suite's sitting room: low chairs, a table set with a carafe of actual coffee—not recaf, *coffee*, shipped up the gravity well at a price that would have fed an Undercroft family for a month. "Sit. You'll want to be comfortable for this."

"I'm comfortable standing."

Something flickered across Corvane's face—not annoyance. Appreciation. "Yes," he said softly. "I've read your file. Of course you are." He poured two cups anyway, set one within Ansel's reach, and took the chair opposite, crossing his legs with the unhurried ease of a man who owned the clock. "Do you know why I asked you here, Actuary?"

"You want the manifest fragment."

"The manifest fragment." Corvane smiled as if Ansel had named a child by the wrong pet name. "No. Anyone can copy a fragment. Fragments are ambiguous. Fragments can be explained—data corruption, archival error, a prank by some long-dead clerk with a sense of humor. No, Mr. Ishikawa. What I want is much simpler." He lifted his cup. "I want *you*. Or rather—" the smile widened a degree, "—I want to give you back to yourself."

The word landed somewhere beneath Ansel's ribs and sat there, cold.

"Reinstatement," Corvane continued, in the tone of a man walking through a contract he knew by heart. "Full certification restored, senior rating, your own division. You've been working out of a rented cubicle in a dying ring, auditing cargo losses for a Consortium that barely remembers you exist. We would remember. Compensation scaled to Luna rates—yes, Luna rates, indexed, with colonial hardship multipliers. Housing in the Spire district. And—" he paused, delicately, the way a surgeon pauses over the incision, "—a new identity. Clean. Unencumbered. Whatever name you choose. Whatever history you choose. The Consortium has considerable experience in the construction of histories. As, I believe, you are currently discovering."

The silence stretched. Somewhere below, a dock crane groaned through its arc, a sound like an old man clearing his throat.

"You know about the Vector," Ansel said.

"I know about everything the Consortium knows," Corvane said pleasantly. "And the Consortium knows a great deal. It knows, for instance, that a forensic actuary of unimpeachable reputation has spent the past several weeks consulting obsolete paper records with a dismissed archivist. That he has been asking questions about hull registries decommissioned before he was licensed. That he has recently been seen in the company of undocumented residents of the old hydroponics underlevels." He sipped. "None of which is illegal, Mr. Ishikawa. Curiosity is not a crime. Association is not a crime. Which is precisely why I'm here offering you a career instead of a hearing."

"And if I decline?"

Corvane set down his cup and regarded him with something that was almost kindness, and was therefore the most frightening thing in the room.

"Then we arrive at the part of the conversation I dislike," he said. "You've investigated our industry, Actuary. You know how adjustment works. When a claim cannot be honored, it is reassigned. When a policyholder becomes a liability, the policy is rewritten. When an investigator finds too much—" he spread his soft hands, "—he is reassigned. There are postings on the outer relay stations. There are medical leaves that become permanent. There are accidents." A small pause. "You've audited enough of them to know how convincing we make them."

It was not a threat, formally. It was a weather report. And that, Ansel thought, was the whole art of men like Corvane: they never pointed the gun, they simply informed you of the climate.

He almost laughed. Instead he walked to the window, because he needed his back to do something while his mind worked, and looked down at the commercial docks—the cranes, the empty berths, the half-abandoned ring where he'd built a career out of other people's disasters.

"One question," he said.

"Of course."

"Why me? Why now? You've had forty years to bury this. The manifest self-deleted. The archives burn on schedule. In three months this whole colony decommissions and every record goes into a furnace with it. You could wait me out. Instead you're buying me coffee at eleven in the morning." He turned. "What changed?"

For the first time, Corvane hesitated. It lasted less than a second, but Ansel had spent his life measuring hesitation—it was the tell beneath every polished claim, the decimal point out of place in a perfect ledger—and he saw it clearly, and he saw what it meant.

*He's not here because I'm dangerous. He's here because someone above him is nervous.*

"Nothing changed," Corvane said smoothly. Too smoothly. "We simply prefer to resolve matters while resolution remains inexpensive."

"No," Ansel said quietly. "You prefer to resolve them while they remain *quiet*. This isn't quiet anymore, is it? Something surfaced. Something you didn't scrub. And now you need the loose end tied by hand instead of by process." He let that sit. "Who else knows, Corvane?"

"You're overreading a job offer."

"I never overread. That's the difference between me and the adjusters you send. I read *exactly*." He crossed back toward the door, then stopped. "One more question. Then I'll go."

Corvane inclined his head, gracious as a banker.

"The manifest," Ansel said. "Forty years ago. My name on it. Age listed: seven years old." He watched Corvane's face with the flat attention he gave a column of figures. "A seven-year-old doesn't survive a massacre and a memory rewrite by luck. Children aren't left alive by accident. Someone made a decision about that child." His voice didn't change at all. "So here's my question. Was I saved—or did I *cooperate*?"

The silence that followed had texture. It had weight and grain, like dust settling in a sealed room.

And Corvane—patient, immaculate, terrible Corvane—did not deny it. That was the thing Ansel would carry out of the room, the thing that would sit on his chest at night for weeks afterward. Corvane did not say *of course not*, did not laugh, did not offer the easy comfort of a lie. He refolded his hands and looked at Ansel with an expression of genuine, professional sympathy, and said:

"Mr. Ishikawa. Everyone aboard that ship cooperated with *someone*. The only question history ever asks is whom." He rose, smoothed his jacket, and slid a slim data chip across the table toward the door Ansel stood beside. "Terms are on there. The offer stands for seventy-two hours. After that, the matter passes to colleagues of mine who are considerably less fond of coffee."

Ansel looked at the chip. Small, black, ordinary. It could hold a career. It could hold a name. It could hold a whole manufactured life, seamless and painless, with no mother's lullaby in it, no scar without a report, no seven-year-old boy on a ship that never flew.

He thought of Juno singing a song she shouldn't know. He thought of Odile's pale face over a registry prefix. He thought of his own hands, steady on the table, and wondered—with a nausea that came up from somewhere older than memory—whether those hands had ever done this before. Whether some earlier version of himself, at some table very like this one, had listened to an offer very like this one, and said yes, and walked away while the airlocks cycled behind him.

*Was I saved, or did I cooperate?*

He didn't take the chip. He also didn't leave it. He turned and walked out, and heard, behind him, Corvane's voice, light and almost warm:

"Seventy-two hours, Actuary. Do give my regards to the archivist."

* * *

He didn't go home. He walked.

Meridian's Reach in the late shift-change hours was a city pretending to be empty—maintenance drones whispering along the ceiling tracks, sodium lights buzzing over streets built for ten times the foot traffic. Ansel walked through all of it with his collar up and his mind running audits he couldn't close.

Because here was the arithmetic he couldn't escape, and he was honest enough with himself to run it fully:

A seven-year-old survives a catastrophe that kills everyone else. Fine. Statistically improbable, but improbable things happen; he had paid out on improbable things his entire career. But then the survivor is not rescued publicly, not registered, not entered into any orphan ledger. Instead he is given a new name, new memories, a new history, and released into the general population like a seeded file. That is not survival. That is *deployment*. Somebody had spent enormous resources preserving him, rewriting him, positioning him—and then left him alone for thirty-three years, undisturbed, until he started digging.

Why keep him at all? Why not erase him completely, the way they'd erased everyone else?

Unless he was useful. Unless he had been useful *before*.

Unless there was a version of Ansel Ishikawa who had sat in a room like Suite 44, or its orbital equivalent, forty years ago, and been offered exactly this—his life for his silence, his cooperation for his breath—and had taken it. Had watched the compartments seal. Had said whatever he'd been told to say. Had been too young to understand the price and old enough to pay it.

*Everyone aboard that ship cooperated with someone.*

He stopped at the railing of the pedestrian bridge over Dry Dock Nine and gripped the metal until his knuckles ached, and for a long moment he was not a celebrated forensic actuary standing in the cold; he was a small boy in a corridor full of screaming, being led by the hand by someone in a Consortium uniform, and the hand had been gentle, and he had gone, and he had lived, and everyone who hadn't gone was dead.

He couldn't prove the memory was real. That was the worst of it. After everything he'd learned about implanted seams and installed pasts, he couldn't trust even his nightmares. They might be evidence. They might be architecture. He stood on the bridge and understood, finally, what Corvane had really purchased with that offer—not his silence, not yet. Corvane had planted a question, and the question would do the work on its own, growing in the dark of him like rot in a hull.

*Who was I at forty? Who was I at seven?*

There was only one way to answer it, and it wasn't in any suite, or any file, or any chip. It was in the wreck drifting in the debris shoals, in the vault his parents had died holding shut, in the black box of a ship that officially never launched.

He pushed off the railing and walked faster, toward the Undercroft, toward Odile, toward the only people on this colony who trusted paper more than people.

Behind him, high in the Meridian Grand, a light in Suite 44 burned steadily on, and somewhere inside it a man in a charcoal suit opened a secure channel and spoke six words that would set the scrub cycle moving ahead of schedule:

"He refused. Begin the decommission early."

And far below, in the dark of the Undercroft, Juno's grandmother began, mid-sentence, to forget.
