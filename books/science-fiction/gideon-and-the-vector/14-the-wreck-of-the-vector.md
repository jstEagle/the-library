## 14. The Wreck of the Vector

The Vector hung in the debris shoals like a question no one had asked in forty years.

She was a colony ship of the old Kestrel Line pattern—two kilometers of spine, habitat rings collapsed inward like a fist closing around something precious. Her hull had been silver once. Now it was the gray of bone left in weather, pitted by micrometeorites, furred along one flank with a bloom of frozen propellant that caught the light of Meridian's Reach's distant sun and threw it back in a faint, dirty rainbow.

Ansel stood at the tug's viewport with his palm flat against the cold glass and felt something in his chest he had no professional vocabulary for.

"That's her," Sefa said. She hadn't slept since the debris field. Her voice was scraped raw from the transit, from the years before it. "Registry prefix K-V. You can still see it on the bow if the sun hits right."

It hit right. The letters emerged out of the shadow like a name being spoken.

*Vector.*

His own handwriting was steadier than his hands. In his case file—the real one now, the one he kept on paper because paper couldn't be edited while he slept—he wrote: *Subject vessel located. Condition consistent with catastrophic decompression followed by forty years of vacuum exposure. No salvage markers. No memorial buoys. No record.*

No record. As if two thousand people had simply stopped.

"Docking collar on ring three is intact," Odile said from the nav station. She'd been cross-referencing the original schematics against what the scopes showed, and her voice had the careful flatness she used when she didn't trust herself to feel anything yet. "If the internal bulkheads held—and they were built to hold—we can walk most of the spine."

"Won't be pressurized," Sefa said.

"No."

"Then we suit up." Sefa looked at Ansel, and there was something in her face he'd only seen in mirrors: a person standing at the edge of a thing they'd spent their whole life walking around. "You ready?"

He wasn't. He said yes anyway, because readiness was a fiction and he had spent his career distinguishing useful fictions from useless ones.

* * *

The airlock cycled, and Ansel stepped into the dark.

His helmet lamp cut a cone through the blackness and found—a corridor. Ordinary. Handrails at waist height. Deck plating scuffed in the pattern of forty years of foot traffic, worn pale down the center line where thousands of steps had polished the metal. A child's height marks stenciled beside a hatchway: *MEASUREMENT DAY — RING 3 COMMONS — ALL AGES WELCOME.*

Someone had drawn a small sun next to the stencil. It was still there. Forty years in hard vacuum, and the little crayon sun was still there, faded but stubborn, and Ansel stood looking at it for longer than the mission clock allowed.

"You okay?" Juno's voice in his ear. They'd argued about bringing her; she'd won, the way twelve-year-olds win arguments, by being right. Someone born after the erasure deserved to see what had been erased. Odile had backed her. So here she was, breathing recycled air three meters behind him, her lamp beam trembling slightly against the wall.

"I'm fine," Ansel said, which was the first lie he'd told her, and he hated how easily it came.

They moved up the spine toward the records vault. That was the mission. That was all this was: recovery of evidence, chain of custody, the methodical work of proof. He recited the procedure to himself like a rosary. *Document. Don't disturb. Photograph everything.* Numbers don't lie. People don't either, not really—not their bodies, not the things their hands leave behind.

His body knew this corridor.

That was the thing he could not put in the case file. His boots found the deck seams without his eyes guiding them. At the junction where the corridor split toward hydroponics, his hand went left to the rail before his mind had decided to turn, and when he forced himself straight instead, some deep animal part of him registered the wrongness the way you register a stair that isn't there—a lurch, a missed step in the dark.

"Ansel?" Odile again. "You stopped."

"Muscle memory," he said. "I'm navigating."

"By what?"

He looked down the left-hand passage, toward hydroponics, toward whatever was growing or had grown or had died there forty years ago, and heard himself say: "By home."

Nobody answered. The comms stayed open and silent, four people breathing, and then Sefa said quietly, "Follow it. We're behind you."

* * *

He did not follow it. Not yet. Discipline first; the vault was the mission, and grief kept. But he logged the junction, photographed the crayon sun, marked the corridor on his slate, and told himself the detour would keep the way grief keeps—which is to say, badly, and not for long.

The vault was where the schematics said it would be: mid-spine, armored, buried under three decks of habitat structure so that even if the rings failed, the heart of the ship would survive. Kestrel Line built their colony ships like promises. *Everything you need to remember will still be there when you arrive.*

The vault door was sealed, and around the seal, someone had welded a barricade—from the inside.

Ansel's lamp traced it slowly. Bulkhead plate, cargo netting, sections of interior frame torn free and fused across the door in a lattice meant to hold against cutting tools, against breaching charges, against time itself. And welded into the lattice, positioned with deliberate care at eye level, a placard. Standard issue, shipboard aluminum, the kind that pointed you to the mess hall or the lifeboats.

This one had been rewritten. Scratched deep into the metal with something harder than a stylus—maybe a welding tip, maybe a piece of broken rail:

**THE RECORD OUTLIVES US. HOLD THE DOOR.**

Below it, smaller, almost apologetic, a second line:

**IF YOU ARE READING THIS, YOU ARE LATE. FORGIVE US. WE COULDN'T WAIT.**

Odile made a sound that was not quite a word. Juno had gone very still. Even Sefa, who had flown this ship and abandoned it burning, said nothing at all.

"They knew," Ansel said. His voice sounded strange inside the helmet, flattened, official, the voice of a man reading an actuarial table. "They knew the Consortium tugs would come. They sealed the vault and they—" He stopped. Recalculated. "How many people does it take to weld a barricade like this?"

"Six," Sefa said. "Maybe eight. Working in shifts, in suits, with the ship dying around them." A pause. "I was on the other side of that seal. I got the order to pull the flight crew back to the shuttle bay and I obeyed it, and they were back here doing *this*."

"We're going to need the plasma cutter," Ansel said. "And we're going to need to do it carefully. Everything behind this door is evidence."

"Yes," Odile said softly. "Everything behind this door is *people*."

Both things were true. That was the lesson of the whole rotten colony, maybe of every colony: the evidence and the dead were never separate ledgers. He keyed the cutter's ignition and began, line by slow line, to unmake the last defense those strangers had built, honoring it by dismantling it, and if his hands shook inside the gloves, the gloves were thick enough to hide it.

* * *

The vault opened at 0340 ship-time, and the past exhaled.

Not air—vacuum doesn't exhale—but something like it: dust, forty years of it, lifting in slow galaxies around their lamps as the pressure equalized and the disturbed particles drifted. Ansel stepped through the breach into a room the size of a chapel, and his lamp found shelves. Physical media. Crystal lattice storage, tape spools, printed charter folios in sealed cases—an entire civilization's paperwork, preserved by people who trusted paper more than power, exactly as Odile always had.

"It's intact," Odile breathed. She was already moving down the shelves, reading labels, her gloved fingers hovering a centimeter above each case without touching, reverence as technique. "Radiation-shielded, temperature-stable—it's *intact*, Ansel. Four decades and it's intact."

"Then they succeeded," Juno said.

Ansel turned toward her voice and found her standing at the far wall, where the vault's designers had done something he hadn't expected: they'd left space. A whole empty panel, blank metal, and on it, in marker that had faded to the color of weak tea, names. Hundreds of them. Columns of handwriting, different hands, added over hours or days—the crew and passengers who had worked on the vault's defenses writing themselves onto the wall beside the records they died holding.

A memorial wall, built in advance, by people who knew no one would come.

Juno's lamp moved down the columns. "These are the manifest names," she said. "From your file. Some of these are—Ishikawa. There are Ishikawas here, Ansel."

He crossed the room the way you cross a room when the floor is made of glass and the glass is already cracking.

He found them in the third column, written in a careful, unhurried hand, the letters of someone who had taken the time to make each one legible for whoever might eventually read it:

**MARA ISHIKAWA — VAULT KEEPER**
**TOMO ISHIKAWA — VAULT KEEPER**

Beneath them, in the same hand, a third line:

**OUR SON WAS OFF-SHIP ON SCHOOL ROTATION. IF HE EVER COMES HOME, TELL HIM THE LULLABY WAS TRUE. TELL HIM WE KEPT IT ALL.**

Ansel did not remember falling. He was simply on his knees, glove pressed flat against the wall between the two names, and the sound he was making was not dignified and he could not stop it and he discovered, with the distant clarity of the forensic mind that had carried him his whole life, that he did not want to stop it. This was the exact shape of the loss. Here. Measurable. Two names, one message, one wall. For forty years the loss had been an absence, a rounding error, a population figure declining faster than any record admitted. Now it had dimensions. Now it had handwriting.

Mara. Tomo. He said the names aloud inside his helmet just to hear them exist somewhere other than scratched aluminum. His mother's voice lived in Juno's grandmother's broken lullaby. His father's hand had welded the barricade he'd just cut through. He had walked their corridors guided by their floor plan printed into his own nerves, and they had spent their final hours making sure that someday, whenever he came—if he ever came—there would be something left to give him.

*Tell him the lullaby was true.*

"He was off-ship," Sefa said gently. She had come to stand beside him, and her reflection ghosted across the wall's dull metal next to the names. "School rotation. That's why you weren't on the casualty list. That's why they had to rewrite you instead of bury you—you were the loose end. A survivor who could remember."

"And I let them," Ansel said. The words came out level, which was somehow worse. "Forty years. Whatever they installed, whatever they took—I've been living their version."

"You were a child."

"I was an actuary of my own life and I never audited it." He laughed, and it came out wrong. "Numbers don't lie. I tell people that. I never once ran the numbers on myself."

Odile knelt beside him. She didn't touch the wall—she touched his shoulder, one firm press, archivist's pressure, the pressure of a woman who handled fragile things for a living and knew exactly how much force they could bear. "Then we audit it now," she said. "All of it. Every name on this wall gets read back into the record. Starting with yours."

They worked for six hours.

It was the strangest labor of Ansel's life: cataloguing a tomb that was also a treasury. They photographed every shelf, every case, every column of names. They located the charter folios Odile had predicted—the original land grants, the gene-bank registries, the shareholder instruments—and sealed them in transport cases with the tenderness due to both evidence and inheritance. Juno copied the memorial wall by hand into her notebook, tracing each letter, saying each name under her breath so that the names were spoken twice: once in marker forty years ago, once in a child's voice now.

And Ansel walked the corridors.

He could not help it. Between cataloguing shifts, he followed the passages his body insisted on, and the ship gave him back his childhood in fragments. The commons where the height marks were measured. The observation blister where someone had etched a star chart into the window frame, teaching navigation to anyone who cared to look. Hydroponics bay two, where the grow-racks stood empty and rusted and he knew—he *knew*, without source, without citation—that the tomatoes had been planted in crooked rows because a five-year-old had helped and no one had corrected him.

At the end of the sixth hour he stood in a narrow cabin, bunk folded, a single toy ship still tethered to a shelf by a rotted cord, and understood that this had been his room. Small. Port side. He could hear, in the silence behind his own heartbeat, a woman's voice singing a melody that Juno's grandmother had carried broken across four decades of dementia and delivered, intact at the core, to a girl born after the erasure.

The lullaby was true. They had kept it all.

When he returned to the vault, the others had finished the inventory, and Odile was standing very straight over the central archive table, her lamp fixed on a folio she had set apart from the others. Her face, seen through the visor, had gone the particular pale he'd learned to read as *this changes everything.*

"Ansel," she said. "There's a second manifest in here. Sealed. Charter-grade authentication." She looked up at him. "It's not a passenger list. It's something else entirely."

Behind them, through the breach they'd cut in the door that eight people had died holding, the tug's comm crackled: proximity alert. Something moving in the debris field. Something with a Consortium transponder code.

Ansel picked up the folio, and the wreck of the Vector seemed to lean closer around them, listening.
