## 15. Black Box

The wreck's spine ran aft like a vertebrae of dead metal, and Sefa Reyes followed it the way you follow a scar on your own body—by touch, by memory, by the sick certainty that you already know what's underneath.

Her suit lights carved a tunnel through the dark. Forty years of frozen condensation hung in the corridor like a field of grey stars, and when her glove brushed the bulkhead, the frost came away in perfect little spirals, undisturbed since the day the air left. Nobody had walked here. Nobody had been allowed to.

Behind her, Ansel's breathing rasped over the suit channel. Odile was back at the tug with Juno, running the tether relays, and every few seconds the girl's voice crackled through—*signal strength nominal, signal strength nominal*—like a prayer repeated until it became true.

"Flight deck's four compartments forward," Sefa said. Her voice sounded wrong inside the helmet. Older than she remembered it. "I could walk this route asleep."

"You did walk it once," Ansel said quietly. "That's rather the point."

She didn't answer. She put one boot in front of the other and let the ship do the talking.

Because the Vector *did* talk, if you knew how to listen. The hull creaked in long, slow groans as thermal shadow crossed it—the same voice it had had the night she brought her down. Every deck plate had a pitch. Compartment Seven sang flat; the ladder wells rang hollow where the insulation had burned away. She'd known this ship for eleven months and thirty-one days. She'd flown her for six hours.

And then she'd sealed her people into the dark and saved herself, and forty years later she could still hear the sound the inner hatch made when it locked.

*Thunk.* Magnetic dogs engaging. A sound like a full stop at the end of a sentence that should have kept going.

"Compartment Six," she said aloud, because saying it made it real and real was what she'd promised these people she would be. "That's where they were."

Ansel's light swung toward a sealed pressure door. The Consortium stencil was still legible under the frost: **C-6 — PASSENGER QUARTERS — OCCUPANCY 214**.

Two hundred and fourteen. She'd read that number off the manifest herself, standing on the flight deck, while the tugs closed in and the comms filled with a voice so calm it could only have been rehearsed.

*"Vector, this is Meridian Approach Control. You are in violation of Charter Directive Nine. Power down and prepare for boarding."*

They hadn't boarded. They'd never intended to board.

"Sefa." Ansel's voice, gentle in the way of a man who made his living finding the exact weight of other people's grief. "We can stop."

"No," she said. "No, we can't. That's the whole point of us."

* * *

The flight deck door was jammed half-open, crusted ice sealing the gap like candle wax. Sefa went through sideways, and there it was: the cockpit, exactly as she'd left it, preserved in cold like a fly in amber.

Her seat still held the impression of her body. Forty years, and the foam had never recovered, because no one had ever sat in it again. She stared at that shallow dent shaped like a younger woman and felt something in her chest come loose and start to bleed in a place blood wasn't supposed to reach.

The console was dark, of course. Dead as everything else. But the emergency recorder housing was where it had always been—a black box the size of a loaf of bread, bolted behind an armored panel beneath the pilot's station, rated to survive reentry, explosion, and apparently four decades of being deliberately forgotten.

Ansel drifted up beside her. "Can you get it?"

"I can get it." She pulled the extraction kit from her thigh pouch. "Consortium standard mount. Same as on every freighter I've stolen since." She laughed, and it came out broken. "You learn a lot of useful things after you help kill two hundred people."

"You didn't kill them."

"I obeyed the order that killed them. On Meridian's Reach those are different crimes. Out here—" she tapped the hull, and the whole dead ship seemed to ring with it, "—out here I'm not sure there's a difference."

The panel bolts fought her. Four decades of thermal cycling had welded them nearly solid, and she worked them in silence, wrench clicking, breath fogging the inside of her visor, and all the while the memory played behind her eyes whether she wanted it or not. It always played. That was her sentence: perfect recall, courtesy of the same implant standard the Consortium used on its pilots. They'd given her a memory that couldn't be edited.

Which was ironic. Hilarious, even. Because everyone else aboard had their past rewritten, but Sefa Reyes got to keep hers, whole and high-definition, forever.

*Six hours into the flight. Alarm cascade. The tugs coming out of the dark with their grapples hot. And then Approach Control again, that warm, reasonable voice:*

*"Vector, your hull integrity is compromised. Seal all internal bulkheads immediately. This is a safety directive. Comply."*

*And she had looked at the board and seen the truth written in pressure telemetry: nothing was compromised. Nothing was breached. The "safety directive" was a butcher's diagram. Compartments One through Eight held the passengers. Compartments Nine and Ten—the flight deck and engineering—were where the crew stood.*

*Seal all internal bulkheads. Cut the passengers off from the air, from the boats, from the flight deck. Turn the ship into eight separate coffins and leave two rooms alive.*

*She had eleven seconds to decide. She'd spent ten of them hoping she was wrong.*

*Then she sealed the bulkheads.*

The last bolt gave way with a shriek. The panel swung free, and there it was: the recorder, scuffed, intact, its status ring glowing a faint, patient amber even now. Still listening. Still holding everything.

Sefa reached in and lifted it out with both hands, the way you lift a sleeping child or an unexploded shell. There wasn't much difference, tonight.

"That's it?" Ansel asked. "Everything?"

"Flight data, cabin telemetry, comms logs, my voice on the open channel for the last nineteen minutes." She turned it over. The amber light reflected in her visor. "Including the part where Approach Control ordered me to seal the bulkheads. Including the part where I said *copy, complying*. My voice, Ansel. Saying yes."

He was quiet a moment. "The order is theirs. The compliance is yours. Both are true."

"And which one does the colony care about? Which one does Juno's grandmother care about?" She hugged the recorder to her chest. "Two hundred and fourteen people died because a woman with a hundred hours on type did what she was told. That's not a footnote. That's the whole story. The Consortium will use it—they'll say the disaster was crew error, pilot panic, one frightened girl who sealed the wrong doors. My testimony doesn't just convict them. It hands them their defense."

"It also proves the order existed," Ansel said. "A defense requires a crime. Right now, officially, there isn't even a crime. There's no ship, no flight, no massacre. You're not handing them a defense, Sefa. You're handing the dead a witness."

She looked at him. In the helmet, in the dark, lit only by the recorder's patient amber glow, he looked like a man reading a ledger and finding his own name in it—and choosing, against every instinct of self-preservation, not to look away.

"When we play this," she said, "everyone hears me say *complying*. Odile. Juno. The whole colony. Whatever's left of my name gets buried under that word."

"Yes."

"You're supposed to tell me it'll be all right."

"I'm a forensic actuary," Ansel said. "Lying about the numbers is the one thing I've never learned how to do. But I'll tell you this: the numbers don't lie, and neither do you. And forty years from now, when someone asks who stood up, the record will show your voice saying *yes* to a monstrous order—and then showing up, four decades later, to take it back. Both entries stay in the ledger. That's more honesty than the Consortium has managed in its entire existence."

Sefa laughed again—wet, ugly, real. "You're terrible at comfort."

"I'm told it improves with practice."

* * *

They were halfway back through Compartment Five when her light caught the wall.

Names. Scratched into the interior paneling, low down, at the height of a kneeling adult or a standing child. Dozens of them, cut deep enough to survive the cold, arranged in careful rows like a class photograph.

Sefa stopped. Ansel stopped beside her, and she heard his breath catch over the channel.

"They knew," he said. His voice had gone very small. "At the end. They knew what the bulkheads meant."

"Of course they knew. Passengers always know before crews do. It's the oldest law of ships." Sefa knelt, bringing her light close. Frost glittered in the cuts. Someone had taken the time to make the letters neat. To make them *last*.

She found names she recognized from the manifest they'd reconstructed—families, whole lines of them, scratched side by side. And near the bottom row, smaller, shakier, a child's handwriting:

**TEACH JUNO'S GRANDMOTHER THE SONGS SO THEY SURVIVE**

Beneath it, another hand had added: **AND THE NAMES. TEACH THEM THE NAMES.**

Ansel knelt there a long time without speaking. When he finally did, his voice was steady in the way of load-bearing structures—something holding up enormous weight.

"This is why the lullabies survived. This is why Juno knows them. They wrote the evacuation plan for memory itself, and they trusted it to a grandmother's failing mind, because it was the one archive the Consortium couldn't reach."

"The one archive nobody thought to check," Sefa said, "because it was kept by people they'd decided didn't exist."

She looked down the corridor, toward Compartment Six, toward the 214, toward the sound she still heard every night of a hatch locking. For forty years she had believed her penance was to remember alone. She'd had it backwards. The dead hadn't left her the memory as a punishment.

They'd left it as a *relay*. Hand off the baton, Reyes. Somebody finally showed up to receive it.

"Come on," she said, and stood, and the recorder rode against her chest like a second heart. "We're not done. The vault's still forward, and if those walls are writing names, I want the world to read every single one of them."

They were fifty meters from the airlock when Juno's voice burst over the channel, too fast, too high:

"—tug contacts, three of them, running dark, they just lit up on my scope—they're *between us and the shoals*, Ansel, they're boxing us in—"

Sefa felt the old reflexes wake, forty years asleep, hands already moving on controls that weren't there yet. Behind them, deeper in the wreck, something shifted—a long metallic groan rolling down the Vector's spine, as if the ship itself had heard, and was waking up angry.

"Well," she said. "Let's go introduce them to the evidence."
