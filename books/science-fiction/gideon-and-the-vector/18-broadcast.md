## 18. Broadcast

The distress array sat on the crown of the old relay spire like a rusted crown on a dead king's head.

Ansel had seen it from the hab rings his whole life—a needle of lattice steel rising out of the decommissioned quarter, its dish half-collapsed, its warning beacons dark for decades. Every child on Meridian's Reach learned that the spire was off-limits, structurally unsound, scheduled for demolition. The Consortium had been scheduling it for demolition for forty years. They had never actually demolished it, and now Ansel understood why: you don't tear down the one weapon in the colony capable of speaking over your head.

"It still works," Odile said, running her palm along the base of the transmitter housing. Dust came away in gray streaks. Beneath it, the metal was clean. Someone had kept this maintained—quietly, illegally, for years. "Of course it works. It was built to survive anything except being noticed."

"Who maintained it?"

"Does it matter?" She was already prying open an access panel with a flathead tool she'd carried since the Undercroft. "Someone who remembered what it was for."

Juno stood at the edge of the spire platform, hugging Sefa's flight bag to her chest, wind pulling at her hair. Below them the colony spread out in its dying geometry—dark hab rings, dark docking cradles, the Consortium tower burning white at the center like a single lit eye. Somewhere down there, suppression fields were cycling through the last neighborhoods where people still remembered. Juno's grandmother was in one of them. Ansel had watched the girl make the decision not to go back for her, and he had watched her not cry about it, and he knew that silence would cost her later, when there was finally time to pay.

He crouched beside the array's control housing and began connecting the black box.

It was ugly work. The recorder's casing had been scorched in the debris field; two of its data ports were fused shut, and the third spat errors every time he tried a handshake. Odile fed him adapters from a canvas roll that looked older than both of them combined. Sefa stood watch at the stairwell door with a pry bar held low against her thigh, saying nothing, doing the thing she did—being the wall between them and whatever came up those stairs.

"The charter first," Ansel said. "Then the box. Then my testimony."

"You're really going to say your name," Odile said. Not a question. She'd heard him rehearse it twice on the ride up, muttering into his collar like a man practicing a confession.

"They'll scrub the transmission if it's anonymous data. Data can be denied. A name has to be answered." He fed another cable into the junction. His hands were steady. He was surprised by how steady. "Corvane taught me that, actually. Everything he did was signed. Polite little adjustment notices, immaculate letterhead. You can't prosecute a ghost, but you also can't ignore one standing in your doorway."

"And after?" Juno asked quietly. "After they hear your name?"

Ansel didn't have an answer he could give a twelve-year-old, so he gave her the truest one he had. "After, I stop being invisible. Whatever that costs."

* * *

The upload crawled.

The array's ancient processors chewed through the charter file at a rate that made Odile wince aloud—"I've seen tape decks faster than this"—but the progress bar moved, and each percentage point was a passenger name being read back into existence. Ansel watched the numbers the way he watched all numbers: without sentiment, with total attention. Numbers didn't lie. Forty years of forged ledgers, and here at the end, the truth was just arithmetic again. Four hundred and eleven names. One charter. One flight recorder full of sealed compartments and screaming hull telemetry and a junior pilot's voice saying *I have authorization* in a tone that would convict everyone who'd given it.

Sefa hadn't listened to the playback. She'd refused, flatly, on the tug, and no one had pushed. But she stood near the housing now, close enough to touch it, her hand hovering over the scorched casing without landing. Ansel pretended not to see. Some testimonies were spoken and some were simply endured.

"Signal strength is garbage," Odile said, squinting at a readout. "The dish alignment drifted decades ago. We're broadcasting in a cone wide enough to hit everything within range and precise enough to hit nothing cleanly."

"That's fine." Ansel checked the relay schedule he'd memorized from the port manifests—the same methodical cross-referencing he'd used on his very first impossible claim, a lifetime of weeks ago. "There are eleven ships in orbit or docked right now. Two Consortium cutters, three freighters, four independent haulers, and the *Kestrel Line*'s own tender, which is going to be very interesting for someone's lawyers. Plus the station ledgers, the orbital buoys, and every archive buffer that auto-ingests distress-band traffic. We don't need precision. We need saturation. Once it's in enough buffers, deleting it means admitting there was something to delete."

"Which they'll do anyway," Sefa said from the stairwell.

"Sure. But they'll do it slowly, and badly, and in front of witnesses. That's how fraud dies—not with a bang, but with paperwork."

Odile laughed, short and sharp, the first sound resembling joy any of them had made in days.

Eighty-one percent.

That was when the lights went out.

Not all of them—the emergency strips came up amber a heartbeat later—but the spire's main power died with a sound like a held breath released, and every screen on the platform went black except the array's own battery-backed status display, which blinked a single line:

**EXTERNAL POWER LOST — SWITCHING TO RESERVE**

"He found us," Juno said.

"No," Ansel said, already moving to the main breaker panel, already doing the math. "He found the grid. There's a difference, and it's the only difference that matters." He yanked the panel open. Reserve cells—forty-year-old reserve cells—showed sixty-two percent charge. Enough for maybe nine minutes of transmission at full power. The charter was at eighty-four percent. The black box hadn't started. His testimony existed only in his throat.

Nine minutes.

"Odile. How fast can we run if we drop everything nonessential?"

"If we kill environmental, navigation beacons, and the secondary dish heater? Maybe fourteen minutes. Fifteen if we're lucky and the cells are liars in our favor." She was already pulling breakers, her movements quick and certain, a woman who had spent her whole life keeping obsolete machines alive and was not about to stop now. "But Ansel—the box alone is six hours of raw telemetry. Even compressed, even stripped to the critical channels, we cannot send all of it. Not even close."

"Then we don't send all of it." He thought fast, actuary-fast, the way he'd once reconstructed a staged airlock death from seven decimal points of pressure variance. "We send the summary hash and the first ninety seconds. Hull breach sequence, tug approach vector, the order to seal compartments. Any insurer worth their license can subpoena the rest from the buffers once they know it exists. Existence is the payload. Everything else is appendix."

Ninety seconds. He looked at Sefa.

She had gone very still by the stairwell. Her face, in the amber light, was the face of a woman being asked to walk back into a room she'd spent forty years boarding up.

"Ninety seconds," she said. "That's—that's from the first breach alarm to the compartment seals. That's the part where I—" She stopped. Swallowed. "That's the part where I'm in it."

"Yes," Ansel said gently. "It is. And it's also the part where the tugs are in it. Their transponder signatures, their burn patterns, their docking authority codes—all of it stamped with Consortium routing. Your worst ninety seconds is their best evidence against themselves." He held her gaze. "I won't pretend that's fair. Nothing about this has been fair. But you told me on the wreck that you wanted truth over absolution. This is what it weighs."

For a long moment the only sound was wind through the lattice spire and the soft tick of cooling electronics.

Then Sefa Reyes walked across the platform, took the black box's manual interface cable in both hands like something living, and plugged it in herself.

"Strip it to channel one," she said. "Hull audio. Let them hear me."

* * *

They ran the final upload on reserve power and prayer, and Ansel had never believed in either, so he ran it on arithmetic instead.

Charter: complete at ninety-six percent of estimated cell life. He felt that one land in his chest like a dropped weight—four hundred and eleven shareholders, restored to the ledger of the living and the dead. Somewhere in the colony's memory banks, inheritance law had just been turned around and pointed at the people who'd wielded it.

Black box: ninety seconds of hull audio, compressing, transmitting. Through the monitoring headset he could hear it as it went out—the groan of a hull opening, alarms layered over alarms, and then voices, young voices, forty years young, calling names and compartments and *hold on, hold on*, and under all of it Sefa's voice, thirty years old and shaking, reading out vectors while the vacuum came for her ship. Beside him, Odile pressed her knuckles to her mouth. Juno did not look away. Ansel made himself listen to every second, because these were his people, his parents' generation, his erased family, and the least he owed them was an audience.

Cell life: eleven percent. Testimony: unsent.

And then, faintly, from far below—down the spire stairwell, across the darkened decommissioned quarter, from the direction of the Consortium tower—came the sound of engines.

Not one engine. Several. The particular disciplined formation hum of Consortium cutters running dark.

"They're coming up the maintenance shaft," Sefa said, head cocked toward the stairwell. "Two minutes, maybe less. Corvane doesn't knock anymore."

"Four percent charge," Odile said. "That's forty seconds of broadcast. Your testimony takes three minutes minimum."

Ansel looked at the status display. Looked at the stairs. Did the math one more time, hoping the numbers had changed.

They hadn't. Numbers never lied. That had always been his creed, and the creed had a corollary he'd learned too late: numbers also never cared what they cost.

"Juno." He pulled the headset off and knelt so they were eye level. "When I start talking, you keep the transmit key down. Both hands. Don't stop for anything—not shouting, not gunfire, not me. The moment my voice stops, you release it and you three get down the north ladder into the conduit tunnels. Odile knows the route. You do not wait for me. Do you understand?"

"I'm not leaving you."

"Juno." He took her hands, small and cold around the transmit key. "You are the heir of Meridian's Reach. Not metaphorically—legally, now, on paper, in the buffers of eleven ships. If they take me, the testimony dies with my voice unless it's already flying. You are how it keeps flying." He saw her jaw set, saw the child in her fighting the survivor she was becoming, and hated every adult who had ever put that war inside a twelve-year-old. "Your grandmother sang songs she shouldn't have remembered. You're going to carry words she never got to say. That's the family business now."

Her eyes were wet and furious and absolutely clear. She nodded once.

Ansel stood, keyed the mic, and began to speak.

"This is Ansel Ishikawa, forensic actuary, license 4471-C, resident of Meridian's Reach. I am broadcasting from the primary distress array on the testimony of the colony ship *Vector*, destroyed in orbit forty years ago this month with four hundred and eleven souls aboard..."

He kept it tight—names, dates, registry prefixes, the charter's probate clause, the pattern of impossible accidents he'd audited for a decade without seeing they were one crime wearing different clothes. Behind him he heard the stairwell door bang open, heard Sefa's pry bar meet armor, heard Odile shouting the tunnel route, and he did not turn around. Three percent. Two. He spoke faster, stripping adjectives, keeping only what an auditor would need: *the tugs bore Consortium routing codes, the seal order originated outside the ship, the manifest was rewritten under directive K-9, I am a surviving passenger whose memory was altered without consent—*

Boots on the platform. A voice he knew, calm and immaculate even now, saying, "Mr. Ishikawa. Step away from the array."

One percent.

"—and I sign this testimony with my own name, Ansel Ishikawa, son of Kenji and Mara Ishikawa, passengers four-oh-two and four-oh-three, so that no one can call it anonymous, so that no one can call it deniable, so that whoever hears this knows that the dead of the *Vector* had a witness, and the witness is *still talking*—"

Hands seized his arms. The display went dark. The world went quiet except for his own pulse.

But somewhere above the broken colony, in the buffers of eleven ships and a hundred archives, a voice was still flying—his mother's name, his father's name, his own—unerasable now, multiplied past recall, loose in the dark like a lullaby no one could make anyone forget.

Corvane's face swam into view, close and composed, studying Ansel with something almost like professional respect.

"Well," the adjuster said softly. "Now we know exactly who you are."

Above them, unseen, the first reply pings were already arriving—dozens of them, then hundreds, ships and stations and ledgers across the Reach waking up and asking, in the universal language of liability, the only question that mattered:

*Who do we bill for this?*
