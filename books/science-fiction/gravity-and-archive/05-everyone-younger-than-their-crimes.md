## 5. Everyone Younger Than Their Crimes

The announcement came through every speaker on Meridian Station at 0400 station time, and by 0430 the corridors were full of people who should have been asleep.

*The Halcyon has entered final approach. Crew status: alive. Repeat: all hands alive.*

Rosalind stood in the doorway of her borrowed quarters — her original's quarters, though the council had not said so out loud when they assigned them — and listened to the station wake up around her like a body remembering it had a heart. Doors opening. Feet in the halls. Someone laughing, actually laughing, three decks up, and the sound traveling down the stairwells the way sound only travels in places where people have stopped expecting good news.

She had been on Meridian eleven days. Long enough to learn that the colony's laughter had a half-life of about four minutes before someone remembered the air scrubbers and went quiet again.

She dressed quickly and joined the current of bodies moving toward the observation ring. Nobody questioned a woman walking with purpose at this hour; purpose was contagious tonight. She passed Iyo's mother in the crowd — the woman from the lower decks who'd once, carefully, neutrally, told her *you have her walk, you know* — and the woman grabbed her sleeve and pointed upward as if Rosalind might otherwise miss the entire sky.

On the great curved viewport of the observation ring, the Halcyon hung against the black like a held breath finally let out.

Rosalind had read everything in the archive about the ship. Deep-range vessel, launched twenty-two years ago, crew of forty-one, mission profile classified into uselessness by three successive administrations. It was supposed to come home eight years ago. The station had mourned it in the efficient way colonies mourn things they can't afford to mourn properly — a plaque, a minute of silence, a reallocation of its reserved docking power within the fiscal quarter.

Now it was here, and the crowd pressed against the glass, and Rosalind watched the docking clamps extend across the gap like slow silver fingers.

She felt nothing she could name. That was the trouble with being a copy: other people's joy arrived secondhand, pre-owned, slightly too large in the shoulders. She watched a man beside her weep openly for a crew he'd never met, and she catalogued her own face in the window's reflection — composed, attentive, faintly hungry — and thought: *she would have felt this properly. The original would have known what this was.*

Then the airlock cycled, and the Halcyon's crew came home, and Rosalind stopped thinking about herself entirely.

They were young.

Not young the way returning sailors are young — weathered, salted, older than their years. Young the way recruits are young. The first officer through the lock couldn't have been past thirty, and the manifest Rosalind had memorized put Commander Okafor at fifty-six at launch. The woman walking through the airlock now had skin like someone who had never spent a day under anything but standard lighting, and she moved with the bewildered grace of a person whose body had been returned to her without explanation, like luggage delivered to the wrong address.

The crowd roared. Okafor stopped, looked at all of them, and put a hand over her mouth.

Rosalind didn't cheer. She watched.

She watched because her original's notes had a page on the Halcyon — one page, buried under the life-support analysis, almost an afterthought — that said only: *Ask why the telemetry doesn't match the hull age. Ask who signed off on the return vector. The anomaly is real but the story around it is being written by the same people writing the scrubber logs.*

Her original had suspected the miracle before the miracle arrived. That was the thing about her, Rosalind was learning, day by day, file by file: she had never once been able to let a gift go unexamined. Rosalind felt a kinship with that so sharp it was nearly grief.

The crew came down the gangway in ones and twos, embraced, lifted, photographed. Medical teams hovered at the edges with scanners they kept lowering in confusion, recalibrating, raising again. Rosalind heard a doctor say, twice, to no one: *the telomeres don't make sense. The telomeres don't make sense.*

And then the navigator came through the lock, and the crowd noise seemed to step back from him, and Rosalind understood — in the way you understand a fall only after it's already happening — that she was in trouble.

He was tall in the way that suggests he used to be taller, or would have been, some version of him. Dark hair cut short and growing out unevenly, as if he'd been cutting it himself for twenty years and had recently stopped caring. He walked like a man reading his own body for errors — each step checked against some internal manifest. His eyes did a thing she recognized from her own mirror: swept the room, counted exits, found her, dismissed her, then snapped back and stayed.

He stopped three meters away. Around them the reunion chaos continued, but it bent around the two of them like water around a stone.

"You're staring," he said. Not hostile. Curious, in a tired way.

"You're Elias Voss," she said.

Something crossed his face — fast, gone — that was not the pleasure of recognition. "That's going around tonight."

"It's on the crew manifest." She held out her hand. "Rosalind Hartwell. Investigator."

His grip was dry and careful, a handshake conducted like a calibration test. "Investigator of what?"

"Whatever needs investigating." She let the pause sit. "Your testimony is in my files, Mr. Voss. Given fourteen months ago, station time, to my predecessor. Then you shipped out, or were shipped out, depending which record you believe. You were the last witness interviewed before the case went cold."

The noise of the crowd seemed to thin around them. Voss looked at her hand still holding his, then up at her face, and she watched him do the arithmetic everyone on this station eventually did — the resemblance, the name, the way she said *my predecessor* like a eulogy.

"You're her," he said quietly. "The clone."

"I'm the inheritor. The clone is a legal technicality someone invented to keep me off the payroll."

"Does it work?"

"No."

Almost, he smiled. It was a good smile, ruined slightly by how hard he worked to keep it small. And Rosalind felt the attraction land in her chest like a dropped tool — heavy, inconvenient, something she'd have to pick up and carry whether she wanted to or not — and immediately after it, arriving on schedule, the suspicion.

Because here was the shape of the file she'd memorized: *E. Voss, navigator, testimony incomplete. Subject recalled events in correct sequence but wrong emphasis. Interview terminated when subject received priority recall to Halcyon prep. Subject departed station 72 hours later. No follow-up possible.*

Testimony incomplete. Wrong emphasis. Departed seventy-two hours after giving it.

And now he was standing in front of her twenty years younger than the man who'd given it, wearing a face that had never testified to anything, looking at her like a man meeting a debt he couldn't locate.

"Fourteen months ago," he said slowly. "That was—" He stopped. Recounted. She watched him recount. "I remember the interview."

"Do you."

"Some of it. A room. Bad tea. She asked me about—" His brow folded. "Power routing. Why I remembered power routing questions."

"What did you tell her?"

"I don't—" He laughed, short and unhappy. "I don't know. Isn't that absurd? I can remember the taste of the tea and not my own answers. Twenty-two years in cryo will do that, they say. They say a lot of things tonight." He looked past her at the crowd, at the cheering, at the doctor still muttering about telomeres, and his voice dropped to something meant only for her. "Does anyone else find this strange? What happened to us? Because everyone keeps saying *miracle*, and I keep wanting to ask what the word costs."

"Everything on this station costs something," Rosalind said. "Usually paid by someone who isn't in the room."

Voss turned back to her. For a moment neither of them performed anything — no charm, no caution — and Rosalind felt the strange vertigo of being seen accurately by a stranger, which is either the beginning of trust or the end of it.

"The investigator before you," he said. "Hartwell. Did they ever—"

"She was murdered," Rosalind said. Flatly, because there was no kind way to build up to it, and because she wanted to watch his face when it landed.

It landed. Whatever performance he'd been running — the bemused survivor, the honest sailor — dropped for a full two seconds, and underneath it was something raw and frightened and guilty, and then it was gone, smoothed over so fast she might have imagined it.

Except she never imagined evidence.

"I'm sorry," he said. "I didn't know her well. I barely—"

"You gave her the last testimony of your old life, Mr. Voss. Seventy-two hours later you were gone. Now you're back, younger than your own signature, and the case you testified in is open again with me holding the file." She stepped back, released him from the intensity of it, made her voice almost light. "Get some sleep. Eat something that isn't ship rations. Relearn gravity. And then I'd like to finish the interview."

"Finish it," he repeated.

"You left in the middle. So did the truth, presumably. I'm told they travel together."

Around them, the celebration swelled — someone had produced music from somewhere, and Commander Okafor was being handed a child to hold, and the whole observation ring glowed with the reflected warmth of a miracle nobody had audited. Voss looked at all of it, then at her, and said, very quietly:

"You don't believe in it either. The miracle."

"I believe in anomalies," Rosalind said. "Miracles are just anomalies with better publicity."

This time the smile got all the way out. It transformed him — took ten more years off, took the fear out of his posture for exactly one breath — and she hated how much she noticed, and noticed that she noticed, and filed both observations in the folder she was starting to think of as *later*.

"Elias!" someone shouted from the crowd. "Voss! Get over here, they want the flight crew!"

He hesitated. "The interview," he said. "When?"

"Tomorrow. 0900. My office." Her original's office. Her original's desk, chair, dead plant she hadn't yet thrown out.

"Your office," he said, and something in the way he said *your* told her he knew exactly whose office it had been. "Investigator Hartwell. The tea in that room really is bad. Bring your own."

He went, absorbed into the crowd, into the arms of crewmates and strangers, into the bright noise of the homecoming. Rosalind stood alone in the eddy he left behind and became aware that her heart rate was elevated and that she had, without deciding to, memorized the exact cadence of his voice.

*Attraction,* she noted clinically. *Logged. Contained. Cross-referenced against witness list.*

Except containment implied a wall, and what she felt was less a wall than a door standing open in a house she'd only just moved into.

She pulled up her slate and opened the witness file. E. VOSS — TESTIMONY INCOMPLETE — SUBJECT DEPARTED. Below it, in her original's handwriting, scanned and decrypted, a single marginal note she'd read a dozen times without it landing until now:

*Voss knows more than he said. Watch his hands when he lies. He counts exits. He's afraid of the council, not of me. Find out why.*

Rosalind looked up through the viewport. The Halcyon sat docked against Meridian's spine, forty sleepers still frozen in its belly, its crew celebrating a youth no one could explain, its navigator counting exits across a crowded room.

*Find out why.*

She was going to. Starting at 0900. And God help her, she was going to bring good tea, and wear the grey jacket her original had left hanging in the closet, and pretend — for one hour, maybe two — that the man she was about to interrogate wasn't the most interesting thing that had happened to her since she woke up wearing a dead woman's face.

Below the note, her thumb found one more line she'd missed, smaller, added later, the ink of a different day:

*If he comes back before this is over, assume they sent him.*
