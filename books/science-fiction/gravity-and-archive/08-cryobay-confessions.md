## 8. Cryobay Confessions

The cryobays smelled the same as they had twenty years ago, which was the first problem.

Elias Voss stood in the doorway of Bay Three and breathed it in — antiseptic and cold-plasma ozone, the faint sweetness of coolant that had leaked once in his first year aboard and never fully scrubbed out. His body knew the smell. His body knew everything. That was the trouble. The Halcyon's medical scans said he was thirty-one years old. His memories insisted on fifty-three. And somewhere between those two numbers, whole seasons of himself had gone missing like cargo jettisoned to save weight.

He'd spent four days since docking doing what he'd always done best: navigation. Except the coordinates were internal. He sat with a slate in the crew mess at odd hours and drew timelines of his own life, the way you'd plot a course through debris, marking each year with what he could verify. Birthdates of crewmates' children. Storms they'd threaded around Kessel-9. The taste of the protein loaves in year eleven versus year twelve, because memory hoarded stupid things and lost important ones.

The gaps were not random.

That was the thing he kept circling back to, the way you circle a nav hazard before committing to the burn. Random loss looked like weather — soft-edged, scattered, worse under stress. What he had looked like surgery. Clean margins. He could remember testifying before Meridian's review board in vivid, humiliating detail: the fluorescent hum, the administrator's aide with the chipped tooth, his own hands flat on the table because if he lifted them they'd shake. He could remember walking out. He could remember the corridor after, gray walls, a woman's voice saying something kind he couldn't retrieve.

And then nothing. Eleven days. Not blurred — *absent*, the way a chart shows uncharted water, blank on purpose. When he tried to push into it, his mind slid off, smooth as ice over a vent. Then he surfaced again on the shuttle back to the Halcyon, packing his kit, feeling oddly light, oddly *finished*, without knowing what he'd finished.

Nobody loses eleven days to fatigue. Nobody loses them to grief, either, though there'd been plenty of that by then. You lose them to drugs, or to editing, or to a decision so heavy your mind refuses to carry it forward.

He'd asked Dr. Okafor about it obliquely, third day dockside, framed as a joke: *If the anomaly rewound us, did it rewind the bad parts too?* She'd laughed and said the process wasn't selective, it was cellular, it didn't care about guilt. But her eyes had done a small thing when he mentioned the review board. A flicker, half a degree off true. Elias had spent decades reading instrument drift; he knew deviation when he saw it, even in a colleague's face.

So now he stood in Bay Three at 0300 station time, ostensibly running a pre-revival systems check nobody had assigned him, actually looking for evidence in the only crime scene he had access to: himself.

* * *

The sleepers lay in their long drawers like letters waiting for postage. Four hundred and six of them across six bays, each pod's status light a patient green heartbeat. The station's power allocation meant maybe half would ever get their postage paid. Elias had heard the council's people using words like *triage* and *criteria* in the corridors, spoken in the careful neutral tone of people who have already decided and are only now inventing reasons.

He moved down the row, checking seals he didn't need to check, and let his hands work while his mind went backward through the ice.

Here was what he knew for certain. In the last month before Halcyon's departure from Meridian — the original departure, outbound, twenty-two years ago — he had been summoned to testify. Not volunteered. Summoned, twice, and the second time the summons had come through ship's command rather than civilian channels, which meant someone with authority wanted him compliant and documented. He remembered the subject: maintenance procurement. Air-scrubber contracts, filtration media, the boring plumbing of staying alive. He'd been navigator, but early in his career he'd done a rotation as logistics officer, and apparently that made him a witness to numbers.

He remembered giving honest answers. He was nearly sure he remembered that. The chipped-tooth aide had asked him whether he'd ever been instructed to falsify intake records, and he'd said no, and the strange thing — the thing that had nagged at him for two decades and now gnawed with teeth — was how easy the *no* had felt. Too easy. Like answering a question he'd rehearsed. Like the answer had been installed.

And then the eleven days. And then a lightness he'd carried all the way to the dark and all the way home again, an unearned peace he'd mistaken for closure.

Elias stopped at pod 114. Inside slept a woman named Anouk Reyes, hydroponics tech, forty when she boarded, biologically still forty because the anomaly hadn't touched the sleepers — only the waking crew, a cruelty or a mercy depending on who you asked. He'd known her husband. He'd drunk with her husband the night before launch, and the man had made him promise, hand on shoulder, breath full of celebration whiskey: *You'll look after the ship till we're back.* And Elias had said yes, and meant it, and then somewhere between that night and the departure he had done — or failed to do — something that had helped kill a woman whose face he was looking at right now through frost-fogged glass.

Because that was the shape of it, wasn't it. Rosalind Hartwell had died in that gap. He didn't know how he knew — he'd learned it secondhand, from the whispers that followed the clone through the corridors like static — but his body reacted to the name with a specific dread, a falling sensation, the way you flinch at a star you know you've nearly hit. Whatever he'd surrendered, she'd died for it. Or she'd died because he'd handed someone the means.

His reflection hovered faintly in the pod glass, superimposed on Anouk's sleeping face. Young. Smooth. A man with a boy's skin and an old man's inventory of mistakes, minus the receipts.

"You're going to burn out the seal if you keep touching it."

He turned. She was standing at the bay entrance with two cups of station coffee and the expression of someone who had been looking for him longer than she'd admit. Rosalind Hartwell. The dead woman's face, alive, watching him with eyes that catalogued everything and forgave nothing yet.

"Preventive maintenance," he said.

"At 0300."

"I'm a navigator. My clock is broken by profession." He took the coffee. It tasted like hot cardboard, which was somehow comforting — some things survived every anomaly.

She didn't smile. "You've been down here three nights running. Okafor told me. She also told me you've been asking about the review board." A pause, calibrated. "I read my original's files, Elias. Her last entry names a witness. E. Voss. So before I decide what you are — witness, suspect, or weapon someone else aimed — I'd like to hear what you remember."

"That's the problem," Elias said. "I don't."

* * *

They ended up in the observation blister off Bay One, a curved window of composite glass looking down at Meridian's night side, the colony lights arranged below them like a circuit diagram someone had spilled coffee on. Whole sections were dark. He watched her notice that. Watched her file it.

He told her all of it, start to finish, in the flat voice he used for incident reports: the summons, the testimony, the too-easy answers, the eleven days, the lightness. He told her about asking Okafor, and the flicker in her eyes. He told her the gaps weren't fuzzy — they were *shaped*. Bounded. Edited.

Rosalind listened the way he imagined her original must have listened: completely, unnervingly, leaning slightly forward as if proximity improved resolution. When he finished she was quiet for a long moment, turning her cup.

"Describe the room," she said. "The second hearing."

"Small. Sub-basement level, I think — no windows, air handling loud enough to hear. Table bolted down. Two chairs on their side, one on mine. The aide with the chipped tooth. A woman taking notes who never spoke. And—" He stopped.

"And?"

"And Corven." The name came out of him with its own momentum. "Administrator Corven. He wasn't supposed to be there. He came in late, stood by the door, never sat down. He watched me the whole time like he was checking a pressure gauge." Elias stared at the dark sections below. "I forgot he was there. I've been reconstructing this for days and I forgot the most powerful man on the station was in the room until just now. That's not memory decay. Memory doesn't lose the apex predator and keep the chipped tooth."

"No," Rosalind said softly. "It doesn't. But suggestion does. And certain drugs. And—" She caught herself, jaw tightening. "My original's notes reference a compound. Off-formulary, station-manufactured. She called it 'the polite drug.' Compliance-friendly interrogation aid. Short window, deep suggestibility, and a retrograde amnesia tail that erases roughly—"

"Eleven days," Elias said.

She nodded slowly. "Give or take."

The cold in the blister wasn't all from the glass. Elias set his cup down because his hands had begun, at last, to shake — twenty years late, arriving exactly on schedule. "Then I didn't lie to that board. Someone put the lie in me and took the receipt." He laughed, and it came out wrong. "Do you understand what that does? I can't even confess properly. I don't know what I gave up. I don't know what I signed, or handed over, or agreed to forget. Every instinct I have says I'm innocent and every fact pattern says innocence is exactly what someone engineered me to feel."

"Yes," Rosalind said. "I know."

Of course she knew. She was living the mirror image — a self built from another woman's files, trusting memories she'd never made. Two investigators, both working cases where the primary evidence was a person, and both persons compromised by design. He saw her see it too, the symmetry landing between them like a third presence.

"So here's my offer," Elias said. "Use me. Interrogate me. Properly, no polite drugs, whatever protocol your original left behind. Pull the thread in front of witnesses, on record, so nothing we find can be dismissed later as my word against my own blank spots. If there's a buried confession in here, dig it up where everyone can watch you dig." He met her eyes. "I can't promise you'll like what's in me. I can't even promise I didn't put it there myself, willingly, and then pay to forget. But I'd rather be guilty and useful than innocent and hollow."

Rosalind studied him for a long moment. Outside, a section of the lower city flickered — brownout, brief, the grid clearing its throat — and neither of them looked away from each other to check it.

"There's a risk you should price in first," she said quietly. "If we open this, and we find you carried my original's evidence to Corven — or away from her — then everything you help me build afterward gets attacked as tainted. Corven's lawyers will say the archive is built on a compromised witness. Your cooperation could cost us the case."

"My cooperation could also win it," Elias said. "A man who testifies against his own interests reads as credible everywhere except a courtroom. Fortunately—" he gestured at the dark sections below, at the whole rigged quiet of the station, "—we may not end up in a courtroom."

Something almost like a smile crossed her face and was rationed away. "First session tomorrow, 0600. Bring your timeline charts. We start at the edges of the gap and work inward." She stood, then paused at the hatch. "Elias. The woman in pod 114. Anouk Reyes. Why did you stop there?"

He'd hoped she wouldn't ask. He was glad, in the way of masochists, that she had.

"Her husband made me promise to look after the ship," he said. "I intend to find out whether I kept that promise or sold it. Either way, she wakes up owed an accounting."

Rosalind held his gaze a beat longer than comfortable — long enough that he understood she was adding this to the file she was keeping on him, the one labeled with his name and, currently, no verdict.

"0600," she said, and was gone.

Elias stayed in the blister a while, watching the brownouts spread below like a slow tide going out. Somewhere in his skull, behind ice eleven days thick, a door waited that he'd paid good money — or been paid — never to open. Tomorrow a woman wearing a dead woman's face was going to pick the lock.

He found, examining himself honestly, that he was afraid. And underneath the fear, quieter and more damning: relieved.
