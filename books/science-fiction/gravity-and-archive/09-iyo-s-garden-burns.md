## 9. Iyo's Garden Burns

The message came at 04:12 station time, which on Meridian meant nothing and everything — the station kept a twenty-six-hour day for its founders' circadian comfort, and the lower decks had long since stopped pretending to live by it. Rosalind was awake anyway. She was always awake now, it seemed, her original's notes spread across her bunk like an autopsy of a life.

*Security action scheduled, Deck 7 sublevel C, 05:00,* the anonymous line read. *They're coming for the garden.*

She was moving before she finished reading.

* * *

She'd been to the garden once before, three days after Iyo showed it to her, and she'd made herself stay away since — not because she didn't want to return but because she did, too much. The garden was the first honest thing she'd found on Meridian. Everything above Deck 5 was polished composite and scripted courtesy, administrators smiling with their mouths while their eyes did arithmetic. But down in the sealed sections, where the air ran thin and sweet with recycled moisture, people lived the way roots live under pavement: stubbornly, invisibly, against all official record.

The garden itself occupied what had once been a hydroponics training bay, decommissioned forty years ago according to the manifests, which meant it had never existed at all. Iyo's people had rebuilt it from salvage — grow-lights strung from stolen power conduits, planter beds welded from hull scrap, a drip irrigation system assembled out of medical tubing. Tomatoes. Beans. Something leafy and green that Iyo called *amara* and refused to translate. Real soil, hauled down bag by bag over years, in defiance of every regulation that said the lower decks were storage space, not living space.

Rosalind came through the maintenance hatch at 04:40 and found them already packing.

They worked in silence, which was worse than panic. Panic would have meant they still believed something could be argued. This was the silence of people who had done this before, or watched it done, and knew the choreography. Iyo's mother — a broad, weathered woman named Sefa whose hands were stained green to the wrist — was lifting seedlings into crates with the tenderness of someone handling the wounded. A dozen others moved through the rows, harvesting everything that could be harvested, abandoning the rest.

"Where are they taking you?" Rosalind asked.

Sefa didn't look up. "Deck 11."

Rosalind felt the number land in her chest like a dropped tool. Deck 11 was in the failing sections — the ones her original's notes had flagged eight months ago, the ones where the air scrubbers ran at sixty percent capacity and the power grid browned out nightly. The council's own internal memos, the ones Corven probably believed were buried deep enough, described Deck 11 as *non-viable within eighteen months.*

"They're relocating you," Rosalind said slowly, "to a section that's dying."

"We're being relocated," Sefa corrected, with the flat precision of someone who had learned that words mattered. "Whether the section is dying is a separate question they've decided not to ask." She set a crate down and finally met Rosalind's eyes. "You're the investigator. The clone one. So investigate this: why does it take security forty people and a full shift to move twelve families who have no weapons and nowhere to go?"

* * *

At 05:00 exactly, the hatch at the far end of the bay opened and security came through.

There were more of them than Rosalind expected — thirty, maybe forty, in grey tactical gear with the station crest stenciled on the chest, carrying equipment she recognized from the manifests as *environmental remediation tools.* She knew what environmental remediation looked like when it was aimed at plants. It looked like incinerators.

A lieutenant — Vane, her file said, though his name tag said nothing — consulted a tablet and announced to the room in a voice trained for announcements: "By order of the Station Council, this unlicensed facility is hereby reclaimed for critical infrastructure conversion. All occupants will proceed to designated relocation points. Personal effects are limited to two containers per person. Resistance will be documented."

"Documented," Sefa repeated softly. "That's new. Last time it was just *discouraged.*"

Rosalind stepped forward. "I'm invoking investigative authority. Hartwell case, continuity claim filed with the Colonial Registry. This facility is material evidence in an ongoing inquiry into falsified life-support records. No one touches it until my review is complete."

It was a good speech. She'd built it in the corridor on the way down, stitching together clauses from her original's warrant templates, and she delivered it with a steadiness she almost felt. The lieutenant looked at her with the patient expression of a man who had been briefed.

"Investigator Hartwell." He pronounced the name carefully, as if handling something fragile and slightly ridiculous. "Your authority covers records access and witness interview. It does not cover obstruction of council infrastructure directives." He turned back to his tablet. "If you'd like to file a protest, the administrative office opens at 08:00."

"The administrative office," Rosalind said, "is the subject of my investigation."

"Then you'll be filing your protest with yourself." He gestured, and the security detail began to move. "Ma'am, I'd ask you to stand clear. The burn order executes at 06:00 regardless of occupancy."

*Burn order.* Not dismantled. Not salvaged. Burned — the biomass incinerated, the soil sterilized, the whole small living world reduced to ash that could be officially logged as *contaminated material disposal.* Because ash couldn't be photographed. Ash couldn't be shown to a tribunal. Ash was just ash.

"You're burning food," Rosalind said. "On a station with rationed calories. In front of me. While telling me there's no crisis worth investigating."

Vane's composure flickered — one muscle near his jaw, quickly mastered. "Caloric allocation is managed at the council level, ma'am. You'd know that if you attended briefings instead of skulking in restricted sections with—" he glanced at Iyo, small and rigid beside her mother, "—unregistered minors."

"Iyo is registered," Rosalind said. "Population census, cycle 214, family unit 7734. She exists. Your own database says so. What doesn't exist is any record of this garden, these families' actual oxygen consumption, or the fact that Deck 7 sublevel C has held three hundred residents for eleven years while drawing power that appears nowhere in the grid logs." She let that sit. "Someone has been keeping two ledgers, Lieutenant. And this morning one of them is on fire."

For a moment — half a second, maybe less — something moved behind Vane's eyes that wasn't loyalty or procedure. It might have been shame. Then it was gone, folded away like a document into a drawer, and he said only: "Stand clear, please."

* * *

They stood clear. There was nothing else to do, and everyone in the bay understood that better than Rosalind did; they had learned the grammar of power here long before she arrived. The families filed out through the maintenance corridors toward Deck 11, carrying their two containers each, and Rosalind carried what she could — a crate of seedlings Sefa pressed into her arms without a word, as if handing her something to hold hostage, or hold sacred.

Iyo lingered. She was eleven, maybe twelve, small for her age in the way children grew small when the air was thin, and she looked up at Rosalind with the terrible patience of a child who has already understood more than the adults around her.

"You'll write it down?" Iyo asked. "What happened? In your archive?"

"I'll write it down."

"Not the official version. The real one. That we grew things. That it was beautiful." Her chin trembled once and stopped. "Because if nobody writes it down, then it never happened, and then they can do it again somewhere else and call it cleanup."

Rosalind crouched to her level, crate balanced on her hip. "I keep an archive," she said. "Everything goes in it. Everything. Even the parts that make powerful people look like what they are."

Iyo considered this with grave attention. "Then put in that Mr. Okonkwo cried. He thinks nobody saw. He grew the tomatoes from seeds his grandmother brought from Earth and he cried when they pulled them up." She swallowed. "And put in that I hid four seedlings in my coat. They didn't find them. So it's not all gone."

"It's not all gone," Rosalind agreed, and meant it as a promise, and hated how small a promise it was.

Then Iyo ran to catch up with her mother, and Rosalind stood alone in the emptying garden with the grow-lights humming their stolen hum, waiting for the fire.

* * *

She stayed. She told herself it was evidence preservation — documenting the site before destruction, timestamping, imaging — and all of that was true, but underneath the procedure was something rawer. She wanted to watch. She wanted to know exactly what it looked like when an institution erased a true thing, so that she would recognize it again, because she was beginning to understand she would need to recognize it often.

At 06:00, the incineration team came through in heat suits and did their work with the efficiency of men following a checklist. The planter beds went first, dragged into the thermal unit whole, soil and roots and all. Then the trellises. Then the irrigation lines. The grow-lights resisted — welded frames, decades of improvised repair — and the team had to cut them apart with plasma torches, and the smell of burning metal mixed with the smell of burning green, and Rosalind stood in the corner with her recorder running and thought: *this is what the murder looked like too.* Not a knife. Not a struggle. A signature, a schedule, a crew of tired men doing a job. Administrative violence, executed on time.

Her original had found the second ledger. Her original had walked through rooms like this one and seen the gap between what the station claimed and what it contained. And then her original had died, quietly, officially, of causes that fit neatly on a form.

Rosalind had come to Meridian believing the murder was the mystery. Standing in the smoke of a tomato patch, she understood the murder was only the perimeter fence around a much larger crime. Nobody kills an investigator over paperwork. They kill over what the paperwork protects.

* * *

She rode the freight lift up to Deck 5 with the seedlings in her lap, and somewhere between Deck 8 and Deck 6, the shape of it came to her — not gradually, but all at once, the way a dark room resolves when a door opens.

The triage list already existed.

She had spent days assuming the council was scrambling — that the Halcyon's arrival had presented them with a sudden moral problem, half the sleepers versus the whole colony, criteria to be drafted, arithmetic to be done. That was the story Corven told, the story the council would tell publicly: *we are responding to crisis, we are making hard choices in good faith.*

But the relocation orders for Deck 7 had been signed ten days ago. Before the Halcyon docked. Before the revival question was even formally raised. Twelve families moved out of a functioning section and into a failing one — moved *down*, toward the dark — and the paperwork cited *infrastructure optimization,* and the same week, maintenance crews had begun rerouting power conduits from the lower decks upward, toward Deck 2, toward the cryobay support systems and the council chambers and the transmission array.

They weren't preparing to choose who lived. They had chosen months ago. The Halcyon, the sleepers, the genetic diversity scores and skill matrices Corven was surely drafting even now — none of it was the decision. It was the theater. The public spectacle of agonizing over the sleepers' fates would absorb every eye on the station while the real list — quiet, unsigned, deniable — was executed family by family, deck by deck, in the language of logistics. Relocation. Optimization. Reclamation. Words that never once said *you will die* but arranged, with perfect bureaucratic cleanliness, for exactly that.

The sleepers weren't the dilemma. The sleepers were the diversion.

Rosalind sat in the humming lift with dirt under her fingernails and Iyo's tomatoes in her arms and felt the anger arrive — not the hot flare she'd known since docking, the disoriented rage of a woman wearing a dead woman's face, but something colder and far more durable. This was anger with structure. Anger with a filing system. Her original must have felt it too, in the end, standing where Rosalind now stood, seeing the same pattern assemble itself out of dates and signatures. It was the last thing she'd seen. It was the thing that killed her.

*Fine,* Rosalind thought. *Then it's the thing that finishes you.*

* * *

Back in her quarters, she cleared a space on the desk — pushing aside her original's notes, which felt suddenly like cowardice, inheriting a dead woman's map instead of drawing her own — and opened a fresh file. Not in the official case system; Corven could read that, seal that, sign that away. A parallel file, encrypted, mirrored to three off-station nodes through the old survey relay she'd found in Chapter Two of her original's mess, the one everyone had forgotten because it appeared on no current schematic.

She titled it with the date and then, beneath the date, with a sentence:

*Meridian Station maintains two populations. One is counted. One is consumed.*

Then she began to enter records. The relocation order for family unit 7734. The burn authorization for sublevel C, signed by Vane, countersigned by an office code that traced back — she checked twice, three times — to Corven's own administration. The grid rerouting schedules. The Deck 11 scrubber telemetry showing sixty percent efficiency against a manifest claiming ninety-four. Photographs of the garden, taken over three visits, timestamped. And at the top of the testimony section, a placeholder she filled in from memory while the images were still sharp:

*Witness: Iyo, age approx. 11, family unit 7734. Statement: "Put in that Mr. Okonkwo cried. He thinks nobody saw."*

When she finished the first pass, the cabin was lighter outside — station morning, such as it was — and her hands were steady, and she understood that something in her had changed permanently, some threshold crossed that had no ceremony. She had stopped investigating a murder. She had started building a prosecution.

There was a knock at her door. Two knocks, hesitant, spaced wrong for security or administration.

She opened it on Elias Voss, younger than his memories, holding a data chip in his hand like something that might detonate.

"I remembered something," he said. "Or part of something. And I think you need to hear it before the council makes its announcement tomorrow — because I don't think the list they show you is the list they're using."

Rosalind looked at him for a long moment, the seedlings green behind her on the desk, the archive running silent in the background, uploading.

"Come in," she said.
