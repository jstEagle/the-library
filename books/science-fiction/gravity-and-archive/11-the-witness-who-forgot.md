## 11. The Witness Who Forgot

The room Rosalind chose for the interview was a decommissioned comms booth on Deck Six — small enough that their knees nearly touched across the console, dim enough that the only light came from her slate and the amber standby glow of dead transmitters. She'd picked it, Elias suspected, because it had no windows and no official function. Nothing about what they were about to do would look legitimate on any log.

"Tell me about the night you testified," she said.

He'd been dreading this for two days, ever since she'd shown him the decrypted file with his initials in her original's handwriting. *E. Voss — witness. Carries proof.* He'd told her he didn't remember testifying at all. She had nodded like she believed him, which was worse than if she hadn't.

"I've been trying," he said. "It's like — you know when you wake up from a dream and for three seconds you can still see the room? I keep reaching for those three seconds and finding nothing."

"Start earlier. The day before."

Elias closed his eyes. The Halcyon's return had given him back a body that worked too well — knees without ache, lungs that pulled clean air like a young man's, because he was one now, biologically speaking. But memory didn't live in the body. Memory lived somewhere the anomaly hadn't touched, and in there he was still forty-one years old, standing in rooms he could no longer furnish.

"The day before," he repeated. "We were in port rotation. Meridian was our last long stop before the return leg. I remember the dockyard smell — coolant and fish protein from the hydroponics barges. I remember being tired. Ship-tired, not sleep-tired." He opened his eyes. "I remember your original."

Rosalind went very still. "What do you remember?"

"A woman with my face's future in it." He almost laughed; it came out wrong. "Sorry. That's — everyone here looks at me like that. Like I'm a photograph of someone they knew. You especially. You look at me like I'm a letter that arrived late."

"Describe her."

"Tall. Tired in a way she was hiding behind competence. She found me at the navigator's guild annex off Dock Three. Said she needed an honest instrument reading and couldn't trust anyone whose salary came through the council." He frowned, chasing it. "That part's clear. It's the clearest thing I have. After that—"

"After that?"

"Static." He spread his hands. "I know we talked more than once. I know it mattered, because when I try to approach the memory my whole chest tightens like I'm bracing against acceleration. Your brain doesn't brace around things that don't matter. But the content is gone. Not faded — gone. There's a shape where it used to be, and the shape has edges."

Rosalind turned her slate toward him. On it: a timeline of Meridian Station, eleven months of air-scrubber maintenance logs, the mid-year gap, the identical phrasing after. "Edges like this?"

"Worse. These logs were written by someone trying to look normal. My gaps were made by someone who understood how memory works." He heard himself say it and felt the floor tilt slightly under the sentence. "Or how mine works."

* * *

She started with the easy questions and moved outward in rings, the way he imagined she must have been trained — or the way her original had been trained, the same curriculum poured into a different vessel. When did you first meet her. Where did you meet her. Who else was present. Each answer he gave, she logged. Each answer he gave, he also heard as if from a distance, checking it against the man he believed himself to be, and finding the fit imperfect.

Because here was the thing he hadn't said aloud yet, the thing sitting in him like ballast: he could not verify a single thing about himself.

The Halcyon's crew had spent four days in medical after docking, and the doctors had pronounced them miraculously healthy and psychologically intact. Intact. As if the mind were a hull you could pressure-test. What they'd actually confirmed was that his memories were *consistent* — that the story Elias Voss told about himself matched the story in his service file, matched the stories his crewmates told. Consistency proved nothing. A forged ledger is consistent. That was practically the station's founding principle.

"You're doing it again," Rosalind said quietly.

"Doing what?"

"Leaving. Your eyes go to the middle distance and you stop being in the room."

"Sorry." He dragged his attention back. "It's just — every answer I give you, I'm also asking whether I'm the kind of man who'd lie about it. And I don't have any evidence either way except my own testimony, which is exactly what's in question. Do you see the trap? I'm the witness and the crime scene and the only forensic team allowed inside."

"I see it," she said. "I live next door to it. Every time I look in a mirror I'm looking at the victim."

He hadn't thought of it that way — that her displacement ran parallel to his. Two people wearing lives they couldn't fully claim. It should have comforted him. Instead it frightened him, because it meant she of all people would know how easily a person could be rewritten, and she was looking at him with a forensic patience that never quite became warmth.

"Let's try something different," she said. "Not what happened. What your body remembers. Sensory detail survives editing better than narrative does. Close your eyes. Go back to the annex. What was on the walls?"

He closed his eyes. Reached.

"Charts. Old ones, paper, the guild keeps them for tradition. A poster about deep-range certification with a comet on it." His voice dropped, going somewhere below thought. "Coffee. Real coffee, someone had real grounds, which was expensive, which means someone wanted the meeting to feel—" He stopped.

"Feel what?"

"Safe. Someone wanted me to feel safe." His eyes opened. "Rosalind. If she was recruiting a witness, why make it feel safe?"

"Because she needed something from you that fear would ruin." Rosalind leaned forward. "What did she ask you for?"

And there it was again — the edge. He pushed against it the way you push a bruise, deliberately, to map its borders. The annex. The coffee. Her face, tired and certain. Her voice saying—

Saying—

Nothing. A wall with no seam. But his hands had curled into fists on his knees without his permission, and his pulse was audible in his ears, and some animal part of him was braced against deceleration so hard his shoulders ached.

"My hands," he said. "Look at my hands."

She looked. "You're gripping."

"I grip when I fly manual corrections. It's a habit from a bad approach in my twenties — well." He gestured at himself, the twenty-year-old body, the joke that wasn't one. "In my first twenties. The point is, my body thinks I'm holding something. Right now. In this memory that isn't there. My body thinks I'm holding something and won't let go of it."

Rosalind set down the slate very carefully, the way you set down something that might detonate.

"Elias. What did she give you to carry?"

* * *

They walked afterward, because neither of them could sit still, and because Deck Six at second shift was empty in ways the upper decks never were. Their footsteps echoed off bulkheads that hadn't been repainted since the station's optimistic decade. Somewhere below them — two decks, maybe three — families breathed rationed air that appeared on no manifest, and above them the council polished numbers until they shone.

"She gave me a data core," Elias said. The words came out of him slowly, each one tested before release, like a man walking ice. "I can't see it happening. I can't see her handing it over. But I know the weight of it. Forty grams, maybe less. Small enough to close your fist around. She told me—" He stopped walking. Put his hand flat against the bulkhead, steadying himself against a ship that wasn't moving. "She told me not to know I had it."

"That's a strange instruction."

"It's a *navigator's* instruction. We do it all the time. You don't put your escape trajectory in the flight plan — you put it somewhere no auditor looks, in a routine burn nobody questions, and you don't *know* it's there, officially, so you can't be made to reveal it, because you genuinely can't." He turned to face her. "She hid the evidence in me. Not in my quarters, not in my locker. In my testimony-shaped ignorance. She made me a dead drop that didn't know it was a dead drop."

"And then you left Meridian."

"And then I left Meridian." The recitation of it felt obscene. "Eleven months ago. The Halcyon departed on schedule. I went into cryo. I woke up twenty years younger with a hole in my memory the exact size of her murder." He laughed, one dry note with no humor in it. "Do you understand what the cryogenic anomaly did? Everyone's celebrating it like a miracle. It handed every guilty person on that ship a perfect defense. *I don't remember.* And it's true. We don't. The anomaly ate the recent year — everyone's lost the same window, departure plus the pre-freeze weeks. Convenient window. Suspiciously convenient."

"You think it was engineered?"

"I think I think that at three in the morning and then I think I'm paranoid, and I can't tell which thought is mine." He pressed the heel of his hand against his eye. "This is what it's like, Rosalind. This is the weapon. Whoever did this — whoever took her evidence out of me — they didn't just steal a data core. They installed doubt. Because now even when I remember something true, I have to ask: did I remember it, or was it left in me? Am I a witness or a delivery system that's still in transit? Maybe the core is still *in* me somehow, buried deeper, waiting for instructions. Maybe everything I've told you today was scripted by whoever scrubbed me, designed to surface exactly when a clone with her face asked exactly these questions."

She was quiet for a moment. The corridor hummed around them, the station's failing heartbeat.

"Do you believe that?" she asked.

"I don't know. That's the whole horror of it. I used to be a man who trusted his own instruments. That was the entire job — read the telemetry, trust the telemetry, thirty thousand people alive because Elias Voss believes his own readings. Now I'm the reading, and I'm showing anomalous values, and protocol says distrust the instrument." He looked at her. "Protocol says ground the ship."

"I'm not grounding you."

"You should. By your own standards you should. I'm a compromised source carrying possibly-forged memories toward an active investigation, and the only reason you're still walking beside me is—" He stopped himself.

"Say it," she said softly.

"Is that you look at me like I'm worth the risk. And I want that to be about me. I need one thing in this to be about *me*, and not about her plans, or the council's plans, or whatever was done to my head. I want to be a man choosing, not a package arriving." His voice cracked on the last word and he hated it, hated the youth of the throat producing it, hated that even his shame sounded nineteen.

Rosalind reached out and took his fist — the one still clenched around a data core that wasn't there — and unfolded his fingers one by one, gently, like defusing something.

"Empty," she said. "See? Whatever you carried, it's gone. Which means someone took it. Which means someone *knew*. And Elias — whoever took it had to get it from you while you were awake, or open you up while you slept, and either way there's a record, a person, a moment. Gaps have edges, you said. Edges mean tools. Tools mean hands."

"Whose hands?"

"That's what we find next." She closed his fingers back around nothing, around the absence, and held them there a beat longer than forensics required. "One more question. Then we stop for tonight, because you're grey around the mouth and I need you functional."

"Ask."

"The night before departure. After she hid the core in you. Did anything happen that scared you?"

He reached for it expecting the wall. Instead something slipped sideways through a crack he hadn't known was there — not a scene, just a sensation, raw and unfiled: the dockyard at night, sodium lights, his own reflection stretched in a dark viewport, and the feeling of being *watched by someone patient*. Not threatened. Patient. The way you're patient with cargo.

"Yes," he said, and the word came out steady, which surprised him. "Someone followed me to the ship. I reported it to dock security. There'll be a log."

"There won't," Rosalind said. "I've read every security log for that week. Twice."

The cold that went through him then had nothing to do with the station's failing heat. Because if the report had been erased, then the erasure wasn't random decay, wasn't anomaly, wasn't accident. Someone had cleaned up after themselves along a line that led directly through him — and had done it thoroughly enough to reach into dock security's records, which meant reach into the council, which meant reach into Corven's office.

Which meant the hands had a name, and the name had authority, and Elias Voss had walked onto the Halcyon carrying that authority's stolen property in his skull, and come back twenty years lighter and none the wiser.

"They're going to know we talked," he said. "If they edited me once, they'll check whether I've been reopened."

"Then we're past the point where caution protects us." Rosalind's jaw set in a way he was beginning to recognize as her version of courage — not softness at all, but decision. "Tomorrow morning I'm requesting a formal meeting with Administrator Corven. I'm going to tell him his witness remembers."

Elias stared at her. "That's insane. That's handing them the timeline."

"No. It's showing them a mirror." Something moved behind her eyes that was equal parts her and her original, the inheritance expressing itself. "Men like Corven only make mistakes when they believe they're still in control. I want him comfortable. I want him generous. I want him to offer us something."

"Offer us—"

But she was already turning down the corridor toward the lift, and the amber emergency lighting caught her face in profile — her face, the murdered woman's face, lit like a fuse — and Elias understood that he had just been recruited all over again, by the same method, into the same war, and that this time, whatever it cost, he intended to remember.
