## 10. Interrogation of a Language

The countdown read 41:17:06 when Casper finally stopped panicking and started working.

They marked the moment deliberately — a small ritual of self-command, the way they'd been trained to do in the field, on first-contact listening posts, when a signal arrived that made no sense and every instinct screamed at once. *Panic is a translation error,* Reyes used to say. *You're rendering the unknown into fear because fear is the only language you already speak. Find a better one.*

Reyes. Casper pushed the name away like a hot pan handle and turned back to the memory-cores.

They had laid them out on the Meridian's archive-bay console in a row of nine — matte black cylinders, each the size of a forearm, each stamped with a serial and a date-range and a redaction seal so old it had gone amber. The bay around them was a cathedral of dead language: racks of phoneme libraries, training decks, translation engines grown obsolete three generations ago. Dust hung in the emergency lighting like a held breath. Somewhere below, the reactor ticked its slow arithmetic, and Casper's suit radio kept whispering the countdown to them whether they asked or not.

Forty-one hours. Maybe less. Sefa's last transmission had been characteristically precise and characteristically grim: *The failsafe is real, the countdown is real, and the people who built it built it to be irreversible. Whatever you're going to do, do it fast and do it quietly. If security hears you're aboard, they'll let the clock finish for you.*

So: quietly. And fast. And against a lockout architecture that had, by every indication, been designed by the same people who had designed Casper's own training.

Casper sat down at the console, cracked their knuckles — an old habit, useless in gloves, performed anyway — and did what they had done a hundred times before, in a hundred contexts, with a hundred species that never existed in any human grammar.

They stopped trying to break the encryption.

They started reading it.

* * *

The insight had arrived, as insights did, sideways — in the gap between two failures. For the first six hours aboard, Casper had attacked the cores the way any competent records officer would: brute-force the seal, spoof the credentials, exploit the hardware. All of it failed, and failed *identically* — every intrusion attempt bounced by the same polite refusal, the same standardized error, the same bureaucratic shrug. ACCESS DENIED. QUERY LOGGED. And Casper had understood, with a lurch, that *query logged* was not a threat.

It was a tell.

Every falsified system lies the same way a nervous witness lies: too consistently. The Meridian's archive wasn't just locked. It was *edited* — and edited records, Casper knew from thirty years of professional intimacy with them, could never quite suppress their own grammar. Somewhere in the structure of the refusal, in the rhythm of the denials, in the things the system chose to log and the things it conspicuously did not, there was a second text. A palimpsest. The truth, written underneath, the way a language learner's errors reveal their mother tongue.

Casper had spent a decade doing this with alien speech. The Vess-ha — no. The *Kessari*, the deep-orbit species whose vocal catalog Casper had helped build, whose language had colonized their dreams — had no word-for-word translatable grammar at all. Their language was built on omission: meaning lived in what was *not* said, in the shape of the silence between phonemes. To translate Kessari, you didn't listen to the sounds. You listened to the gaps.

The Meridian's archive had been built by humans. But it had been built by humans *hiding* something, and hiding, Casper realized, its own kind of foreign language. It had a grammar. It had rules. And every grammar could be interrogated.

They started with the denials.

* * *

The console's interface was ancient but obedient, and Casper fed it their own query history — the log of their own failed attempts, forty-one of them now, each one stamped and refused. To a security analyst, this was garbage. To a xenolinguist, it was a corpus.

Casper read it the way they'd been taught to read a first-contact signal: not asking *what does it say* but *what does its structure assume*. The refusals came in three variants — full denial, partial denial, and a third type that returned a *sanitized* response: a fragment of data, real but hollowed, the informational equivalent of a redacted page with the black bars left in.

It was the third type that sang.

Casper pulled every sanitized response and laid them side by side, and there it was — the thing that made their breath go short and their hands go still on the keys. The sanitized fragments were not random. They were *systematically* incomplete. Every one of them had been scrubbed of the same class of information: personal names, timestamps within a specific eleven-day window thirty years ago, and — this was the beautiful, damning part — any reference to *audio*.

Text had survived the sanitization. Video metadata had survived. But audio — audio recordings, audio logs, audio testimony — had been excised with a thoroughness that bordered on obsession. Whoever had built the redaction had not merely deleted the sound of Kestrel Landing. They had deleted the *concept* of sound, everywhere it appeared, across thirty years of records.

Which meant the sound still existed. You cannot redact a category that thoroughly without the category itself becoming the map to what you buried.

"They were afraid of voices," Casper said aloud, to the dead bay, to the dust. Their own voice sounded strange in the helmet — too loud, too human, in a place where a child had once hidden among phoneme drills. "Four hundred people, and what they couldn't stand was the voices."

The countdown whispered: 38:52:19.

* * *

The next step was the one that made Casper feel, for the first time in years, fully alive — and they noted that feeling with a kind of clinical unease, the way you'd note a fever. Because this was the part they were *good* at. This was the part the dreams had been rehearsing.

They built a model of the redaction system's grammar.

Not by breaking it — by *learning* it, the way you'd learn a language from a restricted corpus. Every sanitization was a sentence. Every redaction bar was a morpheme. Casper fed the console hundreds of sanitized fragments and let the pattern-extraction routines — routines they had originally written for Kessari omission-grammar, routines the Records Corps would have been very interested to know were being used here — chew through them, hunting for the rules underneath.

The rules emerged over six hours, and they were gorgeous, and they were terrible.

The redaction system didn't delete randomly. It *paraphrased*. That was its genius and its confession: it rewrote compromised passages into bland, true-sounding alternatives, preserving surface coherence while gutting content. A log reading *EVAC ORDER DISPUTED — CREW REFUSAL LOGGED, AUDIO ATTACHED* would emerge as *EVAC PROCEEDURES CONFIRMED — STANDARD COMPLIANCE*. The lie was never a deletion. It was always a *translation*.

And every translation has a source-language accent.

Casper had a name for this, from their old fieldwork: *calque-shadow*. When a text is translated under duress, the original bleeds through in the syntax — in word order that doesn't quite fit, in prepositions chosen oddly, in rhythm that stutters where the source text had insisted on something the target language couldn't hold. The falsified records of the Meridian were full of calque-shadows. Sentences that scanned smoothly but *rhythmically* wrong. Passive constructions stacked three deep where active voice would be natural. A peculiar, recurring hesitation — a doubled qualifier, *confirmed and verified*, *logged and recorded* — that appeared 217 times across the corpus and appeared nowhere in any legitimate Halcyon Reach document style Casper had ever catalogued.

217 instances. Casper pulled them all, stripped the paraphrase away using the grammatical model, and let the console reconstruct what the doubled qualifiers were nervously papering over.

The first fragment resolved at 33:04:51 on the countdown.

It was not testimony. It was a maintenance log — but the reconstruction pulled the paraphrase off it like skin off fruit, and underneath, in plain original text, it read:

*Core 7 seal compromised during evacuation. Child present in bay. Child unaccounted for in final manifest. Audio recording of bay ambient (4 hrs 12 min) appended to Core 7. DO NOT PURGE. — M. Adeyemi, Archive Warden*

Casper read it three times. Their hands had begun to shake, and they let them.

*A child present in the bay.* A child unaccounted for. An audio recording — four hours and twelve minutes of it — appended to Core 7, the very core sitting third from the left on the console, amber seal and all, with a warden's note *DO NOT PURGE* written in defiance of whatever order was coming down from above.

Someone had hidden the evidence inside the archive itself. Not in defiance of the redaction — *inside* it, smuggled through the one place the falsifiers would never look with sufficient care, because they believed their own edit was total. The warden had understood the falsifiers' arrogance and had used it as a hiding place. And the child —

The child in the bay. The child's voice Casper had heard in the dark, reciting phoneme drills. Not a ghost. Not a malfunction. A recording. A child who had been in this room, in this exact dark, while —

Casper made themselves not finish the thought. Not yet. Finish the decryption first. Feelings later. That was the discipline, and for once the discipline felt like a kindness.

* * *

The work after that was the best work of Casper's life, and they knew it even as they did it, the way you know a perfect wave while you're inside it.

Core 7 was sealed under the same grammatical lock as the rest, but now Casper had the accent, and the accent was everything. They queried Core 7 with the doubled qualifiers, mimicking the falsification system's own voice — speaking to it, in effect, in its mother tongue — and the seal responded the way a suspicious border guard responds to a flawless native speaker: with doubt, with delay, and finally, grudgingly, with access to the *sanitized* layer.

The sanitized layer of Core 7 was four hours of audio, scrubbed to silence. But scrubbing audio, Casper discovered, was not the same as deleting it. The redaction system worked on text and metadata natively; for audio it had done something cruder and more desperate. It had overwritten the audible band with generated noise — but noise, true noise, is expensive to fake, and the system had been a bureaucracy, and bureaucracies cut corners. Beneath the generated hiss, at -40 decibels, in the sub-audible floor of the recording, the original signal survived like a body under snow.

Casper stripped the noise floor, amplified, filtered, and listened.

The first voice was a woman's, mid-sentence, as if the recording had caught her already speaking:

*"—telling you they're not going to process the manifests, they're going to burn the whole bay, Adeyemi said twenty minutes, we have to move the cores to the—"*

A pause. Then a different voice, farther from the pickup, young, male, frightened in the specific way of someone trying not to frighten someone else:

*"Then we move them. All of them. And the—"*

And then a third sound that stopped Casper's heart in their chest like a hand closing around it: a child's voice, small, asking a question that the noise floor had half-eaten, and the woman answering — and Casper knew the answer's shape before the reconstruction resolved it, knew it the way you know a chord's name from its color:

*"—because names are how they find people, baby. So for right now, you don't have one. You're just a sound in the archive. Can you be a sound in the archive for me?"*

And the child — Vess, it had to be Vess, the ghost on the manifest, the name that shouldn't exist — the child said, in a voice so steady it broke something open in Casper's chest:

*"Yes. I'm good at sounds."*

Casper sat back. The emergency lights hummed. The countdown said 29:47:33 and Casper did not hear it, did not hear anything but the after-ring of that small voice, *I'm good at sounds*, a nine-year-old's taxonomy of her own survival, and somewhere above them Sefa's voice crackled through the suit radio asking for a status report and Casper could not, for a long moment, make their mouth form words, because the words that wanted to come out were not report-words, they were grief-words, and there would be time for those later, later, later —

* * *

They worked the next nine hours in a state Casper had no honest name for. Exhilaration was the clinical term and it was true, but it was exhilaration with a blade in it — the razor joy of a pattern completing, each decrypted fragment clicking into the next with the terrible rightness of a proof assembling itself. The falsified record, read as a foreign language, gave up its secrets the way all coerced languages do: reluctantly, then all at once.

The evacuation logs, de-paraphrased, told of an order disputed. The disputed order, reconstructed, told of a crew that refused. The refusal, timestamped against the forged compliance logs, gave Casper the shape of the lie's architecture: the massacre had happened in the eleven-day window the redaction obsessively guarded, and the falsification had been applied *afterward*, in a single coordinated pass, by someone with system-wide authority — someone whose credentials appeared, half-scrubbed, in the edit metadata, truncated to a rank and the first letter of a name.

*CMDR R—*

Casper looked at those five characters for a long time and did not let themselves finish the name. Not yet. The record would give it up when the record was ready, and until then they would keep the grief in a sealed compartment, the way Vess's mother had sealed her daughter in the archive bay: protected by the same walls that made it a prison.

What they had, when the countdown read 21:12:40, was this: four hours and twelve minutes of ambient bay audio from the day of the massacre, partially recovered. A maintenance warden's defiant note. A child's voice. A crew's refusal, in fragments, timestamped. The skeleton of the proof — not whole, not yet whole, but *load-bearing*. Enough to show Sefa. Enough to show that the method worked, that the grammar of the lie could be fully interrogated, that given time —

Given time. Casper laughed, once, without humor, and the suit radio carried it to no one. Given twenty-one hours, and a failsafe built to erase everything, and a station full of people who would very much prefer this ship to become a fireball before anyone read its grammar aloud.

They were assembling the next fragment — a crew manifest reconstruction, the names of the four hundred re-emerging from their paraphrased graves one by one, and Casper was whispering them aloud as they surfaced, a roll call, a resurrection, *Okafor, Adisa — Okafor, Amara — Okafor, Vess, age nine* — when the console chimed.

Not the countdown. Not the archive. An external channel, riding the Meridian's dead comms like a voice through a keyhole. Casper's hand went to the accept key and stopped, because the channel ID was one they knew, one they'd know anywhere, one that meant the dock, the *Kestrel's Lament*, and a co-pilot who was supposed to be sitting on her hands and asking no questions.

Dree. Calling from the dock. Calling *now*, when Casper had left her three messages and she'd returned none, and the channel was flagged — Casper saw it with a cold drop in the stomach — *flagged as monitored*.

The message was text, not voice. Dree was too good to speak. It was six words long, and Casper read it twice, and the countdown read 20:58:03, and outside in the dark of the drydock something large and official began to dock.

*They found the haul contract, Cas.*
