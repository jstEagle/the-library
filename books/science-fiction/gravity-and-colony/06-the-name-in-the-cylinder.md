## 6. The Name in the Cylinder

The decoder took eleven hours.

Beatrix spent most of them pretending she wasn't afraid. She cleaned the galley twice, recalibrated the air scrubbers that didn't need it, and reorganized a cache of smuggled memories she'd hauled for three years without once opening. Busywork. The oldest trick in the loner's handbook—keep your hands moving so your mind can't catch up with what you already know.

Juno sat across from her at the workbench, sleeves rolled, hair pinned up with whatever came to hand—a stylus, then a length of wire, then nothing at all. She'd been like that since they'd cracked the cylinder's outer shell: silent, focused, lit from within by the terminal glow and something fiercer underneath. The archivist had gone quiet the way other people went loud.

"Integrity check on the memory lattice," Juno said, not looking up. "It's degraded at the edges. Whatever they used to wipe the official records, it wasn't clean. It was fast."

"Fast means sloppy," Beatrix said. "Sloppy means we get to read it."

"Sloppy also means fragments." Juno finally looked at her, and there was something careful in her face, the way you're careful around someone standing on ice. "Beatrix. When this plays back, it won't be tidy. It'll be a dying woman's last hour. Are you ready for that?"

*No,* Beatrix thought. *I've never been ready for anything in my life. That's how I've survived.*

"I bought contraband grief for a living," she said instead. "I've carried other people's worst days through customs checkpoints in my own skull. One more won't kill me."

Juno held her gaze a moment longer, then nodded and pressed the key.

* * *

The memory opened like an eye.

They experienced it the old way—direct feed, no mediation, because the cylinder predated modern interface protocols by two centuries. It poured into them raw: cold, noise, the taste of copper and burnt polymer.

A corridor. Emergency lighting strobing red. A woman running—not away from something but *toward* it, toward a sound, a voice, a child's voice calling a name that the memory's owner knew better than her own heartbeat. The deck shuddered underfoot. Somewhere behind them, hull plating screamed as it tore.

*This is the Meridian,* Beatrix realized, and her stomach dropped through the deck. *Not a station. Not a port. This is the colony itself—the orbital ring, before it fell.*

The woman reached a junction. Turned. And Beatrix saw her face.

Not the runner's—the memory-owner's, reflected in a viewport as she passed. Young. Dark-haired. Cheekbones Beatrix had seen every morning of her childhood in a cracked mirror above a washbasin on Ceres, in a face that leaned over hers to sing songs in a language no one else spoke.

The reflection wore her grandmother's face.

"No," Beatrix said aloud. Her voice sounded like it came from underwater. "No, that's—that's not possible, she was born on Vesta, she told me—"

"Beatrix." Juno's hand found hers on the bench. Warm. Steady. "Keep watching."

But the memory kept watching *her*. Because now the runner had stopped, and the memory-owner—the young woman with the familiar face—had turned to look directly into the recording implant of whoever held the cylinder, and she was speaking, and the words were in Meridian Standard, and Beatrix understood every one of them without translation because her grandmother had sung her to sleep in that language and called it a lullaby from nowhere, a dead tongue, just sounds, just music:

*"If anyone finds this—I am Ilsa Sterling-Vane, second engineer, Meridian Orbital Ring. They told us it was quarantine. They told us the corporation was sending help. The ships that came were not help. If you are hearing this, do not trust the record. Do not trust the ledger. We existed. Write us down. Please. Write us down—"*

The name hit like decompression.

*Sterling.*

Her name. Her family's name, spoken by a dying woman two hundred years dead, in a language Beatrix had been told was nobody's inheritance.

The memory lurched forward—fire now, the runner dragging the cylinder's original owner through smoke, the child's voice cutting off mid-cry—and Beatrix felt herself being pulled under by it, drowning in someone else's final hour, except it wasn't someone else's, it was *hers*, it was written into her bones, her grandmother's face burning in a corridor while corporate boarding torpedoes breached the ring—

The playback ended. The terminal dimmed. Silence rushed in like vacuum.

* * *

For a long time neither of them moved.

Then Beatrix stood up too fast, knocked her chair over, and didn't bother catching it. She crossed to the porthole and stared out at the black, at the distant glitter of the shipping lanes where Halcyon Dynamics' freighters crawled like patient insects along routes her family's blood had paid to open.

"She lied to me." Her voice came out flat and strange. "My grandmother. Every story about Vesta, about the mining camps, about how we came up from nothing—she lied. Or—" She turned. "Or she was the lie. Someone made her a new history the way they made the colony a new absence."

Juno had righted the chair. She sat very still, hands folded, the way she did in archives when she was handling something irreplaceable. "Ilsa Sterling-Vane," she said softly. "Second engineer. Beatrix, do you understand what this is? Not the cylinder. The *name*. Vane. Inspector Vane's house has been hunting your family since before either of us was born. They didn't just erase the Meridian—they absorbed its survivors' identities, scattered them, rewrote their lines. You're not descended from the erased."

She looked up, and her eyes were wet and furious on Beatrix's behalf.

"You *are* the erased. You're what's left of them."

Beatrix laughed. It came out wrong, jagged, nothing like laughter. "Twenty years I built myself into nobody. No family registry, no lineage flags, no biometric ties past my mother—scrubbed, all of it, scrubbed so clean I thought I'd done it myself. I thought I was hiding from creditors." She pressed the heel of her hand against her sternum, as if she could hold something in. "I was hiding from a massacre. My body knew before I did."

"Hey." Juno rose, crossed the space between them, and took Beatrix's face in both hands. Her palms were warm; her grip was certain. "Look at me. Whoever they tried to make you forget—you remember now. That's not a wound, Beatrix. That's a rescue."

Beatrix closed her eyes. For one unguarded moment she let herself lean into it—into Juno, into the terrible new weight of a name that meant fire and betrayal and a child's voice cut off mid-word. When she opened her eyes again, something had settled in her, hard and bright as slag cooling into steel.

"The broker," she said. "Rennick. He offered me four million for the cylinder yesterday. Four million to make it disappear into some syndicate vault." She stepped back, wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, and her voice steadied into the flat cadence she used for business. "He also let slip that Halcyon knows I have it. Which means the offer wasn't an offer. It was a leash."

"So we don't sell."

"So we don't sell." Beatrix turned back to the terminal, where the cylinder's index still glowed, patient as a gravestone. "We decode the rest. Every fragment, every corrupted sector, every name in that crew manifest. If they wrote my grandmother out of existence, then I'm going to write her back in—in her own words, in her own language, in front of the whole damn system."

Juno was already pulling up the manifest fragment, cross-referencing against the cylinder's metadata. Her fingers paused on the keys.

"Beatrix. There's a secondary file embedded in the lattice. Encrypted—but the cipher seed is a name."

Whose name? Beatrix almost asked. But she was already reading it off the screen, and the floor tilted beneath her, because the cipher seed, the key to everything the survivor had hidden inside her dying memory, was a single word in Meridian Standard.

*Ilsa.*

Her grandmother's name was the lock. And somewhere in the dark beyond the shipping lanes, she knew—she *knew*, with the certainty of blood—Inspector Vane's people had already decoded the same manifest, and were asking themselves the same question:

*Who is Beatrix Sterling, and why does she carry a dead colony's name?*

The comm panel chimed. An incoming transmission, priority-flagged, corporate seal.

Vane.

Beatrix looked at Juno. Juno looked back. Neither of them reached for the button.

Outside the porthole, a patrol cutter's running lights bloomed against the black, closing slow.
