## 6. Veyra Kostas, Deceased

The name would not leave him alone.

Sable told himself it was procedure. An anomaly in a manifest was an anomaly, and anomalies got investigated, and investigating meant records, and records meant the station's public archive terminal — a slab of flickering glass bolted into the wall of his quarters, older than most of the software running through it, kept alive by patches he'd written himself out of boredom during his first year aboard. Seven years of patchwork. The terminal hummed when he woke it, a sound like a wasp trapped in amber.

He sat down with a bulb of cold tea and typed: **KOSTAS, VEYRA**.

The search spun for four seconds — an eternity on a direct line to the settled nets — and returned almost nothing. That was the first wrong thing. He'd looked up cargo clerks before, dockhands, drone pilots, people whose entire lives were footnotes in logistics databases. Everyone left a wake. Birth registry, work permits, transit visas, tax filings, the sediment of being a person in a networked civilization. Veyra Kostas had left a crater instead.

Three results.

The first was a death certificate. Issued seven years ago, same week as the Meridian Dawn's destruction, by an administrative authority he didn't recognize — a Halcyon Combine subsidiary called Meridian Claims Resolution, which sounded less like a company and more like a euphemism. Cause of death: *unconfirmed, presumed lost in transit*. No body. No wreckage attribution. Just a stamp, and beneath the stamp, in the flat language of bureaucracy: *This record is closed.*

*Closed.* Not sealed. Sealed records could be opened with warrants and grief and lawyers. Closed records were just doors painted onto walls.

The second result was a court docket fragment, partially redacted, from a civil proceeding filed six months before her death. *Kostas v. Halcyon Combine Orbital Holdings.* The document had been gutted — names blacked out, exhibits struck, whole pages replaced with the gray placeholder bars that meant someone had paid a great deal of money to make sure this text would never be read casually. But redaction has a shape. You can tell what was removed by what they bothered to keep. And what they kept was the case number, the filing date, and one surviving sentence of the complaint, sitting there like a bone in ash:

*"...the deliberate depressurization of Habitat Block C-7 at the Kestrel's Reach extraction facility, resulting in the deaths of no fewer than 412 persons, conducted to conceal documented labor-safety violations..."*

Sable read it twice. Then a third time, moving his lips, the way you do when the words refuse to mean anything.

412 people.

He set down the tea because his hands had developed a fine tremor he didn't want to witness. On the Perihelion, nothing happened fast enough to make your hands shake. That was the whole point of the place. It was a hinge between traffic lanes, a place where cargo paused and moved on, where nothing was ever born or buried. He had spent seven years cultivating the art of not shaking.

The third result was a news archive entry, and it was worse than the other two combined, because it was ordinary. A trade publication, *Haulage & Orbit Weekly*, dated the week of the Meridian Dawn's loss. A two-hundred-word item wedged between an ad for thruster lubricant and a piece about tariff disputes in the Jovian approaches. The headline: **AUDITOR IN HABITAT CASE MISSING; COMBINE DENIES INVOLVEMENT**.

He made himself read every word.

Veyra Kostas, thirty-four, had been a senior logistics auditor contracted through an independent firm to review supply-chain records at Kestrel's Reach, a Halcyon-run extraction habitat mining a nickel-iron body out past the frost line. Her audit had found something. The complaint said what: pressure seals logged as inspected that had never been inspected, maintenance budgets routed into shell accounts, a safety certification signed by an engineer who had, according to payroll records, been dead for eleven months. And then — the sentence Sable kept circling back to, the one with teeth — a scheduled decommission test of Block C-7's atmospheric systems that had been run *with full occupancy*, against protocol, against physics, against any reading of the word *test* that a human being could stand behind.

The article quoted a Halcyon spokesperson calling the allegations "categorically false and defamatory." It quoted a labor guild representative saying an investigation was "pending." It did not quote a single survivor, because — Sable checked the follow-up articles, all three of them, each shorter than the last — there weren't supposed to be any. Block C-7 had held 412 workers. The official incident report listed 39 deaths from "a rapid decompression event of undetermined origin," and the discrepancy between those numbers was itself unexplained, unexamined, and eventually unmentioned. The remaining 373 were carried on the books as *contract non-renewals*.

Non-renewals. Four hundred twelve human beings, and the corporation's ledger had absorbed them the way a hull absorbs micrometeorites — without structural consequence, without even a sound anyone was required to hear.

Sable became aware that he was standing. He didn't remember getting up. His tea sat forgotten, going colder, and the terminal light lay across the room like something spilled.

He knew that habitat.

Not the name — he'd have remembered the name, surely, he'd have felt it land — but the *shape* of it. Rotating hab rings around a processing spine, the worker blocks stacked along the inner curve where gravity ran honest, the company sections up in the low-spin crown where everything was carpeted and quiet. He knew the smell of recycled air in a place like that, faintly sweet with hydroponics runoff. He knew the sound the deck plates made at shift change, hundreds of boots finding their rhythm.

And he knew it because Mei had worked one.

His sister. The word arrived in his mind with no attached memories, which was the cruelest way for a word to arrive — pure definition, zero weight, like knowing the price of something you'd never owned. *Mei Ishikawa. Sister.* He had reconstructed her existence over the years from fragments: a personnel file he'd found early on listing her as a life-support systems technician, a photograph in his own effects showing a young woman with his same dark eyes making an obscene gesture at the camera, the fact that when he thought the word *home*, some ruined circuitry in him still tried to point somewhere before failing.

Mei had spent nine years as a habitat tech. Nine years crawling through air ducts and scrubber banks and pressure bulkheads exactly like the ones that had failed — or been failed — in Block C-7. She used to joke, according to nothing, according to no memory at all, only to the shape of a joke he somehow knew she would have made: that her job was to be the person who stood between four hundred people and vacuum, and that nobody thanked the wall.

Four hundred twelve.

He pulled up the Kestrel's Reach casualty annex, such as it existed — a supplemental table in the back of the incident report, names reduced to ID numbers, and the ID numbers themselves mostly redacted under privacy statutes that Halcyon's legal division had presumably helped draft. But redaction has a shape. And when he cross-referenced the visible fragments — initials, birth-year bands, job classifications — against the one document he had of Mei's, her old employment contract that he'd kept folded in a drawer for reasons he couldn't explain and had never needed to,

the classification matched.

Life-support systems technician. Habitat interior ring.

He didn't know if she was in Block C-7. He didn't know if she was alive or dead, if she'd transferred off years before, if the woman in the photograph had gone on to some other rock, some other ring, some other shift change rhythm of boots on deck plate. The record didn't say. The record had been *closed*.

But he knew — with the certainty that lives below thought, in the body, in the scar tissue where his wiped years used to be — that she had been there. That she had been standing at a bulkhead control when the atmosphere left, or running toward one, or screaming into a comm that nobody answered because answering would have created a record.

Something in Sable's chest went very quiet and very large, the way space does.

He thought about the manifest. *Meridian Dawn, passenger: V. Kostas, timestamp one hour before the ship burned.* He thought about the sequence assembling itself in the dark of the archives: an auditor finds a massacre, files suit, becomes a problem, books passage on a freighter under whatever protection anonymity offered — and within the hour, the freighter is a fireball falling out of the sky, and within the week, she is legally dead, and within the month, the docket is gutted and the story is a two-hundred-word item between ads for lubricant.

That wasn't coincidence. Coincidence didn't have that kind of efficiency. That was a machine doing what machines do — and Halcyon Combine was the largest machine in inhabited space, with tentacles in every port authority and every claims office and every newsroom that mattered.

They hadn't killed a whistleblower. They had killed a *record*. They had killed the version of events where 412 people died on purpose, and they had done it so cleanly that the only evidence left was a dead woman's name on a ghost manifest, surfacing seven years later in a routine audit on a relay station at the ass end of nowhere, in front of the one man in the galaxy who had a reason to care.

Or — and here the thought turned over, showing its underside —

in front of the one man who had been *placed* here.

The unease from the archives came back, colder now. Someone had queried the Meridian Dawn data with his credentials three years ago. Someone had put him on this station after a wipe he never consented to. Someone had built his seven quiet years the way you build a dam — to hold something back, or to hold something *down*.

Sable closed the terminal. The wasp-in-amber hum died, and the room went dark except for the slow rotation of status lights, green and patient, the heartbeat of a station that had never lied to him because it had never been asked anything that mattered.

He stood at the viewport for a long time. Contested orbits wheeled below, debris and drones and the distant burn of legitimate traffic, all of it indifferent, all of it continuing. Somewhere out there was a burned ship and a closed file and a habitat-shaped hole where 412 people used to breathe, and somewhere out there — maybe — was a grave with his sister's name on it, or worse, no grave at all, just a non-renewal in a corporate ledger.

Grief needs an object. That's what makes it bearable — you can aim it, bury it, visit it. What Sable felt had no object yet, only a direction, and directions are worse, because you can't stop walking toward them.

"Okay," he said aloud, to the empty room, to the dead woman in the file, to the sister he couldn't remember. His voice sounded strange, unused to anger. "Then I'm asking."

He reopened the terminal. And because he was a technician, because he had spent seven years learning every patched seam of this station's nervous system, he didn't go back through the public gate where his search would be logged and priced and sold. He went in through the deep archive, through the maintenance layer, through the dark.

The query he ran next was simple, and it was the kind of simple that gets people killed:

*Show me every packet that has ever transited Perihelion carrying the string KOSTAS.*

The search began to spin.

It was still spinning — longer than four seconds now, much longer, chewing through seven years of buffered traffic — when an alert chimed at the edge of his display. Automatic, routine, ignorable.

Except it wasn't routine. It was a handshake request from deep space. From the direction of Terminus Deep, where there was supposed to be nothing but static and dead relays.

A signal, faint and repeating, trying to say something.

* * *

The alert pulsed again, patient as a second heartbeat, while the archive search ground through its seventh year of packets.

Sable stared at it. Two impossible things on one screen, and his hands — steady now, steadier than they'd been all day, because fear past a certain threshold stops being a tremor and becomes a kind of clarity — hovered over the controls.

He could answer the handshake. Or he could let the search finish.

He touched neither. Outside the viewport, the contested orbits turned, and somewhere in them, he understood, the machinery that had erased Veyra Kostas was still turning too — patient, efficient, and very good at finding people who started asking questions.

The signal repeated. Waiting.
