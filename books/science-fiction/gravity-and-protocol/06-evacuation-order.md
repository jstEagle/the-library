## 6. Evacuation Order

The notice arrived the way all bad news arrived on Meridian-9: late, badly formatted, and addressed to a colony designation that hadn't been accurate in eleven years.

Sefa Okonjo read it three times at her kitchen table while her tea went cold. *By order of Colonial Command, Meridian-9 is hereby designated structurally condemned. Mandatory evacuation to commence within ten (10) standard days. All residents will be processed for relocation.*

"Processed," she said aloud, to the empty room. "Like meat."

She'd spent thirty years as an administrator on worlds that mattered — budget hearings, water rights, a term on the Meridian sector council before anyone had heard of this rock. She knew what "processed" meant. It meant cargo manifests. It meant families assigned numbers instead of names. It meant that somewhere in a warm office orbiting a warmer star, someone had finally gotten around to signing the paperwork that turned four hundred people into a rounding error.

And she knew, with the particular clarity of a woman who had survived too many committees, that the timing was no accident. Not ten days after an unregistered transmission started whispering through their dead relay stations. Not ten days after a military intelligence officer arrived asking surgical questions with a smile like a scalpel.

Condemned. The colony had been falling apart for decades. The hydroponics bay leaked, the southern hab ring had been on backup power since the war's second year, and Command had never once sent so much as a spare gasket. But now, suddenly, urgently, they cared.

Sefa finished her cold tea, pulled on her coat, and went to wake the colony.

* * *

The town hall — which was really a converted grain silo with folding chairs and a heating unit that sounded like a dying animal whenever anyone spoke above a murmur — filled faster than Sefa had ever seen it.

She stood at the front with the notice projected on the wall behind her, its official seal rendered in flickering amber because the projector bulb was older than most of the audience.

"Ten days," she told them. "That's what they're giving us. Ten days to pack up our lives and board transports to processing stations I wouldn't wish on my worst enemy, and I have served with several of my worst enemies, so I know whereof I speak."

A ripple of grim laughter. Good. They'd need it.

Marta Chen, who ran the seed bank and had opinions about everything, raised her hand without waiting to be called on, as usual. "Where are they sending us?"

"The notice says 'relocation facilities appropriate to displaced populations.'" Sefa let the words sit there like something unpleasant found under a chair. "Which is bureaucratic for: wherever's cheapest."

"And if we refuse?" That was old Devi, from the back row, who had refused things since before Sefa was born and considered it her primary contribution to colonial governance.

"Then we become squatters on condemned property, and they have legal grounds to remove us however they like." Sefa held up a hand before the murmuring could swell. "I'm not saying we roll over. I'm saying we think carefully. Because here's what I've noticed, and I want you all to notice it too."

She tapped the projector. The notice glowed.

"This order is dated nine days ago. Nine days ago, Captain Sol walked off that transport with a warrant and a list of questions. Nine days ago, somebody's relay station started singing in a language nobody here speaks." She paused. "Command has ignored our maintenance requests for six years. Our last supply ship came fourteen months late and short-staffed. But now, suddenly, we're an emergency. Now, suddenly, we must be moved *immediately*, before anyone can finish asking inconvenient questions."

Silence. Then Marta again: "You think they're clearing the field."

"I think," Sefa said, "that when powerful people suddenly remember you exist, it's worth asking why. And I think the answer is standing right there." She nodded toward the third row, where Lior Pemberton sat very still, half in shadow, watching her with those careful eyes that always seemed to be translating even silence.

She hadn't planned to say that part. But she was tired, and frightened, and tired-frightened people tell the truth.

Lior didn't flinch. That was the thing about them — Lior flinched at kindness, at inclusion, at being handed a cup of tea, but accusation just slid off. They'd been accused by professionals.

After the meeting broke up, colonists clustered in the cold outside, talking in low voices, and Sefa watched the shape of it happen: not panic, not obedience. Conversation. People pairing off, planning, deciding — informally, wordlessly, the way colonies decide things when nobody wants to be officially on record — that they were going to help.

Devi caught Sefa's elbow. "The linguist," she said. "They know something. Something worth condemning a whole colony over."

"Yes."

"Then we keep them safe until they can do whatever it is they need to do." Devi shrugged, eighty years of stubbornness in one shoulder. "We've got nothing left to lose except each other, and I've been losing things my whole life. I'm out of practice caring. Might as well practice on something important."

Sefa squeezed her hand. "Careful, Devi. That's how revolutions start."

"At my age? Let them come. I'll bore them to death."

* * *

Veyra Sol did not wait for the chaos to settle. She waded into it.

Sefa watched her work the evacuation lines over the next two days with something close to professional admiration. The captain had traded her interrogation-room courtesy for helpfulness — carrying crates, checking manifests, offering to expedite "priority cases." Every conversation was a net. *Where were you during the transmission window? Who uses the north relay path? Your son seems nervous — has he been under strain?*

"She's fishing," Sefa muttered to Lior on the second evening, in the admin office that doubled as her bedroom. "And she's good at it. Better than the last three intelligence officers Command sent, and I've buried all three in paperwork."

"You've met officers like her before?"

"I've *been* officers like her before, dear. Thirty years ago, briefly, before I realized the job was mostly making cruelty look like procedure." Sefa looked at Lior properly — thin, sleepless, hands stained with ink from hand-copying translations because paper couldn't be remotely seized. "How much longer do you need?"

"A few more days. The message is long. It wasn't written to be read quickly — it was written to survive being read slowly, by people who might be afraid." Lior's mouth twisted. "Whoever sent it understood fear very well."

"Well, they picked the right audience." Sefa rubbed her eyes. "Get back to work. And Lior — stop going through the east corridor. Veyra's posted a drone there. Use the old laundry ducts. Ugly, but ugly is safe."

Something flickered across Lior's face — surprise, then that painful gratitude Sefa had learned to expect and gently deflect. "Why are you doing this? You barely know me."

"Because you're a terrible liar, a worse houseguest, and the only person on this rock who can read whatever's killing us." Sefa waved a hand. "Also because nobody should have to disappear alone. I did it for two years after the council. It's overrated."

* * *

It fell apart on the fourth day, and it fell apart because of Tam.

Tam Okonjo-Reyes — Sefa's grandnephew, seventeen, all elbows and conviction — had appointed himself Lior's courier sometime around day two, without consulting anyone, because that was how Tam did everything. He ran messages between Lior's shelter and the handful of colonists helping copy and cache the translation. He was quick, loyal, and seventeen, which meant he believed himself invisible in exactly the ways he was not.

Sefa learned of his detention from a neighbor: soldiers had stopped Tam near the laundry ducts, found the handwritten pages sewn into his jacket lining, and taken him to the transport at Landing Field Two.

She was at the perimeter checkpoint within twenty minutes, moving with the unhurried authority of a woman who had terrified bureaucrats on five worlds.

"I want to see my grandnephew."

The soldier at the checkpoint — young, tired, visibly uncomfortable — consulted nothing. "He's being processed, ma'am. Suspicion of aiding unregistered communications. You can file a—"

"I don't file things. I *am* things. I am the senior colonial administrator of this settlement, which makes that boy a minor in my custody, which makes your captain currently in violation of eleven articles of the Colonial Protection Act, and I will recite all eleven if you'd like, I have time, I'm being evicted in six days." She smiled. It was not a kind smile. "Fetch her."

Veyra came herself, of course. She always did. She stepped out of the command tent into the grey afternoon light, hands clasped behind her back, expression composed into something almost apologetic.

"Administrator Okonjo."

"Captain Sol. You've arrested a child."

"I've detained a person in possession of classified material during an active security operation. He'll be treated well. He's cooperating." A pause, delicate as a blade laid flat. "He's very loyal, your grandnephew. Loyalty is admirable. It's also legible. He's already told us quite a lot without meaning to — about routes, about timing, about who else has been helping."

Sefa felt the ground tilt under her, the specific vertigo of realizing that other people's courage has a price tag and you're holding the bill. Tam. Sixteen hours of interrogation at most, and he'd already given away the laundry ducts, the copying circle, probably Devi's name.

"He's a boy protecting a guest," Sefa said. "In some jurisdictions, that's called hospitality."

"In mine, it's called evidence." Veyra's voice stayed gentle, which somehow made it worse. "Here's what happens next, Administrator. In six days, this colony is evacuated. Everyone boards transports. Everyone is processed. Unless—" she let the word hang, courteous, terrible, "—certain materials are surrendered voluntarily beforehand. In which case certain detentions might be resolved as misunderstandings. Children make mistakes. So do adults who shelter them."

There it was. Not an arrest — an invoice.

Sefa looked past Veyra's shoulder, toward the transport where Tam sat in a lit room answering questions because he was seventeen and brave and brave people break beautifully. She thought of Lior in the dark with their ink-stained hands, translating a dead language's plea for peace. She thought of Devi, bored and ready for revolution. Of four hundred people who had decided, without a single vote, to care about someone — and of what that caring now cost.

For eleven years, Sefa had kept her head down. Had filed nothing, protested nothing, been nobody, and it had protected no one. Not Tam. Not the colony. Only herself, and only barely.

"No deal, Captain," she said quietly. "You can process every one of us. But you should know — you're not evacuating a colony anymore. You're evacuating witnesses."

Something moved behind Veyra's eyes — fast, quickly suppressed. Doubt, maybe. Or recognition.

Sefa turned and walked back toward the settlement, already composing the message she would send Lior through the ugly safe ducts: *They have Tam. They know the routes. Whatever you're finishing — finish it tonight.*
