## 7. The Relay Graveyard

The ash flats began where the last hydroponics dome ended, which was to say: abruptly, without ceremony, the way most things ended on Meridian-9. One moment there was the smell of nutrient solution and Sefa's stubborn tomato vines; the next, a plain of grey powder stretching to a horizon that seemed embarrassed to exist.

"The array is forty kilometers out," Sefa said, cinching the strap on her pack with the efficiency of a woman who had been packing for disasters her whole life and found them mostly disappointing. "We walk tonight, we shelter at the old survey station when the weather turns — and it will turn, it always turns — and we reach the relays by tomorrow dusk."

"You say that like the weather has a schedule."

"Everything here has a schedule, Lior. It's just a schedule written by someone who hated us." She handed me a rebreather mask, its filter canister rattling loosely in its housing. I looked at it. She looked at me looking at it. "It works," she said, in the tone of a woman daring the universe to prove otherwise.

I put it on.

We walked.

* * *

There is a particular silence that lives on an ash flat, and I want to be precise about it, because precision was the only prayer I'd ever learned. It is not the silence of empty places. It is the silence of buried ones. Under our boots, somewhere beneath two meters of grey, lay the first settlement — the real Meridian-9, the one from the brochures, with its promised rivers and its terraforming gardens. The colony hadn't failed so much as been quietly covered over, the way inconvenient documents get covered over, the way languages get covered over, the way people do.

Sefa walked ahead of me with a surveyor's lamp and the unhurried stride of someone who considered forty kilometers a reasonable morning. She was sixty-three years old and had outlived three colonial administrations, two evacuations-that-weren't, and one husband who had apparently died mid-argument, which she described as "the only time he ever won."

"Tell me about the relay," she said, not turning around. Her voice carried strangely in the flat air, close and far at once.

"It's pre-collapse infrastructure. Twelve towers in a ring, built to bounce signals off the orbital mirrors back when this system still had orbital mirrors." I adjusted my mask; the filter rattled its skeptical rattle. "If even three of the towers are intact, I can drive a signal through the whole planetary grid. Every receiver. Every helmet. Every screen in every barracks."

"And Veyra Sol will hear it too."

"That's rather the point."

"Mm." A pause, filled only by wind and boots. "And after she hears it, what exactly stops her from putting a bolt through your chest and calling it static?"

I had thought about this, in the way one thinks about falling — often, uselessly, with a certain grim fascination. "The truth is heavy," I said finally. "Once it's loose, you can't shoot it back into the box. Everyone who hears it becomes a witness. Shooting me would only make me louder."

"You sound like a woman who's read her own obituary and found it flattering."

"I sound like a woman who's run out of alternatives and decided to find that liberating."

That got a laugh out of her — short, rough, genuine. It did something strange to my chest. I realized, with a small internal jolt, that I couldn't remember the last time I'd made anyone laugh on purpose.

The towers rose out of the dark around midnight, or what passed for midnight under Meridian-9's permanent bruised twilight. Twelve of them, or the skeletons of twelve, leaning at angles that suggested they had been arguing with gravity for two centuries and were losing gracefully. Their guy-wires hummed in the wind like enormous detuned strings. Ash had drifted against their bases in long pale dunes, so that they seemed to grow out of the ground rather than stand on it — a graveyard of giants, half-buried, still faintly warm with purpose.

I stood among them and felt something I hadn't felt since before the tribunal: competence. Not hope. Hope was a luxury good. But competence — the plain animal pleasure of being the right person in the right place with the right terrible knowledge.

Then the weather turned, because everything here kept its schedule.

* * *

The storm came out of the west like a debt collector. We saw it first as a smudge, then a wall, then a fact. Sefa didn't panic; she simply changed course with the calm of a woman rerouting a delivery, and shouted over the rising wind that the old survey station was six kilometers north, dug into a ridge, built to outlast "optimism."

We reached it as the sky came down.

The station was a concrete bunker half-swallowed by the ridge, its door swollen shut until Sefa hit it twice with her shoulder in a manner that suggested the door and she had unresolved history. Inside: dust, four bunks, a rusted heater, a crate of emergency rations expired during the previous century, and a wall of small square windows glazed with something that had stopped being transparent decades ago.

We got the heater lit. We got our masks off. We sat on opposite bunks while the storm leaned its whole weight against the building and the building, bless it, declined to comment.

Sefa opened two ration packs, sniffed both, gave me the less offensive one, and settled in with the sigh of a woman preparing to wait out something larger than weather.

"So," she said.

"So."

"You've been living among us for eleven years, Lior Pemberton. You fix our translators, you teach Tam's little sister reading, you attend every community dinner and eat exactly one portion and say exactly nothing about yourself." She bit into her ration bar with the deliberation of a judge. "Tomorrow you're going to commit treason on my behalf. I figure I'm owed a story."

The wind screamed. Somewhere above us, a piece of the roof chose violence and lost.

I looked down at the ration bar in my hands. And I understood, suddenly, that this was the moment I had been walking toward for eleven years — not the relay, not the broadcast, but this bunk, this dust, this stubborn old woman asking for the truth the way other people ask for salt.

"You know why I was exiled," I said. "The official version."

"Refused a direct order. Weaponized translation protocols. Disgraced the Linguistics Corps." She recited it flatly, the way one recites a weather report. "I also know the official version of why this colony exists, and I live in it, so forgive me if I assume the paperwork lies."

"The paperwork doesn't lie, exactly. It just tells the truth in a language designed so no one can read it." I turned the ration bar over. "You know what xenolinguistics actually is, Sefa? At the top levels? It's not poetry. It's not first contact wonder. It's targeting. You learn how a species thinks so you can predict what it will do, and then you learn how to say things to it that will make it do what you want. Surrender. Retreat. Trust."

The heater ticked. The storm pressed.

"My assignment was the Kesseth. The 'enemy.' Eight years embedded with their diplomatic transmissions, learning their language family from the inside." I heard my own voice go distant, the way it did when I recited things I'd rehearsed hating. "In year six, I found a pattern in their communications. A recurring structure in their formal registers — a grammatical construction that appeared only in messages directed at us. I ran it through every framework I had. Do you know what it was?"

Sefa shook her head slowly.

"A borrowed grammar. They'd taken the structure of a human language — a dead one, Ashkari — and rebuilt their own peace-language inside it. Because two hundred years ago, when the Ashkar Purges erased that nation from Earth, some of their scholars' work survived in the deep archives, and the Kesseth found it, and studied it, and learned it. As a gift. As a gesture. *We will speak to you in the tongue of your wounded, because we believe your wounded deserve a voice.*" My hands had gone still. "They have been asking for ceasefire in a human language for generations. And the Corps classified my finding, reassigned me, and asked me to build them a phrase-set instead — constructions that would trigger compliance behavior in Kesseth listeners. A leash made of grammar."

"And you said no."

"I said no in front of a review board, which in retrospect was not the venue for nuance." I laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "They took my clearance, my archive access, my name from the public record. They sent me here. And I told myself exile was a mercy — that disappearing was the dignified choice, the safe choice. Eleven years I told myself that." I finally looked up at her. "But it wasn't dignity, Sefa. It was hiding. I let them bury me the way they buried this colony, and I called the ash a home."

The wind howled through some gap in the roof, a long descending note, almost musical.

Sefa chewed her ration bar. Swallowed. Took her time, because Sefa took her time with everything that mattered.

"When my husband died," she said, "I found letters he'd written his brother during the first evacuation order. Never sent. Forty of them. All saying the same thing in different ways: *I should have spoken up.*" She brushed crumbs from her knee with great care. "He wasn't a coward. He was tired, and tired looks like cowardice from the inside. So does fear. So does grief." She met my eyes. "You hid. Fine. You're done hiding. That's the whole biography as far as I'm concerned."

"Sefa—"

"Eleven years, and you fixed every translator on this rock, taught half the children to read, and never once asked any of us for anything." She pointed the remains of her ration bar at me like a small edible gavel. "People think belonging is something you're given. It isn't. It's something you use up by needing each other. You've been spending it all along, Lior. Tomorrow you're just making the payment visible."

I don't remember deciding to cry. I only remember that it happened, quietly, undramatically, while the storm tried its best to bury the ridge and the ridge held, the way Sefa held, the way — I was beginning to suspect — most of the world held, not through strength but through sheer refusal to leave.

She pretended not to notice, which was the kindest thing anyone had ever done for me.

* * *

The storm broke near dawn. We climbed out into air scrubbed clean and cold, the ash flat glittering under the bruised light like a sea of ground glass. The relay towers stood against the brightening east, twelve broken fingers pointing at a sky that had finally, briefly, agreed to be beautiful.

Sefa checked her lamp, her mask, the charge on the old signal booster she'd brought against my objections. Then she paused, squinting north along the ridge line.

"Lior," she said. "Those tracks weren't there last night."

I followed her gaze. In the fresh ash, crisp and unweathered, a line of boot prints descended from the north — military tread, regulation spacing, at least six walkers — heading directly for the tower ring.

And behind them, smaller, half-obscured, the prints of someone being escorted. Someone whose stride I knew. Someone who limped slightly on the left, because Tam had twisted that ankle jumping the drainage channel behind the schoolhouse two winters ago, and I had been the one who taped it.

"They found him," I whispered.

Sefa was already moving. "Then we stop talking," she said, "and start running."
