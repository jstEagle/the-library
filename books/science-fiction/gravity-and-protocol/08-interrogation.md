## 8. Interrogation

Veyra Sol had conducted two hundred and eleven interviews in her career, and she had a system for all of them. She arrived early. She arranged the chairs at an angle — never opposite, which invited combat, never side by side, which invited conspiracy. She brought tea she didn't drink and offered it anyway, because a cup in someone's hands was a small surrender.

She broke every rule of her own system the morning she sat down across from Lior Pemberton.

The colony's administrative building had been a grain depot once, and it still smelled faintly of rust and old wheat, which was more than could be said for most of Meridian-9. Sefa Okonjo had given her a room with a working lamp and a door that locked from neither side, which Veyra understood to be both an accommodation and an insult, delivered with the same flat courtesy the administrator used for everything.

Lior came in when summoned. That was the first thing that unsettled her. They weren't escorted, weren't nervous, weren't performing innocence. They simply walked in like a colleague arriving late to a seminar, sat down without being asked, and looked at her with the mild, patient attention of someone who had spent thirty years listening to languages nobody else could hear.

"Captain Sol," they said. "You've kept my file in good condition."

"You've read your file?"

"I wrote half of it. The interesting parts." They folded their hands. "The parts where I refused."

* * *

Veyra had rehearsed this conversation on the dropship, in the shower, in the gray hour before dawn when guilt does its best accounting work. She had rehearsed the threat version: *cooperate or be detained*. She had rehearsed the cold version: *you are already guilty of espionage under Statute Nine; the only variable is what happens next*. She had even rehearsed the honest version, once, at three in the morning, before deciding honesty was a luxury she'd forfeited years ago along with sleep.

What she had not rehearsed was Lior Pemberton being *comfortable*.

"You understand why I'm here," she said.

"You think I've been transmitting unregistered signals on colonial bandwidth." Lior said it the way one recites a shopping list. "You're right, incidentally. Though I'd argue about 'unregistered.' I registered with the colony. Sefa has excellent records. Better than yours, probably, given the state of your relay stations."

"That is not—"

"A joke, Captain. I'm told I make them now. It's new. Exile does strange things to a person." They leaned back. "Ask your questions. I'll answer honestly. It's a habit I picked up late in life and I find I rather enjoy it."

Veyra opened her slate. Closed it again. Opened it.

"The transmission," she said. "The one sweeping through the derelict relays. What is it?"

"It's a plea for ceasefire," Lior said. "In Ashkari. Timestamped six years ago, repeating ever since, from a source inside territory Command classifies as hostile. It says, roughly: *We learned your language so you would know we meant peace. We have been saying so for a generation. Please stop killing us long enough to listen.*" A pause. "There's more. A great deal more. But that's the shape of it."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere outside, Tam — the boy Veyra had released yesterday because detaining him had felt less like procedure and more like drowning something small — was arguing cheerfully with a hydroponics pump.

"You expect me to believe that."

"I expect you to verify it. You're Intelligence. Verification is your entire personality." Lior's tone wasn't cruel; it was almost fond. "I'll give you the translation matrix. I'll give you the frequency, the timestamps, the linguistic analysis showing Ashkari grammar patterns that no human organization has used since the Purges. You can take it all back to Command and let their own analysts confirm what I'm telling you."

"And then?"

"And then you'll discover that Command has known for approximately four years, and buried it, and continued authorizing strikes against a species that has been begging to surrender." Lior said it gently, which was somehow worse than if they'd shouted. "That's the part you haven't rehearsed, Captain. I can see it in your face. You rehearsed everything else."

* * *

She should have arrested them then. That was the thought that would keep her up for weeks afterward — *I should have arrested them then.* The evidence was thin but sufficient, the statute was clear, and doubt was not a defense. Two hundred and twelve interviews, and in two hundred and eleven of them she had done exactly what the file required.

Instead she heard herself say: "Why Ashkari? Why would they learn a dead human language?"

"Because dead was the point." Lior's voice softened. "Ashkari was spoken by people your government displaced two centuries ago. Learning it was a message: *we studied your history, including the parts you're ashamed of, and we chose you anyway.* It's the most eloquent peace offering I've ever encountered. And it was designed to be unreadable by anyone who might want to suppress it — because the only people who could read it were the ones who cared enough to keep a murdered language alive." They smiled, tiredly. "Which turned out to be me. One person. On a rock at the end of nowhere. Their entire diplomatic strategy rests on a xenolinguist who was exiled for insubordination."

"There were other linguists."

"There were other linguists who agreed to weaponize translations. I was the last one who didn't." Lior shrugged. "Command made sure of that. Every refusal was quietly ended. Every dissenter reassigned, discredited, retired. I used to think it was efficiency. Now I think it was housekeeping."

Veyra stood up. Walked to the window. The ash flats stretched out gray and patient under a sky the color of dishwater, and somewhere out there was a relay array, and somewhere beyond that was an enemy that had spent six years shouting into a void that had been muffled on purpose.

"I knew your name before I came here," she said. It came out before she could stop it. "Pemberton. The Pemberton Refusal. My instructors used it as a case study. *Here is what happens when sentiment compromises security.*" She laughed, and it sounded wrong in her own ears. "They never mentioned you were right."

"They wouldn't. Being right isn't in the curriculum." Lior watched her. "Captain. What did they promise you for this assignment?"

The question landed like a needle finding a nerve. Because there had been a promise — a reinstatement of her stalled career, a posting she'd wanted, the quiet understanding that bringing in Pemberton would wash away a decade of being the officer who asked inconvenient questions in briefing rooms.

"Restoration," Veyra said finally. "Reinstatement. To be remembered as the one who closed the case."

"To be remembered," Lior repeated. "By whom? By the people who buried a ceasefire for four years?" They shook their head slowly. "I took that offer once, in reverse. They offered me restoration if I'd translate targeting grammars. I said no, and I lost everything, and I have spent nine years telling myself the loss was the price of being decent." They met her eyes. "It wasn't. The loss was nothing. The price was the dreams. Do you know what it's like to stop dreaming in your own language? To feel yourself being *unmade*, word by word, by an institution that doesn't even hate you — you're just a line item?" They paused. "You do know. That's why you rehearsed the honest version at three in the morning. I recognize the symptoms."

"I don't know what you're talking about."

"Of course not." Lior rose, unhurried. "Here is what will happen, Captain. In approximately forty hours, I'm going to broadcast that message across every open channel on this planet, in Ashkari and in Standard, with full authentication and my own testimony attached. Not because I want to defy you — I genuinely don't, defiance is exhausting at my age — but because the alternative is that I die here having been the last person who could have said something, and chose silence twice." They moved toward the door, then stopped. "You can arrest me. You can shoot me. But you cannot unknow what I just told you, and we both know your analysts will confirm it within the day. So the real question isn't whether I transmit."

Veyra's hand was resting on her sidearm, and she hadn't decided to put it there.

"The real question," Lior finished, "is what you'll have restored when you go home. A career? Or a self?" They opened the door. "Think it over. I'm told I make jokes now, but that part wasn't one."

* * *

Afterward, Veyra sat alone in the grain-depot room for a long time.

She ran the verification herself, because she trusted no one else to. Frequency, timestamp, grammar analysis. Her own cryptolinguistic suite — the one Command had certified — flagged the Ashkari structures as authentic within eleven minutes. The source triangulation put the transmitter inside the Kessarin home territories. The message had been repeating for six years.

Six years. She pulled the strike authorization logs. Cross-referenced. There it was, clean as surgery: forty-one engagements authorized against a population transmitting an unconditional ceasefire request, with a redaction stamp over the intelligence annex dated four years ago. Someone had known. Someone senior had known, and stamped it shut, and gone to lunch.

Veyra closed the slate and pressed her palms flat against the table until her hands stopped shaking.

Her orders were clear. Detain Pemberton. Secure the array. Silence the transmission. The law was the law, and she had built her whole life inside it precisely because the alternative — judgment, conscience, the terrible weight of choosing — had seemed unbearable.

But the law, she thought, looking at the redaction stamp glowing on her slate, had apparently been edited too.

Outside, the wind was picking up off the ash flats, and Sefa Okonjo's voice carried through the thin walls, organizing something, arguing with someone, alive in a way that institutions never managed. Veyra checked her chronometer. Thirty-eight hours.

She opened a channel to her own command, hesitated, and closed it again.

Then she opened a different file — her personal one, the one with the reinstatement offer — and read it twice, and found, to her considerable alarm, that it read like a ransom note.

Thirty-eight hours, she thought. Enough time to follow orders.

Or enough time to decide, for the first time in twenty years, what she actually believed.
