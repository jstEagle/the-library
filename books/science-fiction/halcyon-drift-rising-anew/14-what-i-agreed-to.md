## 14. What I Agreed To

Cyrus Sandoval has a way of asking questions that doesn't feel like interrogation. It feels like being read aloud.

We'd been at it for six days by then—sessions in the small room off the Anamnesis's aft gallery where the light comes through amber glass and everything smells faintly of old coolant and someone else's coffee. He'd sit across from me with his journal open, that battered thing he carries like a third hand, and he'd say a word in the fold-language, and I'd say what it did to me, and somewhere between us a sentence would form that neither of us had planned.

The fleet psychologists had tried to recover my missing eleven years with standard techniques: hypnogogic prompting, mnemonic reconstruction, the little electrode cap that made my teeth ache and produced nothing but a headache shaped like regret. Nothing worked. The memories weren't damaged, they said. They were *absent*. A clean excision, surgical, professional.

Cyrus took one look at their reports and laughed—not cruelly, but with the particular delight of a man who has found a typo in an official document.

"They didn't cut anything out," he said. "They translated it."

"Translated it into what?"

"Into nothing you can read." He tapped his temple. "But I can."

I want to be honest here, since honesty is apparently the theme of my second life: that frightened me more than the fold had. The fold had been vast and indifferent, a weather system of time. Cyrus was a person, looking at me, offering to walk into the locked rooms of my own head carrying a key I couldn't see. There are worse things than amnesia. One of them is finding out what you paid to forget.

* * *

On the sixth day he brought the navigation grammar.

He'd spent three nights with the Anamnesis's flight logs—the ones we could still read—and he'd noticed something that made him go quiet in a way I'd learned to distrust. The ship's course corrections through the fold region weren't just numbers. They were structured. Recursive. Self-similar at every scale, from the macro burns down to the micro-adjustments of the attitude thrusters. Nobody pilots like that. Nobody *can* pilot like that; human hands don't think in fractals.

"Somebody taught the ship to speak," he said, "while it was inside."

"Inside the fold."

"Inside the part of the fold where language works differently." He laid out the log printouts on the table between us like a man dealing cards he already knew the value of. "Vess. When you were sixty-two, you were the best navigator in the fleet. You'd have felt this structure even if you couldn't parse it. And your older self—the one who signed off on these courses—she knew exactly what she was doing."

"You're saying I hid something in the flight path."

"I'm saying somebody did. Using your hands." He looked at me with that terrible gentleness of his. "Ready?"

No. Obviously no. But I said yes, because I have never once in two lifetimes said no to a heading.

* * *

Here is how it actually worked, as best as I can explain it to anyone who hasn't had their mind rewired by an alien grammar: Cyrus spoke, and the words weren't words. They were shapes. The fold-language doesn't describe things—it *positions* them, the way a chart positions a ship, except the territory it charts is memory. When he said the word for *return*, I didn't hear *return*. I felt the specific gravity of going back to a place that had changed while you were away, which is, when you think about it, the entire story of my life now.

And the locked rooms started opening.

Not all at once. In pieces. A smell first—ozone and old fabric, the smell of the Anamnesis's command deck in its last year, when the upholstery was failing faster than the crew. Then a sound: my own voice, older, rougher, saying something I couldn't yet make out. Then light—the wrong light, the folded light, the color of a star seen through time like tissue paper.

I was standing on my own bridge. I was sixty-two. My knees hurt. I remember being surprised by how much my knees hurt, which tells you something about what the mind chooses to preserve.

"Cyrus," I said, or tried to say. My mouth wasn't moving. "I'm there."

"I know." His voice came from very far away, steady as a lighthouse. "Don't fight the grammar. Let it carry you. Where are you standing?"

"Helm. My station." The old station, with the worn armrest and the coffee ring I'd never managed to scrub out of the console. "There's someone with me. At the nav well."

"Who?"

And the answer arrived not as a name but as a certainty, cold and complete, the way you know your own handwriting:

*Me.*

* * *

She turned around. Older me. Sixty-two years of deep-space mileage, gray shot through the black, lines around her eyes like stress fractures in a hull. She looked tired in the way that isn't about sleep—the tiredness of someone who has been carrying a decision for a long time and has finally set it down and can't pick it back up.

She was crying. That undid me before anything else did. I have a memory—had a memory—of being composed at sixty-two. Of being the officer who held. Watching myself cry on my own bridge was like watching a load-bearing wall crack.

The scene assembled itself around her voice in fragments, the way salvage assembles itself into a shape you pretend is the original ship.

"We're nine days inside the fold," she was saying—to the crew? to herself? to some recording? "Nine days where the clocks agree with nothing, including each other. And I've worked it out. I've finally worked out what the fold wants."

Someone asked—I think it was Okonkwo, our engineer, though his voice came to me warped, underwater—asked what she meant by *wants*.

"It's not a place," she said. "It's a ledger. Everything that passes through gets weighed. Time against time. Memory against memory. It's been reading us since we crossed the boundary, and it's found the imbalance."

"The imbalance being what?" Okonkwo again.

"The massacre." Her voice didn't break. She'd have hated it if it broke. "Meridian. We all know, Vess—we all know what the fleet says happened there and we all know what actually happened, because we've seen the sensor archive. Raw. Unedited. Timestamped. The kind of record that ends careers and starts tribunals. We've been carrying it for four months thinking we'd deliver it home and let the admiralty do the right thing."

A pause. In the recovered memory I could feel the pause physically, the way you feel a ship change trim.

"But the fold showed me the future," she said. "Or a future. Enough of one. The Anamnesis goes home and the data cores go dark within the hour—fleet security scrubs them remotely, they've had the capability for years, we were naive to think distance protected us. The crew gets debriefed, reassigned, scattered. Eleven years of our lives get filed under *anomaly* and forgotten. And Meridian stays erased, because erasure is cheaper than confession, and the truce is cheaper than either."

Okonkwo: "So we run. Take the ship somewhere else—"

"And do what? Grow old in the dark holding a secret nobody lives long enough to use?" She wiped her face with the heel of her hand, briskly, like clearing condensation off a viewport. "No. The fold offered terms. It's been offering them since day one; I thought it was noise. It can hold the archive outside of time—in the grammar, in the structure of the passage itself, written into the courses we fly. Time-proof. Scrub-proof. Fleet security can wipe every core we own and the archive will still be there, in the shape of our own wake, waiting for someone who can read it."

"And the price," Okonkwo said. "There's always a price."

Older me looked at him for a long moment. Then at the rest of us—younger me among them, forty-nine years old and trusting her captain completely, which is the part that makes me sick, that's the part I'd give anything to un-know.

"The years," she said. "It takes the years. All of ours. It sends us home young, with the middle torn out, and everyone we left behind will be eleven years older while we're twenty-six again. Our children will be older than us. Our—" and here her voice did catch, once, briefly, on a name I couldn't hold onto, a name that slipped through the recovery like water through a net, and I heard Cyrus make a small sound across the table, and I understood that he'd caught it, that he was writing it down, that someday he would give it back to me and I would have to survive receiving it—

"Our whole lives get spent," she finished. "To buy one true thing."

* * *

The memory gave me the vote. That was the worst part. She put it to the crew—all hands, formal, in the mess hall with the fold-light coming through the ports like light through bruised skin—and she told them the terms plainly, and she told them the alternative plainly, and then she said the words I have been dreading for six days without knowing why:

"I won't decide this for you. But I'll tell you what I'm voting, and why."

And younger me—forty-nine-year-old me, the one who still trusted everything—stood up and voted yes first.

Why? Because it was right? Because it was brave? Because I trusted her? I've turned it over every way since the session ended and I keep landing on the same ugly truth: I voted yes because she asked, and she was me, and I have never in my life known how to disappoint myself.

Twenty-two crew. Twenty-two yes votes. Not one abstention. I keep waiting for the shame to attach to the decision itself and it won't; the decision holds up; the archive was worth it; the truth was worth it. The shame attaches elsewhere—to the ease of it. To how quickly we spent ourselves. To the fact that none of us, casting our votes, thought to ask what it would feel like to be the ones who woke up young, holding a secret we couldn't remember buying.

We agreed to be the receipt and threw away the purchase.

* * *

When Cyrus brought me back—slowly, the way you surface a diver—I sat in the amber light of the aft gallery and shook for a while. Not sobbing. Shaking. Like a structure shedding load.

He didn't touch me. He'd learned that by day two. He just sat there with his journal closed on his knee, which from Cyrus is a gesture of enormous respect: the recorder choosing, for a moment, not to record.

"She hid it in the navigation grammar," I said finally. "The archive. It's in the flight paths. It's been in the flight paths the whole time we've been flying this ship around Halcyon Drift like a lost dog."

"Yes."

"And the fold kept its end. Sent us home young. Wiped the middle." I laughed, and it came out wrong, jagged. "You know what the fleet psychologists wrote in my file? *Memory loss consistent with temporal exposure. No evidence of external tampering.* No evidence of tampering. I tampered with myself. We all did. We signed the consent forms—we must have signed consent forms, there are forms, there are always forms—"

"Vess."

"—and then we paid, and the payment was *us*, and the goods are sitting in a shipping lane written in a language nobody reads except—"

"Except me." He said it quietly. Not boastful. The tone of a man accepting a delivery he's not sure he can lift.

I looked at him. This strange, gentle, broken-open man who dreams in a dead tongue and keeps a journal for an audience of no one, who has spent years documenting a colony the universe is trying to forget, and I thought: of course. Of course it's him. The fold sent the archive home addressed to the only mind in human space that could open it, and the only mind in human space that could open it is sitting across from me smelling faintly of old coffee, watching me with eyes that have seen too much dying to flinch from one woman's grief.

"It's still there," I said. "In the logs. The full raw sensor archive of Meridian. Every timestamp. Every signature. Everything Corvane's people scrubbed from the cores—it's in the courses we flew."

"It's there." He opened the journal. His pen hovered. "Decryption will take time. The grammar is dense—your older self packed it tight, bless her paranoid heart. Days, maybe. And Vess—"

"I know."

"—once we start pulling threads, the fleet will feel it. Security systems notice when their secrets start moving."

"I know." I wiped my face. My hands were steadier than I expected. Younger hands. Hands that hadn't earned their tremor yet. "Then we'd better be fast, and we'd better be sure, because when this comes out, there's no fold to hide behind. No terms. No do-over. Just us and the truth and whatever Corvane does when she finds out her clean little erasure has been sitting in plain sight for forty years, written in the wake of the ship she sent to die quietly."

Cyrus clicked his pen. Somewhere above us, in the amber-lit silence, the Anamnesis hummed—the sound of a ship carrying a massacre in its bones, patient as a letter that has finally reached the address written on it.

"Then let's not waste the hours she bought," he said.

And we got to work.
