## 4. The Woman Who Counts Seeds

Ilsa Reyes had counted eleven thousand four hundred and six seeds that morning, and she was in a foul mood about it.

Not because of the counting. Counting was the one language she trusted completely — numbers never flattered you, never lied to make a meeting go smoother, never said *we'll circle back on that* when what they meant was *no*. Numbers were honest the way people only managed to be when they were asleep or dying.

No, she was in a foul mood because seed number eleven thousand four hundred and seven was missing.

She stood in the doorway of Arboretum Bay Three, tablet in hand, inventory ledger open, and stared at Elodie Winterbourne as though he had personally eaten it.

"You're short one lupine," she said.

Elodie looked up from where he knelt in the loam, soil to his elbows, a smear of it across his cheekbone like war paint applied by someone who didn't understand war. He blinked at her with the mild, unhurried expression of a man who had never once been on time for anything but had also never once missed anything.

"Am I?"

"Eleven four-oh-seven. *Lupinus halcyonis*. Blue-flowering variant, drought-hardy, germination rate sixty-two percent." She let the silence do some work. "It's not in the tray."

"Ah." He wiped his hands on his apron with great ceremony, as if preparing to address a matter of state. "That would be the one I planted."

"You *planted* it."

"I did. Tuesday." He gestured toward a modest patch of turned earth near the bay's eastern window, where the light came down amber and patient through the drift's rotation cycle. "There. Third row, between the yarrow and the thing I'm pretending is going to be lavender."

Ilsa crossed the deck and looked down at the unremarkable square of soil. It looked like soil. It looked like nothing at all.

"That seed is fleet property," she said. "Catalogued. Tracked. It has a lineage going back six generations of cultivation aboard the *Halcyon Ascendant*. You can't just—" she waved a hand at the dirt— "*put it somewhere.*"

"It's a seed, Ilsa. Putting it somewhere is the entire job description."

"The job description is *preserving* it. For the generation ships' future colonies. For children who haven't been born yet, on worlds we haven't found." She heard herself and stopped, because she'd just recited the mission statement from memory, word for word, including the comma placement, and Elodie was looking at her with that small, infuriating warmth that suggested he found this endearing rather than alarming.

"You know it by heart," he said.

"Of course I know it by heart. I wrote the current revision."

"All nine hundred pages?"

"There are appendices."

He laughed — a low, easy sound, the kind of laugh that made other people laugh without knowing why — and went back to his knees in the dirt. Ilsa stood over him with her tablet clutched to her chest like a shield and felt the familiar irritation curdle into something more complicated, which was usually how things went with Elodie Winterbourne.

She had been auditing his garden for three years now. Quarterly inspections, full inventories, germination audits, viability reports. In three years she had never once caught him falsifying a record, skimming stock, or trading cuttings on the gray market that flourished in Halcyon's lower decks like mushrooms in a damp cellar. In three years he had also never once given her a straight answer to a simple question, because every answer came wrapped in a story about his grandmother, or a parable about roots, or a digression about the mating habits of the pollinator drones he'd named after deceased composers.

He was, she had concluded long ago, either the most trustworthy man on the drift or the best liar she'd ever met, and the fact that she genuinely could not tell the difference bothered her more than she admitted to anyone, including herself.

"Sit," he said, nodding at the bench beside the hydroponic racks. "There's tea. The real kind, not the algae kind. A trader owed me a favor."

"I don't have time for tea. I have a discrepancy."

"You have one lupine in the ground where a lupine belongs. That's not a discrepancy, that's a miracle. Do you know how long it's been since anyone on this ship put a seed in actual dirt instead of a nutrient gel sleeve?" He was already pouring tea into two chipped ceramic cups — ceramic, she noted, not standard-issue recyclable polymer, another quiet irregularity she chose not to log. "Besides. You came here to talk about something else. You've got your audit face on, but it's the wrong audit face. This is your *seed* face. Your manifest face is different. Tighter around the eyes."

Ilsa sat down, mostly because refusing would have taken more energy than accepting, and took the cup.

"My manifest face," she repeated.

"Mm." He settled onto the bench beside her, close enough to be friendly, far enough to be deliberate. "You've been pulling dock records since before first shift. I checked."

"You *checked*."

"I have friends in records. I tip well." He sipped his tea. "Which is why I know you pulled the Meridian's file yesterday. Twice."

The tea was excellent, which annoyed her. Everything about him annoyed her, in the specific way that things annoy us when they might be true.

* * *

Here is what Ilsa Reyes knew about the Meridian, as of 0600 that morning:

The Meridian was a long-haul courier vessel, hull class C, registered to the Halcyon fleet forty-one years ago. It had never flown. Not once. Its construction had been completed and then its commissioning cancelled — quietly, without explanation, in the same administrative season that saw the last formal contact between Halcyon and the Ashfall convoy degrade into the cold silence both fleets now called peace. The ship had sat in Docking Cradle Nine ever since, officially mothballed, officially empty, officially a line item in a maintenance budget so old it was denominated in a currency nobody used anymore.

And yesterday, at 0312 station time, a departure manifest had updated itself.

Not been filed. *Updated itself* — pushed through legacy routing, stamped with authorization codes that resolved to offices that no longer existed, listing the Meridian as scheduled for active service in nineteen days. Crew complement: seven. Cargo: unspecified. And on that crew list, in the passenger-advisor slot, a name that made Ilsa's spine go cold in a way she hadn't felt since the incident with the coolant breach on Deck Twelve:

Cassian Vey.

She had looked up the name expecting a clerical error, a recycled identifier, a ghost in the system. What she found instead was a personnel file sealed at the highest clearance tier she could access — and above that, three more tiers she couldn't. Death record: confirmed, eleven years past. Cause: redacted. Service history: redacted. Photograph: withheld.

A dead man, cleared to fly on a ship that had never launched, authorized by departments that had been dissolved before either fleet's current leadership took their chairs.

Ilsa Reyes had survived twenty-six years of fleet bureaucracy by understanding one thing better than anyone else aboard: paperwork doesn't lie, but it can be *made* to lie, and the effort required to make it lie is directly proportional to how much somebody wants the lie kept.

Somebody had worked very hard on this one.

* * *

"So," Elodie said, watching her over the rim of his cup. "The Meridian."

"How do you even know about it? The manifest update isn't public."

"No. But the dock crews gossip worse than the botany section, and Cradle Nine's maintenance contract suddenly got funded. First funding that berth has seen in a decade. Somebody's paying to make an empty ship look ready to fly." He set his cup down. "And then there's my part."

"Your part."

"My name showed up too. On the same manifest. Courier liaison, route certification — my exact credentials, my exact clearance codes." His voice stayed light, but Ilsa had spent years reading ledgers, and she could read the fine print under a voice as easily as under a number. "Except I never signed anything. And the signature on file isn't quite mine. Close. Very close. Like someone practiced it."

The greenhouse hummed around them — water cycling, grow-lights ticking through their spectrum shifts, the soft respiration of eleven thousand living things waiting to become food and medicine and beauty for people who did not yet exist. Ilsa loved this room more than she would ever say out loud. It was the only place on the drift where the future was something you could hold in your hands.

"Some names are bait," she said.

Elodie turned to look at her fully.

"In intelligence work — and yes, before you ask, I did two years in fleet records security before they realized I was better at catching thieves than being one — we used a technique called seeding. You plant a false entry in a system. A fake account, a fake shipment, a fake name. Something juicy enough to attract attention. Then you wait. Anyone who touches it, queries it, follows it — they've identified themselves. You don't catch spies by hunting them. You catch them by leaving out honey and watching who comes to the jar." She met his eyes. "Cassian Vey is honey, Elodie. And so, I think, are you."

The words hung in the warm air between the tomato racks.

"You think fleet intelligence planted my name on a ghost ship's manifest," he said slowly, "to see what I'd do."

"I think *someone* did. Fleet intelligence, Ashfall's people, or a third party I haven't identified yet. The authorization trail is deliberately tangled — whoever built it wanted it to look old, official, and slightly wrong all at once. That's craftsmanship. That's not a clerk's error." She set down her cup. "What I know is that in nineteen days, the Meridian is scheduled to depart, and everyone whose name is attached to it is either dead, fictional, or sitting in this greenhouse drinking smuggled tea."

"And you're warning me because?"

Because you're the only person on this drift who talks to plants like they're crew, she almost said. Because you brought me tea when my mother died and never mentioned it again. Because I've audited you for three years and the worst crime I've found against you is sentimentality.

"Because," she said instead, "if it's a trap, you're standing in it. And if it's not a trap — if it's something else — then someone wants *you*, specifically, wearing that name, on that ship. Either way, the person who benefits is not you." She stood, brushing off her uniform, businesslike again, armor back on. "My professional advice is to report the anomaly, request reassignment, and let the matter die in committee, where all dangerous things go to be forgotten."

"And your personal advice?"

Ilsa paused at the door. Through the great eastern window, Halcyon Drift's rotation brought the distant fleet into view — the *Ascendant*, the *Long Patience*, the *Verity*, a string of sleeping cities falling in formation behind their flagship, home to ninety thousand souls who had never known any sky but the one inside the hull.

"My personal advice," she said, "is that I've read the sealed file index on Cassian Vey. All that's visible is the header. One line of summary text. You want to know what it says?"

"Tell me."

"'Asset declined erasure protocol. Monitor for reappearance.'" She let that sit. "Elodie — they weren't surprised he died. They were *waiting* for him to come back. Whatever game this is, it started eleven years ago, and you've just been dealt into it holding a dead man's cards."

* * *

She was three steps into the corridor when his voice caught up with her.

"Ilsa."

She turned. He stood in the greenhouse doorway, still dirt-smudged, holding his tea, and there was something in his face she hadn't seen in three years of inspections — not fear exactly, and not curiosity either, but the place where those two things meet and shake hands.

"Why did you really come here today?" he asked. "You could have flagged the discrepancy remotely. You could have sent a summons. Instead you walked across half the drift at dawn to tell me in person that my name might be a trap." A faint smile, careful at the edges. "Quartermasters don't do house calls."

It was, annoyingly, a fair question. Ilsa considered several answers — procedural diligence, interdepartmental courtesy, the chain of command — and discarded each one for the same reason she'd discarded them all morning: none of them were true.

"Because whoever built that manifest knew our systems better than we do," she said finally. "They routed through dead offices, forged dead signatures, resurrected a dead man. Which means they've been inside our records for a very long time. Which means everything I trust — every number, every ledger, every clean line of data I've built my whole career on — might already be compromised." Her hand tightened on the tablet. "I count seeds, Elodie. It sounds small. It's not. Eleven thousand viable seed stocks are the difference between these fleets surviving another century and becoming a very well-preserved tomb. If someone is playing games with manifests, they're playing games with the only cargo that matters. And I need one person I can trust who isn't inside whatever this is."

"And you picked me."

"You're either the most honest man on this drift," she said, "or the best liar I've ever met. And I've decided that either way, I'd rather have you next to me than across the table."

He laughed again — quieter this time — and raised his chipped cup in something that wasn't quite a toast.

"To the woman who counts seeds," he said. "What do we do first?"

Ilsa was already pulling up the manifest, her thumb hovering over the sealed file header, the one line of summary text she couldn't stop turning over in her mind. *Monitor for reappearance.*

"First," she said, "we find out who requested the Meridian's maintenance funding. Because whoever paid to make that ghost ship look ready to fly left a payment trail — and money, unlike people, always tells the truth eventually."

She tapped the query. The result loaded.

And Ilsa Reyes, who had counted eleven thousand four hundred and six seeds and trusted every single number she'd ever met, felt the floor tilt beneath her, because the funding authorization for the Meridian bore a single approving signature —

her own.

Dated fourteen years ago.
