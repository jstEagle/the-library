## 7. The Loop

Ilsa Reyes had counted seeds for thirty-one years, and in all that time she had learned exactly three universal truths.

One: every manifest lies a little.

Two: the people who lie least are usually hiding the biggest things.

Three: never audit anyone's garden before lunch, because they will feed you, and you will be compromised.

She was standing in Elodie Winterbourne's arboretum at 0700 ship's time, deliberately before lunch, holding a slate full of numbers that refused to behave, and she was being compromised anyway. The smell of wet loam and flowering nightshade-vines did something to a person. It made you want to believe in things.

"You're staring at my tomatoes," Elodie said, without looking up from the irrigation manifold he was elbow-deep in.

"I'm auditing your tomatoes."

"There's a difference?"

"A significant one." She turned the slate toward him. "You've logged four hundred and twelve seed packets in cold storage. My count says four hundred and eleven."

He wiped his hands on his apron and came over, frowning with what looked like genuine concern. "That can't be right. I count them every month."

"Every month."

"First of the month. Like clockwork."

"And yet."

"And yet," he agreed, and then, after a pause, "Ilsa, if I were smuggling seeds, where exactly would I be smuggling them *to*? We're a generation ship parked next to another generation ship, both of us going nowhere at a very dignified speed. There is no seed black market. There isn't even a seed gray market."

"That's what worries me," she said. "The absence of a market means the absence of a motive, which means the discrepancy isn't theft. It's something else."

He gave her the look she'd come to expect from him — patient, faintly amused, the look of a man who talked to unborn children for a living and had therefore developed an enormous tolerance for strange conversations. "Something else like what?"

"Like bait."

* * *

They took it to her office, because Ilsa did not discuss conspiracies among the tomatoes. Her office was a closet with a desk in it, which suited her; closets didn't pretend to be anything they weren't. She sealed the door, killed the recorder — a small ritual that always made her feel like a character in one of Elodie's novels — and pulled up the Meridian file.

"A ship that never launched," she said. "Decommissioned eleven years ago, hull number reassigned twice, crew roster scrubbed. And yesterday, a name appears on its manifest update. Cassian Vey."

Elodie sat very still. She'd noticed that about him over the past week, since he'd first brought her the anomaly: stillness was his tell. A man who spent his days listening to silence had learned to make his own.

"You've run the name," he said.

"Of course I've run the name."

"What did you find?"

"Nothing. That's the problem. Cassian Vey has no birth record, no service record, no death record. In a fleet that records the caloric intake of every citizen to four decimal places, this man is a hole in the paper." She leaned back. "Holes don't happen by accident, Elodie. Paperwork doesn't develop cavities on its own. Someone drilled that hole, and someone just dropped a name into it, and someone wanted a seed-counter to find it."

"Why a seed-counter?"

"Because I'm boring," she said, with some pride. "Nobody watches the quartermaster. And because you trust me, and you're the most trusted man in either fleet, and whoever set this wants the trail to start with someone nobody will suspect." She tapped the slate. "This is intelligence work. Fleet Intelligence plants anomalies the way fishermen plant chum. The question isn't whether it's deliberate. The question is what's on the other end of the hook."

Elodie was quiet for a long moment. Then he said, "There's something I haven't told you."

Ilsa had been waiting for that sentence for six days. She folded her hands and let him get there at his own pace, which was the pace of a man walking through a garden deciding which truth to pick first.

"The night I found the name, I ran a facial match. Standard procedure — manifests cross-reference against crew registries, it's automated, I barely thought about it." He swallowed. "It returned a match. Ninety-four percent confidence. Cassian Vey." He looked up at her. "Ilsa, the match was my face."

The closet hummed. Somewhere down the corridor, a maintenance drone went about its cheerful, oblivious business.

"Well," Ilsa said finally. "That's not chum. That's a harpoon."

"You don't seem surprised."

"I'm a quartermaster. Surprise is a luxury item and I've never been able to justify the expense." She pulled up a second file, older, encrypted under a clearance she technically shouldn't have but had acquired years ago through the simple method of being so dull that nobody checked. "I need to show you something. And I need you to understand that once you see it, you can't unsee it, and neither of us gets to go back to arguing about tomatoes."

"Is it worse than the face thing?"

"It explains the face thing."

He braced himself theatrically, which she appreciated. Humor was load-bearing in her experience. You could build quite a lot of dread on top of a good joke.

* * *

What she showed him was a transmission log.

Not the public one — every citizen knew the public one, the one taught in nurseries and recited on Remembrance Day. Earth's final message, received forty-one years ago, fragmented, desperate, cut off mid-sentence. The reason two fleets existed instead of one. Halcyon had heard the message and interpreted it as a warning to scatter. Ashfall had heard it and interpreted it as a summons to return. They had argued, then fought, then stopped speaking entirely, and for four decades the silence between them had been maintained by mutual grievance and the occasional carefully deniable incident.

"This," Ilsa said, "is what we actually received."

She played it.

The voice that came out of the speakers was calm. Tired, but calm — the calm of someone who has made peace with something enormous. It spoke for ninety seconds. It did not scream. It did not fragment. It ended with a complete sentence, and then it repeated, because it was a loop, and loops repeat.

Elodie listened to it twice. His hands were flat on his knees, the way he held them when he was talking to the unborn, steadying himself against something that hadn't happened yet.

"That's not the nursery recording," he said quietly.

"No."

"The nursery version ends mid-word. *'Do not—'* and then static. Every child in this fleet grows up on that static. It's the sound of everything ending."

"Somebody edited it," Ilsa said. "Somebody took humanity's last words and cut them off at the exact point where they stopped being ambiguous. Because ambiguity wouldn't have given either fleet a founding myth. Fear would." She brought up the power telemetry, a long declining curve, gentle as a hill. "And here's the part that keeps me up at night, and I want you to appreciate that I am famously difficult to keep up at night. This loop is still transmitting. From a relay station in the deep dark between here and Sol. It's been repeating for forty-one years on whatever power source the original broadcast team left running. And that power source—" she traced the curve to its end, "—fails. Within months. Six, maybe seven. After that, the last words of Earth go silent forever, and all anyone will have left is the edited version. The lie. Permanently."

Elodie stared at the curve. "Both fleets know?"

"High commands know. Both of them. Which means both fleets are currently pretending to hate each other while quietly racing to control a relay station that will stop working before either of them reaches it." She let that sit. "The war is theater, Elodie. It has been theater since the day the founders realized that a shared enemy — even a dead planet, even grief itself — was easier to govern than a shared future. And now the theater is scheduled to close, and nobody knows what happens when the curtain comes down."

* * *

He didn't say anything for a while. He walked her office closet the way he walked his garden rows, slow, touching nothing, thinking with his whole body. Ilsa watched him and felt the particular tenderness she felt for few people and admitted to none of them.

"My parents named me after the static," he said at last. "Did you know that? Not the message. The static. They thought the cutting-off was the truest part — that humanity's story ends unfinished, and our job is to finish it somewhere else. Whole theology built on half a sentence." He laughed, softly, without much humor in it. "I talk to children who don't exist yet about a dying world, and the dying world never actually said what I told them it said."

"People aren't wrong for building meaning," Ilsa said. "They're only wrong when someone builds it for them on purpose and locks the door behind it."

"And the face? Cassian Vey? Where does a dead man's face fit into a forty-year lie?"

"That," Ilsa said, "is the question I've been chewing for six days, and here's what I've got. Somebody knew this moment was coming. Somebody knew the loop would decay, knew the fleets would converge on the relay, knew the lie would need either shoring up or burying. And they prepared for it the way you prepare for anything long-term." She nodded at him. "They planted something. Left a key where a trusted neutral courier would eventually trip over it. A ghost ship, a ghost name, and a living man wearing a dead man's face, who is honest enough that everyone believes him and curious enough that he won't leave it alone."

"You're describing a trap."

"I might be describing a trap. Or I might be describing a seed." She held up a hand before he could react to that. "Don't make that face. I spend my life around seeds. A trap and a seed look identical from the outside — small, inert, buried, waiting. The difference is only ever what grows."

Elodie sat down across from her, and for a moment he looked very tired, the tiredness of a man who had been tending a garden for the unborn and had just learned the past was also his responsibility. "If I pull this thread, Ilsa. If I follow the Meridian, the name, the face — there's no row I don't have to walk. Both fleets. High command. Whatever's at that relay."

"Yes."

"And if I don't?"

"Then in six months the last true words Earth ever spoke go dark, and two armadas full of people raised on a doctored sentence meet in the dark with their weapons hot, each convinced the other is coming to steal the ending." She closed the file. "I've audited a lot of ledgers, Elodie. This is the worst one I've ever seen. The debt is coming due either way. The only choice we get is whether we're holding the books when it does."

He was quiet again. Then, in the tone of a man who talks to the unborn and has decided to extend the courtesy to the living: "The Meridian. The ship that never flew. If someone seeded it as a key — where is it now?"

Ilsa hesitated. This was the last card, and she had been holding it face-down for six days, and she found, somewhat to her own irritation, that her hand wasn't steady.

"That's the other thing," she said. "I ran its registry status this morning, before you arrived. Eleven years of *decommissioned*, of *never launched*, of *hull unassigned*." She turned the slate around. "As of 0400 today, the Meridian is listed as *docked*. Berth seventeen, lower ring, the old survey bays nobody's used since the split. Life support active. Power draw consistent with a fully crewed vessel."

Elodie stood up slowly.

"A ship that never flew," he said, "is asking us to come aboard."

"Or it's asking *you*," Ilsa said. "Berth seventeen's access log shows one name cleared for entry. Guess whose face matches."

* * *

Later — much later, after he'd gone, after she'd sat alone in her closet-office doing the arithmetic she always did at the end of a bad revelation — Ilsa opened her private ledger and added a line beneath the missing seed packet, because she was thorough, and thoroughness was a form of prayer:

*411 packets present. 1 unaccounted. Subject: unknown. Status: germinating.*

Then she keyed a message to Elodie's queue, short and unsentimental, because sentiment was also a luxury item:

*Whatever you find in berth seventeen — count everything twice. And Elodie: if it breathes, don't tell it I sent you.*

She sealed the ledger, turned off the light, and stood in the dark listening to the hum of a ship full of people who believed in a static-filled sky, wondering how many months were left on the curve, and who, exactly, had been waiting all this time inside a ship that never flew.
