## 9. Cassian Vey's Log

*Recovered from the Meridian's sealed archive, eleven years after the vessel's official decommissioning. Timestamps follow the old Earth calendar.*

* * *

**Personal Log — Cassian Vey — Day 1 of the Meridian Survey**

First entry on a ship that doesn't exist. That sentence should bother me more than it does.

The Meridian is beautiful in the way that secrets are beautiful: all her best features are hidden. On paper she's a hull number and a budget line that was closed out before her keel was laid. In reality she has a hydroponics bay the size of a cathedral, a comms array sensitive enough to hear a whisper from the Oort cloud, and a coffee machine that Chief Okoro swears was "liberated" from a Halcyon admiral's private quarters. I have chosen not to ask questions about the coffee machine. Some gifts come with ghosts attached.

My assignment, as explained to me by a woman with no name badge and an accent from nowhere: survey the deep relay stations along the old courier route. Catalogue signal decay. Routine.

I said yes because I am, apparently, the kind of man who says yes to nameless women about ships that never launched. I'm noting this as a character flaw for future reference.

Also the pay is absurd. Also I get my own cabin. Also — and I want this on the record — the bunks here smell like cedar, which no ship has any business smelling like. Someone made choices when they built this ghost. Good ones.

End entry. The coffee is excellent. I hate that it's excellent.

* * *

**Personal Log — Day 19**

Found something today that I probably shouldn't write down, which means I'm going to write it down, because if I don't say it out loud somewhere it will keep growing teeth in the back of my skull.

We passed Relay Station Kestrel-9 on our survey loop. Dead station, per every chart both fleets share. Except it isn't dead. It's transmitting. Has been, according to the Meridian's passive array, continuously, for — and I've checked this three times because I assumed I'd botched the math — forty-one years.

One message. On repeat. From Earth.

Everyone knows about Earth's last transmission. It's taught to children in both fleets before they learn long division. *The home you loved is gone; do not look back; build forward.* The Founders' Broadcast. The reason Halcyon sails one way and Ashfall the other, the reason there's a cold war older than most of the people fighting it, the reason my mother cried at the anniversary observances every single year like clockwork.

Except the version playing from Kestrel-9 is not the version in the history files.

It's longer. There are passages in it that appear in no archive I can access — and I can access quite a lot, another thing I should perhaps have asked more questions about. And the passages that *do* match? They've been cut. Spliced. Words removed mid-sentence, seams smoothed over so carefully that only someone listening to the original would ever hear the joins.

Someone edited humanity's dying words. And then built two fleets on the edit.

I haven't told anyone yet. Not even Okoro, who I trust with my life and half my snacks. I want to be wrong first. I want very badly to be wrong.

Tomorrow we swing back through Kestrel's shadow. I'm going to record the whole thing clean.

If I'm right, this log is either going to matter enormously or get me disappeared. Possibly both. History suggests those aren't mutually exclusive.

* * *

**Personal Log — Day 22**

Okoro knows. She caught me running the comparison at 0300 and instead of reporting me she sat down, poured two cups of the haunted coffee, and said, "Took you nineteen days? Embarrassing. I found it in six."

So. The crew knows. All nine of us. And here's the thing I keep turning over: none of them were surprised. Not surprised-scared. Surprised-*relieved*. Like I'd finally confirmed a diagnosis they'd been carrying for years.

Nour Haddad — our archivist, a small fierce woman who alphabetizes things that don't technically have an order — pulled me aside after shift. She showed me fragments. Older fragments. Maintenance logs from the relay stations dating back to the Separation, all flagged with the same maintenance code, all routed to the same office that officially ceased to exist forty years ago.

"Someone's been keeping it alive," she said. "The real message. Tending it." She looked almost embarrassed. "Like a garden."

I laughed, and she looked offended until I explained that I wasn't laughing at her, I was laughing at the universe, which apparently has a sense of humor I'm only now being let in on.

A garden. A tended thing, kept alive in the dark, waiting for someone to come find it.

Fine. I'll bite. What's it waiting to say?

* * *

**Personal Log — Day 30**

I've transcribed the differences between the original broadcast and the Founders' version. Nour checked my work twice. There's no ambiguity left to hide behind, so I'm putting the summary here, plain, where future-me (or whoever finds this) can't squirm away from it:

The original message is not a warning. It is an apology.

Earth's final hours weren't spent telling her children to run and never look back. They were spent saying *we're sorry*. Sorry for the century of bad decisions. Sorry for launching the colony fleets half-prepared because the alternative was watching them die on a dying world. Sorry — and this is the part that's been cut entirely, excised so cleanly you'd never know it existed — for lying to them about why.

There was no single catastrophe. There was a slow one, and the founders of both fleets knew about it decades in advance, and they each decided their own fleet's survival doctrine mattered more than the truth. Halcyon's founders cut the apology because contrition implied fault, and fault implied the Separation was a choice rather than a necessity. Ashfall's founders cut it because forgiveness implied reconciliation, and reconciliation meant sharing what little they'd hoarded.

Two fleets. Two edits. Same lie, told in two dialects.

And underneath all of it, the part that makes me sit down on the deck sometimes without meaning to: the last thirty seconds of the true broadcast contain coordinates. A destination. A world surveyed and seeded by automated probes generations ago — a place prepared. An invitation home, or at least toward home, or at least toward *something*, together.

Both fleets have been sailing away from each other for forty years, armed and grieving, over a message that ends with *come find us*.

I need a drink. The coffee machine will have to do. It owes me that much.

* * *

**Personal Log — Day 44**

Tried the proper channels today. Composed a report through the Meridian's official uplink, encrypted, addressed to Fleet Intelligence, Halcyon branch, since that's who signs my checks.

The uplink refused it. Not failed — *refused*. A polite system message informed me that my transmission privileges had been "provisionally adjusted pending review." My clearance, which was top-tier yesterday, is now decorative.

Okoro didn't even look up from her console. "Day four," she said.

"Day four of what?"

"Of them noticing you noticed. We've all been through it. You send nothing for a month, you're invisible. The moment you try to tell someone upstairs, the walls get ears." She sipped her coffee. "Welcome to the Meridian, Vey. We're not a survey ship. We're a leash."

Nine crew. All people who found the truth and tried to hand it upward. All quietly reassigned to a ship that never flew, doing a job nobody audits, aboard a vessel that exists in no registry — because a ghost ship can't testify.

They didn't disappear us. That would raise questions. They just made us irrelevant. It's tidier.

I should be terrified. Mostly I'm furious, and under the furious, embarrassingly, I'm — glad? These are good people. Okoro cheats at cards and hums when she thinks no one's listening. Nour names the relay stations after poets. Beck the engineer once spent six hours fixing a door nobody uses because, quote, "a door that doesn't open is a lie."

I've been alone a long time, in the way you can be alone inside a fleet of ten thousand. This leaky, unofficial, cedar-scented prison is the least alone I've felt since Earth went quiet.

That's probably how they get you. I'm aware. I don't care.

* * *

**Personal Log — Day 61**

New plan, and I want it noted that it is a *good* plan despite what happened next.

You can't report the truth upward — the walls have ears. Fine. Then we go sideways. Both fleets' civilian archives, the public ones, the boring ones nobody guards because nobody reads them. Nour says the founding-era records were never classified, just *misfiled* — scattered across a thousand databases so thoroughly that reassembling them looks like madness rather than sedition.

Madness is underrated. We're nine people on a ghost ship with a comms array that could hear a sneeze from Pluto. If anyone can misfile the truth back into visibility, it's us.

Beck rigged a burst transmitter disguised as routine telemetry. Nour started fragment-mapping. Okoro, who turns out to have opinions about everything and receipts for most of it, began drafting the cover story: a maintenance audit, dry as toast, with the reconstructed broadcast embedded in the checksums where no auditor ever looks.

Dry as toast. That was the plan. Toast.

Then yesterday a shuttle docked with the Meridian unannounced, and three visitors came aboard, and none of them had name badges, and I recognized the accent of the lead one from Day 1.

She smiled at me like a teacher at a promising student. "Cassian," she said. "You've been busy."

I said the first honest thing available: "Is the coffee machine yours?"

Her smile didn't move. "We'd like you to stop the project."

And here's the part I keep replaying, the part that keeps me up: she didn't threaten me. She didn't need to. She just stood there in our corridor, in our cedar-smelling home, and said, gently, "You have families. So do we. Nobody wants this to become a problem."

Nobody wants this to become a problem. As though the edited dying words of an entire species were a scheduling conflict.

They left. We watched the shuttle go. Okora put her hand on my shoulder and said, "So. They know."

"They always knew," Nour said. "We were never a leash. We're a pressure valve. They let us find it so they'd know exactly who finds it."

Silence around the table. Then Beck — sweet, stubborn Beck — said, "Well. Now they know we know they know. Checkmate eventually?"

God help me, I laughed.

* * *

**Personal Log — Day 63**

We voted. Nine of nine. We finish the reconstruction, and we send it everywhere at once — both fleets, civilian channels, every relay on the route, all simultaneously, so fast and so wide that no office anywhere can catch it all. Truth as weather. You can't arrest rain.

Timeline: ninety days. Nour needs sixty to finish the fragment map. Beck needs thirty to harden the transmitter against jamming. Okoro is writing the cover audit and claims it will be "aggressively boring," which from her I believe.

I'm keeping this log detailed because if it goes wrong, someone should be able to read *why*. Not the politics — the human part. That nine ordinary people on a ship that never flew decided that a lie told for forty years was still a lie, and lies about the dead are theft from the living.

Also because Pip — no, wait. No Pip yet. I'm getting ahead of myself. There's a baby coming, Okoro's sister's kid, due to arrive with the next supply window, born aboard because her parents are Meridian crew and there's nowhere else. A child born on a ship that doesn't exist. Nour says the registry won't have her. I say registries are just paperwork's opinion.

Maybe that's the whole lesson of this mess. Earth's founders thought a broadcast was the truth. The fleets think a registry is reality. But a child is a child whether the paperwork agrees or not, and a message means what it means whether anyone filed it correctly or not.

Ninety days. Then it rains.

* * *

**Personal Log — Day 88**

Two days from launch. Everything's ready. Nour finished early, grinning like a lighthouse. Beck's transmitter is hardened, hidden, gorgeous. Even the coffee machine has been conscripted — its power coupling now routes to the burst array, which Beck assures me is poetic.

Last night I walked the corridors alone, end to end, saying goodbye to a ship that was never officially born. I touched the hydroponics bay glass. Somebody's tomatoes are coming in. Real tomatoes, on a ghost ship, for a crew that doesn't exist, growing food for a future nobody upstairs believes in.

That's when I understood what I actually feel, and I want it written plainly: I'm not afraid for myself anymore. Whatever they do to me, the transmission goes out in forty-eight hours and no power in either fleet can call it back. I'm afraid of something stupider. I'm afraid I'll miss this. The cedar smell. The card games Okoro cheats at. The door Beck fixed. The tomatoes.

You spend your whole life wanting to matter, and then you matter, and it turns out what you really wanted was Tuesday evenings with people who know your name.

Forty-eight hours. Then, whatever comes — rain.

* * *

**Personal Log — Final Entry**

Something's wrong.

Beck found it twenty minutes ago: a second signal riding our carrier wave, buried so deep only his paranoid masterpiece of a receiver could see it. It's not jamming. It's *mirroring*. Every packet we transmit, something transmits too — same content, altered source, routed back through fleet channels stamped with our ship's phantom registry.

They're not going to stop the truth. They're going to *poison* it. Let our reconstruction out wearing our name, then produce forged evidence that the Meridian fabricated the whole thing — dissident propaganda, a hoax, proof that "Cassian Vey" is a traitor manufacturing lies. Our truth becomes their exhibit A. Our names become the cautionary tale they teach children alongside the Founders' Broadcast.

Unless we go now. Ahead of schedule, off-pattern, before their mirror net is fully positioned. Beck says he can push the full burst in eleven minutes if we abandon the staggered sequence. Eleven minutes versus certainty of failure.

We're doing it. Nour's already feeding the map into the buffer. Okoro's on the door. Nobody argued. Nine of nine again — we're a very agreeable mutiny.

If anyone ever reads this: the message from Earth ends with coordinates and an invitation. Check Kestrel-9. Listen past the edit. And if you meet a child born on this ship, tell her the ship existed. Tell her we did.

Tell her the tomatoes came in.

Transmitting in nine min—

* * *

*The log ends here. The final entry bears no closing timestamp. The Meridian's burst transmitter fired once, at 0347 ship-time, carrying a partial reconstruction — enough to alarm, not enough to convince — before the array was remotely fused by an authority that does not appear in any registry.*

*Cassian Vey was never formally reported missing. His file was closed eleven years ago, marked DECEASED — PRE-LAUNCH, a clerical impossibility preserved with great care.*

*The ship that never flew continued to fly. Its crew waited.*

*In the arboretum of the generation ship Halcyon Drift, a terraformer with a familiar face watered seedlings and spoke aloud to children who might never be born — unaware that somewhere in the dark, a ghost ship had been holding a seat for him all along.*
