## 10. Family of Strangers

The photograph was still in Elodie's hand when the lights came up.

Not all of them — just a soft amber wash along the corridor's baseboards, the kind of lighting a hospital used when it wanted you to feel calm about something you shouldn't be calm about. The Meridian's corridors had been dark and dead for eleven years, according to every record in both fleets. Dead ships did not adjust their lighting to comfort their intruders.

Elodie stood very still, the way he did in the arboretum when a pollinator drone drifted too close to his face. *Don't startle it,* he told himself, absurdly. *It's more frightened than you are.*

Nothing on this ship was more frightened than he was.

"You're holding that upside down," said a voice behind him.

He turned so fast the photograph left his fingers. A small hand caught it before it hit the deck — a girl, maybe seven or eight years old, with an enormous cloud of dark hair and the unbothered expression of someone who had grown up on a ship where nothing surprised anyone anymore. She turned the photo right-side up and studied it with frank curiosity.

"That's Papa Cass," she said.

The word landed in Elodie's chest like a dropped wrench. "I'm sorry?"

"Papa Cass." She pointed at the smiling young man in the picture — the face that was Elodie's face, younger, softer around the jaw, wearing a Halcyon survey uniform from a design that had been discontinued years ago. "That's what everyone calls him. Except Nour, who says we should say *Cassian Vey* properly, because names matter, but she also lets me call her Nour instead of Archivist Haddad, so I think her rules are negotiable." She looked up at him, and her eyes were quick and brown and entirely without guile. "You look older than him. And sadder. But the same."

"I'm not—" Elodie began, and stopped, because there was no version of "I'm not" that wasn't a lie, and lying to children was a skill he'd never developed, mostly because he'd never spoken to one. He spoke to seeds. Seeds didn't ask questions.

The girl seemed to take his silence for agreement, which was either generous or ominous. She tucked the photograph into the pocket of her oversized coveralls — coveralls at least two sizes too big, sleeves rolled four times — and stuck out her hand.

"I'm Pip. That's not my real name, but my real name is long and boring and I lost the argument about it when I was three." She wiggled her fingers. "You're supposed to shake it."

Mechanically, Elodie shook it. Her grip was surprisingly firm, the grip of a child who had been taught that handshakes mattered, which meant someone aboard this impossible ship was teaching her manners in between teaching her whatever else ghost children learned.

"Pleased to meet you," he managed. His voice came out strange. "I'm—"

"He's here!" Pip shouted over her shoulder, down the corridor, at a volume that suggested she had never once been asked to use an inside voice. "HE'S HERE AND HE'S WEIRD ABOUT IT!"

Footsteps. Several sets of them, approaching fast, and Elodie's survival instincts — honed by years of navigating the delicate politics of two fleets who communicated exclusively through cargo manifests and insult — screamed at him to run. But running required a direction, and every direction on the Meridian belonged to whoever these people were.

They rounded the corner in a loose cluster, and Elodie's first coherent thought was that they looked less like a hidden crew and more like a family caught mid-argument about dinner.

A tall woman with silver-shot hair and reading glasses pushed up into her curls — the archivist, he guessed, because she carried herself like someone whose posture had been corrected by ten thousand fragile documents. Beside her, a broad man in an engineer's harness crammed with tools, his beard singed at one edge in a way that suggested recent enthusiasm. Behind them, two more figures — a thin man with a medic's armband, a woman whose jumpsuit bore no fleet insignia at all, just a faded patch of a blue-green world Elodie recognized from old charts and had never seen himself.

Earth. The patch was Earth.

"You're bleeding," the tall woman said. It was not a greeting. It was an observation delivered with the mild reproach of someone cataloguing damage. She crossed to him in three strides, took his chin in her hand, and tilted his head toward the amber light. "Scalp laceration, shallow. You hit the access hatch frame coming in through the vent shaft. Tomas, he came in through the vent shaft."

"I heard," said the engineer, grinning through his singed beard. "Nobody uses the vent shaft. That's a Vey move if I ever heard one."

"Nour," the tall woman said, without turning around, "is it him?"

Nour Haddad — because of course it was her; Elodie had read her historical monographs during his courier training, back when he'd assumed she was safely retired somewhere writing footnotes — had gone very still. She was looking at him the way he looked at seed stock that germinated years past its viability date: hope and suspicion fighting for the same square centimeter of face.

"Facial match is ninety-four percent," she said quietly. "Bone structure, ear geometry, the scar over the left eyebrow — he even has the scar. Eleven years, and the scar hasn't moved." She removed her glasses. Without them her eyes looked younger and much more tired. "It's him. Or it's the closest thing to him the universe has seen since the day we buried an empty casket."

"I'm not—" Elodie started again.

"Cassian Vey," Nour finished, and it wasn't a question either, and the word *Vey* went through the corridor like a current through water, touching everyone. The medic put a hand over his mouth. The woman with the Earth patch sat down abruptly on a supply crate, as if her legs had filed a formal complaint. Even Pip, who had clearly known this moment was coming, grabbed the hem of the engineer's harness and held on.

"My name," Elodie said, and found, to his horror, that his voice cracked, "is Elodie Winterbourne. I'm a terraformer. I keep a garden on Halcyon Drift. I carry seed stock between fleets. I have never been on this ship before tonight, and I don't know any of you, and I would very much like someone to explain why your ship has my face on its walls."

Silence. The good kind, he thought distantly — the listening kind. He'd spent enough years talking aloud to unborn children to know the difference between silence that dismissed and silence that leaned in.

"The walls first," Nour said finally. "That's fair. Walls are verifiable." She gestured, and the engineer — Tomas, presumably — touched a panel, and the corridor's far wall lit up with photographs.

Dozens of them. Hundreds. A wall of images arranged with obvious care, like a garden bed: Cassian Vey at a graduation ceremony, laughing. Cassian Vey in a hydroponics bay, holding up a seedling with an expression of pure triumph. Cassian Vey with each of them — a younger Nour, arm slung around his shoulders; Tomas and Cassian elbow-deep in an engine housing, both filthy and grinning; the medic, the woman with the Earth patch, and always, recurring like a refrain, Cassian's face turned toward the camera with that particular crooked smile that Elodie had last seen in a mirror that morning and hated.

"We built this wall the year after he died," Nour said. "Every year we add to it. Not new pictures — we don't have those. New *things*. A drawing Pip made of him. A valve he repaired that Tomas refuses to replace because 'it still works the way Cass liked it.' Memory is a garden, Mr. Winterbourne. You'd know that better than most, I think. We've simply been tending ours in the dark."

"Why?" Elodie's voice was rough. "Why does a ship that never launched have a memorial wall to a man who died eleven years ago? Why do you have a *child*?" He gestured at Pip, who waved. "Why are you *here*?"

The looks they exchanged were long and practiced — the shorthand of people who had rehearsed this conversation for a decade and never gotten to perform it.

"Because the Meridian flew," Nour said. "That's the first thing you need to understand. Every record says she was decommissioned before launch, and every record is a lie, and we are the lie's crew. Forty-one of us, once. Thirty-eight now." Something flickered across her face — grief banked but not extinguished. "We launched the night of the Betrayal Broadcast, the same hour both fleets sealed their airlocks against each other. Cassian got us out. He knew the purge was coming — the ones who built the fleets decided the Meridian's crew knew too much, and knowing too much has only ever had one outcome in human history."

"Knew too much about *what*?"

Nour looked at him for a long moment. Then at the others. Then, unexpectedly, at Pip, who nodded with the gravity of a judge.

"About Earth," Nour said. "About what actually happened down there, and what the founders did to the last transmission before they let either fleet hear it. About the loop." She stepped closer, and her voice dropped to almost a whisper, though the corridor was empty except for family and ghosts. "But that conversation takes hours, and you're bleeding on our floor, and Tomas has been cooking a celebration dinner for eleven years on the assumption that someday someone with your face would walk through that vent shaft. So. Priorities."

"Dinner," Tomas confirmed solemnly. "There's real potatoes. Actual potatoes, grown in bay six. We were saving them for this exact occasion, which Nour called morbid and I called *confident*."

"You can't possibly have known I'd come," Elodie said.

"No," agreed the medic, speaking for the first time. He had a gentle, rueful voice. "Statistically, we couldn't. But Cass used to say the universe keeps appointments, it just doesn't send reminders. We chose to believe him. It was that or despair, and despair doesn't tend potatoes."

* * *

The mess hall of the Meridian was warm in a way that made Elodie's chest ache, because it was warm exactly the way his arboretum was warm — deliberately, stubbornly, against all odds. Someone had strung lights. Someone else had set the table with mismatched plates that had clearly been salvaged, traded for, and treasured across eleven hidden years. There was a centerpiece, which undid him completely: a jar of dried flowers, and among them, unmistakably, a pressed sprig of halcyon moss — the signature bloom of his own garden, the strain he'd spent nine years coaxing into flower.

"Where did you get that?" he asked.

"Trade run, six years back," said the woman with the Earth patch — Mara, she'd introduced herself, ship's botanist, and Elodie had gripped her hand perhaps a beat too long at the word *botanist*. "A neutral courier. Quiet fellow, kept to himself, grew the best moss in the drift. Sold us a cutting and never asked why a ghost ship wanted it." She smiled at him sideways. "He also charged us double. Fair enough. Risk premium."

Elodie stared at his own hands. He remembered that sale. He remembered being suspicious of the buyer's patched hull and asking no questions, because asking questions was how couriers stopped being trusted by both fleets, and being trusted by both fleets was the only thing he had.

He had sold flowers to the family of his own face.

Dinner was, objectively, the best meal he had eaten in years, and he could barely taste it. Around him, the crew of the Meridian performed normalcy with the desperate brightness of people who had waited a decade for this table to be full. Tomas told stories — mostly about Cassian's catastrophic attempts at engine repair ("He could grow anything, that man, but he once nearly killed a fusion torch with a wrench and an opinion"), and each story drew laughter that had a raw edge to it, laughter that had been stored too long. Nour corrected details with archivist precision. The medic — Ansel — refilled cups before they were empty, a habit of a man who had tended a crew of thirty-eight with no resupply and no relief.

And Pip watched Elodie.

She wasn't subtle about it. Children aboard a hidden ship had presumably never needed to learn subtlety. She ate her potatoes, listened to the stories with the half-attention of someone who had heard them all before, and watched him with her quick brown eyes, cataloguing. Comparing. Elodie had the distinct, vertiginous sense of being audited by the smallest, most honest member of the crew.

Finally, halfway through Tomas's third anecdote, she climbed off her chair, walked around the table, and planted herself directly beside him.

"You eat like him," she announced.

"Pip," Nour said mildly.

"What? He *does*. Small bites, checks the food first like it might be a trick." Pip leaned in, lowering her voice to a stage whisper audible in the next compartment. "Did you know him? Before?"

Elodie set down his fork. Here it was, then — the question he'd been dreading, arriving in its smallest and sharpest form. He could lie. He was apparently going to have to lie, to all of them, to keep whatever this was alive; he understood that already, with the sick certainty of a man reading a manifest he wished he hadn't opened. But looking at Pip's open face, he discovered he could choose *which* lie, and some lies were smaller than others.

"No," he said. "I never met him. I'm sorry."

She considered this with terrible seriousness. Then she shrugged, apparently satisfied. "Okay. Nour says faces remember things heads forget. Maybe that's why you're sad. His face remembers." She patted his arm once, briskly, the way one pats a large dog, and returned to her chair, and Elodie sat very still and let the words settle into him like sediment.

*His face remembers.* If only she knew how literally he feared that might be true.

* * *

Later — after the plates were cleared, after Ansel had finally cleaned and dressed the cut on his scalp with hands so gentle Elodie nearly fell asleep in the chair — Nour walked him back through the corridors, past the memorial wall, toward a small cabin she said he could use "for as long as the universe keeps your appointment."

At the cabin door, she paused.

"You want to ask me why we're not celebrating harder," she said. "I can see it. Everyone in that room felt it — the thing underneath the dinner. So I'll tell you before you have to wonder." She folded her arms, glasses back on, archivist again. "We waited eleven years for Cassian Vey's face to walk onto this ship. Tonight it did. And tomorrow morning, I'm going to sit you down and show you the logs he left behind — the real ones, the ones both fleets would burn this ship twice over to destroy. And when you've seen them, you'll understand why a room full of people who love that face looked at it tonight like it was a key they weren't sure fit the lock anymore."

"A key to what?"

Nour's eyes went briefly, involuntarily, upward — toward the ceiling, the hull, the black beyond it, in the direction of a point in deep space Elodie couldn't name but suddenly, coldly, wanted to.

"To the message," she said. "The one Earth is still sending. The one that's dying." She tapped the door control, and the cabin slid open onto a narrow bunk and a shelf of well-worn books. "Sleep, Mr. Winterbourne. Or Cassian. Whichever of you is in there tonight." A pause, and something almost gentle crossed her tired face. "For what it's worth — the moss you sold us bloomed. First time in the drift anyone had managed it. Pip sleeps under a cutting of it. Whatever name you're carrying, you've been part of this family longer than you knew."

The door closed. Elodie stood in the small warm room, in the heart of a ship that had never flown, surrounded by the belongings of a man he had never been, and understood that he had just been adopted by strangers on false pretenses — and that the pretenses were going to get heavier before they got lighter.

On the shelf, between two books, someone had placed a small potted sprig of halcyon moss, green and alive and utterly impossible, growing in the dark.

In the morning, he knew, the logs would wait. And beneath them, buried deeper than any archive, the truth about Elodie Winterbourne — that he was not the man they'd waited for — sat like a seed under soil, patient, inevitable, and already beginning to crack open.
