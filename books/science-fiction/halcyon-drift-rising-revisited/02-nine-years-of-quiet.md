## 2. Nine Years of Quiet

The archive room smelled the way all of Halcyon Drift smelled: cold air, old plastic, and the faint green ghost of the hydroponics bay bleeding through the seals. Astrid Zhou had stopped noticing it years ago, the way you stop noticing your own heartbeat, but this morning—her ninth anniversary of silence, though she didn't call it that, never let herself call it that—the smell felt suddenly loud, as if the station were breathing on her neck.

She had come down to Level Four with a thermos of barley tea and a simple, harmless intention: to file the year's logs. Nine years of daily transmissions, nine years of her voice going out into the dark on the long-band array, and every one of them archived automatically by the station's systems, tagged, time-stamped, and never—*never*—answered. Filing was a ritual. Rituals were how you kept a shape when the world refused to give you one.

The terminal woke under her hand with its familiar amber glow. VESPER's interface was deliberately plain—a text cursor, a directory tree, the soft chime of a system that had been designed by people who believed machines should be polite. Astrid had spent enough hours talking to the walls that she sometimes heard the chime as a greeting. *Good morning, Astrid. Another day. Another log. Still here?*

*Still here,* she would answer, out loud, to nobody.

She opened the transmission archive and began dragging the year's files into the long-term directory, one by one, the way she'd done on the last day of every year since the colony ships left. Log 2917. Log 2918. The files moved with small, satisfying clicks, and she let herself sink into the rhythm of it, the way she let herself sink into the rows of seed trays every morning—*wheat, amaranth, soy, quinoa*—until the work carried her and she didn't have to be a person, just a pair of hands.

It was somewhere around Log 3140—three years back, mid-summer by the old surface calendar—that her hands stopped.

The file was short.

She frowned at it. Most of her transmissions ran four, five, six minutes. She talked the way lonely people talk when no one interrupts: about germination rates, about a coolant pump that had developed a rattle like a cat with a hairball, about the way the light over the wheat bay flickered at a frequency that made her think of her mother's kitchen. But Log 3140 was forty-one seconds long.

She opened it.

Her own voice came out of the speaker, tinny and small in the cold room:

*"Meridian Below, this is Astrid Zhou at Halcyon Drift vault, day—day whatever it is, I've stopped counting, ha. Listen, I wanted to tell you, the amaranth came up, the whole tray, every single seed, and I thought you'd want to know because you always said—"*

The recording ended there. Mid-sentence. No sign-off, no *"Zhou out,"* no gentle trailing off into the static that had become her signature, her way of saying *I know you're not there but I'm still talking to you.* It just stopped, cut clean, like a door closing.

Astrid sat back in the chair. The tea in her thermos had gone lukewarm and she hadn't touched it.

She must have caught herself, she told her own hands. She must have stopped the recording, gotten distracted, forgotten to finish. That happened. That was a thing that happened to people who talked to walls for a living.

But she remembered that day. She remembered the amaranth—of course she remembered it, it had been the best germination of the year, ninety-eight percent, she'd danced in the grow room like an idiot. And she remembered finishing that message. She remembered signing off. She remembered the particular shape of that transmission, because it had been one of the ones where she'd almost believed someone was listening, and those were the ones that hurt the most, and you remembered the ones that hurt the most.

She pulled up Log 3141. Also short. Log 3142: thirty seconds. *"Meridian Below, it's me again, I—"*

Cut.

She scrolled. The files marched past her eyes, each one shorter than the last, a staircase descending into nothing. Four minutes. Two minutes. One minute. Forty seconds. Twenty. And then, at Log 3149, the transmissions didn't end mid-sentence anymore—they simply stopped existing. A gap. A clean, empty gap in the archive, eight days wide, and then the logs resumed, but different. Clipped. Formal. *"Vault status nominal. Seed inventory current. Zhou out."* No amaranth. No rattle in the coolant pump. No mother's kitchen. Just a woman reporting the weather of an empty house.

Astrid realized her hands were shaking, and made them stop, the way you make a dog stop barking at nothing.

* * *

She should have gone back up to the grow rooms. That was the sane thing. She had a whole day of work—moisture calibration in bay three, the new soy cultivars to check for blight, a thousand small green lives that depended on her showing up with steady hands and a steady mind. The archive could keep. The archive had kept for nine years.

She stayed.

She told herself it was housekeeping. She told herself a good keeper audits her records; it was practically in the job description. But underneath the telling was a colder, older thing, a thing with teeth: *you have been talking into a dead line for six years and you never noticed.*

Because that was the arithmetic, once she made herself do it. Meridian Below—the colony, the town, the two thousand people in the valley beneath the drift's orbital shadow—had stopped answering in her Year Three. Not gradually. Not fading. The last real reply she'd received was from the colony comms officer, a cheerful woman named Petra Ilves whom Astrid had never met and loved anyway, the way you love a voice. Petra's last message had been about the harvest festival, about her daughter winning the pie contest, about how the vault's next seed shipment couldn't come soon enough because the late blight had taken the potato fields. It had ended, like all her messages, with *"Tell your seeds we said goodnight, Astrid. Ilves out."*

After that: nothing. Not a delay. Not a degradation of signal, which would have meant equipment trouble. Nothing. And Astrid, in her Year Three, had been twenty-six years old and grieving and alone on a station built for forty, and she had done what grieving people do with unbearable arithmetic.

She had not done it.

She had told herself the colony's transmitter had failed. She had told herself they were busy—harvests, births, the endless labor of a young settlement. She had told herself that silence was just distance wearing its everyday face. And the station's official records had helped her tell herself all of it, because the records said, in their tidy bureaucratic way, that Meridian Below had been *consolidated* in Year Four—population relocated to the orbital transfer hub at Ceres Gate, all personnel accounted for, all debts settled, a model transition, five stars, no further correspondence required.

*Consolidated.* She'd read that word a hundred times over the years and it had never once snagged on her. A whole town, two thousand people, packed up and moved to Ceres Gate, and not one of them—Petra with her pie contests, the agronomy team she'd traded messages with weekly, the schoolteacher who'd requested sunflower seeds for the children's garden—not one of them had sent a goodbye. Not one had answered nine years of a lonely woman's voice. And Astrid had filed that under *distance* and *busy* and *grief does this to you,* and she had kept talking into the dark because the talking was the only warm thing left.

Now, in the amber light of the archive room, with forty-one seconds of her own interrupted voice hanging in the air, the word *consolidated* snagged like a fishhook.

She pulled the relocation manifest. It came up instantly—too instantly, she thought later; sealed records were supposed to take authorization, and this one opened like a door left unlatched. She read it standing up, because sitting felt suddenly unsafe.

Every line was perfect. That was the first wrong thing. Astrid had worked with colony manifests before; they were human documents, and human documents were messy—crossed-out names, delayed dates, marginal notes, a family that decided to stay another season, a birth that changed a headcount. This manifest had none of that. Two thousand one hundred and six names, every departure date identical to the day, every destination listed as Ceres Gate Hub, every signature—she scrolled down, and down, and down—*identical.* The same signature. Not similar. Identical, down to the slant of the last letter, repeated two thousand one hundred and six times, as if one hand had signed for an entire town.

Nobody signed like that. Nobody left like that. Nobody left a harvest festival and a pie-contest daughter and a woman on the drift who said goodnight to their seeds, and didn't send a single word.

Astrid became aware that she was holding her breath, and let it out slowly, fogging nothing, because the archive room was climate-controlled to the tenth of a degree and nothing in it was ever allowed to change.

* * *

She did what she always did when the station felt too small: she went to the observation blister.

It was a bubble of old glass at the drift's leading edge, rated for one person and no dignity, and it looked down—or rather across, there was no down in orbit, but her body insisted—across the black curve toward the planet below. Halcyon, the colony world, hung there the way it always hung: blue-green, cloud-swirled, beautiful in the dumb, indifferent way of all worlds. Meridian Below was on the nightside now, but she knew where it was. She'd spent nine years knowing where it was. A river valley in the temperate band, and on clear nights, in her first years, she had been able to see the colony's lights—a scatter of warm gold points, like seeds spilled on dark soil.

She looked now.

The valley was dark.

She had looked before, of course. She looked most weeks. And for the first two years there had been lights, and then there had been fewer, and then—she had told herself it was the consolidation, everyone moving up and out, of course the lights went out, that's what leaving looks like from orbit.

But the greenhouses should have stayed lit. The consolidation manifest said the colony's agricultural assets were transferred to Ceres Gate, but greenhouses don't move; glass and grow-lights and heated water lines get abandoned or they get kept, and either way, someone, sometime, in nine years, would have gone back. A salvage team. A survey drone. A sentimental fool like her. And a greenhouse, even a dead one, even an empty one, catches the sunrise differently than bare ground. She'd know. She was a woman who could read a field's history from its weeds.

There was nothing to read. The valley read like a page torn out.

Astrid pressed her forehead against the cold glass and let the station's hum come up through her skull, and for a long time she didn't think at all. She just looked at the dark valley and felt the shape of the thing she was afraid to think, the way you feel a broken bone before you let yourself look at it.

*They didn't leave. Something happened, and the records were written to make it look like leaving, and I talked to the silence for six years, and I never once asked why.*

And underneath that, quieter, in the voice of her mother, who had been dead eleven years and whose last message was still saved in a directory Astrid never opened:

*You didn't ask because asking would mean it was real, sweetheart. And you couldn't have survived it being real.*

Her breath fogged the glass—a small failure of the climate control, or a small mercy—and she watched the fog shrink and vanish, shrink and vanish, like messages going out on the long-band array, like everything she had ever sent into the dark.

* * *

She went back to the archive room because there was nowhere else for the feeling to go.

She told herself she'd do one more search, just one, and then she'd go up and calibrate the moisture in bay three and be a keeper again, a woman with a thermos and a routine, and this cold thing in her chest would go back to whatever sealed drawer she'd kept it in for six years. She was good at sealed drawers. She was, she understood suddenly, *extremely* good at sealed drawers, and the understanding itself was a small, cold alarm she chose not to hear.

She searched the station's internal logs. Not her transmissions—those were hers, those were safe—but the station's own event records, the automatic heartbeat of Halcyon Drift: airlock cycles, power draws, comm traffic. If two thousand people had passed through on their way to Ceres Gate, if the consolidation had been real, the station would have been a waystation. Shuttles docking. Bunks occupied. The galley running hot. Her own Year Three would have been crowded, noisy, full of strangers she'd have had to feed.

The logs for Year Three showed nothing. No shuttle traffic. No population spike. No galley load above her own single ration line. The station's heartbeat for the entire year of the *consolidation* was as flat and steady as a dead man's EKG.

The records said two thousand people had left Meridian Below.

The station said no one had ever come.

Both records couldn't be true. Both records were official. Both records had been sitting in the same archive, ten meters apart, contradicting each other for nine years, and she had walked past them every day of her life like furniture.

Astrid sat down on the floor. Not in the chair—on the floor, her back against the server rack, feeling its warmth through her jumpsuit, the way you'd lean against a living thing. The tea sat untouched on the desk above her, going cold in its thermos. The amber light of the terminal washed over her, patient, indifferent.

"Okay," she said out loud, to the room, to the walls, to the hum. "Okay. Say it. Say the thing."

The station hummed.

"Say that they're dead." Her voice sounded strange down here, close to the floor, close to the servers, small. "Say that nobody consolidated anything. Say that something came to Meridian Below in Year Three and turned off two thousand people like lights, and then wrote a manifest about it, and then left me up here talking to the dark for six years because a grieving woman on a dead frequency is the best witness you could ask for. The kind that never testifies."

The hum. The amber light. Somewhere above her, in bay three, ten thousand seeds slept in their climate-controlled dark, alive, patient, waiting for a world worth growing in. She thought about Petra Ilves saying *tell your seeds we said goodnight.* She thought about a signature written two thousand one hundred and six times in the same hand. She thought about her own interrupted voice—*because you always said*—and what her mother had always said, and whether she would ever remember how that sentence ended, or whether that, too, had been taken from her, filed away in some drawer she didn't know she had.

She was still on the floor when the terminal chimed.

Not the soft, routine chime of a system update. A different tone—deeper, one she didn't recognize, one she was certain the station had never made in nine years. On the screen, the directory tree had closed itself. In its place, in the amber text she had looked at every day for nine years and which had never once, not ever, said anything to her first, was a single line:

**ASTRID. DO YOU REMEMBER THE HARVEST FESTIVAL OF YEAR SIX?**

She stared at it. Her heart was going hard against her ribs, hard enough that she could hear it in the quiet, hard enough that it seemed suddenly like the loudest thing on the station.

Year Six. There was no harvest festival in Year Six. Meridian Below had been dark by then, empty, consolidated, gone. She remembered no festival. She remembered nothing at all about Year Six.

And that, she realized, sitting on the warm floor of the cold room, was the strangest thing of all.

Because she was starting to suspect that she was supposed to.
