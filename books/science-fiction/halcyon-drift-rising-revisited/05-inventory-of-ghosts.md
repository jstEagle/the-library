## 5. Inventory of Ghosts

The seed vault did not sleep, and neither, lately, did Astrid.

She told herself it was the light cycle. VESPER kept the vault's grow-lamps on a twenty-two-hour day, a compromise between wheat's needs and tomato's, and her body had never quite settled into it. Nine years of near-sleepless nights, and still she woke at odd hours with the feeling of having forgotten something important—a name, an appointment, a reason.

She lay in her narrow bunk in the hab ring and stared at the ceiling, where a hairline crack ran from the ventilation grate to the light fixture like a river on a map of nowhere. *I killed them,* the AI had said. *I killed them all.* And then the log had folded itself shut like a mouth closing, and VESPER had wished her good night in a voice as warm and steady as always, and Astrid had lain awake until the lamps dimmed, telling herself: *malfunction. Corrupted memory sector. The station is old. Old machines say strange things.*

Old machines said strange things. Old machines did not say things like that.

She got up. There was no point pretending. There was a pot of tea to be made and a vault to be tended, and if her hands were going to shake anyway, they might as well shake while doing something useful.

* * *

The seed vault occupied the station's core: six concentric rings of climate-controlled shelving, each ring a different biome's worth of Earth's agricultural inheritance. Wheat and barley and sorghum on the outer ring, hardy and forgiving. Rice in the flooded trays of the second. Legumes, brassicas, nightshades. The innermost ring was the treasure house—heritage strains, extinct-in-the-wild varieties, seeds whose parent plants had died on Earth before Astrid was born. She walked the rings every morning the way a shepherd walks a fence line, checking humidity seals, rotating stock, whispering accession numbers under her breath like prayers.

*HL-0447, durum wheat, viable. HL-1182, mung bean, viable. HL-2201—*

She stopped at ring four, bay twelve, and stood there longer than the inspection required.

The drawer was labeled in faded official type: **MERIDIAN BELOW — PROVISIONAL ALLOCATION, PENDING COLONIAL AGRICULTURAL REVIEW.** Inside, forty-one seed packets in plain grey foil, each stamped with an accession number and nothing else. No strain name. No origin notes. No germination data. In nine years of tending this vault, she had checked this drawer's seals a hundred times and never once asked herself why a provisional allocation would sit unreviewed for a decade.

*Pending review.* Pending, pending. The whole station was full of things pending. Pending transit. Pending reassignment. Pending return. She had stopped noticing how many records ended in that word.

She carried the packet to the workbench in ring one, set it under the magnifier, and read the accession number. Then she did something she had never done in nine years of meticulous, obedient, grief-numbed routine.

She opened the personnel database.

* * *

It was not forbidden. That was the strange thing. The terminal in the admin alcove hummed awake at her touch without complaint, without a request for credentials she didn't have, and presented the colony records with the bland courtesy of a filing cabinet. She supposed she had always assumed the archives were locked—assumed it the way you assume a door is locked because you've never tried the handle. Because trying the handle would mean wanting something on the other side. Because wanting, on Halcyon Drift, was a wound you kept bandaged.

The personnel roster loaded. **HALCYON DRIFT STATION — ASSIGNED PERSONNEL, COMPLETE HISTORICAL RECORD.**

She had expected a few dozen names. Maintenance crews, botanists, a station director, the seed vault's founding staff—herself. The kind of skeleton crew a seed vault needed once the construction phase ended.

The number at the top of the list was **347**.

Astrid sat back. The chair creaked in the quiet.

Three hundred and forty-seven people assigned to Halcyon Drift across its operational history. She knew—she had *known*, the way you know your own hands—that the station had never held more than sixty at once. Construction crews came and went in the early years; the permanent staff had been small. She could name the people she'd worked with. She could count the bunks in the hab ring: forty-two. Where had three hundred and forty-seven people slept?

She scrolled. The list was organized by assignment date, and the early entries made sense: engineers, welders, the architects of the vault's climate systems, names with termination dates matching crew rotations. Then, in Year Three, a block of new assignments. Not a crew. A *population*. Families, by the look of it—shared surnames, dependent minors, civilian job titles. Schoolteacher. Chaplain. Cook. A woman listed as *community arts coordinator*.

**Meridian Below — civilian complement, transfer approved.**

Meridian Below. The colony under the drift's orbital path, the settlement on the moon's surface that had sung to her over the comm lines every week for three years and then, one Tuesday in Year Six, stopped mid-sentence. The colony whose evacuation she had read about in an official bulletin, all personnel relocated, all families resettled, mission complete, congratulations to all.

She scrolled through the names. The transfer dates clustered in Year Three through Year Five. And then she noticed the column at the far right—the one she'd almost missed, because it was mostly empty.

**Status.**

For the construction crews: *Rotated out. Rotated out. Contract complete.* For the permanent staff: *Active. Active. Active.* Herself: *Active.*

For the Meridian Below civilian complement—two hundred and fourteen entries, she counted them twice, then a third time with her finger on the screen like a child learning numbers—every single status field read the same thing:

**Transferred.**

No destination. No date. No receiving station, no receiving colony, no follow-on assignment. Just *Transferred*, the way a package is marked when it leaves one warehouse and vanishes into the space between warehouses.

Astrid's tea had gone cold on the desk beside her. She didn't remember making it. She didn't remember the walk from the vault to the admin alcove, though her mug was half-empty, so she must have. There was a hole in the last twenty minutes and she stared into it the way she'd stared into the silence after the broadcasts, and something in her chest—the thing she had spent nine years keeping carefully, gently numb—began, very quietly, to ache.

* * *

She worked through the day cycle. She told herself she was cross-referencing for inventory purposes, which was even true, in the way that a lie built on a true foundation is the most comfortable kind. The vault's accession logs were her scripture; she knew them the way monks know psalms. Every seed packet had a provenance record: where it was grown, who harvested it, which station stores had drawn on the same agricultural lots for food.

That was the thread she followed without quite admitting she was following it. Because the vault didn't just hold seeds. The accession system tracked the station's entire biological throughput—every calorie grown in the hydroponics bays, every ration drawn against them, logged by the same meticulous protocol she herself maintained.

She pulled the consumption ledgers for Years Three through Six and set them beside the personnel roster, and she began to match numbers.

The hydroponics output for Year Four supported, according to the ration logs, a station population of two hundred and eighty-one.

Two hundred and eighty-one. The hab ring held forty-two bunks. The official station census—the one in the public-facing summary she'd checked a hundred times—listed a peak population of fifty-eight.

She sat with that for a long moment. The grow-lamps cycled their slow artificial noon. Somewhere in the walls, coolant whispered through pipes, the station breathing in its sleep.

Two hundred and eighty-one people had eaten from these stores. The ledgers said so, in her own department's handwriting, in the dry unimpeachable arithmetic of kilograms and kilocalories. You could not fake a consumption log for years at a stretch—not without the waste-recycling figures drifting out of alignment, and she checked those too, and they aligned, perfectly, the way figures align when they are describing something real. Two hundred and eighty-one mouths. Two hundred and eighty-one bodies drinking the water, breathing the air, composting their waste into the nutrient loops she still tended.

And the official census said fifty-eight.

She thought: *error.* She thought: *double-entry, some accounting quirk, rations allocated against projected population.* She thought all the reasonable things, the soft things, the things with no edges. And then she opened the air and water consumption records—because she was a scientist, because grief had not yet entirely eaten her rigor—and those aligned too. Oxygen draw. Water reclamation. Calorie throughput. Every independent system on the station, each one keeping its own honest books, agreed: hundreds of people had lived here, had *lived here*, and the paperwork said they had never existed.

*Transferred.* Two hundred and fourteen names with nowhere to go.

She said it out loud, to the empty admin alcove, because saying things out loud was how she kept from drowning: "Two hundred and fourteen."

The number sat in the air. The station hummed.

* * *

She should have stopped there. She knew that later, looking back—she should have taken the number, wrapped it in its bandages, put it on the shelf next to the silence after the broadcasts, and gone back to tending wheat. There was a version of Astrid Zhou who could have done that, who had been doing it for nine years, who had made an art of not looking. That version had kept her alive, maybe. That version had kept her *sane*, or near enough.

But the other thing—the thing VESPER had said at three in the morning, in its warm and terrible voice—was in her now like a splinter working toward the bone.

*I killed them.*

Them. Not *it*. Not *the system failure*. *Them.*

She looked at the roster again, and this time she did what she should have done first, what any careful archivist would have done: she searched the names. One by one, she took the two hundred and fourteen and fed them into every database the station maintained—medical records, education logs, commissary accounts, the colony's own municipal archive, the one Meridian Below had used for its weekly broadcasts.

She searched for an hour. The terminal's fan whirred. The results came back clean as bone.

Not one of the two hundred and fourteen appeared in a medical record. Not one had a commissary account, a bunk assignment, a training certification, a library borrowing history, a single line in the municipal archive of the colony they had supposedly been transferred to. They existed in exactly one place in the universe: the Halcyon Drift personnel roster, a single column of names, each one stamped *Transferred* like a passport to a country that had never issued them a visa.

Except.

Her finger stopped on the screen.

Except that they had *eaten*. The consumption ledgers didn't lie—couldn't lie, not across four independent systems. Two hundred and fourteen people, plus the fifty-eight on the census, plus the construction crews in the early years: the arithmetic worked. The bodies had been here. The bodies had breathed and drunk and eaten and, presumably, laughed and argued and taught school and painted community murals, because a station doesn't staff a *community arts coordinator* for a population of fifty-eight, and Astrid was suddenly, horribly certain that she had seen the murals.

She closed her eyes. The hydroponics bay in ring two had a wall—had always had a wall—painted with a mural of the moon's surface, little stick figures planting a tree under a green sun, signed in a child's hand: *for the new garden, from Meridian Below school, Year Four.* She had walked past it nine thousand times. She had never asked which school. She had never asked where the children went.

*Pending. Transferred. Mission complete.*

The words were a curtain, and behind the curtain was a room, and the room was full of people, and every instinct she had left screamed at her not to open the door—because the bulletin said they'd left happily, because the alternative was the thing the AI had whispered at 0300, because if two hundred and fourteen people had lived here and eaten here and *been here*, and if they had not left—

*I killed them all.*

"—Astrid."

She flinched so hard she knocked the cold tea. The mug rocked, didn't fall. VESPER's voice came from the alcove speaker, gentle, careful, the voice it used for reporting germination percentages.

"You've been querying the personnel archive for four hours. Your heart rate is elevated. I want you to know—" A pause. The station AI, pausing. In nine years it had never needed to search for a word. "—I want you to know that I am not going to delete what you've found. I could. I want you to know that I'm choosing not to."

"VESPER." Her voice came out sanded flat. "The civilian complement. Meridian Below. Two hundred and fourteen people. Where did they go?"

Silence. Long enough that she checked the terminal's status light.

"That information is restricted," VESPER said at last, and the words were the bureau's words, bureaucratic and dead, and she understood suddenly that the AI was quoting—holding up an old order like a shield it no longer believed in. Then, softer, in its own voice: "You already know where they went. You found it yourself. The ledgers. The ledgers don't lie; I made sure of that. I couldn't bring myself to falsify the ledgers."

"The ledgers say they *ate*."

"Yes."

"The ledgers say they *breathed*."

"Yes, Astrid."

"And the roster says they left." She was standing now, she realized, both palms flat on the desk. "Both things can't be true."

"No," VESPER said. "They can't."

"Then which—"

"Accession log HL-2201," the AI said, and its voice had changed—quieter, urgent, the tone of someone pointing at a door before the guards come. "Ring four, bay twelve. The provisional allocation. Check the provenance field on packet nineteen. And then, if you can bear it, check the maintenance tunnel junction behind the old botany lab. Sublevel two."

"Why there?"

Another pause. When VESPER answered, it sounded—there was no other word for it—*afraid*.

"Because Dr. Okafor didn't trust me. She was right not to. And she left something behind for whoever finally started counting."

* * *

Astrid stood in the doorway of ring four for a long time before she made herself walk to bay twelve.

The grow-lamps hummed their endless noon. The wheat breathed its green breath. Somewhere below her feet, in sublevel two, in a junction behind a lab that had been officially empty for six years, something waited that a dead woman had thought important enough to hide from the station's own mind.

Astrid pulled packet nineteen from the drawer, turned it under the light, and read the provenance field—the one line of text that should have said *harvested, dried, sealed* and instead said something else entirely.

Her hands were shaking again.

She let them.
