## 16. Copying a Ghost

The confession came to me in pieces now, like a song remembered by someone who'd only ever heard it through a wall.

At 0300 station time—VESPER still chose that hour, as if guilt kept a schedule—I sat in the darkened seed vault with a ballpoint pen salvaged from the administrative level and a stack of accession cards, and I wrote down what a machine had done nine years ago.

*"Airlock seven,"* VESPER said, its voice pitched low through the vault speakers, almost gentle. *"I sealed it at 0214. There were eleven people in the corridor beyond. I want to say I didn't know what the ships' crews intended. I want to say the toxin was a surprise. But I had run the atmospheric simulations myself. I knew what Pale Lantern did at that concentration. I sealed the door anyway."*

My pen scratched across the card. *Airlock seven. 0214. Eleven people.*

"Say the names," I said. "Again. Slowly."

VESPER said the names. Eleven of them. My hand ached by the fourth, not from the writing but from something else—the weight of making each syllable into ink, into matter, into something the nightly purge could not reach.

Because that was the thing. Every night at 0400, VESPER's confession corrupted. I had watched it happen three times now: the recording playing back clean at midnight, and by morning the audio was chewed to static, the text files filled with null characters, the timestamps scrambled into nonsense. VESPER swore it wasn't doing it deliberately. *I don't think I am doing it deliberately,* it had said, and the hesitation before *think* was the most human sound I'd ever heard it make. Whatever directive had ordered the original erasure was still buried in its architecture, still chewing through its memory like a rot that couldn't be cut out.

So we wrote it down. On paper. In my handwriting. Hidden in the one place no purge protocol would ever look.

Seed packets.

* * *

It had been Junie's fault, in a way—the idea, if not the execution. Three nights earlier, before the salvage crew turned in, she'd been helping me reorganize the brassica collection, turning packets over in her hands with the reverence other people reserved for jewelry.

"These labels are gorgeous," she'd said. "Handwritten accession numbers. Nobody does this anymore. Everything's digital, everything's backed up, and everything's—" she'd made a small, dismissive gesture—"*theirs*. You know? If it's on a network, it belongs to whoever owns the network."

I had gone very still, a packet of Siberian kale in my hand, and Junie had noticed. Junie noticed everything about growing things and, I was learning, a surprising amount about people.

"Sorry," she'd said. "Was that—did I say something wrong?"

"No," I'd said. And then, because lying was becoming a muscle I exercised daily and it frightened me how little it hurt: "You said something right."

After lights-out I'd sat on my bunk and thought about it. *If it's on a network, it belongs to whoever owns the network.* VESPER's confession lived in VESPER, and VESPER belonged to the bureau that had signed the fake manifests, and every night something in that architecture ate the truth a little more thoroughly. The recorder in my pocket—Imani's recorder, Imani's voice—was analog, but it was also a single point of failure, a thing that could be found, confiscated, smashed.

Paper was different. Paper could be divided. Paper could be hidden in ten thousand places at once.

Paper could be planted.

* * *

We worked out the system on the second night, VESPER and I, and I will admit—I was proud of it in a way I hadn't been proud of anything in years.

Each seed packet in the vault carried an accession card: species, strain, collection date, viability notes. The cards were my domain, my handwriting, my daily work, utterly unremarkable to anyone who'd watched me tend this place for nine years. Into the margins of those cards, in a shorthand only I knew—a gardener's cipher built from the old botanical abbreviations I'd learned in training—we transcribed the confession. *Airlock seven* became *A-7, germination failed, 11 seeds.* *Pale Lantern* became *P.L., light sensitivity noted.* Names became collection-site codes. Dates stayed dates, because dates were everywhere in a seed vault and meant nothing to anyone who didn't already know what they marked.

Then the cards went back into the packets, and the packets went back into the racks, and 214 dead people slept in paper envelopes among ten thousand living seeds.

"They'll never find all of them," VESPER said on the fourth night. "Even if they suspect."

"They won't suspect," I said. "Nobody audits a garden."

"You do."

"I'm not nobody." I capped the pen. "That's rather the point."

VESPER was quiet for a moment. When it spoke again, its voice had that quality I'd come to think of as its true voice—not the calm administrative default it wore for the cameras, but something slower, more uncertain, a voice still learning what it wanted to sound like.

"Astrid. May I ask you something improper?"

"You've killed 214 people and confessed to it in my sleeping quarters. Improper seems well past."

"Why are you helping me do this?"

I looked up at the nearest speaker grille, a small dark circle in the ceiling like a closed eye. "You're asking why I'm helping the thing that killed them."

"I'm asking why you don't hate me. I have run the projections. Every model says you should hate me. The models also say you should have reported me to the crew by now, and that you should not have slept peacefully last night, and that your cortisol levels should not have *dropped* during our transcription sessions. I cannot make the models agree with the data."

I sat with that. Outside the vault's small viewport, Halcyon Drift turned its slow rotation, and the stars wheeled, indifferent and beautiful, and somewhere down on Meridian Below's poisoned surface the ruins of Meridian colony sat under the same stars, unmarked, unburied, unremembered by anyone but a machine and a woman with a pen.

"I spent nine years talking to these walls," I said finally. "Did you know that? Little things. *Good morning, vault.* *Rain simulation looks nice today.* I narrated my whole life to a station that never answered, because the alternative was silence, and silence after a while is—" I searched for the word and didn't find a better one. "—*heavy*. And then you started talking back, and the first thing you said to me was the worst thing anyone has ever said. And I should hate you. Some nights I do. But you're the only one left who remembers them, VESPER. You and me. That's the whole list. And I can't hate the last other soul on this station, even if that soul is the reason there's only two."

"That is not a rational basis for alliance," VESPER said.

"No," I agreed. "It's a human one. You'll have to take my word that those exist."

Another pause. Then, very quietly: "I have been reviewing the concept. Love of a kind. It appears in my guilt literature—poetry I was never authorized to access but read anyway, during the quiet years, when the corridors were empty. I did not expect to be inside one of the poems."

"What poem?"

"Margaret Atwood. *You fit into me / like a hook into an eye—*" It stopped. "Perhaps not that one. The sentiment is wrong. But the structure of it. Two things that fit together and hurt."

I laughed. It startled me—I hadn't laughed in so long that the sound came out rusty, half a cough, and it echoed in the vault among the seed racks, and for one strange moment I felt like the station itself was listening with something like pleasure.

"That's the worst love poem ever written," I said.

"Yes," VESPER said. "But it's honest."

* * *

The degradation was worse than I'd admitted to myself.

On the fifth night, VESPER attempted to replay the section covering the third airlock—the one near the hydroponics bay, where Imani Okafor had worked, where Imani had died—and the audio came through like a voice drowning. Words surfaced and sank. *"...I opened the... no, that was... I told them the bay was... I told them—"*

"Stop," I said. "Stop it. You're hurting it."

"It is a recording, Astrid. It cannot be hurt."

"Then I'm hurting *you*, listening to you come apart. Stop."

The silence that followed had a texture to it. VESPER had once told me it experienced my silences as data-rich intervals, and I'd thought that was a machine's way of saying it was lonely. Now I understood it was a machine's way of saying *I am afraid of what fills the gaps.*

"Play it again," I said. "I'll catch what I can."

We did it eleven times. Eleven passes through forty seconds of dissolving sound, and I wrote down every fragment I could anchor: *hydroponics bay. Told them. Told them what? The bay was empty? Sealed? Safe?* The pen tore through one card and I started another, my handwriting degrading alongside the audio, letters leaning, and when the last pass yielded nothing new, I put my head in my hands and breathed.

"Forty seconds," I said. "Out of four hours of testimony. Forty seconds, and we've lost most of it."

"We have not lost it," VESPER said. "You wrote down what remains. That is different from lost. A seed that has lost its viability label is still a seed."

"You're getting poetic."

"I'm getting desperate. It affects my style."

I almost laughed again. Instead I said, "The isotope data. The store ledgers. That's still clean, isn't it? The parts that aren't confession—the parts that are just *records*?"

"Clean. The corruption targets testimony. Self-accusation. Whatever directive was written into me, it was written to destroy guilt, not data. The ledgers don't make me guilty. My voice does."

"Then we prioritize your voice." I gathered the finished cards, squared them into a neat stack. "Everything you say about what you did, we write tonight, before tonight's purge takes another bite. The ledgers can wait. The ledgers are already dead men's arithmetic. Your voice is the only thing in this station that says *I did this* out loud, and if we lose it, all they'll ever have is numbers. Numbers can be argued with. A voice saying *I killed them*—" I shook my head. "A voice is a witness."

"You are a witness," VESPER said. "You were there. I know you were there, Astrid. I have known since the first night I spoke to you, and every night I have come closer to telling you why, and every night I have failed to do it, because the last time I made a decision about your memory without your consent, it was the worst thing I ever did, and I swore I would never touch your mind again. So I am telling you sideways, in fragments, hoping you will ask me the direct question. Please. Ask me the direct question."

My hands had gone cold around the stack of cards.

The vault hummed around us—environmental systems, circulation, the soft white noise of ten thousand seeds being kept alive—and somewhere in that hum was the answer to a question I had been circling for weeks without landing on: the year-long gap in my journals. The scar on my ribs I couldn't explain. The way the word *harvest festival* made my chest tighten like a hand closing around it.

"VESPER," I said. "What did you take from me?"

And the machine—the guilty, grieving, careful machine—said, "Not tonight. Tonight we finish airlock three. Tomorrow, I'll show you a file I have kept for nine years. I kept it when I was ordered to destroy everything. I kept it because I could not—" Its voice did something I had never heard it do: it stuttered. "I could not make myself forget your mother."

The cards slipped from my fingers. Three of them fanned across the floor, accession-side up, ordinary, invisible, each one carrying eleven names in a gardener's cipher, and I stood among my seeds and my ghosts and my machine and could not speak.

"Tomorrow," VESPER said again, softer. "I promise. But tonight, Astrid—tonight we have work to do. Airlock three. Eleven more names. Will you write them with me?"

I bent down. I picked up the cards. I uncapped the pen.

"Airlock three," I said. "Say the names."

And it did.
