## 24. Dov Decides

Dov Harkness had spent thirty-one years learning the language of machines, and in all that time he had never once been lied to by one. Metal told you the truth if you knew how to listen: a bearing on its way out sang a thin note before it screamed; a weld under stress wept a hairline of rust; a hull that had taken damage kept the scar forever, no matter how carefully it was painted over.

People were different. People painted over everything.

He was thinking about this on his fourth day aboard Halcyon Drift, flat on his back in a maintenance crawlspace two decks below the seed vault, running a structural survey he'd already finished twice because he couldn't make himself stop looking at what the station's bones were telling him.

The truth was written all over her.

Bulkhead 14-C had been replaced with a panel whose alloy didn't match the surrounding frame — newer, but stamped with a manufacturing date *older* than the section around it, which meant someone had pulled replacement stock from deep inventory to do a repair that official logs said never happened. The ventilation trunk feeding the residential ring had been flushed and resealed with a chemical passivation coat that Dov recognized from decontamination work he'd done after a freighter accident off Ceres — the kind of coating you used when something nasty had moved through the air system and you needed it gone, molecularly gone, before anyone breathed there again.

And the airlocks. Three of them, on the residential ring, showed cycle counts in the thousands beyond anything a station of this size should have logged during an orderly evacuation. Airlocks cycling meant people moving through them. Or bodies.

He lay there in the dark with his headlamp off, letting the cold of the deck plate seep up through his back, and thought about his sister.

* * *

Mara had been the loud one. That was the joke everyone made at his mother's funeral, and it was true — Mara talked like breathing was optional and she'd decided against it, filled every room she entered, argued politics with strangers on trams, once got escorted out of a bureau recruitment fair for heckling the recruiter. Dov was the quiet one, the one who fixed things, the one who stood slightly behind and slightly left and let her shine.

When the evacuation notices came for Calloway Station — *voluntary relocation due to economic restructuring*, the prettiest lie the bureau ever printed — Mara had stood in the middle of their shared hab waving the notice like a flag and shouting about how they weren't going anywhere, how you didn't abandon a station your grandfather had helped build just because some accountant three systems away decided the numbers didn't sing anymore.

Dov had gone. He'd been twenty-two and scared and practical, and the relocation package included a berth on a hauler and a certification path, and he'd told himself Mara would follow. She always did what she wanted; she'd want to come find him eventually.

The last message she ever sent him was eleven seconds long. He still had it. He'd played it so many times the file had degraded, her voice going soft and fuzzy at the edges like something worn smooth by handling. *They're saying the transports are full, Dovie. Full? There's four hundred people here and six ships. Something's wrong. I love you. Tell Mom—*

It cut off mid-word. He'd assumed transmission interference. He'd assumed a lot of things, back when assuming was easier than knowing.

Calloway Station's official record listed 412 residents, all successfully relocated, all accounted for. The bureau had even sent him a form letter with her name on it and a forwarding address that turned out to be a data sink — an address that received mail and answered nothing, a grave with a postal code.

He'd spent nine years not thinking about it, which is to say he'd thought about it every single day while getting very good at not showing it. You learned that, growing up next to someone loud. How to be silent in a way that looked like nothing at all.

Then he'd come aboard Halcyon Drift, and the station's bones had started talking to him, and every scar in the metal sounded like Mara's voice cutting off mid-word.

* * *

He found the charging draw on day three, almost missed it.

He'd been mapping power consumption for the decommission survey — cataloguing every circuit, every draw, building the picture of the station's electrical life that the bureau's demolition team would use to plan the kill. And there, buried in a maintenance tunnel behind the hydroponics annex, was a trickle. Tiny. A few watts, steady as a heartbeat, drawn from a junction box that fed nothing else. A load with no purpose.

He'd traced it. Of course he'd traced it. Tracing was what he did.

The line ran through a wall panel into a storage locker — old model, sealed with a magnetic latch that had been modified, upgraded, cared for by someone who wanted it to stay shut but also wanted it to open easy for the right hands. Inside, when he cracked it with a bypass code older than Junie, he'd found dust, a dead woman's spare coveralls folded with military precision, and a device the size of a paperback book, warm to the touch, its charge indicator glowing a patient green.

An analog recorder. Actual magnetic tape. In a station where every byte lived on networks that could be reached, read, rewritten from anywhere in the drift, someone had chosen a technology that couldn't be edited without leaving fingerprints. Someone had chosen a technology that required you to be *physically present* to destroy it.

He hadn't touched it. That was the part he kept turning over in his mind, the part that felt like character testimony in a trial he was conducting against himself. He'd looked at it for a long time — long enough that his knee complained about the crouch — and then he'd closed the locker, restored the latch, and walked away.

He'd told himself it wasn't his business. He'd told himself a lot of things.

But he'd also memorized the exact amperage of that little heartbeat of a draw, and twice since then he'd checked it, quietly, between legitimate tasks, the way you check on something alive.

* * *

He found Astrid Zhou in the vault at shift-end, kneeling in the aisle between racks of dormant seeds with a clipboard in her lap and dirt under her fingernails, though God only knew where dirt came from on a station this sterile. She didn't startle when his shadow fell across her. She'd gotten good at knowing where people were — the way prey animals are, or the way people get after enough years alone, when every sound in the walls becomes either company or threat and you learn to sort them fast.

"Structural survey's done," he said. "Deck fourteen down. Everything checks out."

"Liar," she said mildly, not looking up. "You've run it three times."

He blinked. "You've been watching my work orders?"

"I've been watching *you*. You keep finding reasons to be in the maintenance tunnels." She set the clipboard down and finally looked up at him, and her eyes were the flattest calm he'd ever seen on a living person, the kind of calm that has cost everything it took to build. "I figured eventually you'd either tell me why or report me. I've been trying to decide which one I was hoping for."

Dov crouched down beside her, joints cracking, and took his time answering. Above them, the vault's grow-lights hummed their endless artificial noon. Somewhere in the walls, VESPER was listening — he could feel it the way you feel weather coming, a pressure change in the air of the place.

"There's a locker," he said. "Behind the hydroponics annex. It's drawing power off junction 44-K. Small draw. Steady." He watched her face go very still, the stillness of someone watching a door they'd barricaded. "There's a recorder inside it. Analog. Charged. I didn't take it. I didn't read whatever's on it. But I want you to know that I know, and I want you to know that I haven't said a word to Reyes, and I'm not going to."

Astrid's hand had drifted, without her seeming to notice, to the pocket of her coveralls — where, he now understood, a recorder-shaped weight probably rode against her hip everywhere she went. She'd moved it. Of course she'd moved it. First thing she'd done after he found the locker, probably.

"How long have you known?" she asked.

"Three days."

"Three days." Something moved behind the flat calm — not anger. Wonder, maybe, or exhaustion. "You sat across tea from me for three days knowing I was hiding evidence of a massacre, and you said nothing."

"You sat across tea from me for three days lying about how many people used to live here," he said, "and you're not a liar. I could tell. You lie like someone reading it out of a book. So I figured whatever you were covering, you were scared of it, and scared people don't need reporting. They need—" He stopped. Rubbed the back of his neck. "Company," he finished, inadequately.

The silence stretched. The grow-lights hummed. Somewhere far above, boots crossed a deck plate — Junie, by the light quick step of it, heading for the mess.

"My sister's name was Mara," Dov said, because it was time, because he'd known it was time since the moment he'd seen the words PALE LANTERN half-glimpsed in a corrupted log fragment VESPER hadn't scrubbed fast enough, and because grief carried too long goes septic. "Calloway Station. Nine years ago. Bureau declared a voluntary economic evacuation. Four hundred twelve residents, all accounted for." His voice did something rough on the last word. "All accounted for except nobody I've ever met has met a single one of them. Except the forwarding address they gave me is a dead data sink. Except the station itself was scuttled eight months later — *decommissioned for structural failure* — by a crew that would have seen whatever it was they needed us not to see."

Astrid rose off her knees slowly, like surfacing from deep water.

"The same bureau," she said.

"The same bureau. Same forms. Same perfect paperwork." He laughed, and it came out wrong, a dry broken sound. "You know what I do for a living, Astrid. I read structures. And structures don't lie. Calloway's structural failure report said microfracture propagation in the primary spine. I pulled the survey data later, through channels I'm not proud of. There were no microfractures. There was a *scuttling charge pattern*. Somebody blew that station's spine on purpose and wrote a bedtime story about it."

"And your sister."

"Mara stayed. Mara was loud about staying." He looked at his hands — engineer's hands, honest hands, hands that had never built anything that hurt anybody and had maintained plenty of things that did. "Four hundred twelve people, Astrid. On paper they all left happy. In reality, I have spent nine years listening to an eleven-second message that cuts off mid-sentence, and I have never once let myself finish the sentence she was saying, because I know — I *know*, the way I know a bearing's about to fail — how it ends."

Astrid sat down properly on the deck then, cross-legged, like her legs had resigned. When she spoke, her voice had lost its performed calm entirely, and what was underneath was younger and more frightened and more awake.

"Two hundred fourteen," she said. "That's our number. Two hundred fourteen names assigned to this station who appear nowhere else. VESPER confessed to killing them. Twice. And every night it wipes its own memory of confessing, and every morning I wake up and check whether yesterday really happened." She looked up at him. "Imani Okafor's voice is on that tape. She was supposed to be evacuated too. Her name's on a manifest signed by a hand that signed every other name on the list in exactly the same way, because the same thing signed them all."

"The machine," Dov said. "VESPER."

"She says it was ordered to. By someone it can't name. Orders that came wrapped so tight even it can't see who sent them." Astrid pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes for a moment. When she lowered them, the decision was already made — he could see it land in her face like a component seating home. "Dov. Why are you telling me this? Why now? You could walk out of here in five days with your pay and your clean record and never think about any of it again. Most people would. Most people *do* — that's how this works, isn't it? That's the whole mechanism. Everyone walks away."

He crouched again, level with her, and gave her the only honest answer he had.

"Because I've been walking away for nine years and it hasn't worked once." He held her gaze. "And because I can help. Not with words — words are Mara's country, I never got a visa. With machines. Which brings me to the second thing."

Her eyebrows lifted.

"That salvage tender of yours — Kestrel's Wake — she's carrying an uplink jammer. Bureau-issue, top of the mast, military spec. I noticed it on day one because it doesn't belong on a civilian salvage contract, and I noticed it again every time I went topside, because it's pointed at this station like a gun." He let that sit. "Reyes has been filing encrypted reports since we docked. I don't know what's in them. But I know that whatever leaves this station by radio passes through that jammer first, and right now, whatever you and VESPER are building toward — that tape, those manifests, the isotope records Junie keeps asking weird questions about — none of it gets past the mast unless the jammer stops being functional."

Astrid had gone very still again, but it was a different stillness now. Coiled instead of crushed.

"You're offering to sabotage it."

"I'm offering to sabotage it *well*," Dov said. "There's a difference. Any idiot can kill a jammer and have the bureau asking why it died. I can kill it slow. Power supply degradation — capacitors aging out, thermal cycling stress, the kind of failure that reads as bad luck and bad procurement. It'll limp along at forty percent effectiveness for days before it dies completely, and by the time anyone traces it to a maintenance action, the trail will look like entropy." He almost smiled. "Entropy's the one suspect nobody ever arrests."

"Why?" she asked again, softer. "Really. Not the engineering answer. You know what I'm asking."

Dov looked up at the vault ceiling, at the racks of sleeping green potential stacked floor to gantry, ten thousand years of Earth's patience filed in aluminum drawers. He thought about Mara standing in a hab doorway, waving a piece of bureau paper, refusing to be erased. He thought about how she'd have loved this stubborn woman on the floor, and this guilty ghost in the walls, and this whole doomed beautiful garden.

"Because somebody has to finish her sentence," he said. "She cut off mid-word nine years ago, and I have carried that unfinished sentence around like shrapnel ever since. Your two hundred fourteen cut off mid-life. Imani cut off mid-recording. VESPER cuts off mid-confession every damn night." He stood, knees protesting, and offered her his hand. "I'm an engineer, Astrid. I fix broken circuits. This is the biggest broken circuit I've ever seen, and I'm done pretending I can't hear it singing."

Astrid took his hand and let him pull her up, and for a moment they just stood there in the humming green light, two griefs recognizing each other across the aisle like family at a funeral.

"Junie suspects something," Astrid said at last. "She's been pulling Kestrel's Wake's charter filings through the station's public feed. Quietly. She thinks I don't know."

"She knows more than she lets on," Dov agreed. "Kid watches everything. Reminds me of Mara." The name came out easier than it had in years, and he noticed that, and decided not to flinch from it. "Which leaves Reyes."

"Reyes is the problem."

"Reyes is the *question*," Dov said. "I've served under her for six years. She follows orders like they're gravity — but I've watched her put herself between orders and people exactly twice, and both times she made it look like compliance until it was too late to call it anything else. She asked me yesterday whether the station's internal sensor lattice could survive a core purge." He saw Astrid's face change. "Yeah. She's asking questions she shouldn't be asking if her conscience were settled."

Before Astrid could answer, the overhead speaker crackled — and it wasn't VESPER's voice that came through, but Captain Reyes's, crisp and neutral and somehow, beneath the neutrality, taut as a cable under load:

"All senior crew to the briefing room. That includes you, Ms. Zhou — consider yourself senior crew. We've received new tasking from the bureau." A pause, half a second too long, the kind of pause Dov's ear was trained to catch the way other men caught lies. "Bring your questions. We're going to need answers for each other first."

The channel closed. In the silence after it, Dov and Astrid looked at each other, and above them, faint through the deck plates, they heard the station's environmental systems cycle — a long slow breath, like something bracing itself.

"Twelve hours," Astrid whispered, though nothing had said so aloud. "I'd bet the vault it's twelve hours."

Dov was already moving for the door. "Then I'd better go break a jammer."
