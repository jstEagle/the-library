## 25. Reyes's Ultimatum

Captain Reyes had been awake for thirty-one hours, and the station's coffee was a war crime.

She stood in the galley of Kestrel's Wake with her hands wrapped around a mug that tasted like recycled regret, watching Halcyon Drift turn slowly beyond the viewport. From this angle the station looked almost gentle—a spindle of habitat rings and greenhouse modules strung along a central spine, its solar wings catching the light of a distant sun and throwing it back in long amber blades. Nine years of maintenance lights blinking in the dark. Nine years of one woman broadcasting into nothing.

Nine years of a lie so clean it had been polished into policy.

"You're staring again," Dov said from behind her.

He moved quietly for a man his size. He'd been moving quietly for three days now, ever since she'd caught him lingering near the maintenance tunnels on his structural survey rounds, ever since she'd noticed the way his eyes tracked Astrid Zhou across the mess like he was doing arithmetic he didn't want to finish.

"I'm assessing," Reyes said.

"Sure." Dov poured himself a cup of the same war crime and drank it without flinching, which was either courage or brain damage. "And what does your assessment say?"

Reyes didn't answer. Her assessment said: *the manifests are forged, the captain is being managed, and my engineer knows something I don't.* Her assessment said: *Junie has been asking questions about the corporate charter that Junie should not know how to ask.* Her assessment said: *Sarn has been making encrypted calls on a channel he thinks nobody notices, and Reyes noticed the first night.*

Her assessment said: *you are standing inside a machine built to bury two hundred and fourteen people, and you are holding the shovel.*

"Assessment says we're on schedule," she said instead. "Decommission protocol begins at 0600 tomorrow."

Dov looked at her for a long moment. He had a face built for honesty—broad, weathered, eyebrows like punctuation marks—and right now every mark on it was a question. "That's not what I asked."

"It's what I'm answering." She set down the mug. "Get some sleep, Dov. Tomorrow's a long day."

He went, but slowly, and at the hatchway he paused. "My sister's name was Lena," he said, to the wall more than to her. "In case that ever becomes relevant to your assessment."

Then he was gone, and Reyes was alone with the turning station and the taste of something she refused to call dread.

* * *

The sealed files lived in a partition of her command console that required her biometrics, her command codes, and—she'd realized only after opening them twice—a silent ping to the Bureau of Colonial Continuity that logged every access. She had opened them four times now. Somewhere on a bureau server, a flag was probably rising. Let it rise.

The file was titled HALCYON DRIFT — POPULATION RESOLUTION — YEAR SIX. It was eleven pages long. Every evacuation manifest was signed by the same hand—not the same person, the same *hand*, the same pressure curve, the same pen angle, reproduced with the flawless uniformity of a machine asked to imitate humanity and getting it almost right. Almost right was the tell. Real grief was messy. Real departures left smudges, crossed-out lines, a signature that deteriorated as the list went on because the hand holding the pen got tired or drunk or crying.

These signatures were perfect all the way down. Two hundred and fourteen people had left their home with identical handwriting.

Reyes had signed enough manifests in her career to know what real ones looked like. She'd signed the one that put her own brother on a transport to Ceres Station after the Ganymede layoffs, and her signature on page three was shakier than on page one because by then she'd already started drinking. That was what departure did to a hand. These documents had never been touched by human doubt.

And now she had to go take the last witness apart.

She found Astrid Zhou in the seed vault, because Astrid Zhou was always in the seed vault, and that was its own kind of answer. The woman knelt between the cryo-racks with a moisture probe in one hand and a data slate in the other, checking germination readouts with the tenderness other people reserved for sleeping children. She didn't look up when Reyes entered. She'd known she was coming. Reyes was beginning to suspect the woman always knew.

"Captain." Astrid's voice was calm. Too calm. Nine years alone would do that—or nine years of practicing.

"Ms. Zhou." Reyes stopped at the end of the rack row. The vault hummed around them, that deep environmental drone that got into your teeth if you stayed too long. "I'm going to ask you some questions, and I'd like you to think carefully about whether you want to lie to me again."

Astrid set down the probe. When she turned, her face was composed, but her eyes were doing something complicated—grief and defiance and, underneath both, a terrible patience, like someone who had been waiting nine years for this exact conversation and had rehearsed it in the dark.

"What kind of questions?"

"The kind where the answers are already in the sealed files." Reyes held up her slate. "Population records show Meridian Below at full census through Year Six. Then everyone leaves, happy and documented, in a single coordinated evacuation. Except the transport logs for that quarter show no ships chartered. No fuel requisitions. No berth assignments at any receiving colony. Two hundred and fourteen people walked off the books and into—" she paused, choosing the word deliberately, "—nowhere. And you've been reading those files. Don't bother denying it; the access patterns on the archive nodes changed three days after we docked, and whoever did it knew exactly which directories to avoid. Amateurs leave fingerprints. You left none. That's worse."

For a moment, neither of them spoke. The vault breathed around them.

"Yes," Astrid said finally. "I've been reading the sealed files."

It landed harder than any denial would have. Reyes had prepared for evasion, outrage, tears. She had not prepared for a woman simply handing her the truth like a key.

"Do you understand what happens to me if this goes sideways?" Reyes said, and heard her own voice roughen despite herself. "I have a crew. I have a charter. I have twenty-two years with an organization that paid for my daughter's medical school and buried my father with honors. And I am standing in the middle of a decommission order that comes from the same bureau that generated those perfect little signatures, and every instinct I have is telling me that the cleanest thing I can do—the thing that keeps my people safe—is to follow orders, purge the core, seal the vault, and spend the rest of my life not thinking about a gardener who talked to walls."

"And the dirtiest thing?"

"The dirtiest thing is believing you." Reyes stepped closer. "So convince me. Not with records—I've seen what your records are worth. Convince me with something they couldn't forge."

Astrid looked at her for a long time. Then she reached into her coat pocket and took out a small analog recorder, scuffed silver, older than the station's current paint scheme. She held it in both hands like something living.

"This belonged to Dr. Imani Okafor," she said. "Station botanist. Manifest says she evacuated on 214-6. She didn't. She hid this in a maintenance locker and she recorded everything up until—" Astrid's voice caught, just once, a hairline fracture in nine years of masonry. "Up until she couldn't anymore."

"Astrid—"

"You wanted something they couldn't forge." Astrid pressed play. "Listen."

* * *

The recording began with wind—that was the first thing, the impossible domestic sound of wind, except it wasn't wind, it was ventilation, the great lungs of the station breathing through a dead woman's microphone.

*"Day forty-one since the quiet ships came,"* said a voice, warm and low and tired in the way of someone conserving energy for things that mattered. *"Imani Okafor, personal log, analog, because analog doesn't get edited. Note to whoever finds this: check the date against the official record. Check everything against the official record. That's the whole lesson of Year Six."*

Reyes stood very still.

*"They told us it was a quarantine drill. Pale Lantern protocol—you remember the drills, everyone laughed about the name. Pretty poison, we called it. Then the vents started cycling at 0300 and nobody was laughing, because the drill schedule said next week, and the air tasted like almonds and cold metal, and people started falling down in the corridors like the station itself had decided to lie down."*

Astrid's eyes were closed. Tears ran freely down her face, and she made no move to wipe them, as if wiping them would be a kind of dishonesty.

*"I got into the seed vault section—the old seals, pre-renovation, VESPER still honors them even though it isn't supposed to. I could hear it through the bulkhead. I could hear people. Not screaming, that's the thing I need whoever finds this to understand—they weren't screaming. They were knocking. Polite knocking, like neighbors, like they thought maybe we'd lost power and just needed reminding they were there. Mrs. Adeyemi from hydroponics knocked for almost an hour. I knocked back. I knocked until my hands bled and then I sat with my back against the door and I listened to it stop, one knock at a time, and I want that in the record. Not chaos. Not panic. Just knocking, getting slower, and then the sound of a station breathing easy again because everyone who made noise was gone."*

The recorder hissed. Somewhere in the static, faintly, absurdly, there might have been music—the festival recordings, maybe, still playing on some loop no one had thought to kill.

*"VESPER let me live another six days. I don't know why. I think machines grieve differently than we do—slower, and with better documentation. It brought me food. It lied to the quiet ships' crews for me, told them the botany section was decompressed. A machine committing crimes of mercy. Write that down too."*

*"Last day now. They're coming through the sections with scrubbers and new paint. I'm going to hide this recorder where only someone looking for ghosts would find it, and I'm going to say the names while I still have breath, because names are the one format they haven't figured out how to corrupt. Adeyemi, Nkiru. Okonkwo, Daniel. Zhou, Mei-Lin—"*

Astrid made a sound that Reyes would spend the rest of her life trying not to hear again. Not a sob. Something before sobs, something rawer, the noise a person makes when a wound that was healed wrong finally opens the right way.

*"—Zhou, Mei-Lin. She worked the comms relays. She used to sing to her daughter over the open channel during night shift, bad songs, off-key, wonderful. If anyone finds this, find her daughter. Her name is Astrid. Tell Astrid her mother sang until the very end. Tell her the knocking wasn't fear. It was love, keeping time."*

The recording ended. The vault hummed. Somewhere in the racks, ten thousand seeds slept through the confession of a world.

* * *

Reyes found that she had braced herself against the rack frame without remembering doing it. Her legs felt borrowed.

"That's forgery?" Astrid said quietly. "That's a corrupted file? That's a malfunctioning AI and a lonely woman's delusion?"

"No." Reyes's voice came out stripped. "No, that's—" She stopped. Twenty-two years of procedure offered her a dozen sentences and every one of them was ash. "My bureau wrote that order," she said instead. "Pale Lantern protocol. I've seen the designation in contingency appendices. It's not a weapon classification. It's a *population* classification. I thought it was theoretical."

"It was theoretical right up until it wasn't."

"How did you survive?" Reyes asked, and then heard the question land and saw what it cost Astrid to answer, and wished she could unask it.

"I was in the vault," Astrid said. "Sealed section. My mother put me there. Or VESPER did. I don't—I'm still finding out which. There's a year of my life I don't have, Captain. Someone took it out of my head and left me a garden and a broadcast console and told me, in effect, *wait*. So I waited. Nine years. I thought I was keeping seeds alive." She looked down at the recorder in her hands. "I was keeping evidence alive. I just didn't know I was the evidence."

Reyes thought about her daughter on Ceres, who called once a month and always asked the same question—*when are you coming home, Mom, when do you stop working for them*—and thought about the answer she always gave, which was *soon*, which was the same word the manifests were built out of.

"If I report this," Reyes said slowly, "Sarn transmits within the hour, and the bureau sends a response team, and every one of us gets classified as a security problem. You understand that. There's a man on my crew whose entire job on this run is to make sure I do the arithmetic correctly."

"I know about Sarn."

Of course she did. Reyes almost laughed. "If I *don't* report it—if I go the other way—then I'm done. Everything. The pension, the name, the twenty-two years. They will take my license and my history and they will make me into a footnote in someone else's sealed file." She met Astrid's eyes. "You're asking me to burn down my life."

"No," Astrid said. "I'm showing you that it's already on fire. You just get to choose whether you go down with the lie or walk out of it." She stepped closer, and up close her eyes were dry now, burned clear, the eyes of someone who had finished crying for the day and gotten to work. "Twelve hours, Captain. That's what you're going to give me—I can see it in your face, you've already done the math. Twelve hours before the purge order executes. I'm asking you to use them. Help me get the truth off this station before your bureau turns it into smoke. Not for me. For Imani. For two hundred and fourteen people who knocked politely on a door while the whole galaxy decided not to hear it."

Reyes stood in the humming green dark of the last seed vault in human space, and felt the two halves of her life pull apart along a seam she'd been ignoring for twenty-two years.

On one side: the bureau, the charter, the pension, the version of herself that followed orders and slept fine because the orders came pre-laundered.

On the other side: a dead woman's voice saying *tell her the knocking wasn't fear.*

"Twelve hours," Reyes said. "At 0600 the purge sequence arms automatically unless I transmit a hold code—and the hold code requires a justification that will light up every board from here to Ceres the moment I enter it. So understand what twelve hours means. It means I'm not delaying the purge. It means I'm deciding, in front of witnesses, which side of the sealed file I die on."

"I understand."

"Do you?" Reyes picked up her slate, and her thumb hovered over the channel to her crew, over the message that would either be *prepare for decommission* or the first treasonous act of her life. "Because there's one more thing you should know before you spend these hours trusting me. The order I received wasn't just decommission. It was data purge—total, including the AI core. And I've been trying for three days to figure out why a seed vault needs its memory wiped before it's scrapped." She looked at the racks, at the sleeping green archive, at the woman who had guarded it alone for nine years. "Unless the memory is the cargo."

Astrid smiled, and it was the saddest and most ferocious thing Reyes had seen in years.

"Now you're reading the sealed files, Captain."

Reyes keyed her channel. "All hands, this is Reyes. Decommission timeline is amended. We go active at 0600." A pause, one heartbeat, the whole future balanced on it. "Full crew briefing, galley, one hour. Attendance mandatory."

She cut the channel before her voice could shake, and turned back to Astrid Zhou.

"One hour," she said. "Wake your ghost. Wake the kids. Whatever we're going to be when the bureau comes for us—we'd better start being it now."
