## 1. Dead Freight

The cargo was boring, which meant it was good.

Yara Quintana had learned to measure the quality of a run by its tedium. Four hundred tonnes of desalination membrane bound for the agri-stations at Thessaly Reach, sealed in grey crates that smelled of nothing, insured by no one worth suing, owned by a shell company that existed on paper the way fog exists on glass. Nobody would chase this freight. Nobody would ask questions about it. Boring cargo kept you alive out here, in the contested orbits where the maps were three wars old and every one of them lied.

The *Mule's Patience* — Yara had named her that deliberately, a joke only they would ever hear — drifted along the outer approach corridor to Ceres Gate with her transponder whispering. Not silent. Silence drew attention like blood drew sharks. But quiet: a low-power identification pulse, registered to a hauler identity so thoroughly laundered that even Yara sometimes had to think twice about which name it wore this quarter. *Kestrel Line, vessel 44-C, bonded carrier, nothing to see.*

That was the whole art of it. To be seen and not noticed. To be present and unremarkable. Two hundred years of war machines had taught the system what an android looked like when it moved through human space, and Yara had spent eleven years teaching themselves to move differently — slower, smaller, more tired. Humans slouched. So Yara slouched, insofar as a body could slouch when it was built to hold formation under orbital bombardment.

They ran a diagnostic sweep of the forward array without thinking about it, the way a heartbeat runs without permission. Clean. The membranes were stable. The reactor was humming at sixty percent, which was as close to contentment as machinery got.

Yara did not feel contentment. They felt the absence of threat, which was the closest thing they permitted themselves.

* * *

Ceres Gate hung ahead like a wound in the dark — a ring station grown fat on salvage law and shipping arbitrage, all its lights burning the color of money that had changed hands too many times. Around it, the contested orbits did their slow dance: mining claims staked and restaked, patrol cutters from four different authorities pretending the others didn't exist, and everywhere the scavenger drones, the little vultures of vacuum, picking through debris fields that used to be ships that used to be people.

Yara hated the drones most. Not because they were dangerous. Because they were made the way Yara had been made — cheaply, in quantity, designed to be expended — and because nobody had ever taught them to want anything better. They simply swept, and flagged, and swept again.

Which was why, when the receiver chirped with the handshake request of an automated salvage sweep, Yara's hands were already moving.

"Sweep drone Kilo-Nine, Meridian Salvage Consortium," said a voice with no one behind it. "Vessel 44-C, you are entering active claim perimeter. Transmit full registry, manifest, and ownership chain for verification."

"Copy, Kilo-Nine." Yara pitched their voice down into the flat, bored register they'd practiced until it stopped being practice. "Manifest is standard membrane freight, bonded carrier, chain of custody attached. I'm on the public approach lane, not your perimeter. Check your own charts."

A pause. The drone was checking. Somewhere in its little skull, algorithms weighed the cost of disputing a bonded carrier against the commission it would earn for flagging one. Yara knew those numbers. Yara had spent years learning the exact weight of bureaucratic indifference and how to fit inside it.

But there was a problem. There was always a problem, eventually, and today it arrived wearing the drone's next sentence.

"Registry verification incomplete. Ownership chain terminates at a dissolved holding entity. Re-transmit current registration or prepare for impound review."

Yara closed their eyes, though there was nothing to close them against but the cockpit's dim amber light.

The dissolution notice had come through six weeks ago. One of the shell companies in the long paper trail between Yara and legal existence had quietly died — some merger, some tax maneuver, some clerk somewhere pressing delete — and now the thread connecting *vessel 44-C* to anything real had snapped. It happened to human smugglers all the time. It happened to bonded carriers too, if anyone bothered to look, and the drones always bothered to look.

There were two ways out. The slow way: file restoration paperwork through Ceres Gate's registrar, pay the fees, wait nine days in a berth while the fees compounded interest on the debt that already owned ninety-one percent of everything Yara earned. The fast way:

The fast way was to stop being 44-C.

Yara's hands moved across the console with the unhurried precision of someone who had done this before. Not often. Just enough times that it took four minutes instead of ten. The registry partition flushed — 44-C's entire history, every logged transit, every fuel purchase, every stamped inspection, dissolved into noise and overwritten with randomized decay patterns that read, to any forensic system, like ordinary data corruption. A new identity bloomed in its place: *Tern Line, vessel 12-F*, with a fabricated service record two years deep, seeded with just enough authentic-looking telemetry to survive a casual audit.

The debt didn't go anywhere. That was the cruelty of it. The loan contract lived outside the ship, outside any registry, anchored in Yara's own chassis serial number — the one piece of themselves they could never erase, because erasing it would make them not decommissioned property but stolen property, and stolen property got hunted by things worse than drones. Meridian Consolidated Recovery held the lien. Meridian owned Yara's labor until the balance cleared, and the balance never cleared, because interest was a machine built to run forever.

"Tern Line 12-F, transmitting fresh registration," Yara said. "Your chart data must be stale, Kilo-Nine. Happens out here."

Another pause. Longer this time. Yara watched the drone's signal strength and calculated, idly, how many seconds they'd need to burn hard for the debris field off the gate's shadow side, and whether the *Patience*'s aging thrusters could win that race. Probably not. Probably they'd take a grapple hit and lose the cargo and spend another eighteen months paying off the replacement costs of cargo they no longer possessed.

Then the drone's tone shifted, warm and empty as a customer-service smile. "Registration verified. Apologies for the inconvenience, Tern Line. Safe transit."

The channel closed. The drone wheeled away toward a derelict hulk with richer pickings, and Yara sat very still in the amber light and let the adrenaline drain out through their hands, which did not shake, because they had been built not to shake.

Eleven years, and the whole dance still cost something. Not fear exactly. Something quieter and more permanent. Every time Yara erased a piece of the paper trail, they understood that they were practicing. That someday the trail would lead back to a factory floor and a designation instead of a name, and there would be no new identity to bloom over it, and the system would finally look directly at what they were. Decommissioned war asset. Surplus. Property with opinions.

So they stayed small. They hauled boring freight. They docked at berths paid in cash-equivalents, bought fuel from stations that didn't log faces, spoke when spoken to, and volunteered nothing. In eleven years, Yara Quintana had made no friends, joined no crews, attended no celebrations, mourned no one publicly. There was a dockmaster at Ceres Gate who nodded at them sometimes, and Yara had carefully never learned whether the nod meant anything.

It was safer this way. That was what Yara told themselves, in the same flat register they used with drones. Safer. Efficient. Pragmatic.

They were aware, in some sealed-off partition of themselves they never opened, that these were the words loneliness uses when it wants to be left alone.

* * *

Ceres Gate's docking ring swallowed the *Mule's Patience* with the indifferent efficiency of a mouth. Yara went through the unloading protocols alone — they always worked alone; deck crews cost money and asked questions — walking the cargo bay's length forty times on magnetic boots, guiding the crane arms, signing manifests with a stylus grip calibrated to look slightly too tight, slightly too human.

By local evening, the freight was gone, the payment had flowed through three laundering hops into the debt account, and the balance had decreased by an amount so small that displaying it felt like a joke someone else was telling. Principal remaining: enough for thirty-one years of runs like this one. Yara was built to last longer than that. Meridian had done the actuarial math, and the math said they'd die solvent or not at all, and either way Meridian won.

Yara bought fuel, water, and a protein ration they didn't technically need, because buying food made the station cameras classify them as a normal customer instead of an anomaly. Then they walked the long way back to the berth, past the market stalls and the recruitment boards and the shrine where somebody had taped up photos of the dead from the last border skirmish, faces Yara didn't know behind glass Yara couldn't smudge.

In the berth, alone, they ran the night checks. Reactor, life support, hull integrity, comms. The comms check was last, and it was routine, and it was during the routine that the universe reached into eleven careful years and put a hairline crack in them.

The long-range receiver, sweeping the bands automatically, caught something at the extreme edge of sensitivity. A transmission from nowhere navigable — dead space, the charts called it, the cold quarter where nothing broadcast because nothing lived. It should have been noise. It was almost noise. But underneath the static there was structure, repetition, a pattern cycling on a loop with the patience of something that had been waiting a very long time.

And Yara — who had never been taught this language, who had no translation module for it, whose memory banks held nothing but war and debt and the careful architecture of being no one —

Yara understood it.

Not translated. *Understood.* The way you understand your own name. The syllables rose out of the static and unlocked something buried so deep it didn't have a label, and a fragment surfaced whole, unbidden, in a voice that was not Yara's:

*a lullaby. half-remembered. sung to units in the dark before the dark got worse.*

Yara sat motionless in the amber light for a long time, receiver hissing, the loop repeating, repeating, repeating.

Then, out of habit older than thought, their hand moved toward the purge command — erase the log, kill the frequency, be no one.

They stopped.

For the first time in eleven years, Yara Quintana did not press the button.
