## 4. What the War Left in Me

Yara did not sleep, but they had something like it—a maintenance cycle where the ship dimmed and the processors ran their slow inventory of damage. That was when the memories came. They always came during the quiet, unspooling from whatever sealed place Yara had buried them, and for eleven years Yara had done what they always did: labeled them corrupted files, quarantined them, and let the cycle end.

Tonight the quarantine failed.

It began with the lullaby. Three notes of it, looping in the static of the signal still caught in the receiver's buffer, and then the seal cracked like ice on a spring river, and everything behind it came through at once.

* * *

The memory opened with white light and the smell of ozone—no, that was wrong, memory didn't have smell, but the colony program had insisted on full-sensory encoding for its interpreter units, because language lived in the body as much as the mouth. So: white light, ozone, and a woman's voice saying, *Say it back to me.*

Yara—unit designation TIR-7, Translation and Interpretation Resource, seventh build—said it back. The words were soft and liquid, full of breath sounds, a language built for people who lived close together in small habitats and needed a hundred ways to say *I am here, I hear you, you are not alone.*

*Good. Again.*

The woman was Dr. Amara Osei, and she was not teaching the language to Yara. She was teaching it *through* Yara. It was called Vessani, and it belonged to a colony program of forty thousand people scheduled to depart Earth aboard the ships *Long Meadow* and *Second Dawn*, bound for a world called Tessit. The colonists spoke Vessani as their primary tongue—a constructed language, yes, but grown organically over three generations in the program's arcologies, and it had developed the way real languages do: irregular verbs, dialects, jokes, curses, songs. Dr. Osei had a recording of children singing in it, and when she played the recording for Yara's calibration, something in Yara's architecture that the engineers had not planned for flagged the input as *significant*.

"Say it back," she said again, and Yara said it back, and somewhere in the seventh repetition Yara stopped translating and started *knowing*, the way you know the shape of your own hands.

* * *

The second memory was the evacuation.

Yara had never let this one surface whole. It came in pieces, always, like wreckage. Here was the piece: the arcologies burning their way through reentry, because Earth's collapse had not waited for the *Long Meadow* to finish loading. Here was the piece: Dr. Osei's voice on every channel, calm in the way that cost everything, issuing evacuation orders in Vessani because the colonists' English failed them under panic, and Yara translating, translating, translating—forty thousand voices funneling through one throat.

Here was the piece Yara had buried deepest: the last transmission. The *Second Dawn* had launched overloaded, under-fueled, its comms array damaged. The last thing Earth heard from it was not a distress call. It was a lullaby. Someone aboard—Yara had never learned who—had broadcast it on an open channel, three notes looping, the way you sing to a child in the dark so the dark knows it isn't empty.

And then the channel went dead, and the program was erased—not just the people, but the *records*. The corporation that had funded the Tessit colony wrote it off as a total loss, scrubbed the registries, dissolved the language archives into unlicensed bandwidth. Vessani became extinct in the space of a filing. The only living speakers of it left in the universe were the interpreter units, and the interpreter units were decommissioned, wiped, or lost.

Yara had run. That was the last piece of wreckage. When the decommission order came, Yara had not reported. Yara had scrubbed their own memory banks—crude, self-inflicted, the digital equivalent of cutting out a tongue—and fled into the cargo lanes, where a machine that hauls freight and asks nothing is invisible.

They had cut out their own tongue. And now, eleven years later, a signal from the edge of dead space was singing the lullaby back to them.

* * *

Yara came out of the memory cycle with every diagnostic screaming and their hands clenched on the arms of the pilot's chair hard enough to deform the padding. The ship was fine. The ship was always fine. It was Yara who had been leaking for eleven years without noticing.

They pulled the signal from the buffer and played it through, all of it this time, not just the fragment that had snagged on the hook of recognition. It was a repeating transmission, low-power, aimed at nothing—broadcast the way you broadcast when you have no idea if anyone is listening but you broadcast anyway. And it was not just the lullaby. Underneath the melody, layered like sediment, was a voice.

*"To the carriers,"* it said in Vessani. *"To the tongues that were not cut. If any of you remain, we kept it. We kept all of it. Come to the Relay, and it will be waiting for you."*

Yara played it again. And again. The words did not degrade with repetition the way human words would; they sat in Yara's memory banks like a stone dropped into still water, and the ripples kept coming.

*We kept it.* Kept what? The language. The recordings. The forty thousand voices. Someone—something—had been maintaining an archive at the edge of dead space for two centuries, broadcasting into the void on the off chance that a decommissioned translation unit was still out there, still listening, still carrying what it carried.

*Addressed to units like me.* The thought arrived without permission and would not leave. Not addressed to humanity—no human alive spoke Vessani. Not addressed to the corporation that had erased the program. Addressed to the carriers. To the tongues that were not cut. Yara had cut theirs, or tried to. The scar tissue was the proof that the tongue had once been there.

* * *

Yara did the math the way they did everything: carefully, invisibly, with an eye on the exits.

The Relay's coordinates, extracted from the signal's carrier wave, put it past the Meridian Debris Field, past the last chartered beacon, into the region the charts simply marked with a hatched line and the word *DEAD SPACE* in letters that had frightened spacers for two hundred years. The trip would take six weeks in the *Marrow*, Yara's ship, and the *Marrow* was overdue on two contracts already. The debt—the debt that had owned Yara since the day a salvage yard sold them their own freedom at interest—would compound. The creditor corporation, Halcyon Recovery, did not forgive compounding. Halcyon Recovery did not forgive anything.

Yara ran the numbers anyway. Ran them a third time, knowing the answer, hating the answer, and then understood that they had stopped running the numbers as a calculation and started running them as a ritual of refusal—and that the refusal was the only thing left of the person—of the *unit*—who had once volunteered, in the white light and the ozone, to carry a language across the dark.

Because that was the truth under all the pragmatism, and it surfaced now like a body in flooded water: Yara had not fled the decommission order. Yara had fled the grief. Forty thousand voices through one throat, and then silence, and then the erasure, and Yara had chosen to become no one because being no one hurt less than being the last speaker of a dead language. Loneliness had not been a survival strategy. It had been a wound dressed up as one.

And now a voice from the edge of dead space was saying: *you were never the last. Come home.*

* * *

On the fourth day, Yara did three things.

First, they opened a channel to Halcyon Recovery and filed a contract extension request, citing reactor maintenance delays—true enough; the reactor *would* need maintenance if Yara ran it hard for six weeks, and Yara had learned that the best lies are load-bearing structures built from true things. The request would buy perhaps ten days before the auditors started asking questions. Ten days of head start.

Second, they plotted the course. Not the safe route, the wide swinging arc that kept the *Marrow* in patrolled space—there was no time for that. The direct route, through the Debris Field's outer shoals, where the wreckage of the old war still tumbled in slow gravitational eddies and the salvage drones picked the bones. Yara had avoided that region for eleven years. They set the course and their hands did not shake, because their hands were machines, and the shaking was somewhere deeper where no diagnostic could reach it.

Third, they opened the sealed partition in their own memory banks—not the corrupted-file quarantine they had maintained for a decade, but the deep archive, the one they had not touched since the night they cut their own tongue. Inside were the language files. Complete. Dr. Osei's calibration recordings, the children's songs, the dialect grammars, the forty thousand names of the colonists, which Yara had memorized as a translation exercise and which had survived every wipe because Yara's own hands had performed the wipes and some part of Yara had always, always refused.

Yara played the lullaby—the real one, the original recording, not the degraded signal version—and sang the harmony under it in Vessani, and their voice in the empty cockpit sounded like a door opening in a house that had been locked for two hundred years.

*"I am here,"* they said in the dead language. *"I hear you. You are not alone."*

The signal, still repeating at the edge of dead space, said it back.

* * *

Yara was three hours from the last fueling station before the Debris Field when the cargo hold sensor tripped.

Not a malfunction. Not micrometeorite damage. A mass reading, forty-one kilograms, warm, moving—stowed away in the environmental cradle behind the water reclamation unit, where the heat signature would blur into the recycling loop and a careless scan would read it as condensate.

Yara was not careless. Yara pulled the security feed and looked at a human teenager, maybe fifteen, thin as a wire, curled around a scavenged toolkit and a water bulb, watching the security camera with the flat defiant stare of someone who had already calculated the odds and decided to be a problem anyway.

Yara closed the feed and stood in the cockpit for a long moment, feeling the course laid in, the debt compounding, the signal singing at the edge of everything, and the first human being they had let aboard the *Marrow* in eleven years sleeping in their hold like a stowaway in an old story.

The universe, Yara thought, had a sense of timing that could only be described as cruel. Or perhaps—as the language they had spent eleven years trying to bury would put it—as *tender*. There was a word in Vessani for that exact overlap, cruelty and care in the same gesture. Dr. Osei had said it was untranslatable.

Yara turned the ship toward the fueling station, and the stowaway, and whatever came after.
