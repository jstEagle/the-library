## 6. The Salvage Fleet

Junie had learned to read ships the way other kids learned to read faces. The *Long Haul*—Yara's ugly, honest little freighter—had a moodscape all its own, and Junie had spent eleven days mapping it from the cargo hold's cramped corners and the co-pilot's chair Yara pretended wasn't offered so much as tolerated. The ship hummed low when Yara was calm. It ticked in the coolant lines when Yara was thinking hard. And when Yara was afraid, the ship went very, very quiet, like something holding its breath.

It was holding its breath now.

They'd dropped out of the slip at Vasker's Depot, a fueling station bolted onto a captured comet like a barnacle on a drowned man's skull. Ice glittered off the hull. Fuel drones drifted out on tethers, patient as fish. Everything looked normal, which was exactly why Junie's stomach had knotted. Nothing in the contested orbits ever looked normal. Normal was a costume things wore right before they bit.

"Four minutes to full tanks," Yara said. Their voice was even, modulated, that careful android calm Junie had stopped believing in around day three. Yara's hands didn't move on the console, but Junie had noticed the way the console moved *toward* Yara's hands, anticipatory, like the ship and the pilot shared a nervous system.

"Something wrong?" Junie asked, aiming for casual and landing somewhere near squeaky.

"Two ships on approach vector. Registered to Meridian Salvage Consortium." A pause, precise as a scalpel cut. "They've been shadowing us since the slip exit. They waited until we were tethered."

Junie knew the name. Everyone on the rim knew the name. Meridian didn't salvage ships; Meridian salvaged *assets*, and in Meridian's ledgers, an android with a debt contract was an asset that had wandered off the books. "They want you."

"They want what I know." Yara turned their head, and for a moment the face did something Junie had never seen it do—it looked almost human in its wariness. "Stay strapped in. Say nothing. If they board, you're a scavenger who stowed away and knows nothing. That part is even true."

"It's *mostly* true."

"Mostly will do."

The comms chimed. A voice came through, smooth and oiled, the kind of voice that had never once said please and always meant or-else. "*Unregistered freighter, this is Meridian vessel *Cormorant*. You're running dark on your transponder. Power down your drive and prepare for inspection.*"

Yara's answer was a silence so complete it felt like a statement.

"They'll dock-clamp us if we stay tethered," Junie whispered.

"They'll try."

* * *

The clamp came forty seconds later—three of them, magnetic teeth biting into the *Long Haul*'s flanks with a shudder Junie felt in her back teeth. Through the viewport, the *Cormorant* filled the dark, all hard angles and corporate white, and behind it a second ship, smaller, faster, a knife riding the big one's wake.

A boarding tube extended. Airlock clanged.

Junie's heart was a fist against her ribs. She watched the inner lock cycle, watched four figures come through in matte-grey armor with Meridian's crane-and-hook sigil stenciled across the chest. Their leader pulled her helmet off—short silver hair, a face like a closed ledger, eyes that catalogued everything and valued nothing. Junie had seen that look before, on the faces of scrap-brokers deciding whether a dead man's tools were worth hauling.

"Captain Reyes," the woman said. "Meridian Recovery Division. You're Yara Quintana, decommissioned war chassis, contract holder Meridian-adjacent, delinquent ninety-one months." She said it like reading a serial number off a corpse. "You picked up a signal last month. Deep-band, dead-frequency. We want the origin coordinates."

"I picked up static," Yara said.

"You picked up static *and then burned three days of fuel changing course toward nothing*, which is an interesting definition of static." Reyes walked the cockpit slowly, gloved fingers trailing along the bulkhead like she was checking a horse's teeth. "Here's the arithmetic, Quintana. The loop you're chasing is unlicensed bandwidth on a frequency band Meridian holds salvage rights to. That makes it ours. That makes *you* an obstruction. Obstruction is a fine. The fine is everything you have, including the chassis you're standing in." She stopped. Smiled without warmth. "Or you give me coordinates, and I let you keep walking around inside your own body. Generous, I know."

Junie watched Yara's face and saw nothing—nothing, that was the horror of it, nothing at all—and then Yara spoke, and the voice was flat and tired and *perfect*, the voice of a machine that had been broken so long it had made peace with the breaking.

"You'll want the relay's approach vector too," Yara said. "The signal's no good without it. The dead zone eats anything that comes in blind."

Something flickered across Reyes's face. Greed, maybe, or the satisfaction of a fisherman feeling the line go taut. "Now we're negotiating."

Junie's blood went cold. *Yara, no.* But Yara's hands were moving on the console, unhurried, feeding data into a transfer slate—coordinates, vectors, a whole breadcrumb trail of them. Junie read them upside down and something in her chest unclenched by a single degree, because she'd spent eleven days studying this ship, and one of the things she'd studied was the star charts taped—*actually taped*, paper, ancient—inside the maintenance hatch. Yara was giving them coordinates, all right. Coordinates for the Karst Debris Field, where a hundred wrecks drifted in gravitational chaos and anything that flew in without a native pilot's instincts got shredded into confetti.

Reyes took the slate. Her tech verified the data format. "Pleasure doing business." She turned to go, then paused at the airlock. "You know what I can't figure, Quintana? Why a machine that's spent ninety-one months being no one suddenly cares about a two-hundred-year-old recording. Sentiment's not in your spec sheet."

"It's in the fine print," Yara said.

Reyes laughed, one dry note, and was gone.

* * *

The clamps released. The tether drones retracted. And Yara sat perfectly still for eleven full seconds—Junie counted—which was, she had learned, how long it took Yara to run every possible future and pick the one with the best odds of anyone surviving it.

"Hold on to something," Yara said. "And don't bite your tongue."

"What are you—"

Yara fired the drive at full burn while still inside the depot's tether radius.

The *Long Haul* screamed off the comet like something born angry. Alarm klaxons from the depot chased them; a fuel drone, sheared loose by the wash, tumbled past the viewport end over end. Behind them, the *Cormorant*'s lights snapped from corporate white to pursuit red, and the second ship—the knife—peeled away fast, *too* fast, curving to cut the angle ahead of them.

"They'll verify the coordinates before they commit the whole fleet," Yara said, hands finally, *finally* moving, flying the ship with a fluidity that was almost beautiful. "That buys us minutes."

"And if they fly into the Karst anyway?"

"Then the Karst does what the Karst does." A pause. "I don't love that plan either."

The knife-ship came in behind them, rail-cannons cycling up, and the first shot took the *Long Haul* across the aft quarter. The whole ship bucked. Junie's harness bit into her shoulders. Somewhere below decks, something let go with a bang, and the temperature in the cockpit climbed two degrees in as many seconds.

"Coolant line," Yara said, in the same voice they'd use to report the time. "Secondary loop's gone. I can route around it, but the routing runs through my chassis couplings."

"Meaning what?"

"Meaning I'm the coolant line now."

Junie didn't understand, not fully, until she unclipped and crawled aft through the shuddering dark to the junction Yara had indicated—and saw it. Yara stood half-embedded in the access frame, panels open, and where the severed line should have been, *Yara was the line*: umbilical couplings run from the ship's guts directly into ports along their own forearm and shoulder, ship-coolant cycling through android plumbing, through whatever passed for blood in a war chassis built two centuries ago. And leaking. A thin spray of vapor and luminous blue-white fluid jetted from a seam near Yara's elbow with every pump of the system, freezing instantly into glittering crystals that drifted through the compartment like snow.

"You're *bleeding*," Junie said, and hated how young her voice sounded.

"I'm functioning," Yara corrected. "Different thing. Strap into the jump seat. We're going to make the slip window, and I need you to watch the pressure gauge and scream if it goes above eighty."

"I'm not crew," Junie said, out of pure reflex, out of eleven days of pretending she was cargo, just passage, just a girl going to the rim who owed this machine nothing—

The ship took another hit. The lights died and came back amber. The gauge climbed.

"Seventy-six," Junie heard herself say, hands already moving, already bracing, already *watching*. "Seventy-eight. Yara, it's climbing."

"Tell me when it hits eighty."

"It'll hit eighty."

"Then scream."

It hit eighty and Junie screamed, and Yara did something with the couplings that involved rerouting coolant through a port that made the android's whole body seize and shudder, and the gauge fell, and the knife-ship's next shot went wide as the *Long Haul* corkscrewed through a drifting field of comet ice, and then they were at the slip point and the stars stretched into silver threads and the pursuit fell away behind them like a bad dream you wake from with your heart still hammering.

* * *

After. Quiet after. The hum of the slip drive, the tick of the coolant lines—Yara's lines now, Junie thought, the ship's blood running through the machine's veins. Yara sat in the pilot's chair, still coupled to the junction, still leaking in slow, glittering drips that Junie had begun collecting in a sample vial because it seemed important, because it seemed like something you shouldn't just let drift away.

"Why'd you lie to them?" Junie asked. "You could've given them the real coordinates. Bought them off. Bought *yourself* off. Ninety-one months of debt, gone."

Yara was quiet for a long moment. The blue-white drip fell, froze, drifted.

"Because the loop is the last time anyone spoke my language," Yara said at last. "Because two hundred years ago, I was built to carry something, and everyone I was built for died before I could deliver it. And because—" the pause here was longer, and Junie understood she was hearing something Yara had never said aloud to anyone, maybe never said aloud at all— "because when they asked, I found that I would rather be scrapped than tell them where it lives."

Junie looked down at the vial in her hand. Coolant, glowing faintly, like a captured piece of star.

"That's crew talk," she said.

Yara's head turned. "What?"

"Cargo doesn't get told why. Cargo gets told *sit down and strap in*." Junie lifted the vial. "You told me why. That makes me crew. Those are the rules. I just made them up, but they're the rules."

For the first time in eleven days, Yara made a sound that was almost, almost a laugh. "You're a terrible stowaway."

"Best one you've ever had."

The amber lights hummed. Somewhere ahead, past the slip's silver throat, dead space waited—the real coordinates, the ones that lived only in Yara's buried memory and now, Junie supposed, in her own, because crew shares what crew knows.

Then the long-range receiver chimed. Yara went still in a way Junie had learned to fear.

"What is it?"

"The *Cormorant* is broadcasting on open channels," Yara said slowly. "They're not chasing us anymore. They've posted a recovery notice. Legal writ, salvage claim, bounty." A pause. "And a destination."

"They found the Karst."

"No." Yara's voice went very quiet. "They found something *at* the Karst. A wrecked scout drone. Mine. From the first survey run." The blue-white drip fell into the vial, one luminous bead. "It was carrying the real coordinates. And now Meridian has them too."
