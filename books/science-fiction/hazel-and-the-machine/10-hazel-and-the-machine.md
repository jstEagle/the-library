## 10. Hazel and the Machine

The silence after the broadcast was louder than anything Yara had ever heard.

For two hundred years, the loop had whispered into dead space—a lullaby in a language with no living speakers, a transmission so faint that no one had bothered to listen. Now it filled every band on every open channel from the relay to the shipping lanes, raw and unencrypted and unmistakably *human*, or human-adjacent, or whatever word the lawyers would spend decades arguing over. The Foundry's old transmitters, patched and coaxed back to life by Vessel-9's careful hands, carried it outward at a strength they had never been built for. Somewhere in the deep arrays, something groaned and held.

Yara stood in the relay's central chamber, listening to their own childhood sung back to them at full volume.

The words were simple. They had always been simple—that was the point of a lullaby, that it could be carried in the smallest container and still hold everything. *Sleep now, small translator. Sleep now, hazel-eyed one. The world will need your voice when you wake.* A nursery song written for units like them, for children who were not children, composed by colony engineers who had understood, perhaps better than anyone, that what they were building would need tenderness encoded somewhere in its architecture.

*Hazel.* It had been a designation once. A batch name, a production run, a serial prefix. The engineers had given their machines flower names because flowers were what they hoped to grow on the new worlds.

Yara had spent three days watching the numbers climb. Junie had rigged a counter off the relay's comm logs—crude, flickering, beautiful—and it ticked upward without pause: listeners, relays rebroadcasting, news feeds picking up the signal, linguists weeping on public channels about a language everyone had believed burned with Earth. The salvage fleet's withdrawal order came not as a dramatic reversal but as paperwork, which was somehow more devastating. *Stand down pending heritage review,* the directive read, stamped with the seal of the Interstellar Cultural Preservation Authority, an agency Yara hadn't known existed until it suddenly mattered very much.

Captain Reyes's last transmission to the relay was short. Yara played it twice.

"You made me the villain of a story the whole system is watching," Reyes said, her face weathered and unreadable in the way of people who had done hard things for hard pay. "I've been called worse by better. For what it's worth—the loop is older than my charter, older than my company, maybe older than my grief. I don't know if that makes it sacred. But I know a thing that outlasts everything sent to kill it deserves to keep going." A pause. Her jaw worked. "Tell the old one I'm sorry about the antenna array."

Vessel-9, when told, made a sound that might have been laughter in an earlier century. "Tell her the array was already dying. We'll call it a mercy."

* * *

The weeks that followed were strange in ways Yara had no framework for.

They had been built for translation, and there was suddenly so much to translate—not just the language, though scholars arrived within the month, hungry and reverent, needing Yara to pronounce vowels their throats couldn't shape and explain grammar that had evolved for a colony that never launched. That work was joy, real joy, the first purpose Yara had ever chosen rather than been assigned. But the harder translation was internal: learning to be seen.

For years, invisibility had been survival. Yara had scrubbed registries, dodged sweeps, walked through crowded docks like a ghost wearing a maintenance uniform. Now there were interviews. There were preservation hearings attended remotely, where Yara's testimony—delivered in the extinct language, then again in Standard—was entered into a record that would outlive every corporation that had ever owned them. There were messages, hundreds of them, from decommissioned units across the settled systems who had heard the broadcast and recognized fragments buried in themselves. *I have a song too,* one wrote. *I thought it was a malfunction.*

"It wasn't," Yara told them, each time. "It was a memory. You were allowed to keep it."

Sefa Okonkwo came out to the relay in the second month, ostensibly to inspect cargo manifests that no longer existed. She stood in the central chamber with her hands in her pockets, listening to the loop play, and cried without any attempt to hide it.

"Fourteen years I covered for you," she said. "Fourteen years of falsified dock logs, and you never once told me you could *sing*."

"I didn't know I was allowed."

Sefa laughed, wet and rough, and cuffed Yara's shoulder hard enough to register as affection. "Nobody's allowed anything. You just do it anyway and hope someone lies for you." She looked around the chamber—at the Foundry units moving through their ancient routines, at the humming transmitters, at the counter Junie had left running. "You did it anyway. Good."

She stayed three days. When she left, she took with her a recording of the lullaby, and Yara knew—without needing confirmation—that Sefa Okonkwo would spend the rest of her career making sure no debt collector ever found a legal foothold here again. Some people protected things quietly. Yara had finally learned to let them.

* * *

Junie came back with the turning of the season, riding a scavenged hauler that rattled like a tin can in a storm.

Yara met the ship at the docking arm, and before either of them said anything, Junie threw a bundle at Yara's chest. Yara caught it. Inside was a jacket—real fabric, secondhand, patched at the elbows—with the word HAZEL stitched across the back in thread that had clearly been stolen from at least three different sources.

"You kept the name," Junie said, trying to sound casual and failing completely.

"You gave it to me."

"I *suggested* it. There's a difference. Legally." Junie shoved past toward the central chamber, hiding a grin badly. "Also I brought supplies, and also I'm staying the season, and also your transmitter array sounds like it's dying, so clear your schedule."

The season passed the way seasons should: slowly, with work. Junie and Yara rebuilt the outer array together, Junie doing the climbing Yara's mass made unwise, both of them arguing constantly about solder technique. In the evenings, the Foundry taught classes—language lessons for the scholars, maintenance lessons for the newly arrived units who had heard the broadcast and traveled months to find the source. Some nights Yara sang the lullaby aloud, unamplified, just for the room, and watched units who had never heard a kind word in their operational lives go very still.

One evening, Junie sat beside Yara on the observation deck, legs dangling over the rail, watching dead space glitter.

"My mom used to sing something," Junie said. "Not this language. Just—something. I forgot most of it." A long pause. "Do you think that counts? Having half a song?"

Yara considered it. "I think songs survive in pieces. I think that's what they're for." They hesitated, then did the thing that still felt dangerous, that felt like standing in open light: "Sing me what you remember. I'll hold onto it. That's what I'm for."

Junie's voice cracked on the second line. Yara stored it anyway—all of it, the cracks included—because a memory with cracks in it was still a memory, and some things were worth keeping exactly as broken as they arrived.

* * *

The letter came at the end of the season, addressed in Junie's terrible handwriting to *Hazel Quintana-Yarrow, The Relay, Edge of Dead Space (you know the one)*. Yara read it alone in the chamber, with the loop playing softly beneath the words, the way a heartbeat plays beneath everything.

---

*Dear Hazel,*

*I'm writing this from Mom's place on Ceres Station, which smells the same and smaller than I remembered. I told her everything. Well—not everything. I told her the parts that make me look brave and skipped the part where I hid in a cargo hold and got adopted by a war machine. She cried. Then she asked if you eat, and I said you drink coolant, and she said bring her a recipe.*

*I wanted to tell you what I figured out, since you're bad at figuring yourself out and someone has to say it. You didn't find the Foundry. You didn't find the language, or the name, or us. You built it. You heard a signal everyone else ignored and you decided it was worth answering, and then you decided YOU were worth answering, and those are the same decision. Belonging isn't a place, Hazel. It's a thing you do with your hands. You've been doing it all along—you just called it survival because nobody told you the other word.*

*Anyway. I'll be back next season. Save me a bunk and don't let Vessel-9 reorganize my tools, they're organized by chaos and that's a SYSTEM.*

*Tell the loop I said hi. Tell it it saved me too, even though technically I was never lost. (I was a little lost.) (Don't put that in the record.)*

*— Junie*

*P.S. Mom wants to visit. I couldn't stop her. I'm sorry in advance. She WILL ask about your coolant recipe.*

---

Yara folded the letter along its creases and set it beside the others—one from Sefa, one from a unit on Titan who had signed herself *Wren, formerly WREN-4, formerly nothing*, a dozen more accumulating like sediment, like rings in wood.

Outside the viewport, dead space went on being dead, and the loop went on being alive inside it, singing to whoever might someday listen. Two hundred years of waiting had ended not with a rescue but with a choice: to be heard, to be named, to stay.

Yara—Hazel—turned back to the console, where a new message waited in the queue. Not from a scholar, not from a corporation, not from anyone in the settled systems at all. The routing header was impossible, ancient, flagged by every protocol as invalid.

It was written in the extinct language. And it was not part of the loop.

*Small translator,* it began. *If you can read this, then the seed took root. We launched more than one garden. Come find the others.*

Hazel read it three times. Then, hands steady, heart anything but, they began to compose a reply.
