## 3. Passengers of the Impossible

The Calloway's Wake had been docked at Meridian for eleven hours before anyone asked me to look at it, and I want it on the record that I went up on my own initiative. Nobody assigned me. Nobody ever assigns me anything; I am the kind of person who assigns herself, quietly, the way you'd assign yourself to water a neighbor's plants while they're away—useful, expected, invisible.

I told myself I was going aboard to verify life-support draw against the station's power budget. That was even true, in the way a key is true while being much smaller than the door it opens.

* * *

Sef Okafor's testimony, recorded in Meridian Beacon guest quarters, day two:

"You want to know what it was like coming back? It was like this. My mother used to keep a jar of buttons on the shelf, and every so often she'd restring them onto new thread, and they were all the same buttons but the necklace came out different lengths. That's my memory of the voyage home. Same buttons. Wrong string.

"I remember nine years out. I remember the taste of the coffee in the galley—bad, burnt, ours—and I remember Kesh complaining about it every single morning like a prayer. I remember the long dark between jumps, and the card games, and Dara Voss teaching me to whistle through my hands because I never learned as a kid.

"And then we made our final approach here, and I caught my reflection in the airlock glass, and I thought: who let a boy aboard?

"Because that's what I saw. A kid. Twenty-four, maybe twenty-five years old. And Sef Okafor is thirty-eight years old, or he was when we left, and I have the gray coming in at the temples to prove it. Had. Had the gray."

* * *

I boarded through the cargo ring, which was the polite way in, and found the ship in a state I can only describe as *held breath*. Freighters come into Meridian tired—crews sprawled, decks sticky, someone always laughing too loud somewhere. The Wake was silent. Not empty-silent. Watched-silent. Every person I passed tracked me with their eyes and then looked away fast, the way you look away from something you're afraid will notice you looking.

The captain met me at the central spine. Captain Imke Rell, per the manifest, though the woman in front of me looked like she should be running a school field trip, not a nine-year deep run. She was maybe thirty. Her eyes were fifty.

"You're from the beacon," she said.

"I'm Ada Almeida. Station operations." I held up my scanner case like a hunting license. "Routine verification. Power draw, atmosphere, that sort of thing."

"There's nothing routine about us," Captain Rell said, "and I think you know it."

I did. Of course I did. But there's an art to being nobody, and part of the art is not confirming things. "Your crew complement reads ten," I said. "Your service records read ten. Your biometric intake scans—" I paused, because this was the part where I could still turn around. "Your biometrics don't match your records by a margin of roughly two decades. Younger. All of you."

Nobody gasped. They'd had time to gasp already, privately, in shifts.

"How do you feel?" I asked, because it seemed like the question underneath all the other questions.

Captain Rell laughed—a short, cracked sound. "How do I feel. I feel like I left my knees somewhere between jump points and nobody will tell me where the lost-and-found is." She rubbed her face with both hands, and I watched her hands shake. "We're healthy. That's the obscene part. We're healthier than we've ever been. My old shoulder injury is gone. Kesh's asthma is gone. Whatever happened to us, it didn't hurt us. It just... unwrote us. Backward."

* * *

Sef Okafor's testimony, continued:

"The doctor—ship's medic, Priya Chandra—ran our bloodwork four times. Four. She kept saying the same thing: 'These are the cells of people who haven't lived yet.' Telomeres like newborns'. Scar tissue resolved. Priya has a scar from a reactor burn she got six years ago, and it's gone, and she keeps touching the place where it was, over and over, like checking a pocket for keys that aren't there anymore.

"But here's the thing I need you to write down, Ms. Almeida, because everyone else looks at me funny when I say it.

"We were eleven."

* * *

That word—eleven—was the first crack in the hull of the whole affair, and I heard it in the mess hall, sitting across from Sef Okafor over cups of the legendary bad coffee, which was indeed bad, and which I drank anyway out of solidarity.

"Eleven," I repeated.

"Eleven," he said. He was younger than me by a decade now, technically, but he had the oldest eyes I'd seen since my grandmother's. "The manifest says ten. The records say ten. But I set eleven places at the table every night for nine years, because that's how many of us there were, and if you tell me I hallucinated one place setting for nine years, then I'm not somebody you want flying your freight."

"Who's the eleventh?"

And here is the thing I will remember until I die, however long that turns out to be: his face *reached* for the answer. You could see it happen—the name rising in him like a bubble through oil, getting slower and slower as it rose, and then, just below the surface, popping. Gone.

He sat there with his mouth open. Then he pressed both fists against his temples and said, very quietly, "There was a bunk. There was a bunk between mine and Dara's, and it had a blanket on it, a green one, with a repair patch shaped like a fish, because whoever owned it couldn't sew and didn't care who knew. And I can see that blanket right now, clearer than I can see your face. But I cannot tell you whose bunk it was. I can't even tell you if it was a man or a woman. It's like..." He searched. "It's like a word on the tip of your tongue, except the tongue too."

"The unmanifested," said a voice from the doorway. Dara Voss—whistling-through-the-hands Dara, also impossibly young, also impossibly old-eyed. "That's what we call them. Them, plural. Because Sef's not the only one. Ask Priya about the extra med kit. Ask Kesh about the fifth chair in the card game. We all brought somebody home who doesn't exist."

* * *

I spent that evening doing what I do best, which is reading things nobody asked me to read.

The Wake's logs were beautiful. That was my first clue. Real logs are messy—they stutter, they contradict themselves, they contain forty entries about a clogged recycler and none about the actual crisis. These logs flowed. Nine years of voyage data with the smooth, confident cadence of a story told so many times the teller believes it. Gaps had been filled. Edges sanded. Where a real log would say *anomaly, investigating*, these logs said nothing at all and moved along whistling.

Someone hadn't deleted the truth. Deletion leaves scars—I've spent enough lonely nights auditing my own station's archives to know what a scarred file looks like. This was different. This was *authorship*. Someone had gone through nine years of records and written a better version, entry by entry, timestamp by timestamp, with the patience of a monk and the access of—

I stopped.

I ran the metadata twice. Then a third time, standing up, walking away from the console, making tea I didn't drink, and coming back to check whether the tea-drinking had changed anything. (It hadn't. Data doesn't respect ritual. I respect ritual anyway.)

The rewrite tools carried signatures. Old ones, buried deep, the digital equivalent of fingerprints inside gloves—but I know glove-fingerprints when I see them, because I have spent nine years maintaining systems built by hands I've never seen, and maintenance teaches you a maker's habits the way marriage teaches you a spouse's moods.

The toolchain that rewrote the Calloway's Wake was built on Meridian Beacon architecture.

Not *run through* the beacon. Built *on* it. Rooted in code that predates my assignment, that predates, as far as any record shows, the beacon itself. Whoever had done this had privileges I don't have, on a station I supposedly run alone.

I stood in my quiet server room, in my quiet station, in my quiet life, and felt the floor tilt by exactly one degree—not enough to fall, just enough to make everything lean slightly wrong forever after.

Because here is what I knew, holding that metadata like a hot coal: the impossible thing wasn't the young crew. The impossible thing was that someone with beacon-level access had scrubbed their voyage before the Wake ever reached me—which meant someone had known, months ago, what would happen out there in the dark. Known, and prepared the erasure in advance, the way you'd prepare a grave before the funeral. Before the death, even.

* * *

Sef Okafor's testimony, final fragment, day two, 0300 hours. He knocked on my workroom door at 0300 hours, which tells you everything about how well either of us was sleeping:

"I remembered something else. Just now. Woke up with it sitting on my chest like a cat.

"It was near the end. We answered a distress call—I remember the shape of it, the protocol chime, off-register, like a bell with a chip in it. And I remember Dara saying 'we're going, obviously,' because that's what you do, you answer. And I remember... going. Going across, or toward, or down—somewhere. And I remember coming back and counting heads and getting ten and thinking that was right, thinking ten was right, and it isn't, it was never right, it was eleven, we were eleven—

"Ms. Almeida. Ada. Can I ask you something strange?"

"You already have," I said. "Several. Go ahead."

"When you look at your own records—you're the keeper here, you must have personnel files, history, the usual paper trail—does it all fit? Does it all *match*?"

And I opened my mouth to say yes, of course, my files are immaculate, and found that I couldn't. Because they are immaculate. Too immaculate. Perfect credentials, seamless history, not one scar in the record anywhere—and I had just spent the evening learning what perfection means. Perfection means authorship. Perfection means someone wrote you.

"I'll check," I said.

He nodded slowly, like a man watching a second ship sink. "That's what I thought," he said. "You've got the same look we have. Like your own reflection owes you an explanation."

* * *

Dread, I have decided, is not a dramatic thing. It doesn't announce itself with klaxons. Dread is the slow discovery that the floor of your house has been hollow for years and you've been walking on it lightly without knowing why—some animal part of you always taking small steps, always keeping to the edges.

I walked my rounds that night with dread taking root in me like a seed finding soil. The Wake rode its clamps above me, full of young-old ghosts. Somewhere in my own walls sat code older than my own history, patient as a spider. And eleven—or more—people had been unwritten so thoroughly that even grief couldn't find them.

I stood at the main viewport, looking out at the coordinates where the Wake swore something had happened, and there was nothing there. Nothing at all.

Which, I was beginning to understand, was exactly the point.
