## 5. A Woman Without a File

The courier pinnace arrived on a Tuesday, which Ada found offensive on principle. Nothing important ever happened at Meridian on a Tuesday. Tuesdays were for coolant flushes and the slow, meditative descaling of the primary antenna's thermal shrouds—work that asked nothing of her except patience and a steady hand, and gave nothing back except the small, clean satisfaction of a job that would hold.

She was elbow-deep in shroud housing when the docking klaxon chirped its polite little chirp—the one reserved for scheduled traffic, which meant someone had filed paperwork, which meant trouble. Unscheduled arrivals knocked. Scheduled ones brought lawyers.

"Meridian Beacon, this is Halcyon Route Authority vessel *Ledger's Grace*, requesting berth for one passenger, corporate audit division." The pilot's voice was young and bored. "Clearance code follows."

Ada wiped her hands on a rag that had seen more years than most of her tools and watched the codes scroll across her console. They were immaculate. Perfectly formatted, perfectly authorized, stamped by three different departments whose signatures cross-verified down to the checksum. She'd spent twenty-two years at Meridian learning what authentic paperwork looked like, and this looked like a portrait of paperwork painted by someone who had only ever had it described to them.

Which, she reflected, unsealing her maintenance suit, was not the same as saying it was fake. Sometimes the most dangerous documents were the ones that were real all the way through.

She keyed the channel. "*Ledger's Grace*, you're cleared for berth two. Mind the approach corridor; I've got a thermal plume off the starboard shroud that bites."

"Acknowledged, Beacon. Passenger requests your presence at arrival."

"Passenger can find me," Ada said, and cut the channel, and then stood there for a moment feeling the old familiar itch between her shoulder blades—the one that said *be somewhere else when they arrive*. It was a good instinct. It had kept her invisible for two decades. But she had a freighter full of impossible passengers docked at her own station, and an auditor from the corporation that owned both the freighter and the route it had come home wrong on, and if Ada had learned anything in nine days of quiet investigation, it was that the story was no longer going to come to her politely.

She put the kettle on instead. If you couldn't be elsewhere, be armed with tea.

* * *

Ilsa Vane came down the boarding ramp like weather. Not storm weather—nothing so honest. She came the way high pressure comes, all pleasant blue sky and the faint sense that something somewhere was about to be crushed by it.

She was perhaps fifty, silver at the temples in a way that was either natural or very expensive, wearing a travel suit tailored close enough to count as a legal document. Her smile arrived a half-second before the rest of her face did.

"Ms. Almeida." Her voice was warm as fresh bread. "Ilsa Vane, Route Authority compliance. Thank you for receiving me on such short notice."

"You filed eleven days ago," Ada said. "That's not short notice, that's a slow boat."

Something flickered behind Vane's eyes—amusement, maybe, or the recalibration of a machine encountering unexpected resistance—and her smile deepened by exactly one degree. "Even better. A keeper who reads her own queue. You'd be astonished how many don't."

"I'd be astonished how many have a queue worth reading." Ada handed her a mug of tea without being asked. It was a test dressed as hospitality: take the mug and you're a guest; refuse it and you're an inspector. Vane took it, sipped, and said, "Lovely. Assam?"

"Whatever the supply manifest says it is this quarter."

"*Real* Assam," Vane said, and there was something almost wistful in it. "You know, in thirty years of audits, this is the first beacon that's offered me tea before offering me an alibi."

"You haven't asked for an alibi yet."

"Oh, I will." Vane turned a slow circle in the operations gallery, taking in the consoles, the hand-labeled cable runs, the wall where Ada kept her maintenance logs in actual ink because ink could not be remotely edited. "Beautiful station, Meridian. Old architecture. Pre-consolidation, isn't it? Nobody builds relay cores like this anymore—they don't know *how*."

"They don't know why," Ada said. "The how is in the manuals."

"Mm." Vane set down her mug. "Shall we do the part where I ask to see your credentials and you pretend to be surprised?"

"We could skip it," Ada said. "Save us both the tea."

Vane laughed—a real laugh, brief and unguarded, gone as fast as it came. "God, I hope you're as good as you look, Almeida. It's been such a dull year."

* * *

What followed was, on the surface, the most boring hour of Ada's professional life. Vane sat at the guest terminal with her fingers dancing and pulled thread after thread out of Ada's file while Ada sat opposite, hands folded, watching her do it.

Employment history: verified. Twenty-two years, four months, eleven days at Meridian Beacon, transferred in from Ceres Relay with exemplary references. Certification stack: verified. Every license current, every recertification logged on schedule, every continuing-education credit precisely meeting requirements without ever exceeding them. Medical history: verified. Dental records, genomic baseline, psychological evaluations—all present, all consistent, all signed.

Vane worked through it with the unhurried pleasure of a sommelier, narrating as she went. "Ceres, 2241 through 2244. Your supervisor there writes—let me quote—*'the most reliably competent technician I have supervised in nineteen years of supervision.'* Lovely sentence. Do you know what I notice about it?"

"That he never says anything specific."

"He never says anything *specific*," Vane agreed warmly. "No anecdotes. No incidents. No 'she once talked me out of venting deck three during a fire.' Just reliability, in the abstract, forever." She scrolled. "And here's your transfer order. Approved in nine days, which is fast but not suspiciously fast. And here—" a small flourish, "—is the interesting part."

Ada already knew what the interesting part was. She'd known for years, in the way you know a room has one more door than it shows you. But knowing a thing privately and hearing it said aloud in a pleasant voice over tea were different animals entirely.

"Your pre-Ceres record," Vane said, "is flawless."

"Thank you."

"It's *flawless*, Ms. Almeida. Do you understand the distinction? Real lives are not flawless. Real files have gaps—a year working dockside under an uncle's informal arrangement, a certification lapsed because of a divorce, a medical deferral, a name change, a bankruptcy, *something*. Yours has none. Every date dovetails. Every reference corroborates every other reference. Your entire documented existence fits together like—" she paused, savoring it, "—like a forgery. Or like a gift. Someone built you this file, top to bottom, and they built it *well*. Better than well. They built it the way I build them."

The words landed in the quiet of the ops gallery and sat there.

Ada kept her face still. It was not difficult; she'd had decades of practice at stillness. But underneath the stillness, something cold and bright opened its eyes.

Because Vane was right. Ada knew the technique—not from the outside, from the inside. She knew how you built a person out of paper: start with a real skeleton, borrow verifiable bones, let a few mundane flaws show through so the whole thing breathed. She knew the rhythm of it, the craft of it, the way you left yourself one seam only you could find. She had never done it to herself—or she believed she hadn't—but she recognized the work the way a carpenter recognizes another carpenter's joinery across a crowded room.

Someone had made her. Or remade her. And the file sitting in Halcyon's archives, so perfect it sang, was either her history or her cage, and she genuinely could not tell which.

"Say something," Vane prompted gently. "People usually say something. 'There's been a mistake.' 'My records are my own.' Occasionally someone cries."

"What do you say?" Ada asked. "When you pull a file apart and find it's been built?"

Vane regarded her for a long moment, and the charm banked itself down to coals, and what was underneath was older and much more tired. "I say: whoever made this was very good, and I wonder what they were hiding, and whether they're still alive." She closed the terminal window with a soft click. "But that's my problem. You have a different problem."

* * *

"The Wake," Vane said, refilling both mugs herself this time—an auditor pouring tea for a beacon keeper, which was either courtesy or a message about who intended to control the tempo of this conversation. "Nine-year run. Comes home with a crew biologically younger than their departure physicals. Reactor logs corrupted. Manifest inconsistent with life support draw by—you found this, didn't you, I read your preliminary report—by a factor suggesting eleven additional persons."

"Eleven additional *draws*," Ada corrected. "Draws aren't persons."

"No," Vane agreed. "They're usually not." She handed Ada her mug. "Here's what happens next, officially. The Wake is classified as an insurance anomaly. Navigational hazard, category four. The crew is compensated generously, sign binding nondisclosures, and rotated home through three separate transfers so no single port sees more than a few of them. The ship is impounded pending structural review. Everyone goes home. Everyone forgets. It's the kindest possible outcome, Ms. Almeida, I want you to believe that. I've seen the alternative outcomes. This is the kind one."

"And my report?"

"Yours is the linchpin." Vane leaned forward, and her voice dropped into a register of genuine intimacy, the tone of a woman letting you in on something. "As investigating officer of record, your findings become the anchor document. Everything else hangs off your signature. So." She slid a slate across the table. On it: a promotion order, drafted, awaiting only Ada's acceptance. Senior Inspector, Route Authority, field commission, a salary that would have bought Meridian Beacon twice over, a posting anywhere in the inner system she liked. "You write that the temporal readings are instrument error. Sensor drift, aging hardware, a beacon this old practically manufactures anomalies. You note the crew's condition as 'unresolved, pending medical review,' and recommend the standard compensation package. You sign. And Monday morning you are the youngest senior inspector in Authority history, with a staff, a budget, and a life that no longer smells like coolant."

Ada looked at the slate for a long time.

It was a beautiful offer. That was the thing nobody understood about corruption—it was almost never ugly. It was tailored. Whoever had drafted this offer knew exactly what shape of key would fit her lock: competence rewarded, solitude dignified, a bigger and emptier room to be invisible in. Somewhere in Halcyon there was a profile of her, built as carefully as her own file had been built, and it had *worked*, and that frightened her more than the demolition charges she suspected were already being loaded somewhere.

She thought about Sef Okafor's face when he'd described his crewmate's cabin, sealed from the inside. She thought about the child's drawing taped to the bulkhead—two suns and a long thin ship and eleven stick figures holding hands, one of them drawn smaller than the rest, in crayon that had faded unevenly, the way crayon does when it has crossed nine years and something else besides.

"Can I ask you a question?" Ada said.

"You can ask me anything. I may not answer."

"When you pulled my file apart tonight. When you saw the seams." Ada met her eyes. "Did you write it down?"

Vane went very still. It was the first completely honest moment she'd shown, and Ada understood suddenly that this woman's honesty was the most dangerous thing aboard the entire station, including the reactor.

"No," Vane said quietly. "Not yet."

"Then here's my answer." Ada pushed the slate back across the table, gently, the way you return something borrowed. "I'm going to finish my investigation. All of it. And then I'm going to write a report that says whatever the evidence says. If the evidence says sensor drift, I'll write sensor drift. If it says eleven people who don't exist—" she shrugged, "—then I'll write that too, and we'll both find out what my signature is worth."

Vane studied her the way she'd studied the forged file: looking for the seam. Whatever she found—or didn't—made her smile again, and this time the smile reached all the way up and looked, briefly, almost like respect.

"You know what the tragedy of you is, Almeida?" she said, standing, collecting her coat. "Whoever built your file built it to survive exactly this. Every audit, every background check, every auditor like me. It's bulletproof. It will hold against anything the Authority can throw at it." She paused at the gallery door, silhouetted against the corridor light. "The only thing it won't survive is *you* deciding to look behind it. And I have the terrible suspicion you're going to."

She left. The airlock cycled. The kettle ticked as it cooled.

Ada sat alone in the ops gallery for a long while, turning the empty mug slowly in her hands, listening to the station breathe around her—the hum she'd tended for twenty-two years, the hum that had been humming, she now suspected, since before she existed in any form the paperwork would admit to.

*Authored,* Vane had called her file. Not accumulated. Authored.

Fine, Ada thought. Two could author.

She got up, crossed to the console, and pulled the one thing she'd been circling for nine days without quite touching: the beacon's original survey charts, the ones predating its supposed construction date, the coordinates everyone had stopped asking about. If her past had been written by someone else, then the fastest way to find the author was to follow the ink.

The charts bloomed across her screen. The anomalous coordinates sat dead center, patient as a wound.

"Right," Ada said softly, to the station, to herself, to whoever had built them both. "Let's see what you were watching."

Deep beneath the ops gallery, in a core nobody had opened in forty years, something answered—a single status light waking amber, like an eye deciding whether to open.
