## 8. Terms of Settlement

Ilsa Vane had a ritual for reports like this one, and the ritual began with tea.

Not because she was sentimental—she had excised sentiment from her professional life the way a surgeon excises a tumor, cleanly, with gratitude—but because the act of preparing tea gave her eleven minutes of enforced stillness, and eleven minutes was exactly long enough for the mind to settle into the register the work required. Too fast, and the language came out sharp, accusatory, human. Too slow, and it came out soft, apologetic, human. Eleven minutes produced the correct temperature: a prose that was neither kind nor cruel, merely *accurate*, which was the only mercy the universe actually offered.

She measured the leaves. She waited. She watched the Meridian Beacon's guest quarters perform their small pantomime of hospitality around her—the lamp that dimmed on a schedule, the window that showed a rendered starscape because an actual window this far out on the relay's spine would have been structurally irresponsible. Someone had maintained this place meticulously for years. Someone had dusted the vents and calibrated the gravity plating and replaced the tea itself, which was good tea, better than Halcyon Route Authority issued, which meant someone here had opinions about tea, which meant someone here was a person.

Vane noted the observation and set it aside. Persons were data. All data was manageable.

At minute eleven she sat down at the desk, opened a fresh document, and addressed the cursor the way she addressed all her correspondents: with respect, and without warmth.

*TO: Halcyon Route Authority, Office of Continuity, Priority Channel*
*FROM: Ilsa Vane, Senior Auditor, Field Division*
*RE: Calloway's Wake (Registry HRA-4471), Meridian Beacon, Incident Classification*

She paused over the word *Incident*. Considered *Anomaly*. Considered *Event*. The corporation's own style guide preferred *Anomaly* because it implied a deviation from a norm rather than a rupture in it, and Vane had learned long ago that the vocabulary of a report was ninety percent of its outcome. Call a thing an anomaly and it gets studied. Call it an incident and it gets litigated. Call it a *tragedy* and it gets memorialized, and memorialization was the one outcome that could never be permitted, because memorials had a way of growing names, and names had a way of growing questions, and questions had a way of growing into subpoenas.

She typed *Anomaly* and moved on.

*Summary: The freighter Calloway's Wake, nine years outbound on the Halcyon Deep Route survey contract, has returned to Meridian Beacon unscheduled. Crew biometrics are inconsistent with service records by a margin of approximately two to four decades in the younger direction. Manifest discrepancies are present but contained. Logs have been reconciled. The crew is disoriented but cooperative. The situation is stable.*

Every word of that paragraph was true. Vane took a small, private satisfaction in this. It was the satisfaction of a mason looking at a wall: each stone placed honestly, the whole structure bearing weight that no individual stone had to acknowledge. *Logs have been reconciled.* True. She had reconciled them herself, remotely, through the audit channel, in the four hours since her shuttle docked—overwriting the raw telemetry with the smoothed version, the one in which the Wake's temporal displacement read as a sensor calibration error rather than a passage through something that should not have been passable. *The crew is disoriented but cooperative.* Also true, in the sense that disoriented people, given warmth, food, and a gentle authoritative voice, will cooperate with nearly anything. *The situation is stable.*

Stable. She sipped her tea and let the word sit in her mouth like a stone fruit with a pit in it.

The pit was this: eleven crew members who did not exist.

Not *were dead*—Vane had processed deaths before, and death was administratively trivial, a form and a flag and a benefits disbursement. These eleven were worse than dead. They were *unrecorded*. No service history, no biometric baselines, no emergency contacts, no next of kin to grieve them, which meant no next of kin to *ask questions*. They existed now only as a child's crayon drawing taped to a bulkhead, a sealed cabin that smelled faintly of someone's soap, and the accumulating, unspooling grief of a crew that remembered people the paperwork insisted had never been aboard.

Vane had read the crew psych evaluations that morning. Sef Okafor, the engineer, had written—actually written, in the margin of a standard form, in handwriting that pressed hard enough to dent three pages beneath—*You can't unmake my sister. I carried her. I remember her hands.* And beneath it, in the same furious script: *What did you do with her hands?*

Vane had read that line twice. Then she had closed the file, and her hands had been perfectly steady, and she had thought: *This is exactly why I do what I do.*

Because here was the truth that Vane had arrived at, twenty years and forty-one files ago, and that she held now with the calm of a woman holding a proven theorem: grief like that did not stay contained. Grief like that became testimony. Testimony became inquiry. Inquiry became scandal, and scandal became *worse* scandal, because the underlying truth—the Wake had passed through something, something old, something the beacon had been built to watch and therefore built to hide—was a truth Halcyon Route Authority could not survive in the open. Not because the corporation was uniquely wicked. Vane had no patience for the popular fiction that she worked for monsters. Halcyon was merely an institution, and institutions were like bodies: they would sacrifice a limb to save the trunk, and they would do it without malice, and the limb, in the end, rarely complained, because the limb had never been consulted and never learned to speak.

But the crew would learn to speak. That was the pit in the fruit. Eleven unmanifested crewmates, remembered by forty living witnesses, aboard a ship that would eventually dock somewhere with journalists, or lawyers, or worse, *independent insurers*. The grief would find its voice. It always did. Unless—

Unless the voice was never given a throat.

Vane finished her tea. She returned to the report.

*Assessment: The anomaly is contained. Crew psychological exposure is significant but manageable. No external parties have accessed raw data. Meridian Beacon personnel exposure is limited to the station keeper, Ada Almeida, who has been cooperative within expected parameters.*

She paused at Almeida's name, and here Vane permitted herself the closest thing to pleasure her working day contained, which was professional admiration.

She had pulled Almeida's file the moment her shuttle cleared the beacon's outer ring, as she pulled everyone's file, and what she had found was—there was no other word for it—*beautiful*. Not the file's contents; the file's *construction*. Twenty-two years of service history with no gaps, no anomalies, no promotions that came too fast or too slow, no commendations, no reprimands, no hobbies listed on the wellness survey, no emergency contact more specific than "Halcyon Personnel Office." A biography so smooth it read like it had been sanded. Vane had spent her career studying files the way gemologists study stones, and she knew a natural one from a cut one, and Ada Almeida's file was cut. Faceted. *Authored*.

Someone had built that woman a past the way Vane built reports: honestly, stone by stone, each element true, the whole bearing weight no element had to acknowledge.

It was, Vane reflected, pouring a second cup—unorthodox, but the work was long—the finest piece of erasure work she had ever encountered, and she had encountered the work of the best. There were perhaps four people in the corporation capable of it. Possibly the woman had done it to herself. That happened, occasionally, at the top of the field. The really gifted practitioners eventually turned the craft inward, unable to resist the temptation of their own material.

Vane made a note in her private ledger—*Almeida, A.: origin questionable. Watch. Possibly asset.*—and returned to the report, and felt, watching the cursor blink, something that a less disciplined woman might have called loneliness and that Vane called *inventory*.

*Recommendation,* she typed.

This was the paragraph she had been building toward for eleven minutes and twenty years, and she wrote it the way she did everything, plainly, because plainness was the last refuge of the true.

*It is the recommendation of this office that the Calloway's Wake be declared a navigational hazard. The vessel's reactor signature is irregular—see attached telemetry, reconciled—and its structural integrity cannot be guaranteed following nine years of deep-route service. Standard hazard protocol applies: the vessel should be scuttled at a designated deep-field coordinates, with all hands transferred to Halcyon vessels prior to disposal.*

*All hands,* she wrote, and did not flinch, because the phrase was doing quiet work. *All hands* meant the crew. It meant Okafor and his sister's name and the child's drawing and the sealed cabin. It meant that in approximately seventy-two hours, forty people who remembered an impossible voyage would board Halcyon transports for what the paperwork would call *reassignment and debriefing*, and the paperwork would be accurate for as long as it needed to be, and then it would be superseded, and the superseding paperwork would be accurate too, in its way. Some would be reassigned genuinely, scattered across the route like seeds, their stories thinned by distance until they sounded like tall tales even to themselves. Some would accept settlements. Some—the ones whose grief had already hardened into something that looked like testimony—would be handled by colleagues Vane respected, using methods Vane had helped refine, and those methods were not cruel. They were *thorough*. A new identity was a kindness, properly administered. A new past with no sister in it was a wound that closed clean, versus the ragged wound of a sister the record denied, which never closed at all.

Vane believed this. It was important to understand that she believed it, the way she believed in load ratings and reactor tolerances. She had entered this work twenty years ago as a young auditor who had discovered, in her first field posting, a colony station that had been erased—eleven thousand people reduced to a survey footnote—and she had lain awake for a week understanding that if the truth came out, the route would close, the colonies it fed would starve by schedule rather than by accident, and the corporation would simply build a new route over the grave. Erasure had not saved those eleven thousand. Nothing could. But erasure had saved the *next* eleven thousand, the ones who never knew how close the arithmetic had come. Mercy was not the absence of harm. Mercy was the redirection of harm away from the many and onto the few, and the few, being few, could be carried by professionals like Vane, who did not sleep badly, or who slept badly but reported for work anyway, which amounted to the same thing.

*The crew will experience this as loss,* she wrote, in the section the style guide required, *Recommendations for Personnel Welfare.* *This office recommends generous severance, priority reassignment, and full psychological support services upon arrival at Halcyon facilities. It is further recommended that no crew member be returned to Meridian Beacon or its vicinity.*

She stopped. Read the sentence back. *It is further recommended that no crew member be returned.*

There it was. The whole architecture, in one clause. The Wake would die at deep-field coordinates with its logs smoothed and its ghosts unlisted, and the forty who remembered would be dispersed into the corporation's vast and gentle machinery, and in five years the story would survive only as the way Sef Okafor sometimes went quiet near bulkheads, and in ten years not even that.

And the beacon? The beacon was a separate file. The beacon's founding records had a void in them—a void Vane had noticed on her way in, a void that someone at the Office of Continuity had presumably created decades ago and that Vane had no intention of examining too closely, because a void that old was load-bearing. You did not pull at load-bearing voids. She would recommend, in a supplementary memo, that the station keeper be retained. Almeida was competent, isolated, and—if Vane's read was right—already erased once, which made her either the safest person on the route or the most dangerous, and Vane genuinely did not know which, and the not-knowing itched at her like a seam in a glove.

She finished the report at 0300 station time. She read it through once, the whole thing, stone by stone, and found it true, and found it complete, and found it—this was the word she chose, deliberately, privately—*kind*. Kind the way a good anesthetic is kind. The crew would never know what had been taken from them, and what you never know, you never grieve. The eleven would remain unrecorded, and the unrecorded cannot be murdered twice. Somewhere in her chest, in the small sealed cabin where Vane kept the things she did not audit, a voice that sounded faintly like a young auditor on her first field posting asked *but what about their names*, and Vane answered it the way she always answered it: *names are load-bearing, and this structure cannot bear them*, and the voice went quiet, the way it always did, and she signed the report.

*Ilsa Vane, Senior Auditor. Continuity is mercy.*

She transmitted it on the priority channel and watched the receipt confirm, a small green chit in the corner of her screen, and allowed herself the eleven-minute tea ritual in reverse, which was simply sitting still until the work drained back out of her hands.

It was during those eleven minutes—she would reconstruct the timeline later, precisely, obsessively, and the precision would not save her—that the beacon's traffic log, which Vane had access to and monitored with professional diligence, recorded a routine maintenance access to the archive core. Station keeper Almeida, performing scheduled integrity checks. Nothing unusual. Keepers checked things; it was what they were for.

The access lasted four minutes. The query signature was elegant, minimal, and touched exactly one archive partition—the oldest one, the one with the void in it.

Vane, pouring out the last of her tea, did not see it.

* * *

The reply from the Office of Continuity arrived at 0412, and it was, in its way, a masterpiece.

*RE: Calloway's Wake. Recommendation approved. Hazard declaration effective immediately. Scuttle authorization follows under separate cipher. Interdiction assets redeployed; ETA Meridian Beacon 60 hours. Note: advance element dispatched early under reactor-fault pretext to secure vessel and personnel prior to main arrival. Do not inform station keeper of hazard determination. Maintain standard audit cover. Continuity is mercy.*

*Do not inform the station keeper.*

Vane read it twice. The first time, she noted the operational content: the interdiction fleet was coming early, which meant someone above her had less confidence in the timeline than her report projected, which was fine, that was what redundancy was for. The second time, she noted the *tone*, which was a thing reports were not supposed to have, and this one had it anyway, the way a knife has a tone. *Do not inform the station keeper of hazard determination.* Not *the keeper need not be briefed*. Not the usual bureaucratic cushioning. A direct order of concealment, aimed at a woman whose file was too clean, whose beacon had a void in its founding records, and who had—Vane pulled the traffic log up now, idly, the way one checks a lock one has already checked—touched the void partition four hours ago.

Four minutes. One partition. The oldest one.

Vane set down her cup. It was good tea. It was, she thought with a clarity that felt almost like grief, very good tea, and someone with opinions had stocked it, and she had been in this woman's guest quarters for nine hours calling her *cooperative within expected parameters* while the woman quietly put her hands into the one archive that could unmake everything.

Vane did not panic. Panic was a resource expenditure. She opened her private ledger, found the entry she had made that first evening—*Almeida, A.: origin questionable. Watch. Possibly asset*—and amended it with a stylus stroke that was, for her, the equivalent of a shout:

*Almeida, A.: possibly the author. Possibly the architect. Watch everything.*

Sixty hours. An advance element already inbound under a lie she would have to deliver with a straight face to a woman who was, by every measurable indication, better at this than Vane was.

She looked at the rendered starscape in the false window, at the beacon's steady light sweeping its slow arc across a darkness that someone, decades ago, had gone to great lengths to make sure stayed dark.

"Let's see your file, Ada Almeida," she said to the empty room, and reached for the terminal.
