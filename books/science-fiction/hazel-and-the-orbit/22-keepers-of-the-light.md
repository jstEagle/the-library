## 22. Keepers of the Light

The light turned on a Tuesday.

Not because Tuesdays meant anything out here—Meridian Beacon didn't keep days so much as orbits—but because Hazel Almeida had decided that if she was going to aim the oldest transmitter in the corridor at the coordinates Halcyon had spent forty years pretending were empty, she wanted to do it on a day with a name. Something ordinary. Something that would never appear in an official record as significant.

Sef Okafor stood beside her in the relay cradle, arms crossed, watching the great dish swing through its final degrees of arc. It moved slowly, the way old things move when they've been asked to hurry their whole lives and have finally been given permission not to.

"There," Hazel said quietly. "Locked."

The coordinates hung on her display: the place where something had folded nine years into three, where eleven volunteers had crossed over and been subtracted from existence, where a gutted colony station had called for help and been answered by people who were then made to never have lived. For a year the beacon had kept its light elsewhere—polite, neutral, pointed at shipping lanes like a good blindfold. Now it stared directly at the wound.

"Any word from the Authority's lawyers?" Sef asked.

"Three cease-and-desists, two injunctions, and one letter that I'm fairly sure was written by an actual human being expressing genuine concern for my safety." She smiled without looking away from the display. "I framed that one."

"You framed a legal threat."

"The concerned one. The others I use to line the compost bin." She tapped the console, and the main relay hummed up through its frequencies—not the whisper-quiet maintenance pulse it had sung for decades, but a full, open broadcast: *This is Meridian Beacon. These are the coordinates we watch. This is why.* "Let them sue the light."

* * *

Down in the maintenance gallery—the long spine of the station where the air always smelled faintly of ozone and old solder—Hazel had a student.

Amara Okafor was eleven years old, Sef's niece, all elbows and questions, with her uncle's exact way of frowning at machinery as though it owed her money. She walked the rounds behind Hazel with a datapad clutched to her chest like a shield, and she had already, in six weeks, developed opinions.

"You check the same bolt twice," Amara said, watching Hazel run her palm along the coolant junction for the second time. "You checked it this morning."

"I did."

"So why again?"

Hazel crouched, letting the girl see the weld seam properly. "Because the first time you're checking whether it's broken. The second time you're checking whether it's *becoming* broken. Everything on a station is either fine or becoming something else. Your job is to catch it while it's still becoming."

Amara considered this with the gravity of a judge. "My uncle says you talk to the station."

"I do."

"What does it say?"

Hazel pressed her ear lightly against the junction housing—a habit she'd had since before she could remember having habits—and listened to the pump's rhythm. "It says bearing four is going to want attention in about three weeks. And it says you're standing too close to the vent."

Amara stepped back so fast she nearly dropped the datapad. "It said that?"

"It said the airflow changed when you moved. But yes," Hazel admitted, straightening up, "it also said that." She held out her hand for the pad. "Your turn. Junction seven. Tell me what it's becoming."

The girl took the pad, squared her shoulders, and approached the junction the way soldiers approach fortifications. Hazel watched her go and felt the particular ache-warmth of passing something on—not just the maintenance rounds, but the *stance*: the belief that a machine kept well was a promise kept, that attention was a form of love, that nobody who tended a light should ever be invisible herself.

She'd spent most of her life perfecting invisibility. Watching Amara take up visible space in the corridor, loud and curious and unafraid, felt like watching someone spend money Hazel had hoarded for decades.

"Bearing four," Amara announced from down the gallery. "It's becoming noisy!"

"Good ear! Write it down. In your own words."

Amara wrote, tongue between her teeth: *Bearing 4 is starting to complain. We should listen before it screams.*

Hazel read it later and entered it verbatim into the official log. The station's records, she had decided, would be messy from now on. Human. Signed. The opposite of everything the old files had been.

* * *

The coded message arrived on a Thursday, which also meant nothing, except that Vane had once told her that Thursdays were when bureaucracies slept, and Vane never sent anything on a day anyone might be awake.

Hazel decoded it in the vault—the deep archive chamber the founder had built beneath the beacon's heart, the room where the eleven voices still lived, where the corrupted logs rested in triplicate across forgotten architecture. She'd turned part of it into a garden, actually. Sef had laughed at her for a week. Now tomato vines climbed the archive racks, and the dead crew's testimony ripened alongside actual fruit, and somehow that seemed right.

The message was short, as Vane's messages always were. A shipping manifest number. A route designation. A date nine months out. And at the bottom, in the cipher they'd built together during the inquiry—one part professional paranoia, one part apology—three words:

*Pattern matches. Watch.*

No name signed. There didn't need to be. Ilsa Vane had surrendered her ledger at the inquiry, testified for eleven hours, and then walked out of the hearing room and back into the machine that had made her, on the theory—stated flatly, without self-pity—that she could do more damage from inside than out. So far she'd been right four times. Four manifests, four routes, four places where paperwork had eaten people. Each time, independent investigators had found what Vane's cipher pointed them toward. Each time, the names had stuck.

Hazel sat with the message for a long moment, the tomato leaves ticking against the archive racks in the ventilation draft.

Somewhere out there, another station was keeping a blindfold. Another crew was coming home wrong. Another set of names was waiting, patient as seeds, for someone to write them down.

She forwarded the manifest to the watchdog network—the ragged constellation of independent stations, rival insurers, and stubborn colonists that had grown up around the broadcast like moss around a fallen tree—and added a note of her own: *Meridian has eyes on. Send us anything strange.*

Then she went back upstairs and checked bearing four, because promises were promises.

* * *

The Calloway's Wake hung in its permanent drift a thousand kilometers off the beacon's flank, no longer a freighter but something better: a monument, a school, a home. The crew had voted unanimously not to repair her engines. She would never make another run. Instead they'd rebuilt her interior deck by deck—the sealed cabin opened at last and made into a memorial, the child's drawing preserved under polymer glass on the bulkhead where it had been taped, the mess hall converted to classrooms where spacers' kids learned navigation on a ship that had come back from impossible.

Sef captained her now, or curated her, or whatever you called tending a ship full of ghosts who had finally been allowed to exist. On visiting days he gave tours. He always ended in the same place: the bulkhead drawing, crayon-bright, a stick-figure family holding hands under a lopsided sun, signed in a child's scrawl with a name that was now carved into eleven memorials across four systems.

"That's my sister's student," he told the visitors. "Marisol drew it the day before the crossing. She was eight." He let the silence sit, because he'd learned—from Hazel, actually—that silence was a kind of honesty. "The corporation deleted her from every record in existence. And now there are statues. You tell me which one lasted."

Today's visitors were a delegation from the Corridor Historical Institute, and after the tour, one of them—an elderly woman with ink-stained fingers—lingered by the drawing.

"My granddaughter wants to know," the woman said, "if Marisol liked the sun."

Sef blinked. "What?"

"In the drawing. The sun is very deliberate. Children draw what matters." The woman smiled gently. "Did she like sunshine?"

Sef thought about his sister's letters, the ones recovered from the deep archive, the ones describing the colony station before it died: the grow-lights, the solar gardens, the way Marisol used to fall asleep in the hydroponics bay because she said plants made better dreams. He laughed, and it cracked a little in the middle, the way his laughs still did.

"Yeah," he said. "She really did."

That night he recorded the exchange and sent it to Hazel with no commentary. She understood. This was how grief worked now—not quieter, exactly, but *wider*. Room enough in it for sunshine.

* * *

One year, almost to the day, since the broadcast.

They marked it the way they marked everything now: together, badly, with food. The beacon's observation ring had become the station's de facto dining hall, and tonight it held the whole improbable family—Sef and half the Wake's crew, Amara asleep under a table with a datapad still glowing on her chest, the two young keepers who'd come from the watchdog network to learn the rounds, and a surprising number of tomatoes.

Vane hadn't come, obviously. But there was a package in the incoming queue timestamped an hour before the anniversary, containing a bottle of genuinely terrible synthetic wine and a note in plain text, which from Vane was practically a love letter:

*One year of the pattern holding. Statistically, they'll try again somewhere within eighteen months. When they do, you'll be ready, and so will I. Drink something. You've earned worse. — I.V.*

Hazel read it aloud to general cheering, and Sef poured the terrible wine into whatever vessels came to hand, including, at Amara's groggy insistence, a coolant measuring cup that everyone agreed not to think about.

"To the eleven," Sef said, raising his cup.

"To the eleven," the room answered.

"And to the light," Hazel added, and the room answered that too, and outside the viewport the beacon's beam swept its slow arc across the dark, steady as a heartbeat, aimed at nothing hidden anymore.

Later, when the party had thinned to embers, Hazel took her usual solitary walk along the outer gallery—the round she'd done ten thousand times, the round she'd taught to a child, the round that was hers and no longer only hers. The stars were doing what stars did: burning, indifferent, beautiful. Somewhere among them were the coordinates. Somewhere beyond them, whatever had folded time and swallowed people was still there, patient, unexplained. They watched it now. That was all anyone could do, and it was not nothing. Watching was not nothing. She had built her whole second life on that conviction.

She was halfway around the ring when the alert chimed—soft, routine, the kind of ping that usually meant a transponder glitch or a comet with delusions of importance.

She glanced at it. Then stopped walking.

An inbound signature, far out past the shipping lanes. Small. Slow. Running dark, the way ships ran when they didn't want to be found—or the way they ran when their navigation had been scrambled by something that shouldn't exist.

And trailing behind it, faint but unmistakable, a temporal wake that matched, point for point, the signature of the Calloway's Wake.

Hazel stood very still in the cold gallery light, her breath fogging the viewport, and felt the old fear rise—and pass through her, and keep going, because fear needed a person with something to lose, and she had finally, expensively, rebuilt herself into someone with plenty.

She keyed the station-wide channel. Her voice came out calm. It surprised her how calm.

"All hands. This is Keeper Almeida. We have an inbound vessel, temporal signature confirmed, origin consistent with the anomaly. Amara—" she softened, just slightly, "—wake up, sweetheart. Bring your datapad. You're going to want to log this one yourself."

Out in the dark, the impossible ship came home. And the light went out to meet it.
