## 6. The Forger's Fingerprints

The dead relay hung in the maintenance bay like a fish someone had given up on cleaning.

Idris had hauled it out of the comms crawlspace at 0400 station time, which was a phrase that meant nothing on Meridian anymore — we kept the clocks running out of politeness to no one — and now it sat on my potting bench between the propagation trays and a jar of rooting hormone I'd been using as paperweight for six years. It was the size of a loaf of bread, scorched along one edge, and it had been transmitting a lie into the dark for longer than either of us had been alive.

"Careful," Idris said, as I reached for it. "The capacitor bank may still hold charge."

"It's been dead for decades."

"So have some capacitors. Briefly, and then very loudly." He slid a discharge wand across the bench to me without looking up from the schematic he'd projected onto the greenhouse glass. There was something about the way he handled equipment — reverent, almost tender, like everything aboard this station was a patient he'd promised to keep alive — that made me want to both trust him completely and check my pockets afterward.

I discharged the relay. Nothing exploded. Small victories.

* * *

Here is what we knew by dawn: the distress signal hadn't come from Meridian Station. That much Idris had suspected from the start — "the modulation profile is wrong for our transmitter array," he'd said, in the tone of a man informing you your own house has an unfamiliar smell — but suspicion became certainty when we cracked open the relay's physical log.

The signal originated from Relay Kestrel-9, a comm buoy parked forty thousand kilometers off our port axis, positioned precisely where a ship limping in from the old trade lanes would look first. Someone had towed it there. Someone had programmed it. And then someone had walked away from it and let it scream *help us* into the void on a loop, faithful as a heartbeat, for thirty-one years.

"Why?" I asked, and immediately hated how small the question sounded. "Why build a fake cry for help? Who benefits?"

Idris didn't answer right away. He was doing the thing he did when he was thinking, which was to hold his stylus vertically against his lower lip like a tiny mast. I'd known him four days and I already had a catalog of these gestures. It was becoming a problem.

"Think about what the signal does," he said finally. "Not what it says. What it *does*. Two fleets are converging on this system. Vossan and Halcyon. They've been converging for months, decelerating in perfect mirror of each other, because neither one wants to arrive second at a colony world with intact infrastructure. But they're not arriving. They're hovering. Why would two armed armadas hover?"

"Because someone told them there were survivors."

"Because someone told them there were survivors who needed rescue, and rescue is the one mission both fleets can claim without shooting each other over it. The moment survivors stop being the objective—" He let the sentence die, which was its own kind of eloquence. "The signal isn't a plea. It's a fence. Somebody built a fence around this graveyard and painted it the color of hope."

I looked down at the relay. Its casing bore a maintenance stamp, half worn away: **MERIDIAN STATION DIRECTORATE — PROPERTY OF THE COLONY**. Below it, a serial number, and below that, in smaller type, a name I recognized from the station's founding plaques in the central corridor, the ones I dusted every month because nobody else would:

**A. CHEN, DIR.**

* * *

Dr. Amara Chen had an office on Deck Three that I had never once entered, because entering it felt like trespassing in a tomb, and I say that as someone whose entire life was tomb-adjacent. Now I stood in its doorway with Idris behind me and the smell of thirty years of sealed air rolling out like a held breath.

Her office was immaculate. That was the first thing. The second thing was that it was immaculate *wrongly* — the desk arranged for a left-handed person, the chair pushed in at an angle suggesting she'd left mid-thought, a mug on the blotter with a fossilized ring of tea at its bottom. She hadn't tidied before she died. Or she had died before she could tidy. On a station where everyone else had packed their lives into evacuation shuttles, Amara Chen had left her tea unfinished.

"She stayed," I said. It wasn't a question, but Idris answered it anyway.

"The evacuation manifests list eleven hundred and six souls departing Meridian. Station census at final audit: one thousand one hundred and five accounted for, plus one unaccounted." He said it gently, the way you'd set something breakable down. "Chen filed her own name under 'unaccounted' rather than 'remaining.' I think she wanted the record to show an error. A loose thread. Something for whoever came looking to pull."

"Did anyone come looking?"

"You're standing in her office."

The terminal on her desk still booted. Of course it did; Meridian hardware was built to outlast its makers, possibly outlast its makers' regrets. Her files were organized with a precision that made my chest ache — directories labeled not by function but by *reader*: FOR WHOEVER FINDS THIS, FOR THE FLEETS, FOR EARTH IF ANYONE IS STILL READING, and one simply titled DO NOT OPEN UNTIL YOU HAVE READ EVERYTHING ELSE, which is the most director thing I have ever encountered, and I include myself, a man who alphabetizes seed packets by cultivar and then by germination temperature.

We started with FOR WHOEVER FINDS THIS.

* * *

Her voice filled the office. That's the only way I can describe it. Audio logs, hundreds of them, and Amara Chen spoke the way I imagine she did everything — clearly, dryly, with the faint amusement of a person who has seen the joke in every situation including her own funeral.

*"Log one. If you're hearing this, the relay worked, or the station's power grid finally gave out, or you're a scavenger stripping my office for copper, in which case: the copper's in the walls, please take the north wall first, the wiring's cleaner. My name is Amara Chen. I was director of Meridian Colony for nineteen years, and for the last eleven of them, I have been its entire population. This log series documents the largest fraud in colonial history, committed by a staff of one. I'd ask you not to judge me too harshly, but I know how history works. Judge away. Just read the whole file first."*

I paused the playback. My hands were not entirely steady.

"She sounds like—" I started.

"Don't," Idris said quietly. But he was smiling, faintly, at the terminal, and I understood: he felt it too. Four days ago this woman was a name on a plaque. Now she was a voice in a room, making jokes about copper thieves, and grief had arrived ahead of schedule for a person we'd never met.

We played the next log. Then the next. We were there until the station's artificial dusk cycle dimmed the corridor lights outside her door.

* * *

The shape of it, assembled over hours: When Earth recalled the evacuation fleet early — budget collapses move faster than light-speed guilt — Chen had argued, petitioned, and finally threatened. None of it mattered. Eleven hundred people left. One stayed. And Chen, who understood fleets the way farmers understand weather, knew exactly what would happen when the Vossan and Halcyon expansion waves eventually reached this system: they would not find a graveyard and mourn. They would find a graveyard and *quarrel*. Territory, salvage rights, strategic depth. Dead colonies don't get memorials. They get flags.

So she built the fiction. A distress call in Keshari — deliberately, she explained, because Keshari was the trade tongue both fleets' older officers still dreamed in, the language of shared mercantile history, impossible to dismiss as forgery without insulting your own command structure. A relay positioned where rescuers would look. A slow drip of fabricated telemetry: failing life support, dwindling supplies, a population too proud to beg until it had to. Enough hope to keep two armadas polite.

*"Log two hundred and nine,"* she recorded, in what I calculated was roughly year twenty-eight of her vigil. *"The Vossan vanguard adjusted course today in response to my last transmission. They believe there are children here. There are no children here. There is a garden, and it is doing better than I deserve. I have decided this counts as a moral victory and I am going to eat the last of the real coffee to celebrate."*

I laughed. It came out of me sideways, unexpected, and turned into something else halfway through, and Idris pretended very hard to be fascinated by the directory structure until I was done.

"I watered her garden," I said, when I could speak. "For years. I thought I was keeping it ready for colonists who'd come back someday. Turns out I was maintaining the set dressing for her con."

"Is that how it feels? Set dressing?"

I thought about it honestly, which is a discipline I recommend mostly to other people. "No," I admitted. "It feels like she left me the only honest thing on the station and didn't tell me, because telling me would've meant admitting the rest was false."

"That's not cruelty," Idris said. "That's a woman who couldn't carry it alone and refused to hand anyone else the weight. There's a word for that in Keshari. *Saveth.* It means a burden carried so long it becomes part of the spine."

"Do you have a word for someone who finds out they've been carrying a stranger's burden without knowing it?"

"We have several. Most of them are insults." He touched the terminal, waking her voice again. "Keep listening. It gets worse. It always gets worse before it gets useful."

* * *

It got worse in the way he predicted. Chen's later logs documented her decline with the same unsentimental clarity she applied to everything — the radiation exposure from her solo repair of the reactor shielding in year twelve, the illness it seeded, the arithmetic she ran on her remaining time versus the fleets' arrival window. She lost the race by approximately nine years.

But she didn't lose it cleanly. In her final months she restructured everything: the relay's power draw reduced to stretch its lifespan past a century, the signal's content layered so it could survive degradation, and — this was the part that stopped my breath — a complete operational manual. FOR MY SUCCESSOR, the directory was called. Protocols for answering fleet hails. Voice samples, phonetic guides, a pronunciation lexicon for Keshari compiled by a dying woman working alone. Character notes on the fictional crew she'd invented, so detailed they included birthdays and grudges. A schedule of lies, cross-referenced against plausible fleet behavior, maintained with the rigor most people reserve for scripture.

She had built a church and left the liturgy on the altar, waiting for a priest who might never come.

"She expected someone," I said. "All of this. The loose thread in the manifest, the manual, the—she expected a successor."

"She hoped," Idris corrected. "Expecting is for people with better odds." He scrolled through the phonetic guides, and I watched his face do something complicated. "Sable. Listen to me. These logs aren't just a confession. They're an inheritance. Everything we need to keep the deception alive is in this room. The fleets are coming, the relay is aging, and the woman who built this lie left us the blueprints to maintain it."

"*We*," I repeated. "There's a word I haven't agreed to yet."

He had the grace to look sheepish. "You decoded half of this yourself."

"I held the flashlight while *you* decoded it."

"A crucial role. Historically underappreciated." But he wasn't joking, really, and neither was I, and we stood there in a dead woman's office surrounded by the architecture of a magnificent fraud, and I felt the terrible vertigo of realizing that the loneliest job in the universe had just acquired a co-signer.

* * *

I went back alone that night. Idris had retired to the guest quarters — my quarters, technically, since I owned every bunk on the station and had slept in exactly one of them for a decade — and I told him I needed to finish cataloging Chen's files, which was true, and also that I needed to be alone with her, which was truer.

I played her earliest log again. Log one, the beginning, where her voice was younger and quicker and still believed someone might listen soon.

*"One more thing,"* she said, near the end of it. *"If you're the successor — and if you've gotten this far, you probably are, because scavengers don't read directories, they rip them — I need you to understand what you're inheriting. Not the protocols. Those are just scaffolding. I mean the debt. Every day those fleets stay polite, people live who would otherwise die in a war over our bones. That's the ledger. You don't have to sign it. But know that I signed it gladly, and that the garden on Deck One was never part of the lie. I grew things because growing things is what people do. Whatever else you decide — keep the garden. It's the only true sentence on this whole station."*

I sat in her left-handed chair, at her desk, in front of her unfinished tea, and I cried for a woman I'd never met, in an office I'd avoided for ten years, over a debt I hadn't yet decided to pay.

Then I washed the mug. It seemed important. Some inheritances begin with dishes.

* * *

In the morning I found Idris in the greenhouse, reading Chen's phonetic lexicon against the seed labels on my trays, cross-referencing her fictional crew names with plant varieties, and when he looked up at me his expression was careful, hopeful, and slightly braced for rejection — the expression of a man who had already guessed my answer and was giving me one last exit anyway.

"The fleets will demand direct contact soon," he said. "Weeks, maybe days. When they do, someone will have to speak for Meridian Command. Live. In Keshari. As a dead woman's deputy, backed by a dead woman's script, in front of two armadas."

"I know."

"And you'll need to learn the language. All of it. Fast."

"I know that too." I picked up the lexicon — three hundred pages of a dead woman's handwriting, dense and precise and occasionally annotated with jokes in the margins, because apparently even martyrs get bored — and handed it to him. "Start with the alphabet. And Idris?"

"Yes?"

"If you ever tell anyone I cried in her office, I'm feeding you to the compost recycler."

He smiled, and opened the book, and began, in a language extinct for two centuries, to teach me how to lie beautifully.

The first lesson took four hours. Somewhere in hour three, tracing the looping curves of the Keshari character for *voice*, I realized I was learning to speak for the dead — and that Amara Chen, wherever she was, had planned for exactly this, had counted on a stranger's stubborn heart the way she'd counted on everything else.

The margin note beside that character read: *They always come. Someone always comes. It's the one bet I never lost.*

Outside the greenhouse glass, at the system's edge, two fleets waited for a voice.
