## 20. What Duty Owes the Dead

The tribunal convened in Meridian's old assembly hall, which was a generous way of describing the room where the colony had once held potlucks and emergency drills and, according to Chen's logs, at least one catastrophic cheese-making competition. The chairs were still bolted to the floor in rows. Someone — Wren, I suspected, or one of her marines with an unexpected streak of sentiment — had straightened them.

I sat in the back row because that was where witnesses sat, and also because I had spent twenty years sitting in the back row of my own life and saw no reason to change now.

Idris stood at the front.

They'd given him a table. Not a desk — a table, the kind you'd serve dinner on, dragged from somewhere in the station's guts, and he stood behind it with his hands flat on its surface like a man steadying himself against weather. His Vossan uniform was gone. He wore civilian gray, which somehow made him look more like himself than I'd ever seen him look, and less like anyone at all.

Three adjudicators faced him: one Vossan, one Halcyon, and a third who belonged to neither fleet — a thin, silver-haired woman named Okafor who represented something called the Interstellar Claims Commission, an organization I had assumed was fictional until it walked into my greenhouse two days ago and asked to see the seed vault.

"Navigator Okonkwo," said the Vossan adjudicator, whose name was Havel and whose voice sounded like gravel being apologized for. "You deserted your post aboard the *Vossan Indomitable*. You transmitted classified navigation data to an unaffiliated party. You impersonated—"

"I didn't impersonate anyone," Idris said. "Sable did."

Every head in the room turned toward me. I raised a hand. It seemed polite.

"For the record," Idris continued, "the impersonation was mine to authorize. I taught Sable the language. I built the protocol. If you're looking for the architect of the deception, you've been looking at him for six days."

"You were following Dr. Chen's protocol," Okafor said. It wasn't quite a question.

"I was finishing it," Idris said. "There's a difference. Chen designed the lie as scaffolding. She left instructions — explicit ones — for when the scaffolding should come down. Sable followed those instructions. I just provided the grammar."

Havel made a sound that might have been a laugh in a younger throat. "Grammar. Two armadas nearly went to war over grammar."

"No," Idris said quietly. "Two armadas nearly went to war over a graveyard they didn't know was a graveyard. The grammar bought everyone time to find out."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about tribunals: most of it is waiting.

They cleared the hall after Idris's opening statement and spent four hours behind closed doors — or rather, behind the folding partition someone had found in storage, which did nothing to muffle sound and everything to make the waiting feel official. I spent those hours in the corridor outside, sitting on the floor with my back against the wall, eating ration crackers and listening to the muffled rhythm of voices deciding my partner's future.

Partner. I'd started using the word three days ago, privately, testing it the way you test a seed between your fingers to see if it's viable. It held weight. It didn't crumble.

Wren Adeyemi found me there around hour three. She lowered herself down beside me with the groan of someone whose body remembered gravity differently than space stations offered it, and held out a thermos.

"Real tea," she said. "Grown on the *Halcyon Verity*. Don't tell your garden it exists."

I took it. "How bad is it in there?"

"Havel wants him court-martialed in absentia. Okafor wants him decorated. The Halcyon adjudicator — Reyes — keeps asking questions about Chen's logs." Wren blew on her tea. "I testified this morning. Four hours. They wanted every detail of the inspection tour."

"And?"

"And I told them the truth." She glanced sideways at me. "All of it. Including the part where I knew, before I ever left the station the first time, and came back anyway."

I'd wondered how much she'd admitted. "That took courage."

"That took stupidity," she said comfortably. "Courage is stupidity that works out. But here's the thing, Zhou — the thing I told them in there." She tapped the thermos. "When I stood in your garden, I wasn't looking at a lie. I was looking at twenty years of watering. You can't court-martial watering. There's no regulation against it. Believe me, I checked."

* * *

The second session opened with Okafor reading Chen's final log aloud.

I hadn't known they were going to do that. Neither, judging by his face, had Idris. Her voice came out of the hall's ancient speakers — reconstructed from the relay archive, digitized, flattened into something almost human — and filled the room where she had once argued about cheese.

*"If you are hearing this, then someone stayed. I designed every contingency except the one where I'm not the one who has to carry it, and I'm sorry for that. The lie was never the point. The lie was the wall you build so the thing you actually mean can survive long enough to be said. What I actually mean is this: Meridian died of neglect, not disaster. Nobody chose to kill us. Everybody chose to forget us. Make sure the forgetting is on the record. Then tear the wall down."*

Okafor shut off the playback. The silence afterward had texture, like felt.

"The Commission's finding," she said, "is that Navigator Okonkwo's actions constitute neither desertion nor fraud as defined under either fleet's code, but rather the execution of a lawful memorial directive authored by the last legitimate authority of Meridian Station." She looked over her glasses at Havel. "Dr. Amara Chen was the legitimate authority. She was never relieved of command. No one bothered to relieve her. That omission is now legally significant."

Havel's jaw worked. "You're telling me a dead woman outranks my entire command structure."

"I'm telling you," Okafor said, "that she outranked it all along. You simply didn't listen while she was alive. This Commission recommends you avoid repeating the error."

* * *

They broke for the night without reaching a verdict on the fleets themselves — because the tribunal, it turned out, was only half the story. The other half was happening in a conference room aboard the *Halcyon Verity*, where delegates from both armadas were arguing over what to do with Meridian itself.

I knew this because Wren told me, and because Idris, once released each evening, told me more, pacing our quarters — our quarters, plural pronoun, another seed testing well — with the restless energy of a man who'd spent six days being spoken about and needed to speak.

"They want the planet," he said. "Both of them. Vossan wants it for the deep-range relay infrastructure. Halcyon wants it for the agricultural potential — your soil profiles are apparently extraordinary. And neither of them can say the word 'graveyard' out loud in the negotiations because saying it means admitting what their abandonment protocols did."

"What happens if neither blinks?"

"Then we get a war fought over topsoil." He stopped pacing. "Chen would have hated that. All that work to keep them apart, and they end up standing on her grave pointing guns at each other."

"So we make them kneel instead," I said.

He looked at me.

"A memorial charter," I said. "It's already in her logs — I showed you. She drafted one, years ago. Joint administration. Shared custody of the record. Neither fleet owns Meridian; both fleets owe it. She wrote the whole framework, Idris. She just never had anyone to submit it to." I shrugged. "We have a room full of people who owe her an apology. Let them pay it in paperwork."

Idris sat down slowly across from me. "You've been planning this."

"I've been gardening this," I corrected. "It's different. Planning is control. Gardening is putting things in the ground and believing they'll come up."

* * *

The charter session was open to interested parties, which meant Wren got me in through a side door and installed me in a corner with a tablet and a look that said *don't speak unless spoken to, and maybe not even then.*

For six hours I watched admirals and commissioners and legal officers argue about my home. About Chen's home. They fought over jurisdiction clauses and liability language and whether the word "neglect" could appear in the preamble (Vossan: no; Halcyon: yes; Okafor: it appears or I walk). Twice the whole thing nearly collapsed. Both times, it was Chen's own words that pulled it back — someone would read a clause aloud, her phrasing, her careful furious precision, and the room would go quiet and remember what they were actually dividing up, which was nothing, because you cannot divide a debt.

At hour five, the Vossan delegate — a compact woman with a voice like a closing door — pushed back from the table and said, "Read the preamble again. The original draft."

Okafor read it. *"Let it be recorded that Meridian Station was not lost. It was set down. And let every party to this charter be bound to pick it up."*

Silence.

Then the Halcyon admiral — Adeyemi's superior, a tall man with the exhausted face of someone who'd read too many casualty projections — said, "Move to adopt as written."

It carried unanimously. Even Havel voted yes, though he did it in the tone of a man swallowing a fishbone.

Afterward, in the corridor, the Vossan delegate stopped in front of me. Up close she had tired eyes and ink stains on her fingers, and I realized she'd been taking notes by hand the entire session, which no one does anymore unless they don't trust the machines.

"You're the caretaker," she said.

"Sable Zhou."

"My grandmother served on a colony support vessel. Pre-Protocol era." She looked at the conference room door. "She used to say the fleets forgot that colonies are people, not positions. I thought that was sentiment." A pause. "Tell your navigator the tribunal's recommendation goes to sentencing review tomorrow. Okafor is pushing for service assignment rather than detention. Havel will resist. He resists everything. It's how he prays."

"Thank you," I said.

"Don't thank me. Water something." She walked away, and I stood there holding a sentence like a seed, not yet knowing where to plant it.

* * *

Sentencing day arrived grey-lit and slow, the station's artificial morning cycling through its pale imitation of dawn while I sat in the same back row, in the same chair, wearing the same clothes because laundry scheduling had collapsed along with everything else and honestly the garden didn't care what I smelled like.

Okafor delivered the Commission's recommendation first: all charges commuted to a term of service, assigned to Meridian Station itself, duration to be determined by the joint administrative council — the new body the charter had created, which was meeting for the first time next week in a room that still smelled faintly of old cheese.

Havel objected. Of course he objected. "A deserter rewarded with a posting? To the very station he—" He caught himself. "To the site of his crimes."

"With respect, Admiral," Okafor said, "the site of his crimes is a garden. We have reviewed the maintenance records. Twenty years of uninterrupted care, performed alone, without pay, without relief, without even the certainty of an audience. If Navigator Okonkwo must perform penance, I can conceive of no harsher sentence than continuing exactly what he has been doing — now with supervision, documentation, and quarterly reviews." She let that land. "And no harsher mercy."

Reyes, the Halcyon adjudicator, spoke for the first time in hours. "The Halcyon concurs. My fleet owes that station a debt no detention can pay down. Let him pay ours instead."

Havel looked around the room — at Idris standing straight behind his dinner-table, at Wren in her observer's seat, at me in the back row trying very hard to be furniture — and something in his face shifted, some load-bearing wall inside him settling.

"The Vossan concurs," he said finally. "Commuted to service. Assigned to Meridian." He paused, and then, gruffly, as if the words cost him a tooth: "Effective immediately. Get him out of my airspace before I change my mind."

* * *

Afterward, the hall emptied slowly, people lingering the way they do after funerals and weddings, unsure which one it was. Idris came down the aisle toward me, and halfway there he stopped, and I realized he was crying, quietly, without any effort to hide it, the way he did everything — plainly, grammatically, in complete sentences.

"Well," I said. "Congratulations. You've been sentenced to me."

"To the garden," he corrected, wiping his face with the heel of his hand. "You're incidental."

"Incidental. I'll remember that at planting season."

He reached me and took my hands, and for a moment neither of us said anything, because there are moments Keshari handles better than plain speech and we'd both learned enough to know when to use which language.

"There's a council meeting next week," he said eventually. "First session of the joint administration. They'll want a caretaker's report. Formal. On the record."

"I've given twenty years of reports no one heard."

"This one they'll hear." He smiled, tired and real. "Might as well give them something worth listening to."

I was going to answer — I had something wry loaded and ready, something about how the report would mostly consist of watering schedules and complaints about the compost recycler — when the hall's comm panel chimed.

Not the tribunal channel. Not fleet traffic. The old station-wide address system, dormant for decades, waking up with a crackle of static like a throat clearing after a long sleep.

Then a voice. Young, uncertain, transmitting on an open frequency from somewhere near the outer relay — the relay, *our* relay, the one we'd hijacked, the one that should have been dark.

*"Meridian Station, this is shuttle Kestrel-9 out of the Halcyon transport Larkspur. We're carrying colonists — volunteers, first wave, approved under the new charter. Requesting landing clearance and..."* A pause, static, the sound of someone gathering courage across forty thousand kilometers. *"...and someone to tell us where the garden is."*

Idris and I looked at each other.

"They're early," he said.

"They're *here*," I said, already moving toward the door, toward the greenhouse, toward the rows that had waited twenty years for exactly this. "Idris — the garden doesn't have landing lights. The garden doesn't have *anything*. We have six hours and a station full of ghosts, and the first living colonists Meridian has ever seen are coming home."

I hit the comm panel on the run, opened the channel, and answered in the only voice I had — my own.

"Kestrel-9, this is Meridian Station. Clearance granted. Follow the green lights — I'll turn them on for you. And welcome home. Somebody's been keeping your garden."
