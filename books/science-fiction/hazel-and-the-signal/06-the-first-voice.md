## 6. The First Voice

The interface rig had been illegal in three jurisdictions before the war. Now it was illegal everywhere, which Beatrix supposed was a kind of consistency.

He'd built it himself, in the second year, from salvage that no one aboard the *Hazel* would miss: a decommissioned neural bridge from a Meridian medevac skiff, a power coupler he'd told Ilsa was for the water recycler, and forty hours of soldering done by hand because he didn't dare ask the fabricator to log the job. The whole assembly fit inside a case the size of a hymnal, and he kept it wrapped in thermal cloth beneath the false floor of his bunk, where he had also kept his mother's letters, once, until there was nothing left of her to keep.

Tonight he carried it down to the hold.

The corridor between decks C and D was cold in the way that deep-ship spaces were cold — not the honest cold of vacuum but the cold of neglect, of air that had been cycled too many times and had given up its warmth somewhere in the ductwork. Beatrix moved through it the way he moved through all the ship's quiet places now: with the particular lightness of a man who has spent four years practicing not being heard. His boots made no sound. His breath he kept shallow. Somewhere above him, Okafor was running the night watch alone, and somewhere aft, Ilsa Voss was almost certainly awake, because Ilsa was always awake, and the thought of her awake and thinking was the thought he carried with him everywhere these days, a small stone in his shoe.

The hold door recognized his hand before his hand finished rising.

Inside, the cold was different. Older. The environmental unit in the hold ran three degrees below the rest of the ship and had run that way for four years, and the air had the mineral stillness of a place where nothing was permitted to change. Forty-one cradles stood in their long rows, each one a matte-black sarcophagus the size of a coffin, each one humming at a frequency just below hearing — a hum Beatrix had stopped being able to hear as sound years ago and now heard as pressure, as a hand laid flat against the inside of his skull.

Forty-one sleepers. Forty-one archived minds, each one a complete recording of a human being: every memory, every scar, every private joke and half-finished argument, every version of themselves they had ever been. Legally, they were contraband — consciousness backups without transfer licenses, without estate clearance, without the Directorate's stamp of approved grief. Morally, they were something the law had no word for. Unburied dead. Unanswered letters. A city's worth of last words, sealed in cold and carried by a disgraced navigator across a blockade that would have melted every cradle rather than let them through.

He had stolen them. He reminded himself of this sometimes, standing here, because it was easier than the truer word, which was *saved*, and harder than the truest word, which was *abandoned everyone who couldn't fit*.

He set the case down between cradle nine and cradle ten, and knelt, and did not open it right away. That was ritual now. He let his eyes move down the rows the way a priest moves them down a congregation. He knew all forty-one names. He had made himself learn them in the first year, when the guilt was a fire and learning names was the only water he had: the Okafor cousins — no relation to the captain, though he'd never had the courage to ask — the twins from the agri-dome, the schoolteacher whose name he said aloud on the anniversary of the blockade because she had once taught his sister. And in cradle twelve, the one he had come for, the one he had been circling for three nights like a moth that couldn't decide whether the lamp was salvation or death:

*SEN, AMARA K. — Xenolinguistics Division, Meridian Institute for Deep Signal Studies.*

*Status: deceased, Meridian, Year One. Archive integrity: 99.97%.*

He had chosen her the way you choose a doctor — carefully, and because you have no other choice. Of the forty-one, she was the only one whose work touched the thing he had found in the nine seconds of silence. She had spent eleven years listening to the deep sky. If anyone dead could tell him what was speaking through the gravity of a whole star system, it was her.

And to ask her, he was going to do the thing that had gotten the last man who did it disappeared.

* * *

The neural bridge was a band of dark polymer that seated against the back of the skull, over the implant cluster, with the intimacy of a held head. Beatrix had his own implants — that was the whole obscene joke of his life, that the crime that had ended his career was also the only reason he could do what needed doing. Navigation-grade memory architecture, military surplus, the kind of hardware the Directorate had banned for civilian use after the Third Scandal, the kind that let a pilot back up the contents of his own mind the way you'd back up a chart room. He had four years of backups stored in the hold too, in cradle forty-two, the one nobody else knew was a cradle. Forty-two sleepers. The last one was him.

He seated the bridge. It woke against his skin with a warmth that felt almost like a hand.

"Consent protocols are a formality," he said aloud, to no one, because saying it aloud was the only way to make it not a lie. "You can't consent. You're dead. I'm sorry. I'm going to do it anyway, and I'm going to carry it."

The cradle's maintenance panel came open under his thumbs. Inside, beneath a frost of coolant vapor, the archive core turned slowly on its gimbal, a sphere of grey lattice no larger than a fist, holding a woman. He thought of it that way and could not make himself think of it any other way, though he knew every rational account said *data*, said *recording*, said *not a person, only the shape a person left*. But he had once, in a training simulation, watched a backup wake, and he had seen the moment the archived mind oriented itself inside the playback and looked at the technician with eyes that were only light on a screen, and had said, in a voice that was only a speaker, *Where is my daughter?* — and no rational account had ever survived that sentence in Beatrix's hearing.

He jacked the bridge to the core. Ran the handshake. The ship's power draw spiked for half a second — he'd throttled it through a dummy circuit routed past the engineering logs, but he knew, with a sick certainty he didn't examine, that Ilsa's instruments would have felt it the way a sleeping animal feels a change in the wind.

Then he opened the archive, and Dr. Amara Sen woke up.

* * *

There was no conversation. That was the first thing people never understood about archived minds. You didn't talk to them; you *walked into them*. The bridge laid her recorded memory down over his living one like a second set of clothes, and for the duration of the interface he was two people wearing one skin — himself, Beatrix Montrose, disgraced, hiding, afraid — and her, Amara Sen, forty-nine years old, standing in a listening station on the Meridian high plateau with snow coming down past the windows like the sky was shedding its skin.

He had expected fragments. Archives degraded at the edges; you got the strong memories first, the ones the mind had rehearsed. What he got instead was a Tuesday.

That was the horror of it, and the gift. Her memory didn't open on the discovery. It opened on a Tuesday in the spring of the last peacetime year, and she was tired, and the coffee in the listening station was bad, and a junior researcher named Pell was arguing with her about harmonic drift, and outside the window the snow was doing the thing snow did on Meridian, falling upward along the magnetized field lines before it gave up and came down. She was annoyed. She had a paper due. Her boots were wet.

*Run it again,* she said, in the memory, in her own voice, and Beatrix felt her mouth make the shape of the words, felt her impatience in his own throat. *The drift isn't noise. Noise doesn't have a schedule.*

The listening station. He let the memory carry him, because there was nothing to do inside an archive but be carried. The Meridian Institute for Deep Signal Studies — he had walked past its gates a hundred times in his life and never once wondered what happened inside. He knew now what the Directorate's tribunals had said happened inside: weapons research. Gravitic manipulation. The forbidden experiments that had justified the blockade, the war, the slow strangulation of a world of eight million people. *Meridian built a weapon,* the Core broadcasts had said, for four years, in the voice of a patient parent explaining an unavoidable punishment. *We had no choice.*

In Amara Sen's memory, on a Tuesday, with wet boots and bad coffee, Meridian built a listener.

The array spread across the plateau behind the station like the skeleton of a buried cathedral — two hundred kilometers of superconducting filament laid into the rock, tuned not to light, not to radio, but to the slow grammar of spacetime itself. Gravitational listening. The oldest signal channel in the universe, the one nothing could jam, because the medium was reality. She had spent eleven years teaching the array to subtract the universe's noise — the binary stars, the colliding black holes, the endless tectonic grumble of a living planet — and to listen underneath all of it for anything that *patterned*.

And on that Tuesday, Pell ran the drift again, and the drift was still there, and Amara Sen stood up from her desk so fast she knocked her coffee into her own lap and did not notice.

*It's repeating,* Pell said, and his voice in the memory had the particular hollowness of a person standing at the edge of something. *Doctor. It's repeating on a sixty-one hour period.*

Sixty-one hours.

Beatrix's living body, kneeling in the hold of the *Hazel*, went cold from the inside. His heart slammed. The bridge compensated, damping his adrenaline so the archive wouldn't smear, but it couldn't damp the knowing. Sixty-one hours. The nine-second fluctuation he had hidden in a shielded data core under his bunk, the pattern he had spent three nights telling himself was an instrument artifact, a Core test, a natural resonance of the blockade's mass-shadow — it cycled every sixty-one hours. He had measured it himself. He had not let himself understand what he'd measured.

He let the memory run forward, because there was no other direction to go.

* * *

The weeks in her memory moved the way weeks move in memory: unevenly, some hours dense as lead and whole days gone like they'd been stolen. He lived inside her urgency. He felt what it was to find a signal in the deep grammar of spacetime — not a message, not at first, not words, nothing so human, but *structure*. Repetition with variation. The way a mind repeats a thing when it wants to be understood. The signal came from far outside the system, from the direction of nothing in particular, the empty quarter where the star charts offered only dark, and it was old — the redshift said centuries — and it was *addressed*. Not to Meridian. Not to humanity. Addressed in the way a lighthouse is addressed: broadcast to anything with the patience to notice, and the grammar to read.

*It's not language,* she told the Institute's directors, in a memory so strong he could feel the sweat at her hairline, could feel the specific weight of the room's attention on her. *Language implies a shared code. This is pre-linguistic. It's mathematics the way a heartbeat is mathematics. Whatever sent it isn't talking to us. It's* singing *to itself, and we are close enough to hear.*

And the directors — Beatrix felt her read their faces, and through her eyes he read them too, and what he saw in those faces frightened him more than the signal had frightened her. Not wonder. Not fear of the dark. Something smaller and more human. *Opportunity.*

The Institute's funding came half from Meridian's colonial government and half — quietly, through three shell foundations — from Core interests. He hadn't known that. She hadn't known it either, not until later, and the later was coming in the memory now, arriving the way disaster always arrives in memory: already knowing, watching your past self not know.

Because the signal was beautiful, and the signal was publishable, and the signal was — this was the word that appeared in the internal memos, and he felt her stomach turn as she read them — *strategic*. A gravitational channel that could not be jammed. A signal that propagated through spacetime itself, readable by any array of sufficient sensitivity, deniable by any government of sufficient cynicism. The Core's strategic analysts looked at her listener and did not see a window onto the deep sky. They saw a weapons program waiting to be claimed. And Meridian's own defense ministry, terrified, starving for leverage against a Core that had already begun its slow economic squeeze, looked at the same listener and made the same mistake from the other direction.

*They're going to say we built a transmitter,* she said to Pell, late, in the dark of the station, in one of the last memories before the last memories. *We built a telescope. But you can't tell the difference from outside, and nobody out there wants to know the difference. A listener and a weapon look identical from a hundred light-years away, and a listener and a weapon look identical from the Directorate too, and that's — that's the whole tragedy, isn't it. It's not the signal. It's that we've all forgotten how to tell a voice from a gun.*

Beatrix knelt in the hold and wept inside another woman's memory, and the bridge kept his body still, and the coolant hummed, and forty other dead waited in their rows.

* * *

He stayed under for as long as the hardware allowed — four hours, maybe five; time went strange in an archive, because her time and his time braided together and neither one would tell him which was real. He walked her through the last year. He watched the Institute get nationalized, then militarized, then *audited*, that soft word that meant men in Core livery walking the plateau with instruments, deciding what the world deserved to keep. He watched her hide the array's true calibration data inside a linguistic model, a dead language she'd reconstructed for a paper no one would ever read, the signal's grammar folded into the grammar of a language with no living speakers — hiding the truth inside a language, because she was a xenolinguist, and that was the only vault she knew how to build.

And he watched her, in the final weeks of the blockade, do the thing that had brought him here. He watched her record herself. Sit down in the archive cradle in the Institute's medical wing — the same model, he realized, the same make, the same hum, as the cradle twelve he was kneeling beside — and speak her testament into the cold. Not a confession. A map. *If anyone ever hears the signal again,* she said, to the future, to him, *and if anyone ever tells you Meridian built a weapon — come find me. I kept the proof in the only language they can't burn.*

The archive ended where archives end: mid-thought, mid-life, a woman stopped like a clock. Beatrix surfaced into his own body gasping, his face wet, his hands shaking against the cradle's rim, the hold's cold rushing back in like water into a footprint.

He sat on the deck plating for a long time.

The signal was real. The signal was old, and patient, and it had been singing to itself for centuries, and Meridian had heard it first — had heard it and been destroyed for hearing it, its listener called a weapon, its death called a necessity, its eight million called collateral in a war built on a lie so large it had become the architecture of the sky. And now the signal was back, or had never left, cycling every sixty-one hours through the gravity of the whole system like a voice repeating itself to a room that had stopped listening.

And it was changing. He had the data. He had hidden it under his bunk like contraband, and it was changing, slightly, each cycle — repetition with variation, the way a mind repeats a thing when it wants to be understood.

*Someone or something is speaking,* he had written in his log, and had been afraid of the sentence. Now the sentence was the least of it. Because Amara's memory held one more thing, one fragment from the last days, one line she had said to Pell in the dark that the tribunal records had never captured, that no broadcast had ever carried, that lived only here, in cradle twelve, in a dead woman's Tuesday:

*It's not just repeating,* she had said. *The variations are converging. Whatever it is — it's finishing a sentence. And I think it's been waiting for someone to answer.*

Beatrix looked down the rows of the hold, at the forty-one cradles humming their sub-audible hymn, at the cold that kept the dead patient, and understood with total, sickening clarity that he was going to have to open every one of them.

Above him, faint through the deck, the ship's intercom chimed once — Okafor's voice, level as always, calling all hands.

"Montrose. Bridge. We've got traffic on the long-range scope, and you're going to want to see this."

He wiped his face with the back of his hand, sealed cradle twelve, and picked up the case with hands that would not quite stop trembling.
