## 10. Sixty-One Hours

The pulse came at 0347 ship-time, and Beatrix knew it before the instruments did.

He had been asleep in his bunk with one hand curled around a data slate like a child holding a toy it refuses to surrender, and he woke not to sound but to absence — a wrongness in the hum of the deck plates, a fractional lightening in his chest, as if the Hazel had exhaled. Nine seconds. He counted them against his own heartbeat, the way you count the gap between lightning and thunder, and when gravity settled back into its grooves he was already swinging his legs off the bunk, already moving.

Down the corridor. Past Ilsa's cabin, where the light under her door said she was awake too — of course she was; she felt everything the ship felt — past the galley where the coffee bulb he'd left warming had gone cold in nine seconds of weightlessness and now hung at an angle that defied explanation. He didn't stop. He climbed the ladder to the sensor blister with the economy of a man who has rehearsed this exact route in his head for sixty-one hours, because he had.

The console was still recording. It always recorded; that was the first lie he'd told himself, that the equipment would do the work so he wouldn't have to watch. But watching was all he'd done for two days, and now the waveform bloomed across the screen in amber threads, and Beatrix Montrose sat down slowly, because this one was different.

The first pulse — the one over Meridian, nine seconds of silence that had stopped every heart in the system — had been raw. A shout into a cupped hand. This one was *shaped*. The gravitational fluctuation carried structure the way a river carries silt: invisible until you looked at what it left behind. And what it left behind, rendered in the false-color grammar of his instruments, looked almost like language.

He ran the comparison twice. Then a third time, hands steady in the way they only became when panic went so deep it came out the other side as calm. The amplitude had increased by a factor of eleven. The source bearing had shifted — the first pulse had seemed to come from everywhere, from the spacetime substrate itself, diffuse as weather. This one had a direction. A point of origin somewhere in the outer dark beyond Meridian's orbit, precise enough to plot, close enough to be a place rather than a phenomenon.

And threaded through the fluctuation, repeating with a regularity no natural process permitted, was a sequence he had seen before. Not heard. Seen. In the hold, on the cold-storage manifest, in the header block of forty-one encrypted memory archives: a coordinate string, a shipping designation, a berth assignment.

*Hazel.*

His own handwriting on the smuggler's manifest, transcribed by something that spoke through gravity itself.

Beatrix sat very still for a long time. The blister's viewport showed him Meridian below, the colony's night side stitched with the few stubborn lights of the hydroponics levels — fewer than last month, he noted, and hated himself for noting it, the way a witness always hates the accuracy of his own eyes. Beyond it, the stars. Somewhere out there, something had just said his ship's name in a voice made of falling.

*They know we're here.*

He didn't let himself finish the thought. Not yet. First: verify. Second: hide. Those were the two verbs of his life now, and he performed them in order, with the discipline of ritual.

* * *

Verification took four hours and cost him more than he wanted to admit. He cross-referenced the coordinate string against the manifest three ways — raw text, hashed, and encoded in the backup headers' checksum architecture — and all three matched to the last character. The probability of coincidence was not small. It was absurd. It was the kind of number you got when the universe was trying to make a point.

Which meant the sender knew about the backups. Knew their designations, their encryption scheme, the specific bureaucratic fingerprint of Meridian's archive authority. Knew them well enough to recite them back like a password — or like a threat. *I know what you're carrying. I know where you're carrying it.*

But there was a second layer, and this was the part that made him get up and pace the blister until his knees ached. The signal wasn't just addressed. It was *keyed*. Buried in the variation between repetitions — the same repetition-with-variation he'd catalogued in the first pulse, the thing that had convinced him someone was speaking rather than shouting — was a modulation pattern that matched, exactly, the neural encoding format of memory-backup implants. His implants. The illegal lattice of stored selves woven through his hippocampus, the contraband inside the contraband, the thing he had never logged, never mentioned, never even thought about in words when anyone might be listening.

The signal wasn't just talking to the Hazel. It was talking to *him*. To the specific architecture of his mind. Someone out there knew what he was — not the smuggler, not the disgraced navigator, but the man with forty-one dead colonists sleeping in his hold and a forty-second ghost living behind his eyes.

Beatrix pressed his forehead against the cold viewport glass and breathed until the fog of his breath made a small gray galaxy on the pane.

There were perhaps six people alive who knew about his implants. Two of them were dead. One was on Meridian, possibly dead, possibly worse. The other three he had spent four years making sure could never find him. And none of them — none — could speak through the fabric of spacetime.

Unless they hadn't built the voice. Unless they'd merely learned to use a listener that someone else had built, the way a thief learns to drive a stolen car.

The thought arrived whole, unbidden, and it fit so perfectly into the shape of everything he didn't know that he actually laughed — a short, ugly sound in the empty blister. Amara Sen. The xenolinguist sleeping in cradle seven. Whatever she had recorded before the blockade, whatever truth sat coiled in her archived mind, it had just become the only map to the territory he was standing in.

He would have to wake her again. Deeper this time. And waking her meant power draws in the hold, and power draws meant Ilsa Voss asking questions with her engineer's flat, patient eyes, and questions were a luxury the Hazel could no longer afford.

Because there was a third thing in the signal. He'd saved it for last the way you save the worst news, and now he made himself look at it directly. At the tail of each repetition, beneath the coordinates and the keying pattern, the fluctuation decayed in a way that instruments kept flagging as anomalous. He'd assumed it was noise. It wasn't noise. It was a countdown signature — a decaying envelope, mathematical and patient, marking intervals of exactly sixty-one hours. Three pulses so far, if the first one over Meridian had been the opening bell. The interval was shrinking. Not much. But shrinking, the way a drumroll shrinks.

Something was counting down, and it was using his ship's name as one of the numbers.

* * *

He hid the data the way he always did: split across three innocuous files, disguised as navigation corrections and reactor telemetry and — this was the flourish he was quietly proud of — a fragment of Ilsa's own maintenance scheduling code, because no one on this ship would ever voluntarily open Ilsa's maintenance schedules. Then he sat in the galley at 0600 with a fresh coffee bulb he couldn't taste and waited for the crew.

Okafor came through first, as she always did, moving with the deliberate calm of a captain who believed — who needed to believe — that her ship was boring. She nodded to him. He nodded back. Four years of nods, each one a small treaty ratified between people keeping each other's secrets without naming them. Did she know? He'd wondered for two years. He'd decided it didn't matter, and then wondered again every single day.

Deklan Trask shuffled in after, smelling of engine degreaser and the previous night's cheap amphetamines, complaining before he'd fully cleared the hatch about a coolant loop that was "haunting" him. Beatrix watched him the way he'd started watching everyone lately — measuring, weighing, looking for the hairline crack that pressure finds first. Trask owed money to people in three systems. Trask talked in his sleep. Trask had been asking, casually, over cards, how long the Hazel planned to stay in this system.

*I'm doing it again,* Beatrix thought, and made himself stop. Paranoia was a solvent; it dissolved everything eventually, including the things holding you together.

Ilsa came last. She stood in the hatchway a moment longer than usual, coffee bulb in both hands, looking at him with an expression he couldn't parse — and then she crossed the galley and sat down across from him, which she never did, which broke some unspoken seating arrangement the crew had maintained for years like ballast.

"You were up in the blister," she said. Not a question.

"I couldn't sleep."

"The whole ship couldn't sleep." She turned the bulb in her hands. "Nine seconds. You feel it in your teeth, you know? Like the ship forgot us for a moment." Her eyes came up, and they were not suspicious, exactly. They were *careful*, which was worse. "Second time in sixty-one hours. Almost to the minute. That's not weather, Beatrix. That's a clock."

He held her gaze and gave her the truth, or the shell of it. "Some kind of pulsar interaction with the debris field. I'm logging it for the charts."

"Pulsars don't keep appointments." She said it mildly, sipping her coffee, and then stood, and paused beside him on her way out — close enough that he caught the machine oil under her skin, the smell of someone who lived in the ship's bones. "Whatever it is," she said quietly, "the reactor drew extra from the reserve cells right when it hit. Both times. Like something answered it." She let that sit in the air between them, terrible and precise, and walked out.

Beatrix stared at the table. *Like something answered it.* The hold. The cold storage. Forty-one sleepers drawing current in the dark while gravity stuttered overhead — and he had told himself the draw was thermal cycling, had adjusted the logs himself, had built the lie with his own hands.

Had the backups responded to the signal? Or had the signal been calling *to* them?

He finished his coffee without tasting it, and went down to the hold.

* * *

The hold at cycle-dark was a cathedral built by cowards. That was how he'd come to think of it — the racks rising in dim blue ranks, the frost-fog drifting low across the deck plating, the hum of the cryo units pitched just below hearing, felt in the sternum rather than the ears. Forty-one cradles. Forty-one people who had died on Meridian and refused, in the only way the law allowed them to refuse, to stay buried there.

He walked the rows without touching anything, the way you walk a cemetery. Cradle seven was third from the portside bulkhead, and its status light burned the same patient green as all the others, but Beatrix stopped in front of it anyway and stood there like a man outside a door he's afraid to knock on.

"Dr. Sen," he said aloud. His voice sounded obscene in here, too large, too alive. "I need you to tell me what you found. Before the blockade. Before any of it." He swallowed. "I think it's found me instead."

The cradle hummed. Frost crawled along its seams in slow fractal ferns. He had woken her once already — a shallow surfacing, ninety minutes of archived memory played back through his implant interface, enough to learn that she'd been part of the Deep Listening project, enough to leave him shaking. Waking her properly meant hours of interface. Meant power. Meant risk. Meant going deeper into a mind that had recorded things worth killing a colony over.

Meant admitting, finally, that he was not the smuggler running this cargo. He was the cargo. He was the last living piece of the delivery, and something out in the dark had just read his address label.

Beatrix put his palm flat against cradle seven's lid. The cold bit through his skin, honest and simple, the only uncomplicated thing aboard.

Sixty-one hours. He had sixty-one hours — less, now, the interval shrinking — to decide whether to answer a voice that spoke in falling, or to run, and he already knew, with the weary certainty of a man who has read his own ending in the shape of his choices, that the Hazel had never once in her long ugly life run from anything she should have.

Behind him, at the top of the hold ladder, a floor plate creaked.

Someone was standing in the dark, watching him stand among the dead.
