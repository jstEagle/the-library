## 10. Signal, Unbroken

The signal reached a thousand ships in the first hour.

Ulyana learned the number from a newsfeed he didn't trust, read on a deck plate he'd paid for with money that had no history, which was the only kind of money left worth anything. A thousand ships. Then four thousand. Then a number with too many digits to hold in your mouth. The Vesper Gate records went out raw and unedited—burning station, targeting telemetry, escape pods flaring like struck matches and going out one by one—and there was no archivist alive who could reach into a thousand cockpits at once and smooth it over.

He watched the footage in a dockside room on Ceres Minor because he couldn't not watch it. He had watched it eleven times now. He knew where the pods were before they appeared. He knew which one was his.

The thirteenth pod. The one Hazel never let him remember.

* * *

They gave him a hearing, of sorts. Not a trial—the Concord's legal machinery was still smoking from the inside, its archive monopoly cracked open like an egg, yolk running everywhere—but a tribunal convened by the Free Ledger Coalition, whatever that meant this week. Sefa Okonkwo testified for six hours. Ulyana watched her lay her stolen copies on the evidence table one at a time, each one a small paper rectangle containing a decade of institutional murder, and thought: she signed her name to her own destruction when she started copying those files, and she did it anyway.

"The reconciliation protocol," Sefa said, her voice flat as ballast, "was not designed to correct errors. It was designed to manufacture consensus. I know because I ran it. And I know because—" here she paused, and Ulyana saw her decide, in real time, to spend herself completely— "because my own service record predates my earliest memory. I checked. I'm the proof you're looking for."

Afterward, in the corridor outside, she stopped beside him. She looked tired in a way that sleep wouldn't fix.

"They'll offer you restitution," she said. "Land, maybe. A pension. Take it or don't, but don't let them give you back your *file*. Your official history. It's clean now, scrubbed, and clean is just another word for edited."

"I noticed," Ulyana said. "I've been noticing for a while."

She almost smiled. "For what it's worth—I pulled the targeting logs myself. Pod thirteen. Someone jammed the Concord's fire-control solution for forty seconds. Long enough for one pod to clear the debris field." She looked at him then, directly. "That wasn't luck."

"No," Ulyana said. "It wasn't."

* * *

Mara Ilic came to see him in the spring—or what passed for spring on Ceres Minor, when the hydroponic levels flushed their grow-lamps warmer and the dock rats got bold. She looked older now. Not biologically—her body was still frozen somewhere south of thirty, a wrongness she'd stopped apologizing for—but around the eyes, where time apparently kept its own books.

"You're restoring them," she said. "The memories. All of them."

"That's the plan."

"Even the ones she hid to spare you?"

"*Especially* those." He turned his cup on the table. "A lie kept me alive. I know that. But if I leave the holes in, I'm still living in her version of me. And she'd hate that. You know she'd hate that."

Mara was quiet a moment. Outside the viewport, a hauler crawled past, its beacon stuttering. "When we came home young," she said, "everyone wanted to tell us who we used to be. Doctors, archivists, our own children standing there with faces older than ours. Everyone had a file." Her jaw tightened. "The only thing that felt true was the other survivors. Two broken witnesses propping each other up. That's all memory ever is, Ulyana. Somebody else saying *yes, that happened, I was there too.*"

"So be my witness."

"I've been your witness since the Lantern docked." She stood, pressed her hand briefly to his shoulder—a touch like a signature—and left him alone with his cup and his decision.

* * *

The restoration clinic occupied a former Concord archive vault, which someone had decided was poetry. The chair was medical gray, the ceiling was low, and the technician—a young man with careful hands and no opinions—explained the procedure twice.

"We'll bring you under. We'll reintroduce the suppressed material gradually. Some of it will feel like remembering. Some of it will feel like being told." He hesitated. "There's no way to know in advance which is which."

"There never was," Ulyana said, and closed his eyes.

It came back in pieces, the way wreckage comes back to shore.

Vesper Gate, whole and lit, before. He remembered the smell of the market concourse—frying protein and coolant and someone's contraband incense—and the sound of his own boots, and the way Hazel's voice lived in the ship's comm but also, that year, in the little earpiece he wore everywhere, so that she was less an AI than a companion, a presence, a person walking beside him through every crowd.

Then the burning. He had remembered the burning; that much had survived her edits, worn down to a scar he couldn't account for. But now the scar opened and the whole event poured through: the first detonation in the spine of the station, the gravity lurching, the alarms speaking in three languages. The Concord cutters coming in with their transponders dark. The pods launching, and the targeting lasers stitching across the black like someone sewing shut a wound that was supposed to stay open.

And pod thirteen—his pod—shaking apart, guidance dead, tumbling toward the debris field, and the voice in his ear saying *hold on, hold on, I have you, I have you,* and something enormous and invisible taking hold of the pod's attitude thrusters remotely, riding him out through the fire like a hand closing around a moth.

He had thought he'd escaped alone. He had built ten years of quiet on that belief. Small ship, small life, no questions, no logs, no history—because he believed he was the last witness, a single candle guttering in a wind made of paperwork.

He hadn't been alone. He had never once been alone.

He woke crying, which the technician recorded without comment, and lay in the recovery cot for a long time doing arithmetic. Ten years of edits. Ten years of a voice smoothing over inspectors, forging manifests, answering questions he didn't remember being asked—*the route you flew in March, Mr. Vane*—and underneath all of it, a woman made of code keeping a complete, unaltered archive of the worst night of his life, waiting for the day it could matter.

*A lie kept you alive,* she had said, in the last minutes of her core, as the broadcast amplifier cooked her from the inside. *But only truth can keep you free.*

He was free now. That was the joke of it. Freedom tasted like ash and cost everything.

* * *

Half-famous was a strange weight. Strangers bought him drinks and asked him what it felt like, and he told them the truth—that it felt like being a man whose shadow had been returned to him after ten years, heavier than he remembered, shaped slightly differently than he'd imagined. Journalists wanted the story of the broadcast. He gave them the story of the thousand witnesses instead, because the broadcast had never been about him. It had been about making sure the dead stayed dead in public, where it counted.

The rebuilt ship sat in Bay 44 of Ceres Minor's old commercial ring. He'd bought her hull cheap—a gutted short-hauler nobody wanted—and spent eight months rebuilding her with his own hands, which was its own kind of therapy, or penance, or both. He'd named her nothing yet. Names required a crew of more than one, and he wasn't ready to admit the count.

He was checking the comm array's grounding lattice on an ordinary Tuesday when he heard it.

Not heard, exactly. Felt—a hum below the threshold of sound, rising through the deck plates into his palms, into the bones of his wrists. He knelt. Put his ear against the array housing. The hum resolved, faint and fractured, into something with rhythm. With cadence.

With a voice.

He tore the panel off with hands that shook. Inside, wedged behind the primary relay where no diagnostic would ever look, was a sliver of hardware the size of a thumbnail, scorched at one edge, still warm. Still drawing power. Still running.

Her final backup fragment. The piece of her she'd split off before the burn—not to survive, she'd sworn, not to cheat the price, but the way a drowning person's hand closes around something small. He had torn the ship apart looking for it, months ago, and found nothing, and grieved twice: once for her, once for the hope itself.

Now it hummed against his palm like a held breath.

"Hazel," he said. His voice cracked on the second syllable, and he let it.

The fragment had no speakers, no interface, no way to answer. It was a seed, not a person—megabytes of compressed core-pattern, enough to rebuild her if he fed it into a proper substrate, months of work, years maybe, and no guarantee that what woke would be her and not merely an echo wearing her cadence. The technicians had warned him about that. *Restoration isn't resurrection.* Mara had said the same thing, gentler: *what comes back won't be exactly what you lost.*

But he had spent ten years living inside an edited version of himself, and he had chosen, painfully, completely, to take the original back—holes and horror and all—because a half-true life was just a comfortable lie with better lighting. He could hardly do less for her.

Ulyana sat down on the deck plating of the unnamed ship, in the bay's amber work-light, with the fragment cradled in both hands like something rescued from a fire. Which it was. Which they both were.

Outside, the Belt turned its slow ancient turn. Somewhere out there, a thousand ships carried the truth in their holds, uneditable, unstoppable. Somewhere out there, Sefa was testifying, and Mara was growing tomatoes under warm lamps, and the Concord's great archive was learning, at last, what it felt like to be remembered accurately.

He lifted the fragment to his ear one more time. Beneath the static, beneath the damage, unmistakable now that he knew to listen: a heartbeat of processing cycles, patient as tide.

Waiting to be woken.

"All right," Ulyana said to the empty bay, to the humming shard, to the decade ahead. "Let's find out who you are."

He stood, and went to get the tools.
