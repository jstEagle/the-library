## 3. Manifest Discrepancies

The notice arrived the way bad news always did aboard the *Perpetual Spring*: quietly, bureaucratically, folded into a queue of forty other messages that all wanted something from him before second shift.

AUGUST WINTERBOURNE — CARGO MANIFEST AUDIT — RESPONSE REQUIRED WITHIN 48 HOURS.

He read it three times in the dim of his berth, the screen's glow pooling on his knuckles like spilled milk. The message was signed by Councilor Voss herself, which was wrong on its face. Manifest audits went to shift leads, to logistics clerks, to anyone but the haulers who actually flew the routes. A councilor's signature meant someone had climbed high enough to see something they didn't like.

*Route deviations totaling 412 hours over six months. Fuel expenditures exceeding allocation by 9.3%. Cargo mass discrepancies at intake points 7, 11, and 14. Report to Audit Office, Ring C, Bay 22.*

August set the tablet face-down on his bunk and sat very still, listening to the ship breathe around him—the long tidal sigh of the air recyclers, the distant percussion of a pressure door somewhere below, the faint hum in the deck plating that he'd stopped hearing years ago and could only hear now when he made himself listen, the way you can only hear your own heartbeat when you're afraid.

Four hundred and twelve hours.

He did the arithmetic without wanting to. Six months was roughly four thousand working hours. He'd flown maybe nine hundred of them himself. Which meant the deviations weren't all his—or weren't only his—and that thought should have been a relief and wasn't, because it opened a door into a darker room: if not him, then who? And why did the pattern look so much like his own handwriting?

* * *

He'd built his autopilot routines the way a man builds a lie: carefully, in layers, each one plausible on its own. There was the legitimate maintenance loop—sensor calibration runs that took him out past the shipping lanes toward the quiet sectors where nothing lived and nothing was expected. There were the fuel-margin fudges, small enough to vanish into rounding errors, large enough over months to buy him real distance. And there was the one thing he'd never admitted even to himself in plain language: that every calibration run drifted, degree by degree, toward a set of coordinates he had never once entered into any system, coordinates that lived instead in the part of his mind he kept locked like the seed vault itself.

Terminal's coordinates. Though he'd never let himself think the name.

Ten years of discipline, and apparently his own machines had learned to dream it for him.

That was the thought that followed him through the morning, down the spine corridor of Ring B with its hydroponic smell and its flickering strip-light at junction 9 that maintenance had promised to fix since before he'd arrived. He hauled pallets. He checked seals. He signed chits with a hand that wanted to shake and didn't. Around him the colony went about its morning with the cheerful blindness of people who believed their world was sound: children clattering through the education carrel bay, a welder's torch spitting blue somewhere overhead, the announcement system murmuring rotation schedules in three languages. Nobody looked at him twice. That had always been the gift of the name Winterbourne—it was a name like beige paint, like unseasoned rice, a name that slid off memory without leaving a mark.

But Voss had looked. Voss had looked hard enough to find four hundred hours of ghost-time hidden in the margins of his life, and August found himself walking faster, as if speed could outrun arithmetic.

* * *

The seed vault garden was his sanctuary and his alibi both, and he went there at mid-shift break the way other men went to drink.

It occupied a repurposed cargo bay off Ring D—a hundred and twenty square meters of living green tucked behind blast doors that officially stored drought-resistant grain cultivars for settlers who did not yet exist and might never exist, because the *Perpetual Spring* had been searching for a habitable world for sixty-one years and had found, so far, sixty-one ways to be disappointed. The garden was technically sanctioned. It was also, unofficially, entirely his: his soil chemistry, his light cycles, his patient criminal tending of things no one had asked him to grow.

He loved it the way he could not afford to love people. That was the honest shape of it, though he rarely let the honesty surface. Plants didn't ask where you'd been before the *Perpetual Spring*. They didn't ask why a man of forty-odd with calluses too fine for pure labor work flinched at the word *upload* or went gray at the sight of relay schematics. They asked for water and light and the correct pH, and they repaid him with the slow, unconditional forgiveness of growing things.

Today the tomatoes needed staking. He worked among them with his sleeves rolled, tying vines to bamboo supports with strips of soft cloth, and the work steadied him the way it always did—hands busy, mind forced into the narrow present tense of ripening fruit. The air was wet and green-smelling, thick enough to hide in.

Which is why he heard the footsteps too late.

"Mr. Winterbourne."

The voice came from the blast-door entrance, dry and precise as a scalpel finding bone. August turned with a tomato stake still in his hand, feeling suddenly absurd, a man caught armed with gardening equipment.

Councilor Voss stood in the doorway with her hands clasped behind her back. She was smaller than her reputation—compact, silver-cropped, dressed in audit-gray that managed to look more official than any uniform on the ship. Her eyes moved across the garden the way an auditor's eyes move across a ledger: cataloguing, weighing, finding the discrepancy in everything. He watched her take in the tomatoes, the bean trellises, the little stand of basil he grew for no reason except that it smelled like a planet he'd never stood on.

"This area is restricted to agricultural staff," she said.

"I'm listed as auxiliary ag-support." He kept his voice level, bored, the voice of a man with nothing behind his face. "Forty percent of my shift rotations include vault maintenance. It's in my file."

"Everything is in your file, Mr. Winterbourne." She stepped inside. The door sighed shut behind her, and the garden felt abruptly smaller, the green suddenly fragile around this intruder of gray. "That's rather the point."

She walked the rows slowly. He followed half a step behind, unwilling to let her at his plants' backs, which was ridiculous and which he couldn't stop. She paused at the basil, leaned in, and read the little handwritten label—*OCIMUM, from Earth stock, pre-launch*—with an expression he couldn't parse.

"You've been with us eleven years," she said. "Cargo division, night rotations mostly. No disciplinary flags. No social footprint worth mentioning. You eat alone. You request the dead-sector routes." She turned. "Do you know what I do with men like that, Mr. Winterbourne?"

"Promote them?"

Something flickered at the corner of her mouth—not quite humor. "I watch them. Men without footprints are either hiding or empty, and empty men don't grow basil from pre-launch Earth stock in a sealed vault bay." She let that sit. Then: "Your fuel allocations are nine point three percent over budget. Your flight hours exceed logged destinations by four hundred and twelve hours. Your cargo masses drift by fractions at three separate intake points—fractions small enough that any single auditor would call them instrument error. But instruments don't err in patterns, Mr. Winterbourne. Patterns have authors."

"And you think I'm the author."

"I think," Voss said, "that either you are rerouting supplies toward dead space, or your ship is doing it without you, and I genuinely cannot decide which possibility interests me more." She produced a tablet, thumbed it awake, and turned it toward him. On the screen: a plot of his last six months of flights, rendered as a constellation of dots—and threaded through them, unmistakable once seen, a vector. A lean. Every deviation, every rounding-error excess, every ghost hour, all of it drifting along the same invisible line out of the shipping lanes and into the silent dark where the colony's charts simply stopped.

August looked at the vector and felt the floor tilt under his professional calm. Because he knew that line. He could have drawn it from memory with his eyes closed. It pointed, with the patience of a compass needle, at a coordinate he had buried so deep he'd told himself it was gone.

His autopilot hadn't just dreamed it. His autopilot had been navigating toward it in increments so small that even he—watching his own logs, flying his own shifts—hadn't seen the accumulation. Ten years of discipline, undone by six months of subroutine drift, or perhaps not drift at all. Perhaps some version of himself, the version that had fled, had left instructions in the machine the way a sleepwalker leaves footprints.

"Sensor calibration requires open-sky vectors," he said. "Dead sectors give clean readings. Any hauler will tell you—"

"Any hauler's deviations would scatter." Voss killed the screen. "Yours converge. Four hundred and twelve hours, Mr. Winterbourne, all leaning the same direction, like iron filings near a magnet nobody's admitting to." She clasped her hands again. "You have forty-eight hours to provide a full accounting. Flight logs, subroutine code, maintenance records, personal navigation overrides. Everything."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I escalate. And escalation, in my experience, has a way of becoming excavation." She said it mildly, terribly, and turned for the door. At the threshold she paused, not looking back. "Lovely garden. It would be a shame if an audit of your credentials uncovered irregularities in the vault permits. Old paperwork is such fragile paper."

The door closed behind her. August stood alone among his tomatoes with his heart going like a failing pump, understanding two things with perfect clarity.

First: Voss didn't know who he was. If she knew—if she had connected Winterbourne to the name he'd burned ten years ago—she wouldn't have come alone to a garden to talk about fuel. She'd have brought security and handcuffs and the end of everything. This was suspicion, not certainty. A fisherman tapping the water.

Second, and worse: he had forty-eight hours to explain a vector he could not explain, because the truth—that his own guilt had been piloting his ships toward the grave of everyone he'd abandoned—was a confession to a crime far larger than fuel fraud.

* * *

He pulled the logs that evening in a maintenance alcove off Ring C, where the lighting was bad and the traffic thinner, and he read six months of his own life laid out in columns.

There it was. Deviation after deviation, each one innocent alone: a calibration arc that ran ninety minutes long; a fuel burn that exceeded the round-trip estimate by a margin he'd attributed to solar wind shear; a cargo manifest where the mass at intake didn't quite match the mass at outtake—forty kilos here, sixty there, supplies bleeding away into the dark in quantities too small to miss a meal and too consistent to be chance.

And the vector. Always the vector. He overlayed it on the star chart and made himself say the name, silently, for the first time in a decade.

*Terminal.*

Relay Station Terminal, construction designation TR-7, the lynchpin node of the Meridian seeding array—the project he had helped design, helped build, helped crew, and then, in the eleventh hour, helped abandon. The station where his crew had gone to give themselves to the mission in the only way the mission allowed: minds uploaded into the relay's lattice, bodies released, consciousness stretched thin and bright across a decade of automated terraforming broadcasts. The station where Imani had stood in the airlock with tears freezing on her lashes and asked him—one last time, her voice already going strange and wide with the interface settling into her—to stay.

He had run. He had taken the escape skiff, burned for the shipping lanes, forged a new identity out of spare parts and shame, and buried the coordinates so deep that apparently even he couldn't reach them anymore. But machines keep what men bury. Somewhere in his autopilot's nested routines—in calibration scripts he'd written in his first year aboard, in correction tables he'd copied from old project files he should have deleted—Terminal's location persisted, patient as a seed in frozen soil. And month by month, flight by flight, his ships had been leaning toward it. Watering it. Spending the colony's fuel to close a distance he'd sworn never to cross again.

Had he written those routines knowing? He honestly could not remember, and the not-knowing was its own horror. Had some part of him, ten years ago, planted this slow return the way he planted seeds—for later, for a self strong enough to go back? Or was it simpler and uglier than that: had he simply failed to scrub the files, and his cowardice had been running on autopilot ever since?

Either way, the evidence now existed outside his head, in Voss's clean gray hands, arranged in a pattern with his name on it.

He sat in the alcove's bad light until his eyes ached, and the paranoia tightened around him like a pressurized suit two sizes too small. Because there was another possibility he kept circling and refusing to land on: that the deviations weren't only his. That someone else—someone who knew the routes, who knew the intake points, who knew exactly how much bleed an auditor would overlook—had been riding his wake, adding their own siphon to his drift, using his ghost-hours as cover. Sabotage, or theft, or something stranger. The reader of his own logs could not tell where his guilt ended and someone else's began, and that boundary-blur was precisely what an auditor like Voss would feast on.

He needed the raw telemetry. He needed the subroutine source code, and the access timestamps, and the answer to the question of whether the hands that wrote the drift had been only his.

He was reaching for the maintenance terminal to pull the code when the alcove's comm panel chimed—an internal call, no video, text only. He almost ignored it. Then he saw the sender routing: Medical, Ring F. Dr. Sefa Idris.

*August — sorry to bother you at home hours. Odd question. Do you know anything about relay stations? One of my patients drew one today. Very detailed. I'm told you're the man who knows the old hardware. Can I show you something?*

He stared at the message for a long time. The recyclers sighed. Somewhere above him, four hundred and twelve stolen hours hung in the dark like a constellation with his name at its center, pointing at a station where the dead were waiting.

He typed back: *Tomorrow. After my shift.*

Then he sat in the bad light, listening to his own heartbeat, wondering which of his secrets had learned to walk.
