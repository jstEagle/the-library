## 8. The Signal Strengthens

The dream came to Mara Osei in the middle of a mathematics lesson, in the middle of a sentence about orbital mechanics, and she stopped speaking because the garden had started singing.

She was thirty-four years old, a teacher, a woman who had not believed in anything unmeasurable since her grandmother died on the long haul out from Earth. She stood at the front of Classroom Six with her stylus hovering over the board, and the song rose through her like water through dry ground—not heard, exactly, but *remembered*, the way you remember a language you spoke before you could speak. Green light behind her eyelids. Soil that smelled of rain that had never fallen anywhere in this system. And underneath it all, a melody in a minor key that made her chest ache with a homesickness for a place she had never been.

"Mara?" said a child. Petal, one of the dreamers. The girl was watching her with an expression that was not surprise but recognition, the way you look at someone arriving late to a table where your seat has been kept.

"I'm fine," Mara said, and her voice came out strange, resonant, as though the classroom had grown walls of wood instead of polymer. "I'm—give me a moment."

She sat down at her own desk, in front of thirty students, and put her face in her hands, and let the song move through her, and was not afraid.

That was the part she would remember afterward, when the council came for her name and her classroom keys. She had not been afraid. She had been *welcomed*.

* * *

In the comms bay on Deck Fourteen, Technician Yusuf Adeyemi watched a carrier wave bloom out of dead space like a flower opening in timelapse.

He had been running routine spectral sweeps for six hours, the kind of work you did with half your mind while the other half composed letters you would never send to a woman on the agricultural ring. The sweep was boring. The sweep was always boring. The sweep was a waterfall of noise—pulsar hash, thermal static, the endless gossip of hydrogen—and then, at 0347 ship's time, the noise *organized*.

It didn't arrive. That was the thing he would struggle to explain to Voss's investigators later, the thing that made them look at him the way you look at a man who claims to have seen a ghost. The signal did not arrive from somewhere. It *condensed*, the way dew condenses, as though it had been in the noise all along and had simply decided, now, to be visible. A carrier wave, narrow-band, phase-coherent, rising out of a region of space marked on every chart in the ship with a single word: *TERMINAL*, and beneath it, in smaller type, the word that meant nothing and everything: *abandoned*.

Yusuf ran the verification twice. Then a third time. The signal was not drifting. It was not echoing. It was *pointed*—aimed down the long dark corridor of space at the Perpetual Spring with the precision of a hand cupped around a candle flame.

He should have reported it immediately. Protocol said immediately. Instead he sat for eleven minutes with his hand hovering over the transmit key, listening to the wave on the audio monitor, because he had routed it through the speakers the way you'd route any signal, and the carrier wave, demodulated to its baseline, was not a beep or a burst or a data handshake.

It was a hum. A low, patient, green hum, like a meadow holding its breath before dawn.

It sounded, though he would never say this aloud, never, not under questioning, not to save his career—it sounded like the humming his mother had done in the kitchen of his childhood, wordless, contented, the sound of a person who loves a thing growing.

At 0358, Yusuf Adeyemi pressed the transmit key and reported an anomalous carrier wave from dead space, origin unknown, and his voice, he noticed, was shaking in time with the hum.

* * *

August Winterbourne felt it in the garden.

He was on his knees in the seed vault at the dead end of third shift, thinning seedlings under grow-lights the color of late afternoon, when the change came. Not a sound. A *pressure*—the way a storm announces itself to the joints before the first thunder. The basil nearest the vault door had gone strange, its leaves trembling though the airflow was steady, and the tomato starts had all turned their cotyledons toward the same wall, the western wall, the wall that faced, if you drew the line far enough, out past the shipping lanes, past the buoys, past the edge of everything charted, into dead space.

August knelt there with soil under his nails and his heart going like a fist against a locked door.

*They're awake,* he thought, and did not know whether *they* meant the plants or the children or the thing he had left behind ten years ago with its lights still on and its people still inside. *They're awake and they're calling.*

He had spent ten years telling himself that Terminal was silent. That the upload had either failed or succeeded and either way it was finished, sealed, a letter posted into the dark with no return address. He had built his whole life on top of that silence the way you build a house on a filled-in well—carefully, never digging, never letting the floor creak.

The floor was creaking now.

He pressed his palm flat against the soil of the seedling tray, an old habit, a way of checking temperature without instruments, and the hum came up through his hand, faint, unmistakable, in time with the melody the children had been drawing on every surface of the ship for three weeks. The calibration tones. Terminal's calibration tones, the ones he himself had tuned, kneeling in a server bay with Imani laughing at him through the comm because he could never leave the seventh interval flat, never, he'd sooner die—

He pulled his hand back as if the soil had bitten him.

A seedling had bent toward him. Just one. A tomato start three days out of its casing, leaning its whole small body against the tray wall in his direction, the way a plant leans toward light.

August sat back on his heels in the green light and understood, with the calm that arrives at the center of panic, that his ten years were over. Not because of Voss, not because of the auditor's impossible cargo routes, not because of Sefa's careful, wounded eyes. Because the signal was strengthening, and a signal that strengthens is a signal that *intends*, and somewhere out in the dead dark a garden full of people he had abandoned was singing its way through every wall he had built.

He should run. He was good at running. He had a talent for it, a decade of practice.

Instead he stayed on his knees in the dirt and listened until shift's end.

* * *

Sefa Idris kept a ledger of the dreaming, because a ledger was the last shape her certainty could wear.

*Day 22: Eleven children, synchronized dream reports, 94% narrative overlap. Hypothesis: social contagion via shared dormitory environment.*

*Day 24: Nine additional children. Overlap holds. Dormitory hypothesis fails—new dreamers are in separate habitat rings, no contact with original cohort. Revised hypothesis: airborne agent? Environmental toxin?*

*Day 26: Thirty-one children. Two adults. The adults are a schoolteacher and a comms technician who have never met.*

She sat in her office at 0500 with the ledger open and her cold tea untouched, and she wrote the next line slowly, because once it was written it could not be unwritten:

*Day 26, addendum: Contagion spreads along no physical pathway I can measure. It spreads the way a story spreads. Or the way a name spreads, when someone is calling it.*

The scans were worse than the ledger. She had run fMRI-equivalent imaging on the original eleven children twice now, and the patterns were not merely similar—they were *stacked*, the same activation cascades firing in the same order at the same millisecond offsets, thirty-one brains playing one recording. She had shown the overlays to her assistant, who had assumed a machine error. She had re-run everything on the backup imager. The machine was not wrong. The children were, in some functional sense, *one*.

And the pattern was growing. Not spreading—growing. The synchronized region was larger with every scan, expanding outward from the visual cortex into the auditory, into the limbic system, the way roots expand, the way mycelium runs through soil, patient and total. At the current rate, the pattern would reach the regions governing language within a month. After that, Sefa did not know. Nobody knew. There was no literature on this. There was no *this*.

She thought of Noor Haddad, seven years old, who had looked up at her during yesterday's examination with the terrible serenity of the deeply convinced and said, "It's not a dream, Doctor. It's a place. We're not sleeping wrong. We're *listening* right."

She thought of the drawing pinned inside her desk drawer, where no auditor would find it: a relay station rendered in a child's crayons, all wrong in every proportion and exactly right in every detail—the antenna array canted at seventeen degrees, the heat-exchange fins, the little scar of the micrometeorite strike on the dorsal hull that appeared in no public schematic, in no file the children could possibly have seen. A scar Sefa had never seen either. A scar she knew, now, as well as her own hands, because she had spent three nights with the ship's restricted engineering archive open on her screen, cross-referencing a seven-year-old's crayon against classified hull diagrams, and the crayon was *correct*.

The station in the drawing was labeled, in Noor's careful letters: *THE GARDEN THAT WAITS.*

Sefa put her head back against her chair and closed her eyes, and the hum was there, faint, at the edge of hearing, the hum she had been pretending for two days not to hear. She was a physician. She did not yet hear the song. But she could feel the door of it, the way you feel a wave coming through the hull of a ship before it strikes—pressure first, then sound.

*Thirty-one children,* she thought. *Two adults. And every one of them pointed, like antennas, at a coordinate in dead space where nothing has lived for ten years.*

*Nothing,* she wrote in the ledger, and then, because she was honest even when honesty was dangerous, she crossed it out.

*No one we know of.*

* * *

Councilor Voss received the comms report at 0612 and the medical briefing at 0630, and by 0700 she had filled three pages of her private slate with a single word written over and over in her precise, punishing hand: *vector, vector, vector.*

The carrier wave changed things. She understood that immediately, the way she understood all threats immediately—fast, cold, completely. A dream was a problem for physicians. A dream with a *transmitter* was a problem for the council. A dream with a transmitter that was strengthening, that had reached thirty-one children and was now recruiting adults, that was phase-locked to a source in dead space the ship's own charts had written off as a grave— that was not a problem. That was a *siege*, and the colony ship Perpetual Spring, eleven thousand souls in a tin can between the stars, had survived ten years of the void by treating every unknown as hostile until proven otherwise.

She convened the emergency session for 0900. She would stand before the council with the spectral analysis glowing on the wall behind her and she would say the words she had already rehearsed: *We are being addressed. We are being changed. And we did not consent.*

But in the hour before the session, alone in her office with the ship humming around her, Voss allowed herself the thought she would never say aloud, the thought that had been growing in her since the first child's drawing crossed her desk:

The signal was beautiful.

She had listened to the demodulated carrier. Everyone in the chain of command had, once, before the order came down that listening was a security risk. It was a hum, a green and patient hum, and Voss had sat in the dark of her office with it playing through her desk speakers and had felt something in her chest unclench that had been clenched for ten years—since the surface survey that killed her husband, since the word *terraforming* became a word she could not say without tasting ash.

The signal was beautiful, and that was precisely what made it dangerous. Beautiful things did not ask permission. Beautiful things came in through the ears of children and made homes in them before any adult thought to check the doors.

Voss locked the spectral analysis in her secure files, straightened her collar, and went to convene the council.

* * *

By second shift, everyone on the ship knew something was happening, even if they didn't know what.

The children had stopped hiding it. That was the change, the thing that made the corridors feel different, the thing parents reported in the mess halls in voices pitched low. For three weeks the dreamers had been secretive, furtive, drawing their garden on the undersides of desks where adults wouldn't look. Now they drew in the open. Now they sang—quietly, under their breath, in the food lines and the lift bays, a melody in a minor key that no one had taught them and that every dreamer knew. Now, when a child who did not dream sat next to a child who did, the dreaming child would lean over and say, not unkindly, the way you tell someone a secret they already half-know:

"It's a garden. It's waiting for you. You don't have to be scared."

And the children who did not dream would say, "I know."

In the hydroponics bay, workers found that the crops nearest the outer hull had begun to lean—not toward the grow-lights, but toward the hull, toward the dark beyond it, all of them, in unison, like a congregation turning toward a door.

In the observation lounge, an old man who had not spoken since his wife went into the long sleep three years ago stood at the window looking out at nothing—at the stretch of black between the ship and the stars where there was nothing to see—and said, clearly, to no one, "It's coming from Terminal," and then wept without knowing why, and could not be made to explain what Terminal was, because he didn't know. He had never known. He had only, suddenly, and with total certainty, *remembered*.

* * *

August found Sefa in the medical archive at shift change, and they stood in the aisle between the cold-storage cabinets with the ship's alarm-quiet all around them, and neither of them said anything for a long moment, because there was a new thing between them now, humming.

"You felt it," she said at last. Not a question.

"The plants did," he said. "I listened to the plants."

"Thirty-one children, August. Two adults. A carrier wave out of a region of space that's been dead since before this ship launched." Her voice was level, physician-level, but her hands were not; she was holding her slate the way you hold a railing. "The signal strengthened forty percent in six hours. It's not degrading like a broadcast. It's not fading like a distress call. Do you know what it's doing?"

He looked at her. Ten years of careful, guarded looking, and now he could not stop looking, because the thing he had buried was singing through the hull of the ship and through the woman in front of him and through the soil under his fingernails, and the mask he had worn so long was cracking along its own fault lines.

"It's growing," he said.

"Yes."

"Like a root system. Like—" He stopped.

"Say it," Sefa said quietly. "Like a garden."

The hum was there in the silence between them, faint, patient, in the seventh interval he had never once been able to leave flat.

Down in the children's ward, thirty-one sleepers turned over in their beds, all at once, all in the same direction, facing west—facing the dark, facing the dead coordinates, facing the garden that waited—and the carrier wave out of dead space strengthened again, and this time it carried, threaded through the hum like a ribbon through green wheat, something new.

Not the melody.

A voice. Faint, frayed, ten years deep, saying a name.

The name of a man who had once answered to it, before he buried it and became someone else.

*August.*
