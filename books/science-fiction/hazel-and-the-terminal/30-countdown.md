## 30. Countdown

**August**

The alarm didn't wail. That was the first thing August noticed, and the last mercy the station would show them.

It was a hum instead—a low, subsonic shudder that came up through the deck plates and into the soles of his boots, into the bones of his ankles, the way a body knows a fever before a thermometer does. He had been kneeling in the relay's central spine for six hours, splicing fiber-line with hands that remembered this work the way a tongue remembers a burned mouth, when the hum began.

He froze. The splice half-made, the gel-cable weeping faint light against his knuckles.

"No," he said, to no one, to the station, to the decade he'd spent pretending this day was far enough away to be someone else's problem. "Not yet."

The spine of Terminal was a cathedral that had been built by engineers and then colonized by gardeners. Engineered vines the color of deep water ran the length of the corridor overhead, their leaves broad as serving trays, and through their translucent flesh August could see the pulse of the core below—amber, then amber again, then a flicker of sick white that had no business being there. The light came faster. The hum climbed a quarter-tone.

Cascade failure. Early. Hours, maybe, where the projections had given them two days.

He was moving before he finished the thought, running the corridor toward the children's berth with the splice still unfinished behind him, and the vines overhead seemed to lean after him as he passed, leaves whispering against each other like a crowd inhaling.

The children were already awake.

Of course they were. He understood that as he burst through the hatch and found all four of them sitting upright in their sleeping nets, Noor in the center, their eyes open and fixed on nothing—or on something that was not in the room. The dream had stopped being a thing that visited them hours ago. Down here in dead space it was constant, a second sky behind their faces, and August had watched it settle over them during the long approach with a feeling he couldn't name, something between reverence and grief.

Now their eyes refocused, all four at once, and turned to him.

"It's starting early," Noor said. Not a question. Eleven years old and she said it the way a harbor pilot says *storm coming*, with the flat calm of someone who has already decided what she will do about it.

"The core's destabilizing." August's voice came out rougher than he intended. "The broadcast window may close before—"

"Then we sing now."

She said it simply, the way children say the true things, and August felt the words land in his chest like a splice seating home.

* * *

**Noor**

Noor had known the song before she knew it had a name, which was the way she knew most things now. The dream had taught her. Not the words—the song had no words, not exactly—but the shape of it, the way it rose and folded and rose again, a melody that behaved like water finding a channel.

Imani had shown them. Not in the dream exactly, and not out loud. In the space between, where the garden grew.

*When the light stutters,* Imani had said, in the way the garden said things, which was less like speech and more like remembering something together, *the song is a hand on a wheel. Hold it steady.*

The station was failing. Noor could feel it the way she could feel a loose tooth—not pain exactly, but a wrongness, a loosening, the sense that something that belonged inside was working its way out. The vines in the walls had gone quiet. Usually they murmured. Usually the whole station hummed with the slow green thoughts of things that had been growing for ten years in the dark, patient as moss, and now that hum had a crack in it, a white note running through it like a hairline fracture in glass.

The others were looking at her. Devi, who was nine and bit her nails to the quick. Tomas and his sister Lira, twins who finished each other's dreams. And August, standing in the hatch with his face doing the thing grown-up faces did when they were afraid and didn't want the children to see it, which never worked, because children always saw.

"We need to be in the spine," Noor said. "Close to the roots. That's where the song carries best."

"How do you know that?" August asked, and there was no challenge in it, only the genuine bewilderment of a man watching children navigate a place they'd never seen with their eyes open.

Noor considered how to explain. She thought about the dream, the garden singing in the dark, the way the melody had a floor to it the way a house has a floor, and how when you stood on that floor in the dream you could feel through your feet where the roots went.

"Because we've been here before," she said. "Every night. For months. You just got here."

* * *

**August**

They moved through the station's spine in a loose procession, and August found himself doing something he had not done in ten years: following.

The children walked like they belonged here. That was the thing he could not get past, the thing that kept catching in his chest like a hook. They turned at junctions they had never seen. They ducked under a collapsed conduit brace that August himself had only found by walking into it. When they reached the root chamber—the old coolant nexus where the engineered flora had long since broken through the decking and woven itself into the station's skeleton—the four of them spread out without a word, each finding a position among the great root masses as naturally as instruments finding their places in an orchestra.

The roots were enormous. He'd known they would be—Imani had designed the flora to integrate, not merely to grow—but knowing and seeing were different animals. The roots filled the chamber like the veins of something buried alive, thick as his torso in places, banded in rings of pale green and deep violet, and where they met the station's structure they had grown *around* it, cradling the relay's bones the way a banyan cradles the wall it consumes. The core housing sat at the chamber's heart, wrapped in root, and through the gaps August could see the light inside stuttering faster now. Amber. Amber. White.

The hum climbed again.

"August." Noor was crouched at the base of the largest root, her small hands flat against its surface. "You said you have to wire the broadcast. Do it. We'll hold the core."

"You can't hold a fusion core with a song, Noor."

"You can't," she agreed, with terrible patience. "But the roots can, if the roots can hear. Imani said the calibration song was how the crew kept the core balanced before the upload went deep. The roots remember. They just need reminding."

And God help him, it was true. He had been there for the calibration trials, back when Terminal was bare steel and the song was nothing but a sequence of tones piped through the station to tune the core's magnetic containment—seven tones, rising, folding back on themselves. The crew had hummed it on shift out of boredom. Imani used to whistle it while she welded. He had spent ten years trying not to remember it and had failed every single night.

The children had learned it from the crew's own dreaming minds. The crew had learned it from the station. The station had learned it from its builders. The song had come all the way around, ten years and a betrayal wide, and arrived back at its beginning in the mouths of children.

"Okay," August said. "Okay. Sing."

* * *

**Sefa**

The patrol ship *Vigil's Answer* came out of its final burn with the dead space pressing against every viewport like a held breath, and Dr. Sefa Idris stood on the command deck of a ship she had mutinied forty hours ago, surrounded by people who had no legal right to be here and every human reason.

Eleven of them. That was the crew that remained after the split—eleven patrol officers who had looked at Councilor Voss's orders, and then at the footage of their own children sleeping with synchronized smiles, and had chosen. Sefa still marveled at it. She had spent her career believing that institutions held because people obeyed, and the mutiny had taught her otherwise: institutions held because people were afraid, and fear was a door that the dream had quietly unlocked.

"Contact," said Officer Reyes—no relation, she'd checked, and been surprised by how much she'd wanted there to be. "Terminal is... Doctor, you should see this."

She should not have been able to see it at all, not at this range, not in dead space where the only light was starlight and the faint green glow of the relay itself. But the station was *blooming*. Even at forty thousand kilometers the long-range optics resolved it: a skeletal relay frame wrapped in vegetation, and across that vegetation, pulses of light moving in waves—amber waves rolling from the core outward along the root-lines, then reversing, then rolling again, like breath. Like a heartbeat remembering how.

"It's the song," Sefa said quietly. She could hear it now, faintly, through the comm array—a carrier tone, seven notes rising and folding, threaded through with small voices. "The children. They're stabilizing the core with the calibration sequence."

"Can a song stabilize a fusion core?" asked the helmsman, a broad, skeptical woman named Okafor whose daughter dreamed.

"No," Sefa said. "But the roots can. And the roots are listening to the children." She found she was gripping the rail hard enough to hurt her hands. "Get us closer. Faster."

They had lost the window once already. Voss's original patrol had been left drifting with a dead engine and a very confused first officer, and the delay had cost them—cost them hours they did not have, because August's last transmission, fragmentary and half-swallowed by static, had contained three words that Sefa had been carrying in her chest like a stone ever since:

*Cascade. Early. Hurry.*

Now the hum of the dying relay reached them even here, translated through the carrier wave into something Sefa felt in her sternum, and beneath it, small and clear and impossibly brave, the children's voices.

* * *

**August**

The broadcast array was a kilometer of antenna spine wrapped in ten years of vine, and August climbed it with a tool harness and no safety line, because the harness points had been grown over and cutting them free would take time they didn't have.

Below him, through the root chamber's open dome, he could hear the children singing.

They had been singing for forty minutes. Their voices were small and the chamber was vast, but the roots carried the sound the way the roots carried everything—drinking it in, amplifying it, feeding it down into the core housing where the seven-tone sequence did its old, patient work. And it was working. He could see it from up here: the stutter in the core's light was slowing. Amber, amber, amber. The white flickers came less often, like a fever breaking in slow motion.

But a song was a hand on a wheel, not a repair. The core was dying. The song was only teaching it to die in rhythm.

*Focus,* he told himself, and cut another vine from the antenna, and spliced.

The work was brutal and beautiful. The array had been built for a crew of forty with powered assists; August had two hands and a borrowed multi-tool and a body that was forty-six years old and had spent a decade hauling pallets instead of climbing spires. His fingers bled where the vine-sap stung the cuts. The sap, he remembered—Imani had told him, laughing, the day they seeded the first flora module—was mildly psychoactive. *Don't lick your thumbs, August.* He didn't. But he could feel it anyway, faintly, a green warmth in the wounds, as if the station were trying to heal him while he worked to save it.

*You're doing it wrong,* said a voice in his memory, warm and exasperated. *The phase coupler goes before the splice, not after. You never listened.*

"I'm listening now," he said out loud, and moved the coupler.

The vine nearest his hand shivered.

He told himself it was the core's vibration. He kept working.

* * *

**Noor**

The song was hard work. Noor had not expected that. In the dream, singing was like breathing—you didn't decide to do it, it did itself through you. Here, in the cold root chamber with her knees aching against the deck and her voice going hoarse, singing was a thing her body had to make happen, note after note after note, like rowing a boat.

Devi's voice cracked first. Then Lira's. Tomas went quiet, rubbing his throat, his eyes wet with something that wasn't sadness exactly—more like the exhaustion of carrying something too heavy for too long.

Noor kept singing. Alone, for a few bars, her voice thin in the vast chamber, and the roots around her dimmed, the amber pulse in the core stuttering back toward white—

And then August's voice came down from above.

It was not a good voice. It was rough and unpracticed and it wobbled on the fourth tone, and Noor had never heard anything so welcome in her life. He was somewhere up in the antenna spine, singing the calibration sequence into his work, into the station, into the ten years of silence between himself and the people he had left here—and the roots drank his voice in with the children's, and the amber light steadied, and Devi and Lira and Tomas lifted their heads and came back in.

Five voices. Then, faintly, from the station's own systems, from the old comm speakers in the walls that August had patched into the array feed—six. A tone that was not a child's voice and not August's, but something older, worn smooth, like a stone in a river.

The roots were singing back.

Noor pressed her forehead against the great root and felt it hum against her skin, and in the space between the song and the silence, Imani's presence brushed hers like a hand on her hair.

*Good,* the garden said. *Hold. He's almost done. Hold.*

* * *

**August**

The last splice went home at what his chronometer insisted was 0317, ship-time, dead-time, no-time. The array lit along its full kilometer length, vine and antenna alike flushing with power, and August clung to the spine and laughed—a cracked, disbelieving sound that the dead space took and kept.

Below, the core's pulse had found a rhythm. Not healthy. Not healed. But rhythmic—amber, amber, amber, a heartbeat with a limp, holding.

He began the climb down, and his arms were shaking, and he did not care.

* * *

**Sefa**

They made orbit around Terminal as the array finished its power-up, and through the viewport Sefa watched the station breathe its slow amber light, and she thought: *he's alive, he's done it, he's—*

The comm crackled. August's voice, hoarse, threaded with static and, beneath the static, the children's song still going, tireless, patient.

"Sefa. The core's holding, but it's a holding action—the cascade's still coming, the song just slowed it. We can transmit, but the window is..." A breath. She could hear him choosing honesty over comfort, and loved him for it. "One pass. Maybe two. The array aligns with the Perpetual Spring's position in ninety minutes. After that—"

"After that the window closes."

"After that everything closes."

Sefa closed her eyes. Ninety minutes. Somewhere behind them, hours away at full burn, Voss's second patrol was coming, and somewhere ahead of them, ten light-minutes and a lifetime away, the Perpetual Spring hung with its forty thousand sleepers who had never known what had been done for them, and to them, in the dark.

"Then we don't waste a second of it," she said. "August—when you transmit, it won't just be the archive. It'll be you. Everything. Voss knows. The whole colony will know who you were. Who you *are*."

A long silence, filled with the seven-tone song.

"I know," August said, and his voice was quiet and steady and more afraid than she had ever heard it, and more free. "That was always the price. I'm done pretending I can't pay it."

Sefa opened her eyes and looked at the blooming, burning, dying station, and at the small green light of the children's voices wrapped around its core, and keyed the channel for the approach.

"Coming in to land," she said. "All of us. Hold the song—we're almost home."

And from the station, faint through the static, Noor's voice answered first, clear as a bell in dead space:

"Hurry. The garden's waiting for you too."
