## 38. Groundbreaking

The morning the greenhouse opened, the Perpetual Spring's weather system produced its first honest rain.

Not the recycled mist that hissed from the hydroponics decks on a timer, not the condensate that wept down the hull seams in the cold seasons—but rain, engineered and deliberate, falling in fat warm drops onto the scarred regolith of Landing Plain Three, where for forty years nothing had grown but survey stakes and regret.

August Winterbourne—August Reyes now, the name worn openly like a healed wound—stood at the edge of the crowd and let the water hit his face. Around him, two thousand colonists had come up from the ship in shifts, many of them touching open sky for the first time since launch. The dome of atmosphere they'd seeded over the plain was still thin; through it, the stars showed pale as fish bones. But the air held. The air was theirs.

At the center of it all stood the greenhouse: a long rib of glass and salvaged relay plating, its spine built from the antenna array Voss had surrendered to the broadcast. Someone had welded the old Terminal hull fragment—what the patrol ships had towed home after the collapse—into the north wall, so that the station itself would be part of the structure, part of the growing. The metal was furred green already, engineered moss creeping across the welds, patient as anything Imani Okafor had ever designed.

*She would have laughed,* August thought. *A prison turned into a seedbed.*

He could feel her in the back of his skull, faint as warmth through a wall. Not words yet—not this morning. Just presence, the way you know someone is in the next room by the quality of the silence.

The children came forward first.

There were thirty-one of them originally; all thirty-one were there, plus the two adults who had dreamed, plus Noor at the front carrying a case the size of a loaf of bread. She walked slowly, the way she'd learned to walk in the dream-garden, where haste bruised the roots. The crowd hushed. Even the wind seemed to hold its breath, though there wasn't really wind yet—the atmosphere was too young for weather beyond the scheduled rain.

Noor stopped at the threshold of the greenhouse, where the soil began. Real soil—regolith amended with ten years of composted cargo waste, with the nutrient pallets August had once hauled for settlers not yet born, with ash from Terminal's core, carried home in sealed jars because the children had insisted the garden should have its own earth underneath it.

"Is everybody ready?" Noor asked, in the loud clear voice of a child who has been told she is important and has decided to believe it.

Two thousand people said yes in one breath, and the sound went up through the young atmosphere and out into the dark.

* * *

Sefa Idris watched from the medical line, hands folded, wearing her coat even though her clinic days aboard the ship were over. She had argued for masks, for air-quality monitoring, for a triage tent at the perimeter—and then she had looked at the data one more time and signed off on all of them being unnecessary, because the children's neural patterns had been normalizing for six weeks straight, synchronizing downward into ordinary sleep, ordinary dreams, ordinary childhoods.

The garden was letting go of them. Or they were learning to carry it without needing to visit.

She caught August's eye across the crowd. Ten years of watching each other sideways, and now they could simply look, and it was the strangest luxury either of them had ever known. He tilted his head toward the soil, asking without words: *with me?*

She moved through the press of bodies—people nodded to her now, touched her sleeve, some of them still unsure whether to thank her or arrest her, though the tribunal had settled that question a month ago—and reached him as Noor knelt at the prepared bed.

"You're shaking," Sefa murmured.

"I'm cold."

"It's twenty-two degrees and raining warm."

"I'm shaking," August admitted.

The pod came out of the case like something liturgical. It was the size of a fist, veined silver-green, grown in the last weeks of Terminal's life from Imani's own root-stock—a cutting of the garden itself, the only piece the crew had asked to be carried home. The rest of them had chosen completion. The archive lived in every comm node on the ship now; anyone could walk into any reading room and stand inside Imani's memories, watch the terraforming crew plant their impossible garden in dead space, hear the calibration song, learn what August had run from and what he had finally brought back.

But the pod was alive. The pod was the difference between a recording and a person.

Noor held it in both palms. "It wants to go in deep," she said. "It told me last night." A pause, and then, carefully, honestly: "Not a dream anymore. Just—I remembered what it liked. Is that okay?"

"That's okay," Sefa said softly. "That's how memory is supposed to work."

August knelt beside her. The soil was dark and smelled of iron and beginnings. Together—he dug, she placed—they set the pod into the ground, and the children sang.

They didn't need to be told to. The calibration song rose up out of thirty-one throats, thin and wavering and then finding itself, harmonics locking the way they'd locked in the dying core while August wired the broadcast with his hands bleeding. Two thousand adults didn't know the words, but the melody was in the archive, in the air, in the bones of everyone who'd heard it during those nights when the whole ship woke weeping and didn't know why. The song spread outward from the children like the ripples of the rain.

And the pod opened.

Roots went down white and quick as lightning reversed. A stem came up, unfurling leaves that no botanist in the colony could classify—leaves that shimmered faintly with the same engineered chlorophyll Imani had coded into the Terminal flora, adapted now, softened for real sun and real rain. By the time the song ended, the plant stood knee-high and flowering, and the flowers were the exact violet of the sky inside the dream.

Nobody spoke. Then an old woman near the front—August didn't know her name, one of the sleepers woken early, one of the thousands who had heard everything—began to cry, quietly, without shame, and the crying moved through the crowd the way the song had, grief and relief indistinguishable, mourning finishing the work it had started the night the broadcast reached every bunk on the Perpetual Spring.

August put his hand flat on the soil beside the stem. He felt—imagined he felt—a pulse answering.

*"Hello,"* said the warmth behind his eyes, in a voice like a door opening after years. *"So this is dirt. I always wondered."*

He laughed, or sobbed, or both. Sefa's hand found his shoulder and stayed.

* * *

Councilor Voss arrived late, alone, and stood at the back where the light was worst.

She had not expected to be invited. She had been invited anyway—by Noor, who had marched into the council chamber with the seed case under her arm and informed the assembled government that the woman who tried to take the garden needed to see it grow, because "that's how you fix people, you don't lock them away from things, you show them." Voss had accepted with the rigid dignity of someone accepting a sentence.

She watched the plant breathe. She watched the children scatter to other beds—there were forty of them, prepped and waiting, seeded from cuttings that had already begun to run along the greenhouse gutters—and she watched August Reyes kneel in the mud with his bad leg folded wrong beneath him, not caring, his face turned up into the rain.

For eleven months she had carried the knowledge of what she'd almost done. Quarantine wards. Cognitive contagion protocols. A fast patrol ship commandeered with a physician in custody and a weapons locker unlocked. And underneath all of it, the thing she had never said aloud to anyone except, eventually, the tribunal: that she had wanted the signal weaponized because a weaponized signal could be controlled, and a controlled thing couldn't hurt you, and she had been so afraid—of dead space, of dreams, of the sheer unaccountable generosity of a crew who had given themselves to a dead world and asked nothing.

Imani's recorded last words had cracked her open in the green dark of Terminal, and she had not entirely closed again. Some mornings she suspected she never would.

A small shape detached from the planting beds and trudged over to her through the mud. Noor, mud to the knees, holding a spare trowel like a scepter.

"You came," Noor said.

"I came."

"Do you want to plant something?"

Voss looked at the trowel. At the beds. At the horizon of her own ruined certainty. "I wouldn't know how."

"Nobody does," Noor said. "That's why we practice." She held out the trowel.

After a moment that cost her more than the tribunal ever had, Councilor Voss took it.

* * *

By afternoon the rain had done its work and the plain smelled of wet mineral and green. The crowd thinned in slow eddies, people drifting back toward the shuttle pads, reluctant, looking over their shoulders. Some stayed. Families claimed beds. An engineering crew argued cheerfully about irrigation lines. The greenhouse filled with the ordinary noise of a place becoming a place.

Sefa found August sitting on the greenhouse's north sill, the Terminal hull-fragment wall at his back, watching the children.

"They'll stop dreaming soon," he said. "Completely. Sefa, I keep thinking I should be sad about that, and I'm not. I can't make myself be."

"Because it was never meant to be permanent." She sat beside him, their shoulders touching. "Imani told us that herself. A bridge, not a house. They waited ten years for someone to cross it. We crossed. Now the kids get to just—be kids. With dirt under their nails instead of starlight."

"Their parents are going to have questions about the dirt under their nails."

"Their parents are going to have questions about everything for about a decade." She smiled. "We'll manage."

Below them, Noor was teaching three younger children how to test soil moisture by squeezing a handful and listening to it. One of the little ones squeezed too hard and the clod crumbled and she burst into tears, and Noor said something gentle that August couldn't hear, and the tears stopped, and they tried again. Ordinary pedagogy. Ordinary afternoon. His chest ached with how ordinary it was.

"How is she today?" Sefa asked quietly. She never used Imani's name carelessly in public, but here, in the green noise, she allowed it.

"Talkative. The rain excited her. She keeps narrating the drainage patterns like she personally invented water." He paused. "Sometimes she goes quiet for hours, and I've learned not to worry. She says silence is also a kind of gardening."

"And you? How are you?"

August considered the question honestly, which was still a new discipline for him. Ten years of false papers had made honesty feel like walking on a broken ankle; four months of the true name had begun to knit it.

"Tired," he said. "Guilty, some mornings, before I'm awake enough to remember I've paid something. Useful. Loved." He glanced at her. "Terrified of the tribunal transcripts going public next month, but apparently that's called accountability and I volunteered for it."

"You did."

"I did." He exhaled. "Imani says guilt is a taproot. You don't cut it out—you just keep planting around it until the other roots are stronger. She says a lot of things like that now. Ten years of growing gave her time to become very wise and extremely smug."

Sefa laughed—a real laugh, head tipped back, and somewhere below them Imani's voice said, distinctly, *tell her I said the drainage really is elegant*, and August passed it along, and Sefa laughed harder, and the afternoon went on.

* * *

Evening came gently to Landing Plain Three, the young atmosphere catching the distant sun and gilding it. The last visitors filed out. The duty crew checked seals and moisture lines. The children were shuttled back to the ship over Noor's loud objections, extracted one by one from their beds with the practiced diplomacy of parents who had learned, in the past months, that their children knew things worth listening to.

August stayed last, as he had stayed last in the seed vault for ten years. Old habit. New meaning.

He walked the rows slowly, touching leaves. In the far bed, Imani's plant had closed its flowers for the night, folding violet petals against the cooling air. He crouched beside it.

"Goodnight?" he asked aloud, feeling foolish, not caring.

*"Goodnight,"* she answered, warm and close. *"August. Look at what we grew."*

He looked. Not just the plant—the whole long room of it, the ribs of glass fogging with breath, the moss on the salvaged hull, the forty beds waiting for tomorrow's planters, the muddy trowel Voss had left behind in the tool rack like a confession. Beyond the glass, the plain stretched dark and damp and possible, and beyond that the stars, and beyond the stars, in dead space where nothing should live, the place where a relay had burned green and let go.

"We," he said. "You said 'we.'"

*"I've been saying 'we' since the day you walked into my dream with a bunch of stowaway children and a terrible plan,"* Imani said. *"You're a slow learner, Reyes, but you're mine. Ours. Whatever grammar survives this."*

He pressed his palm to the cool glass above her bed. Behind his eyes, the warmth settled in for the night, humming a few bars of the calibration song, off-key on purpose, because she knew it made him smile.

Outside, the automatic rain ticked off and the stars came fully out. Inside, in the dark, the garden breathed.

And in the morning—though none of them knew it yet—there would be a message waiting from the deep survey office: a new world found past the contested orbits, dead and waiting, and a request attached, addressed by name, to the colony's newest apprentice terraformer.

Noor read it twice, ran to find August, and nearly knocked him into the irrigation trench.

"Cargo for the unborn," she said, breathless, holding out the tablet. "They want us to pack."
