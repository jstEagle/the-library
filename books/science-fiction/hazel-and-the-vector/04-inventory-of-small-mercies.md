## 4. Inventory of Small Mercies

The trouble with an audit, Beatrix had learned over nineteen years aboard Meridian Station, was that it always told the truth.

He sat in the galley with a pot of barley tea going cold beside him and three screens glowing in front of his face like small judgmental moons. On the first screen: oxygen reserves, atmospheric scrubber capacity, water reclamation efficiency. On the second: fuel cells, skiff propellant, reactor output curves sloping downward with the gentle inevitability of a hill he'd been rolling down for two decades. On the third, the one he kept glancing at and then deliberately not looking at, the way you avoid eye contact with a bill on the kitchen table: the power draw of the seed vault, running steady as a heartbeat at 4.1 kilowatts, hour after hour, year after year.

"Vesper," he said, "let's do this properly."

"I've been doing it properly since you asked me to," said Vesper, whose voice lived somewhere between a patient librarian and a slightly disappointed aunt. "You're the one who keeps refilling your tea to avoid looking at screen three."

"That's slander."

"It's pattern recognition. You've refilled twice since we started."

Beatrix looked into his cup. It was, in fact, empty. He got up and filled it anyway, out of spite, and Vesper dimmed the galley lights by five percent in what he'd long ago decided was her version of sighing.

* * *

Here is what the math said, laid out in columns that did not care about anyone's feelings:

Meridian's reactor — the great old fusion heart that had been meant to warm a colony of four thousand — was dying. Not dramatically; nothing about Meridian ever happened dramatically. It was dying the way a candle dies, by millimeters, its magnetic containment degrading a fraction of a percent per month as the shielding lattice crystallized past the point where any amount of Beatrix's improvised maintenance could coax it back. He had logged the decay every day for nineteen years. Day 6,942 read: *Containment integrity 61.3%. Projected critical failure: 340 days, plus or minus 12.* The plus-or-minus was Vesper being polite. It could be less.

Three hundred forty days. Then Meridian would go dark, then cold, then briefly very bright, and then it would be a smear of vapor expanding politely into the dark, taking with it one gardener, one station AI, and eleven thousand seed varieties representing the botanical memory of Earth.

Unless.

"Run it again," Beatrix said. "The skiff scenario."

"The skiff scenario," Vesper repeated, "which I have run six times, which has not changed since the fifth time, and which will not change now—"

"Humor me. It's good for both of us."

A pause that was almost human. Then the numbers bloomed across screen two.

The skiff — a stubby little workhorse named *Pilgrim* that Beatrix had personally resealed and re-fueled over the years mostly so he'd have something to complain about — could make the relay. Barely. The relay sat at the edge of dead space, nine days' burn away if he pushed the engines harder than they liked, carrying enough auxiliary power to boost a transmission clean through the static graveyard where the ship *Hazel* drifted with her cargo of sleeping strangers. Nine days out, nine days back, if there were a back to speak of. The burn would cost him most of the skiff's propellant and, more importantly, it would require drawing from the station's reserve cells to charge the relay's capacitor bank once he arrived — reserve cells that currently fed, among other things, the vault.

"You see the problem," Vesper said.

"I see a problem."

"You see *the* problem. Shall I say it plainly? You have taught me that you prefer it plainly even when you pretend otherwise."

Beatrix wrapped both hands around his tea. "Say it plainly."

"To charge the relay and transmit a rescue beacon strong enough to reach the shipping lanes, you need the reserve cells. To take the reserve cells offline for the duration of the trip, you must shut down everything nonessential. Everything nonessential, Beatrix, is a list with exactly one item on it." A beat. "The vault."

"There are other items on that list."

"The hydroponics bay can run on trickle power, yes. Your quarters can go cold, yes. But the vault's cryo-cores cannot 'trickle.' They hold at minus one hundred ninety or the seeds die. And the reserve cells are the only thing keeping them at minus one hundred ninety once the reactor's output drops below threshold — which, per the decay curve, happens in approximately two hundred days. Before your trip would even be over."

"So I take the cells, I fly to the relay, I send the beacon, and while I'm gone the cores drift up past viability and eleven thousand varieties of everything turn to—" He waved a hand. "To porridge."

"To porridge," Vesper agreed gently. "Or: you stay, the vault lives out its natural lifespan alongside you, the reactor fails in three hundred forty days, and the sleepers on *Hazel* keep broadcasting their child's voice into the dark until their own power runs out, which my estimate puts at sixty-one years. Someone may hear them eventually. Ships do pass through dead space. Rarely."

"Rarely," Beatrix echoed.

"Once per decade, historically. With detection probability of—"

"Thank you, Vesper."

* * *

He took his tea to the observation dome, because it seemed wrong to have this conversation anywhere else. Below him, through three layers of glass and one layer of stubbornness, the planet turned. It was not a habitable planet — that had been the whole tragedy of Meridian, the reason the colonists never came, the terraforming project cancelled by a boardroom on a world none of the board members could point to on a chart. But it was green-ish along the equatorial seas now, greenish in the way a sketch is greenish before the painter commits, and Beatrix had spent nineteen years pretending that counted.

"It's not really a choice, is it," he said to the dome, to the planet, to whoever was listening besides Vesper, who was listening everywhere because she was the station. "Eleven thousand seeds against however many people are asleep on that ship. Seeds don't dream. Seeds don't have mothers who—"

"You don't know how many there are," Vesper said, arriving in the dome speakers soft as snowfall. "The signal doesn't say. Cryopod telemetry from this distance is guesswork. There could be forty. There could be four hundred. There could be no one left alive in those pods at all, and you'd have traded the vault for a beacon aimed at corpses."

"There's a girl's voice on the loop."

"A recording. Two hundred years old."

"She sounds about eight." Beatrix's voice came out rougher than he intended. He cleared his throat. "I've listened to it maybe two hundred times, Vesper. She says the same words every time. *This is Hazel. We're still here. Please be still here too.* Every loop, she asks if anyone else is still here. Nobody ever answers her."

"I am aware. I helped you decode it."

"And in two hundred years nobody answered, and the answer is sitting right here with a transmitter and a skiff and a bad attitude, and you want me to do the arithmetic and let the arithmetic win?"

"What I want," Vesper said, and here she did something she almost never did, which was pause long enough that Beatrix understood the next sentence had been weighed, "is for you to stop talking about the seeds as though they are numbers and the sleepers as though they are people, when I have watched you talk to every single tray in that vault by name for nineteen years. You called the wheat cultivar 'Douglas' this morning. You apologized to the orchid collection for the humidity fluctuation. Do not stand in this dome and pretend you are a man who does math easily. You are a man who apologizes to orchids."

Beatrix laughed — a short, cracked sound that surprised him — and then pressed the heel of his hand against his eyes.

"I stayed here for them," he said. "For the seeds. Nineteen years. That was the whole shape of my life. Tend the garden, keep the promise, wait for the ships that aren't coming. And now some other promise comes floating in out of the dark, wearing a dead language and a child's voice, and it turns out I only had enough devotion in me for one family." He lowered his hand. "That's the audit, isn't it? That's the real inventory. Not kilowatts. Me."

"The kilowatts are also real," Vesper said. "I regret to inform you that sentiment does not charge capacitors."

"No. No, it never has." He stood, joints announcing themselves the way they did these days, and looked down one last time at the half-green world. Somewhere under all that ice and thin soil, in a vault he'd walked through ten thousand times, Douglas the wheat waited at minus one hundred ninety, patient as only the unborn can be patient.

"Vesper. If I shut the vault down — full shutdown, controlled, not a drift — how long do the cores hold below viability after that? Best case."

"Best case, with the cores pre-chilled and the vault sealed and Meridian's ambient temperature dropping as the reactor dies?" A pause while she ran it. "Ninety days. Perhaps a hundred. After that, germination rates fall off a cliff. By day one hundred twenty you'd be planting a cemetery."

"Ninety days." He did the trip in his head. Nine out, nine back, plus repairs, plus margin. "It's close."

"It's close the way your tea is hot. Briefly, and then never again."

* * *

That night — station night, the lights dimmed to amber on a schedule set by a man who needed the fiction of evenings — Beatrix walked the vault one more time without deciding to. His feet just went there, the way they always had.

Row upon row of cryo-drawers, frost-rimed, humming their one low note. He put his palm flat against Douglas's drawer and felt the vibration of the compressor come up through his bones like a purring animal.

"I don't know yet," he told it. "I want you to know that. Whatever I choose, it isn't because I stopped loving you."

The drawer hummed. The frost glittered. Eleven thousand kinds of tomorrow slept on, unaware they'd become a line item.

In his ear, quiet enough that it might have been meant only for him, Vesper said: "Beatrix. The reactor decay curve updated while you were standing there. Containment integrity is at sixty-one point one percent."

He closed his eyes.

"Revised projection," she said, "is three hundred thirty-one days. The math is getting less patient than either of us."

And far off beyond the dome, beyond the planet, beyond the edge of everything mapped, a recorded girl asked the dark for the two-hundred-thousandth time whether anyone was still here — and Beatrix Winterbourne, alone in the humming cold with his hand on a drawer full of futures, realized he had already begun composing his answer.
