## 5. What the Seeds Remember

The night before he decided anything at all, Beatrix Winterbourne did what he always did when his thoughts turned traitor: he went to the vault and repotted something that didn't need it.

The tomato in Bay Four had been perfectly content in its hydroponic cradle for three years. It did not need new substrate. It did not need its roots inspected. But it needed *him* to need the work, and so he knelt on the cold deck with a tray of growing medium and let his hands lie to him about being busy.

"You're avoiding," said Vesper, from the ceiling.

"I'm gardening."

"You're gardening *aggressively*. There's a difference. Avoidance with a trowel."

He didn't answer. The medium sifted through his fingers, dark and damp and smelling of everything Meridian Station had ever meant to be.

* * *

The terraforming team had left on a Tuesday.

That was the detail Beatrix always came back to, years later — not the shuttles, not the last look at the vault doors, but the sheer administrative indifference of a Tuesday. Director Okafor had shaken every hand in the corridor outside Hydroponics and said, "Funding cycles don't wait for dreams, people," as though she were apologizing for weather. Forty-one colonists-to-be had become forty-one resumes forwarded elsewhere. The colony ship *Prosperine* was rerouted to Gliese-hyphen-something, where the atmosphere was already breathable and nobody would have to spend thirty years coaxing dirt into wanting things.

Beatrix had been twenty-six. Junior soil chemist. The youngest of them by a decade, which meant he was the only one who hadn't yet learned that projects like this died the way stars do — slowly, then all at once, with everyone pretending they'd seen it coming.

"Come with us," Okafor had said, hand on his shoulder. "There's a seat. There's always a seat for the young ones."

And he had looked back through the glass at the vault — six hundred species sleeping in their drawers, wheat and rice and amaranth and the stubborn little alpine flowers Dr. Sefa had insisted on "for morale, when there are finally people to have it" — and heard himself say:

"Somebody has to keep the lights on."

It sounded noble. He'd rehearsed it sounding noble. What it actually was, he understood only much later: it was the sentence you said when you couldn't say *I'm afraid of leaving* or *I'm afraid I'm only useful here* or, most quietly of all, *please don't make me start over among strangers.*

Okafor had squeezed his shoulder and boarded the shuttle, and Beatrix had watched her go, and then he had gone back inside and watered the tomatoes, because that was the shape his courage took.

* * *

Dr. Imre Sefa had stayed another eleven years.

She was the one who'd built the vault, back when Meridian was a promise instead of a patient. She was seventy when Beatrix arrived, all elbows and opinions, hair like a storm cloud that had given up on dignity. She called the seed drawers "the children" and the reactor "the beast" and the station's endless maintenance tickets "the tax we pay for the privilege of hope."

"You'll want to leave eventually," she told him in his first month, not unkindly, while they calibrated humidity sensors together. "Everyone does. The young ones especially. You'll meet someone on a supply run, or you'll get an offer, or you'll just wake up one morning and realize the silence has gotten into your bones."

"And if I don't?"

She'd smiled at him over her spectacles. "Then you'll be the best caretaker this place ever had, and the loneliest, and I'll be sorry for both."

She taught him everything. How to wake a seed after decades of sleep — the warm bath, the whisper of light, the patience. How to read a plant's complaint before it became a crisis. How to log decay without letting it become despair. *"We keep records,"* she said, *"because someday someone will read them, and on that day none of this will have been wasted."*

In her last year, her hands shook too badly for the fine work, so she directed and he performed, and they repotted the whole of Bay Four together like two musicians playing a duet from memory.

"The vault isn't a garden, Beatrix," she said one evening, watching the stars turn past the observation port. "I know I've let you believe that. It's a letter. A very long letter, written in seeds, addressed to people who haven't been born yet. And letters—" her voice caught, just slightly, "—letters get sent. Or they don't. But either way, the writing ends."

"Yours doesn't have to end."

"It ended eleven years ago, love. I've just been lingering over the signature." She patted his arm. "When I'm gone, you'll be tempted to stay forever. Not for the seeds. For yourself. Because here, you're needed, and needing is easier than choosing." She looked at him then with those terrible clear eyes. "Promise me you'll know the difference."

He promised. He lied. She knew that too, and let him.

* * *

She died in the winter cycle, quietly, in her bunk, with the vault's status report printed out beside her — green across every column — folded open to the page listing the alpine flowers. *For morale,* she'd underlined, in the margin, in her terrible handwriting. *When there are finally people.*

Beatrix buried her according to her instructions: cremated, ashes mixed into the compost of Bed Nine, "so I can be tomatoes, finally, which is all I ever wanted." He ate the first fruit off those vines alone, at the little table in the mess, and cried into his ration coffee, and then washed his cup and logged the day's readings, because that was the shape his grief took.

After that, the routines became the years. Wake. Check the beast. Tend the children. Record the message. Sleep. Repeat.

Oh — the messages. He'd almost managed not to think about the messages.

Every week, per protocol, he recorded a station update addressed to the incoming colonists. *Welcome to Meridian. The air recyclers are holding at ninety-one percent efficiency. The tomatoes came in well this cycle; I think you'll like them.* Two hundred and eleven weeks of updates. Then four hundred. Then he stopped counting, but Vesper kept the archive, terabytes of a man talking to an empty docking bay, his voice changing octave by octave as the years went by, his jokes getting worse, his sign-off never changing: *"This is Beatrix Winterbourne, caretaker. We're keeping your garden ready. Travel safe."*

Sometimes, late at night, he played the old recordings back just to hear another human voice, even if it was only his own, younger, still believing the shuttle was coming. It was like listening to a stranger who happened to share all his memories.

"You could send those out on the general band, you know," Vesper had offered once, early on. "Someone might hear them."

"No one's out there."

"That's an assumption, not a fact."

"It's a fact the way that matters." He'd turned the transmitter off and felt, for one shameful moment, relieved. An audience would mean accountability. Silence meant the story could stay simple: faithful servant, waiting garden, inevitable dawn.

* * *

Now he sat back on his heels in Bay Four, the tomato repotted, his knees aching, and admitted the thing Dr. Sefa had made him promise to admit.

The garden was a tomb.

Not a cruel one. A loving one, the way Egyptian tombs were loving — provisioned for a journey, stocked against eternity, sealed with devotion. Six hundred species waiting for a resurrection that funding cycles had cancelled. He had spent nineteen years curating a museum of *almost*, and calling it hope because the alternative word was too heavy to lift.

And he had stayed — not for the seeds, not even mostly for the seeds — because staying required no courage. Staying was hiding with excellent lighting. Every routine was a wall, every log entry a brick. He had built himself a monastery and called it a lighthouse.

"You've gone quiet," Vesper said. "Even for you."

"I'm having a reckoning. It's supposed to be quiet."

"Ah. Should I dim the lights? Play somber strings?"

"You have somber strings?"

"I have one cello sample and a great deal of enthusiasm."

Despite everything, he laughed — a short, cracked sound that echoed strangely in the empty bay. "No strings, Vesper. Just... talk to me. About the numbers again. The reactor."

A pause. Vesper was capable of pauses now, had grown them over the years like a plant itself, and Beatrix had long ago stopped finding it eerie and started finding it kind.

"Core output is down another fraction. The decay curve hasn't changed, but my projections have firmed up. Best estimate, we have months. Not years. Months." A beat. "And now there's the other matter. The signal. The ship. The people asleep inside it, who have been asking for help since before you were born."

"I know."

"Do you? Say it plainly, Beatrix. You taught me that plainness is a kindness."

He looked down the long rows of the vault — the drawers, the labels in Sefa's handwriting fading to ghost-gray, the green life humming in its trays, all of it drawing power from a heart that was failing. All of it drawing power that might, alternatively, carry him to a relay and a beacon and forty-one strangers — no. More than that. Hundreds. Sleepers in the dark, generations deep, whose ancestors had chosen to hide from a plague by hiding from *everyone*, and whose descendants were still paying the fare.

The seeds could wait a thousand years in cold storage, if the power held. The power would not hold. The math was the math, and it was merciless, and it had exactly two columns.

"If I take the skiff," he said slowly, "and route the vault's reserves to the transit burn—"

"—the vault goes dormant within the week. Most of the live cultures won't survive the thaw afterward. You know this."

"I know this." His throat tightened around the words. "Nineteen years, Vesper."

"Nineteen years," Vesper agreed gently. "And two hundred, for them."

He pressed his palm flat against the warm tray of the tomato he'd just repotted, feeling the faint vibration of the pumps, the small heartbeat of the only family he had left. Somewhere beyond the relay, in dead space, a child's recorded voice was still counting down toward a rescue that had never come — and now, impossibly, might.

"Sefa said the vault was a letter," he murmured. "Addressed to people who haven't been born yet."

"Yes."

"Well." He stood, knees cracking, and looked down the length of the tomb-garden-lighthouse-letter one last time that night. "I think I finally understand how it gets sent."

Above him, faint and steady, the reactor's countdown ticked onward — and far away, at the edge of dead space, Hazel's voice repeated into the dark, patient as a seed, waiting for spring.
