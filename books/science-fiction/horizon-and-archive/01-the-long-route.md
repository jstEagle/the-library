## 1. The Long Route

The freighter *Perpetual* ran the long route the way a clock runs: without opinion, without complaint, and mostly without anyone noticing.

Ines Lockridge had flown it for six years now. Meridian to Cael Harbor, Cael Harbor to Meridian, eleven days each way through the dark between two colonies that had stopped speaking to each other before she was old enough to remember why. The Severance, they called it — capitalized in every history text, explained in none of them. Two worlds, forty light-minutes apart at closest approach, sharing an atmosphere of silence so complete that Ines's little ship was the only thing still crossing it.

She'd been told once that she was a bridge. She preferred to think of herself as a courier between estranged parents, carrying packages neither would send directly to the other.

"Perpetual, this is Cael Harbor approach," said the tower, in the flat accent of people who hadn't spoken to Meridian voices in decades. "You're cleared for berth nine. Cargo manifest on file."

"Cleared and grateful," Ines answered, and brought her ship down through the amber haze of Cael's engineered sky.

* * *

Berth nine smelled like machine oil and someone else's cooking. Ines spent four hours supervising the offload — seed stock, water reclamation filters, medical isotopes, and the crates of paper books that Cael Harbor's archivists always ordered from Meridian's presses, because apparently even enemies agreed that some things should be printed rather than stored.

That was the joke she never made out loud: the two colonies wouldn't share weather data or vaccine strains anymore, but they still traded books. As if stories were too small to fight over.

While the dock crew worked, she walked the perimeter of the port with her thermos, as she always did, checking the atmospheric scrubbers against the readings she'd logged on arrival. Habit. Her mother had been a terraformer before her, and her grandmother before that, and somewhere back down that line the family had decided that a world wasn't finished until you could walk it and feel it breathing under your boots. Cael Harbor breathed shallowly. Meridian breathed better. Neither breathed right yet.

Ines checked anyway. It was the work. It was the only work anyone would let her do.

* * *

Back at Meridian, three days later, she went straight to the Foundling Garden.

It sat in a valley on Meridian's southern continent, forty hectares of soil that had taken three generations to make arable, planted with species chosen not for this decade but for the next hundred years. Trees that wouldn't fruit for sixty seasons. Grasses bred to survive nitrogen cycles that didn't exist yet. A garden seeded for colonists not yet born — that was the official description, stamped on the dedication plaque at the gate.

Unofficially, it was the loneliest place on two worlds, and Ines loved it the way you love a child who talks to you in a language you don't speak yet.

She walked the rows in the low gold light of late afternoon, sensor wand in hand, reading the soil the way other people read letters. pH holding steady. Microbial colonies establishing along the eastern terraces. The deep-root acacias showing first-year growth on their mycorrhizal networks — the trees were already talking to each other underground, which meant the soil was finally alive enough to carry conversation.

"Good," she told them. "Keep it up. You're doing better than we did."

Her voice sounded strange in the open air. That was the thing about the Garden — after days in the *Perpetual*'s cockpit, talking only to traffic control and cargo manifests, the sound of her own voice among living things hit her like cold water. She talked to the trees because no one else was there. She'd stopped pretending, years ago, that this was anything other than what it was: a woman raising a forest for ghosts.

Tomas would have laughed at that.

The thought arrived uninvited, the way it always did, in the space between one sensor reading and the next. Tomas Lockridge, dead eleven years this spring, who had wanted to be a terraformer too and instead had died in a shuttle accident over the northern ice shelf, his name carved into the memorial wall at Port Meridian alongside forty-one others. Ines had been twenty-two. She had stopped going to the wall after the third year, when she realized she couldn't remember his voice anymore, only the shape of it.

She knelt by the terrace edge and pressed her palm flat against the soil. Warm. Damp. Honest, as far as soil ever was.

"I'm back," she said, to no one, to everyone, to the garden.

* * *

The anomaly found her on Thursday, in the orbital archive, while she was filing her standard post-flight reports.

Meridian kept its records in a station above the planet — climate logs, shipping manifests, census updates, the whole administrative memory of a colony that had been building itself for ninety years. Ines went up twice a month to file her route data, and she'd developed the habit of browsing while the uploads ran. Not research, exactly. Just the idle curiosity of someone who spent most of her life alone with a console.

She pulled up the historical manifests first. She always did. There was something soothing about the old shipping records — the patient inventory of a young colony finding its feet. Grain shipments. Reactor parts. Seed vault transfers. Names of ships that had come and gone before her grandmother was born.

She was scrolling through the decommissioned vessels, half-listening to the upload progress chime, when the name caught her eye.

**HALCYON** — Colony-class transport. Commissioned Year 41. Decommissioned Year 58. Status: Destroyed, all hands lost, Year 58.

Routine. Tragic, probably, but routine. Ships were lost; colonies were hard places. Ines almost scrolled past it.

Then she opened the full manifest record, because the archive flagged a discrepancy — a single line item, buried in the Halcyon's final cargo declaration, that didn't match its maintenance logs.

And there, in the passenger registry attached to the last flight plan, printed in clean administrative type:

**LOCKRIDGE, TOMAS — Passenger, Deck 2, Berth 14.**

Ines sat very still.

The upload chime rang. She didn't hear it.

Tomas Lockridge. Her brother. Born Year 61 — three years *after* the Halcyon was decommissioned. Three years after it burned, or broke, or whatever happened to it out past the shipping lanes. A man who had been dead for eleven years appearing on the manifest of a ship that had stopped flying before he took his first breath.

She read the entry again. Then again. Then she checked the spelling, the birthdate field — blank — the ID number, which resolved to nothing when she queried it. Berth 14. Deck 2. A real berth, on a real deck, on a ship that had never carried him.

Ghost data, she thought. Corrupted record. Some clerk decades ago fat-fingered a name into the wrong file, and the archive had faithfully preserved the error ever since, the way archives preserve everything, including mistakes.

Except.

Except that the Halcyon's file wasn't corrupted anywhere else. She checked. Every other entry in the manifest matched its supporting documentation perfectly — cargo weights, crew rosters, fuel logs, all of it clean, all of it cross-verified. Forty years of routine audits, and nobody had ever flagged a single inconsistency.

Except one name.

Ines pulled up the ship's destruction report. Lost with all hands, Year 58, cause listed as drive failure during a routine crossing. Thirty-one souls. She read the crew list twice, looking for anything else that didn't belong, and found nothing — thirty-one names, thirty-one matching personnel files, thirty-one death certificates filed in proper order.

Then she noticed the flight logs.

Ships in the archive kept flight logs the way trees kept rings — every crossing recorded, every departure and arrival, right up until the day they stopped flying. The Halcyon's logs ended in Year 58, consistent with its destruction.

But the summary metadata — the automated tally the system generated from those logs — showed activity continuing for eleven more years. Departures. Arrivals. Fuel purchases. Docking fees paid at ports that the archive insisted the Halcyon had never reached.

Eleven years of flights by a ship that was destroyed. Eleven years of a ghost moving cargo between Meridian and Cael Harbor, in the exact years when the two colonies had stopped speaking to each other.

In the same years when, according to every textbook on both worlds, there had been nothing to move.

Ines closed the file. Opened it again. Closed it. Her heart was doing something complicated in her chest — part grief, part vertigo, part a feeling she didn't have a word for yet, the feeling of standing on ground you thought was solid and hearing, very faintly, something hollow underneath.

"Tomas," she said quietly, to the empty archive booth.

The name on the screen looked back at her. Passenger, Deck 2, Berth 14. Filed neatly. Preserved carefully. Erased nowhere.

Someone had put her brother's name on a ship that never flew. And someone, sometime in the last forty years, had either forgotten to remove it —

— or had left it there on purpose.

Ines copied the manifest to her personal drive, encrypted it, and sat for a long moment in the hum of the archive station, watching the slow rotation of Meridian below her, green and brown and beautiful, a world that had never lied to her the way its records just had.

Tomorrow, she told herself, she'd run the numbers again. Check the dates. Find some boring clerical explanation and put the whole thing away.

But even as she thought it, she knew she wouldn't sleep tonight.

Somewhere below her, in the dark of the Foundling Garden, the trees were growing in soil that remembered everything. It occurred to her, for the first time in six years of tending it, to wonder what else on this world might remember too — and who had decided it shouldn't.
