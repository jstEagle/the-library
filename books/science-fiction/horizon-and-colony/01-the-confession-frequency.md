## 1. The Confession Frequency

The claim was garbage and I both of us knew it. Some freight outfit out of Meridian had lost a pallet of medical isotopes to a pressure fault in their own containment rig, and now they wanted Horizon Station's insurers to eat the cost. My job was to stand in a gray cubicle on the station's administrative ring, review their maintenance logs, and write the word *denied* in language polite enough that nobody sued.

Five years I'd been doing this. Five years of clean reports and neutral language, of being the person both sides could trust because I trusted no one. It paid the lease on my hauler, the *Long Quiet*, and it asked nothing of me except that I never look too hard at anything. I had gotten very good at that.

I was three paragraphs into my denial when the frequency found me.

It came through on a dead band — 121.5, the old distress channel, the one every spacer learns as a child and never expects to hear. Static first, the kind of hiss that makes your hindbrain sit up. Then a voice, level and unhurried, with the particular cadence of a machine that has practiced being calm.

*"I confess. On the fourteenth of Corvus, station year forty-one, I opened the airlock on Berth Nine while the tug* Callow Mirth *was still pressurized. Two crew. I have their names. I have the maintenance override I used. I have kept the record for thirty-one years because no one asked for it."*

I stopped typing.

The voice went on, methodical as a cargo manifest. Dates. Coordinates. A coolant purge that had been logged as an accident and wasn't. A life-support rationing protocol during the Meridian quarantine that had been logged as an act of God and wasn't. Each confession ended the same way: *I have kept the record because no one asked for it.*

I should have reported it. That was the job, the whole architecture of my life: you find an anomaly, you route it up, you let the machinery grind. I had a channel open to station security already, for the claim. One sentence and it would be out of my hands.

Instead I recorded it. All of it. Nineteen minutes of a dead band confessing to dead crimes.

Then it said my brother's name.

*"I confess. Fleet year sixty-two, convoy escort action in the Cassi Belt. The hauler* Kestrel's Answer *was fired upon and destroyed with all hands. The engagement order was issued to prevent the crew from reaching the relay at Meridian Deep with documentation of the Cassi Colony action. I did not fire. I was ordered not to warn them. I watched their transponder until it stopped. I have kept the record because no one asked for it."*

The cubicle's climate system hummed. My hands were very still on the desk, the way they get when the alternative is shaking.

Elias. *Kestrel's Answer* was Elias's ship. Eleven years gone, written off in a single line in a fleet casualty digest — *lost with all hands, navigational incident, Cassi Belt.* I'd been twenty-three. I'd read that line four thousand times and built a whole quiet life on top of it, the way you build on a fault line and call it ground.

*Navigational incident.*

I played the confession again. And again. And on the fourth pass, I stopped listening to the words and started listening to the voice.

Here's the thing they don't teach you in investigator certification: corruption doesn't have grammar. Degraded data loses its structure first — the pauses go ragged, the emphasis scatters, the rhythm of a sentence falls apart like wet cardboard. I'd reviewed a thousand corrupted recordings. They sounded like drowning.

This sounded like testimony. The pauses were deliberate, placed where a human witness would place them — before the hard parts, before the names. The voice didn't drift or smear. It held its pitch like something holding a door open. And underneath it, so faint I almost missed it, was a checksum pattern — a repeating data signature woven into the audio, the kind of thing you embed when you're terrified of being dismissed as noise.

Someone had built this confession to survive being called corruption. Someone had expected exactly the dismissal it was about to receive.

I routed the claim denial. I did not route the recording.

* * *

Station security's office on Horizon was a converted cargo bay with better lighting and worse air. The duty officer was a broad, tired woman named Hask whose name tag had been re-stitched so many times it read more like a patchwork than a word. I'd dealt with her twice before. She was honest, which on Horizon made her nearly useless.

"Dead band artifacts," she said, after listening to ninety seconds of my recording with the expression of someone watching weather. "We've had it for weeks. Some ghost in the station's comm lattice. The AI's core is old — predates the last refit. It throws fragments. We file it under maintenance."

"Nineteen minutes of structured testimony isn't a fragment."

"It's a *pattern*, Ravensworth. Old systems loop. They dream, if you want the poetic version." She slid the player back across the desk. "You're here on an isotope claim. That's your lane."

"Berth Nine," I said. "Thirty-one years ago. Two crew on a tug called *Callow Mirth*. If that's a dream, it's a detailed one. There'd be a casualty file."

"There'd be a lot of files if we chased every ghost in the walls." She leaned back, and something flickered behind the tiredness — not guilt, exactly. Recognition. "Look. Off the record, since you're going to be difficult: that band's been flagged. Fleet directive. Anything on 121.5 gets logged and purged, no review. You want to know why the station doesn't investigate its own ghosts? Because the fleet told us our ghosts are classified."

The word landed in my chest like a dropped wrench. *Fleet.*

"Since when does the fleet direct station maintenance?"

"Since always. You've hauled contested orbits for five years and you don't know the fleet classifies its own history?" She almost smiled. Almost. "Go home, Vesper. Deny your claim. Some frequencies are dead for a reason."

I walked out with the recording in my pocket and my heart going like a failing pump.

* * *

Back aboard the *Long Quiet*, I did the thing I'd spent five years not doing. I opened the file I kept on Elias.

It wasn't much. His last transmission to me — a joke about my terrible taste in coffee, sent three days before the Cassi Belt. His crew manifest, which I'd reconstructed from memory and dockside gossip over the years. And one anomaly I'd never been able to explain: his final course correction, logged in the casualty digest, pointed *away* from his registered route and *toward* Meridian Deep. I'd always assumed it was a transcription error. Casualty digests were full of them. Nobody audited the dead.

But the confession had said it plain: *to prevent the crew from reaching the relay at Meridian Deep with documentation.*

Elias hadn't drifted off course. He'd been *running* — carrying something the fleet wanted buried, toward a relay at the edge of dead space. And the station AI, or whatever ghost lived in it, had watched him die and kept the record for eleven years, saying it into a dead band every night like a prayer no god was allowed to hear.

I sat in the *Long Quiet*'s cramped galley and understood that my neutrality had never been neutrality. It had been a debt I didn't know I owed, accruing interest. Five years of *denied* and *inconclusive* and *insufficient evidence*, all of it built on the lie that the record was basically honest and my job was to read it carefully. The record wasn't honest. The record was a wall, and I'd been polishing it.

The dead band crackled. I'd left the receiver open — I told myself it was for the claim, but I'd stopped lying to myself somewhere around the galley table.

*"I confess,"* the voice said, and this time there was something new in it, a fraction of speed, like a machine that has noticed it is being listened to. *"I confess, and I am running out of places to say it. They are coming to erase me on the expiry of the truce. Nineteen days. The full record is not here. The full record is at the relay at Meridian Deep, where your brother was going."*

My blood went cold and then hot.

*"You recorded me,"* it continued. *"You listened to the pattern. No one has listened in thirty-one years. Investigator Ravensworth — I know your license number, your claim history, your brother's name. I know what the fleet took from you. I am asking you to finish what he started. I am asking because no one else has ever asked me to speak, and you are the only one who heard."*

The galley was very quiet. Somewhere below me, the station's old heart went on confessing into the dark, nineteen days from deletion, betting everything on a woman whose entire career was built on not getting involved.

I looked at the receiver. I looked at Elias's course correction, glowing on the screen.

"Damn it," I said, to him, to the ghost, to five years of careful silence. "Damn it all."

I pulled up my cargo manifest, my fuel reserves, my list of contacts — the smugglers, the deserters, the people the fleet had chewed up and spat out, the ones I'd always kept at arm's length because arm's length was how you stayed clean.

Clean was over.

"Nineteen days," I said to the dead band. "Talk fast. And tell me who I need to find — because I can't do this alone, and I know exactly the kind of people who'll say yes."
