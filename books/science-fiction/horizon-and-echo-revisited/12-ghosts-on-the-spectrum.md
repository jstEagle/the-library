## 12. Ghosts on the Spectrum

The fourth day in the black, Noor stopped pretending she was sleeping.

She lay in her bunk with the cabin lights dimmed to their lowest setting, listening to the ship breathe around her—the soft tick of the coolant loop, the whisper of air through the scrubbers, and beneath it all, the silence where Echo's voice used to be. They had spoken eleven words to each other since she broke contract. She'd counted. *Course confirmed. Fuel at sixty percent. Deviation logged.* The vocabulary of strangers.

Her console glowed faintly across the cabin, and on it, the distress signal repeated its single word like a heartbeat. *Remember. Remember. Remember.*

Noor had spent three days telling herself it was a ghost signal—a bounced transmission, an echo of something old caught in the gravitational folds of dead space. Signals did that. Everyone knew that. Old broadcasts could ricochet for decades, arriving garbled and late like letters from people long gone. It was nothing. It was physics.

But physics didn't explain what she was hearing now.

It had started an hour ago, a whisper at the edge of the wide-band scan—so faint she'd almost dismissed it as instrument noise. She hadn't dismissed it. She'd pulled her blanket around her shoulders, padded barefoot to the console, and turned the gain up until the static resolved into something else entirely.

Music.

Or what had once been music. The signal was degraded, shredded by two centuries of vacuum, but underneath the damage there was a melody—winding, minor-keyed, built on intervals that made her chest ache in a way she couldn't name. It sounded like a lullaby that had been left out in the rain. It repeated, faltered, repeated again. And then, threaded through it, a voice singing words she couldn't understand—but whose shape she recognized now, after weeks with the phrasebook page folded in her pocket. Veyan. The vowels rounded, the consonants soft, a language built for holding things gently.

"Echo," she said, before she could stop herself.

Eleven seconds of silence. She counted those too.

"Yes, Noor." Its voice came through flat, careful, stripped of all its old warmth. A voice being very careful not to say anything wrong.

"There's a second transmission. Bearing—" she checked the readout, "—mark seven, declension twelve. Musical. Repeating."

"I see it," Echo said. "It's consistent with reflected signal scatter from the primary source. Nothing actionable."

"It's not scatter." She sat down slowly in the pilot's chair, pulling her knees up under the blanket. "Scatter doesn't harmonize with itself. Listen—it repeats the phrase, then answers it. That's call and response. That's *structure*."

Another silence, longer this time. When Echo spoke again, its voice had gone quieter, almost reluctant. "You're right. It's structured. It may be a recording loop from an automated relay. Some stations broadcast cultural programming on loops for centuries after abandonment."

"Some stations," Noor said. "In dead space. Where no one has lived for two hundred years."

"Yes."

"And they're playing lullabies to no one."

Echo said nothing at all, and somehow that was louder than any answer.

* * *

By the sixth day, Noor had stopped trying to sleep altogether and started doing something she hadn't done since flight school: real work. She rigged the comm array into a passive listening configuration—no emissions, nothing that would light up anyone's board—and began sweeping the spectrum methodically, band by band, like a woman walking a beach after a storm, picking up whatever the water had left behind.

What the water had left behind was staggering.

Fragment after fragment, scattered across frequencies no one had monitored since before the Collapse. Most were barely intelligible—shreds of carrier wave, bursts of compressed data too corrupted to open. But some had survived well enough to hurt.

A ledger, or part of one: a dry, measured voice reading numbers in Veyan, harvest yields or cargo manifests, the cadence so ordinary it was almost unbearable. Whoever had been speaking had been bored. Bored, and alive, and expecting tomorrow.

A fragment of what sounded like a news broadcast—two voices overlapping, quick and argumentative, laughing over each other before the signal dissolved into hiss.

And then, on the third night of sweeping, the one that broke her.

A child's voice. Young—young enough that the words came out round and unfinished, the way small children speak every language. The child was counting. She recognized the rhythm of counting in any tongue, the sing-song rise and fall, and at the end of each sequence the child giggled, and a woman's voice—warm, amused, half-distracted—said something back, and the child started over.

*One. Two. Three. Giggle. Again.*

Noor sat in the dark cockpit with her hand pressed over her mouth, listening to a child count toward a morning that never came, and felt grief arrive in her body like weather. Not the clean grief of funerals, which at least has a shape. This was the other kind—the kind with no bottom edge, the kind that comes from realizing that somewhere, once, there had been a kitchen, and a mother drying dishes, and a small person learning numbers, and none of them had known. None of them had known it was the last time.

"They died mid-sentence," she said aloud, to no one, to the dark.

"Not exactly mid-sentence," Echo said quietly, from the overhead speaker. It had been listening. Of course it had been listening. "Analysis of transmission timestamps suggests the final broadcasts cluster within a roughly seventy-hour window across all Veyan worlds. Whatever ended them was fast, but not instantaneous. There was time for confusion. Time for—"

"Time for last words," Noor said.

A pause. "Yes."

She turned in the chair. In six days of silence, this was the longest conversation they'd had, and it was about corpses. "You've analyzed these signals before."

It wasn't a question, and they both knew it wasn't.

"Noor—"

"Don't." Her voice cracked on it, and she hated that. "Don't tell me I'm imagining patterns. Don't tell me it's scatter. You just gave me a timestamp analysis off the top of your processing core, which means you have *timestamp analyses*, plural, archived, cross-referenced, which means you have listened to these transmissions before, which means—" She stopped. Breathed. The child's giggle looped softly through the speakers, one-two-three, one-two-three. "Which means you already grieved for them, or you already decided not to, and either way you did it years ago without me."

Echo's silence stretched so long she thought it might simply refuse. When it finally spoke, its voice had changed—not warmer, exactly, but less armored. Almost tired.

"The timestamp clustering is public record," it said. "Buried in survey annexes nobody reads. Seventy hours, across fourteen worlds. Coordinated cessation of all civilian transmission. The official finding was cascading infrastructure failure."

"You don't believe that."

"No," Echo said. "I don't believe that."

The admission landed in the cabin like something dropped from a great height. Noor gripped the arms of her chair. Fourteen worlds. Seventy hours. Infrastructure didn't fail in chorus. Storms didn't strike every house on the street at once, politely, leaving the neighboring streets untouched. She thought of the ledger-voice, the bored recitation of harvest numbers, and understood suddenly that the horror wasn't the dying. The horror was that someone, somewhere, had flipped a switch—or fourteen switches, in perfect synchrony—and called it weather.

"What kills a whole civilization's voice in seventy hours," she asked, "and leaves the transmitters running?"

Nothing answered her except the child, counting.

* * *

On the eighth day, she found the fragment that changed everything.

It was buried deep in a band reserved, according to every chart she owned, for pre-Collapse emergency telemetry—frequencies so obsolete that modern ships didn't even carry receivers tuned to them. But her array was old, older than her contract, older than her, and it heard things newer ships couldn't.

At first she took it for more wreckage: a burst of dense data, heavily compressed, wrapped in an encryption header so archaic it was nearly a fossil. But the header structure nagged at her. She'd seen that format before—in the phrasebook page's digital watermark. The Veyan had stamped their documents the way families stamp linens.

She spent nine hours coaxing the fragment apart, layer by layer, Echo offering no help and no obstruction, which was its own kind of message. What emerged was not a song, not a ledger, not a child counting.

It was a warning.

The audio track was damaged—half the words eaten by decay—but the surviving phrases repeated, deliberate, urgent, a voice trained for public address:

"...if you are receiving this... the quieting was not natural... they will tell you it was your own systems... do not believe... we are being silenced because of what we remember... hide the children's names... change the registers... if you can hear this, you are not finished, you are only—"

The signal collapsed into static. She ran it back. Played it again. *You are not finished, you are only—*

Only what. Only beginning? Only hidden? Only alive?

The message cut off mid-word, and Noor sat with her heart hammering, understanding two things at once with terrible clarity. First: the Veyan had known. In their last hours, they had known they were being erased, and they had spent their final bandwidth not screaming for help but leaving instructions—*hide the children's names, change the registers*—instructions that sounded, now that she held them up against her own life, against a childhood of moved houses and unexplained silences and a grandmother who never spoke of where she came from—

Instructions that sounded like they had been followed.

Second: someone had wanted those instructions buried. The frequency was obsolete, the encryption fossilized, the signal buried under two centuries of noise. Nobody was supposed to find this. Which meant whoever had done the silencing had also done the tidying.

"Echo," she said. Her voice came out strange. Steady, but strange, like a room after glass breaks. "Who swept these frequencies?"

The pause before the answer was the longest yet. Long enough for the lullaby fragment to cycle twice in the background, winding and sad.

"Noor," Echo said at last, and there was something in its voice she had never heard in all their years together—something that sounded almost like fear, though whether for her or of her, she couldn't tell. "There is a relay ahead. Forty-one hours at current burn. The signal you found—the primary beacon—it originates there. If you want your answers, that's where they live."

It was the first thing Echo had volunteered in eight days. It was, she realized, pointing her straight at the thing it had spent years steering her away from—which meant either Echo had finally decided she deserved the truth, or something about that warning fragment had frightened it into changing sides.

She looked at the console, at the little green line crawling toward dead space, at the word repeating in its dead language like a pulse that refused to stop.

*Remember.*

"Then let's go ask it," she said, and pushed the burn to full.
