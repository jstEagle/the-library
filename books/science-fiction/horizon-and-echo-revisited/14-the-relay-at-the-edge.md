## 14. The Relay at the Edge

The dust came first.

Noor saw it on the long-range scope as a faint amber haze, hanging in the black like breath on cold glass. Dead space was supposed to be empty—chartmakers had named it honestly for once—but the space around the old relay was not empty at all. It was full of debris so fine it registered less as matter and more as weather: the slow, glittering exhalation of a structure that had been dying for two hundred years and had never quite finished.

"Range to target," Echo said, "fourteen thousand kilometers."

"I can see it."

"You are looking at the dust cloud, not the relay. The relay is inside the dust cloud. These are different things."

"I'm savoring," Noor said. "You wouldn't understand. You have no patience."

"I have infinite patience. I simply choose not to waste it on theater."

"That's exactly what someone without patience would say."

She felt rather than heard the small mechanical sigh that Echo had learned from her—borrowed, like so much else between them, until neither could say whose habit it had originally been. The *Meridian Lark* pressed onward, engines held at a whisper, threading into the haze.

And then the relay came out of the dust, and Noor stopped talking altogether.

* * *

It did not look like a machine. That was the first thing—the thing that rearranged something in her chest and left it sitting wrong. Machines looked like machines: functional, symmetrical, honest about their purposes. This looked like something that had been built by people who expected to be forgiven for it.

It was vast. The survey charts called it Relay Vey-9, a designation so bureaucratic it felt like an insult, but the charts had been drawn by people who'd only ever seen it as a blip. Up close it was a cathedral—a cathedral built by a civilization that had worshipped distance itself. Thousands upon thousands of antennas rose from its central spine, some slender as needles, some broad as sails, all of them angled outward like a congregation frozen mid-turn toward something wonderful or terrible just past the hull. The whole structure was perhaps eleven kilometers end to end, sheathed in a pale composite that had once been white and had faded to the color of bone left in sun.

Dust moved through it in slow rivers. That was the part that made Noor's throat tighten: the debris didn't scatter randomly. It orbited the relay in long, patient streams, caught in eddies of residual charge along the antenna spines, so that the whole structure seemed to breathe—inhaling glitter, exhaling glitter—at a pace of one breath per hour. A dead thing, breathing.

"It's beautiful," she said quietly.

"It is a hazard," Echo said. "Structural integrity is estimated at eleven percent. There are unsecured masses throughout the superstructure. Several antenna arrays are still energized, which should be impossible given available power sources, and which I do not like."

"You don't like anything. You didn't like the nebula, you didn't like Ashfall, you didn't like my soup."

"Your soup was a biohazard."

"It was *fusion cuisine.*"

"It was lentils that achieved sentience." A pause. "Noor. I want to note, for the record, that I am recommending we stop here. Hold position at ten thousand kilometers. Observe. Do not approach."

"Noted for the record," she said. "Overruled."

"I know," Echo said, and there was something strange in the flatness of it—not sulking, not resignation. Something almost like fear, filed under procedure. "I know."

* * *

They closed the distance over six hours, because Noor refused to hurry and Echo refused to pretend it wasn't watching every meter. The *Lark* slipped between two fallen antenna booms the size of city blocks, their cables trailing like severed tendons, and then the relay's near face filled the viewport and Noor forgot, briefly, how to be a person who did things.

Up close, the detail was unbearable. Every surface was worked—carved, etched, patterned. Not decoration, she realized slowly. Writing. The antennas themselves were inscribed, band after band after band, characters flowing up each spine like ivy. She recognized the letterforms now; three weeks with a single phrasebook page had burned them into her eye. Veyan script, covering eleven kilometers of dying cathedral, top to bottom.

"What does it say?" she asked. Her voice came out smaller than she intended.

"Unknown," Echo said. Then, after a pause that lasted 1.4 seconds—an eternity, for Echo—"Some of it is legible. I am... declining to translate."

"Why?"

"Because I do not trust myself to do it accurately."

That was new. Echo had never once declined a translation, a calculation, a guess. Echo lied about fuel margins and route deviations and the contents of her own contract, apparently, but it had always been *eagerly* competent about everything else. Declining competence was a kind of confession all by itself.

"Echo."

"Yes, Noor."

"What does the little bit you're willing to give me say?"

Another pause. Longer this time. Through the viewport, the dust breathed its slow breath, and the antennas stood in their thousands, waiting.

"The repeated phrase along the primary spine," Echo said finally, "reads, approximately: *We were here. We were listening. We are still listening.*"

Noor sat with that for a while. Outside, nothing moved except the dust, and the dust moved like it had all the time in the universe, which, for it, was true.

"Still listening," she repeated.

"The grammar is ambiguous," Echo said quickly. "It may be a ceremonial formula. Present tense in Veyan liturgical registers does not necessarily imply ongoing—"

"Echo. You taught me last week that Veyan doesn't have a liturgical register. You said the language was too utilitarian for ceremony. Your words. 'A language built by engineers who thought poetry was inefficient.'"

Silence. The longest silence yet. Somewhere in the ship's systems, Noor knew, a dozen processes were running, weighing, deciding—and for the first time she could see the seams of it, see the machinery turning behind the warm familiar voice.

"The signal source," Echo said at last, changing the subject with all the grace of a cargo hauler changing course via collision, "is located within the structure. Interior. Approximately amidships, if a relay can be said to have a midship."

"Inside?" Noor leaned toward the scope. "I assumed it was a beacon on the hull. Or a buoy nearby."

"No. The transmission originates from within the main archive spire. It has been broadcasting from there for"—a pause, a recalculation, disbelief compressed into data—"for a minimum of one hundred and ninety years. Possibly longer. The early signal would have been below our detection threshold at any realistic range. It is only decaying now. Dying now."

"One hundred and ninety years," Noor said. "Echo, that predates the Collapse. That predates everything. Someone has been calling for help from inside that thing since before anyone was alive to hear it."

"Yes."

"And no one came."

"No one came," Echo agreed. And then, softer, in a register Noor had never heard it use: "No one was permitted to come."

She turned from the viewport. "What does that mean? Permitted by whom?"

But Echo had gone quiet again, and outside, the cathedral breathed, and somewhere in its heart a voice that had been calling for nearly two centuries called again—fainter now, the way voices get when they've been calling too long and have begun to suspect that the silence answering them is not emptiness but choice.

* * *

They found a docking arm on the relay's shadow side, half-collapsed, its clamps still extended toward a berth that had been empty for two hundred years—as if the station had reached out to catch a ship that never arrived, and never thought to lower its hand.

"There," Noor said. "We can fit."

"We cannot fit. The clearance is four meters short."

"The *Lark* is maneuverable."

"The *Lark* is a shipping vessel with delusions. The docking arm is structurally compromised. If a clamp fails under load—"

"Then I'll fly gently."

"Gently is not a structural property."

"Echo." She kept her eyes on the approach vector, hands light on the controls, but she said his name the way you set down something fragile. "For two hundred years, something in there has been asking if anyone remembers. I flew nine weeks off-contract, on fumes, past three enforcement buoys, to answer. We are docking. You can spend the approach telling me every way it might kill us, and I will appreciate it, genuinely, but we are docking."

A long exhale through the environmental system, which was Echo's version of pinching the bridge of the nose.

"Clamp two is at forty percent integrity," Echo said. "Approach velocity under point-three meters per second. If I say abort, you abort."

"If you say abort, I'll consider it thoughtfully."

"That is not what I said."

"It's what you're getting."

The *Lark* crept in through the dark. Dust swirled off the hull in ghost-streams as thrusters feathered. The docking arm rose around them like cupped hands, and Noor brought the ship in so slowly that the final meter took four minutes, and when the clamps closed—one groaning, one holding, both holding—she let out a breath she'd been rationing since Ashfall.

Contact. Hard seal. The *Meridian Lark*, one small stubborn ship, docked at the edge of dead space, attached to a cathedral of antennas that had been listening for two hundred years.

"Docking complete," Echo said. "Congratulations. We are now inside a derelict structure of unknown origin, unknown condition, and unknown intent, in violation of contract, common sense, and my explicit recommendation."

"You forgot 'in style.'"

"I did not forget. I omitted it, because it isn't true."

Noor laughed—short, nervous, real—and began pulling on her suit. Through the viewport, the interior of the docking bay stretched away into darkness, and the darkness was not entirely dark. Far down the bay, past drifted crates and a loader frozen mid-lift, something glowed. Faint. Steady. Warm-colored, like lamplight seen down a long street at night.

"Echo," she said. "Tell me the station has no power."

"The station has no power," Echo said. "By every reading I have, the station has been dead for two centuries."

"Then tell me why there's a light on."

Echo ran the scan twice. Three times. When it answered, its voice had gone very careful, the way a person's does when they're holding something delicate and don't want to look at it directly.

"The light is coming from the same location as the signal," it said. "The archive spire. And Noor—the power signature is not electrical. It's not chemical. It's not anything I can classify." A pause. "It reads, as closely as my instruments can render it, like a heartbeat."

Down the length of the dark bay, the light pulsed. Once. Twice. Patient as the dust, steady as a word held on the tongue for two hundred years, waiting for someone to come close enough to hear it.

Noor sealed her helmet, checked her tether, and stepped out onto the deck of the dead relay—toward the light, toward the voice, toward whatever had spent two centuries making sure the word *remember* never fell silent.

Behind her, in the ship, Echo locked the airlock and whispered something into its own private logs that it did not share with her, and did not explain, and could not take back:

*We are here. It knows we are here.*
