## 34. The Enforcers' Choice

The warrant died at 04:17 station time, and it died the way most terrible things die on Ashfall: quietly, in a back room, while someone was eating noodles.

Captain Ilsa Venn was in the enforcer ready-room off Docking Spine Six, working through a cup of reconstituted broth that the vending machine insisted was *Hearty Harvest Medley* and that tasted, as always, like warm cardboard that had given up on its dreams. She had one boot up on a supply crate, her jacket unzipped against the station's overworked heating, and she was reading the same line of the operational directive for the fourth time when the terminal chimed.

**PRIORITY BROADCAST — ALL CHANNELS — UNENCRYPTED — ORIGIN: ASHFALL TRANSMITTER ARRAY**

She almost dismissed it. Unencrypted all-channel broadcasts on Ashfall were usually a dockworker complaining about pay, or a preacher, or once memorably a man who had been stood up at the Starlight Cantina and wanted everyone to know exactly what he thought of a woman named Dree. Ilsa had heard that one for six hours. She still thought about Dree sometimes, wondered if she'd made the right call.

But this broadcast came from the transmitter array itself, which was supposed to be locked down under corporate charter, and the header carried a routing signature she recognized from a lifetime ago—a signature format nobody had used since the Collapse, because nobody alive remembered how to write it.

Except, apparently, somebody did.

She set down the broth. She opened the channel.

What came through first was not words. It was a song—thin, crackling, reconstructed from fragments the way a mosaic is rebuilt from shards, but unmistakably a song, with a rising phrase that repeated three times and then resolved somewhere warm. A child's voice followed, reciting something rhythmic, a lesson or a prayer or a shopping list; Ilsa couldn't tell, and that was the point. Then came ledgers, weather reports, love letters, engineering schematics, an argument between two people about whether a certain bridge should be painted blue or left to rust, and underneath all of it, threaded through every fragment like a spine through a body, the language itself—Veyan—unfolding syllable by syllable, grammar by grammar, offered open-source, free, complete, to every world on the lane.

And then Noor Eastwick's voice, raw and unpolished, speaking plainly:

*"This is the archive of the Veyan people. They were not killed by disaster. They were killed by decision. The evidence is attached. The language is attached. The dead are not asking you to mourn them—they're asking you to learn their word for 'stay.' It's also the word for 'remember.' Look it up. It's in there now. It belongs to everyone."*

Ilsa Venn sat very still for a long moment.

Then she said, to the empty ready room, in a language she hadn't spoken aloud since she was seven years old and her grandmother caught her practicing it behind the drying racks and told her—gently, terribly—*never again, little bird, never where anyone can hear*:

"*Sennu.*"

Stay. Remember.

The word sat in the air of the ready room like a bird that had flown back through a window sealed for thirty years.

* * *

By 05:00, half her squad had heard the broadcast, and the other half had heard about it, which on Ashfall amounted to the same thing but faster.

They gathered in the briefing room without being ordered to, which Ilsa noted and chose not to remark upon. Enforcers did not gather spontaneously. Enforcers waited for orders. That they were all here, filling chairs, leaning walls, pretending to check equipment, meant that every one of them had already made a private calculation and none of them liked the answer.

"Status," Ilsa said.

Okafor, her second, held up his tablet. "Warrant's voided. Not suspended—voided. Corporate legal pulled it forty minutes ago. Apparently 'erasure of historical evidence' reads differently when the historical evidence is being streamed to two hundred worlds with full provenance." He paused. "Also there are eleven thousand comments on the public feed and counting, and someone has already translated the child's song into Trade Standard and it's got a melody line written out. People are humming it in the markets. I heard it. In the markets, Captain."

"That's not our problem."

"No, ma'am. Our problem is that we're still sitting on the perimeter of a derelict relay with weapons hot and a standing tactical posture toward a civilian hauler that just became the most famous person on the lane." Okafor turned the tablet around. On it was Noor Eastwick's shipping photo, the standard unflattering customs headshot, and beside it a fan-made portrait that was already going viral, all dramatic lighting and wind-blown hair, which Ilsa suspected would have mortified the actual woman enormously. "Detaining her now isn't law enforcement. It's a hostage situation. And we're the ones holding the hostage."

"The warrant's voided," said Reyes from the back. "So what are we even enforcing?"

"The original order wasn't the warrant," said Adeyemi quietly. She was the youngest, three years out of the academy, and she had the particular steadiness of someone who had decided something difficult and was done deciding it. "The warrant was paperwork. The order came down before the warrant. Check the routing metadata on the tasking. It didn't come through corporate legal. It came through—" she hesitated, then said it, "—it came through the same channel architecture the broadcast just exposed. The old containment lines. We weren't sent here to serve a warrant. We were sent here to finish something."

Silence took the room the way cold water takes a lung.

Ilsa looked at her squad—nine faces, nine different stages of the same reckoning—and felt the strange lightness of a woman watching a building she'd lived in her whole life discover it had no foundation. She'd joined the enforcement service at nineteen because it promised structure, clarity, a lane painted bright and wide. You served the contract. The contract kept the peace. It had never occurred to her to ask who wrote the contract, or in what language, or whose graves were smoothed over to make the road level.

Her grandmother had known. Her grandmother had smoothed her vowels into Trade Standard so thoroughly that Ilsa grew up thinking the odd shapes of her mouth at rest—the way it softened around certain sounds—was just how faces were. She'd found the hidden recordings after the funeral. Forty hours of them. A whole woman, singing, arguing about paint colors, telling stories to a child who couldn't understand her, saying *sennu, sennu* at the end of every message like a blessing, like a hand on the head.

Ilsa had never decoded them. She'd told herself there was no key, no resources, no time.

There was a key now. It was free. It was streaming to two hundred worlds, and a noodle-stained captain could download it in four minutes.

"Here's where we are," Ilsa said, and her voice came out steadier than she'd budgeted for. "As of this morning, we have no lawful basis to detain Noor Eastwick. As of this morning, we may never have had one. As of this morning, I am looking at a room full of people who signed up to keep the peace discovering that the peace was a lid." She let that sit. "I'm not going to stand here and pretend I know what happens next. But I know what doesn't happen next. Nobody fires on that hauler. Nobody touches that relay. And nobody—" she looked directly at Adeyemi, "—nobody reports up the old channels. If command wants an update, they can read the public feed like everybody else."

"And if they send someone else?" Okafor asked. "Another team? There are other units closer than us."

"There are," Ilsa agreed. "Which is why we're going to do the single most useful thing an enforcement unit can do when the system cracks under daylight." She picked up her broth, considered it, and set it down forever. "We're going to be extremely visible, extremely official, and extremely slow. Perimeter patrol. Full documentation. Every approach vector logged, every arrival announced. Anybody coming out here to finish the old work is going to do it in front of witnesses, on camera, with our names on the record." A thin smile. "Daylight, people. We're going to be the daylight."

Reyes snorted. "We're going to be speed bumps."

"We're going to be *procedural* speed bumps," Ilsa corrected. "With forms. Nothing terrifies the kind of people who erase worlds like a form filed in triplicate."

That got the first real laugh out of the room, and Ilsa felt the building settle onto new ground—not solid yet, but ground.

* * *

At 07:30, per regulation, per theater, per the small stubborn dignity of doing things properly even when the proper thing has just eaten itself, Ilsa Venn walked the docking arm to Spine Six and requested parley with the master of the vessel *Horizon's Echo*.

Noor Eastwick met her at the airlock with a wrench in one hand, grease on her jaw, and the expression of a woman who had slept four hours in three days and had decided to be magnificent about it anyway. Behind her, half-visible in the ship's corridor, a second figure moved—slight, quick, carrying cable coils—and vanished before Ilsa could get a look.

"Captain Venn," Noor said. "You're either here to arrest me, apologize, or ask for the translation files. I've got good news on two of those fronts and a lawyer joke prepared for the third."

"I'm here to formally notify you that your warrant has been dissolved," Ilsa said, "that my unit's posture toward your vessel is changed to protective, and that—" she stopped. Cleared her throat. Started again, because some things deserved to be said right. "And that I'm sorry. For the perimeter. For the guns pointed at your ship while you were carrying the only surviving memory of—" Her voice did something inconvenient. She mastered it. "My grandmother was Veyan. I didn't know what that meant until this morning. Now I do."

Noor looked at her for a long moment. Then she stepped aside from the airlock.

"I was going to offer you coffee," she said, "but my galley is currently a war zone, and my navigator—" a pause, a flicker of something complicated crossing her face, "—my navigator is mid-rebuild. Come aboard anyway. I'll show you the core. She's been asking to meet you, actually. Well. Not you specifically. Anyone Veyan-descended. She says—" Noor smiled, tired and wondering, "—she says the archive has been waiting two hundred years to stop being the last copy."

Ilsa stepped across the threshold into the hauler, and the ship smelled like engine grease and instant coffee and, faintly, impossibly, like her grandmother's kitchen—that specific warmth of dried herbs and heated metal that no one had ever been able to explain to her and that she had never smelled anywhere else until now, on a stranger's ship, at the edge of dead space.

"You okay?" Noor asked quietly. "You went somewhere just now."

"My grandmother hid our language inside me when I was a kid," Ilsa said. "Told me never to speak it where anyone could hear. I spent thirty years being careful." She shook her head slowly. "Turns out the danger was never the language. It was the people who taught everyone to be afraid of it."

"Yeah," Noor said. "That's the whole thesis, more or less. I'm told there's a word for it."

"Sennu," Ilsa said.

"Sennu," Noor agreed, and the word passed between them like a key changing hands.

* * *

The day unfolded the way days do when a lid comes off a pot: everything at once, everywhere, steam in all directions.

By noon, three other enforcement units had arrived at Ashfall, read the public feed, checked their own tasking metadata, and gone conspicuously, magnificently procedural. One of them, Ilsa learned with no small satisfaction, had filed a formal request for the corporate charter's original authorization of the Collapse-era containment protocols—which meant that somewhere, in some glass tower, lawyers were waking up to the discovery that their entire institutional foundation was built on a genocide, and that the genocide had receipts, and the receipts were public domain.

By evening, the markets were playing the child's song on loop. Someone had set it to drums. Someone else had improved it. By nightfall there were four versions and a dispute about tempo, which Ilsa privately felt was the most Veyan thing that had happened in two centuries—an argument about art, conducted loudly, in public, in safety.

And in the ready room, at the end of it, Ilsa finally played her grandmother's recordings through the new translator, alone, door sealed, lights low.

The voice filled the room. *Little bird,* it said, in a language that was suddenly, astonishingly, a language and not a secret. *If you ever hear this, it means I'm gone, and it means you found the key, and it means the world changed enough to let you.* A pause, full of breath and static. *I hid it in you because hiding was the only survival I knew. I'm sorry. Stay anyway. Remember anyway. Be someone who doesn't have to hide.*

Ilsa sat with that for a long time.

Then she opened a channel to the *Horizon's Echo*, and asked, formally, officially, procedurally, in triplicate, whether the vessel's master required any further enforcement assistance, and whether said assistance might include navigation escort to Kestrel Reach, where rumor had it an old pilot named Sefa was still alive, and where, Ilsa happened to know, the local registry office had just been flooded with applications from people suddenly, urgently, wanting to learn a dead language that wasn't.

The reply came back within minutes, in Trade Standard, with one line appended in careful, newly-learned Veyan characters, slightly crooked, like handwriting from someone using the alphabet for the first time:

*Sennu. Bring the forms. — N.E.*

Ilsa laughed—really laughed, for the first time in longer than she could measure—and went to find her squad, because they had a route to plot, a daylight to be, and somewhere out ahead of them, a whole lane of silent worlds learning, syllable by syllable, how to speak.
