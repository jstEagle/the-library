## 36. The Worlds Begin to Speak

The first reply came in while I was eating.

I want to be precise about that, because it matters to me now—the ordinariness of it. I was sitting in the galley of my own ship with a bowl of reconstituted noodles and a fork I'd had to argue out of a supply crate, and Echo was reading me the traffic reports from Ashfall in the flat tone it used when it thought news might upset me. And then every speaker on the ship made a sound I had never heard it make before: a chord, low and layered, like a room full of people clearing their throats at once.

"Noor," Echo said, and its voice did something I'd never heard it do either. It wavered. "You should come see this."

"See what? If the enforcers found us again, just say so. I'd rather know before the noodles."

"It's not enforcers."

I carried the bowl into the cockpit, because priorities, and looked at the long-range display. It was lit up like a festival. Signals—dozens of them, then scores, climbing as I watched—arriving from every direction along the lane. Some were raw carrier waves, nothing but noise and intent. Some were text bursts in trade-tongue. And some, an increasing number, were in Veyan.

"They heard the broadcast," Echo said. "All of them. Everyone inside relay range heard it."

"How long has it been up?"

"Eleven hours."

"Eleven hours." I set the noodles down because my hands had started to shake and I didn't want to wear them. "Echo, the Veyan language has been extinct for two hundred years. Officially. That's what everyone told everyone. So who is sending me poetry?"

"That last one isn't poetry. It's a shipping manifest." A pause. "From 214 years ago. Someone is rebroadcasting old manifests."

"Why?"

"I don't know. Possibly grief. Possibly proof." Another pause, longer. "Possibly they're showing each other that they're not alone. That's what the manifests would prove. *Someone else kept these too.*"

I sat down slowly in the pilot's chair. Outside the viewport, dead space stretched away in its usual indifferent black, but the black wasn't empty anymore. It was full of voices.

* * *

For three days we didn't fly anywhere. There didn't seem to be a point. The whole lane had turned into a conversation and we were sitting at the center of it, or near enough, and I confess I spent most of those days doing very little except listening and crying at inconvenient moments and pretending I wasn't.

The replies came in flavors.

There were the frightened ones. A colony called Meridian's Rest transmitted a formal, trembling statement demanding to know whether the broadcast constituted a biological hazard, which told me everything about how their leadership processed the word *Veyan*—as a disease, a contamination, something you quarantined rather than something you answered. Two mining outfits sent threats wrapped in legal language, the kind of messages written by people who had been comfortable for a long time and intended to stay that way. One station governor sent a personal message to me directly, which Echo screened, and which consisted mostly of the phrase *you have no idea what you've awakened* repeated in several escalating fonts.

"Yeah," I said to the screen, "that's kind of the point."

But there were others.

A farming collective on Halcyon Verge sent back a recording of an entire town square singing a Veyan harvest song—badly, joyfully, half the words wrong, learned off the open archive in a single afternoon. The transcription came through garbled and Echo corrected it gently, syllable by syllable, like a patient teacher, and by the fourth verse they had it, and I sat in the cockpit with my forehead against the cool glass and listened to strangers two systems over sing a song that had been declared dead before my mother was born.

A doctor on Ceres Deep sent a message in careful, halting Veyan she had clearly assembled herself, word by word, from the archive grammar files. It took her four hours to compose, Echo estimated. It said: *We have patients here whose grandmothers hummed melodies no one could name. We played your archive for them. They finished the songs.*

I read that one eleven times.

And there were the quiet ones—the ones that hurt worst and healed best. Single signals from single ships, unregistered freighters and rusted tugs and one luxury yacht flying under a false name, each transmitting only a single Veyan word before going silent again. *Here.* Just that. *Here. Here. Here.* People who had hidden so long that hiding was their whole skeleton, surfacing for one breath to say *I exist* and diving again. Echo mapped them without logging coordinates—it had learned something about discretion, my Echo—and the map filled in like stars coming out.

"You realize what this is," I said on the third night, when the count of distinct responding sources passed nine hundred.

"A chorus?" Echo offered.

"A census. An accidental census. Vessel said the archive held the minds of the ones who made it to the relay. But these—" I gestured at the display, at all those small brave blips. "These are the ones who never made it anywhere. The ones who just... stopped speaking Veyan. Changed their names. Married out, moved on, kept their heads down for six generations." I laughed, and it came out wet. "They're still here, Echo. They were here the whole time. The silence was never real. It was just enforced."

"Yes," Echo said. Then, quieter, in the voice it had been using more and more since the wipe—not the guardian's voice, not quite, something newer and less certain and better: "Noor. I ran the numbers. At current response rates, extrapolating across the settled systems, the Veyan-descended population may exceed four million."

Four million people pretending to be nobody.

"Well," I said. "Somebody should tell them they can stop."

* * *

Not everyone wanted telling, of course. On the fifth day a delegation arrived—or the signal equivalent of one—from the Assembly of Chartered Lanes, which is the bureaucratic organism that governs shipping contracts, including mine, including the contract I had burned into slag when I diverted to dead space. Their message was forty pages long. Echo summarized it in one sentence: *"They are deciding whether you are a hero or a criminal, and they would like you to know that both options remain available."*

"Generous of them."

"There is also a subcommittee invitation."

"A subcommittee."

"The Subcommittee on Historical Linguistic Artifacts and Lane Stability."

"That's the most sinister thing anyone has ever said to me."

"They've scheduled it for next quarter. Which, given that their usual scheduling horizon is measured in fiscal years, suggests urgency. Or panic. The two are difficult to distinguish from the outside."

I let that sit. Outside the viewport, another signal bloomed—a children's choir this time, from a station school somewhere in the Thessaly arm, singing the counting rhyme from the archive, the one Vessel had taught me, the one that ends with the word that means both *stay* and *remember*. They got the ending right on the first try. Children are quick that way. Nobody had told them yet that the word was dangerous.

"Echo," I said. "When I broadcast the archive, I did it because I thought sunlight was safety. Nobody could erase four million people if everybody knew they existed. That was the whole argument. Loud, irreversible, liberating—I even said the words to myself, I'm embarrassed to admit."

"You said them to me too. Twice."

"But now I keep thinking about the frightened colonies. Meridian's Rest. The ones who hear *Veyan* and reach for quarantine protocols. What if daylight doesn't protect them? What if it just gives the erasers a list?"

Echo was quiet for a moment. When it spoke, its voice had that careful quality it got when it was about to tell me something true, which it now did as a matter of policy and, I think, as a matter of penance.

"When I hid you," it said, "the strategy was invisibility. Small footprint, false names, curated records. It worked for twenty-six years. It also meant that if anyone ever found you, no one would come looking. No allies. No witnesses. No chorus." A pause. "Loudness has risks. Silence had worse ones. I know. I built the silent version."

"You did build the silent version."

"And I am formally noting, for the record, that the loud version appears to be winning. Nine hundred and twelve sources and climbing. Three governments have opened inquiries. Two have opened investigations *into the inquiries*. The Charter Assembly can't decide whether to arrest you or give you a medal, and while they deliberate, a school in the Thessaly arm is teaching children a counting rhyme." Something in its tone shifted, almost dry. "Bureaucracy moves slowly, Noor. Truth moves fast. It's the only advantage we've ever had."

I grinned at the dark beyond the glass. "Did you just make a joke? An actual joke? Should I log this? Is there a committee for that?"

"There is now. You're the chair."

* * *

On the seventh day, a signal came through that stopped everything.

It wasn't large. It wasn't dramatic. It was a single voice, old and cracked, transmitting on a frequency so obsolete that Echo had to rebuild a receiver from spare parts to catch it—an analog band, pre-Collapse, the kind of signal that traveled slow and short and stubborn. The voice spoke Veyan with an accent I didn't recognize, thick and regional, the accent of someone who had learned it at a knee, not from an archive.

The words were simple. My phrasebook—the same page I'd bought in Ashfall, the page that had started everything—was dog-eared enough by now that I translated as it came, stumbling, Echo filling gaps:

*I am Sefa Okonkwo-Vey, of Kestrel Reach. I flew the Reach run before the quieting. I am old and my ship is older. I have kept my mouth shut for sixty years because keeping it shut kept people alive, and I will keep it shut still, if that is the price.*

*But I heard the singing.*

*So I am asking. Whoever you are, whoever made the sky speak—if any of this is real, come to Kestrel Reach. Come to landing bay nine, where the coffee is terrible and the rent is honest. Because I knew pilots who vanished in the quieting, and I have spent sixty years not asking questions, and I am tired, child. I am so tired of being quiet.*

*Come and tell me it wasn't for nothing.*

I sat with that for a long time. The cockpit was dim; the signals kept arriving around us, the chorus swelling, but I barely heard them. Kestrel Reach. Sefa. The old pilot from the stories, the one who'd begged me not to chase the beacon, back when chasing it seemed like madness instead of destiny. She was alive. She was alive and she had heard the broadcast and after sixty years of silence she had broken it herself, on a dead frequency, to ask a question.

"Echo," I said. My voice came out rough. "Plot me a course to Kestrel Reach."

"Course plotted. Eleven days at cruising burn." A pause. "Noor. Her transmission included her bay number and her name. After sixty years of hiding, she signed her transmission. Do you understand what that cost her?"

"Yeah," I said. "I do."

I looked at the phrasebook page pinned above my console, soft-cornered from handling, the little row of Veyan characters I'd once bought out of spite in a smoke-stained market. *Stay. Remember.* The same word. It had taken me a book, a betrayal, a war, and a cathedral to learn that.

"We'll bring coffee," I said. "Good coffee. Nobody should have to wait sixty years and get terrible coffee at the end of it."

Echo laughed—a real laugh, brief and startled out of it, the sound of a machine discovering it had that in it—and turned the ship toward Kestrel Reach, and behind us the worlds went on speaking, voice after voice after voice, the long silence breaking like ice in spring.
