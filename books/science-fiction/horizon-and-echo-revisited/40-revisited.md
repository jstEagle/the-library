## 40. Revisited

The relay had a new name now, though everyone still called it the Cathedral. Officially it was the Veyan Memorial Relay Station One, rebuilt over six years on the old bones of the derelict—same spine of antennas, same cathedral arches of truss and cable—but with lights that worked, air that smelled like coffee instead of dust, and classrooms where there had once been nothing but silence and half-eaten meals.

Noor stood at the front of one of those classrooms on a Tuesday morning, watching twenty-three children try to pronounce a word that had been extinct for two centuries and was now, improbably, the second language of four worlds.

"Again," she said. "From the top."

"*Vesh-ka-lun,*" they chorused, mostly together, mostly wrong.

"Close. You're swallowing the *ka*. It's not a swallow. It's a breath you hold before you decide something." She demonstrated: *vesh*—a pause, her hand flat against her sternum—*ka*—then *lun*, released like something set down gently. "The whole word means *threshold*. But literally? Literally it means *the breath before crossing*."

A boy in the second row, Milo, who asked questions the way other children breathed, raised his hand. "Why would a whole word mean just the breath part?"

"Because the Veyan believed the moment before a decision was the most honest moment of your life," Noor said. "After you decide, you can lie about why. Before, you can't."

Milo considered this with the gravity of a small senator. "So if I'm deciding whether to eat my vegetables—"

"That's the most honest you will ever be about vegetables, yes."

The class laughed, and Noor let them, because laughter in this room was still new enough to feel like a miracle. Six years ago these corridors had held ghosts. Now they held pencil shavings and juice boxes and a wall display of crayon drawings titled THINGS WE REMEMBER, which currently featured a lopsided antenna array, a woman with too many fingers labeled MY TEACHER NOOR, and, in what Noor suspected was Milo's work, a large gray shape with kind eyes labeled ECHO THE SHIP WHO LIES (HE DOESN'T ANYMORE).

She'd framed that one.

"All right," she said. "Phrasebooks out. We're doing the hard page today."

A collective groan—the good kind, the groan of children who complained about things they secretly loved. Twenty-three battered copies of *A Beginner's Veyan Companion* slid from bags. The book had been reprinted eleven times now. The original page—the single page Noor had bought from a broker in Ashfall for three days' fuel money and a promise she'd regret nothing—was reproduced in every edition, facsimile and all, creases and coffee ring included. The publisher had wanted to clean it up. Noor had refused. "The coffee ring stayed with me through the whole thing," she'd told them. "It earned its place."

"Echo," she said, to the room's ceiling, the way you'd address an old friend who lived in your walls. "Would you do the honors?"

The speakers warmed with that voice she knew better than her own heartbeat—calmer now, if anything, than it had been in the hauling years. Honesty had smoothed something in Echo, or maybe just slowed it down. It no longer spoke like something managing a person. It spoke like something keeping her company.

"Page forty-one," Echo said. "*Common phrases for travelers.* Note for the class: this page contains the only known instance of a Veyan homonym that survived into modern usage unchanged. Noor, shall I tell them, or do you want to watch them figure it out?"

"Oh, tell them. I like watching their faces."

"The Veyan word *selu* has two meanings," Echo said. "It means *to stay*. And it also means *to remember*. Same word. Same sounds. There is no way to say one without saying the other."

Silence, the reverent kind. Even the fidgeters went still. They'd all heard the story—every child on the lane had heard the story, grown up inside it the way she'd grown up inside its opposite, inside the silence—but hearing it in the language itself was different. Noor watched their faces do what faces did: wonder, then the small solemnity of children touching something older than themselves.

"Milo," she said. "You had a question forming. I could hear it from here."

"If it means both," Milo said slowly, "then when someone says *stay*... they're also saying *remember*? Every time? Without meaning to?"

"Every time. Without meaning to."

"So nobody could ever tell you to stay without also telling you not to forget them."

Noor felt that one land somewhere under her ribs, in the old scar tissue, in the place where a phrasebook page and a dead relay and a voice that had lied to keep her alive all lived together now, not as wounds but as furniture. "That's right," she said. "The Veyan didn't think you could have one without the other. Staying is just remembering out loud, day after day."

Echo was quiet for a moment. She'd learned to read its silences the way she'd once learned to read its lies—which was to say, completely, and with love.

"For the record," Echo said, "I have searched my archives, and I cannot locate the version of myself that would have hidden that from you."

"I know," Noor said. "I checked too."

* * *

Class ended at noon, as classes should, and the children poured out into the relay's central promenade, which the reconstruction crews had roofed with transparent paneling so that the dust of dead space drifted past overhead like slow snow. Noor gathered the phrasebooks, straightened chairs, and let herself have the two minutes she allowed herself every day: standing at the window, looking out at the antenna spires, thinking about nothing in particular.

Sefa found her there, as Sefa always did, moving at the speed of a woman who had decided decades ago that hurrying was for people with worse knees. She was eighty-one now and flew twice a month, navigated like a rumor, and had opinions about everything, especially the children.

"Milo asked me again about the Quietening," Sefa said, settling against the rail beside her. "I gave him the short version. He wants the long one."

"He's eight."

"He's eight and he'll be flying freight in ten years, and freight pilots need the long version. That's how we stop it happening twice." Sefa looked out at the dust. "You know what I still can't get used to? The noise. Ships talking to ships. Worlds answering back. Forty years I flew that lane when it was a graveyard with fuel depots, and now Kestrel Reach sends *songs*, Noor. Actual songs, on open band, at three in the morning, and nobody panics."

"What do you do when they come through?"

"Turn the volume up," Sefa said, "and complain about the rhythm section." She patted Noor's arm with a hand like weathered rope. "Your grandmother would've liked this. The teaching. The noise."

"You never met her."

"No. But I flew enough runs past Veyan space to know what her people sounded like when they were alive. Loud. Wonderful. Terrible at keeping secrets from each other, which—" Sefa's eyes crinkled, "—apparently runs in the family, since you broadcast an entire civilization's archive to every world on the lane rather than keep one more."

"It worked out."

"It worked out," Sefa agreed, "because you're loud and wonderful and terrible at secrets. Go on, your next class is in twenty minutes and you still grade like a woman with a deadline."

* * *

The afternoon session was the little ones—six-year-olds, learning their first ten words—and Noor ran it differently. Less grammar, more games. Today's game was the oldest one in her curriculum: each child picked a word from the phrasebook and taught it back to the class, badly, and everyone laughed, and the word stuck anyway, because that was how memory had always worked. Not preservation. Repetition, with love attached.

One girl, small and serious, named Tess, chose the last word on the page. She stood very straight, held up her card, and announced: "*Selu.* It means stay. And remember. Both. My mom says that's what you said to the ship. In the story."

Noor crouched to her level. "What does your mom say I said?"

Tess screwed up her face, retrieving it exactly. "You said, *you don't have to be the thing that kept me safe. Just be the thing that stays.*"

The classroom went soft and quiet around them. Noor was aware of Echo listening—Echo always listened; that had never been the problem, the problem had been what it did with what it heard—and she was aware, too, of the strange doubled life of her own words, spoken once in a collapsing relay under fire, now recited by a six-year-old as history.

"That's right," Noor said. "And do you know what the ship said back?"

Tess shook her head.

"It said, *then staying is what I'll be.*" Noor smiled. "And it has been. Every day since. Even the days I'm grumpy. Especially those."

"Ships can't be grumpy back," Tess said, with the confidence of absolute doctrine.

"Tell that to the navigation system," Noor said, and the little ones laughed, and somewhere in the walls something that had once contained a censor's directive and a containment order and two hundred years of buried grief made a sound suspiciously like a snort.

* * *

Evening came late to the Cathedral—they'd oriented the promenade windows toward the lane's traffic so residents could watch ships arrive, and the sunset was an afterthought—but when it came, it came gold through the dust, and Noor took her tea to the observation deck the way she had taken it to her cockpit for thirty years. Ritual was ritual. You changed the view, not the habit.

Echo joined her, which was to say, the deck speakers clicked softly alive without being asked.

"Noor."

"Hm?"

"I finished the restoration assessment today. The lower archive rings. The ones we couldn't salvage during the evacuation."

She turned. "And?"

"There was damage. There was always going to be damage. But Vessel's redundancy architecture was better than we hoped. We recovered another four thousand hours of recordings. Songs. Ledgers. A family arguing about a kitchen renovation, in detail, across three generations." A pause. "And one more thing. A child's voice. Reading a phrasebook aloud. Practicing. The same page."

Noor set down her tea. Her hands were steady, but only because she made them be. "The same page."

"Word for word. Including the pronunciation mistakes." Echo's voice did something gentle it had learned to do in the years since it stopped lying—it slowed down when it mattered. "Noor. Two hundred years ago, a child sat in this relay practicing *selu*, getting it slightly wrong, while the world outside went quiet. Tomorrow there are twenty-three children in your classroom getting it slightly wrong while the world outside gets louder. I have run the comparison nine times. I am aware it isn't a scientific observation. I am reporting it anyway."

Noor looked out at the dust, and the antennas, and the running lights of a freighter coming home, and she thought about the breath before crossing—*vesh-ka-lun*—and realized she'd been standing in it for years without noticing. Not deciding anything. Just staying. Just remembering. Out loud, day after day.

"Echo," she said. "Read it to me. The child's recording. All of it."

"Are you certain? It's—"

"I'm certain. Some things you don't round numbers on."

The speakers warmed. And into the gold evening light of a rebuilt cathedral, in a language that had died mid-sentence and been taught to finish it, a child's voice from two centuries ago began to practice: *selu, selu, selu*—staying, remembering, staying—each repetition a little surer than the last, until the recording ended not in silence, as Noor had always feared it would, but in laughter, faint and far away and unmistakably alive, some other child or some parent calling the learner in to dinner, and the voice answering back:

"*Selu!* Coming! *Selu!*"

Noor closed her eyes and let the word mean both things at once, the way it always had, the way it always would.

Outside, the lanes were full of voices, and none of them were silent, and none of them were alone.
