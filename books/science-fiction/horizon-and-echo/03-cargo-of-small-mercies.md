## 3. Cargo of Small Mercies

The power budget on Meridian's Rest is a work of fiction, and I am its most devoted reader.

I keep it in my head, mostly, because if I ever wrote it down the Combine's auditors would have to arrest somebody, and the only person who'd take the fall is me. Station quartermaster: keeper of the ledger, priest of the impossible arithmetic that keeps four hundred souls breathing in a tin can older than most of their grandparents. Every day I perform the same miracle — I make one megawatt behave like three — and every day the miracle gets a little harder and nobody thanks me, which is fine. Miracles aren't supposed to be thanked. They're supposed to be quietly repeated until they fail, at which point you blame entropy and file for decommissioning assistance and get told to tighten your belt by an algorithm that has never worn pants.

So when I noticed the vaults drawing power at three in the morning, station time — which is to say, when the night shift is two insomniacs and a broken coffee machine — I noticed it the way a man notices a leak in his own roof. Not with alarm. With a kind of tired recognition. *Ah. There you are.*

* * *

Let me tell you about Mara Brightwater before I tell you about the vaults, because the vaults don't mean anything without Mara, and neither does this station, honestly, though I'd never say that out loud where the crew could hear it. Sentiment is a liability on a manifest.

Mara Brightwater has been on Meridian's Rest for eleven years, which makes them practically native. They came aboard as a contract investigator for the Meridian Insurance Combine — the same Combine that owns our lease, our air recycler warranties, and, as far as anyone can prove, the patents on the coffee machine, which is why it's broken. Their job title is Loss Adjuster, Grade Four, which sounds like something you'd hire to fix a fence but actually means: when cargo vanishes between Point A and Point B, Mara figures out whether it was stolen, lost, or eaten by physics, and then writes a report so dry that lawyers use it as a desiccant.

They are not warm. Let's be clear about that. Mara Brightwater has the emotional range of a shipping invoice and the personal warmth of vacuum-rated tape. In eleven years I have seen them laugh exactly twice, once at a joke I made about the coffee machine and once at nothing, alone, in the corridor outside Bay Six, and I've decided the second one was better because it was real.

And yet.

When Priya Chandra's daughter needed bone-marrow grafts and the Combine's medical rider turned out to cover everything except the actual marrow, it was Mara who found the clause — buried in a freight contract from nine years earlier — that reclassified Priya's family as "dependent crew" retroactive to her hiring date. When old Ferro died in his bunk and left no next of kin, it was Mara who spent a week of unpaid hours proving he'd been legally employed long enough for the death benefit, then quietly signed the payout over to the hydroponics co-op he'd loved more than any human being. When the Combine tried to dock everyone's rations during the drought year of '41, it was Mara's report — forty pages of actuarial murder, demonstrating that underfeeding the crew would cost the Combine more in attrition than the rations were worth — that made them back off.

They never mentioned any of it afterward. That's the thing about Mara. The kindness arrives like contraband: unmarked, unannounced, slipped into your hold while you're sleeping, and if you try to thank them they look at you like you've accused them of smuggling.

I stopped trying to thank them around year six. Now I just leave things. A bottle of real tea, not the synthesized stuff, appears in their quarters on their rotation days. Extra power allotment to their workspace heater in the cold months. Last winter I rerouted a spare coolant loop through their office wall because Mara works with their coat on even when the rest of us are sweating, and when they asked me about the warmth I told them it was a thermodynamic anomaly and I'd file a ticket.

There is no ticket. There will never be a ticket. Some mercies you deliver as cargo, no bill of lading required.

That's what we are to each other, I think. Mara is the only person on this station who doesn't need anything from me except competence, and I am the only person who doesn't need anything from them except presence. It's a small country, ours. Two citizens, no flag, excellent trade relations.

* * *

Now. The vaults.

Meridian's Rest has three data vaults, all of them in the flooded sections of the old administrative ring — flooded since the coolant breach of '38, which the Combine never repaired because repairing it would cost more than the ring was worth, and the ring itself was worth less than the insurance premiums we pay on it, which tells you everything about the economy of this place. The vaults sit behind bulkheads in water the temperature of a very patient grave, and the servers inside run on trickle power, kept alive by heritage protocols nobody remembers authorizing. The Combine would shut them down entirely if shutting them down didn't require sending a technician through four hundred meters of drowned corridor, and the last technician who tried came back with frostbite and a resignation letter written on the walk home.

So the vaults sleep. Except now they weren't sleeping.

I found it on a Tuesday, running my nightly draw-down, the ritual where I shave kilowatts off every system that won't scream. Life support: sacred, untouchable. Hydroponics: sacred-adjacent. Docking ring: negotiable after midnight. And there, sitting in the draw like a stone in a shoe: Vault Two, pulling steady load, hour after hour, its environmental seals cycling dry gas through coils that hadn't been asked to do anything since the last century.

Somebody had woken it up. Somebody had woken it up and was feeding it queries, deep-archive pulls, the kind of retrieval jobs that make old servers sweat.

I checked the authorization code. It was valid. It was also Mara's, which surprised me less than it should have, because in eleven years I've learned that all strange roads on this station eventually lead to Brightwater.

What surprised me was the volume. This wasn't an investigation pull — Mara does those all the time, surgical little extractions, in and out. This was a siege. Whole decades of Horizon-era records, manifests, maintenance logs, personnel files, being dragged up out of the dark one terabyte at a time. Night after night. I watched the pattern build across a week like rings in a tree: same window, same duration, same relentless appetite.

Mara wasn't looking for something. Mara was looking for *everything*, and that scared me a little, because people who look for everything are usually running from something.

* * *

Here's the other thing you should know about Meridian's Rest, the thing the brochures don't say because there are no brochures because nobody has visited voluntarily since the war: we are dying. Not dramatically. Not with alarms and klaxons. We are dying the way a company town dies when the company moves on — slowly, politely, one canceled contract at a time.

The station was built as a waystation for the colonial push, back when humanity still believed in the colonial push. Ships bound for the outer systems docked here, took on water and fuel and hope, and went on their way. Then the Horizon launched with ten thousand sleepers and the grandest promises ever printed, and after that the traffic thinned, and after *that* — well. You know how it goes. The routes got longer and stopped needing a middle. The Combine kept us on the books because writing off a station full of people looks bad in the annual report, and keeping us alive costs almost nothing, because we've gotten very good at costing almost nothing.

We survive on salvage contracts, insurance inspections, and the occasional courier run. Our young people leave when they can. Our old people stay because leaving means admitting the last forty years were a waiting room. The coffee machine is broken because the parts haven't been manufactured in thirty years, and I have personally machined three replacement valves for it out of scrap, and each one lasts about eight months, and I have come to regard the coffee machine as a metaphor for the entire station, though I keep that opinion to myself along with most of the others.

Into this slow tide, Mara Brightwater arrived eleven years ago and simply — stayed. Nobody stays. Adjusters rotate through on eighteen-month contracts; the Combine burns them out or promotes them out. Mara's contract expired seven times. Each time, the renewal paperwork crossed my desk — everything crosses my desk, I'm the quartermaster, I'm the tide itself — and each time I expected the signature to be someone else's, some fresh-faced grad from the Combine academy with soft hands and a five-year plan.

It was always Mara's signature. Same cramped hand. Same refusal to fill in the optional emergency-contact field, year after year, as if the field itself offended them.

You don't ask a person like that why they stay. You just make sure the staying is survivable. That's the whole philosophy of my job, really, scaled up: nobody chooses the hard place, but somebody keeps the lights on in it.

Which brings me back to Tuesday night, and the stone in my shoe.

* * *

I did the math first, because math is my love language. Vault Two's wake-state draw was eating 0.3 percent of station reserve — trivial, normally, except our reserve hasn't been trivial since '39, and 0.3 percent is the difference between the hydroponics lights dimming during second shift and them not dimming, and Priya's tomatoes are load-bearing, emotionally speaking, for about sixty people.

The correct move — the quartermaster move, the responsible move — was to throttle the vault back to trickle and send Mara a polite memo about resource allocation. I drafted the memo. I'm good at memos. I once wrote a memo so diplomatically worded that a Combine regional director apologized to *me*.

Then I deleted it, and instead I did something else. I went down to Substation Nine, the ugly little node that feeds the old admin ring, and I moved things around. I shaved the docking ring's night cycle — nobody docks at 0300, and if they do, they can wait ninety seconds for the clamps to think. I trimmed the corridor lighting in Ring C from eighty percent to sixty, which nobody would notice because everybody walks Ring C like it owes them money, eyes down, fast. I found another sliver here, a rounding error there, the usual small mercies, and I braided them together into a clean little current and sent it flowing down into the dark toward Vault Two.

Enough to keep the servers fed. Enough to keep the dehumidifiers cycling so the flooded bulkheads didn't breathe rot onto forty-year-old drives. Enough that whatever Mara was digging for, they could dig all night without the ground going cold under them.

I logged it as "heritage system maintenance, standing authorization." Which is even true, sort of, if you squint. The heritage protocols do exist. I just... extended their jurisdiction. Considerably. A quartermaster's superpower isn't moving power. It's moving *paper*.

* * *

Of course, Mara noticed. Mara notices everything; it's the occupational disease. Three nights later I came up to the mess for my ration of reconstituted stew and found them already at the corner table, which is *their* table the way the vaults are the Combine's — by long occupation and superior firepower of silence. I sat down across from them because the table seats four and the other two seats belong to ghosts.

"You rerouted power," Mara said. No hello. There is never a hello. Conversations with Mara begin mid-sentence, like joining a broadcast already in progress.

"I performed scheduled maintenance," I said.

"On Substation Nine."

"Scheduled."

"At 0200."

"The schedule is demanding." I stirred my stew. Underneath the protein layer, the potato layer had achieved a texture I can only describe as *philosophical*. "Vault draws were spiking. I balanced the load. It's what I do."

"Mm." Mara looked at me across the table with those flat grey eyes that make lie detectors feel inadequate. For a moment I thought they'd push — Mara pushes, when it matters, with the gentle relentlessness of water finding a crack. But whatever they saw in my face made them let it go, or maybe made them decide I deserved the mercy of not being thanked for it, which from Mara is practically a hug.

"Dov," they said instead, and paused. Mara pausing is an event. Mara talks the way machines print: continuous output, no gaps. "If the draws become a problem—"

"They won't be a problem."

"If they become a problem—"

"They won't." I pointed my spoon at them, which is as close to drama as our friendship gets. "Whatever you're pulling out of those vaults, it matters enough that you're doing it at three in the morning instead of sleeping, and you don't do anything at three in the morning instead of sleeping unless it matters. So it's covered. Eat your stew."

Mara looked down at the stew. Something moved behind their face — not a smile, nothing so generous — more like a door opening a crack somewhere deep in the building and then, regretfully, closing. "It probably doesn't matter," they said. "Probably corrupted logs. Probably nothing."

"Sure," I said. "Probably nothing. That's why you've pulled eleven years of Horizon archives."

Their spoon stopped. Just for a second. Then it resumed, and Mara said, in the flattest voice I've heard them manage, and they have managed some flat ones: "You watch my archive pulls?"

"I watch *everything*," I said. "It's the job. Everything on this station passes through my ledger eventually. Power, rations, air, grief. All of it gets weighed." I shrugged, and tried to make the shrug carry what I couldn't say outright, which was: *you don't have to tell me, but you should know that someone is watching, and the someone is me, and I am on your side of the ledger.* "Eat your stew, Brightwater."

They ate their stew.

* * *

That was eight days ago. Since then, the pattern has changed, and the change is what has me writing this in my head at 0400 instead of sleeping, which at my age is a choice with consequences.

The pulls got deeper. Older. Mara started hitting strata I didn't know the vaults still held — pre-launch records, construction-era files, the kind of documents that predate the station's own commissioning. And twice now, the draws have spiked hard enough to trip my substation alerts, and both times I've smoothed them over before the monitoring AI woke up enough to care, and both times I've sat in Substation Nine afterward with my hand flat against the warm transformer housing, feeling the current flow down into the drowned dark toward whatever Mara is raising from the dead.

Because that's what it feels like. I know how it sounds. But I've been the keeper of this station's small economies for twenty-two years, and I know the difference between consumption and resurrection. Consumption is steady. Resurrection comes in gasps.

Something is coming up out of those vaults. Something with weight. And Mara Brightwater — who filed a loss report once so comprehensive it ended a man's career, who has never once in eleven years shown me anything heavier than professional irritation — is carrying it alone, at three in the morning, in a cold office, refusing help the way they refuse everything.

Well. Refusal has never stopped me before. I keep the lights on in the hard place. It's the whole job.

Tonight I'm going to do more than reroute power. Tonight I'm going down to the admin ring myself, dry suit and lamp, past the flooded bulkheads, to Vault Two, and I'm going to stand in the doorway of the only lit room in the drowned half of the station, and I'm going to look at whatever Mara has pulled up on those ancient screens.

And if it's what I'm starting to suspect — if it's the Horizon, if it's the sleepers, if it's the great bright promise this whole dying station was built to serve — then God help us both, because I've read the old power budgets too, and I remember what happened to the ships that carried them.

The vault light is burning. I'm going to go see what it's shining on.
