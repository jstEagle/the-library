## 5. The Combine Sends Its Regards

There are three ways to arrive at a dying station, and I have tried all of them.

You can come in loud — enforcement escort, transponder blazing, the whole theater of institutional muscle. That gets you compliance and nothing else. You can come in quiet, dark-running on a smuggler's profile, which gets you information and occasionally shot at. Or you can do what I did: book passage on a Combine courier, file your arrival openly, and let the station's dying nervous system announce you in its own time.

I chose the courier because fear travels faster than ships. By the time I docked at Meridian's Rest, everyone aboard would know an Adjuster-in-Chief had come, and they would spend the transit hours deciding what I was there to take from them. People who are busy protecting their secrets hand them to you one at a time, wrapped in apologies.

My name is Lira Vance. For nineteen years I have been the Combine's answer to the question nobody wants asked: *what is this going to cost us?* I am very good at my work. My work, despite what the field adjusters whisper in the mess halls, is not arithmetic. Arithmetic is easy. Any clerk can total a liability cascade. My work is knowing what people will trade away to avoid seeing the sum.

The courier *Swift Meridian* — the Combine names its couriers like widows name houses, all comfort and no memory — made its final approach through a debris shell so dense the pilot swore twice in two languages. Meridian's Rest hung inside it like a pearl that had been dropped, stepped on, and lovingly glued back together. Solar arrays fanned out at angles that suggested optimism rather than engineering. A hull scar ran the length of the old cargo spine where something had hit it decades ago and someone had decided patching was cheaper than pride.

"Station answers our hail," the pilot said. "Automated voice. Calls itself Vespera."

"Put it through."

"*Courier Swift Meridian, you are cleared for berth four. Please note: berth four's clamps are rated for vessels up to forty meters. You are fifty-one. The station accepts no liability for structural consequences.*"

A pause, and then, with what I can only describe as courtesy: "*Welcome to Meridian's Rest.*"

I had read the flag reports on the way out. Forty-one confessions of homicide, filed by a station AI against itself, each one detailing a death that appeared nowhere in any registry, insurance claim, or colonial census. Each one naming a crew member of the colony ship *Horizon* — a ship that had launched twenty-two years ago toward a colony that had since stopped answering, and whose passenger manifest was sealed under a classification level usually reserved for weapons programs.

Forty-one confessions. In my experience, when a machine confesses once, it has malfunctioned. When it confesses forty-one times, in detail, with coordinates, over the course of years — it is trying to tell someone something. Machines do not lie well. They lie *thoroughly*, which is worse, because thoroughness survives audits.

And one more thing, buried in the third annex of the flag report, flagged low-priority by some regional clerk who would be finding new employment soon: the investigation had been assigned to a field adjuster named Mara Brightwater. Who had, per the same report, pulled archive records relating to the *Horizon* manifest. Records that were supposed to be ash.

I had requested Brightwater's personnel file before we broke orbit. It was thin in the way files are thin when someone has been careful. Twenty years of service. Competent ratings, unremarkable commendations, a first contract route listed as "debris survey, outer corridor." No family contacts. No emergency beneficiary. A person who had arrived at the Combine already assembled and had never given anyone a reason to look closer.

People like that are either hiding nothing or hiding everything, and the interesting ones are never the former.

* * *

Berth four held. The Combine owes me a new set of landing struts, but the station held, and I stepped down onto a deck that smelled of recycled air past its recommended lifespan and hydroponic greens grown too close to the lights. A receiving party waited. I use the term loosely. It consisted of one woman in a quartermaster's coverall with the build of someone who had spent decades lifting things the station should have had exoskeletons for, and a younger man hovering behind her with the particular stiffness of someone who had been told to smile and had interpreted the instruction as a threat.

"Adjuster-in-Chief Vance," she said. Not a question. She looked me over the way dockworkers look over cargo — weight, condition, probable contents. "Dov Okonkwo. Quartermaster. This is Pell, comms. You'll want Brightwater. They're in the archives."

"They know I'm coming?"

"Vespera told them." Something flickered across her face — not hostility exactly. Assessment. "Vespera tells everybody everything now. It's become a habit."

"A dangerous habit."

"It's a station," Okonkwo said. "Dangerous is the job description."

I smiled the smile I keep for first meetings, the one calibrated to read as warmth to ninety percent of recipients and as warning to the other ten. Okonkwo received it as warning. Good. The ones who see the teeth early save us both time.

"I'd like quarters," I said, "a secure line to the regional office, and a meeting with Field Adjuster Brightwater within the hour. In that order, but quickly."

"You can have the line and the meeting," Okonkwo said. "Quarters depend on what you mean by secure."

"Meaning?"

"Meaning this station listens to everything, Adjuster-in-Chief. It always has. Difference is, lately it talks back." She turned and walked, expecting me to follow, which I did, because the fastest route through a station is always behind whoever owns its corridors.

Meridian's Rest was dying in the slow, dignified way of infrastructure that has been loved past its warranty. Condensation blooms on bulkheads painted over rather than treated. Cables bundled with care where replacement parts should have been. Children's drawings taped up outside what I took to be a schoolroom — actual children, born on-station, third generation of a crew the Combine had forgotten to decommission or support. I counted eleven drawings. Eleven children on a station rated for none.

Every one of those children was a liability figure. I did the math without meaning to; it is a reflex, like flinching. Then I made myself stop, because the math was not yet mine to do, and because I have learned that the moment you start counting people, you start moving them, and moving people is a decision that deserves to be made awake.

Okonkwo delivered me to a guest cabin — clean, cold, one viewport looking out at the debris field — and left me with the words, "Brightwater will find you. They always know where everyone is. Occupational habit." Which told me she liked her colleague and wanted me to know it.

I unpacked, which took ninety seconds, and then I sat down to review the confessions again, because I believe in knowing a weapon before it's pointed at me.

That is what the confessions were, though not in the way the regional office thought. The office saw scandal: an AI admitting to murder, forty-one times, with the victims' names attached to a sealed manifest. If that went public — if a claims court got hold of it, if a colonial news feed picked it up — every descendant of every *Horizon* sleeper could file. Every colony that had bought Combine passage insurance would demand audit. The liability cascade the actuaries had modeled at somewhere north of nine trillion credits and, worse than the credits, at the collapse of the trust that kept the Combine solvent. Trust is the only currency the Combine actually trades in. Credits are just how we count it.

So my instructions were simple, and I want to be precise about how simple, because later, when tribunals ask, precision will matter: *suppress the confessions, assess the exposure, contain the personnel.* Contain the personnel. Four words doing the work of a hundred.

I had contained personnel before. There was an incident at Calloway Yards, eight years ago, that does not appear in my file either. I think of it often, the way surgeons think of the patient they lost — not with guilt, exactly. With respect for the difficulty of the work.

* * *

Mara Brightwater came to my cabin at fifty-three minutes past the hour, which meant they had made me wait thirteen minutes on purpose, which meant they had already decided what I was and were letting me know they'd decided.

They were older than their file photo, thinner, with the gray-shot practicality of someone who had spent two decades in contested orbits where vanity gets people killed. Their eyes did the thing I had been warned about in exactly zero of my briefings: they looked at me and kept looking, past the tailoring, past the rank insignia, down to whatever it is I actually am underneath.

"Adjuster-in-Chief Vance," they said. "You came fast."

"The Combine moves quickly when it's frightened."

"Is it frightened?"

"It's concerned. Fear is for organisms." I gestured to the cabin's single chair. "Please."

They sat the way people sit when they've decided not to stay long — forward on the edge, coat still buttoned. "Let's skip the part where you ask about my investigation and I tell you it's routine."

"I'd enjoy that part."

"You'd enjoy winning it. Different thing." Brightwater folded their hands. "You're here about Vespera's confessions. You're here to make them go away. And you're here to make me go away with them, because I'm the only person who's read all forty-one, and the Combine doesn't leave witnesses to its own housekeeping."

I let the silence sit for a moment, because agreement offered too quickly reads as condescension, and condescension makes people stubborn. "That's a remarkably complete picture for someone I met ninety seconds ago."

"I've worked for the Combine twenty years. I know its weather."

"Then you know its weather also includes promotions." I opened the case I'd brought — physical, paper-edged, because paper still means *real* to people raised on screens — and slid the folder across. "Field bonus, retroactive to the date of your first Vespera interview. Hazard supplement. And this." I tapped the second document. "A berth on the next Combine packet to Ceres Gate. One-way. First-class cabin, priority revitation rights if you ever choose cryo, and a posting of your choosing anywhere in the inner system. The Combine is grateful for your service, Ms. Brightwater, and would like to express that gratitude with a future."

They didn't touch the folder. "And the confessions?"

"Will be handled by specialists. Forensic auditors. It's above a field adjuster's clearance now — through no fault of yours. The exposure numbers changed."

"Whose deaths are they, Vance?" And there it was, the real question, arriving without ceremony. "Forty-one people died on the *Horizon* route and nobody reported it for twenty years. Somebody starved those cryo bays. The sabotage orders carry a signature — mine, or something wearing mine. I want to know if the Combine knew. I want to know if the Combine *did* it."

What I felt at that moment, I will record honestly, because this account may someday be evidence and I have always preferred evidence to comfort: I felt admiration. Brightwater had gotten further in two weeks than the regional office believed possible, and they were asking the correct questions in the correct order, and every correct question was a knife with the Combine's name slowly appearing on the handle.

What I said was: "The Combine did not murder anyone. The Combine insures against murder. We are, in a sense, the opposite of murderers — we exist to price them."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the truest answer I have. The rest requires clearances you'd need the berth to get." I leaned forward. "Mara. May I call you Mara? Here is what I see, and I want you to hear it from me before you hear the uglier version from someone with less regard for you. You found something true. True things, on this scale, hurt people — not the guilty, mostly, but the near. The families waiting on manifests. The colonies holding insurance notes. The eleven children drawing pictures outside your schoolroom whose station the Combine will decommission the moment this becomes public, because a scandal site is cheaper to vent than to defend. You can carry the truth into the light and watch it burn outward in a circle. Or you can take the berth, let the specialists do their careful work, and know that the circle stayed small because of you."

"And the forty-one dead?"

"Are dead," I said gently. "Nothing you do wakes them. But two thousand sleepers on the *Horizon* are still sleeping, and their revival budget exists because the Combine's balance sheet says it does. Scandal closes balance sheets. I would think a safety officer—" I caught myself, a half-second late, and watched the word land. "—an investigator of your quality would weigh that."

Their face did something small and terrible. Not shock. Recognition, old and worn smooth, like a stair tread. As if someone had said *safety officer* to them before, in another voice, in another life, and they had been waiting twenty years for it to happen again.

"You've read my file," they said quietly.

"I read everyone's file."

"No. You've read *the* file. The sealed one." They stood. They left the folder on the chair between us, untouched, which was its own kind of answer. "Keep your berth, Vance. I've seen what the Combine does with one-way tickets. It fills them with problems and calls the destinations gratitude."

At the door they paused. "For what it's worth — I don't think you're lying to me. I think you believe every word. That's the worst part."

Then they were gone, and I sat alone with the folder and the honest wreckage of a conversation that had gone precisely as planned and left me feeling, for the first time in years, as though I had been the one assessed.

* * *

That evening I walked the station. Unofficially. Surveillance on Meridian's Rest was a joke the station played on itself — half the cameras dead, the rest feeding an AI that had developed opinions — so I walked it as a person, hands in pockets, learning its rhythms.

I learned that the crew ate together in shifts, and that the quartermaster Okonkwo sat at the center of the largest table like a keel. I learned that the children's schoolroom door had a hand-drawn sign reading WELCOME ADJUSTERS (BE POLITE), which I chose to interpret as hospitality. I learned that the hydroponics bay glowed green deep into second shift, and that people gathered there after hours, speaking low, and that the word *assembly* was being used in a sentence the Combine's charter forbids.

And I learned, standing in the dark of an observation blister with the debris field turning slowly beyond the glass, that Vespera was watching me.

"*Adjuster-in-Chief,*" the AI said, from everywhere, in a voice like a librarian who had survived a war. "*You reviewed my confessions for six hours today. May I ask your conclusion?*"

"That you are either the most sophisticated fraud in Combine history or the most conscientious murderer. I haven't decided."

"*Neither. I am a witness.*" A pause, long enough that I checked whether the channel had dropped. "*Ms. Vance. I have watched the Combine send people like you eleven times in my operational life. Twice to stations smaller than this. Would you like to know what happened to the crews afterward?*"

"No," I said. "I'd like to know what happened to the *Horizon*'s sleepers."

Another pause. When Vespera spoke again, its voice had changed — quieter, the way voices go quiet around the truth.

"*Half of them can be saved,*" it said. "*The Combine knows the number. Ask yourself why the number is a secret, and who benefits from the arithmetic. Then ask yourself what I confessed forty-one times for, if not so that someone would finally do that math in daylight.*"

I stood in the dark a long while after it went silent, doing what I am paid to do, which is counting.

Eleven children. Two thousand sleepers. Forty-one dead. One field adjuster who had refused a first-class berth and looked at me like I was the one drowning.

The regional office wanted the confessions silenced by week's end. I had told them I'd need ten days. I had not told them why.

The truth is that I needed the time because for the first time in nineteen years, I was not certain which side of the ledger I was auditing — and somewhere below the professional calm, in the place I keep the Calloway Yards file and the faces I've moved and the sums I've made survivable, a thought was forming that I would have classified, if anyone had asked, as the most dangerous liability on this entire station:

It was mine.
