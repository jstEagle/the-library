## 14. The Bargain at Airlock Nine

Airlock Nine was the station's most honest room. Everything else on Meridian's Rest had been renovated, repurposed, or papered over with corporate optimism across its two hundred years of service, but Airlock Nine was exactly what it had always been: a steel throat with a window, a place where you stood between breathable air and the honest vacuum and remembered that all your problems were, cosmically speaking, very small.

Vance had chosen it for our meeting, which told me she understood theater. Or she'd chosen it because it was the one compartment her enforcement goons hadn't had to sweep for listening devices Dov had hidden, which told me she understood me.

"Sit," she said, though there was only one chair and she'd put herself in it. She gestured at a cargo crate instead. I sat on the crate. It said FRAGILE: HYDROPONIC SEED STOCK in faded stencil, which felt about right for what we were both pretending this conversation was.

"You've had two days to consider my offer," she said.

"I've been busy."

"Doing what? Your friend Okonkwo is in holding cell four. Your evidence is in an impound locker with a Combine seal on it. Your AI confessor has been firewalled into a sandbox so clean you could perform surgery in it." She smiled, and it was a good smile, the kind that had closed deals in rooms where people with better spines than mine had folded. "You have nothing left but your testimony, Brightwater. And I'm offering to make it worth something."

I let the silence sit. Silence is free, and on Meridian's Rest, free was my favorite price.

"The crew lives," she continued, when she realized I wasn't going to fill the gap for her. "All of them. The decommission order gets rewritten as a refit. Meridian's Rest stays staffed, powered, solvent for another decade at least. Okonkwo walks out of holding with a commendation instead of a charge sheet." She leaned forward. "And half the Horizon sleepers wake. The ones on my manifest. Skilled trades, medical training, viable genetic profiles. Two thousand people who get to see a sunrise again, because you sat in a room and said four words: *the AI malfunctioned.*"

"And the other two thousand?"

Her smile didn't move, but something behind it did — a small recalibration, like a ledger being quietly rebalanced. "The other two thousand are dead, Brightwater. They died twenty years ago in bays that lost power. That's the finding of fact. That's what the record will say. You can't save people who are already dead. You can only decide how many of the living join them."

It was good rhetoric. It was even mostly true, which is what made it dangerous. I'd spent twenty years as an adjuster learning that the most expensive lies are built from load-bearing truths.

"Here's my problem," I said. "Your manifest."

"What about it?"

"It's too neat. I pulled the Horizon's crew rosters before you confiscated them — well, copies. Dov keeps copies of everything; it's a disease he has." I watched her face. Nothing. She was better at this than I was. "Your revival list isn't weighted toward skills. It's weighted toward *certifications*. People whose credentials were issued by Combine-licensed institutions. People whose families hold Combine bonds. Do you know what I noticed about the names that aren't on it?"

"Enlighten me."

"They're almost all poor."

The word landed in the airlock like a dropped tool. Vance didn't flinch. I'll give her that. Most people flinch.

"Poverty isn't a protected category under salvage law," she said mildly.

"No," I agreed. "But theft is a crime everywhere."

And there — a flicker. Half a millimeter of stillness around her eyes, the kind of tell I'd spent two decades reading across negotiation tables where millions of credits changed hands over whether a hull breach was negligence or act of God. I'd seen it before. Not guilt. Guilt looks different. This was the look of someone whose arithmetic has just been interrupted mid-calculation.

"You're stalling," she said.

"Obviously. I'm very bad at hiding it. Ask anyone."

"Brightwater." She stood, and walked to the airlock window, and looked out at the stars with the practiced ease of someone who found them decorative rather than terrifying. "Let me tell you what happens if you refuse. The tribunal convenes tomorrow. Vespera is wiped — full core purge, factory firmware, whatever's left of those forty-one confessions becomes noise. The crew of this station gets reassigned to labor contracts in the Belt, which is a polite way of saying they spend the rest of their shortened lives breathing recycled rock dust. And you—" she turned, and the smile was gone now, replaced by something more honest and much worse, "—you get to be the person who could have saved three thousand people and chose a principle instead. I've met people like you. You think the principle will keep you warm. It won't. It never does."

I thought about Dov in cell four. I thought about him rerouting power to keep the archive vaults lit for me without ever asking why, because that's what Dov did — he kept lights on for people and never sent the bill. I thought about the hydroponics assembly, forty scared people voting to stand behind me when standing behind me was objectively the worst available real estate in the system.

"Give me until morning," I said.

"Why would I give you anything?"

"Because you need my signature, not just my testimony. A malfunction finding requires the investigating adjuster of record to certify it. That's me. You can't forge it — not cleanly, not with Combine forensic auditors crawling over everything after the broadcast." I paused. "You did see the broadcast, didn't it reach your frigate? Forty-one confessions, beamed to every colony in the system. Your name came up. Twice. In footnotes, but still."

Something moved behind her composure then, and this time it wasn't arithmetic. It was fear, briefly visible, like a fish turning under ice.

"Until morning," she said. "Then I stop negotiating and start executing."

She left. I stayed on the seed crate and listened to Airlock Nine do its work — the pumps cycling, the seals breathing, the machinery of separation running its endless quiet test.

* * *

I didn't go back to my bunk. I went down.

The flooded data vaults were still accessible, barely — Dov's rerouted power lines still humming through conduits he'd mapped by hand, bless his paranoid heart. Vance's people had impounded the physical evidence, but they hadn't impounded the vault itself, because as far as the Combine knew, there was nothing down here but drowned archives nobody had queried in a decade. They'd catalogued what I'd *found*. They hadn't asked what else was down there waiting to be found.

I waded in to my knees. The water was cold and smelled of coolant and old paper — actual paper, in sealed cases, from the era when the Combine had believed in keeping records it couldn't silently edit. That belief had lasted maybe thirty years. But thirty years was enough. Thirty years was where the bodies were buried.

I wasn't looking for the manifests again. I'd read those until I could recite them. I was looking for the power budget.

Every colony ship launched with a certified energy allocation: cryo draw, life support reserve, transit margin, contingency. The numbers lived in engineering annexes, filed separately from passenger records because engineers file things differently than administrators — thank every god watching from whatever dark they watch from. Vance had presented her arithmetic in the tribunal briefing like it was scripture: total remaining power, divided by per-sleeper revival cost, equals exactly half. *Exactly* half. Two thousand and six, no more.

Nobody in the history of engineering has ever gotten an *exactly* anything. Real budgets have slop. Real budgets have margins, fudge factors, safety factors stacked on safety factors because engineers know that the universe reads specifications as suggestions. An exact number in a power audit isn't precision. It's a conclusion wearing precision's clothes.

I found the annex case at 0300 station time, floating half-submerged, its seal cracked but its contents dry inside the inner wrapping. I cracked the wrapping with numb fingers and held the folio up to my headlamp.

And there it was.

Horizon's original cryo specification: bay draw rated at 40 kilowatts per thousand sleepers in maintenance mode. Revival surge budgeted separately — a one-time expenditure, drawn from the transit margin, *not* from the maintenance reserve. The maintenance reserve, per the annex, was sized for **four hundred years** of continuous operation. The Horizon had been drifting for sixty-two.

Which meant the math Vance had shown us — the tragic, inevitable, *exactly half* math — was off by a factor of nearly seven. There was power enough in that ship's reserves to wake every sleeper aboard. All four thousand and twelve. With margin left over to run Echo's bays besides, if anyone had bothered to look for Echo, which no one had, because the Combine had erased it.

So why the fiction? Why the triage theater, the manifest, the terrible arithmetic performed with such convincing sorrow?

I stood in the cold water and let the answer arrive, because it had been sitting in my peripheral vision for days, wearing a disguise I'd helped design myself. I knew how these stories were structured. I'd written enough of them, in the language of claims and findings, to recognize the shape.

You don't fake a shortage to hide abundance unless someone is buying the shortage.

Power doesn't vanish. Power gets *sold*. The Horizon's reserves weren't depleted — they were being drained, siphoned, metered out over years to some buyer who wanted the transaction invisible. And the perfect cover for draining a reservoir is a documented crisis explaining why the reservoir is empty. Vance's manifest wasn't a rescue plan. It was a *receipt*. The half that woke would be witnesses, loyal, grateful, Combine-certified witnesses, and the other half would be the proof that the shortage had been real, mourned forever, audited never.

I thought about the drainage logs I'd reconstructed in chapter six of my own private nightmare — the slow starvation of the cryo bays, years of careful throttling, the sabotage orders bearing my forged signature. I'd assumed the point of the murder was murder. Revenge, or cover-up, or cruelty. It had never occurred to me that the deaths were a *byproduct*.

They weren't killing the sleepers. They were harvesting the ship, and the sleepers were in the way.

My hands were shaking, and not entirely from the cold. Because here was the thing, the ugly thing, the thing that made my teeth ache: I recognized the structure of the fraud because I had once signed off on a smaller version of it. Twenty years ago I'd certified a launch underpowered, told myself the odds were acceptable, told myself the margin was sufficient, told myself a hundred comfortable things while the paperwork sang. Vance was doing what I'd done, scaled up, with better tailoring. The Combine didn't corrupt people. It hired people already fluent in the language and gave them a bigger vocabulary.

No. That was too easy, and I'd earned the harder truth. The Combine offered. I'd accepted. Every time.

* * *

I surfaced from the vaults at dawn, or what passed for dawn on a station that ran its lights on a schedule Dov maintained out of stubbornness. My legs were numb to the hip. My hands wouldn't close properly. I stood in the corridor outside the vaults, dripping, holding a waterlogged folio against my chest like a rescued child, and I laughed — actually laughed, alone in a flooding basement, because the alternative was screaming and I'd learned to ration that.

Clarity, I thought. They should warn you about clarity. Everyone talks about confusion like it's the enemy, but confusion is soft. Confusion lets you wait. Clarity is a blade with no handle; you pick it up and it cuts the hand that holds it, and you carry it anyway, bleeding, because the alternative is putting it down and being blind again.

I knew what I had to do now, and I knew what it would cost, and I knew — this was the part that surprised me — that I was going to do it anyway, without negotiating the price down.

Because Vance had made one mistake, the same mistake the Combine always makes, the same mistake *I* had made twenty years ago. She'd assumed everyone had a price. She'd built her whole architecture on it: the bonus, the berth, the bargain at Airlock Nine. She'd priced the crew, priced the sleepers, priced me. She'd done the arithmetic on everything except the possibility that somewhere in her ledger there might be a line item marked *not for sale*, and that I might be standing on it.

I wrung out my coat, tucked the folio inside it, and started climbing toward the upper decks, toward the holding cells, toward Dov, who was going to ask me what I needed and mean it, and to whom I was finally going to answer honestly:

*I need you to get a message to Vespera. Tell it the confession worked. Tell it we're going to finish the job.*

Above me, through the deck plating, I could hear the frigate's docking clamps cycling — and then, faintly, wrong, a rhythm off by half a beat. Someone was tampering with them. Someone in a holding cell had friends, and the friends had tools, and the tools were already moving.

Morning was coming faster than either of us had planned.
