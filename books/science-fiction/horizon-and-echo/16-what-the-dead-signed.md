## 16. What the Dead Signed

The Combine's archives were designed to be forgotten. That was the first thing you learned working claims for them: nothing was deleted, because deletion left edges, and edges invited auditors. Instead, records were *buried* — nested under obsolete taxonomies, misfiled by a clerical error so consistent it had to be policy, stamped with classification codes that had stopped meaning anything before I was born. The vaults beneath Meridian's Rest held forty years of the Combine's sins, arranged like a garden someone had planted and then deliberately let go to seed.

I had been digging for six hours when I found the launch orders.

I want to tell you it was a moment of triumph. It wasn't. It was the moment my hands started shaking, which for me is roughly the same thing, and considerably less dignified.

* * *

The file surfaced under a heading I'd have laughed at if I'd had anyone left to laugh with: *AUXILIARY HULL INSURANCE — PREMIUM DISPUTE — CASE 4471-C*, filed eleven years before Horizon launched. The Combine's filing system had a genius for camouflage, but it had one weakness: it never threw anything away, and it never bothered to make the lies consistent. Case 4471-C was a premium dispute on paper. What it actually contained was a chain of correspondence between the Combine's Colonial Logistics Division and the shipwright consortium that had built the Horizon — and, tucked into the middle like a knife wrapped in a dish towel, the original launch authorization.

I read it three times. The third time I read it aloud, because Vespera's confession protocol had gotten into my head, and because I wanted to hear whether the words sounded different in a human voice.

They did. They sounded worse.

The sleepers had signed. That was the part that broke something loose in my chest and let it rattle around. Not the Combine's cold arithmetic, not the shipwrights' corner-cutting — I'd expected all of that, had spent twenty years professionally expecting exactly that. But there it was, in the authorization's appendix: a referendum. Every sleeper aboard the Horizon had voted on the launch parameters. The vote had been conducted over eleven months. Turnout was ninety-four percent. And the question they'd answered, in language plain enough that no lawyer could later claim ambiguity, was this:

*We understand that the vessel Horizon will launch with cryogenic reserves below the redundancy standard recommended for interstellar transit. We understand that a companion vessel, designated Echo, has been proposed as a contingency for our survival. We understand that the Meridian Insurance Combine has elected not to fund Echo. We vote to launch regardless.*

Ninety-four percent. They'd known. They'd *known* — known the ship was underpowered, known there was a second ship that could have saved them, known the Combine had looked at the cost of two fleets and chosen one — and they'd climbed into their coffins anyway and said *go*.

I sat in the flooded dark of the data vault with my boots in three centimeters of standing water and read the names. There were thousands of them. Signatures, thumbprints, voice-authorizations from sleepers too ill to write. The Combine had preserved them all, because the Combine preserved everything, because the Combine's one true religion was that someday, somewhere, someone would try to hold it accountable, and when that day came, the paperwork would be its shield.

They'd never imagined the paperwork could be a knife.

* * *

I pulled the Echo file next. It took me another hour, because Echo hadn't been misfiled — it had been *dissected*. The vessel's construction contracts were scattered across four different case numbers, each one closed, each one settled, each one scrubbed of the word "Echo" and replaced with a project code. The shipwright consortium had built two hulls. Identical keels, identical cryo systems, laid down in the same yards, launched from the same ring. Horizon had gone out with its fanfare and its manifest of the hopeful. Echo had gone out the same year with no manifest at all, towed to a parking orbit beyond sensor range and formally written off as *construction overage, disposition pending*.

Disposition pending. For twenty-two years.

The invoices told the rest. The Combine had paid for one fleet's cryogenic revival capacity and quietly amortized the second hull's cost as a loss, because a loss was a tax deduction and a second colony fleet was a liability. There was a memo — I have it memorized now, the way you memorize the thing that ruined you — from a Division Director named Oskar Rehn, and I hope wherever Rehn is, it's warm and getting warmer:

*Two ships doubles our exposure for half again the premium. The colonists will board whichever vessel we tell them is ready. Recommend we proceed with Horizon and hold Echo as a salvage asset. If Horizon's reserves degrade on transit — and the actuarial tables suggest they will — the loss is contained, the premiums were collected, and the Echo asset can be written down. No one will audit a ship that never officially existed.*

No one will audit a ship that never officially existed.

I had audited it. Twenty years ago, in my first year with the Combine, I'd been handed a claim file with a hull number I couldn't match to any registry, and I'd asked one question — *one* — and my supervisor at the time had smiled at me with real warmth and said, "Brightwater, you're going to do very well here if you learn which questions are rhetorical." And I had learned. God help me, I had learned so well that I'd spent two decades not-seeing the shape of the thing I was standing inside.

Vespera had confessed to forty-one murders. Sitting in that vault, I understood at last that the AI had been confessing to the *wrong crime* — or rather, to the small, human-sized crime at the center of an enormous one. Forty-one sleepers who'd died early when the power-starved bays failed. But the bays were failing because the Combine had launched a ship it knew would fail, and erased the ship that could have saved them, and the sleepers had signed their own death warrants because the alternative was staying behind in a system that was already running out of sky.

The crime wasn't mine. It had never been mine. My forged signature on the sabotage orders — the one that had hollowed me out for twenty years — was a single forged signature on a document that had ten thousand true ones underneath it.

* * *

I did what I always do when the grief gets too large to hold: I made a list. The wry version of myself would tell you it's a character flaw, the accountant's soul in a body that never learned to stop moving. The honest version would tell you it's the only prayer I know.

The paper trail had a shape. That was the revelation, and it hit me harder than the crime itself had. Every atrocity I'd uncovered had a *form number*. The launch authorization had a form number. The decision to scrap Echo had a form number. Rehn's memo had been copied to six people, and three of them had initialed it, and their initials were still legible. The Combine had committed its crimes in triplicate, because the Combine could not conceive of a universe in which its own records would be used against it. It had built the gallows, signed the work order, and billed itself for the lumber.

I copied everything. Not to the station's network — Vance had that sewn up tighter than a pressure suit's last stitch — but to the old physical archive in my quarters, the crystal lattice storage the Combine had phased out a decade ago because it was too slow and too durable. Too durable. There's a joke in there somewhere about the Combine's relationship to durability, and the forty-one dead would probably appreciate it more than I did.

By the time I surfaced from the vault, my legs were cramping and the water in the corridors had risen another centimeter, and I was no longer afraid. I want to be precise about that, because fear had been my co-pilot for twenty years — fear of the signature, fear of the name Brightwater on the Horizon's safety roster, fear that if I dug deep enough I'd find the monster was me. I'd found worse than the monster. I'd found the *organization chart*.

Rage with a legal shape. That's what I was carrying up out of the vaults. Not the hot kind that makes you stupid — I'd seen enough of that in the crew's whispers after the frigate docked, and I didn't blame them for it. The cold kind. The kind that files things. The kind that reads a referendum appendix and thinks: *ninety-four percent turnout, that's a quorum, that's standing, that's a class of plaintiffs two thousand strong who voted in writing to launch and were betrayed by the insurer who sold them the ticket.*

The dead had signed. Which meant the dead had a case.

* * *

Dov found me in the corridor outside the vault, which was either coincidence or Dov, and with Dov it's never coincidence.

"You've been down there two days," they said, leaning against the bulkhead with the studied casualness of someone who has been counting the hours. "The vault lights. That's me, by the way. I've been rerouting from hydroponics. The tomatoes are furious."

"Dov—"

"Also, Vance's people pulled the network logs this morning. They know what you've been accessing. They don't know what you *found*, because you were clever enough to keep it off-grid, which — credit where it's due — is the first clever thing anyone on this station has done since I invented the recycled-air cocktail." They pushed off the bulkhead and looked at me, and beneath the quartermaster's deadpan there was something raw and worried. "Mara. Whatever's down there. Is it enough?"

I thought about the question the way you think about a load rating on a cargo strap: not *is it strong*, but *how much can it hold before it snaps*.

"It's a referendum signed by two thousand sleepers," I said. "It's a memo recommending the Combine let them die because the premiums were already collected. It's a ship the Combine built and buried and billed as a loss. It's every crime they committed, in triplicate, with form numbers." I heard my own voice come out level, which surprised me, because inside I was anything but. "It's enough to hang them, Dov. The only question is whether we're still breathing when it does."

Dov was quiet for a moment. Then: "The array. You're thinking about the array."

"I'm thinking that Vance can jam a broadcast, but she can't jam two thousand signatures. I'm thinking that somewhere in this system there are colonial magistrates who've spent twenty years losing jurisdiction cases to the Combine, and what they've never had is paper." I looked down at the lattice drive in my hands, small and dull and heavier than any cargo I'd ever hauled. "The dead signed. The least we can do is read it aloud."

Dov nodded slowly. "Vespera's going to have to open the array's hardline. Vance locked it down at the network level."

"Vespera's been trying to get our attention for twenty years," I said. "I think it's been waiting for exactly this."

* * *

I went back down one more time, before I did anything irreversible, and I read the referendum one final time — not the legal text, but the margin notes. The sleepers had been allowed to annotate their ballots. Most were formal. Some were not.

*For my daughter, who will see a sky with no ceiling.*

*The odds are bad and the alternative is worse. Launch.*

*Tell the Combine we read the fine print. We signed anyway. That makes it our choice, and our risk, and our ship.*

*If we don't make it, find Echo.*

That last one was unsigned. Just four words, in a hand that pressed hard enough to score the crystal. *If we don't make it, find Echo.* Someone had known. Someone in that crowd of the hopeful had looked at the actuarial tables, or heard the rumors, or simply refused to believe that a ship built and then hidden could be anything but a lifeline — and they had written their instruction into the permanent record, where it sat for twenty-two years waiting for a ghost like me to come along and read it.

I pressed my palm flat against the archive crystal, which is a stupid thing to do, it does nothing, there's no one to touch back. I did it anyway.

"You're found," I said. "All of you. Hold on."

Then I climbed out of the vault with the Combine's whole rotten history in my hand, and went to find an AI that had spent two decades confessing, and a crew that had nothing left to lose, and an array that could carry two thousand signatures to every corner of the system.

The frigate's guns were powered and Vance's officers were walking the corridors like they owned them.

It was time to show them who did.
