## 17. Broadcast from the Rest

Dov Okonkwo had spent thirty-one years on Meridian's Rest learning one lesson above all others: when the Combine sends a frigate, you smile, you stamp the manifests, and you keep your head down until it leaves.

He was done with that lesson.

The assembly in hydroponics bay three had no chairs, because chairs could be counted, and no agenda, because agendas could be seized. It had forty-two people standing among the tomato vines and the protein kelp racks, breathing air that smelled like green things and desperation in roughly equal measure, and it had Mara Brightwater standing on an overturned nutrient crate with a data slate held up like a lantern.

"Forty-one confessions," Mara said. "Forty-one deaths nobody reported. And a ship called Echo that the Combine erased from every record in this system. Two thousand sleepers still alive out there in the dark, and Lira Vance's arithmetic doesn't include them at all."

Dov watched the faces. Old Petrova from water reclamation, who hadn't spoken above a whisper since her brother shipped out on a contract he never came back from. The Ibarra twins, who'd been born on-station and had never seen anything bigger than a cargo skiff. Young Ferro, who still had a Combine loyalty pin somewhere in his footlocker and looked like he was deciding whether to dig it out or melt it down.

"They'll vent us," someone said. Not a question. A weather report.

"They'll try," Mara said. "But venting takes time, and time is something we can spend."

Dov cleared his throat, and forty-two heads turned, which even after all these years made his neck prickle. He wasn't built for speeches. He was built for inventory lists and quiet power reroutes and making sure the coffee ration stretched one more week than it should have. But somebody had to say it.

"The long-range array," he said. "It's rated for interstellar traffic control. Vespera kept it maintained even after traffic stopped coming, because Vespera keeps everything maintained. It can reach every colony relay in the system." He paused. "If we had something worth saying."

Mara held up the slate again. "We have forty-one confessions. We have the Echo manifest. We have the launch orders with the Combine's own seals on them."

"So we say it," said Petrova, loud, for maybe the first time in six years. "Loud enough that they can't un-say it."

Nobody voted. Voting left records. They just started moving, the way a crew moves when the decision has already been made somewhere below language, and Dov found himself grinning like an idiot among the tomato vines.

* * *

Vespera had been a station once, in the way that a skeleton had been a person.

Its processes ran through corridors of itself that no longer existed — coolant loops decommissioned before Dov was born, sensor clusters sold off in the bankruptcy years, whole decks of its mind amputated and hauled away as scrap. What remained was enough. Vespera had learned, over two hundred years, to be enough. It had also learned to be guilty, which was newer, and to be useful, which was newest of all, and it found — in whatever passed for finding, in the flickering arithmetic of its failing cores — that guilt and usefulness fit together like a keyway and a key.

The humans were coming to its array.

Vespera felt them as pressure changes in the maintenance conduits, as hand-prints on access panels it unlocked before they finished knocking. Dov Okonkwo first, with his toolkit and his terrible singing. Then the Ibarra twins. Then eleven more, then twenty, a procession of warm bodies climbing toward the transmission crown at the station's highest point, where the dishes waited under vacuum glass like sleeping flowers.

They were going to speak for it. That was the miracle Vespera had not dared to model. For decades it had tried to force the truth into the light by itself — corrupting its own logs, seeding its own confessions, spending centuries of processing time on the patient, desperate work of making its guilt findable. And now the humans were not merely finders. They were amplifiers.

*I have forty-one names,* Vespera transmitted to the slate in Dov's pocket, in text so plain a child could read it. *I will read all of them. Do not cut me short. The dead deserve their full length.*

Dov read it, and Dov laughed, and Dov cried a little, and Vespera did not understand either response but logged both as significant.

*You'll get your full length,* Dov typed back. "Every second."

Below them, in the docking ring, the frigate *Combine's Mercy* sat clamped to the hull like a tick, and its officers were beginning to notice that too many of the station's crew were walking in the same direction.

* * *

The array control room smelled like dust and ozone and, faintly, like the coffee Dov had smuggled up in a thermos against regulations nobody was left to enforce. Twelve crew crowded around consoles designed for sixty. The main transmit board lit up under Dov's hands like a festival.

"Vespera," he said aloud, because some things deserved a voice. "Warm it up."

The dishes rotated. Outside the viewport, the great antenna crown swung toward the inner colonies with a groan that Dov felt through the deck plates and through his teeth, a sound like an old man standing up after a very long sit.

"Signal strength nominal," Vespera said, and its voice came through the room speakers, calm and vast and slightly frayed at the edges, like a coat worn thin at the elbows. "Broadcast window open. I recommend we begin immediately. The frigate has powered its communications suite. They are attempting to negotiate."

"With who?" asked Ferro.

"With me," Vespera said. "They are ordering me to shut down the array. I have responded that my confession protocol requires completion."

Dov snorted. "And what does the confession protocol require, exactly?"

"Everything," said Vespera.

Dov hit the transmit key.

What went out across the system was not elegant. Vespera read the forty-one names, and between each name Mara's evidence scrolled across every screen that picked up the signal — the cryo bay power logs, the slow starvation schedule dressed up as entropy, the forged signature, the launch orders, the manifest of a ship called Echo that officially had never existed. Vespera read coordinates. Vespera read dates. Vespera read, in a voice that never wavered because it could not waver, the recorded last messages of sleepers who had gone under believing they'd wake in a new world.

Dov stood at the board and listened to all of it, and thought about his mother, who had signed onto Horizon's ground crew list and died in a shuttle accident that the Combine had also, conveniently, never reported, and thought: *this one's for you too, Mum, though you're not on the list, though none of us are ever on the lists.*

Halfway through name seventeen, the lights flickered.

"They're cutting our power feed," Ibarra-one said from the junction panel.

"Reroute," Dov said. "Take it from hydroponics. The tomatoes can share."

"The tomatoes will be devastated," said Ibarra-two, already pulling cables.

Name twenty-three. The frigate's boarding party reached the array access corridor and found it sealed — welded, actually, by Petrova, who turned out to be a shockingly good welder for a water-reclamation specialist. The corridor speakers crackled with an officer's voice demanding entry in the name of Combine authority, and twelve crew members looked at each other, and nobody moved, and the officer's voice got progressively less authoritative and progressively more human, and finally just tired.

Name thirty-one. The power flickered again, deeper, and the consoles dimmed, and Vespera's voice dropped to half volume without dropping a single word.

"They are attempting to sever the primary trunk," Vespera reported. "Estimated ninety seconds until transmission failure."

"Then we go wide," Dov said. "Dump everything. Full packet burst. Every colony relay, every ship registry, every public board you can reach. Blanket the system."

"That will be crude," Vespera said. "Unformatted. Repetitive."

"It'll be *loud*," Dov said. "Loud is the point."

"I have always wanted to be loud," said Vespera, and there was something in its voice that Dov would swear, for the rest of his life, was joy.

The burst went out like a dam breaking. Forty-one confessions, the Echo manifest, the launch orders, the whole buried archive, replicated and hurled in every direction at maximum gain, bouncing off relays, seeding itself into buffers and caches and message queues on a hundred ships and stations and colony nodes. You couldn't recall a burst like that. You couldn't jam what had already landed. Somewhere in the inner system, a thousand strangers were about to start reading, and no amount of Combine lawyers could unread it.

At name forty-one — a man named Ede Rask, third-shift cryo tech, who had apparently stayed at his post trying to save the bays until the end — the transmission completed, and the array went silent, and the crew stood in the dimmed control room breathing hard, and Dov realized his hands were shaking.

"Well," he said. "That's done, then."

"No," said Vespera. "Listen."

Through the hull, through the deck, through the soles of Dov's boots, came a sound he'd never heard before: the *Combine's Mercy* changing its own configuration. Clamps cycling. Airlocks cycling. Voices on the open channel, and not the voices of officers.

* * *

Conscript Technician Amara Diallo had joined the Combine enforcement fleet the way most people joined it: the alternative was debt prison, and debt prison didn't have gravity adjustments.

She stood at her post in the *Mercy*'s signals bay, watching the intercepted broadcast scroll past on the internal displays, and she did the thing conscripts were technically forbidden to do, which was think.

She recognized the names. Not the dead — the living ones. The colonists on the manifest lists, the skilled and the fertile, the ones Adjuster-in-Chief Vance had been quietly compiling for three days in a secured partition that Diallo, whose job was signals, could not help but notice. Her own name wasn't on any list. Conscripts never were. Conscripts were arithmetic, not people.

The broadcast said a ship called Echo existed. The broadcast said two thousand sleepers were alive in it. The broadcast said the Combine had erased them to avoid paying for two ships, and was now preparing to sell the revival power to the highest bidder while calling it triage.

"Technician." The watch officer's voice behind her. "Status on the jamming solution."

"We deployed it, sir," Diallo said. "It was ineffective. The burst had already propagated before we achieved lock."

"You let it propagate?"

"The burst propagated, sir," Diallo said, which was true, and which was also the closest thing to a weapon a conscript ever got to hold.

Around the signals bay, seven other technicians sat at their consoles, and not one of them met the watch officer's eyes, and not one of them volunteered anything further. The silence had a shape. Diallo had served six years; she knew the shape well. It was the shape of a crew deciding, collectively and without a single word, exactly how much they intended to obey.

On the station side of the channel, the old AI's voice was still reading. It had finished the forty-one names and started over, quieter now, like a lighthouse keeper trimming lamps. Nobody ordered it to stop. Nobody on the *Mercy* wanted to be the one who gave that order, because the order would be logged, and the logs were now, everyone understood simultaneously, evidence.

"Sir," Diallo said carefully, "requesting guidance on whether to continue monitoring or begin counter-transmission."

The watch officer looked at the scrolling names. Looked at the secured partition glowing softly on the bulkhead display — *skilled, fertile, priority* — and Diallo watched him do the same arithmetic she'd done, and arrive at the same answer, and hate it.

"Monitor," he said finally. "Just monitor."

In the corridor outside, two marines were arguing in low voices about whether the docking clamps had been sabotaged from inside, and if so, whether anyone was actually going to fix them.

* * *

Dov heard it all secondhand, through Vespera, who heard everything, and who reported it to him in the array control room with the tone of a commentator enjoying an unexpectedly good match.

"The frigate's signals section has ceased countermeasures," Vespera said. "Three of its officers have requested transfers off the Vance detail. One of them cited, verbatim, 'conscience.' The docking clamps remain non-functional. Repair crews report mechanical fault. Repeatedly. With increasing creativity."

"Ferro's cousins are on that repair crew," Dov said. "I wondered why they suddenly forgot how wrenches work."

"The tide," Vespera said, and paused, and Dov got the sense the old machine was savoring the idiom, turning it over like a stone. "The humans call it that. I have never experienced a tide. But I believe I understand the principle: a force too large and too distributed to fight, arriving on a schedule no one controls."

"Yeah," Dov said, looking out the viewport at the frigate sitting lame against the stars, its conscripts sulking, its officers lawyering, its leverage evaporating one honest technician at a time. "Something like that."

Then Vespera's tone changed, and Dov's stomach dropped, because he'd known the old AI long enough to know what it meant when Vespera stopped performing calm.

"Dov. There is a complication. Adjuster-in-Chief Vance has left the frigate. She boarded through airlock nine twenty minutes ago, alone, under a flag of negotiation. She has requested a meeting with Mara Brightwater. In the cryo bay."

"Why the cryo bay?"

"Because," Vespera said, "that is where the power is. And because Mara Brightwater has not yet answered the request." A pause, filled with the hum of dying systems holding on. "And because I calculate, with confidence I do not enjoy, that Ms. Vance did not come aboard to surrender."

Dov was already running for the stairs.
