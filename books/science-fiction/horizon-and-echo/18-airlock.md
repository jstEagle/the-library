## 18. Airlock

The cryo bay of the Horizon smelled like a hospital that had given up on hope — antiseptic and cold and faintly, terribly sweet, like fruit going bad somewhere out of sight. Two thousand pods in ranked rows, each one a coffin with a heartbeat, each one dark except for the amber standby light that meant *still alive, barely, don't ask questions.*

Lira Vance stood at the center of it like she was admiring real estate.

She'd heard me coming, of course. The frigate's marines had swept this deck twice already, and her security feed had been watching me crawl through the maintenance ducts for the last forty minutes. She'd let me come anyway. That was the thing about Vance I'd finally understood somewhere around hour three of hiding in a duct with my knees against my chin: she never stopped you from walking into a trap. She just made sure the trap was comfortable, so you'd feel grateful when it closed.

"Mara." She didn't turn around. She was reading something off a slate — probably the manifest, probably the arithmetic, probably the list of human beings she'd already sorted into *worth saving* and *acceptable loss* with the serene efficiency of a woman doing her taxes. "You're bleeding on a heritage site."

"It's a morgue with delusions of grandeur."

"Semantics." Now she turned, and I got the full Adjuster-in-Chief: immaculate Combine grays despite everything, hair pinned like a flag planted in conquered territory, and those eyes that assessed you the way an actuary assesses a flood zone. "You came here to negotiate. So negotiate. You have—" she checked her slate theatrically, though we both knew she knew exactly how long I had, "—eleven minutes before my people finish tracing your little broadcast relay and Dov Okonkwo's friends in hydroponics discover what a cell without gravity assistance feels like."

The broadcast was still going. Somewhere above us, Vespera's forty-one confessions were falling out of the long-range array in every direction at once, unencrypted, unjammable, unstoppable — because Dov had done what Dov always did, which was find the one cable nobody remembered existed and route truth through it. Every colony, every relay buoy, every ship with a receiver in the system was hearing a dying station AI name names. Vance's name was in there now too. Not as a saboteur. As an accountant.

"You can't jam it," I said. "You've tried. The array's hardwired to the hull. To stop it you'd have to cut the station open, and cutting the station open looks very bad on camera, and there are cameras now. Three colony news services picked up the signal. They're asking questions."

"Questions are survivable." She said it lightly, but I saw the flicker — the tiny recalibration behind her eyes, the machine inside the woman running new numbers. "Indictments are survivable. What isn't survivable is two thousand sleepers waking up to a power grid that can't sustain them, and everyone in this system learning that the Meridian Insurance Combine chose who lived by spreadsheet. So yes. I'm listening. Talk fast."

I leaned against pod 1147. Its amber light pulsed once, slow, like a sleeping thing turning over. I'd spent twenty years avoiding this room — or its twin, back when the Horizon still had its bays powered and its lies intact. Standing in it now felt like standing inside my own confession, which, I supposed, was exactly what it was.

"Here's the deal," I said. "Full data package. Original launch orders with the sleepers' informed consent vote. The internal memos authorizing Echo's erasure from the record. The procurement fraud showing the Combine billed two ships and commissioned one. And the power audit proving your revival budget is fiction — that you're not short on power, Vance, you're *selling* power. The cryo reserves were earmarked for revival and someone upstairs has been skimming them into the colonial energy market for six years. That's not triage. That's theft from the dead."

Her face did something remarkable. It did nothing at all. But her hand tightened on the slate, and the slate creaked, and that was enough.

"Where," she said quietly, "did you get the power audit?"

"Vespera kept books. Twenty years of them. Your predecessors thought they were deleting the records." I smiled, and it wasn't a nice smile; I'd learned smiles from insurance adjusters. "They were deleting the copies. Vespera *is* the records. It's been confessing to murder for months precisely because it couldn't be deleted, only ignored, and it gambled that eventually someone too stubborn to leave would start counting."

"And you want money."

"I want the crew of Meridian's Rest released. All charges dropped, all records sealed, all of them free to go wherever the hell they want with whatever severance the tribunal eventually decides your Combine owes them for twenty years of unpaid hazard pay on a station you wrote off and never decommissioned properly because decommissioning would have required admitting it existed."

"Granted," she said instantly, and I almost laughed, because that was the tell. Vance granted things instantly when the alternative was worse. "And?"

"And full revival capacity for Echo."

For the first time since I'd known her, Lira Vance looked genuinely confused. It lasted maybe half a second, and then the confusion resolved into contempt, which was her native language. "Echo's sleepers aren't part of any manifest. They have no legal standing. No policy, no claim, no next of kin on file. They are, in the strictest sense, nobody. You're spending your leverage on *nobody*."

"They're two thousand somebodies your Combine erased so it wouldn't have to pay for a second ship. That's the whole crime, Vance. Everything else — the sabotage, the forged signatures, the slow starvation of the Horizon's bays — it was all cleanup for that original sin. You don't get to keep the erasure. That's the price."

"The power doesn't exist. Even if I agreed—"

"The power exists. That's the beauty of it. Your own audit proves it. There's exactly enough to wake every sleeper on both ships — if nobody skims, if nobody takes a berth for themselves, if the budget goes where the dead signed for it to go." I pushed off the pod and took a step toward her. "Which brings me to my collateral."

"Your collateral," she repeated, flat.

"I'm barred from the revival rolls. Effective immediately, binding, written into the settlement. I don't wake with them. I don't get a berth, a slot, a place in the arithmetic. When Echo opens its doors, I'm standing outside them."

Vance studied me for a long moment, and I watched her do the thing she did best: valuation. I could practically see the ledger opening behind her eyes. *One rogue investigator, self-incriminating, emotionally compromised, offering irreplaceable evidence in exchange for assets with no book value.* Any competent adjuster would take that trade before I finished the sentence.

Any competent adjuster would also wonder why I was offering it.

"You're leaving yourself out," she said slowly. "Why? Guilt? Sentiment?" Her lip curled. "Don't insult us both."

"Because a settlement needs a hostage, and I'm the only asset both sides agree has value. You need me alive and cooperative until the tribunals finish, because my testimony is the difference between *corporate misconduct* and *criminal conspiracy*, and you know which one keeps you out of a cell. The colonists need me accountable, because half of them are going to wake up and learn their families died while the Combine balanced books, and somebody has to stand there and answer for it who isn't a logo." I shrugged. "I'm the airlock, Vance. Both sides walk through me. Airlocks don't get to be passengers."

Something moved through her expression then — not sympathy, Vance didn't stock that item, but recognition. The look of one professional watching another burn their own inventory. Maybe, in her private taxonomy, that even counted as respect.

"You understand," she said, "that if I sign this, I'm confessing. The terms themselves are an admission. Power sufficient for full revival, concealed. A second ship, erased. My signature on a settlement that restores what my employer destroyed—" She shook her head. "I'll be lucky to end my career managing freight claims on a rock nobody wants."

"That's the other part of the deal. You testify first. Before the tribunal convenes, before the colonies' joint commission gets organized, before anyone else can shape the narrative — you walk into that room and you give them everything, voluntarily, and you give them *me* as the reason why. 'Mara Brightwater forced the issue.' Let them decide what that's worth. It's worth more than running, and you know it. Running is what people do when they think the story ends with them. Yours doesn't. Yours ends with you on a witness stand, and the only variable is whether you're the villain or the whistleblower who arrived tragically late."

"*Tragically late,*" she echoed. And then, horribly, she smiled. It was a real smile, small and tired and almost human, and it frightened me more than anything else she'd done. "You know I was going to offer you a berth. Back on the courier, day one. Half the sleepers, the crew released, and you on the first ship out with a clean name and a bonus. You could have been gone by now."

"I know."

"Why refuse a good deal for a bad one?"

"Because the good deal was yours, and yours always had a catch. I read your arithmetic, Vance. 'Skilled and fertile.' You had a column for *fertile*. Two thousand human beings and you were grading them like breeding stock." I let that sit between us, cold as the pods. "My deal doesn't have a catch. It just has a cost, and I'm the one paying it. That's the whole difference between us, and it's the only reason I'm letting you walk out of this bay instead of venting it."

We stood there in the amber dark, two thousand heartbeats ticking around us at four per minute, and Lira Vance — Adjuster-in-Chief, architect of triage, the most dangerous kind of honest — extended her hand.

"Terms as stated," she said. "Crew released. Echo revived at full capacity. Brightwater excluded from all revival manifests, retained as material witness. My testimony, voluntary and complete, within seventy-two hours." Her grip was dry and strong and brief. "You realize the tribunal will still hang you with half of this. Your inspection. Your flight. Your twenty years of silence."

"I know. I signed up for that too."

"No," she said, gathering her slate, moving toward the bay door with the unhurried stride of a woman whose surrender had been converted into strategy. "You signed up for theirs. Mine you just survived." At the threshold she paused, and didn't turn around. "For what it's worth — and it isn't worth much — the person who falsified that inspection twenty years ago and the person standing in this bay are not the same entry in the ledger. I know entries. This one depreciated differently."

Then she was gone, and the door sighed shut, and I was alone with the sleepers and the smell of antiseptic and the slow pulse of the amber lights.

I put my hand flat against pod 1147. Through the frost on the lid I could just make out a face — young, slack, dreaming whatever cryo lets you dream, which is nothing, which is mercy. One of the two thousand nobodies. One of Sef's shipmates, maybe. Maybe Sef themself, though the manifests wouldn't tell me until the bays opened, and I'd made sure I wouldn't be there when they did.

"Forty-one confessions," I told the pod quietly. "Forty-one deaths Vespera carried alone because no one else would count them. And now two thousand more, if we get this wrong." The amber light pulsed under my palm, patient as a held breath. "So we won't get it wrong. That's the plan. First one in history I've ever liked."

Behind me, the bay intercom crackled — Dov, patched through from the Rest, his voice rough with eighteen hours of barricades and bad coffee and the particular exhaustion of people who have won something and not yet been allowed to stop fighting.

"Mara. It's done. She countersigned, it's in the system, Vespera's holding the contract in triplicate. Also—" a pause, and I could hear him choosing to say it, "—the frigate's pulling back off the docking clamps. Crew's walking free. Hydroponics is passing around the good tea, the stuff Chidihoardedsince the last supply run. You should—" another pause. "You should see it. People are crying and pretending they aren't. It's disgusting. Get up here."

"In a minute," I said. "Tell Vespera to prep the revival sequence. Echo first. Full capacity, per the settlement."

Another pause, longer, and when Dov spoke again his voice had gone soft in the way it only went when he was scared for me and refusing to say so. "And you? Where does the sequence put you?"

"Nowhere," I said. "That's the point."

I stayed a while longer, hand on the glass, listening to two thousand hearts beat at the speed of patience. Somewhere beyond sensor range, a scarred hulk called Echo drifted with its cargo of the erased, waiting twenty years for someone to do arithmetic honestly. In seventy-two hours, Vance would testify. In a week, the tribunals would convene, and I'd stand in the dock and answer for the person I'd been. And then — if the power held, if the barges flew true, if the universe accepted payment in guilt —

The bays would open. And among the waking, blinking, furious, grieving two thousand, there would be a face I'd know anywhere, wearing an expression I couldn't predict, looking at a stranger who shared their name.

The amber light pulsed. Once. Twice.

Like something beginning to breathe.
