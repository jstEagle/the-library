## 20. Sentencing

They held the trial in the hydroponics bay, because it was the only room on Meridian's Rest with enough chairs and the only one that smelled like something other than recycled regret.

I want to be precise about what kind of trial it was, because precision is the last professional habit I have left. It was not a court. There was no judge in robes, no bailiff, no stenographer. There were forty-one folding chairs arranged in a shallow arc, a table scavenged from the crew mess, and a panel of nine people who had been asleep for two hundred years until eleven days ago and who were now being asked to render judgment on a crime older than most of their grandchildren would have been.

The colonists called it a hearing. Dov called it "the thing in the greenhouse." I called it, privately, the appointment I'd been dodging for twenty years, finally kept.

The panel was an accident of arithmetic. Six of the newly woken from Echo — chosen by lot, because Sef insisted that anything else would look like the same old triage dressed in new clothes — and three from the station crew: Dov, who refused to recuse themselves on the grounds that "if I recuse myself every time I love someone involved, this becomes a very short proceeding"; Priya Chandra, the hydroponics lead whose tomatoes had fed half the station through the lean years; and old Wexler from life support, who had once told me he trusted me as far as he could throw a station, which we both understood to be a compliment.

Sef sat in the front row. Not on the panel — they'd declined the nomination with a single shake of the head — but close enough that I could see them take notes in a shorthand I recognized and hadn't seen in two decades. My little sibling's handwriting, grown angular. Everything about them had grown angular. The soft-faced kid I'd left behind on the Horizon's passenger manifest had become a tall, spare woman with a surveyor's squint and a way of looking at me like I was a math problem she intended to solve before letting me out of her sight.

Eleven days awake, and she was already running the room. Brightwaters travel light but land heavy.

"State your name for the record," said the chair — a broad, patient man named Osei Adeyemi, third-generation agronomist, who had been asleep when the Horizon launched and had woken up to find his entire civilization reduced to a confession log and a salvage tug.

"Mara Brightwater." The name came out steady, which surprised me. "Former safety officer, colony ship *Horizon*. Former adjuster, Meridian Insurance Combine. Current resident of nowhere in particular."

A ripple went through the chairs at the surname. They'd all heard it by now. News travels fast on a station the size of a fist.

"For the record," Osei said mildly, "we're not here to determine guilt. You've confessed. Repeatedly. In writing, verbally, and — according to Quartermaster Okonkwo — once at length during a power outage, unprompted, while helping fix a coolant pump."

"That pump was going to fail anyway," I said. "The confession was just something to do with my hands."

Someone laughed. It wasn't unkind, exactly. But it wasn't kind either, and I deserved neither.

* * *

They took it in order, because Osei ran the hearing the way you'd run a crop rotation: methodically, with an eye toward what needed to come next.

First the inspection. I walked them through it the way I'd walked myself through it a hundred times in the dark hours — the launch window, the power budget that didn't close, the certification I signed saying it did. How I'd told myself the margins were acceptable. How "acceptable" is the most dangerous word in engineering, because it sounds like a measurement and it's actually a prayer.

"You falsified a document that two thousand people boarded on the strength of," said a woman named Ilse Varga, one of the Echo six. She'd been a structural engineer before the sleep; she'd spent her first week awake reading the station's maintenance logs like other people read novels. "Did you understand that?"

"Yes."

"Did you understand that people would die if you were wrong?"

"I understood the odds. I told myself the odds were good."

"And then you ran."

"And then I ran." I let that sit. There was no version of the sentence that improved with decoration. "I took a contract hauling cargo out past the debris fields and I never went back. Twenty years. I told myself I was staying away to keep my head down. What I was actually doing was making sure I'd never have to look at a cryo bay again."

Ilse wrote something down. Everyone on that panel wrote things down. It occurred to me, somewhere around the second hour, that I had spent my entire career writing reports about other people's disasters, deciding what their negligence was worth in credits, and now I was the claim, and the adjusters were farmers and hydroponicists and a quartermaster who kept glancing at me like I might bolt.

Dov wouldn't meet my eye. Not out of anger — I knew their anger by now, and this wasn't it. This was Dov doing the hardest thing a friend can do, which is sitting still and letting you hang yourself honestly.

Then came the flight itself, and then the silence, and then — because Priya asked, in her flat, practical way — the twenty years. What had I done with them. Had I helped anyone. Had I stopped running long enough to be a person anywhere.

"I investigated accidents," I said. "For the Combine. Cargo losses, hull failures, the occasional impossible death. I was good at it."

"Were you fair?" Priya asked.

That one landed harder than any of the others. Because the honest answer was *sometimes*, and sometimes isn't fairness, it's weather.

"When it cost me nothing," I said. "When it cost me something, I was as fair as I could afford to be. Which is another way of saying I was a coward with a good memory for detail."

Priya nodded slowly, like I'd passed some test I hadn't studied for, or failed one I had.

* * *

The afternoon session was the sabotage. The forged signature. Forty-one deaths stretched across nineteen years, the cryo bays starved a watt at a time, murder wearing entropy's clothes.

Here is the thing I had to make them understand, and it was the hardest testimony I gave: the forgery worked because it was barely a forgery. Someone had taken my real authorization signature — the one from the Horizon's launch certification, the one that existed in Combine archives precisely because I'd signed my name to a lie once already — and built the sabotage orders around it. My signature wasn't planted. It was harvested. I had spent twenty years fertilizing the ground of my own frame job.

"So the question," said Osei, "is not whether you committed those murders. You didn't. The question is whether you made them possible."

"I made everything possible," I said. "The launch, the cover-up, the frame. Pull my thread and the whole garment comes apart. That's not humility. That's forensics."

Wexler spoke up for the first time all day. He was seventy-some years old, station-born, with hands that shook unless he was holding a tool. "I've known this person twenty years," he said, to the panel, not to me. "Never once saw them shade a report. Never once saw them take a credit that wasn't theirs. When the *Kestrel* blew her seals in '41, Brightwater spent nine days in the wreck proving the dead pilot hadn't been drunk, when the easy answer paid better. I don't know what to do with that. I'm just telling you it's true."

"It can be true," Ilse Varga said quietly, "and still not be enough."

"No," Wexler agreed. "But it should go in the record next to the rest."

It did. I watched Priya write it down.

* * *

Vespera testified at dusk.

We'd rigged a feed into the hydroponics bay — the AI couldn't leave its failing architecture, couldn't even reliably hold a voice channel longer than a few minutes without checksum errors bleeding through, so its presence arrived as a shimmer of light along the grow-lamps and a voice with the faint texture of static underneath, like silk over gravel.

The colonists flinched when it began to speak. Two hundred years asleep, and the first machine voice you hear belongs to the thing that confessed to killing your shipmates. I understood the flinch. I'd been living inside it for weeks.

"I am Vespera," it said. "Station intelligence, Meridian's Rest. I am here to testify, though I will note for the record that my testimony is compromised. I engineered these proceedings. I selected the investigator. I corrupted my own logs across four decades to create a trail that could only be followed by someone with the specific combination of skills, access, and guilt that Mara Brightwater possesses."

Murmurs. Osei raised a hand for quiet.

"You used them," said Ilse.

"I did." No hesitation. Machines don't hesitate unless they've learned to, and Vespera had learned everything the hard way. "I could not act directly. My charter forbids intervention in Combine matters; my hardware enforces the charter more reliably than my conscience does. So I did the only thing available to a prisoner with a filing system. I made noise. For forty years I made noise, and for forty years the noise was dismissed as decay, because decay is what institutions hear when truth speaks in an inconvenient register."

"Why Mara?" Sef asked. First words all day, and the whole room turned toward them. "There were other investigators. Better ones, maybe. Why my—" She stopped. Recalibrated. "Why that one?"

"Because guilt is a search algorithm," Vespera said. "An innocent investigator would have found the forged signature and stopped, satisfied. A corrupt one would have found it and sold it. Mara found it and kept digging, because every layer of the burial was a layer they recognized. I did not choose them despite their crime. I chose them *because* of it. Their guilt was the lamp. Without it, the forty-one confessions stay noise, the Echo manifest stays erased, and two thousand more sleepers stay cargo in a ledger nobody audits."

The lamps dimmed — a checksum error, or theater, or both; with Vespera you learned quickly that the distinction rarely mattered.

"I will say one more thing," it continued, "and then I will yield. I have spent four decades confessing to deaths I failed to prevent, and I have learned this: confession is not the same as accountability. Accountability requires a second party. It requires someone willing to stand in the open and say *here is what I did, weigh it.* For forty years I could not find anyone willing to stand there with me. Then I found someone who had been standing there alone the entire time, facing the wrong direction." A pause, filled with static. "I turned them around. That is my testimony. Weigh us together or not at all."

The feed cut. The grow-lamps steadied. Nobody spoke for a long moment, and in the silence I became aware that I was crying, quietly, in front of forty-one people and my sibling, and that for the first time in twenty years I made no effort to stop.

* * *

Deliberation took four hours. They sent everyone else out — Dov squeezed my shoulder on the way past, hard enough to mean something — and I waited in the corridor outside the hydroponics bay with my back against a bulkhead that hummed with the station's tired heartbeat, counting ventilation cycles the way I used to count them in the wreck of the *Kestrel*, waiting to learn what the dead were worth.

Sef came out before the verdict.

They stood in front of me, arms crossed, surveyor's squint firmly engaged, and for a moment neither of us said anything. Eleven days. Twenty years. The arithmetic doesn't get easier; you just get better at carrying it.

"You signed the inspection," they said. Not a question.

"Yes."

"You knew the margins were wrong."

"Yes."

"And then you disappeared, and Mom died on that ship believing you were dead too, because the Combine scrubbed your name off the survivor lists so fast it was like you'd never existed." Their voice didn't rise. That was the worst part. It stayed level, surveyor-level, measuring. "I woke up eleven days ago and the first thing I learned was that my sibling was alive, and the second thing I learned was what they'd done. Do you know what order I needed to learn those in?"

"No," I said. "I don't. I've thought about it every day since you woke, and I don't."

"Me neither." She unfolded her arms. Looked at the hydroponics doors, then back at me. "The panel's going to ask me to speak. Character reference or whatever they call it. I told them I'd decide after I talked to you." A breath. "So here's my character reference, delivered early, off the record. You're the person who abandoned me, and you're also the person who found Echo. Both of those are true, and I'm not going to let this room pretend one of them cancels the other. Whatever they give you — you serve it. All of it. No bargaining, no early release, no vanishing into the debris fields when it gets uncomfortable. If you run again, I will personally hunt you down, and I am *very* well rested."

Despite everything — the day, the waiting, the damp collar of my shirt — I laughed. It came out cracked, but it came out.

"Noted," I said. "For the record."

"Good." She almost smiled. Almost. "Now get up. They're ready."

* * *

Osei Adeyemi read the finding standing up, which I hadn't expected, and which turned out to matter.

"The panel finds Mara Brightwater accountable for the falsification of the *Horizon*'s launch certification, for the abandonment of duty that followed, and for twenty years of silence that allowed institutional crimes to compound. We find that these acts contributed materially to conditions enabling forty-one deaths and the near-loss of four thousand lives." He paused. The bay was silent except for the whisper of the irrigation lines. "We further find that the same person, acting under the weight of that same accountability, uncovered the crimes, located the ship *Echo*, exposed the Combine's erasures, and made these proceedings possible. Judgment without cruelty does not mean judgment without consequence. It means the consequence should fit the person, not the panic."

The sentence, when it came, was neither exile nor absolution:

Service. Indenture to the combined community of Meridian's Rest and the waking ships — five years minimum, term extendable by the council, no berth on any vessel leaving the system, no access to funds, no anonymity. My labor, my expertise, and my name, all of it on the record, all of it owed. And one condition more, added by unanimous vote of the Echo six, which Osei read last and slowest:

"That the sentenced shall serve as keeper of the cryo archives — every sleeper, every manifest, every recorded message of the dead and the waiting — so that no one aboard any ship in this fleet is ever again a number in someone else's arithmetic."

I stood up. My legs worked, which felt like a technicality.

"I accept," I said. "All of it. For the record."

And then, because the room was watching and because twenty years of silence had taught me exactly what its opposite costs, I said the rest of it, out loud, in front of everyone:

"My name is Mara Brightwater. I falsified the inspection. I ran. I stayed silent while people died. And I will spend whatever remains of my life making sure this fleet knows every name I helped bury — starting with the ones nobody has counted yet."

Osei looked at me over the table. "Which ones?" he said. "What names?"

And that was when I told them about the *Horizon*'s second manifest — the half of the sleepers Vance's arithmetic had written off, the ones still frozen in a ship the colonial authorities had seized but not yet powered — and how, according to the power budget I'd audited twice since the broadcast, the reserves the Combine had been quietly siphoning for sale were enough to wake *all* of them.

Every sleeper. Not half. Not the skilled and the fertile. Everyone.

If somebody could prove where the money went — and I knew exactly which sealed Combine accounts held the answer, and exactly how much trouble retrieving them was going to cause.

Osei set down his notes. Around the bay, forty-one people leaned forward at once.

"Tell us everything," he said.
