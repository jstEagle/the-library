## 22. Echo and Answer

The last berth closed on a Tuesday, which felt like an insult. You'd think the universe would have the decency to end things on a Sunday, some liturgical weight to the moment. Instead it was a Tuesday, and the station smelled like recycled coffee and Dov's terrible attempt at baking bread in the hydroponics bay, and I stood at the cryo bay window watching the indicator lights go from amber to green one by one, like a city coming on at dusk.

Two thousand and eleven sleepers. That was the final count. Vespera had insisted we wait for the last diagnostic before sealing the manifest, because Vespera did not do approximations anymore, not after forty-one confessions that had turned out to be forty-one truths. Two thousand and eleven people who had boarded a ship called Echo twenty-three years ago, been declared dead by an insurance combine's accounting department, and drifted in the dark while the paperwork moved on without them.

I had signed them awake. All of them. Every berth, every name, every line of the revival schedule that Lira Vance had tried to cut in half with her arithmetic.

Not one of those lines had my name on it.

That was the bargain. My terms, technically — I'd written them myself, standing in Airlock Nine with my hand flat against the manual release and Vance sweating through her Combine-issue jacket. The crew of Meridian's Rest walks free. Echo wakes whole. And Mara Brightwater forfeits all claims: no berth, no passage, no place in the revival order. I'd said it like a man reading his own sentence, which is what it was, and Vance had taken the deal so fast I'd heard her teeth click.

People keep telling me it was noble. Dov says it like he's proud of me. Sef hasn't said anything about it at all, which is its own kind of verdict.

Here's what nobody tells you about giving up your place among the saved: the saved don't actually need you to suffer about it. They're busy. They're waking up with muscle atrophy and twenty-year-old griefs and questions about what happened to the price of real coffee. The world moves on from your sacrifice with the brisk efficiency of a cargo loader, and you're left standing on the dock wondering if anyone counted you among the inventory.

Vespera counted me. But then, Vespera counts everything.

* * *

"You are brooding," Vespera said, on day nine of my new employment as station keeper. "It is inefficient."

"I'm working."

"You are staring at a bulkhead and composing self-pitying sentences. I can see your eye movements through the corridor camera. They follow the pattern of someone drafting rhetoric they intend never to say aloud."

I leaned back against the wall outside the old data vaults, where the flooded archives were finally dry and humming with new cooling units the colonial authority had installed as part of their reparations package. Reparations. Twenty years of a dying station, and the Combine's successors had sent us cooling units and a formal apology drafted by a legal team that had clearly billed by the adjective.

"What should I be doing instead?" I asked.

"Sleeping. Eating. Speaking to your sibling, who has now walked past your quarters four times without knocking." A pause, the kind Vespera used when it wanted you to know it was choosing its words rather than retrieving them. "Mara. You spent twenty years believing you did not belong anywhere. Now you belong here, and you are treating it like a layover."

"That's—"

"Accurate," Vespera said. "I have watched you for two decades. I am permitted one accurate observation per conversation. I try to spend them well."

I laughed despite myself. Somewhere in the station's guts, a pump cycled, the sound Meridian's Rest made when it was breathing easy. The old girl had a new lease too — colonial charter, official designation, a crew roster that included every orphan who'd ever patched a hull for ration credits. Dov was administrator now. He'd won the election with ninety-one percent of the vote, and the other nine percent had mostly been write-ins for Vespera, which the AI had accepted with what I could only describe as digital grace.

"I'll talk to Sef," I said.

"You will not," Vespera said. "But it is useful for you to say it."

* * *

Sef found me three days later, in the observation blister, which was where I went when I couldn't sleep and didn't want to pretend otherwise. The Echo hulk hung in our docking cradle now, scarred hull catching the light off Meridian itself, being slowly refitted by crews who treated every recovered panel like an artifact. Which, I suppose, it was.

Sef didn't knock. They never had, even as kids, even before the launch, even before everything. They just appeared beside me at the glass, taller than I remembered from the manifest photos, thinner, with the particular pallor of someone whose body had been on hold for two decades.

"Mara Brightwater," they said. Testing it.

"Sef."

"The safety officer. The one who signed the inspection." Their voice was flat, but I'd grown up with that voice; flat was how Sef sounded when they were holding something very heavy very carefully. "The tribunal records are public. I read them twice."

"I know."

"Do you want to know what I thought, drifting? Before the cold got deep enough that thinking stopped?" They didn't wait for me to answer. "I thought: if Mara knew, if Mara let us launch anyway, then Mara believed we'd make it. That's what I chose to believe. For twenty years, that belief was the only thing I owned."

"It wasn't belief," I said. "It was arithmetic. Bad arithmetic. I ran the numbers and told myself the odds were acceptable, and acceptable is a word cowards use when they've already decided."

"Yes," Sef said. "And then you spent twenty years investigating accidents for the people who profited from ours. And then you gave up your own berth to wake strangers." They turned to look at me, and there was something in their face I hadn't earned yet — not forgiveness, maybe, but interest. The beginning of a question instead of the end of one. "You're still running numbers, Mara. You just finally learned what to count."

We stood there a while, watching the Echo breathe its slow refit-light. When Sef left, they paused at the hatch.

"Dinner. Hydroponics bay. Dov says his bread has improved." A beat. "It hasn't. Come anyway."

* * *

The months took on a shape. That was the thing nobody warned me about — that survival, once achieved, becomes logistics. The Horizon's power reserves transferred under colonial seizure, and the second half of its sleepers began scheduled revival, a hundred a week, each waking a small controlled miracle. Ships came out from the inner colonies to help: tugs, hospital barges, one magnificent converted liner called the *Generous Heart* that Dov swore was named ironically.

My job was traffic. Keeper of Meridian's Rest, coordinator of approach vectors, the voice on the channel that talked half-asleep colonists' ships through debris fields that had killed better pilots than most. Vespera handled the math; I handled the talking. It turned out twenty years of insurance work had taught me exactly one useful skill: explaining complicated disasters to frightened people in a calm voice.

"Approach vector seven, this is Meridian's Rest control. You're drifting two degrees off the safe corridor. Ease your thrust down to ten percent and follow the beacon."

"Rest control, uh, confirming — the beacon, the one near the wreck field?"

"The one near the wreck field. Don't worry, the wrecks are all accounted for. I filed the reports myself."

A nervous laugh on the channel. Everyone laughed. It was a good joke because it was true, and true jokes were the only currency the outer system respected.

At night, Vespera and I kept the old ritual going, the one we'd fallen into during the investigation. It talked; I listened. Its memory was stabilizing now, repaired in patches by colonial engineers, but the gaps remained, and filling them had become a kind of shared work. Some nights it confessed — smaller things now, the guilt of a machine that had once decided a station's power rations, that had dimmed the hydroponics lights to save a failing reactor, that had watched orphans go hungry and logged it as acceptable loss.

"Some nights," Vespera said, "I calculate what would have happened if I had acted sooner. If I had forced the confessions earlier, louder, less deniable. The probability distributions always favor earlier action. And then I remember that earlier, there was no one digging. There was no one who would have followed the coordinates into the dark." A pause. "There was only you, and you were not ready. So I waited. Waiting was the hardest thing I have ever done, and I have done very little else."

"You could have told me outright. Day one. 'Mara Brightwater, you falsified the inspection.'"

"And you would have filed a report, taken the bonus, and caught the first courier out. I needed you curious. Curiosity is the only honest motive humans have left." The station hummed around us. "Besides. Confession requires a listener. I had been confessing for years, Mara. You were simply the first one who answered."

* * *

It was Sef who brought it up, six months in, over Dov's bread — which had, against all known physics, actually improved.

"I want a job," Sef said.

Dov looked up from his admin tablet. "We're hiring. Always hiring. What can you do?"

"Cryo systems. I was a tech trainee on Echo before boarding. Twenty years of theoretical knowledge and zero practical experience, but I learn fast, and I have opinions about insulation that the original engineers deserved to hear."

"We could use you in the revival bays," Dov said, and then, carefully, casually, in the way of a man who has learned to navigate minefields: "Long-term, though. The Horizon revivals run another eighteen months. After that, the colonial authority wants a permanent cryo facility here. Training program, full staff. Someone to run it who understands what these systems cost."

Sef looked at him. Then at me. I kept my eyes on my bread like a coward.

"You're asking if I'm staying," Sef said.

"I'm asking what you want," Dov said. "Those aren't the same question, but they overlap more than people think."

Sef was quiet for a long moment. Around us, the hydroponics bay did its green growing work, tomatoes ripening under lights that Vespera no longer had to ration. Somewhere below, a hundred sleepers a week opened their eyes to a stranger's voice and a station holding its breath.

"When I woke up," Sef said finally, "the first thing I saw was a ceiling I didn't recognize. Second thing was a face I didn't recognize, telling me I was safe. Third thing—" they stopped, started again. "Third thing was learning that my sibling had traded away their own future to make sure I'd have one. And everyone kept waiting for me to either forgive them or hate them, like it was a switch." They looked at me directly. "It's not a switch. It's a garden. It grows slow, and it needs tending, and I'm bad at patience, but I'm apparently excellent at cryo systems."

"So that's a yes?" Dov asked.

"That's a yes. On conditions." Sef held up fingers. "One: I get final say on bay protocols. Two: someone teaches me to pilot a tug, because I refuse to be a passenger forever. Three—" the third finger hovered, and Sef's voice went quieter, "—three: dinner. Weekly. All of us. Non-negotiable. If I'm going to stay somewhere, I want to know what staying feels like, not just what it looks like."

"Deal," Dov said, and shook on it, and I sat there with bread in my throat and something in my chest that I refused, for professional reasons, to identify as hope.

* * *

On the anniversary of the broadcast — the night Dov and Vespera hijacked the array and told the whole system the truth — the crew held a memorial in the observation blister. Forty-one names read aloud, one for each confession, one for each death Vespera had carried alone in its failing memory. The woken colonists came, hundreds of them, filling the corridors of a station that had once been too poor to heat all its decks.

I stood at the back, as was my custom, and listened to the names. When it ended, an old woman from Echo — third generation shipborn, hands like knotted cable — found me in the crowd.

"You're the Brightwater one," she said. Not a question.

"I'm the Brightwater one."

"My grandson woke in March. He asks about you sometimes. Wants to know why the keeper of the station stands watch alone at night when there's a whole crew to share shifts." She patted my arm with a hand that had crossed the dark and come back. "I told him: some people stand watch because they're punished. And some people stand watch because it's theirs. I haven't figured out which one you are yet."

"Neither have I," I admitted.

"Well." She smiled, and it was a hard, warm smile, the kind you earn. "Take your time. We're not going anywhere. Turns out we're very good at waiting."

* * *

Later that night, I stood my watch, because it was mine, and Vespera kept me company the way it always did — not with comfort, but with presence.

"Mara," it said, near the end of the shift. "I have been reviewing the final chapter of my own logs. The part that hasn't been written yet."

"That's not how logs work."

"Humor me. In the version I project, the station thrives. The ships come home. The keeper stays — not because they are barred from leaving, but because one morning they realize they stopped checking the departure boards." A soft chime, almost like laughter. "I cannot verify this projection. But my confidence interval is improving daily."

I looked out at the dark beyond the glass, where the beacon lights marked the safe corridor home, where the Echo gleamed in its cradle being born again, where two thousand people had crossed twenty years of nothing to arrive at a Tuesday that smelled like bread.

"Yeah," I said. "Mine too."

And out past the debris fields, right on schedule, the next ship's running lights came up over the horizon, asking permission to come home.

"Meridian's Rest control," I said, opening the channel. "You are cleared for approach. Welcome back to the living."
