## 20. League Ambush

The Grace's corridors had a way of holding sound the way a cupped hand holds water. Every footstep came back to you a half-second late, softened, as if the ship were considering whether to return it. Coral had been aboard for six hours and still hadn't stopped noticing it — that patient acoustic hush, the sense of a vessel listening.

She stood in the command blister at the Grace's prow, watching Kestrel-9 hang in the viewport like a rusted tooth. The relay's antenna array caught the distant light of the starless dark beyond dead space and threw it back in thin silver filaments. Somewhere inside that derelict, Wren was working through a fever on the countdown systems, and somewhere behind them all, the Combine fleet was coming.

And now — this.

"Contact," Juno said from the sensor station, her voice flat in the way Coral had learned meant *very much not flat*. "Three signatures, inbound from the shadow of the relay. League transponders. They're running dark until they're not."

"They're not being subtle," Coral said.

"No." Juno's hands moved across the console, quick and precise, a defector's muscle memory she couldn't have shed if she'd wanted to. "They want us to see them coming. That's the message."

The first ship resolved out of the black like a knife being drawn slowly from a sheath: a League corvette, long and narrow, its hull painted in the matte grey that ate radar and swallowed light. Then two more flanking it, wedge-shaped interceptors riding close as pilot fish. Their running lights bloomed all at once — green, then white, then green again — the old League signal for *heave to and be counted*.

Coral felt something cold settle into her chest, familiar as an old coat. She'd run this gauntlet before, in miniature, in contested orbit around Earth. But this was different. This time they weren't checking her manifest.

They were coming for her mother's ghost.

"They're hailing," Juno said. "Audio only. Commander Oyelaran, League Third Squadron, acting under Emergency Salvage Authority." A pause. Her jaw tightened. "That authority doesn't extend past the shipping lanes. They're inventing jurisdiction."

"Put them through."

The voice that filled the command blister was calm, cultured, and utterly without warmth. *"Vessel identifying as Horizon's Grace. You are in proximity to restricted relay infrastructure. Power down your drives and prepare for boarding inspection. Resistance will be logged as hostile action against the Free Orbit League."*

Coral keyed the channel. "Commander Oyelaran. This is Coral Dunmore, master of the Horizon's Grace. We are a civilian vessel conducting lawful recovery of abandoned property. I don't recognize your salvage claim."

A silence, long enough to be deliberate. When the voice returned, there was something almost gentle in it, which frightened her more than the guns would have.

*"Ms. Dunmore. We know who you are. We know whose daughter you are. And we know what's in your holds."*

* * *

They docked anyway. There had never really been a choice. Three warships against one wounded freighter-legend with a feverish technician and a defector whose face was still on League wanted-notices — Coral did the arithmetic twice and got the same answer both times.

So she sealed the archive core in the Grace's deep hold, set the failsafes Ilsa had left humming in the ship's bones, and waited in the docking bay with Juno at her shoulder while the airlock cycled.

The boarding party came through in ones and twos, not the boot-stomping column Coral half expected. Eight of them, armored but unhelmeted, rifles slung rather than raised. At their center walked a woman in her fifties with iron-grey hair cropped short and a League commander's insignia worn the way some people wore scars — openly, without apology.

Commander Adaeze Oyelaran stopped three meters away and looked at Coral for a long moment. Not at the ship. Not at Juno. At Coral, with an expression that was almost — almost — kind.

"You have her eyes," Oyelaran said.

Coral's breath went out of her. "You knew my mother."

"I served with her. Twenty years ago, before any of this." Oyelaran gestured vaguely — at the ship, the relay, the whole bleeding edge of dead space. "Ilsa Dunmore taught me navigation. She taught half the League's officer corps navigation, back when we still pretended we were explorers instead of toll collectors." Her gaze drifted up along the Grace's ribbed ceiling, and something moved across her face that might have been grief, or might have been appetite. Coral couldn't tell the difference, and that frightened her.

"She never mentioned you," Coral said.

"No. She wouldn't have. After she—" Oyelaran stopped herself. Recalibrated. "After she died, she wouldn't have wanted her students connected to her. Too many questions."

*After she died.* The words landed wrong, and Coral watched Oyelaran hear them land wrong, watched the commander's composure flicker like a bad connection. So she knew. Or she suspected. Or she'd spent seven years not asking questions she should have asked.

"You're here for the archive," Coral said. Not a question either.

"I'm here to secure it." Oyelaran's voice firmed, the gentleness folding away like a chart being put back in its tube. "The Combine is coming, Ms. Dunmore. You know that better than anyone — you've been running from their interdiction schedule since Kestrel-9. The League can protect this material. We have the ships, the vaults, the infrastructure to keep it safe."

"And then what?"

"And then it stays safe."

"Locked in a League depot," Coral said. "Classified. Accessible to officers with clearance. Studied by committees. Quoted selectively in tribunals that go nowhere." She heard her own voice rising and didn't stop it. "That's not safety, Commander. That's just a different lock."

Oyelaran's expression didn't change, but the temperature around her did. Two of the boarding party shifted their weight. Juno's hand drifted toward her sidearm and Coral shook her head, a small motion, barely a tremor.

"You don't understand what you're carrying," Oyelaran said quietly.

"Don't I?"

"The evidence in that hold could break the Combine. It could end their charter, dissolve their fleets, free forty stations that have been paying tribute since before you were born. Do you know what happens if that data just— *drifts out there*, uncontrolled? Half the outer colonies will secede overnight. The other half will panic. There will be war, Ms. Dunmore, real war, the kind Earth's generation promised itself it had outgrown. The League isn't trying to own the truth. We're trying to *survive* it responsibly."

Coral thought about that word. *Responsibly.* She'd heard it before, in other mouths, wearing other uniforms. The Combine called it stewardship. The League called it responsibility. Everyone had such beautiful names for the same locked door.

"There's a third option," Coral said.

"There's always a third option," Oyelaran agreed. "It's usually where people die."

* * *

They put her in the Grace's old wardroom — Ilsa's wardroom, though no one said so — with tea she didn't touch and two guards at the door who kept looking at the bulkhead plates like they expected the ship itself to object. Maybe they did. Maybe everyone in the League had grown up on the story of the Horizon's Grace, the ship that never flew, the martyr-vessel of the abandonment era. Coral wondered what it did to them, standing inside the legend. Whether it felt like standing in a cathedral or a crime scene.

Juno slipped in twenty minutes later, having talked her way past the guards with a defector's fluency in bureaucratic nonsense. She dropped into the seat across from Coral and put both hands flat on the table.

"Well?" Coral said.

"They've got a prize crew prepping to take the ship to Meridian Station — sorry, *Halcyon Anchorage*, they renamed everything after the schism, you'd hate it. Oyelaran's arguing with her own executive officer about whether to wait for the Combine or run for it. Half her squadron thinks this is a trap. The other half thinks it's a promotion." Juno exhaled. "And they found the failsafes in the hold. They haven't cracked them yet, but they will. Ilsa built good locks, but locks are just puzzles with pride."

Coral stared at the tea going cold between her hands. "My mother flew with these people."

"My mother *paid taxes* to these people," Juno said. "Doesn't make them family."

"That's not what I mean." Coral looked up. "Juno. On the relay, you told me why the Grace never launched. The Combine sabotaged it. The League looked the other way. You told me the truth about that night like it was a wound you'd been keeping clean for years."

"It was."

"So how can you sit there and tell me handing them the archive is anything other than handing the arsonist the fire report?"

Juno held her gaze, and Coral saw the conflict move through her like weather — loyalty to the institution she'd betrayed, loyalty to the woman she'd betrayed it for, and underneath both, the stubborn bedrock of someone who had chosen truth once already, at terrible cost, and knew exactly what it weighed.

"I'm not telling you to hand it over," Juno said finally. "I'm telling you what Oyelaran told me when I cornered her in the corridor. Word for word." She leaned forward. "'If the League doesn't take it, the Combine destroys it. If the Combine doesn't destroy it, the colonies tear each other apart over it. Someone has to hold it. Better us than them.' That's her whole moral universe, Coral. That's the sentence she's built her career on. And the worst part—" Juno's voice caught, just slightly. "The worst part is she believes it. She's not corrupt. She's *tired*. There's a difference, and it matters, and it changes nothing."

Coral looked down at her hands. They were steady. She was almost surprised.

Seven years ago, her mother had sat her down in the vault's cramped galley and told her the same thing in different words. *Someone has to keep the seeds safe, Coral. Someone has to be responsible. That's what we are. That's what we chose.* And Coral had believed it, had built her whole life inside that sentence, had turned grief into duty because duty at least gave the grief somewhere to stand.

But her mother hadn't kept the seeds safe. Her mother had built a second archive in the dark and lied to everyone, including the daughter keeping her first one alive. *Someone has to hold it* was just another way of saying *someone has to control it*. And every faction in the black — Combine, League, whatever came after — had been saying it to each other for decades, passing the same locked door back and forth like a hot stone, calling it stewardship, calling it responsibility, calling it survival.

Nobody, in all these years, had simply opened it.

"Coral?" Juno said. "You've gone somewhere. Where?"

Coral lifted her head. Through the wardroom's small viewport, Kestrel-9 hung against nothing, its antenna filaments catching light. Somewhere in that rusted shell, a countdown was ticking toward broadcast — her mother's last design, rigged to fire unless a human hand chose otherwise. Ilsa had built the choice into the machine itself. Had refused, even in death, to let *someone has to hold it* be the final word.

"Every faction wants to own the truth," Coral said slowly, hearing the shape of it as she spoke. "The Combine wants to bury it. The League wants to warehouse it. The colonies would tear it apart for leverage. And my mother—" her throat tightened, "—my mother wanted to *time* it. Control the release. Decide for everyone when they were ready." She shook her head. "Even her. Even her, Juno. The only person who ever got it right was the machine. Broadcast to everyone, or destroy it forever. No committee. No clearance level. No one holding it."

"That's a terrifying way to live," Juno said.

"Yes," Coral said. "It is."

* * *

The alarm came at 0400 by the Grace's ancient clock — a klaxon Ilsa must have chosen herself, because it didn't shriek so much as toll, deep and bronze, like a bell in a drowned church.

Coral was out of her bunk and moving before the second peal. In the corridor, League crew were stumbling toward stations, and Oyelaran's voice crackled overhead, stripped of all its earlier calm: *"All hands, all hands, contact bearing dead-space ecliptic. Multiple signatures. Combine hull configuration. They found us."*

Coral reached the command blister to find Juno already strapped in, her face lit blue and terrible by the tactical display. Five contacts. No — eight. Running silent until they weren't, unfolding out of the dark like a hand opening, and at their center a cruiser with a drive signature Coral recognized from seven years of running cargo through contested space.

Combine interdiction wing. Here early. Here *now*.

"They followed the League squadron," Juno said grimly. "Or they followed us. Either way—" She looked up, and Coral saw it in her face before she said it. "Either way, nobody's leaving. We're all in the bottle now."

On the display, the League interceptors wheeled to face the incoming Combine formation, and the two fleets hung there in the lightless deep, weapons warming, each waiting for the other to blink.

And in the Grace's hold, sealed behind her mother's failsafes, the truth sat in the dark between them — the one thing in the entire system that both sides wanted, and neither side would ever, ever let go.

Coral unbuckled from the crash couch and stood.

"Where are you going?" Juno demanded.

"To make a phone call," Coral said. "Somebody in this fight has to want something nobody owns."

She was halfway down the corridor when the first shot rang out, and the whole ship shuddered like a struck bell.
