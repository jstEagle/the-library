## 31. Thirty Seconds

The override console sat in a niche carved from the relay's oldest bones, behind a hatch that had not opened in decades until Coral cut it open with a plasma torch and her own two hands. Now she stood before it, glove pressed flat against the cancel switch — a physical thing, mercifully analog, a red-capped lever mounted on a panel scabbed with forty years of dust. Her palm was slick inside her suit. The countdown read **00:00:31**.

Thirty-one seconds.

The relay hummed around her like a hive deciding something. Kestrel-9 had been dying for half a century, its corridors collapsed into ribbons of frost-eaten alloy, its great dish antenna tilted toward nothing, toward everything. And beneath all that ruin, Ilsa Dunmore had threaded a nervous system: capacitors banked in the dark like sleeping animals, fiber runs glowing faint amber where Coral's headlamp touched them, and at the center of it all this one small switch, waiting for her daughter's hand.

**00:00:30.**

Coral could stop it. That was the whole architecture of her mother's design — she understood it now with terrible clarity. The relay would fire on its own unless a Dunmore hand cancelled it. Not any hand. The system wanted *her*. It had wanted her to come here, to stand in this cold, to choose. Her mother had built a machine that could only be silenced by the person it was built for.

Her thumb found the edge of the lever. One motion. Down and left, the way the schematic showed, the way Juno had traced it for her twice with a shaking finger. Cancel. Bury it. Save the Grace, save Wren, save whatever fragile shape their lives still held.

*Save what's left,* Wren had begged her, an hour ago, blood still crusting at their temple. *Coral, please. We can live. Isn't living enough?*

**00:00:28.**

She closed her eyes, and the vault came to her.

Not the vault as it was now — failing, gasping, its atmospheric processors coughing rust into the seed bays — but the vault as it had been on her first day, seven years and a lifetime ago. She was twenty-three and hollowed out by grief, standing in the intake chamber while an old caretaker named Odile walked her past the racks. Ten thousand varieties of wheat. Four thousand beans. Rice from terraces drowned under rising seas before Coral was born. Apples that no orchard on Earth had produced since the Scattering. Each packet labeled in a careful hand, each one a promise someone had made to a future nobody believed in anymore.

*Why do we keep them?* she'd asked Odile, because her mother was three weeks dead and she needed to hear an answer that wasn't a lie about peace or time.

Odile had touched a rack of sorghum seeds older than both of them. *Because someday someone will come asking what Earth was worth. And we'll have an answer they can hold.*

Coral had spent seven years believing that answer was seeds. Dry, patient, innocent. She had hauled cargo through kill-zones and paid bribes to men with Combine pins on their collars, all to keep those racks breathing, all because her mother had told her, in the last transmission she ever sent: *Keep the vault alive, little star. Keep it alive and don't ask why.*

Don't ask why. She had obeyed for seven years. Obedience had calcified into duty, duty into identity, until Coral Dunmore was less a woman than a function — the keeper, the hauler, the one who kept the lights on over ten thousand small sleeping promises.

And the whole time, the real answer had been out here, past the edge of the charts, in a ship that never flew, packed with proof that Earth hadn't been abandoned. It had been *evacuated* — pushed, priced out, starved of transport by men who signed the schedules and called it fate. Her mother had known. Her mother had died — had *pretended* to die — rather than let them find it through her.

**00:00:24.**

*I'm sorry, Mama,* she thought, and didn't know which apology she meant. Sorry for doubting you. Sorry for what I'm about to do. Sorry that you're dead and I'm the one standing here, and you never got to see whether your gamble bloomed or burned.

The relay's hum deepened. Somewhere above her, coolant pumps spun up through bearings that screamed like gulls. The countdown did not care about mothers or daughters. It cared only about arithmetic.

**00:00:22.**

Wren bleeding.

That image would not leave her. Wren in the corridor outside the data core, three days ago, when Calloway's strike team came through the hull breach in a bloom of stun-flares and muzzle light. Wren had thrown themselves across the core housing — across *Ilsa's* core, the archive's beating heart — with their body, arms wrapped around it like a child holding a dog through a storm. The round had caught them high in the shoulder, spun them halfway around, and still they'd held on. Coral had dragged them back by the collar of their suit while Juno laid down covering fire, and Wren had looked up at her through the faceplate fogged with their own breath and said, slurred with shock: *Told you. Told you I'd keep it.*

They were in medbay now, sedated, fevered, the wound infected in the lazy way wounds infect when medbay autoclaves are running on scavenged power. And before the sedation took them, Wren had grabbed Coral's wrist and confessed — *I told the Combine about you, weeks ago, I thought you were compromised, I thought I was saving the vault* — and Coral had stood there holding the hand that betrayed her and felt, absurdly, mostly pity. Because Wren had done exactly what Coral had done for seven years. They had chosen protection over truth. They had decided what someone else was allowed to know.

Everyone in this story was Ilsa. Everyone was guarding something in the dark and calling it love.

**00:00:18.**

Her mother's hands, then. Flashbacks came faster now, the countdown pressing them up out of her like water from squeezed stone.

Ilsa Dunmore in the cargo bay of the *Marrow*, checking a manifest with a stylus tucked behind her ear, humming something tuneless. Ilsa teaching her to read orbital mechanics off a napkin in a dockside noodle bar, drawing gravity wells as circles within circles. *Everything falls, little star. The trick is choosing what you fall toward.* Ilsa the last night, standing at the airlock of some transit station neither of them would name later, because there was no later. She had crouched down — Coral had been sixteen, too old for crouching, and had let her do it anyway — and pressed her forehead against Coral's, and said: *Whatever happens, remember that I chose you. Out of everyone, everything, I chose you.*

Seven years Coral had carried that as comfort. Standing here, she finally heard the second meaning underneath it, the one her mother must have intended all along: *I chose you* — not *I kept you*. Chosen things can be sent away. Chosen things can be left somewhere safe while the dangerous work gets done. Her mother had faked her death, seeded her archive, and abandoned her own daughter deliberately so that no one hunting the truth would ever think to look through a grieving girl hauling seed crates in contested orbit.

It was monstrous. It was brilliant. It was the most Ilsa thing imaginable, and Coral loved her and hated her in the same breath, the way you can only love and hate someone whose blood is in your mouth.

**00:00:14.**

Her thumb trembled against the lever.

Down and left. Cancel. She thought about what cancellation actually meant, now that she'd seen Calloway's stolen intel — the interdiction schedule, the sterilization order, the neat Combine language about *asset denial and sector sanitization*. Cancelling the broadcast wouldn't save the archive. It would just mean the archive died quietly, unwatched, when the Combine fleet burned Kestrel-9 and the Grace and everyone aboard to slag and called it an accident in dead space. There was no version of silence where anyone lived. Silence was just death with better manners.

But broadcasting — broadcasting meant every faction in human space would know her name. Not Ilsa's. *Hers.* Witness: C. Dunmore, custodian, daughter. The Combine would want her dead. The League would want to own her. Every smuggler, every settler, every child born on a station would grow up knowing what her mother had done and what Coral had chosen, and there would be no taking it back, not in fifty years, not ever, not in any universe where records survived.

Her mother had asked her — in the letter, the paper letter, *to the child I chose to protect by leaving* — to decide freely. Not obediently. *You will be tempted to make my choice for me,* Ilsa had written, in handwriting that shook only once, near the end. *Don't. My choice made this mess. Yours has to be yours. Whatever you decide, decide it with your eyes open, and then live with it the way I taught you to live with everything: fully, and without flinching.*

Eyes open, then.

**00:00:11.**

Coral opened her eyes and looked at the switch.

Red cap. Forty years of dust. A machine built by a dead woman who had trusted her daughter more than she had trusted herself — because Ilsa could fake her own death, falsify seven years of manifests, betray every employer and government in the system, but she could not bring herself to press the final button alone. She had needed it to be Coral. Maybe that was cowardice. Maybe it was faith. Coral suspected, with the strange calm settling over her now, that it was both, the way most true things are two things at once.

The hum rose. The amber fibers brightened, banks of capacitors drinking the last stored charge, the whole derelict station waking like something surfacing from a long dive.

**00:00:08.**

She thought of the vault one last time — the real one, the racks, the wheat, the apples — and understood that she was not betraying it. Seeds were promises. This was a promise too. The biggest one Earth had ever made, and the only honest one: *this is what we were, this is what was done to us, this is who did it, remember us accurately or don't remember us at all.*

Odile's voice, across seven years of dust: *Someday someone will come asking what Earth was worth.*

Here was the answer they could hold. All of it. Even the ugly parts. *Especially* the ugly parts.

**00:00:05.**

*Coral.* Wren's voice on the suit channel, thin, feverish, awake somehow against all medical sense. *Coral, whatever you're doing — we're with you. Juno says hurry. I say—* a wet, ragged breath *—I say do what you already know you're going to do.*

Juno, quieter, in the background: *Ten seconds, Dunmore. Fleet's burning hard for the corridor. Whatever happens next—*

*happens fast,* Coral finished silently.

**00:00:03.**

She lifted her hand from the cancel switch.

It was such a small motion. Three centimeters of retreat. Her glove left a perfect print in the dust on the panel, five fingers, her mother's fingers, the Dunmore hand withdrawing from the machine's throat and letting it speak.

**00:00:02.**

*Everything falls,* her mother had said. *The trick is choosing what you fall toward.*

**00:00:01.**

Coral fell toward the light.

The relay fired.

There was no sound in vacuum, but the station *shuddered* — a full-body convulsion as forty years of hoarded charge slammed through the ancient transmitter, and every light on Kestrel-9 blazed white at once, and the great dish, tilted toward nothing, became the loudest voice in human space. Somewhere beyond the frost-eaten walls, a signal older than Coral's grief went out at lightspeed in every direction: the genomic archive, the cultural record, the shipping manifests that proved the evacuation schedules were written years before the "crisis," the Combine's internal memoranda, the League's quiet signatures, and beneath all of it, clear as the day she'd recorded it, Ilsa Dunmore's voice saying *my name is Ilsa Dunmore and I am not dead and here is what I know* — and appended to the end, tagged and timestamped, one line Coral had added herself in the relay's final minutes:

*Witnessed and confirmed by Coral Dunmore, custodian of the Meridian Seed Vault, daughter of the author.*

Irrevocable. Untakeable. Already a light-year wide and widening.

Coral stood in the blazing dark with her empty hand open at her side and felt the strangest sensation of her life: not triumph, not terror, but *weight leaving*. Seven years of dust blowing off her all at once.

Then the deck bucked under her boots — not the relay's firing this time, but something else, something with engines and intent — and Juno's voice tore across the channel:

*Coral! Contacts, multiple, closing fast — Combine hull signatures, right on top of us — they're launching boarding craft!*
