## 35. Return to the Vault

The vault came into view on the ninth day out from Kestrel-9, and it was smaller than Coral remembered.

That was the first lie grief told her — that home would be waiting at its original scale, unchanged, patient. She had left seven years of herself in these corridors and expected them to still fit. Instead the *Meridian's Daughter* — Wren had named her, back when naming things felt like a joke and not a prayer — drifted into a slow approach vector, and the seed vault hung against the black like a knuckle of bone, scarred, dark along one whole flank where the exterior heat sinks had failed sometime during the months she'd been gone.

"Power's at thirty-one percent," Wren said from the nav station. Their voice was steady now, their arm healed enough to move without the brace, though they still favored it when they thought no one was watching. Coral always watched. "Life support's holding in the central ring only. The germination decks are cold."

"Cold how? Cold-cold, or dead-cold?"

Wren didn't answer right away, and the silence answered for them.

* * *

They docked through the maintenance collar because the main airlock hadn't cycled in four months and Coral didn't trust it not to seal around them like a mouth. The moment the inner hatch opened, the smell hit her — dry dust and old nutrient solution gone to salt, the faint green ghost of algae tanks running on minimum, and underneath it all something mineral and ancient, like the inside of a cave that had never known rain.

Home smelled like a tomb.

She stood in the access corridor with her helmet under her arm and let that truth settle into her chest. Behind her, Wren's boots rang on the deck plating. Somewhere deeper in the structure, a coolant pump stuttered, caught, stuttered again — the arrhythmic heartbeat of a body deciding whether to keep going.

"It's worse than the telemetry showed," Wren said quietly.

"The telemetry was me lying to myself from two hundred thousand klicks away." Coral keyed her light and walked. "Come on. Let's see what we buried."

The central ring was alive, barely. Emergency lighting threw amber cones across the corridor walls, and the hydroponic racks in Bay One were down to a handful of survival cultures — the hardy things, the weeds, wheat-kin and lichen strains and one stubborn line of terran moss that Ilsa Dunmore had catalogued in the vault's first year and labeled, in her precise hand, *OPPORTUNIST — KEEP*. The rest of the ring was dark. Frost furred the inside of the observation dome, thick as felt, and when Coral wiped a porthole clear with her sleeve, the stars outside looked smeared, underwater, like the sky seen from the bottom of a drowned world.

The germination decks were worse. She'd known they would be, but knowing is a thin coat against weather. Deck Two had lost atmosphere entirely; the breach had been slow and quiet, a hairline fracture in an old weld, and the vacuum had taken the seedlings in their cradles without violence. They'd desiccated. They stood in their racks like small brown ghosts, intact, weightless-dry, waiting for water that would have brought them back and never came.

Coral walked the rows. She didn't touch them. Her breath fogged inside her suit and she watched it bloom and fade, bloom and fade, counting the dead the way you count beads on a string — not to mourn each one individually, because there were eleven thousand of them, but to feel the full weight of the number pass through her hands.

Seven years she had kept this place. Seven years of cargo runs through contested orbits, of bribes and manifests and flying the gauntlet between the Combine and the League, all of it so that this — this cold room full of small brown ghosts — could keep existing. And the whole time, her mother had been out past dead space with the real archive, the true inheritance, the genomic library and the cultural record and the confession that had cracked the sky open across every channel in human space.

*I kept the shrine,* Coral thought. *You kept the fire.*

She should have felt robbed. She found, standing there in the frozen dark among her mother's careful catalogue, that she mostly felt relieved. Because if the vault had been the whole point — if these seeds had been everything — then its failing would have been the end of something. But it wasn't the point. It had never been the point. It was a door, and doors are allowed to wear out.

"Wren," she said. Her voice came out strange in the dead air, flattened by the comm. "How many of these can we still save?"

Wren drifted up beside her, light playing over the desiccated racks. They reached out — hesitated — and touched one of the seedling ghosts anyway, gently, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal. "Viability testing will tell us. Desiccated isn't necessarily dead. Some of these lines were engineered for exactly this. Cryo-desiccation tolerance." A pause. "Ilsa designed half the backup protocols herself. She built things to survive being forgotten."

Coral laughed — a short, cracked sound that surprised her more than anything else in the room had. "Of course she did."

* * *

They worked for six days before Coral made the decision, and she made it at three in the shipboard night, alone in the vault's old records office, surrounded by paper.

Actual paper. Ilsa had believed in redundancy the way other people believed in gods, and the office shelves held decades of handwritten logs, seed inventories, germination notes in ink that had faded from black to the color of weak tea. Coral had come in looking for the environmental schematics and instead found her mother's earliest journal, the one from before the vault, before the falsified death, before any of it — and there, on the third page, in handwriting younger and less certain than the hand Coral knew:

*If they ask what we're preserving, tell them the future. If they ask why, tell them the truth: because no one else will.*

Coral sat with that for a long time. The coolant pump stuttered somewhere below her, faithful and failing.

Then she got up, went to the central console, and pulled up the vault's access protocols — the ones she herself had written, seven years ago, in the white heat of grief and duty. *RESTRICTED ACCESS. CUSTODIAN AUTHORIZATION REQUIRED. ALL REQUESTS LOGGED AND REVIEWABLE BY CUSTODIAN ONLY.* She had built a lock around her mother's legacy and thrown away the key inside her own chest. She had told herself it was protection. Standing here now, with the broadcast still rippling outward through human space — the engineered abandonment, the Combine's crimes, Ilsa's voice reciting *forgive* into the static, and Coral Dunmore's name attached as witness — she could finally see the lock for what it had been.

A shrine. A way of keeping the dead hers.

She deleted the custodian clause.

Her fingers shook doing it. Not from fear — from the specific vertigo of setting down something heavy you've carried so long your spine has grown around it. The system asked for confirmation twice, politely, the way systems do when they think you've made a mistake, and Coral confirmed twice, and then the vault's manifest went open. Every strain. Every protocol. Every germination record, every failure log, every note in her mother's tea-colored ink, scanned and indexed and flagged for anyone, anywhere, to read.

"You're sure?" Wren stood in the office doorway. Coral hadn't heard them come in. They were holding two cups of recycled coffee, and they set one down beside her without being asked, which was its own kind of forgiveness, still ongoing, still fragile, both of them tending it daily like a difficult culture in a risky medium.

"No," Coral said. "But I'm done confusing guarding with keeping." She turned the console toward them. "Look. I'm opening the germination decks too. All of them. And I'm sending a general broadcast on the open bands — coordinates, status, invitation. Anyone who wants to help rebuild can come. Archivists, botanists, defectors, settlers, kids who've never seen a living plant. Anyone."

Wren looked at the draft message for a long moment. "The Combine will come."

"Some of them. The ones Calloway didn't burn out of his own chain of command, maybe." She thought of Sef Calloway — ex-Calloway now, a man who had traded a career for a data burst and called it the cheapest thing he'd ever bought. He was somewhere in the outer lanes with Juno Reyes, running supplies to the communities that had sprouted, improbably, in the wake of the broadcast, like volunteer seedlings after a fire. "And the League. And people with no flag at all. That's rather the point."

"And if someone comes to take it?"

"Then they'll find a vault full of copies." Coral tapped the console. "Everything here is already in the archive. Already on a thousand ships heading outward. There's nothing left to steal, Wren. That's what my mother understood and I didn't. You don't protect a living thing by hiding it. You protect it by making sure there's so much of it, so widely rooted, that no single act of destruction can ever reach all of it." She smiled, tired and unsteady. "Turns out she wasn't hiding the truth in dead space. She was planting it."

Wren picked up their coffee, drank, and grimaced at it out of habit. "The germination decks need atmospheric reseal on Two, new membrane filters throughout, and the nutrient synthesizers rebuilt from spares. That's months of work with just us."

"That's why I'm inviting everyone."

"You hate people in your workspace."

"I hate empty rooms more." Coral looked out through the frosted porthole at the smeared stars. "I spent seven years learning that the hard way."

* * *

The broadcast went out on the eighth day. Coral recorded it herself, no script, one take, sitting in the observation dome with the frost wiped from a hand-sized circle of glass so the stars showed through behind her. She talked about the vault. About the seeds. About the eleven thousand ghosts on Deck Two and the work of bringing them back. She talked about her mother without flinching, which was new, and about her own lies — the bargain with the League, signed with shaking hands in the dark — which was newer still, and cost more, and she paid it because she had learned on Kestrel-9 that honesty is the only currency that doesn't devalue.

Then she opened the channel and waited.

The first reply came within the hour: a research collective out of the Ceres drift, three archivists and a geneticist, asking about docking clearances. Then a settler family, cautious, half-sure it was a Combine trap. Then a message with no name attached, just a single line of text — *she would be proud of you* — from a relay node that Coral traced, later, to a ship registered nowhere, flying no colors, and she chose to believe it was exactly who it sounded like, and she chose not to chase it further. Some letters are answers. Some are just warmth across a cold distance, and both kinds matter.

By the end of the month there were nineteen people living aboard. By the end of the second month, forty-three. They came with calloused hands and strange accents and warrants outstanding in three jurisdictions, and they fell upon the vault's failing systems with the particular ferocity of people who had decided, individually and without coordination, that this place was worth their labor. Wren took logistics and discovered a talent for command that embarrassed them thoroughly; Coral caught them giving orders in her own cadence once and said nothing, because some inheritances are worth passing on deliberately.

Deck Two came back to life on a Tuesday.

Coral was there when the atmosphere finished cycling, when the temperature climbed past freezing, when the first water touched the first desiccated seedling rack — and nothing happened, for hours, and she almost couldn't bear to watch, and then near the end of shift a technician named Okafor shouted from the far row, and Coral crossed the deck fast enough to bang her hip on a rack frame and didn't care, because there, in tray 2-114, in a culture her mother had catalogued thirty years ago, a seed had split its coat and put out a rootlet, white and absurd and unmistakably, stubbornly alive.

It looked like nothing. It looked like a thread. Coral pressed her gloved finger to the edge of the tray, not touching it, and cried in front of forty-two people, and none of them looked away, and several of them cried too, and afterward nobody mentioned it, which was its own form of grace.

*OPPORTUNIST — KEEP*, the label read. Her mother had known. Her mother had always known which things would come back.

* * *

On the last day of the season, Coral walked the vault alone, top to bottom, the full inspection she used to do weekly when it was just her and the ghosts. The corridors were loud now — welding sparks in the east arm, laughter from the galley, someone arguing cheerfully about nitrogen ratios — and she let the noise wash over her and did not flinch from it.

In the records office, she added a final entry to her mother's journal, in her own hand, beneath the young uncertain ink of a woman thirty years dead:

*We kept it. We opened it. It's growing.*

She closed the book and was turning to leave when Wren appeared in the doorway again — they had a gift for that, arriving at thresholds — holding a tablet with an expression Coral had learned to dread, the expression of someone carrying news that is neither good nor bad but simply *large*.

"Priority transmission," Wren said. "Joint tribunal, Earth-side authority, convened under the new accords." They held out the tablet. "They're requesting testimony. From you. On the record. About the abandonment, the Combine, the Grace — and about your own conduct. The bargain with the League. All of it."

Coral took the tablet. The summons glowed on the screen, formal and cold, and beneath the legalese a single line in plain language: *Witness Dunmore's testimony may determine whether reparations proceed or collapse.*

"They want the whole story," Wren said. "Including the parts that make you look bad."

Coral looked at the summons for a long moment. Then out the frost-free circle of glass, where the stars burned sharp and clean, and beyond them, on a thousand outbound ships, the archive traveled onward, rooted everywhere, owned by no one.

"Good," she said. "Because I'm done telling the parts that make me look good."

She handed the tablet back and reached for her coat.
