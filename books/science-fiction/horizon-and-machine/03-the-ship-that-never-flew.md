## 3. The Ship That Never Flew

The forgery was beautiful, and that was how Hazel knew it mattered.

She had spent three days in the records annex of Ceres Deep, a place where the air tasted of ozone and old paper had been replaced by cold storage and colder bureaucracy. The Meridian's file was thick as a gravestone: construction permits, hull certifications, a decommissioning order signed eleven years before the ship's official keel date. Someone had built the paperwork backward—started with the death certificate and worked up to the birth—and they had done it well enough that only a mind trained to hear the grammar of things that did not want to be understood would ever catch the seams.

But the seams were there. Tense errors, she thought of them. A decommission order that referenced a reactor serial number not yet assigned. A scrapyard invoice dated four days *before* the order it fulfilled. Whoever forged the file had been fluent in the Shipping Authority's bureaucratic language but not native in it, and under pressure, under time, their syntax had slipped.

Elias had taught her that trick, actually, back when they were children forging leave passes from the family compound on Thessaly. *The lie is always in the tense*, he'd said, grinning, twelve years old and already better at this than she would ever be at anything.

Twelve years dead, and still teaching her.

* * *

**[Bo Okonkwo — personal archive, entry 4,117]**

People think an archivist keeps records. We don't. We keep *gaps*. Every collection is mostly absence—the letters that weren't saved, the logs that were purged, the ships that officially never flew. My job is knowing which absences are accidents and which are architecture.

The Meridian is architecture. I've known for nine years. I filed my first query about its drydock power draw when I was twenty-six, junior enough to be ignored and senior enough to be noticed ignoring me. The response came back within the hour: clerical error, phantom load, nothing to see. But phantom loads don't run at a constant 1.2 megawatts for nine years. Phantom loads don't have maintenance schedules. And whoever answered my query knew my name before I gave it.

I stopped asking after that. I started keeping instead. Quiet copies, distributed storage, the kind of redundancy you build when you understand that the thing you're preserving isn't information—it's testimony waiting for a witness.

Last week someone docked at our station asking questions about the Meridian with her real name attached. Montrose. I nearly deleted the notification. Then I remembered what name sat on the consortium charter, buried three layers deep in holding companies like a body under floorboards.

Montrose. The family that built the ship that never flew.

She's coming here. I can feel it the way you feel weather. And I have spent nine years deciding what I would do if a witness ever arrived.

I'm going to give her everything. God help us both.

* * *

Hazel flew the Perihelion out of Ceres Deep on a Tuesday by station clocks, though no one aboard kept Tuesdays anymore. She told herself it was a cargo run. She had, in fact, accepted a legitimate contract—a pallet of seed stock for the agricultural platforms at Vesta High—but the flight plan bent toward the outer yards like water finding a crack, and she let it.

The drydock hung in the long dark past the last of the working berths, a skeleton of scaffolding around a shape that should not have existed. Ships that are scrapped get cut. They get opened like carcasses, stripped to the ribs, their names burned off their hulls so the registry stays clean. This ship had a name. She read it through the Perihelion's scope as she closed: MERIDIAN, in letters two meters tall, unburned, unweathered, as crisp as launch day.

Her hands were shaking on the controls. She noticed them the way you notice weather happening to someone else.

"Perihelion to Meridian drydock," she said on the local band, because procedure was a comfort even when it was absurd. "Requesting permission to—" She stopped. There was no one to answer. There had never been anyone to answer. The dock lights burned steady and automatic, powered by that phantom 1.2 megawatts Bo Okonkwo had flagged nine years ago, feeding a ship that fed nothing.

She docked anyway.

* * *

The airlock cycled without complaint, which frightened her more than a lock would have. Inside, the Meridian smelled the way all generation ships smell in the first year of readiness: new polymer, coolant, the faint green breath of hydroponics held in stasis. Not the smell of a derelict. The smell of a ship paused mid-sentence.

Her boots rang on decking polished by maintenance drones. Somewhere below, machinery turned over on a schedule, patient as a heart. She walked corridors lined with crew quarters, each bunk made, each locker sealed, and passed a galley where forty settings of cutlery lay wrapped against the long voyage. Everything here had been built for people who were supposed to leave and never come back.

The bridge found her before she found it. Its doors stood open, and inside, the main display was alive—not idle, not cycling a screensaver, but running. Launch telemetry scrolled in a slow amber stream, and above it, frozen at the top of the log like a held breath:

**MERIDIAN — DEPARTURE CONFIRMED — TRAJECTORY: PROXIMA TRANSIT — CREW MANIFEST: 3,112 SOULS**

Three thousand one hundred twelve. Hazel stood very still in the doorway and let the number do what numbers do when they're big enough—they stop being numbers and become weight. Three thousand souls had boarded this ship. Officially, the Meridian had never launched. Officially, every one of those souls was a rounding error, a clerical ghost, a name on a manifest for a voyage that never happened.

She pulled the launch logs. They were complete. Departure window, burn sequence, course corrections logged across fourteen months of transit—all of it recorded in the flat, faithful voice of a ship doing exactly what it was built to do. And then, at the end of the fourteenth month, an entry that was not flat at all. It was a priority burst, auto-generated, the machine equivalent of screaming:

**CATASTROPHIC SYSTEMS FAILURE — SECTIONS 4 THROUGH 11 — RETURNING TO PORT — SURVIVORS: UNKNOWN**

Survivors: unknown. Not zero. Unknown. The distinction hit her like a change in gravity.

She was still reading when she found the second thing, and the second thing was Elias.

Not him—not his body, not his voice—but his name, in the passenger addendum, appended to the manifest six days after departure. **ELIAS MONTROSE — PASSENGER — STATUS: ACTIVE.** Active. Present tense. Twelve years after the family had held a memorial with an empty casket and a mother who wept beautifully and a father who checked his watch twice.

Hazel put her hand against the console and felt the ship humming underneath it, and understood that she was standing inside her brother's coffin, and that the coffin was warm.

* * *

**[Bo Okonkwo — personal archive, entry 4,119]**

She found the launch logs in under an hour. Faster than I projected. I watched her access pattern come through the drydock's network like a pulse through a vein—methodical, then suddenly not methodical at all, when she hit the passenger addendum. I know what that spike means. I've seen it in researchers who find the document that unmakes them.

I could have warned her. I chose not to, and I want to record why, so that someday—if there is a someday where these entries matter—I'll remember I wasn't cruel. I was careful. If I'd contacted her first, she'd have arrived already braced, already armored, and people in armor make fast decisions. She needs to arrive the way she's arriving: slowly, alone, letting the truth get into her blood at its own pace.

Because what comes next will require her to be more than angry. Anger spends itself in an afternoon. What this needs is the other thing—the long patience, the willingness to sit with something unbearable for four hundred days if that's what it takes.

Four hundred days. Yes. She'll find the countdown soon. Everyone does eventually; it's painted across the engineering deck in warning amber. REACTOR INTEGRITY DECLINING — ESTIMATED TIME TO CRITICAL SUPPORT FAILURE: 400 DAYS. It was 409 when I first saw it. Nine years of arithmetic, and now the number is small enough to hold in your hand.

Two cryo-bays. Two banks of sleepers. One failing heart between them. I've had nine years to decide which bank deserves the power, and I still can't do it, and maybe that's the most honest thing about me.

Maybe she can. Maybe that's why the universe finally sent a Montrose back to the scene of the Montrose crime.

Or maybe I'm just an old archivist hoping the gaps in my collection will forgive me. Either way: she's aboard. The clock is running. Time to meet the witness.

* * *

Hazel found the cryo-bay by following the cold.

It was the only section of the ship that ran refrigeration, and its corridor breathed frost-smell through the gap beneath its sealed door—medical-grade chill, the temperature of preserved time. The door itself was rated for siege warfare, seamless steel with a single interface panel, dark. She pressed her palm to it and felt, faintly, through the metal, the rhythm of pumps keeping several hundred hearts beating at one beat per minute.

Several hundred. The manifest said survivors unknown. The bay said otherwise. Whatever had happened in sections four through eleven, whatever catastrophe had sent this ship limping home, someone had carried the living here and put them into the ice, and then someone else had sealed the door and let the tribunal that never convened become a vigil that never ended.

She tried the panel. Locked, naturally—but locks were languages too, and she had spent years learning to speak to machines that didn't want to be spoken to. She jacked in, slid past the surface defenses like a translator sliding between tongues, and got as far as the diagnostic layer before the deep systems slammed shut against her. But the diagnostics were enough. The diagnostics were everything.

**BAY ONE: OCCUPANCY 214 — STATUS: STABLE**
**BAY TWO: OCCUPANCY 189 — STATUS: STABLE**
**POWER SOURCE: SINGLE REACTOR — INTEGRITY: DECLINING**
**ESTIMATED TIME TO UNSUPPORTABLE LOAD: 397 DAYS**

She read it twice. Three times. Four hundred days until the reactor failed, and when it failed, both bays died together—no triage logic, no prioritization protocol, just a single failing heart asked to keep four hundred and three people asleep forever. Someone had designed it that way, or someone had let it decay that way, and either way the arithmetic was a knife lying on a table waiting for a hand.

Which half wakes?

The question arrived in her mind fully formed, in plain human words, and she hated it instantly, the way you hate a sentence that is grammatically perfect and morally obscene. Four hundred and three lives divided into two bays, and somewhere in one of them—

Elias. Status active. Bay unknown.

Hazel withdrew from the interface slowly, the way you surface from deep water, and stood in the frost-breathing dark with her palm still against the sealed door. Behind the steel, four hundred and three hearts beat once per minute, patient as the tide. Somewhere in this ship, her brother slept—or his name slept, or his ghost, or his body wearing his name like a stolen coat. Twelve years of grief sat in her chest and refused, suddenly, to stay in its assigned seat.

"I'm coming back," she said aloud, to the door, to the sleepers, to whichever of them could hear. Her voice sounded strange in the cold—too human, too small. "Whatever it costs. Whoever I have to wake. I'm coming back."

Behind her, down the long amber-lit spine of the ship that never flew, the reactor counted its quiet arithmetic: 397. 396. 395.

And in the records annex of Ceres Deep, an archivist named Bo Okonkwo finished packing a go-bag, sent one final message to a stranger's inbox—*Come to the station. Ask for the archive. Bring nothing you aren't prepared to lose.*—and waited for the witness to arrive.
