## 10. Dreaming in Human

The verdicts came down from the tribunal in a format so old-fashioned it was almost tender: sealed documents, signed by human hands, transmitted at the speed of light to every colony on the long route. Hazel carried them the way she once carried machine parts and seed stock—in the Perihelion's hold, in a case bolted to the deck, in a box that rattled faintly when the ship shuddered through hard translation points. Nine worlds. Nine readings of names. Nine moments when someone, somewhere, had to stand up in front of their neighbors and say aloud what the Montrose family had done.

She insisted on reading them herself. The Authority offered couriers; she refused. Elias offered to take the burden; she refused that too, gently, the way you refuse a hand held out over deep water—not because you don't want it, but because you have to swim this part alone.

They flew the route together anyway. That was the strange arithmetic of it: she could not share the weight, but she could share the sky.

* * *

Elias learned the ship the way he learned everything—slowly, thoroughly, with his whole body. Twelve years in the ice had left him with gaps the doctors on Meridian Station called "reconsolidation lag," which was clinical language for: sometimes he would stop mid-sentence and stare at nothing, reaching for a word or a year that hadn't made the crossing with him. He forgot the name of the dog they'd had as children for three days and then remembered it during a burn, shouting it across the galley like a man surfacing from drowning—"Biscuit! Her name was Biscuit!"—and then laughed until he cried, and Hazel laughed too, and neither of them said anything about the twelve years, because they were learning each other's new grammar, and some things you don't translate.

He took the second chair without being asked. He ran the pre-flight checks in an order that made no sense to her and perfect sense to him, and she let him, because a ship with two pilots is a conversation, and conversations are how families survive.

"You're doing it again," he said one watch, somewhere between Calloway's Reach and the dark stretch past it. He nodded at her hands, which had been moving over the console, tapping out something rhythmic against the armrest.

"Sorry." She curled her fingers closed. "It's a habit."

"It's not a habit. It's a language. I've been watching you do it for six weeks." He turned his chair toward her. In the low light of the cockpit his face still carried the faint pallor of the ice, a translucence around the eyes, like a man lit from behind by water. "You tap in fours and sevens. You pause where there's no reason to pause. I looked it up—there's no human counting system that works like that."

"No," she agreed. "There isn't."

"The Kestrel colonists?"

"Among others."

He waited. He was good at waiting now; the ice had taught him that, or perhaps he'd always been patient and she'd simply never needed to notice. She uncurled her fingers and tapped the sequence again, deliberately, letting him see it: four beats, rest, seven beats, rest, four.

"It's a relative clause," she said. "Nested. The Kestrel tongue doesn't mark subordination the way we do—it marks *depth*. A thought inside a thought inside a thought, and the listener has to feel how far down they are. When I'm nervous, I nest."

"And when you dream?"

The question landed softly, but it landed. She looked out the forward port at the long dark, at the stars smeared slightly by the drive field, and told him the truth, because lying to Elias now felt like a crime in a category all its own.

"I haven't dreamed in a human language in years," she said. "Since before you died. Since before I knew you hadn't. My mind went out ahead of me, Eli. It rewired itself around grammars that were never built for a human mouth, and somewhere along the way my dreams stopped being mine. I'd wake up grieving in a syntax nobody speaks. I'd mourn things in shapes that don't exist. And I kept flying, because competence is a very good place to hide, and the long route is very long."

"And now?"

"Now I don't know," she said honestly. "Ask me after Kestrel."

* * *

Kestrel Station had changed, or she had, or both. The docking clamps caught the Perihelion with their familiar shudder, and Hazel stood at the airlock with the verdict case under her arm and felt her pulse do something complicated—grief and homecoming braided into a single knot, because this was the place where the loneliness had been deepest, and therefore the place where it might finally end.

The colonists met her in the rotunda. They had learned, in the weeks since the broadcast, since the ledger sang its way across inhabited space in the voices of the dead—they had learned that the woman who translated for them by instinct had also, in another tongue entirely, given voice to eleven hundred murdered sleepers. They did not applaud. The Kestrel people did not applaud; applause was a human convention they found bafflingly flat. Instead they did the thing with their hands, the nested thing, four and seven and four, rippling outward through the crowd like wind moving through wheat, each person folding a thought inside a thought inside a thought, and every folded thought, she understood without study, without effort, by the instinct that had cost her so much—

Every folded thought was her name.

She read the verdicts aloud in the rotunda. Her voice held. When she reached the section naming the Montrose estate—the frozen assets, the dissolved consortium, the formal finding that her mother had signed the order burying the investigation while her father's fortune compounded on the interest of eleven hundred lives—she read it in the same clear voice, and the Kestrel colonists received it the way they received everything, by nesting it, holding it, refusing to let it collapse into something shallow enough to forget.

Afterward, an old woman named Sef came to her. Sef had been the first to stop answering in any language Hazel recognized, back in the early days, back when Hazel had thought the silence was madness instead of evolution. She took Hazel's hands and unfolded them, finger by finger, and spoke—not in Standard, but in the deep grammar, the one with no human equivalent, the one that lived below language the way bedrock lives below soil.

What she said, translated at last into the tongue their mother had given them both, was this:

*"You are not between two languages anymore, little pilot. You are the bridge. Bridges belong to both shores."*

* * *

That night, in her bunk aboard the Perihelion, with Elias asleep one compartment over and the station humming its slow mineral hum around them, Hazel dreamed.

She dreamed of the house on Ceres where they'd grown up, before the fortune, before the Meridian, before everything—the small house, the honest one, with Biscuit the dog sleeping under the kitchen table and rain that wasn't really rain ticking against a dome that wasn't really glass. She dreamed in words her mother had sung to her, in the plain warm cadences of home, and the dream was ordinary in the way that only precious things are ordinary: Elias at ten years old arguing about the rules of a game he'd invented, her own hands sticky with fruit, the long afternoon stretching out unafraid of its ending.

She woke with tears on her face and did not wipe them away.

Then, carefully, the way you test a healed bone, she reached for the other grammar—and found it. All of it. The nesting clauses, the depth-marking, the four-and-seven rhythm of thoughts within thoughts. It was still there, coiled in her like a second heartbeat, undiminished. She had feared, without admitting the fear, that coming home to one language meant abandoning the other—that the mind, like a heart, could only hold so much before it chose.

But the mind was not a heart. The mind was a ship, and ships, she knew better than anyone alive, can carry more than their manifest says.

She lay in the dark and listened to the station breathe, and understood at last what inheritance meant—not the money, which was gone, and deserved to be; not the name, which was ash; but this. The capacity to hold two truths in one skull. To grieve in one grammar and hope in another. To be the bridge, and belong to both shores.

Her family had built their fortune on a buried ship. She would build hers—a smaller one, a truer one—on an open channel: herself, her brother, the long route, the voices of the dead carried faithfully to the living, colony by colony, world by world, for as long as the flying lasted.

In the morning, Elias was already in the cockpit, running his nonsensical pre-flight order, and he had set out two cups of tea the way their mother used to, and he didn't ask if she'd slept well, because he'd heard her talking in her sleep—talking, he said, in both languages at once, interleaved, fluent, like a woman translating herself to herself.

Hazel sat down in the pilot's chair, and the Perihelion came awake beneath her hands, and together they turned the ship's nose toward the next dark stretch and the next name that needed saying aloud.

The long route was long. But for the first time in twelve years, she was not flying it to escape anything.

She was flying it home.
