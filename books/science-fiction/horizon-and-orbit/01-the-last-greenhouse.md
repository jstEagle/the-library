## 1. The Last Greenhouse

The morning began, as every morning had for eleven years, with the sound of Rosalind Dunmore's own footsteps.

They echoed in the vault's main corridor the way they always did — a soft double-tap of boots on composite flooring, swallowed almost immediately by the long dark throat of the tunnel ahead. She had stopped noticing the echo years ago. Or rather, she had noticed it so completely that it had become indistinguishable from silence itself, the way a person who lives beside a waterfall stops hearing water and starts hearing only the absence beneath it.

She carried her tablet in one hand and a thermos of barley tea in the other, and she walked the corridor at the pace her grandfather had called "the caretaker's walk" — not hurrying, because nothing down here was dying fast enough to require hurry, and not dawdling, because dawdling was how you missed the small failures that became large ones. Tray seven's humidity seal. Pump junction four's bearing noise. The faint sweetish smell near Bay Twelve that meant something organic was composting where it shouldn't be.

Four hundred meters of corridor. Then the greenhouse.

* * *

The vault had been built to be forgotten by everyone except its keeper. That was the design philosophy, anyway — her grandfather Ivo used to say it with a kind of grim pride, tapping his pipe against the doorframe of the control room: *A vault is a promise made to people who will never thank you.* Three kilometers of tunneled rock beneath two hundred meters of permafrost, shielded from the weather above, from the wars above, from the indifference above. Inside: one hundred and twelve thousand seed accessions. Wheat from the Ukrainian steppes before the salt came. Rice from terraces that were now shallow graves of mud. Sorghum, amaranth, quinoa, teff. Orchard stock sealed in wax and nitrogen. The wild cousins of everything edible, gathered from places that no longer existed as places — drowned, burned, poisoned, or simply abandoned when the colony charters went out and half of Earth's remaining farmers decided the sky was more promising than the soil.

Rosalind had been twenty-six when she signed the caretaker's covenant. She remembered the ceremony absurdly well: a room with six other people in it, a document on real paper because paper outlasted formats, and her grandfather's hand heavy on her shoulder while the Consortium liaison read the terms aloud in a voice like a man reciting a warranty. Ten years minimum. Full autonomy. Quarterly maintenance logs transmitted to the colonial network via the deep relay. No dependents permitted on-site. No visitors without ninety days' notice.

*No visitors,* she had thought at the time, *because there won't be anyone who wants to visit.*

She had been right about that. She had been wrong about nearly everything else.

* * *

The greenhouse occupied what had once been a natural cavern, before the builders had squared it off and roofed it with diffuser panels that mimicked, imperfectly, the light of a morning in some temperate latitude that probably didn't exist anymore either. It was the largest single space in the vault and the only one Rosalind thought of as beautiful, though she would never have said so aloud, even if there had been anyone to say it to.

Rows of growing trays ran the length of the cavern under the false dawn. Most held germination tests — the quiet, repetitive heart of her work. Every accession in storage had to be tested on a rolling schedule, a sample drawn, sown, coaxed into sprouting, measured, recorded, and composted. A seed bank wasn't a museum. Seeds were alive, barely, stubbornly, and they died in their sleep if you let them. The whole point of the vault was that somewhere in the future — a future nobody could describe anymore without embarrassment or grief — someone would open these drawers and need the wheat to grow. So Rosalind grew it, over and over, alone, in trays that would never feed anyone, and logged the results for an audience of zero.

Today's list was short: nine accessions. She started at the north end, where the oldest test batch lived.

The durum wheat had come up overnight — forty-one of fifty seeds, pale threads bent into little question marks against the growing medium. She crouched, counted twice, entered the number. *Triticum durum, acc. 04-1187. Germination 82%. Vigor: moderate. Notes:* and here she paused, stylus hovering, because the notes field was where eleven years of loneliness had quietly gone to die. Early on she had written real observations. Then she had written fuller ones, sentences instead of fragments, as though the log might someday be read by a person rather than parsed by a database. Then, somewhere around year five, she had begun writing things that were not observations at all.

*Notes: strong emergence despite medium running 1.2 degrees cool overnight. My fault — forgot to recalibrate the north loop after the pump work. The seeds forgave me. Most things down here forgive me.*

She stared at the line for a moment. She did not delete it. She never deleted them. Somewhere in the vault's archive there were now thousands of entries like this, a diary disguised as a dataset, addressed to whoever — *whatever* — eventually read it. Her grandfather would have been horrified. Or he would have understood perfectly, which amounted to the same thing.

She moved down the row. Amaranth, pushing up red-stemmed and eager, 94 percent, vigor high, *notes: always the optimist of the bunch; you'd never know its wild relatives watched their whole continent burn.* Quinoa, slow, sulky, 61 percent, *notes: give it time; the good ones are like this.* A landrace barley from a valley whose name she had looked up once on the old maps and found replaced by a reservoir. Teff, fine as grass seed, requiring her to lean close and squint, breath fogging the tray's cover.

This was the part of the day she loved, and she hated herself a little for loving it, because loving it felt like complicity. Every seedling that broke the surface of its tray was a small argument that the world still deserved tending. And every seedling was also a lie of sorts — grown in a drawer, measured, recorded, and then tipped into the compost bin, because these tests existed only to prove viability, not to produce food. Eleven years of harvests that fed no one. Eleven years of green things raised and killed with tenderness, in a tomb, for a future that had stopped answering its mail.

She straightened up, pressed the heel of her hand briefly against her sternum — an old habit, a gesture at some pressure behind the breastbone that was not quite pain and not quite anything else — and moved to the next tray.

* * *

At mid-morning she did the rounds of the climate systems, because devotion, she had learned, was mostly plumbing.

The vault breathed through heat exchangers older than she was, and it lived on a reactor buried deeper than anything else — a compact fission plant her grandfather's generation had installed with the smug confidence of engineers who assumed their grandchildren would maintain what they built. The reactor hummed at all times, a sound below sound, felt in the teeth more than heard. Rosalind had lived with it so long that on the rare occasions it cycled down for maintenance she couldn't sleep; the silence of it was worse than any noise, the way a house is worse than empty when you can no longer hear the refrigerator.

She checked coolant loop pressures, logged them, checked them again because the north gauge had a stutter in it she hadn't yet fixed and didn't entirely trust. She greased a fan bearing in Gallery Six. She found a hairline crack in a condensate line and sealed it with epoxy, working slowly, enjoying the small competence of her own hands. This was the secret she had discovered about solitude, the one nobody warned you about: it did not make you simpler. It made you enormous. With no one else to be, she had become the vault's hands, eyes, memory, and conscience simultaneously, and there were days when the sheer size of the job felt less like a burden than like a strange, terrible honor.

Other days it just felt like being buried alive with excellent ventilation.

* * *

Lunch was reconstituted lentils and flatbread baked in the galley oven, eaten standing at the counter because sitting at the table — the table set for one, the second chair pushed against the wall since year three — had become unbearable sometime around year eight, and she had stopped pretending otherwise.

While she ate she ran through the transmission queue, which was the other half of her covenant. Quarterly logs, plus weekly status pings, transmitted up through the deep relay to the colonial network: forty-one settlements scattered across the system, from the lunar agri-domes to the outer habitats, each one theoretically a sibling institution, each one theoretically listening.

The queue display showed the same thing it had shown for years. **TRANSMITTED: 612 MESSAGES. RESPONSES RECEIVED: 0.**

She looked at the zeros the way you look at a scar — knowing exactly how it got there, unable to stop checking that it was still there.

It hadn't always been zeros. For the first fourteen months there had been replies. Routine acknowledgments from the Luna network, a chatty quarterly digest from Ceres, once — she still kept it, flagged and backed up in triplicate — a personal note from a seed technician at the Ganymede annex named Okafor, who wrote that he'd tested her durum samples against his own and hers had won, *no contest, tell your grandfather his lines are still the best in the system.* She had written back immediately, delighted, and told him about the vault, the trays, the smell of the greenhouse at false-dawn. His reply had taken four months to arrive and was shorter. Then the intervals stretched. Then, on a day she could reconstruct minute by minute whenever she hated herself enough to try, the acknowledgments simply stopped.

No failure notice. No final message. Just — nothing, stretching out month after month, until the absence itself became the answer. She had filed formal queries through every channel the vault possessed. She had bounced signals off relay after relay, spending power she couldn't spare. She had composed, in the third year, a message that abandoned all protocol and said plainly: *Is anyone there? Please. Anyone.* She had never sent it. It sat in the drafts folder like a stone she swallowed every time she opened the queue.

The official explanation, when she finally extracted one from a Consortium administrative bulletin two years late, was *network restructuring pending colonial consolidation.* Which meant nothing, and was clearly designed to mean nothing, and she had repeated it to herself for years anyway because the alternative explanations were all worse.

She finished her tea. She closed the queue. She went back to work, because the seeds didn't care whether anyone was listening, and that was precisely why she loved them.

* * *

The afternoon belonged to the cold rooms.

Accession 22-0093 needed its ten-year draw — a viability sample from one of the oldest orchard stocks, apple scions sealed in wax, sleeping at minus eighteen in Drawer Hall C. She suited up partially, pulled the drawer, and stood for a moment in the breath-fogged cold with the small labeled packet in her gloved hands.

Her grandmother had collected this one. Rosalind knew the story the way she knew all the stories, from repetition, from the way family lore gets worn smooth like river stones: Elena Dunmore had hiked into a fire-scorched valley in the last year of the collection expeditions, against advice, and cut scions from the one surviving apple tree on a dead hillside, and carried them out wrapped in wet cloth inside her jacket, and had refused to talk about the rest of what she saw in that valley for the rest of her life. The accession label was in Elena's handwriting — actual ink, actual hand — and Rosalind had touched the label more times than she could count, as though handwriting were a form of resurrection.

*I'm going to grow you,* she told the packet silently, the way she told all of them. *Not today. But someday somebody will, and you'll remember how.*

She drew twelve scions, logged the withdrawal, resealed the drawer, and stood a moment longer in the cold room's blue dark, surrounded on all sides by the sleeping dead-and-living: a hundred and twelve thousand small promises, packed in foil and nitrogen, waiting out the end of the world in the world's most patient cellar.

Her family had built this place. That fact sat differently depending on the day. Some days it was inheritance, warm as bread. Other days it was abandonment dressed up as duty — because her grandfather had died in the control room chair eight months into her tenure, and her parents had left Earth for the colonies when she was nineteen, taking her brother with them, bound for the Tau Ceti charter ships and a new life among the stars, and their messages home had thinned on exactly the same curve as the network's, and then stopped on exactly the same schedule, and she had never learned whether the two silences were the same silence or merely neighbors.

Eleven years. She had been left the keys to the ark and then the ark's crew had walked off into the flood, and she had stayed, and stayed, and stayed, telling herself it was devotion, and most days believing it.

* * *

Evening in the greenhouse was her private liturgy.

The diffuser panels dimmed toward false sunset, amber bleeding down to ember, and Rosalind walked the rows one last time with a watering can she didn't strictly need — the misting system was automated and superior — because watering things by hand was a prayer her body understood even when her mind objected. She topped up the trays. She pinched a dead leaf from the amaranth. She checked the basil cutting she was rooting in a jar on the potting bench, the vault's only ornamental, its only luxury, its only thing grown for beauty rather than proof, and she touched its leaves and smelled her fingers afterward, green and sharp and alive.

Then she climbed to the observation ledge at the cavern's upper rim, thermos refilled, and sat in the old chair with the frayed armrests, and looked out over the green rows glowing under the last of the false light.

This was the hour when the loneliness came for her. Not the daytime loneliness, which was manageable, practical, a scheduling problem. The other kind. The kind that arrived with the dusk and asked its one question, the question she had been refusing to answer for eleven years:

*For whom?*

For whom the trays, the logs, the quarterly transmissions into a dead network. For whom the scions and the soil and the careful, faithful, unwitnessed labor. For whom this entire cathedral of patience, if the future it was built for had already quietly ended somewhere beyond the ice, beyond the relay, beyond the reach of any signal she could send?

She drank her tea. Below her, the seedlings stood in their trays, doing the only thing they knew how to do, which was to keep growing as though someone were coming.

And that, she supposed, was the answer, or the closest thing to one she had: she kept faith the way they did. Not because of evidence. Because stopping was unthinkable.

She was halfway through logging the evening readings when the console chimed.

Not the maintenance alert tone. Not the reactor's low warning purr. Something else — a sound she had heard only in drills, a priority-band handshake, the array announcing an incoming signal on a channel that had been silent for eleven years.

Rosalind set down her tea very carefully, the way you set down something when your hands are not steady, and turned to the screen.

The header resolved first: incoming carrier, nonstandard frequency, signal strength marginal but real. Then the language-identification field, which cycled, and failed, and cycled again, and finally returned a classification that made her sit forward hard enough to scrape the chair against the rock.

**UNKNOWN / LEGACY PROTOCOL DETECTED. PARTIAL MATCH: PRE-COLLAPSE STANDARD (ARCHAIC). CONFIDENCE: LOW. DECODING RECOMMENDED.**

Pre-Collapse Standard. A dead language. Older than her grandfather, older than the vault, a tongue that had been formally retired two centuries ago and survived now only in archives and in the heads of a handful of historians and eccentric archivists — and in hers, because Ivo Dunmore had insisted his granddaughter learn it, calling it *the language the vault was dreamed in.*

Someone was transmitting in it. Now. Tonight.

Rosalind stared at the waveform crawling across the screen, weak and broken and unmistakably, impossibly human, and felt eleven years of silence begin, very slightly, to crack.
