## 2. Silence on All Bands

The communications room sat at the heart of the vault like a chapel at the center of a cathedral — the one chamber built not for the preservation of life, but for the preservation of connection. Rosalind had always loved the irony of it. Everything else in the vault was sealed, insulated, wrapped against time. This room was built with its mouth open.

She settled into the operator's chair, its upholstery long since flattened into the shape of her, and woke the console with a touch. The screens bloomed to life in their familiar amber, and there it was, as it was every morning, the number she had come to think of as her true age, more honest than the one on her birth records:

**4,017 transmissions sent. 0 received.**

Eleven years, four months, nine days. She had stopped counting in weeks around year three, stopped counting in months around year six, and now she counted only in the accumulated mass of her own voice, sent out into the dark like seeds scattered on frozen ground.

"Good morning, network," she said, because she always said it, because the ritual mattered even if the ritual was insane. "This is Rosalind Dunmore, caretaker of the Meridian Seed Vault, transmitting on all standard bands and legacy frequencies. Day four thousand and seventeen. Germination report to follow. Weather report to follow that. If anyone is receiving —"

She paused, as she always paused, in the small silence where a reply would live if a reply were ever going to come.

"— if anyone is receiving, the wheat from bin forty-one came up overnight. All three hundred specimens. You should see it. It's the color of a struck match."

She keyed the transmit stud and felt, as she always felt, the faint tremor through the floor as the vault's antenna array — a great iron latticework older than her grandfather — threw her voice up through the atmosphere and out into the black. Somewhere beyond the ionosphere, past the moon's dead orbit, past the cordon of debris the old charts called the Shatterline, there were supposed to be colonies. Forty-one of them. Kepler Reach, Halcyon Deep, the terraced farms of New Verdance, the orbital nurseries of Saint Anselm. Places where human beings had carried the children of Earth's seeds and planted them in alien soil, and where, presumably, human beings had gone on living, arguing, planting, dying, being born.

Presumably.

The console logged the transmission with a soft chime and added it to the archive, where it joined its four thousand and sixteen siblings: a library of one voice, addressed to no one.

Rosalind leaned back and closed her eyes. She knew what she would find if she opened the historical logs — she had opened them a thousand times — but knowing was not the same as looking, and looking was a discipline she performed the way she performed the germination tests: regularly, carefully, and with her heart braced behind several layers of procedure.

She opened them.

* * *

The screen filled with the transmission record, and the record told its story in the only way records know how — through absence.

The first years of the archive, before her time, were dense with traffic. She scrolled through the headers like a woman walking through a crowded market: daily syncs between the vault and the colonial agricultural consortiums, seed-genome updates, harvest reports, weather negotiations, the chatter of forty-one settlements and one mother vault all talking over and under and through each other in a constant, living hum. Her grandfather's voice was in some of the earliest ones — she could tell without playing them, just from the transmission lengths, because Elias Dunmore had never once in his life said briefly what could be said at length.

Then the density began to thin. She could see it in the data the way you could see a fever in a chart: Year one of her tenure, traffic down forty percent. Year two, down seventy. The colonies had begun to miss their check-ins, first one and then another, the way a row of lamps goes out — not all at once, but one, and then one more, and then a darkness with a pattern to it.

And then — she scrolled to it, because she always scrolled to it, because she had never once in eleven years been able to scroll past it —

**Day 0.**

* * *

It had been spring, or what passed for spring in the vault's buried calendar, when the replies stopped. She remembered it with the terrible fidelity of the things that break us: the exact quality of the light through the greenhouse glass, the smell of the nutrient solution, the fact that she had been eating an apple.

An apple. She remembered the apple. She had grown it herself, from a scion her grandfather had grafted before the Silence — the last of the Meridian Pippins — and it had been small and lopsided and the best thing she had ever tasted, and she had been sitting in the comms room with it, planning to describe it in that evening's transmission, when the console chimed.

Not the send-chime. The receive-chime. She had actually laughed, a startled, undignified bark of a laugh, because the receive-chime was so rare by then that she'd half forgotten the console could make it.

Incoming traffic: Halcyon Deep. Priority flag. Voice.

She had set down the apple. She remembered that. She remembered setting it down with exaggerated care, the way you set down something fragile when your hands have started to shake without your permission.

The transmission was from a woman named Odile Ferro, the agricultural director at Halcyon, a voice Rosalind had known her whole life through yearly reports — warm, clipped, faintly amused by her own formality. But this was not a report. The audio was ragged, compressed, re-sent three times over, as if the sender had been fighting for every kilobyte.

*"— repeating for anyone in range, this is Halcyon Deep, this is Odile Ferro, we are receiving an order of — I don't — they're telling us to power down our long-range array, they're telling us it's a quarantine protocol, but there's no quarantine, there's nothing — Rosalind, if this reaches you, if anyone at Meridian hears this — they've cut Kepler already, Kepler's gone dark, and the ships — there are Consortium ships in the outer approaches and they're not answering hails, and they're telling us it's routine, they're telling us everything is routine —"*

A gap, filled with static, and then her voice again, lower now, faster, the formality burned away:

*"— I've got four hundred people here and two hundred of them are children and I am not powering down anything. I'm going to keep this array hot until someone makes me stop. Rosalind — tell the story. Whatever happens, whatever they — tell the story of us. We were real. We were here. We —"*

The recording ended there. Not faded. Ended, mid-word, as if a hand had come down on a switch somewhere.

Rosalind had sat in the chapel of the comms room with the apple going brown beside her, and she had done what the training said to do: she had queried Halcyon on all bands. She had queried Kepler. She had queried New Verdance, Saint Anselm, the terraces, the nurseries, all forty-one names, all forty-one addresses in the dark. She had sent the queries every six hours for a month, then every day for a year, then every day for eleven years, because Odile Ferro had said *tell the story of us* and Rosalind Dunmore had understood, with a clarity that never dulled and never softened, that she was now the only one left holding the pen.

There had been one more transmission, six days after Odile's. She had never been able to decide whether it was a mercy or a cruelty that there had been one more. It was from Saint Anselm, the orbital nurseries, and it was not a voice but a data packet, compressed and corrupted, most of it unrecoverable. What remained was a fragment of text, a few lines that the decoders had clawed back from the noise:

*...if you are reading this, the arrays are going down one by one and we no longer believe the official —*

*— children were moved first, at least there is that, at least we think —*

*— do not stop broadcasting. Whatever they tell you the silence means, do not believe it. Someone has to keep —*

And then noise, and then nothing, forever.

* * *

Rosalind opened her eyes. The amber screens glowed patiently. The archive waited. Four thousand and seventeen sent, zero received, and somewhere in the middle of that arithmetic, a woman's voice cut off mid-word, and a nursery full of children moved *first, at least there is that.*

She got up, because staying in the chair was the dangerous thing, staying in the chair was how the chair became a grave. She walked the length of the comms room with her arms wrapped around herself and stood at the great window that looked out into the vault's central atrium, where the grow-lights burned their eternal noon over rows of green that no one but her would ever eat.

Here was the thought that came, as it came every year on this anniversary, wearing its reasonable face, its kind face, its face of mercy: *They're gone, Rosalind. All of them. Whatever happened — plague, war, collapse, simple entropy — it happened eleven years ago, and the colonies are tombs now, and you are a woman keeping flowers fresh in a mausoleum. Four thousand transmissions. Not one answer. Even the dead answer eventually, if the dead are listening. Even a tomb has an echo.*

It would be so much kinder, the thought said, in its reasonable voice. It would be kinder to you. Grieve properly. Mourn them, close the book, tend your seeds for their own sake and not as letters to the lost. Stop talking into darkness. Stop waiting for a voice that is never, ever coming.

And she stood at the window with the grow-light noon on her face, and she let the thought say all of it, every reasonable word, because she had learned that the thought deserved its hearing. It might even be true. That was the worst of it — it might be completely, cleanly, mercifully true.

Then she thought of Odile Ferro's voice going out mid-word, and the children moved first, and *do not believe the silence*, and she thought: *if I stop broadcasting, and somewhere out there one array is still hot, one hand still on a switch, one person still fighting for every kilobyte — then my silence is the last silence, and it will be the one that finishes the job.*

She went back to the console.

She queued the evening transmission. She described the apple, the last Meridian Pippin, in loving detail, because Odile Ferro would have wanted the detail. She read the germination numbers for the wheat, the barley, the rice strains, the old-world beans. She reported the weather on the surface — clear, hard frost, the aurora green and slow over the southern ice. She said, as she said every day, "This is Rosalind Dunmore, caretaker of the Meridian Seed Vault, and you are not forgotten. None of you are forgotten. I am still here. I am still listening."

She keyed the transmit stud. The floor trembled. The dark received her.

And then she sat back down to log the entry, and her hand stopped above the keypad, because the console had chimed.

Not the send-chime. She knew the send-chime the way she knew her own heartbeat.

The receive-chime.

She stared at it. Eleven years of discipline, eleven years of braced and braced and braced heart, and every one of those braces failed her at once. Her hand was shaking. The screen said:

**INCOMING TRAFFIC — UNKNOWN ORIGIN — FRAGMENTARY**

**SIGNAL CLASSIFICATION: PRE-COLLAPSE STANDARD**

**LANGUAGE MATCH: EXTINCT — 200 YEARS**

The vault was very quiet. The grow-lights burned their noon over the green rows. And out of the dark that had swallowed forty-one colonies and eleven years of her voice, something had spoken back — in a tongue two centuries dead, in fragments, in a voice that was, even through the static, unmistakably, terribly, human.
