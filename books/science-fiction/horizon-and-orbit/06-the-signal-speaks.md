## 6. The Signal Speaks

Rosalind did not sleep. She had told herself she would — had even lain down on the narrow cot in the hab module, pulled the thermal blanket to her chin, and closed her eyes with the disciplined intention of a woman who knew her body was a machine that required maintenance. But the fragment sat in her mind the way a splinter sits in a palm: small, invisible to anyone else, and impossible to stop feeling.

*Human. Recent. Terrified.*

She got up at what the vault's clocks insisted was 3:14 in the morning, local time being a courtesy the facility extended to her sanity, and walked back down the long corridor to the communications room with her breath fogging in the cold air. The corridor lights came on in sections ahead of her and died in sections behind, the way they always did, the vault conserving its strength like an old man hoarding coins. She had lived eleven years inside that rhythm. Tonight it felt less like frugality and more like something breathing around her in the dark.

The communications room was the smallest of the vault's working spaces, a room built for a staff of six and occupied for a decade by one. Her chair had molded itself to her over the years; the desk bore the pale rings of a hundred mugs of tea; the wall above the console was papered with her hand-drawn star charts, annotated in three colors of ink, because she had never trusted the display screens to keep her honest. She sat down, wrapped her hands around a fresh mug she would forget to drink, and woke the array.

The signal was still there. It had been cycling on a slow repeat since she'd first caught it, a whisper threaded through the static at the bottom edge of the band, as if the sender had set it to call and call and call into a darkness that never answered. She knew that cadence intimately. It was the cadence of her own maintenance logs, eleven years of them, broadcast on schedule to a colony network that had stopped replying before her grandfather's decoder ring had finished cooling on her finger.

But this was not her signal. This came from *out there* — from the direction of the old colonial corridor, the shipping lanes that had gone quiet one by one, the settlements that had reported reactor failures and crop blights and, always, always, the same polite terminal phrase: *no further transmissions will be necessary.*

She had spent years telling herself that phrase meant grief. Reactor failures happened. Blights happened. Colonies were fragile things, little bubbles of breath and warmth balanced on the edge of an indifferent dark, and sometimes the dark won. She had built her whole peace on that arithmetic. Eleven years of talking into silence, and she had made the silence mean something bearable.

The signal did not care what she had made it mean.

She opened the decoding suite her grandfather had built, the one that ran on the vault's oldest, most isolated machine, and fed it the night's capture — four hours of raw signal, cleaned and layered, the fragments stitched where they repeated. The Pre-Collapse Standard lexicon loaded in its amber-on-black window, twenty-two thousand words of a dead tongue, and Rosalind watched the parser work through the first pass the way she had watched it work a hundred times on old archival recordings, with the mild academic pleasure of a woman translating something safely buried.

Then the first clean phrase resolved, and the pleasure curdled.

*—repeat, this is Meridian Hollow, we are—*

Meridian Hollow. She knew the name. Every child of the vault families knew the names, recited like a litany of saints: Meridian Hollow, Callas Reach, the Verdance Ring, Thessaly Station. The great colonial projects, the ones that had carried ten thousand souls apiece out beyond the shipping corridors, the ones the Consortium's histories described in the past tense with a softness that had always struck her, even as a girl, as slightly too smooth. *Losses to cascade infrastructure failure. The colonies were ambitious; ambition carries costs.* She had grown up on those sentences the way she'd grown up on the seed catalogs, absorbing them without question, the way you absorb the color of the walls of the house you're raised in.

*We are* — what? The signal degraded there, syllables crumbling into noise, and Rosalind leaned toward the screen with her tea forgotten, her whole body become an instrument of listening. She ran the fragment again. And again. On the fourth pass, the parser caught enough of the waveform to reconstruct two more words, and the room seemed to tilt around her.

*—survivors. We are survivors.*

She sat back. Her hands had gone cold around the mug. Survivors. Not colonists reporting a failure. Not an automated beacon cycling its last stored message, because automated beacons did not choose the word *survivors*, did not put that particular ragged stress on the second syllable, did not repeat it — and there it was, further down the capture, the same word surfacing again through the static like a drowning man's hand breaking water.

*Survivors. Survivors of—*

Of what? The signal broke apart again. She worked the decoder for an hour, coaxing, cleaning, asking the old machine to find pattern where the universe had buried pattern under noise, and the room grew colder around her and she did not notice. Outside the communications room, down the long corridors, the vault hummed its patient hum, forty thousand seed varieties sleeping in their cryo-drawers, and Rosalind thought suddenly and absurdly of the wheat tests she'd run that morning, the little green shoots in their germination trays, reaching for a light that meant nothing to them except warmth.

*They're reaching too,* she thought. *Whoever is sending this. They're reaching.*

* * *

Dawn — the vault's approximation of it, lights warming along the corridor ceilings on their programmed schedule — found her still at the console, and by then she had the coordinates.

They came through at 06:41, buried in the signal's header, repeated with a stubbornness that suggested the sender understood exactly how damaged their transmission was and had chosen to spend their bandwidth on the one thing that could not be misheard. A set of spatial coordinates in Pre-Collapse Standard notation, old-style, the kind that hadn't been used in two centuries, the kind Rosalind had to translate by hand against a reference table because the parser kept trying to autocorrect them into modern format and losing digits.

She translated them twice. Then a third time, because the answer was impossible, and impossible things deserved the courtesy of verification.

Dead space.

The coordinates pointed into the Kestrel Expanse — the region the old charts had shaded gray and labeled with a single word that had always sounded to her like a warning and a shrug at once. Dead space: the stretch beyond the colonial corridor where the ancient relay network thinned to nothing, where no carrier wave could survive the distance between beacons, where ships went only if they had no choice and came back only if they were lucky. Nobody lived in dead space. Nobody *could* live in dead space; that was the entire point of the name. It was the emptiness the colonies had been built to push against, the dark margin at the edge of the map.

And someone out there, in the middle of that gray nothing, was broadcasting in a dead language to a network that had been silent for eleven years, calling themselves *survivors*, and hoping — this was the part that made her chest ache, the part she kept circling back to — *hoping someone would hear.*

She pulled the old colonial registry, the vault's archival copy, the one printed on paper because her grandfather had distrusted every storage medium newer than paper. Meridian Hollow's registered position was nowhere near the Kestrel Expanse. It sat in the corridor proper, a settled, charted, supplied location — right up until the year the transmissions stopped. She checked the others. Callas Reach: corridor. Verdance Ring: corridor. Thessaly Station: corridor. All of them inside the web of relays and shipping lanes, all of them comfortably, safely *reachable*.

So why were their survivors in dead space?

The question sat in her like a swallowed stone. She made herself tea and did not drink it. She walked to the window at the end of the communications corridor, the little reinforced viewport that looked out across the ice field toward the horizon, where the sun was coming up in its long, slow polar arc, gilding the frost ridges gold and violet, and she stared at that beautiful, indifferent light and let the question turn over and over.

*Survivors flee toward dead space. Survivors hide in the one place no one can hear them. Survivors go where the network can't reach — because the network is the problem.*

The thought arrived fully formed, the way the worst thoughts do, and she rejected it immediately, and then rejected her rejection, and then stood at the window with her heart going hard and slow, like footsteps on metal.

* * *

By the vault's midday she had the rest.

It came in pieces over the following hours, the decoder pulling phrases out of the repeating signal one at a time, each one landing like a stone dropped down a well — a long silence, then the far-off sound of impact. The sender, or senders — the grammar shifted sometimes, *we* and *I* trading places in a way that suggested either multiple voices or one person too exhausted to keep their pronouns straight — had built their distress call from fragments, spliced recordings, a message assembled under conditions that did not permit coherence. But the fragments told a story anyway, the way broken pottery tells you the shape of the pot.

*—no reactor failure. There was no failure. I was in the—* [degraded] *—when they came. They wore—* [degraded] *—they had lists.*

*Lists.* Rosalind read the word and felt the temperature of the room change. Lists. Not chaos, not catastrophe, not the blind cruelty of a universe that didn't care. Lists implied order. Lists implied someone sitting down in advance, with records, deciding who. You did not bring lists to a reactor failure.

*—they took the ships. They took the comms first, always the comms, before anything, so no one could—*

Always the comms first. She thought of the terminal phrase, the one that closed every colonial file in the archive. *No further transmissions will be necessary.* She had read it a hundred times over eleven years, had read it aloud in her logs in the early days, reading the colonies their own obituaries in her careful, level voice, believing she was reciting the facts of accidents. *No further transmissions will be necessary.* Not *we have lost contact.* Not *the signal has failed.* Necessary. As if someone, somewhere, had made a decision about necessity. As if the silence itself had been the deliverable.

Her hands were not quite steady when she keyed the next decoding pass.

And then, near the end of the capture, in the clearest stretch of signal she'd yet recovered — as if the sender had saved their strength for this, as if everything else was scaffolding and this was the message — came the phrase.

It repeated. That was the first thing she noticed, how it repeated, four times in succession with identical intonation, the way you repeat something when you cannot see your listener and cannot bear the possibility of being misunderstood. The decoder rendered it in clean amber characters, twenty-two-thousand-year-old grammar resolving into meaning, and Rosalind read it, and read it again, and the vault's hum faded out of her ears and the tea went cold in her hand and the light through the little viewport seemed suddenly very far away.

***They silenced us. They silenced us. They silenced us. They silenced us.***

She did not know how long she sat there. The clock said forty minutes had passed. It might as well have said forty years.

Because here was the thing, the thing that broke something open in her chest and let the cold air of a new understanding rush in: the phrase was not a plea. She had expected a plea. The whole signal had been shaped like a plea — coordinates, distress markers, the ragged desperation of it. But this phrase, repeated four times with that identical, deliberate intonation, was not asking for anything. It was a testimony. It was the voice of someone who understood they might die before anyone heard them, and who had decided that if they were going to be erased, they would at least spend their last bandwidth naming the erasure. *They silenced us.* Not *we were lost.* Not *we failed.* Passive voice with an active agent, grammar as accusation, the dead tongue itself deployed like a weapon because no one would think to monitor a language two centuries extinct.

Whoever they were, they had chosen Pre-Collapse Standard *on purpose*. They had hidden their cry inside a language the Consortium's monitors no longer spoke, betting everything on the chance that somewhere, in some forgotten place, someone still could.

Rosalind looked down at her grandfather's decoder ring, where it hung on its chain around her neck, worn smooth by three generations of thumbs. He had taught her the language as a game, when she was small — *the old tongue, Rosie, the tongue they used before everything got centralized and simplified, the tongue of people who asked too many questions.* She had thought he meant it romantically. She had thought it was nostalgia.

He had meant it as armor. He had taught her to read a dead language because he had known — the way the vault families had all known, in the marrow, without ever saying it aloud — that one day the living language would not be safe to think in.

*They silenced us.*

The anger came up in her slowly at first, like water rising in a lock, and then all at once, with a force that made her stand up from the chair without deciding to. Eleven years. Eleven years of maintenance logs sent into a silence she had mourned like weather, like fate, like the sad natural end of things — and it had not been weather. It had not been fate. Somewhere in those eleven years, while she had walked her corridors and tested her seeds and talked to the dark with such patient, faithful devotion, there had been *people* — thousands of them, tens of thousands, whole settlements of them — being herded into dead space or into graves, their comms cut first, their names filed under infrastructure failure, their silence *manufactured*, maintained, enforced. And she had kept the vault for them. She had sung to their memory in her logs. She had grieved them the way you grieve the dead, gently, at a distance, with the dignity of resignation.

They had not been dead. Some of them had not been dead. They had been *hiding*, out in the gray nothing beyond the last relay, holding their children close in the cold and broadcasting in a murdered language, and the whole time she had been sending her little logs out to a network of ghosts, the network had not been dead at all. It had been *muzzled*. There was a difference, and the difference was a crime, and the crime had a name, and the name was the Consortium, and the Consortium had lists.

Rosalind stood in the middle of the communications room, in the amber light of the console, and shook. Not with fear — she catalogued that honestly, the way her grandfather had taught her to catalogue everything — fear was there, a cold thread through her middle, fear of what this knowledge would cost her. But underneath the fear, for the first time in eleven years, something was burning. It felt like the germination trays looked on the morning the wheat came up: green, improbable, alive in a place where nothing was supposed to be alive.

"They silenced us," she said aloud, in Pre-Collapse Standard, her voice rusty on the old phonemes, her grandfather's accent surfacing in her mouth like a face in dark water. The words hung in the cold air of the room. She said them again, louder, and the vault's hum seemed to bend around the sound.

Then she sat back down and pulled the signal apart for the coordinates one more time, because a testimony deserved a destination, and a destination deserved a plan, and she was — she realized, with a laugh that came out cracked and fierce and nothing like her own — she was done being the last caretaker of a graveyard.

The array caught the signal's next cycle beginning, that same stubborn whisper rising out of the static, and beneath it, at the very bottom edge of the band, so faint she almost missed it, something new. Not part of the loop. A live addition, transmitted within the last hour, from the same bearing in the gray.

She isolated it, cleaned it, held her breath.

Four words, in a dead tongue, sent by a living hand:

*Is anyone there?*

Rosalind's hand hovered over the transmit key — the key she had pressed eleven thousand times to send logs to silence. Her heart was a fist against her ribs.

Behind her, down the long corridor, the vault's reactor alarm began, very softly, to ring.
