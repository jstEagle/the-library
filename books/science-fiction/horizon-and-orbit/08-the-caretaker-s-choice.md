## 8. The Caretaker's Choice

The survey ship left orbit at 0347, and Rosalind knew it because she had not slept.

She had spent the hours in the cryo-bay's dark, wedged between the racks of dormant embryos and a coolant manifold that breathed cold against her spine, listening to the vault above her. The Consortium skimmer had circled twice, its scanning pulse passing through the permafrost like a hand pressed flat against her chest, patient, thorough, indifferent. Then the thrum of its engines had faded, and the silence that followed was worse — the silence of something that has seen what it came to see and gone away to report it.

She climbed out of the bay at first light, if you could call it light. The vault's emergency mode had dimmed everything to amber, corridors lit at quarter-strength, the grow-lamps over the test beds reduced to a dull ember glow that made the wheat seedlings look like they were burning slowly instead of growing. She walked the main gallery with her hands in her pockets, doing the arithmetic she had been avoiding for two days.

Forty days minus three. Thirty-seven, then. Maybe less. The reactor's countdown did not negotiate; she had checked the logs four times hoping she'd misread them, and four times the numbers had come back the same: steady, mechanical, absolute. Whatever had triggered it — a fault, a command buried in some subroutine older than her tenure, or a deliberate act by whoever owned that ship — the vault was going to burn. And when it burned, it would take with it eleven years of germination records, forty thousand years of agricultural inheritance, and every scrap of evidence that anyone had ever lived beyond Earth and been silenced for it.

She stopped at the test bed where the barley grew. Third generation from the original stock, planted the week after her grandfather died, because planting things had always been how she said goodbye.

"You'd think I'd be used to this," she told it quietly. "Losing things."

The barley didn't answer. Nothing ever answered. But she had learned, over eleven years, that talking to living things was not the same as talking to no one. The plants listened the way stone listens — without judgment, without leaving — and that had been enough. It had been enough for a long time.

It wasn't enough anymore.

* * *

She spent the morning in the archive, pulling data cores.

There were more than she could carry, and she had known there would be, and knowing it did nothing to soften the moment when she stood before the wall of them — hundreds of cores in their foam cradles, each one labeled in her own handwriting, each one holding seasons of work — and understood that she would save perhaps thirty, and the rest would become smoke.

She chose the way her grandfather had taught her to choose seeds for a hard winter: not the best specimens but the most essential ones. The full germination archive, compressed across six cores. The transmission logs — eleven years of unanswered broadcasts, which had seemed like grief-mongering when she'd archived them and now looked like testimony. And the signal itself. The distress call in Pre-Collapse Standard, all 214 fragments of it, decoded as far as her equipment allowed, raw and partial, sitting on its core like a heartbeat in a jar.

*They silenced us.*

She wrapped each core in insulation foil and packed them into a padded case, and her hands were steady, and she noticed that they were steady, and found it strange. Her hands should have been shaking. Everything she had built her life around was about to be destroyed, and her hands were steady, and she thought: *this is what it looks like when someone stops being afraid and starts being useful.* She had read that somewhere once, in an old book about polar expeditions, back when she still read books about people who went outside.

Because here was the thing she kept circling, the way her tongue kept finding the gap of a missing tooth:

She could stay.

She could stay hidden in the cryo-bay when the Consortium came back, let the reactor do its work, let the vault die quietly in the dark the way the colonies had died quietly in the dark, and the fire would erase the evidence and probably erase her too, and either way the story would end the way the Consortium wanted it to end: cleanly, silently, with no witnesses. That was the sane choice. That was the choice that kept her alive, maybe, for a while — long enough to watch the last seeds cook, long enough to broadcast maintenance logs to no one until someone finally switched off her transmitter too.

Or she could leave. Take the cores, take a sledge of seed canisters, cross three hundred kilometers of ice sheet to the old relay station at the edge of dead space — a structure so old the charts marked it with a symbol meaning *unverified*, a relic from before the Collapse that nobody had maintained in two centuries and nobody visited because dead space was dead — and try to make it speak.

Thirty-seven days to cross three hundred kilometers alone, hauling a loaded sledge through terrain that killed people who knew what they were doing.

The odds were not good. She wrote them down anyway, in her logbook, because writing things down was how she thought: *Option A: stay, hide, die with the evidence. Probability of survival: low. Probability of mattering: zero. Option B: go. Probability of survival: low. Probability of mattering: nonzero.*

Nonzero. Her grandfather would have laughed at that. He'd have said hope wasn't a number, it was a discipline — you practiced it daily whether you felt it or not, the way you practiced seeding rows, and some years nothing came up, and you seeded again anyway.

She closed the logbook.

"All right," she said aloud, to the empty archive, to the wall of cores she couldn't save, to the whole green sleeping world of the vault. "All right."

* * *

Loading the sledge took the rest of the day.

It was an old freight sledge from the surface depot, fiberglass and titanium runners, designed for hauling drilling equipment across the ice shelf in the vault's construction era. She hadn't touched it in nine years. Its runners were pitted with frost-scars and one of its tie-down rails had corroded through, and she spent an hour in the machine shop fabricating a replacement bracket while the vault hummed its dying hum around her, and she found, absurdly, that she was enjoying herself — the specific pleasure of hands solving a problem, metal fitting metal, a thing made slightly less broken than it was.

Then she packed it, and the enjoyment drained away, because packing it meant choosing.

Seed canisters first: the master collection, the deep-vault duplicates, the ones her grandfather had called the irreplaceables. Wheat and rice and maize, yes, but also the odd treasures — the salt-tolerant barley bred for drowned coastlines, the high-altitude quinoa, the frost-pear seeds that had taken her grandmother twenty years to coax viable. Two hundred canisters, each the size of a thermos, each weighing just enough. She packed them in insulated layers, cushioned with foam, arranged by thermal sensitivity, and the sledge began to look like what it was: a lifeboat, a library, a coffin for a world that might still be saved.

Then the cores. Then medical supplies, because the relay station might have needs she couldn't predict. Then the repair kit, the fuel cells, the tent, the stove. Then food — ration bars, mostly, dense gray slabs that tasted like sweetened chalk, enough for forty days if she stretched them, thirty if she was honest.

By the time she finished, the sledge weighed nearly four hundred kilograms, and she looked at it the way you look at a decision you can't unmake.

She tested the harness in the corridor, leaning into the load, feeling it resist and then roll, resist and then roll. Three hundred kilometers. At ten kilometers a day — a generous estimate for a woman alone hauling that mass over broken ice — thirty days. Seven days of margin, if nothing went wrong. Something would go wrong. Something always went wrong. Her grandfather used to say the ice forgave nothing and forgot nothing, and he'd grown up on it, and he'd still died inside, in the warm, in the vault, with his boots by the door.

She took off her lab coat and hung it on the hook by the airlock, folding it once, neatly. Underneath she wore the thermal layers she'd slept in for eleven years, and over those, the expedition suit she'd inherited from the construction crews — patched at both knees, seals renewed annually because renewing the seals was the kind of task she never skipped, tasks were the bones she'd built her sanity on. She checked the suit twice. Then a third time, because the third check was free and the fourth would be fear wearing a practical mask.

Last, she went to her quarters.

They were small, and spare, and eleven years of a life fit into two bags' worth of taking. She took the photograph of her grandparents on the ice, young and wind-burned and laughing at whatever had been funny in 2089. She took her grandfather's decoder ring — the brass ring with the rotating cipher wheel he'd worn every day of his life and left to her along with the vault itself, the ring she'd used to crack the first fragments of the dying signal, the ring that had started all of this. It sat warm in her palm, small and stupid and precious, a circle of brass holding more weight than the sledge outside.

And she took the logbook. Blank pages now, mostly. Room, she supposed, for whatever came next.

She stood in the doorway a moment and looked back at the room where she had slept for eleven years, at the narrow bed and the shelf of paper books and the little window that faced the ice, and she felt — not courage. She wanted to be honest about that, even with no one to hear it. What she felt was smaller and harder than courage: a kind of clarity, cold and clean, like the air off the glacier. Courage was a feeling people wrote about in books. This was a decision. You made it, and then you got up in the morning and did the next task, and the feeling, if it ever came, would come later or not at all, and it wouldn't matter either way, because the seeds would either reach the relay or they wouldn't, and feelings had no vote.

She turned off the light.

* * *

The airlock cycled at dusk, and Rosalind Dunmore walked out onto the surface of the Earth for the first time in three years.

The cold hit her like a fact. Minus fifty-one, wind out of the southeast at fifteen knots, visibility good — her suit's readout delivered the numbers calmly, and her body delivered its own verdict, a full-body flinch that faded into a deep, ringing alertness. The sky was doing the thing it did at this latitude in autumn: bruised violet at the horizon shading up into a black so complete the stars looked less like lights than like holes. To the south, the ice sheet ran white and blue-shadowed to the curve of the world. To the north, dead space.

She stood on the loading ramp and let the wind push at her, and beneath her boots the vault lay quiet — her quiet, green, doomed world, forty thousand years of inheritance stacked in the dark under four meters of permafrost, running out its clock. She had half expected to feel the countdown in her chest, some sympathetic tremor. There was nothing. Just the wind, and the stars, and the sledge behind her with its four hundred kilograms of almost-everything-that-mattered.

"Thirty-seven days," she said. Her voice sounded strange outside, flattened by the helmet, swallowed instantly by all that open air. "Give or take."

She clipped into the harness, leaned forward until the load caught, and pulled.

The sledge resisted — the first meter always resisted, her grandfather had said, the first meter is the ice asking if you're sure — and then it rolled, and she walked, and the vault shrank behind her. She did not look back. Looking back was a luxury; she'd budgeted for none.

Ten kilometers the first day, exactly as planned, though *planned* was generous for what the day actually contained: a white expanse that erased distance, a harness that found every weak point in her hips and shoulders and made them sing, and a mind that would not stop generating catastrophes in vivid detail — crevasses, storms, the survey ship returning, her own heart giving out at kilometer two hundred with the seeds scattered behind her like dropped coins. She talked to drown it out. She talked to the seeds, describing the route ahead, the weather, the recipe for the barley soup she'd make if any of them ever got planted somewhere warm. Her voice in the helmet was thin and ridiculous and it helped.

She camped at dusk in the lee of a pressure ridge, anchoring the tent with practiced efficiency, melting snow for water, logging the day's distance in the logbook by headlamp. *Day 1. 10 km. All systems nominal. Seeds secure. Operator secure.* She paused, pen hovering, then added: *Morale: adequate.* It was a lie, or close enough to one that it didn't matter. Morale was a discipline too.

On the third day the storm found her.

It came out of the north without drama — a smudge on the horizon at noon, a brown-white wall by mid-afternoon — and she had just enough time to anchor the tent with double lines and crawl inside before the world ceased to exist. For eighteen hours the wind screamed at ninety kilometers an hour and the tent snapped and cracked like something being tortured, and Rosalind lay in her bag listening to it and doing the only reasonable thing, which was nothing. You did not travel in a ground blizzard. You did not even think about traveling. You lay still, conserved calories, and waited for the planet to finish its tantrum.

She was forty kilometers out. Forty kilometers in three days, against a schedule that demanded eighty. The arithmetic sat in her stomach like a stone. Behind her, the vault burned down its days. Ahead, the relay waited, ancient and unverified, if it even functioned, if it even existed anymore, if the ice hadn't crushed it decades ago the way the ice crushed everything eventually.

Somewhere in the small hours, the wind lulled, and in the lull she heard it — faint, at the edge of perception, threading through the tent fabric like a needle through cloth.

A voice. On the emergency band. Thin, distorted, repeating.

Her heart slammed once, hard, and she fumbled for the receiver with numb fingers, dragging it close, turning the gain up until static filled the tent like a second wind.

The voice resolved, fragmentary, breaking apart and reforming:

*"—anyone receiving— this is— repeat— we are—"* A burst of static tore it apart. Then, fainter, steadier, in the dead tongue she alone seemed able to hear: *"—is anyone out there—"*

Rosalind lay in the dark of the tent with the storm roaring back in around her, holding the receiver against her ear like a shell held to a child's ear, listening to the ocean of static for another voice.

Someone was out there. Someone was transmitting. And the direction-finder, when she dared raise the antenna for ten seconds into the screaming wind, gave her a bearing that pointed north — past the relay, past the edge of the charts, into dead space.

She logged it in the logbook with a hand that, this time, was not quite steady.

*Day 3. Storm-bound. 40 km total. Signal received on emergency band, bearing north-northwest. Someone is alive.*

She clicked the pen shut, and in the dark she smiled — a small, fierce, terrified smile — and began repacking her gear by touch, because the storm was weakening, and thirty-three days were left, and she intended to waste not one hour of them.
