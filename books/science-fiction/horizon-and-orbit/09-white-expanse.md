## 9. White Expanse

The ice field began where the vault's shadow ended, and Rosalind Dunmore walked into it pulling four hundred years of human agriculture behind her on a sledge.

She had imagined this crossing a hundred times — in the long evenings when the reactor hummed its slow arithmetic beneath her feet, in the darker hours when sleep refused her. In those imaginings she had always been brisk about it. Boots laced, sledge harnessed, horizon chosen. What she had never imagined was the sound: the way the wind moved across the plain like something enormous breathing, or the way her own boots cracked the surface in small reports that echoed nowhere, swallowed instantly by all that white.

Three days out from the vault, she stopped talking to herself and started talking to the seeds.

It wasn't madness. She was fairly sure it wasn't madness. It was habit, transposed. For eleven years she had narrated her germination logs aloud because the act of speaking made the work real, made the numbers stick, gave the silence something to push against. Now the logs were gone and the seeds rode behind her in their insulated canisters, forty kilograms of dormancy wrapped against the cold, and she found she could not bear to travel with them mute.

"Day three," she said, adjusting a strap that didn't need adjusting. "Wheat line Kestrel-9 is riding well. Barley's shifted — I'll repack at camp. You'd think after two centuries of storage you'd have learned to sit still."

The canisters said nothing. The wind said a great deal.

* * *

The rover died on the fourth day, and it died the way old things die: without drama, without warning, mid-sentence.

She had been running it low and gentle since the vault, conserving the fuel cells the way she conserved everything, and it had carried her ninety kilometers across the shelf ice before the drive coupling seized with a sound like a tooth cracking. She sat in the cab for a long moment afterward, hands still on the controls, listening to the tick of cooling metal.

"No," she told it. "No, no. Not yet."

But the diagnostics were unambiguous, scrolling their amber verdicts across a screen older than she was. The coupling was fused. She had no replacement. She had known, in some honest corner of herself, that the rover was living on borrowed decades; she had simply never let that corner speak above a whisper.

She unloaded the sledge, transferred the canisters by hand through wind that wanted to take them from her, and stood for a moment looking at the vehicle — her shelter, her warmth, her moving wall against the weather — now just a shape going white at the edges already, snow beginning its patient work of erasure.

"You did well," she said to it, and meant it, and hated how easily the words came to objects and how rarely they had come, in eleven years, to anything that could answer.

Then she turned north, toward the relay station two hundred kilometers away, and began to walk.

* * *

The mathematics of the thing kept her company better than any conversation. Two hundred kilometers. She could make perhaps fourteen a day on the flat with the sledge, less in rough ground, less again as her body spent itself. Fourteen days of walking if nothing went wrong. She had food for twenty. The margin was thin enough to see through, and she turned it over in her mind the way she used to turn soil samples, looking for anything she'd missed.

What she found instead, somewhere past the hundred-kilometer mark, was her grandfather's voice.

He had walked this ice once. That was the family story — Elias Dunmore, third generation keeper, who had gone out in the Year of the Quiet to check the relay stations and come back changed, quieter himself, as though he'd left some part of his voice out there among the antennas. He had never spoken much about it. But he had taught her the ice, before he died: how to read sastrugi for wind direction, how to test a snow bridge with a probe pole before trusting it, how to know the particular blue of ice that would hold versus the grey-green of ice that wanted you dead.

*"The ice doesn't hate you, Rosie,"* he'd said, one winter, his gloved hand heavy on her shoulder. *"It doesn't love you either. That's the whole lesson. Everything else is what you bring to it."*

She brought grief, she supposed. And stubbornness. And forty kilograms of seeds that represented every harvest humanity might ever see again, if humanity got another chance, which was precisely the wager she was walking to place.

On the sixth day she talked to them through an entire whiteout's edge, describing the vault to them as though they were children asking about home.

"There's a corridor," she said, breath ragged, harness creaking, "the long one, B-section, where the light comes through the observation port at noon and makes everything gold. Your ancestors grew there once. Actual dirt. Actual sun. There are photographs. You wouldn't believe the photographs."

The wind took her words and scattered them. She kept talking anyway. That was the whole point of talking — not to be heard, but to keep the machinery of speech oiled, so that when she finally met another human being she wouldn't have forgotten how.

* * *

The storm came on the eighth day, and it came without mercy and without negotiation.

She saw it first as a smudge on the northern horizon, a bruise-colored line under the white, and her stomach dropped before her mind caught up. She had seen storms from the vault's towers, distant and cinematic. This was different. This was the sky deciding to come down and touch the ground, all at once, everywhere.

She had maybe forty minutes. She knew it the way farmers know weather in their bones, and she spent thirty-five of them building.

There was no shelter. The route between vault and relay was empty by design — dead space's threshold was dead ground too, scoured clean of anything worth keeping. So she dug. She cut blocks of wind-hardened snow with the spade from her kit and built a wall in a shallow depression behind a ridge of old pressure ice, and then she dug down, carving a chamber barely large enough for herself and the sledge, roofed with her tarp and anchored snow blocks, packed and packed again until her arms shook.

The storm arrived while she was still sealing the entrance, and it arrived like a verdict.

The sound was beyond description and she tried anyway, later, in her head: it was the sound of the world being erased. Wind at speeds that lifted the snow off the ground entirely and carried it as a solid, screaming medium, a white darkness in which up and down were matters of opinion. Her shelter flexed. Snow found every seam. She lay curled around the seed canisters — around them, literally, her body a final layer of insulation over the insulated cases — and listened to the storm try to bury her alive.

Somewhere in the second hour, the entrance drifted shut completely.

She knew it was happening. She heard the airflow change, felt the cold deepen from uncomfortable toward absolute, understood that the drift sealing her in was also the only thing keeping the structure from being peeled away over her head. Buried was survivable. Exposed was not. She marked the entrance location mentally, checked her air estimate — hours, enough hours — and did the only thing left to do.

She talked.

"Kestrel-9," she said, her voice strange and small in the dark. "You're named for a bird, did you know? A falcon that hunts on the wing. My grandmother chose the line. She said wheat should be ambitious." A pause. The storm screamed overhead. "Rice varieties, you're in the middle case, behave yourselves. Sorghum — sorghum, you ornery old thing, you've survived worse than this. Your whole lineage is drought and spite. This is just weather."

Her breath fogged in the beam of her hand lamp. The cold pressed in like a hand testing whether she'd flinch.

"My grandfather walked out here," she told them. "Did I mention that? Before your time. He went out to the relays when everyone stopped answering, same as me, same direction, and he came back and never said what he found. I used to think it broke him." Her throat tightened. "I think now maybe he just learned how big the quiet was, and carried it home, and nobody could hold it for him. Nobody held it for me either. That's not a complaint. That's just — inventory."

She laughed, a cracked, astonished sound. "Inventory. Listen to me. Eleven years of logging and I inventory my own feelings like tubers."

The storm raged. She slept in fragments, waking to check the canisters' temperature strips, waking to talk, waking to the animal certainty that the weight above her was growing. By what she judged to be morning — there was no morning, only the lamp and her own reckoning — the drift over her shelter was more than a meter thick and the air tasted of her own exhalation, stale and close.

She dug upward with numb hands, breaking through into light so fierce she wept at it, actual tears freezing at the corners of her eyes. The world had been remade overnight. The pressure ridge she'd sheltered behind was a smooth white swell. Her tracks were gone. The rover, ninety kilometers south, was gone in every way that mattered. The landscape offered no evidence that any human being had ever crossed it, or would again.

Except her. Except the sledge. Except forty kilograms of sleeping green.

"Right," she said, hoarse, standing upright in the enormous silence. "Where were we."

* * *

She lost a day digging out and reorganizing, and the arithmetic turned cruel. Food for twelve days now. Distance remaining: perhaps eighty kilometers. She recalculated twice, three times, hoping the numbers would soften, and they refused. The margin hadn't thinned. It had inverted.

So she ate less and walked longer, and the ice became a country she moved through in a kind of trance, and the talking changed. It stopped being log entries and became something rawer.

"You want to know why I'm doing this?" she asked the canisters on the eleventh day, hauling up a long slope of wind-packed snow, each step a negotiation. "Because someone has to say it happened. That's all. That's the entire mission. People lived out there — colonies, generations, children born under other suns — and somebody decided they were a rounding error, and everybody else agreed to forget on schedule. And forgetting is a kind of murder that keeps killing after everyone's dead. So I'm hauling you across the ice so that remembering has a witness. That's it. That's the grand cause. A woman and a sledge."

She crested the slope and stopped, swaying, and the honesty kept coming whether she wanted it or not, because exhaustion had picked the lock on her reserve.

"And maybe," she said, quieter, "maybe I'm doing it because if I stop moving, I'll have to sit with the fact that I spent eleven years keeping seeds alive for people who never came, and I never once let myself ask whether they weren't coming because they couldn't — or because they chose not to. Whether the silence was death or desertion. Because either answer breaks something in me, and I couldn't afford to be broken. The vault needed me whole." She wiped her face with a mitt, roughly. "Well. The vault's burning now. So here I am, broken and walking, and it turns out broken still walks."

The seeds rode on behind her, silent, patient, trusting her in the way that only dormant things trust — absolutely, and without any ability to object.

* * *

On the thirteenth day, half-starved and hollow-eyed, she saw the waystation.

It rose out of the ice like a rib bone — a dark arc of metal against the white, tilted, half-buried, one end collapsed into a drift. Too small to be the relay. Too close to be anything else. She had passed its coordinates two days ago without spotting it, buried as it must have been, and the storm had done her an accidental kindness, scouring it bare.

Hope hit her like a physical blow. Shelter. Possibly supplies. Possibly — her heart stuttered — possibly heat, a working stove, a radio, anything.

She covered the last kilometers in a stumbling run she'd pay for later, dragging the sledge over sastrugi that fought her every meter, and reached the structure as the light failed. Up close it was worse and better than it looked: worse, because the collapse had crushed the eastern section flat; better, because the western end still stood, its door frozen shut but intact, its hull unbreached.

She attacked the door with her spade and her shoulder and pure fury, and when it groaned open at last onto darkness and the smell of old metal and older dust, she stood in the entrance gasping, letting the wind die behind her, letting the stillness take her weight.

A waystation. Old route infrastructure, pre-Collapse probably, built for travelers who had stopped traveling before she was born. She fumbled her lamp on and swept the beam across the interior: bunks along one wall, rusted to sculpture. Storage lockers, most hanging open and long-empty. A heater unit whose status lights were dark.

And then the beam caught movement, and Rosalind Dunmore, thirteen days out from everything she had ever known, froze.

Something was in the far corner. Something small and fast and watching her — a shape coiled in the shadow of a collapsed bunk frame, eyes catching the lamplight, and beside it, glinting, unmistakable even in the murk, the dull shine of a blade.

The shape spoke first, in a voice young and hard and cracked with disuse:

"Drop the sledge handle. Slow."

Rosalind's hand tightened on the strap. Behind her, the wind moaned against the hull. In front of her, forty kilograms of humanity's future sat in the dark between her and a stranger with a knife.

"Okay," she said carefully, not moving. "Okay. Let's talk about this."
