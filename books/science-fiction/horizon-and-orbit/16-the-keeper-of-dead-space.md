## 16. The Keeper of Dead Space

The weapon did not waver.

Rosalind had imagined a hundred versions of this moment during the last weeks — the relay rising out of the ice like a cathedral built by people who worshipped signal instead of god, its antenna spires combing the violet sky at the threshold of dead space — and in none of those imaginings had the first thing to greet her been a muzzle.

"Stop there." The voice was rough, unused, the voice of a man who spoke mostly to machines and had forgotten how to speak to anything else. "Ten more steps and I stop asking."

Juno froze beside her, one hand drifting toward the knife at her belt with the casual insolence of someone who had survived by not being impressed by much. Rosalind put her own hand out flat, low, the way you'd calm an animal.

"We're not Consortium," she said.

"Everyone says that." The man stepped out from behind a frost-sheathed strut, and Rosalind got her first real look at him. He was older than his posture suggested — sixty, maybe more, but lean in the way of someone who had done hard physical labor for decades without ever quite making peace with it. His coat was patched with insulation stripped from a dozen different wrecks. His beard was iron-gray and untrimmed. And his eyes — pale, hooded, red-rimmed from years of staring into screens — held the particular exhaustion of a man who had been waiting a very long time for something he dreaded.

He looked at her sledge. At the seed canisters strapped beneath the tarp. At Juno. At the decoder ring on its cord around Rosalind's neck, half-exposed where her collar had pulled loose.

His expression changed. Not softened — *changed*, the way a landscape changes when cloud shadow crosses it.

"Where did you get that ring?" he said.

"It's mine," Rosalind said. "It was my grandfather's."

"Your grandfather's *name*."

"Dunmore. Elias Dunmore."

The weapon lowered perhaps five degrees. It might as well have been nothing. Behind the man, deeper in the antenna forest, something hummed — a vast, patient, subsonic vibration that Rosalind felt in her back teeth, in the soles of her boots, in the fluid of her eyes. The relay was alive. Whatever else this place was, it was alive.

"Elias Dunmore designed the vault's environmental lattice," the man said slowly. "Third-generation keeper stock. Which makes you either a liar or—" He stopped. Something moved behind his face like weather. "Or the woman who's been broadcasting maintenance logs on a dead band for eleven years."

"You've heard them."

"I hear everything." He said it without pride. He said it the way a drowning man might say *I know about water*. "Every band. Every hour. For twenty years." The weapon came down another degree, then steadied. "That's how I know you're being followed. That's why I'm pointing this at you anyway. Because everything the Consortium follows eventually leads here, and I have spent two decades making sure there is nothing here worth finding."

"There is now," Rosalind said. "There's me."

The man laughed — a single dry syllable with no joy in it whatsoever, a sound like a lock engaging.

"Yes," he said. "That's exactly what frightens me."

* * *

He made them walk ahead of him through the antenna field, sledge runners hissing over rime, while he kept three paces back with the weapon held loosely at his hip — not aimed, not holstered, held in the manner of a man who had decided violence was possible but not yet probable. The spires rose around them on all sides, hundreds of them, slender black needles furred with ice, each one canted at a slightly different angle toward the same patch of sky: the black mouth of dead space, where no ship had gone and no signal had returned for longer than Rosalind had been alive.

"What are they pointed at?" Juno asked. She'd been quiet since the weapon appeared, which for Juno meant she was cataloguing exits.

"The same coordinates," the man said. "For twenty years. Every night, on every frequency that can still carry, I broadcast into dead space." He paused at a hatch set into the base of the largest spire, keyed a code with a gloved thumb, and stood aside to let them enter first — which told Rosalind two things at once: that he trusted the interior more than the exterior, and that he wanted them walking in front of him where he could watch their hands.

"Why?" Juno said. "Who's out there?"

"No one," the man said. "That's rather the point."

The inside of the relay was warm. That was the first shock — after weeks of ice and wind and the vault's dying chill, warmth hit Rosalind like a physical blow, thick and mineral-smelling, the heat of old reactors and old machinery running long past any reasonable service life. Cables ran along the walls in loomed bundles as thick as her torso. Screens glowed amber in alcoves, most of them displaying waveforms Rosalind recognized with a lurch: the slow, repeating signature of a distress call.

Her distress call. No — *a* distress call. The fragmentary signal in Pre-Collapse Standard that had started everything, looping here on the relay's displays, dissected into its component harmonics, annotated in a cramped engineer's hand.

"You decoded it too," she breathed.

"I've had twenty years to decode things." The man pulled off his gloves finger by finger. Up close, his hands were a map of old burns and newer scars, the hands of someone who had wired high-voltage systems alone in the dark for most of his life. "Sit. There's tea, if you don't mind tea that tastes like the kettle. And then you're going to tell me exactly who sent you, because nobody walks four hundred kilometers across the ice carrying seeds unless somebody wants those seeds somewhere specific, and the only somebodies who care about seeds are the ones who burned the colonies."

"Nobody sent me," Rosalind said. She sat, because her legs made the decision for her, and the chair was real and deep and the first furniture she'd touched in a month. "I sent myself. My reactor is counting down. Forty days when it started — fewer now. Someone triggered it remotely, or—"

"Or it was always going to fail," the man said, "and someone simply made sure you'd be looking the wrong direction when it did."

Rosalind stared at him. "How could you possibly know that?"

He didn't answer. He busied himself at the kettle, and the small domestic sounds filled the silence — water, metal, the tick of an ancient heating element — and in that silence Rosalind felt the shape of the thing he wasn't saying, the way you feel a wall in a dark room before your fingers find it.

"Your name," she said. "You know mine. Give me yours."

"Marek," he said at last. "Tomas Marek." He set two chipped mugs down, then a third, and pushed it toward Juno without looking at her; she took it with the wary speed of a stray accepting food. "Formerly senior systems engineer, Consortium Directorate of Communications, retired — if you can call it retirement — approximately twenty years ago."

"Retired," Juno repeated, around the rim of her mug. "People retire from the Consortium?"

"This one did." Marek sat down across from Rosalind, and for the first time he put the weapon aside — laid it flat on the table between them, deliberately, within reach of everyone and no one. "And now you're going to tell me what's in those cores under your tarp, Keeper Dunmore, and you're going to tell me slowly, because depending on your answer, one of us is going to have a very bad night."

* * *

She told him.

She told him about the eleven years of unanswered transmissions, and his face did not move at all, which frightened her more than anger would have. She told him about the signal in Pre-Collapse Standard, and something flickered behind his eyes — recognition, and beneath the recognition, dread. She told him about the countdown, the survey ship, the flight across the white expanse, the waystation, the manifests she'd found in the Consortium's own records. When she reached the phrase — *they silenced us* — Marek closed his eyes.

When she finished, he was silent for a long time. The relay hummed around them, that vast patient heartbeat, and somewhere above them the great antennas turned fractionally on their bearings, tracking stars that weren't there.

"The manifests," he said finally. "Colony registry numbers. Shipping tonnage. Labor allocations."

"Yes."

"And they matched the signal's origin pattern."

"They matched it exactly. Whoever sent that signal knew the shipping schedules. Knew the transfer points. They were *inside* the system, Marek. They watched it happen."

Marek nodded, very slowly, like a man confirming a diagnosis he'd already read in his own chart. Then he stood, walked to a cabinet, and came back with a battered data slate, which he set on the table face-down, his palm flat on top of it, as though holding down something that wanted to escape.

"Twenty years ago," he said, "I wrote the code that killed their voices."

The words dropped into the room and lay there.

"I was a good engineer," he went on, and his voice had gone flat and careful, the voice of a man reciting something he had rehearsed ten thousand times in the dark. "The best they had. They came to my team with a problem: colony uplink instability. Signal degradation across the outer network. They gave us beautiful specifications, clean parameters, a six-month timeline. We built a filtering protocol. Elegant. Self-propagating. It would ride the maintenance windows and quietly re-prioritize bandwidth allocation across the colonial relays." His jaw worked. "It didn't degrade their signals. It *replaced* them. Every transmission a colony sent inward was received, logged, archived — and answered with a synthesized confirmation generated locally. The colonies thought Earth was listening. Earth thought the colonies had gone quiet due to cascade failures. And in between, on the channels nobody was supposed to be watching, the actual traffic flowed one direction only. Inward. Population counts. Resource yields. Labor assessments."

Juno had gone very still. Rosalind realized she herself had stopped breathing and made herself start again.

"When did you find out?" she asked. Her voice sounded strange to her, distant, underwater.

"Eleven days after deployment." Marek laughed again, that terrible lock-clicking laugh. "Eleven days. I found it because of a rounding error. A checksum discrepancy in the archive logs — trivial, the kind of thing a junior tech should have caught. I traced it, and I found the mirror layer, and I found the archive, and I spent one entire night reading what the colonies were actually saying before the filters ate their words." He lifted his palm off the data slate at last, leaving it face-down on the table like a grave marker. "A woman on Meridian Station singing to her daughter over a two-second delay. A mining foreman on Callisto Deep reporting a pressure breach and requesting evacuation for forty-one men. A teacher. Farmers. People saying goodnight to grandparents they would never see again, on channels that were already graves."

"What did you do?" Rosalind whispered.

"What does a good engineer do? I went to my director. I presented the discrepancy. Politely. With documentation." His mouth twisted. "Two weeks later I was promoted. Given a private lab, a generous stipend, and a project of my choosing, on the understanding that my chosen project would be far away from communications architecture. They didn't threaten me. They didn't need to. They showed me what compliance looked like, and they let me understand that the alternative had a shape too." He looked at his scarred hands. "I took the promotion. I told myself I'd work from inside. I told myself a lot of things. And then, fourteen months later, the colony network went dark — officially dark, catastrophically dark, reactor accidents and cascade failures, all of it — and I understood that the filter had never been the weapon. It was just the silencer on the barrel."

"So you ran."

"I defected. I stole a shuttle, I nearly died doing it, and I came here — to the one relay old enough, dumb enough, and isolated enough that the Consortium forgot it existed. And I have spent every night since pointing every antenna I have into dead space, broadcasting the truth on bands nobody monitors, to an audience of absolutely no one." He spread his hands. "Penance, if you like. Or madness. From in here, the difference is academic."

* * *

Juno broke the silence, because Juno always broke the silence, because silence was a luxury she'd never been able to afford.

"So you knew," she said. "All this time, you knew the colonies were murdered, and you sat here talking to the dark about it."

"I didn't know anyone survived," Marek said, and there was something raw underneath the flatness now, something bleeding through. "If I'd known—"

"You'd have what? Sent a stronger signal?" Juno's voice cracked like ice underfoot. "My parents were 'relocated' when I was six. Relocated. That's the word the labor crews used. You want to talk about archives? Their names are in your pretty Consortium ledger somewhere between a shipment of alloy stock and a crate of protein ration. So don't tell me you didn't know anyone survived. You didn't *look*. Looking costs something. Talking to the dark costs nothing."

"Juno." Rosalind's voice was quiet, but it landed. The girl's jaw stayed tight, her eyes bright and furious, but she sat back down.

"She's not wrong," Marek said simply. "That's the worst part. She's not wrong about any of it." He turned to Rosalind, and now his pale eyes were steady, and terribly tired, and pleading in a way he probably didn't know. "But here is what I know that you don't, Keeper. Here is why my hand was on that trigger when you walked up. Twenty years ago I believed exactly what you believe — that the truth is a light, and light saves people. And then I watched what the Consortium did with the truth. They didn't argue with it. They didn't refute it. They *buried* it, and they buried the people who carried it, and they called the graves accidents. Every person who tried to speak — engineers, journalists, two directors, a whole oversight committee on Meridian — gone. Reassigned. Relocated." He let the word sit, and Juno flinched. "You are carrying the single most dangerous cargo in human space across four hundred kilometers of ice, and you think the hard part was the storm."

"The hard part," Rosalind said, "is deciding whether the people who come after me will inherit a lie or a record. Everything else is weather."

"And when the record gets them killed?" Marek leaned forward. "When the Consortium traces your broadcast, finds this relay, finds the survivors you're so certain are hiding in dead space — because yes, I've read your decodes, I've had your signal running through my analyzers since it first brushed my array — and burns that moonlet the way they burned the colonies? Will the truth have saved them then? Will it comfort you?"

"It won't matter," Rosalind said, "if we're smart enough to be faster than they are. And it won't matter at all if we say nothing, because then they die in the dark either way — except this time, *we* chose the dark." She stood. Her legs ached; she ignored them. "You wired the codes, Marek. You know their protocols better than anyone alive. Which means you're not my judge. You're my best chance."

For a moment the only sound was the hum of the relay, and the tick of the kettle cooling, and the wind outside worrying the antenna field like a restless animal.

Then Marek looked down at the face-down data slate between them — twenty years of broadcasts, twenty years of penance, everything he'd ever said to no one — and his hand hovered over it, trembling slightly, as if it weighed as much as the world.

"There's something else," he said quietly. "Something you need to see before you decide how much you trust me. And after you see it, you may wish you'd never crossed the ice at all."

He turned the slate over.
