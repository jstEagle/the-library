## 1. The Long Inventory

There are four hundred and six seed drawers in the Meridian Vault, and I have counted them every morning for eleven years. Not because they move. Because counting them is the first thing that reminds me the world still has edges.

I say good morning to Drawer One the way you'd greet a stubborn relative. "Still there? Good. Keep it up." The drawer does not answer. Nothing down here answers. But I've found that if you stop talking to things, they start disappearing—not physically, nothing so dramatic as that—they just stop being real, and then you follow shortly after.

The recorder is running. It's always running. Log 4,011, if you're keeping score at home, and by *you* I mean the theoretical archivist of a future civilization who will one day sift through my voice like an archaeologist sifting ash, wondering what kind of man spent a decade narrating to drawers of frozen barley.

*Barley*, by the way, is in Drawers 1 through 38. Wheat follows, then rice, then the legumes, then the heirloom tomatoes I am personally, unprofessionally fond of—the Brandywines, the Cherokee Purples, a Ukrainian variety called Oxheart that looks exactly like what it's named for and tastes like a summer that no longer exists anywhere within forty light-years of here. I checked its drawer this morning. Sealed. Viable. Patient in the way only seeds and the dead are patient.

My route never changes. Corridor A runs north-south along the spine of the vault, two kilometers of grey composite and humming conduit, lit by strips that flicker at a frequency just slow enough to make you doubt your own eyes. I walk it at 0600 station time, which is a fiction I maintain with religious devotion because a man without a schedule is just meat drifting in a can. At 0615 I reach the first pod bay.

The sleepers are in Bay One through Bay Six. Two thousand, three hundred and fourteen souls, arranged in ranks like a choir mid-inhale. I do my rounds the way a nurse does rounds, except my patients have been asleep for longer than most human lifespans and will remain so regardless of my bedside manner. Still, I check. Pod 1-001: vitals nominal, cellular integrity ninety-eight point two percent, dream-state activity present. She's dreaming. I like to think it's something nice. I've named her Beatrice in my head, not after anyone real—after the woman on the wheat crate illustration from before the Silence, holding a loaf of bread like it was a newborn. Beatrice has been sleeping under my care for eleven years and has never once complained about my coffee breath.

Pod 1-002: nominal. Pod 1-003: nominal, though his coolant line rattles when the bay pressure shifts, and I've learned to tighten the coupling every third Tuesday whether it needs it or not. He's a big man, this one. Engineer's calluses preserved in amber. I've decided he was kind, because someone has to decide these things and I'm the only electorate left.

You get to know people by their vitals. That sounds insane. It is insane. But when your entire society consists of you and 2,314 unconscious strangers, you develop relationships with their heart rates. Beatrice's pulse sits at fifty-one beats per minute in deep cycle, steady as a metronome, and on the mornings when I'm low—which is most mornings—I stand by her pod and breathe in time with her until my chest remembers how it's done.

This morning her pulse was fifty-one. All is well in the empire of the sleeping.

* * *

Let me tell you how the vault works, because somebody should know, even if that somebody is a hard drive.

Meridian was built as an argument. Earth, in its last coherent decades, had a fierce internal debate about whether humanity deserved a backup copy. One faction said yes—we're worth saving, look at our music, look at our mathematics. The other said the money would be better spent fixing the original. The fixers lost, narrowly, and so the builders came out here to the edge of the settled dark and dug a vault into a rock that orbits nothing in particular, and filled it with seeds and embryos and cultural archives and two thousand colonists in cryosleep, all of it waiting for a world called Horizon to be made ready.

Horizon was supposed to send word. Survey teams, terraforming reports, a simple *come on down*. For the first few years, the transmissions came on schedule—thin, compressed, bureaucratic, but real. Then, eleven years ago, they stopped. Not gradually. Not with static or distress. They just stopped, between one scheduled relay and the next, the way a conversation stops when you realize the other person has left the room.

I was twenty-nine. My sister Iris was thirty-one, and she was down there, on Horizon, doing atmospheric work she loved and complaining about in letters I still have. Her last message is short. They're always short, near the end. *"Sable—dust storm season early this year. Tell the vault I said hi to the tomatoes."* That's it. That's the whole inheritance. Eleven years later and I can still recite it, and I do, sometimes, walking Corridor C, out loud, like a prayer with a punchline.

The council's standing orders were clear enough: maintain the archive, maintain the sleepers, wait for contact. Waiting is a job. Nobody tells you that. Waiting has quotas and overtime and days when you want to quit but there's nowhere to resign to.

So I wait, and I count, and I talk to a recorder.

* * *

Mid-morning, I do the environmental sweep. This is the part of the day where competence stops being a personality trait and becomes a chore list.

Atmospherics first. The vault breathes in long cycles—scrubbers cycling CO2 through the algae walls in Hydroponics, which double as my garden and my sanity. The algae tanks need stirring. Not metaphorically; there's an actual paddle, an actual crank, and every day I stand there turning it like some monastic butter-churner while green water swirls and gives off a smell I can only describe as *pond with ambitions*. The oxygen they produce keeps me alive. In return I keep them stirred and tell them about my day. We have a functioning relationship, the algae and I. Better than most marriages, probably.

Then the cold rooms. Minus eighteen Celsius, rows upon rows of backup seed stock in case anything happens to the primary drawers. I check the seals, the humidity, the frost patterns on the door gaskets. Frost tells you things, if you listen. A feathered frost pattern means a micro-leak; a clean rime means all's well. Today: clean rime, all's well. I logged it. Log 4,011, entry seven. Somewhere a database grows fat with entries saying *all's well*, and if that isn't a monument to the human spirit, I don't know what is.

Lunch is reconstituted noodles with dried chives from my own hydroponics, eaten standing up in the galley because sitting down felt too much like dining alone and standing up feels more like fueling a machine. There's a difference. I've thought about this difference more than any sane person should. The trick, I've found, is to keep the rituals small enough to complete. A whole day is a country too vast to cross. But a noodle bowl—that's a border you can actually get over.

Afternoon belongs to the pods again. Full diagnostic rotation, one bay per day, so each sleeper gets a thorough going-over once a week. Today is Bay Four. I roll the maintenance cart down the aisle, checking cryo levels, neural suppressor feeds, the little green lights that mean *still alive in here, still dreaming, still worth saving.*

Bay Four holds the children.

Forty-one of them, aged four to twelve when they went under, which makes them somewhere between fifteen and twenty-three now, biologically paused at the moment of maximum possibility. The protocol says children go into cryo last, wake first, priority on everything. I read that directive so many times in the early years that it wore a groove in me. Children first. It's the one rule of the vault that feels less like policy and more like conscience.

I don't name the children. I tried, in year two, and got as far as a girl in Pod 4-017—a gap-toothed grin in her intake photo, a stuffed rabbit listed in her personal effects—and then I realized what I was doing, building a family out of photographs, and I stopped. Naming them would make them mine, and making them mine would make losing them possible, and losing them is not on the menu. So they stay unnamed, and I check their vitals with hands that are steady because I have trained them to be steady, and once a week I tell Bay Four, out loud, "Somebody's coming for you. Hold on."

I don't know if that's true. I say it anyway. A lie told weekly for eleven years acquires a kind of truth through sheer attendance.

* * *

Evenings are for the long walk. Corridor D loops the outer ring, two and a half kilometers, and I walk it while the station hums and the stars wheel past the viewports with the unhurried arrogance of things that don't know they're beautiful.

Eleven years of this loop. I've done the math—it's the kind of math you do when the alternative is feeling things—something like nine thousand kilometers. Twice the length of a continent, walked in place, in a tin can, alone. If I ever get off this rock, my legs will arrive before the rest of me does, still marching, still expecting corridors.

Tonight the stars are out in force. No atmosphere to dim them, no city glow, just the raw inventory of the galaxy spilled across black. I used to find it comforting. Now I find it honest. All that light, and none of it addressed to me. Somewhere out there is Sol, a yellow smudge I could point to but couldn't pick out of a lineup anymore. Somewhere beyond it, further, is Horizon, silent as a held breath. And somewhere in between—or maybe nowhere at all—is whatever happened to make everyone stop talking.

I've built theories the way other men build ships in bottles. Maybe Horizon failed—plague, war, weather, the usual apocalypses. Maybe they succeeded so well they forgot us, the way children forget to write home. Maybe the silence is a decision. That one's the worst, so I keep it in the back of the shelf where the light doesn't hit it. Most nights I settle on equipment failure. Relay satellites die. Antennas crack. Civilizations, presumably, misplace their transmitters the way I misplace torque wrenches. It's a small theory. Small theories fit inside a life.

The recorder catches me talking to myself out here, too. It always does. "*Caretaker's log, supplemental:* the starboard viewport needs resealing, third panel, hairline fracture, I'll patch it Thursday. *Supplemental:* the Brandywine seed stock smells exactly the way it did when I sealed it, which is impossible, since smell doesn't survive vacuum-sealing, and yet. *Supplemental:* I miss the sound of rain. Not even real rain. Recording of rain. Any rain."

That last one goes in more often than I'd like to admit. You'd think a man would run out of missing. You'd be wrong. Missing is renewable. It crops up like weeds in the same corner of the same field, and every morning I'm out there pulling it, and every morning it's back, and the field stays green somehow, and I stay upright somehow, and the drawers stay sealed, and the sleepers stay asleep, and the count continues.

Four hundred and six drawers. Two thousand, three hundred and fourteen sleepers. One caretaker. Zero messages.

Tomorrow the numbers will be the same. That's the covenant I live under: sameness as sacrament. As long as tomorrow matches today, everyone I'm responsible for is still safe, still frozen, still waiting for a future that is now eleven years overdue. I am the hinge between their yesterday and their whenever, and hinges don't get to be tired.

* * *

It's near midnight when I finish the last round, and I'm back in Bay One, standing by Beatrice's pod with my hand flat against the frosted glass, watching the green numbers climb and dip and climb. Fifty-one beats per minute. Steady. Faithful. The most reliable heartbeat in my acquaintance.

"Good night, Beatrice," I tell her. "Same time tomorrow."

And then—because the recorder is running, because honesty is cheaper at midnight, because eleven years of routine deserve one crack of daylight—I lean closer to the pickup and say the thing I usually edit out:

"I don't actually think anyone's listening. I've known it for a while now, I think. The logs aren't letters. They're a man talking to keep the quiet from moving in. But if—*if*—someone plays this back someday, whoever you are: the tomatoes were excellent. The people were better. Take care of both."

I click the recorder off. The bay hums. Forty-one children dream their unnamed dreams, and Beatrice keeps her time like a clock that loves me, and I turn toward my bunk—

—and every light in Bay One stutters brown, dims to embers, and comes back.

All of them. At once. In eleven years, that has never happened.

I stand very still in the recovered light, my heart doing something Beatrice's never has, and I think, with the calm of a man who has had a great deal of practice lying to himself:

*Probably nothing.*

I'll run a reactor diagnostic in the morning. Right after the count.
