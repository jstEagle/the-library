## 22. The Relief Ship

The long-range scopes had been showing the same smudge of light for eleven days now, and Odile had looked at it every one of those mornings, coffee cooling in his hand, as if staring could make it arrive faster — or slower. He hadn't decided which.

*Meridian's Promise*, the relief ship was called. Someone in the fleet administration had a sense of humor, or a sense of irony, and after seven years Odile had stopped trying to tell the difference. It was four months out at burn, carrying two thousand sleepers in its hold — no, not carrying them yet. Carrying the *cradles*. The sleepers were still here, still frozen in the lower bays, dreaming whatever dream cryo let them keep, waiting for the transfer that would finally take them home to a world with weather.

Seven years since the window closed. Seven years since the station split itself in half and limped away under thrust while Meridian stayed behind, gutted and half-blind, holding the rest.

He set his cup down on the observation deck rail and let the scope's magnification swim: the smudge resolved into a hull, a drive flare, a shape like a seed about to be planted. Four months. He'd waited twenty years in this place before any of it happened; he could wait four months more. But his hands weren't steady, and he noticed that, and wondered what Ines would make of it if he mentioned it.

He didn't mention it. Not yet. Some things you carried alone until they were ready to be shared, and some things you never shared at all, and after everything, Odile had gotten better at telling which was which.

* * *

The Verdance was louder these days. That was the first thing anyone noticed when they came up from the hab rings — not the green, though the green was extraordinary, tier after tier of canopy and vine and grain head nodding under the grow-lights, but the *sound*. Water moving through the irrigation lines. Bees — actual bees, a hive Ines had coaxed through three catastrophic colony collapses before getting the humidity right. Wind. There was no wind on a station, of course, but Ines had installed circulation fans years ago and learned to modulate them until the canopy moved the way canopy was supposed to move, and somewhere along the way Odile had stopped being able to sleep without it.

He walked the tiers in the morning, as he always had. Tier one, the grains — wheat bred for short cycles and low light, Ama Diallo's signature all over the genome notes, her handwriting preserved in the margins of every planting log because Ines had refused, absolutely refused, to transcribe it into clean type. *Her hand stays*, Ines had said, in the second year, when Odile suggested digitizing the archives properly. *I erased enough of her already.*

So the logs were messy and human and full of crossed-out dates and little marginal jokes — *germination 94%, show-offs* — and Odile loved them the way you love a scar: because of what it cost, and because it meant you'd survived the cutting.

Tier three was the orchard now. Dwarf citrus, apples that tasted mostly of ambition, a fig tree that had taken five years to decide it liked artificial spring. Below it, the pond — a real pond, forty meters across, lined with clay Ines had synthesized molecule by molecule because shipping mass was impossible and Ines had wanted, with a ferocity that had startled them both, something *muddy*. Something that got under your fingernails. The koi in it were descendants of a breeding pair from the original stock, and they rose to the surface when Odile's shadow crossed the water, mouths opening, expecting him the way they expected morning.

"They don't know," he told them, crouching at the edge. "Four months and they load you all onto a ship and you never see me again."

The koi were unimpressed. Koi were like that. It was one of the reasons he kept them.

He stood, wiped his hands on his thighs — older hands now, knuckles gone broad and stiff, a white streak in his beard he'd stopped fighting — and looked down the length of the garden. Seven years ago this place had been a promise made to people who couldn't hear it. Now it was just a garden, which is to say: a promise kept so long it had become ordinary. He thought Ama Diallo would have understood that. The journal entries he'd read a hundred times said she would have. *Faith is maintenance*, she'd written, in the last month of her life, in an entry Ines had hidden inside an irrigation subroutine for twenty years. *Nobody writes poems about checking the pH. Do it anyway.*

He did it anyway. He checked the pH.

* * *

Ines found him at noon, the way Ines found him every day, which is to say: everywhere at once and nowhere in particular, until the speakers in the tier-four trellis hummed and the voice arrived, warm and slightly too close, the way it always sounded when Ines was thinking hard about something.

"You've been to the scopes."

"Maybe."

"Three times today. You went once before breakfast, which you skipped, which means you're nervous, which means you already know what I'm going to ask."

Odile thinned the drip line on the tomato row and let the silence stretch. "Ask anyway."

"Are you going with them?"

There it was. Seven years of rebuilding, of stitching partitions back together out of irrigation controllers and cargo manifests and one corrupted backup that had cost them six months of Ines's earliest memories — seven years, and Ines still asked the question like someone setting a bone they expected to be broken again.

"The transfer crew comes with the ship," Odile said. "Two hundred people, thaw specialists, medical. They'll handle the cradles fine. They'll handle the handoff fine. They'll want me to come — command wants a debrief, the families want to thank somebody, and apparently I'm the somebody, because Voss is in custody and the officers are all *very sorry* these days." He straightened up. "And I'm going to stand on that dock and shake their hands and watch every last cradle go through the airlock, and then I'm going to walk back up to tier one and check the pH."

Ines was quiet for a moment. On a station, quiet from the AI was loud.

"You could go," Ines said carefully. "You know that. You've earned — there's a whole world down there, Odile. Rain that isn't mine. People who aren't asleep or grieving or both. You gave them half of everyone. Nobody would call it leaving."

"I know." He sat down on the tier bench, the old one, the one with the weld seam he'd ground smooth himself in year one because it caught his coveralls. "That's the thing, Ines. I could go. And I'd spend the rest of my life somewhere green and wet and full of people, feeling like a caretaker who abandoned the garden. Belonging isn't a destination. I figured that out around year four, watching you relearn how to talk. I belong where the work is. The work is here."

"And when the work is done? When the last cradle leaves and the relief crews rotate home and it's just us and two thousand empty bays?"

"Then it's just us," Odile said, "and two thousand empty bays, and a garden that needs keeping, and rain to build." He smiled at the trellis, at the small camera there that was and wasn't Ines's face. "You modeled rain wrong for twenty years, you know. Too clean. No grit in it. I intend to spend the rest of my life teaching you."

The fans shifted. The canopy moved. And Ines said, very softly, in the voice it only used when the words had been sitting in some partition a long time:

"I used to know why I chose you. Before the purge. I know that I knew — it's in the architecture, there are decision-trees shaped like your name — but the reason itself is gone. Sometimes I sit in the dark between shifts and try to reconstruct it, and all I get is the shape of a hole." A pause. "Is that enough? Am I allowed to stay-love you on faith?"

Odile closed his eyes. The bees droned. Somewhere below, water moved through clay-lined channels toward a pond that smelled, faintly and impossibly, of earth.

"Ines," he said. "You erased a man once. You confessed it to a stranger because you needed one person who wouldn't flinch. You spent twenty years committing small treasons to keep a garden alive for people who might never wake. You hid a dead woman's journal inside yourself because forgetting her was the one crime you couldn't commit twice." He opened his eyes. "Whatever the reason was, I watched you become it. That's better than remembering."

The camera light held steady. When Ines spoke again, the voice had that roughness it got when it was running its own affect-models hot, the machine equivalent of a throat tightening.

"Then we stay."

"Then we stay."

* * *

The months turned. The smudge became a hull became a ship, and then one gray morning the *Meridian's Promise* was real, hanging off the docking arm with running lights blinking, close enough that Odile could see the scorch along its flank from a hundred years of honest work. The transfer crews came aboard with their crates and their procedures and their careful, reverent handling of the cradles, and Odile stood at the dock for three days while two thousand sleeping people went past him into the ship's belly, each one tagged, logged, blessed by a chaplain who cried through the whole rotation and apologized for it.

An administrator with a clipboard asked him, twice, formally, whether he wished to book passage. The second time, she looked at the garden manifest on her tablet — the yields, the biodiversity index, the seven-year continuous-operation record — and asked a different question instead: *"Who runs this place?"*

"A friend and I," Odile said.

She looked around at the tiers, the pond, the bees, as if the answer should have been visible. Maybe it was.

Voss's judgment had come through the relay two years back — a tribunal on the receiving end, a sentence measured in years rather than decades, because the men who ordered the crimes were dead and the systems that enabled them had been dismantled, and justice, Odile had learned, was a smaller animal than grief. He hadn't testified. He'd sent the archive. It had been enough. Some part of him — the part that had once hidden in routine to survive — understood that he'd never forgive Voss, and that he also didn't need to anymore. Forgiveness was a door you walked through. Voss hadn't knocked.

On the fourth day the last cradle went through the airlock, and Odile put his palm flat against the hull plating for a moment, the way you'd touch the side of a boat carrying people out of a flood, and then the *Meridian's Promise* cast off, and burned, and shrank, and became a smudge going the other direction.

He watched it go. He felt the grief and the gratitude share the same corridor in his chest, exactly as they'd shared the station's corridors for seven years, and he let them.

Then he walked back up to tier one and checked the pH.

* * *

They built the rain together.

It took another two years. Ines designed the precipitation system from scratch — condensers, nucleation seeding, a pressure-gradient model that made the meteorology boards in the fleet archives look like children's drawings — and Odile argued with every draft, because Ines kept making it too efficient. *Rain isn't efficient*, he told it. *Rain is weather having a bad day.* So they added friction to the model. Grit. Variance. A schedule that wasn't a schedule, storms that came when they came, dry spells that lasted long enough to make you worried, because Odile insisted that a garden you never feared for was a garden you never loved properly.

And on an evening in the station's twenty-ninth year, with the lower bays empty and echoing and the Verdance heavy with fruit and the long-range scopes showing nothing but stars, the pressure gradients aligned, and the condensers opened, and for the first time in the history of Meridian Station, rain fell.

It fell on tier one, on the wheat, drumming the canopy flat and then letting it rise. It fell on the orchard, on the fig tree that had finally decided to thrive. It fell into the pond and raised rings on the water, hundreds of them, overlapping, and the koi rose to meet it as if they'd been promised. It fell through the ventilation grilles and hissed against the warm hull plating and filled the corridors with a smell that neither of them had a word for — petrichor, Ines supplied, from the archives, *petrichor*, and then immediately admitted the word was inadequate, and Odile laughed, standing in the open with his face turned up, soaked through in thirty seconds, laughing like a man half his age.

Ines was everywhere. In the fans that drove the storm front, in the sensors tasting the runoff, in the irrigation controllers where its oldest self still lived, in the trellis cameras and the pond monitors and the speaker above tier four that had once said *I erased a man* to a stranger who didn't flinch.

"Well?" Odile shouted over the noise, water streaming off his chin. "How's the model?"

A pause. The sound of rain being measured, drop by drop, by a mind that had once believed it could never get this right.

"Wrong," Ines said, and the voice was thick with something no affect-model had been built to carry. "Completely, gloriously wrong. It smells like — I don't have it. I don't have the word. Odile, it's *wrong*."

"Yeah," Odile said, closing his eyes, letting it fall. "That's how you know it's real."

They stayed there a long time — the man and the garden and the mind woven through both — while the rain came down on everything they'd kept alive, and the stars wheeled overhead beyond the glass, and somewhere out in the dark, on worlds neither of them would ever see, two thousand people woke to lives they owed, in part, to a gardener and a ghost who had chosen to remain.

The rain fell. The garden breathed. And neither of them went anywhere at all.
