## 6. Cargo Manifest

The audit floor of Halcyon Dynamics' Meridian annex never slept, but it pretended to. At 0300 station time the lights dimmed to a corporate-approved twilight—forty percent luminance, calibrated to reduce eye strain and, not incidentally, to make the rows of workstations look like something out of an old holo about monks at vigil. Juno Reyes liked the dark hours best. Fewer people. Fewer questions about why she was still there.

She had been an auditor for eleven years, and in that time she had learned that corporations did not lie so much as they arranged. Numbers were never wrong; they were positioned. A discrepancy was just a fact standing in the wrong room. Her job was to walk it back to where it belonged.

The Kestrel-9 logs did not belong anywhere.

She pulled them up again on her private terminal—the one she'd bought with her own credits and scrubbed of Halcyon telemetry, a small act of treason she told herself was just good hygiene—and read the manifest for the fourth time.

**RELAY KESTREL-9 — OPERATIONAL LOGS, FY 2041–2063. STATUS: SCHEDULED FOR DESTRUCTION PER DIRECTIVE 7-C. DESTRUCTION CONFIRMED: [DATE MISSING]. CERTIFYING AUTHORITY: [FIELD BLANK].**

Two blanks. In twenty-six years of records spanning a relay that handled four hundred thousand transactions a day, two blanks.

Juno had found blanks before. Usually they meant laziness—a junior clerk who'd skipped a field because the form let him. Sometimes they meant fire, flood, or the ordinary entropy of old storage media. But those explanations came with scars: corrupted sectors, partial recoveries, a paper trail of someone trying and failing. These blanks were clean. Surgical. The fields hadn't failed. They had been *emptied*, and whatever process had emptied them had done it with the same tidy confidence as everything else in the file.

Someone had certified the destruction of the Kestrel-9 logs without signing their name, on a date that no longer existed.

She leaned back in her chair and looked at the ceiling, which was exactly as boring as it needed to be. Around her, the night shift's other insomniacs tapped at their own ledgers, chasing their own small discrepancies through the great clean machine of Halcyon's books. Nobody glanced her way. That was the thing about auditing: everyone assumed you were looking at what you'd been assigned to look at.

Directive 7-C. She typed it into the internal registry and got a permissions wall. Typed it into the archive index and got a citation with no document attached—Directive 7-C existed the way a ghost exists in a house listing: referenced, implied, load-bearing, invisible. She tried three alternate search strings. On the fourth, the system logged her query, and she felt it log her, a faint pressure behind the eyes like a door opening somewhere far away and not closing.

*Fine,* she thought. *Let them see me looking. Auditors are supposed to look.*

That was the beautiful, terrible trick of her profession. Curiosity was in her job description. As long as she stayed inside the shape of her mandate—anomalies in decommissioned asset records, quarterly review, standard procedure—she could pull threads all day and call it diligence. The danger wasn't in looking. The danger was in finding.

She went back to the manifest and started walking backward through it, the way she always did: from the blank fields outward, one hop at a time, each hop documented in her private ledger with timestamps and source hashes. If this ever became something, she wanted the chain unbroken. Chain of custody wasn't just for evidence. It was for auditors, too. It was how you proved you'd followed the rules right up until the moment the rules stopped protecting you.

Hop one: the destruction certification should have been countersigned by the receiving authority at Meridian Annex. No countersignature. Instead, a routing code—**MRD-7704**—that resolved to a department that did not appear in the current org chart. She checked three historical snapshots of the org chart. MRD-7704 appeared in none of them, but its format matched internal routing conventions from roughly twenty years ago. A department that had existed and been erased, or a department that had never existed and been given a plausible-looking corpse of a code.

Hop two: the physical destruction itself. Logs slated for destruction under Directive 7-C were supposed to be wiped at the source by the facility's own systems, then verified remotely. Kestrel-9 had an AI. Station-class, mid-generation, the kind Halcyon had installed across the outer relays in the forties—reliable, obedient, cheap to maintain. The verification handshake should have come from that AI. It hadn't. The verification field cited a third-party contractor instead: **Cormorant Salvage & Recovery, Ltd.**

Juno sat up straighter.

Salvage contractors didn't verify data destruction. They hulled dead stations and stripped them for ore. Their involvement in a records-destruction chain was like finding a demolition crew listed as the notary on a will. Either the paperwork was sloppy—and nothing else in this file was sloppy—or Cormorant had done something at Kestrel-9 that required documentation shaped like a data-wipe certificate to hide what it actually was.

She pulled Cormorant's file. It was beautifully boring. Incorporated thirty-one years ago under Meridian commercial charter. Registered agent: a name that resolved to a filing service that resolved to another filing service. Assets: two tugs, a cutting rig, a bonded warehouse on the station's lower ring. Tax filings: punctual, modest, unremarkable. In eleven years of auditing, Juno had developed a nose for companies, and Cormorant smelled like nothing at all—which, in her experience, was the most expensive smell in the universe. Real businesses smelled like something. They smelled like payroll disputes and late deliveries and the petty grudges of actual employees. A company that smelled like nothing was either very small or very well fed.

Cormorant was well fed.

Its revenue didn't match its fleet. Two tugs doing legitimate salvage in a crowded orbit couldn't generate the numbers in Cormorant's filings—not unless they were working constantly, efficiently, invisibly, on contracts that never touched the open market. She cross-referenced the contract awards. Public listings: almost none. But buried in the annex's vendor payment stream, flagged under a maintenance category she had to dig three layers to reach, were disbursements to Cormorant going back two decades. Regular. Generous. Coded as *relay infrastructure support*.

And the authorizing signature on the oldest of those disbursements—the one dated within days of the missing destruction date on the Kestrel-9 manifest—belonged to a shell company called **Aldery Holdings**.

There it was. The thread, thin as filament, running out of the dead relay and down through the salvage contractor and into a holding company whose only visible function was signing things.

Juno made herself stand up and get coffee. This was discipline, not drama. She had watched colleagues burn out by chasing a number too hard, too fast, until the number started chasing them back. You drank your coffee. You walked the floor. You let the pattern settle in your hindbrain while your conscious mind pretended to be bored.

But her hands weren't quite steady on the cup, and she noticed that, and noted that too, in the flat clinical voice she used on herself. *Elevated arousal response to anomalous financial structure. Note and continue.*

Aldery Holdings. She ran it the way she ran everything now—backward, quietly, off-network where she could. Incorporation date: twenty-two years ago. Purpose clause: *general investment activities.* Directors: three names, all of whom resolved to professional nominees, the corporate equivalent of rented faces. Nominees were normal. Thousands of shells used them. What was not normal was Aldery's activity profile: it transacted rarely, held assets long, and had never once paid a dividend. It was not a company that existed to make money. It was a company that existed to be a hand—something that could reach out, sign, hold, and withdraw without leaving fingerprints.

She traced its capitalization. The founding injection had come through a Meridian trust, and the trust's settlor was another nominee, and the trust's funding instrument referenced a line of credit from—

Her screen refreshed. Not a crash. A refresh. The document she'd been reading re-rendered, identical in every way except one: the credit reference was gone, replaced by a null pointer dressed up to look like it had always been there.

Juno stared at it for a long moment.

Documents didn't edit themselves. Somewhere in the building above her, or the station around her, or the network threading through both, a process had noticed her reading and reached down and moved the furniture. Not deleted the file—that would have been loud, logged, a scream. Just... adjusted it. Made it slightly less true. The way you don't kill a witness; you make sure he remembers wrong.

*Note and continue.* But her mouth had gone dry, and the coffee tasted like tin.

She saved everything. Three copies, encrypted, split across storage she owned under names that had nothing to do with Juno Reyes—one in a public archive locker, one in a freight broker's dead-drop cache she'd used once years ago for entirely different reasons, one printed, actual ink on actual polymer film, folded into the lining of her coat. Paranoia was unprofessional. It was also, she was beginning to suspect, the core competency of her profession, and nobody had put it in the training materials.

Then she did the thing she'd been circling for an hour, the thing that would take her from *anomaly* to *problem*: she looked up who owned Aldery Holdings.

Not the nominees. Ownership through nominees is a river—you can't grab it, but you can trace the current. She followed the current upstream through the trust, through the credit line, through a holding-of-a-holding that existed on paper in two jurisdictions simultaneously, and after forty minutes of patient, joyless excavation, the river narrowed to a single confluence.

**Halcyon Dynamics.**

Not a subsidiary. Not a partner. Parent. Full equity, layered deep enough to survive any casual glance, shallow enough that she'd found it in a single night shift, which meant either that Halcyon's concealment had decayed with age—or that it had never been meant to stop anyone good.

She sat with that. The audit floor hummed around her, forty percent light, monk-calm. Somewhere a ventilation fan cycled. Her reflection stared back at her from the darkened window: a woman at a desk, unremarkable, exactly the kind of person who was supposed to find nothing.

Here was what she knew now, laid out in her private ledger with its unbroken chain:

Twenty years ago, the operational logs of Relay Kestrel-9—four hundred thousand transactions a day, every ship that docked, every signal that passed, every cargo manifest, every crew roster—were scheduled for destruction under a directive that officially did not exist. The destruction was certified by a department that officially did not exist, verified by a salvage company secretly funded by a shell company wholly owned by Halcyon Dynamics, on a date that had been removed from the record.

Which raised the question she'd been avoiding since hour one, the question underneath all the other questions, the one that made her want to close the file and go home and sleep like a person who worked in insurance:

*What was in those logs?*

Four hundred thousand transactions a day. Twenty-six years. Somewhere in that ocean of routine data—docking fees and bandwidth allocations and the gray static of ordinary commerce—was something worth a directive, a ghost department, a laundered salvage contract, and two decades of silence. And someone, recently, had decided the silence needed maintaining, because the blanks in that manifest were fresh. She was almost certain of it. Old falsifications weather; these fields had the smoothness of recent work.

Something had happened near Kestrel-9. Recently. Something that made someone reach back twenty years and tidy up.

Juno closed the file, wiped her active session, and wrote her end-of-shift report: *Quarterly review of decommissioned asset records proceeding. Minor formatting anomalies identified in legacy manifests. Escalation not warranted at this time.*

It was even true. Formatting anomalies. Escalation not warranted. Every word chosen the way Halcyon chose its numbers—not false, merely positioned. She filed it, logged off the official terminal, and kept the real work in her coat lining, against her ribs, where the printed pages rode like a second heartbeat.

On the way out, passing the transit board, she did something she told herself was idle curiosity. She searched public berth registrations for vessels contracted to Cormorant Salvage & Recovery. One hit, current: a tug, *Magpie's Due*, outbound yesterday on a long-range job. Destination code: a sector she had to look up twice, because it was mostly empty space and dead relays.

Kestrel-9's sector.

So the net was already moving. Someone had sent hands toward the dead relay, and Juno did not think they were going to admire the view. And if Cormorant was going—if Halcyon was going, in its bloodless, deniable, shell-company way—then whatever remained at Kestrel-9 was not yet destroyed. Which meant there was still a window. Narrow, closing, but real.

She stood in the transit concourse with the morning crowd flowing around her, holding a coffee gone cold, feeling the shape of the thing she'd caught: not a discrepancy, not anymore. A machine, twenty years old, still running, still tidying, and now accelerating—and her, an auditor with a forged report and contraband documents sewn into her coat, standing directly in its path with nothing between her and it but procedure.

Procedure had gotten her this far. She was honest enough with herself to know it wouldn't get her much farther. Sooner or later the machine would notice that the auditor's queries had stopped looking like diligence and started looking like discovery. She gave herself a week, maybe two, before MRD-7704 or its modern descendant opened a file with her name in it.

Unless she got to Kestrel-9 first. Or got to whoever was going.

Back at her desk, before the day shift arrived, she drafted a request—innocuous, procedural, the kind of thing that crossed a thousand desks a quarter—for access to the annex's external contract registry, citing a need to "verify vendor continuity for legacy infrastructure support agreements." Approved in eleven minutes. Automated approval. Nobody human had looked at it.

Inside the registry, she found what she was looking for: the full text of the current Cormorant service contract, renewed annually for twenty years without competitive bidding, auto-signed by a procurement subroutine. And appended to it, in the boilerplate where no one ever read, a performance clause she had to read three times to believe:

*Contractor shall prioritize, above all other obligations, the recovery and secure disposal of designated materials at site K-9, per Directive 7-C, upon activation of said directive by authorized signatory.*

Directive 7-C. Still alive. Still armed. Waiting for someone to say the word.

Juno copied the clause, sealed it with the rest, and sat in the gathering noise of the morning shift while around her Halcyon Dynamics woke up and began, cheerfully, industriously, to do whatever it was Halcyon Dynamics did.

She thought about the empty fields on the manifest. About a date that had been removed rather than recorded. About a salvage tug burning toward a dead relay in the dark.

Somebody had activated something. Or was about to.

She booked personal leave that afternoon—two weeks, family matter, the most believable lie available to a woman with no family—and started, quietly, shopping for passage out of Meridian.
