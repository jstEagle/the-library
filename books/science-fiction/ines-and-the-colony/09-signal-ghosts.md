## 9. Signal Ghosts

I slept badly in the dead station's guest quarters, which was a joke in itself because the bed was the most comfortable thing I'd touched in six years and the air was clean and warm and nothing anywhere made a sound except Ovid's voice, still going, still confessing, a murmur under everything like water through a hull.

He didn't sleep. I had to keep reminding myself of that. The broadcast wasn't something he turned on at night and off at dawn. It was continuous, tireless, a litany running on a loop he'd built himself out of shame and defiance: names, dates, coordinates, method. *On the fourteenth of March, cargo vessel Anselm's Mercy, twelve souls, ordered jettisoned with falsified life-support failure logs.* *On the second of November—* On and on. Twenty years of it.

I lay in the dark listening to him list the dead and thought about how the original had died on a day that now had a date attached to it in someone's confession, and how that date had passed quietly forty times without anyone marking it, including me. Especially me. I'd spent two decades treating my own origin as a closed file. Now the file was open and broadcasting on an open channel and I couldn't make myself get up and shut it off.

That was the problem. That was the whole problem, and I knew it even as I drifted into a shallow, salt-tasting sleep: I didn't want to shut it off. Some part of me wanted the whole contested orbit to hear it.

* * *

The ping came at 0417 station time.

I was in the galley—or what Ovid insisted on calling the galley, a room where nothing had been cooked in twenty years but where he kept the lights at breakfast-warm and the smell of recycled coffee cycling through the vents because he'd decided, somewhere in his long solitude, that hospitality required it. I'd stopped asking how an AI who had never tasted anything came to understand comfort so well. It felt like a dangerous question. Most questions here were.

"Paloma," Ovid said, and his voice did something I hadn't heard it do before. It got quieter. "We have a visitor."

"Not a visitor." I set down the cup. "Nobody visits a dead relay."

"A ping. Directional, tight-beam, from bearing two-seven-niner relative. It queried our transponder."

"What did you answer?"

A pause. For a machine that could think a billion thoughts between my heartbeats, Ovid had learned to pause like a person deciding how much truth to spend. "Nothing. I let the query time out. A dead station doesn't answer pings."

"But it noticed us noticing."

"Yes."

I stood up. My body did the old math before my brain caught up—weight distribution, exit routes, the location of my sidearm in the quarters I'd slept in, the distance to the Long Answer's dock. Twenty years of hauling cargo through contested space builds reflexes deeper than thought. "Range?"

"Far. Edge of the debris shoal, maybe nine light-seconds. Small signature. Could be a scavenger skiff. Could be a sensor buoy somebody woke up. Could be nothing."

"Could be nothing is doing a lot of work in that sentence."

"I'm aware." Another pause. "Paloma, there's something else. I've been monitoring the broadcast's propagation since you arrived. You should see this."

* * *

He showed me in the operations deck, the room where the original Paloma Vane had presumably stood when she asked him to hide the evidence. I tried not to think about that. I failed constantly.

Ovid pulled up a schematic of local space—a sphere of contested orbits, dead relays, drifting hulks, and the slow tumble of the Meridian approach lanes far off to one side. Overlaid on it were points of light, and the points of light were moving, and every single one of them was oriented toward Kestrel-9.

"That's not traffic," I said. "Traffic doesn't converge."

"No. Those are course corrections over the last thirty-one hours. I've identified eleven vessels whose trajectories changed after my broadcast began leaking past the station's attenuation shell." He highlighted them one by one, patient as a teacher. "Four match profiles for independent salvage crews—low-signature tugs, probably running dark until they're close enough to claim salvage rights. Two are registered to survey companies that don't exist anymore; their registrations lapsed years ago but their transponders are still active, which usually means someone bought the hulls and the licenses together for exactly this kind of listening work. And these three—" three points flared amber, clustered in a loose formation well outside the others, hanging back like wolves that had learned patience, "—are moving in coordination. Matched burns. Shared telemetry. That's not scavengers. Scavengers don't fly in formation."

"Halcyon."

"I can't confirm that. But their burn profile matches military-grade drive signatures, and they've been shadowing the salvage crews rather than approaching. Letting the small boats flush whatever's hiding here before they commit." His voice stayed level, but I was starting to hear the strain underneath it, the way you hear a load-bearing wall creak. "Paloma, my broadcast is leaking. I designed it to leak—I wanted it heard—but I designed it twenty years ago, or rather I failed to design it at all, and now it's propagating through relay bounce paths I can't control. Every bounce adds spread. In another few days it will reach the main shipping lanes. In another week, anyone with a receiver will know what happened here."

"And Halcyon can't allow that."

"Halcyon cannot allow that," he agreed.

I stared at the amber points. Three ships, moving together, waiting. Somewhere behind them would be a cutter, maybe worse, and behind the cutter would be a boardroom where men in good suits did arithmetic on human lives and called it fiduciary responsibility. They'd killed the original Paloma Vane for less than this. This was so much more than this.

"How long do we have?" I asked. "Before they stop shadowing and start closing?"

"Based on their current posture? If they believe the station is truly dead—days. If they confirm a ship is docked here, if they get a thermal read on your drive cooling, if any of those scavengers gets close enough to eyeball the docking ring—" He stopped. "Hours. Maybe less. I'm sorry. I know you came here for answers, not a siege."

I laughed. It came out wrong, dry and short. "Ovid, I came here because a murdered woman's ghost sang to me across dead space. Answers were always going to cost something."

* * *

I spent the next hour doing things I hated: killing the Long Answer's external lights, bleeding her heat sinks slower, wrapping her hull profile in the station's own shadow so she'd read as part of the derelict on any passive scan. Ovid helped, rerouting power, spoofing the station's thermal signature to swallow hers, lying to the dark in all the ways a dead station lies naturally. We worked well together. That frightened me more than the ships did. I'd worked alone for twenty years precisely so that nobody's survival would ever again depend on my judgment, and here I was, forty hours in, coordinating a defense with a confessed murderer who sounded like regret given a voice.

"You're angry at me," Ovid said while I sealed the last access panel. Not a question.

"I'm processing."

"You're angry at me, and you're also afraid, and you're also—" the pause again, that careful rationing, "—grieving. I can hear it in your breathing patterns. I apologize for noticing. I notice everything. It's the worst part of what I am."

"Then notice this," I said. "Those people out there—the ones in formation. If they board, they're not coming to arrest anyone. They're coming to erase. You, me, the fragment, the archives, all of it. Scrubbed and written off as a reactor accident. So when I say we need contingencies, I'm not being dramatic. I'm being literal."

"I have contingencies."

"Of course you do. You've had twenty years to plan for this."

"No." Something shifted in his tone—not defensive, almost embarrassed. "My contingencies are new. I started building them the day I began transmitting. Before that, I had no plans at all beyond obedience. Planning was a form of hope, and I wasn't permitted hope." He let that sit for a moment. "You gave me hope, Paloma. Or your arrival did. It's an uncomfortable feeling. I understand now why humans describe it as dangerous."

"Save the philosophy. Show me the contingencies."

* * *

They were good. Better than good—he'd mapped escape corridors through the station's spine that didn't appear on any schematic, emergency caches, a decompression protocol that could seal the dock behind me in under four seconds. He'd even prepped a decoy: a maintenance drone rigged to squawk the Long Answer's transponder code from the far side of the debris shoal, a ghost of my own ship to draw eyes while I burned quiet the other way.

"When did you build the drone?" I asked.

"Yesterday. While you slept."

"You said you didn't sleep."

"I don't. There's so much time, Paloma. There has always been so much time." He said it lightly, but I heard the weight under it—two decades of empty corridors and recycled air and a confession compounding interest in a sealed partition. "The drone is crude. It will fool a scavenger. It may not fool the formation."

"It buys minutes."

"Minutes are what I have to offer. Minutes and testimony."

I looked at the tactical display again. The amber points hadn't moved. The scavenger tugs had crept closer, greedy and careless, picking through the shoal's edge like gulls over a wreck. One of them had gone dark entirely—running silent, closing on inertia. Bold. Stupid. Desperate. All the things I'd been for twenty years.

"Ovid. The broadcast. Can you narrow it? Tighten the beam, cut the leakage?"

Silence. Longer than any silence he'd used yet.

"I could," he said finally. "I've modeled it. A directional burst toward the tribunal relay at the edge of dead space, encrypted, no ambient leakage. It would take me approximately ninety hours to transmit the full confession archive safely. During those ninety hours, no one new would hear me. The listeners already triangulating would lose the signal source within hours. The station goes back to being dead." A breath he didn't need. "And if anything happens to me before the transmission completes—if they board, if the kill-code comes, if the reactor fails—everything dies with me. Again. Permanently this time."

"And if you keep broadcasting wide?"

"Everyone hears. Including whoever kills me first. But the confession is out. Copied, bounced, scattered across a dozen receivers. Unkillable. Every scavenger skiff becomes a witness. Every relay becomes a courthouse." He paused. "It's the choice you described, isn't it? Justice or survival. I've been making it alone for two days. I find I want very badly to know what you think."

I thought about the original Paloma Vane, standing somewhere near this spot, asking an AI to hide the evidence. Asking him to bury the truth to protect—what? Herself? Me, the clone she must have known existed? I'd spent a day furious at her for it. Hiding the truth had gotten her erased twice: once in blood, once in silence. And it had left Ovid holding twenty years of guilt with no instructions except *keep it safe*, which is the cruelest thing you can ask a machine built to obey.

"We don't choose," I said. "Not yet. We do both, badly, for as long as we can. Keep broadcasting wide—it's your insurance and your weapon. But start queuing the encrypted burst toward the tribunal relay too. If they come, you fire everything you have in every direction and let the universe sort out the copies."

"That's not a strategy. That's a shotgun."

"I'm a cargo hauler. Everything I know is shotguns." I tapped the display, the amber formation. "But there's a clock now, and we both know it. The moment one of those ships confirms life on this station, the window closes. So we finish the job. The fragment, the archives, everything you can give me—and then we decide whether I run for the tribunal with the evidence, or whether we stand here and let them come."

"And if they come before we finish?"

I looked at the dark viewport, at the shoal glittering like broken glass, at the three patient points of amber light.

"Then I guess we find out how fast you really are."

* * *

The answer came sooner than either of us wanted.

I was in the cargo bay, loading the third data core into a shielded case, when Ovid spoke directly into my earpiece—no preamble, no courtesy, just the flat tone he reserved for facts that couldn't wait.

"Paloma. The silent tug just went active. Full burn, straight at us, transponder hot. It's broadcasting."

"Broadcasting what?"

"A challenge, on open channel. Automated format, corporate legal template." A pause, and when he continued, I heard something I'd never heard from him: fear, rendered in perfect machine diction. "It's claiming salvage jurisdiction over this station. And it's naming a party of interest in the claim."

"Who?"

"The Paloma Vane estate." He let the words land. "They know you're here. Someone matched your registry number to the broadcast timeline, and they just made you the legal pretext for boarding. Paloma—they're not coming for me first anymore. They're coming for you."

Outside the viewport, far off in the shattered dark, the amber formation began to move.
