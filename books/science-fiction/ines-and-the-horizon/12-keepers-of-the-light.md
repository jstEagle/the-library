## 12. Keepers of the Light

My grandmother used to say that waiting is a skill, like welding or bread-making. You can do it badly — impatiently, resentfully, letting the whole thing burn while you stare at the clock — or you can do it well, with your hands busy and your heart steady and your eyes on the door.

The Keepers of Relay Nine had been waiting for forty years. Some of us had gotten very good at it. Others, like me, had been born into the middle of the sentence and were still trying to figure out what the beginning was.

I was on watch rotation when the ship came in — a battered ore hauler running dark until the last possible moment, then blinking its transponder like a man knocking politely on a door he'd been told was condemned. I remember thinking: *that's either a ghost or an idiot.* It turned out to be both, which is the most common combination in this system.

"Relay Nine, this is the freighter *Kestrel's Folly*," said a voice over the comm, dry as vacuum. "Requesting dock. We come in peace, more or less."

I keyed the channel. "You named your ship after yourself?"

A pause. "It came with the hull."

That was my introduction to Kestrel Ravensworth. I mention it because everyone else in this story will tell you he arrived solemn and haunted, a man carrying forty years of grief in a duffel bag. And maybe he did. But the first thing he ever said to me was a joke about his own ship's name, and I've always thought that mattered. Grief that can still make jokes is grief that hasn't won yet.

* * *

We brought them through the airlock in ones and twos, because Relay Nine was built for maintenance crews of twelve and we were thirty-one, and hospitality here means pretending you have room when you absolutely do not. I took point on the tour, mostly because Elder Maren was busy and because I'd volunteered to be the friendly face before anyone could assign me something worse, like plumbing.

Let me describe our home for you, since outsiders always get it wrong. They expect a derelict — cold corridors, flickering lights, skeletons at consoles. What they find instead is a place that looks *lived in*, because it is. We've got hydroponics in what used to be coolant bays. We've got a schoolroom with a chalkboard someone salvaged from a mining platform two decades back. We've got murals on the bulkheads, painted by three generations of people who never saw Earth and never saw the Horizon and still call both of them *home*. The lights don't flicker because we're dying. They flicker because Tam — no, not their Tam, our Tam, there are apparently two Tams now, the universe has a sense of humor — because our Tam keeps rewiring things without telling anyone.

The visitors walked through it all with the expressions of people reading a letter addressed to someone else. The engineer — Odile Voss, though she didn't offer the name right away; I recognized her from the scandal feeds my mother's generation still gossips about — kept touching walls like she was checking them for lies. The kid, Tam-the-younger, drifted toward our server racks with the naked hunger only teenagers and hackers have, and honestly those are the same hunger. And Brannoc Hale stood by the airlock with his arms crossed, wearing the face of a man cataloguing exits.

But Kestrel stopped at the memorial wall.

Every station has one, I suppose. Ours runs the length of the old observation corridor: four thousand names, hand-painted, refreshed every year by whoever lost the lottery. Names of the crew and passengers of the *Horizon*, the generation ship that vanished forty years ago with eleven thousand souls aboard. Except — and this is the thing, this is the thing my grandmother died knowing and I was raised knowing and the rest of the galaxy has never once suspected —

Except it didn't vanish.

Kestrel stood in front of that wall for a long time. Long enough that I got uncomfortable, and I'm the patient one. His fingers hovered over the names without touching, the way you'd hover over a stove burner you knew was hot.

"You knew them," he said finally. Not a question.

"My grandmother was born aboard," I said. "She was six when they jumped. She used to say she could still smell the orchard deck." I hesitated, then decided he'd earned the rest. "She kept a list. Everyone she remembered. She made me memorize it when I was small. Said someday someone would come asking, and I should be ready to answer."

He turned and looked at me, and I watched him decide whether to believe me. I know that look. Outsiders always do the math — *thirty-one people, forty years, a relay station at the edge of dead space* — and come up with either cult or con. I let him look. The truth holds up under inspection better than any lie I could build.

"Why would you wait?" he asked. "Forty years. For what?"

Elder Maren answered from behind him, which is her gift, arriving exactly when questions get too big for juniors. She's ninety-one and moves like gravity owes her money. "For a translator," she said. "Obviously."

* * *

We fed them first. This is policy, not sentiment — nobody thinks clearly hungry, and what we had to tell them required clear thinking. So there was a strange, tense meal in the mess hall, real greens from hydroponics and protein grown in vats older than the kid, and I watched our people and their people circle each other like dogs deciding whether to fight or form a pack. Our Tam taught their Tam a card game. Odile Voss ended up elbow-deep in our water reclamation unit within twenty minutes, muttering about crimes against fluid dynamics, and I thought: *well, she'll fit.*

Then Maren cleared her throat, and the hall went quiet the way it does when she clears it, and she told them the story. I've heard it a hundred times. I'll hear it a hundred more. But I watched Kestrel hear it for the first time, and that's worth writing down.

"The *Horizon* didn't vanish," Maren said. "Ships don't vanish. Ships get *taken*."

She laid it out plain, the way our elders always have, because ornament is for people with time. Forty years ago, the *Horizon* made its scheduled jump out of Halcyon space — the first long-range jump in history, the pride of the system, eleven thousand colonists bound for Gliese orbit. The jump core was new technology. Revolutionary. Worth more than a planet's GDP, if planets had GDPs, which ours barely do.

"The Aurelian Combine held the core's patent," Maren said. "And the Combine had a problem. A working jump drive meant open borders. Meant anyone with a hull could leave, could trade outside Combine tariffs, could escape Combine contracts. Eleven thousand debt-bonded laborers walking free into the black? Unacceptable."

"So they hijacked it," Brannoc said flatly. Not shocked. He'd worked for them; shock wasn't in his toolkit anymore.

"They hijacked it," Maren confirmed. "Mid-jump. A second vessel — Combine-built, Combine-crewed — locked tractor fields onto the *Horizon* and dragged it off its jump vector. Out here. To dead space. Where no one would ever look, because no one looks for things in dead space; that's rather the point of it."

"And the people?" Kestrel asked. His voice had gone very quiet. I recognized the quality of it. It's the voice of someone standing on a floor they no longer trust.

"Some died in the taking. The fields aren't gentle." Maren's voice didn't waver, but her hands folded tighter in her lap. "The rest — the survivors, thousands of them — were left aboard. Stranded. Life support failing. Comm array burned out — sabotaged, we'd learn later, by a failsafe written into the ship's own systems. The Combine reported the *Horizon* lost with all hands. A tragedy. A mystery. A cautionary tale about the dangers of open jumping. Very useful, a story like that."

"And the survivors' descendants," Odile said slowly, "are *you*."

"Our grandparents escaped in the lifeboats during the first week, before the Combine finished stripping the ship's outer systems. A few hundred of them. They made it here, to this relay — it was a fueling waypoint even then, half-abandoned. And they stayed." Maren spread her hands. "Not because they couldn't leave. Because leaving meant agreeing that the *Horizon* was gone. And it isn't gone. It's *out there*, past the quarantine line, drifting with however many souls still breathe aboard it. And every one of our families has someone on that list."

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight and temperature.

Then Kestrel said, "The signal. The nine seconds. That was them."

"That was them," I said, because Maren nodded at me, and this part was mine to tell. "The comm array's dead — has been for forty years. But the *Horizon* is still a ship, and ships have mass, and mass speaks gravity whether you want it to or not. Someone aboard figured out how to modulate the drive's gravitational field. To make the ship itself shout." I looked at Kestrel directly. "In Vessic. Because human radio gets monitored, intercepted, erased. But who monitors gravity? And who alive can read Vessic syntax?"

His face did something complicated. I gave him privacy by looking at my plate.

"We received fragments over the years," Maren continued. "Whispers. Never enough. The pattern needs a mind that dreams in that language to hold it whole. Our grandparents waited for such a mind. My generation waited. Now Sefa's generation waits." She smiled, and it was not a warm smile. "Patience is our inheritance. So is anger. We keep both in good repair."

* * *

Afterward, the hall broke into clusters — our people and theirs, trading stories across the gap of forty years like neighbors leaning over a fence that shouldn't exist. I found myself with Kestrel at the memorial wall again, because apparently that was where he lived now.

"Your grandmother," he said. "The list she made you memorize."

"Ines Merav," I said. "Deck horticulturalist. She grew the oranges." I recited it the way I'd recited it at six years old, kneeling by her cot while she drilled me: name, trade, something loved. "Name, trade, something loved. That was her format. She said a person is all three."

Kestrel was silent for a moment. Then: "There was a woman aboard the *Horizon*. An engineer. Amara Ravensworth."

I knew the name before he finished saying it. Of course I did. Every Keeper knows the names on the wall, and some names we know better than others, because our grandparents spoke them like prayers.

"She was my mother," Kestrel said.

I looked up at him. Here's the thing about growing up in a community built around a wound: you learn to recognize when someone new has just discovered the wound is theirs too. It happened once a decade, maybe. A trader with a grandmother's ring. A drifter who turned out to carry a surname from the manifest. Each time, the whole station rearranged itself around that person for a week, feeding them, telling them stories, stitching them into the fabric whether they wanted it or not.

But this was different. This man hadn't just lost someone on the *Horizon*. He'd received its dying signal in a language only he could read. He'd crossed half the system chasing a ghost and found a wall with her name on it, painted by my grandmother's hand.

"She helped design the jump core," he went on, almost to himself. "Her handwriting is in the schematics. I found it in the signal fragment. I thought—" He stopped. Started again. "I thought I was chasing a wreck."

"You're not chasing a wreck," I said. "You're chasing a ship full of people who have been shouting your name into the dark for forty years. Well. Not your name specifically. But names. Thousands of them." I shrugged, aiming for wry and landing somewhere near fierce. "We're the ones who kept the light on. You're the one who finally heard it."

He laughed — a short, cracked sound, surprise more than humor. "You rehearse that?"

"Grandmother made me practice the whole speech when I was nine. 'Someday the translator will come, Sefa, and you must be ready.'" I rolled my eyes at the memory. "I thought she was being dramatic. She was Vessic-taught; drama was her native tongue."

Something moved through his face at the word *Vessic-taught* — a flinch, a hunger, hard to say which. I filed it away. I'm good at filing things away; it's the other half of waiting.

* * *

Late that night — station night, which is a decision we make collectively via the light schedule — I couldn't sleep, so I did what insomniac Keepers do: I walked the observation corridor. And I wasn't surprised to find Kestrel there, because where else. He stood at the great viewport, looking out at nothing, which out here is a lot to look at.

"Can't sleep?" I asked.

"I'm trying to remember how many people fit in a lifeboat," he said. "If a few hundred escaped, and the ship carried eleven thousand..." He didn't finish. He didn't have to. The arithmetic hung there, ugly and hopeful at once: *thousands might still be alive.* Forty years of survival against odds, aboard a stripped hulk in dead space, tending gardens in coolant bays and teaching children a language of patience.

"It's why the signal matters," I said. "Why it had to be an invitation, not a distress call. A distress call gets rescue teams, maybe. Eventually. If budgets allow." I leaned on the rail beside him. "An invitation gets *witnesses*. Gets the truth broadcast. Gets the Combine's crime dragged into the light where it can't be buried twice."

"The Combine will come for it," he said quietly. "For the signal. For us. They erased the *Horizon* once."

"Mm." I'd been expecting this conversation. "Brannoc Hale says their hunter ships are already sniffing the jump lanes. He'd know; he used to fly one." I turned to look at Kestrel, and I put away the wry, because this next part deserved a straight face. "Here's what my grandmother taught me about the Combine, translator. They think ownership is the same thing as reality. They erase a ship, they believe the ship is erased. They buy a government, they believe the truth is bought. They have never once in forty years imagined that a few hundred refugees on a broken relay would spend four decades raising children whose entire inheritance is *remembering*."

"And when they come?"

"Then they'll find out what remembering is worth." I smiled at the viewport, at all that patient dark. "They're coming for a signal. What they'll meet is a family. Ours, yours, whatever this is becoming. We've had forty years to practice being underestimated."

Kestrel was quiet a long moment. When he spoke, his voice had changed — steadier, but underneath it, something raw.

"There's a woman on that ship," he said. "Or there was. My mother's crewmate. Sefa — the signal, the invitation, it was structured like a message chain, and one thread repeated, over and over, like a name being called." He exhaled. "I think they're calling for her. I think she's alive."

"Then we'd best make sure the Combine doesn't reach her first," I said.

As if the universe had been waiting for its cue — and I swear on my grandmother's orange trees this is true — the station's proximity alarm sang out, soft and terrible, the sound we'd rehearsed responding to for forty years without once hearing for real.

Contact. Jump signature. Coming in fast.

Maren's voice came over the all-station, calm as bread rising: "Keepers to posts. Guests to shelter. It seems," and here even her iron voice paused, just briefly, "our wait is over."

I looked at Kestrel. He was already moving.

"Welcome to the family," I told his back. "We do things dramatically. It's genetic."
