## 3. What Grandmother Buried

The signal had stopped at dawn.

Casper had sat through the whole of the night with her grandmother's grammar primers spread across the console like the wings of a shot bird, and somewhere between the last page of the third primer and the first grey light leaking down the stairwell, the frequency had gone quiet again. Not dead—she could still feel it, a pressure behind the static, like someone standing on the other side of a door, breathing. But quiet. As if whoever spoke Old Meridian across that impossible distance had said what they meant to say and was now waiting to see whether anyone alive still knew how to listen.

She had transcribed everything. Fourteen minutes of signal, rendered in her own careful hand into a notebook she'd bought nine years ago for grocery lists and never used. The glyphs looked strange in ballpoint ink, too clean, too modern—a dead language wearing living clothes. She'd copied them three times, cross-referenced them against the primers until her eyes burned, and by the time the sun cleared the eastern ridge and laid its long gold bar across the vault floor, she had fragments. Coordinates. A countdown expressed in some unit she couldn't yet convert. And one word, repeated like a heartbeat through all fourteen minutes:

*Ceasefire.*

The word sat in her chest like a swallowed stone. She had grown up on stories of the Collapse, of course—everyone had—but the old tongue itself was supposed to be gone, burned out of the world along with the people who'd spoken it. Two centuries of silence. And now someone out there was using it to beg for peace, or to demand it, and they were using *her* beacon to do it.

Her beacon. Which raised the question she had been circling since midnight, the way you circle a wound to see how deep it goes.

Why would a message in a dead language come to a seed vault?

Not to a relay. Not to a military listening post, not to the Concord's great orbital arrays with their thousand ears. To a buried agricultural archive on an unremarkable hillside, tended by one woman whose only qualification was a dead grandmother's signature on a very old contract.

Unless the vault wasn't what she thought it was.

Casper stood in the middle of the main hall, morning light falling across the seed racks in dusty columns, and let herself finally say the thought out loud, because saying things aloud made them real and she had spent nine years being very careful about what was real.

"Grandmother," she said to the empty room, "what did you build?"

The room, naturally, said nothing. It had been saying nothing for nine years, and Casper had made a kind of peace with its silence, the way you make peace with weather. But this morning the silence felt different. This morning it felt like the silence of something *withholding*.

She thought about the beacon's architecture—the way she'd always privately found it odd, in the unexamined way you find things odd when they've always been there. The dish array on the surface was oriented not skyward, as any transmitter should be, but angled low along the horizon, cupped toward the deep relay corridors like two hands held out to catch rain. The power draw was enormous for a system that, by every manual in the sublevel office, transmitted nothing but a maintenance ping once per cycle. Nine years of calibration, and she had never once questioned why calibrating a machine to do almost nothing required so much care.

Because it wasn't built to speak. She was almost sure of it now. It was built to *listen*. And if it was built to listen—if her grandmother had constructed a vast, patient ear pointed at the old relay corridors—then the signal hadn't found the vault by accident. It had been *addressed* here. Someone, somewhere, had known this ear existed, and had spoken into it deliberately, in the one language guaranteed to be understood by exactly one person.

The thought made her skin prickle. She went to the office, pulled the employment contract from the drawer where it had lived untouched since her first day, and read it again with new eyes. Standard custodial language. Duties, compensation, succession clauses. But clause eleven—the one her grandmother had insisted on, the lawyer had told her mother, against all convention—clause eleven granted the custodian "full access to all sublevels."

All sublevels.

The vault had two. Sublevel one was operations: climate control, power, the server room where the beacon's systems lived. Casper knew it the way she knew her own hands. Sublevel two was listed on no schematic she'd ever found. When she'd asked about it, years ago, during a supply call, the logistics officer on rotation had checked his records and told her, pleasantly, that the Meridian Vault had one sublevel. She'd assumed he was right. She'd assumed a lot of things.

The access hatch, when she finally found it, was hidden in plain sight, which was somehow worse than if it had been locked away. It was under the floor of the cold-storage annex, beneath a rack of heritage wheat she had inventoried perhaps four hundred times. The wheat was real; the seeds were viable; she'd germinated test batches every spring. But the rack sat on a frame bolted to a steel plate, and the plate, when she knelt and ran her fingers along its edge in the way you check a seam you're suddenly suspicious of, gave a soft pneumatic sigh and lifted.

Cold air rose out of the dark underneath, smelling of dust and machine oil and something older, something like paper.

Casper knelt there for a long moment, her hand on the plate, her heart doing something complicated against her ribs. There was a version of her—the version that had signed that contract at twenty-three, grieving and rootless and grateful for any door that opened—that wanted to close the plate and go back upstairs and inventory wheat and pretend the signal had been interference. That version had kept her sane for nine years. But that version had also been alone in a way she was only now beginning to understand, tending a monument to a woman who had never once, in all those years of letters and recorded instructions, told her the truth about anything that mattered.

"Fine," Casper said quietly, to the dark, to the dead, to herself. "Show me."

She took a lamp, water, and the primers—because whatever was down there, she suspected she'd need them—and climbed down the ladder into the cold.

* * *

Sublevel two was smaller than she'd expected and stranger than she'd feared.

It was not a bunker. It was a study. The stairs opened into a long room lined floor to ceiling with shelving, and the shelves held books—not data slates, not encrypted cores, but actual bound books, hundreds of them, their spines cracked and their pages foxed with age. Old Meridian script ran down most of them. Grammar primers, she realized, dozens of them, generations of them, each more complete than the three her grandmother had left upstairs. Dictionaries. Transcription manuals. A full pedagogy of a language that was supposed to be extinct, preserved the way you'd preserve seeds: carefully, deliberately, against a future someone clearly believed was coming.

Beyond the shelves, the room opened into a workspace. A desk buried under papers. A wall covered in diagrams of the beacon—not the simplified schematics from the operations manual, but the true anatomy of the thing, annotated in her grandmother's small, precise hand. And at the center of the far wall, mounted like a shrine, a single console, dark, with a keyboard whose keys bore characters Casper had spent the last twelve hours learning to read.

She walked the room slowly, touching nothing at first, then unable not to touch everything. Here was a child's exercise book, filled with practice glyphs in a rounder, younger version of her grandmother's handwriting. Here was a photograph she'd never seen: a young woman standing in front of the half-built dish array, squinting into the sun, laughing at whoever held the camera. Her grandmother, decades before Casper was born, before the war, before the silence. On the back, in Old Meridian, a caption Casper could mostly read: *The ear is finished. Now we wait for a voice.*

Now we wait for a voice.

Casper set the photograph down with hands that weren't quite steady and turned to the desk. The papers on top were recent—recent meaning within the last decade, the ink barely faded. Letters, unsent, addressed to her. Her name on each envelope in her grandmother's writing. Dozens of them. She didn't open them. Not yet. She couldn't. Instead she lifted the topmost folder, the one labeled in both scripts, the Old Meridian word for *instruction* sitting above its Concord-common translation like a title above a confession.

Inside was the truth of the beacon, laid out in her grandmother's methodical hand.

It was built to receive. Of course it was. The dish geometry, the power draw, the horizon-cupped orientation—all of it resolved now into sense. The Meridian Vault was not a seed bank that happened to have an antenna. It was an antenna that happened to have a seed bank, a listening station disguised as agriculture, planted in the one place on the surface no one thought worth watching. Her grandmother had built it after the Collapse, in the years before the war hardened into its present shape, when it was still possible to believe that someone might someday want to talk. She had aimed it at the old relay corridors because those corridors were where the survivors of the old world had gone—to ground, to orbit, into the long silence—and she had waited thirty years for a voice.

And then, on the final page of the folder, the instruction. The thing the whole archive existed to deliver. It was written in Old Meridian, dense and formal, and Casper worked through it glyph by glyph, primer open, lips moving, feeling the language assemble itself in her mouth like a muscle remembered from a life she'd never lived.

*If you are reading this, then the voice has come, and you are the one I hoped would hear it. The message carries what I could not say while I lived: the origin of the war, written by those who began it, sealed in the old tongue so that only our own could read it. Broadcast it, and the killing may end. But know this—the message is half a key, and the other half I could not keep. They watch what we write and burn what we hold. So I split it, as our people split their songs, and gave the second half to the wind. It lives now in the keeping of the enemy. Without it, the instruction cannot be completed. Without it, you will hold a door with no handle.*

Casper read it three times. Then a fourth, slower, letting each clause land like a stone dropped into still water.

Half a key. Held by the enemy.

She sat down hard in her grandmother's chair—it was exactly the right height for her, which felt like a joke at her expense—and stared at the wall of diagrams. The betrayal arrived not all at once but in layers, the way cold does. First: her grandmother had known. Had known the war's origin, had carried that knowledge for decades, had chosen silence over disclosure and called it protection. Second: she had built this entire underground cathedral of language and then structured it so that no single person—no, not even her own heir—could ever act on it alone. Third: she had died and left Casper nine years of letters upstairs, letters Casper now understood she could not bring herself to open, because every one of them was probably another beautiful, loving, incomplete sentence.

Her inheritance was a locked door. Her inheritance had *always* been a locked door. The job, the vault, the beacon, the lonely decade of calibration and wheat inventories—all of it a waiting room furnished by a woman who loved her enough to prepare her and trusted her too little to arm her.

"That's not fair," Casper said aloud, and her voice cracked in the middle of it, and she let it. "You could have told me. You could have—" She stopped, because the end of that sentence was a lie and she knew it. *You could have trusted me.* But her grandmother had been hunted, probably; had buried a language and a truth in a hole in the ground because the alternative was graves. Casper pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes and breathed until the breathing evened out.

Then she got up, because grief was a luxury with a countdown attached, and went back to the instruction page. *The second half lives in the keeping of the enemy.* Enemy. In the old tongue the word was specific—it didn't mean foe, it meant *the ones across the water*, the separated kin, the family divided by a flood. Whoever had written the formal Concord-era translations had sanded that word down into hostility. But her grandmother's primers preserved the older grain of it. The enemy was not an adversary. The enemy was the other half of the family.

Which meant the key wasn't lost. It was *elsewhere*. Held by people who still sang in Old Meridian, who still taught it to their children in defiance of two centuries of extinction—which meant the extinction itself had been a story, a cover, maintained on both sides, and her grandmother had known that too, had let Casper grow up believing a language was dead when it was merely hiding.

The signal. Fourteen minutes, coordinates, a countdown, *ceasefire*. If the message was half a key, then the sender needed the other half—and the sender had spoken to *her*, which meant the sender believed the vault's keeper could finish it. Or the sender believed the keeper couldn't, and the countdown was a deadline for finding someone who could.

Either way, she was no longer alone in this. Somewhere out past the relay corridors, someone was waiting on her answer. And Casper understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that the moment she answered—really answered, in Old Meridian, from the vault's great hidden ear—she would stop being invisible. The Concord listened to everything. The Reclamation watched the relays. A transmission in a dead language from a buried vault would travel through every intelligence apparatus in the system like a lit match through dry grass.

She looked around the study one last time—at the primers, the photograph, the shelf of unsent letters—and made herself a promise she wasn't sure she could keep: she would read the letters later, when there was time, when the door had a handle. For now she took the instruction folder, the oldest and most complete dictionary, and the photograph, and climbed back up into the daylight carrying the weight of a woman she was only beginning to understand.

Upstairs, the console's standby light pulsed its slow green rhythm. The frequency behind the static still breathed.

And as Casper set the folder down and reached for the transcription notebook, the beacon did something it had never done in nine years of silence.

It chimed.

One clear tone, deliberate, unmistakable—an incoming handshake, the machine equivalent of a knock at the door. Casper froze, notebook halfway to the table, every hair on her arms rising.

Someone was hailing the vault directly. Someone who knew the protocol.

On the display, in glyphs she could now read, a single line appeared:

*WE KNOW YOU HAVE THE FIRST HALF. WE ARE COMING FOR IT.*
