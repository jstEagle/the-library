## 10. Stolen Hours

The paperwork was the easy part. Ines had spent nine years learning how Concord bureaucracy breathed—when to feed it, when to starve it—and a supervised translation session between a detained civilian consultant and her assigned escort was the kind of request that moved through Halcyon's channels like water through old pipe: slowly, noisily, and without anyone actually looking at it.

She filed it as *linguistic continuity support*. She cited Directive 44-C, which technically existed. She attached Casper's partial translation of the countdown fragment, redacted so heavily it said almost nothing, which was the point. Sef's office countersigned in eleven hours without comment, and Ines understood that too: they were watching her, but they believed she was loyal enough to be useful, and useful people were given rope.

She tried not to think about what she intended to do with the rope.

* * *

The hydroponics wing occupied three tiers of Halcyon's lower ring, a cathedral of green light and recycled air where the station grew forty percent of its own food and one hundred percent of its illusions about self-sufficiency. Ines had been there twice before, both times for security sweeps, and had found it oppressive—the wet smell of growing things, the way the grow-lamps turned everyone's skin slightly gold, like everyone was pretending to be alive.

Casper Eastwick was waiting at a worktable under a trellis of dwarf beans, and the sight of her rearranged something in Ines's chest that she resented immediately.

They'd put her in a grey consultant's jumpsuit that fit badly. Her hair was pushed back behind her ears, and there was soil on her knuckles—she'd been working the beds, because of course she had; give that woman a locked room and she'd find something to plant in it.

"You're late," Casper said, not looking up from the tablet in front of her.

"Your escort had to clear three checkpoints." Ines sat down across from her, set the guard's posture into her spine like a brace. "You've been busy."

"Beans don't wait." Casper slid the tablet across. On it, the countdown fragment glowed in Old Meridian glyphs above her working transliteration. "I made progress on the deadline language. I thought you'd want to see it."

"That's what this is," Ines said. "A work session."

"That's what this is," Casper agreed, and looked up, and her eyes were doing the thing they did—steady, amused, seeing straight through the uniform to whatever Ines was trying to hide underneath it. "How's the coffee in the officers' ward?"

"Worse than yours."

"Then you're suffering. Good."

Ines almost smiled. She caught it at the last second, converted it into a professional nod, and pulled her chair closer to the table anyway. The camera in the corner of the tier watched them with its patient red eye. She'd checked its field of view before sitting down. It saw the table, the tablet, their hands. It did not see their faces well, and it did not see what passed between them at all.

That was the thing about surveillance, Ines had learned: it recorded everything except meaning.

* * *

For two hours they worked, and it was real work. Casper had cracked another eleven glyphs using the grammar primers' frequency tables, and together they assembled the sentence that had been haunting the message's opening:

*Before the fortieth day turns, the voice must speak whole, or the sleeping ships will wake unsent.*

"The sleeper fleet," Casper murmured. "It's real. Grandmother wasn't warning them about an enemy fleet. She was warning them about ours—or theirs. Someone's. Ships that launch if the truth doesn't come out in time."

"A dead-man's switch," Ines said. "Built into a ceasefire."

"Built into an apology." Casper rubbed her eyes with the heel of her hand, leaving a faint smear of soil on her cheekbone. "Whoever wrote this wanted peace so badly they were willing to hold a gun to it. That's—" She stopped. Shook her head. "That's my grandmother. That's exactly who she was."

Ines watched her grieve in that quiet, sideways way she had, grief folded into analysis like a letter slipped into a report, and felt the guard's posture slip a centimeter.

"Tell me about her," Ines said. "Not the founder. The grandmother."

Casper looked up, surprised. Then, slowly, she told her. About a woman who sang off-key while she calibrated instruments. Who kept a jar of surface dirt on the vault's console like a relic. Who left without explanation when Casper was seven and came back in a box eight years later, and the vault along with her, and a nine-year-old girl inherited a silence the size of a mountain.

"She never talked about before the Collapse," Casper said. "Except once. I asked her what Earth smelled like, and she got this look—like I'd asked her to open a wound—and she said, *'Wet. Everything smelled wet.'*" Casper laughed, a small broken sound. "Two hundred years in orbit and that's what survived. Wet."

"My father said the same thing," Ines said, before she could stop herself.

The words hung there. Casper went very still.

"He was Reclamation?" Casper asked carefully. "Surface-born?"

"Born in the Kestrel basin, before the flooding took the lowlands. He came up in the second evacuation wave. Worked hull maintenance his whole life." Ines heard herself continuing, helpless against it, like a door coming off its hinges. "He died in the Meridian Corridor action. Eleven years ago. A Reclamation mine took his skiff. They never recovered—" She stopped. Recalibrated. "They sent me his tool kit. That was what came back. His tools."

Casper didn't say *I'm sorry*. Ines braced for it anyway, and was grateful when it didn't come. Instead Casper reached across the table and turned the tablet face-down, so the dead language couldn't watch them, and said:

"What did he grow? Before he came up. Everyone from the surface grows something."

"Tomatoes," Ines said, and her throat closed around the word. "Ugly ones. He used to say the pretty ones were bred by people with nothing better to do."

The red eye of the camera blinked its patient blink. Somewhere above them, forty days ticked down toward zero, and two fleets drifted toward a war neither of them had chosen, and Ines Varga sat under dwarf bean vines in a grey jumpsuit's shadow and talked about tomatoes with the enemy's translator, and understood, with the calm of a woman reading her own court-martial, that she was already lost.

* * *

The second meeting took six days to arrange and cost her a favor she'd been saving for four years—a debt owed by a scheduling clerk named Oyelaran who now considered them even and would not meet her eyes in the corridor anymore.

This time the session was logged as *extended linguistic review, overnight authorization*, justified by the countdown's accelerating decryption schedule. Sef signed it himself. That frightened Ines more than a refusal would have. He was giving her room to move, the way a hunter gives a snare room to close.

She decided not to care. There would be time to care later, or there wouldn't.

The wing at night was a different country. The grow-lamps dimmed to a deep agricultural red, and the plants breathed out their slow oxygen, and the station's hum dropped below hearing. Casper was waiting at the same table, but she'd brought things: a battered thermos, two tin cups, and a paper packet that she opened to reveal actual dried leaves.

"Don't ask where I got these," she said. "The botany staff owe me. I fixed their irrigation scheduler and identified a blight in tier two before it spread."

"You've been here five days and you're already running the place."

"I'm not running it. I'm just the only person who talks to the plants." She poured tea that smelled of something green and wild, nothing like the standardized rations of Concord life. "Sit down, Varga. You look like you haven't slept."

"I haven't."

"Good. Me neither. We'll be useless together."

They drank tea under red light, and the work happened in fits and starts—an hour of genuine progress on the abort-code preamble, then long silences that weren't empty but full, the kind of silence Ines had only ever known alone and hadn't known could be shared.

It was Casper who broke one of them, quietly, turning her cup in her hands.

"When did you know? About your father. That he was gone."

"They told me at 0400. Standard notification protocol. An officer I'd never met read a statement off a screen." Ines stared into the dark red distance of the tier. "I remember thinking the statement was very well written. Whoever writes those has practice. And then I remember being furious that I was thinking about the writing instead of—" Her jaw tightened. "Grief has bad manners. Mine showed up late and in the wrong clothes."

"And after?"

"After, I enlisted. Which everyone called brave and was actually just grief looking for a job." She glanced at Casper. "Your turn. What did losing her make you do?"

"Hide," Casper said simply. "Nine years in a hole in a mountain talking to seeds. Grief with excellent manners. Very polite. Never interrupts." She smiled, and it didn't reach anywhere near her eyes. "Turns out it was just waiting for someone to arrive."

The red light lay across her face like a sunset that had forgotten to end. Ines became aware, with terrible precision, of the distance between their hands on the table—thirty centimeters, maybe less, a distance she could cross in half a second and never uncross.

"This is a mistake," Ines said.

"Yes," Casper agreed.

"We're being watched. Maybe not right now, maybe not this camera, but Sef watches everything, and everything we are is evidence."

"Yes."

"If anyone finds out, you lose whatever protection detention gives you, and I lose—" She gestured vaguely at her whole life. "All of it. Career, clearance, probably freedom."

"I know." Casper set down her cup. "And yet you engineered an unsupervised overnight session with a detainee, and you're sitting here drinking contraband tea, and you haven't touched the abort-code preamble in forty minutes." Her voice was gentle and merciless. "So say the true thing, Varga. You're good at true things. You deleted nothing at Kestrel. I've seen how you are with true things."

Ines looked at her—at the soil still somehow under her fingernails even in orbital detention, at the stubbornness in the set of her mouth, at the loneliness that matched Ines's own like two halves of a cipher key—and every calculation she had ever run came back with the same result, and the result was this.

She leaned across the table and kissed her.

It lasted perhaps two seconds. Casper's mouth was warm and tasted of the strange tea, and her hand came up and caught the front of Ines's uniform—not pulling her closer, just holding on, anchoring—and then Ines broke away as if scalded, chair scraping, heart slamming against her ribs like something trying to get out.

"I'm sorry," she said. "That was—I shouldn't have—"

"No," Casper said, breathless, upright, eyes wide in the red light. "You shouldn't have."

Silence. The plants breathed. Forty days became thirty-nine somewhere far above them, indifferent.

And then Casper crossed the thirty centimeters herself, took Ines's face in both soil-rough hands, and kissed her again—slower, deliberate, a correction to a first draft—and Ines stopped regretting anything at all.

When they finally parted, foreheads nearly touching, Casper laughed softly against her cheek.

"Well," she said. "Now we're really compromised."

"Now we're really compromised," Ines agreed, and found, to her astonishment, that she was smiling, wide and unguarded, in full view of a camera she'd forgotten existed.

* * *

She remembered the camera eleven minutes later, halfway through a third cup of tea with Casper's shoulder warm against hers, and went cold all over.

"It's fine," Casper said, when she said it aloud. "Night cycle. Tier cameras log to the agriculture subnetwork, and the ag subnetwork archives on a ninety-day rotation nobody audits unless there's a crop failure. I checked my first day."

"You checked the camera coverage your first day in detention."

"I checked *everything* my first day." Casper's smile turned wry. "My grandmother raised me paranoid. It's the family inheritance—along with the vault, the guilt, and the world-ending message."

But Ines couldn't shake the cold. She knew Sef. She knew how he hunted—not with alarms, but with patience; not by catching you, but by letting you go exactly as far as he wanted and then closing the ledger. An overnight authorization signed personally. A favor from a scheduling clerk that had come through *too easily*. The corridor outside the wing, empty tonight in a way stations never were.

She said none of this. She stayed until the shift bell, walked Casper back to her quarters under the pretense of escort protocol, and stood in the doorway a moment longer than protocol allowed, memorizing her.

"Thirty-nine days," Casper whispered.

"Thirty-nine days," Ines said. "We'll finish the translation. We'll find a way."

She walked back through the sleeping green tiers with her pulse still unsteady, and it wasn't until she reached her own cabin that she found the message waiting on her private terminal, priority-flagged, from Director Sef's office.

No accusation. No summons. Just a single line, courteous as a blade laid flat on a table:

*Translator Eastwick's access privileges will be reviewed tomorrow at 0900. Please ensure all session materials are submitted beforehand. — S.*

Below it, a calendar reminder she had never created: *0900 — Review.*

He knew. Or he suspected enough to tighten the screw, and either way the clock on her stolen hours had just started running faster than the clock on the war.

Ines sat down slowly in the dark of her cabin, father's ghost and new love and old duty all crowded into the small space, and began—to her own complete lack of surprise—to plan a theft.
