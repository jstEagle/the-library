## 13. Down to the Surface

The skiff came apart in stages, and Casper learned each one by sound.

First the port stabilizer went—a shriek through the hull like a knife drawn along glass—then the atmosphere itself began to argue with them, thickening from vacuum-silence into a roar that pressed against her eardrums and made the deck plates tremble like a struck bell. Ines had the controls and did not look away from them once, not even when a warning panel burst into red light and spat sparks across the ceiling.

"Casper." Her voice was flat, professional, terrifyingly calm. "Strap in. Hard."

Casper was already strapped in. She had been since they'd cleared Halcyon's debris field with two Concord interceptors peeling off their tail and Ines burning every reserve the maintenance skiff had ever been issued. She gripped the harness anyway, knuckles white, because holding something seemed better than holding nothing.

"How bad?" she asked.

Ines's jaw worked. Below them, through the forward viewport, the surface of Earth rolled into view—and it was nothing like the maps in the vault's archive, nothing like the soft blue marble on the old Concord recruitment posters. It was brown and gray and scarred, continents veined with burn scars that had not healed in two hundred years, oceans the color of gunmetal. The Reclamation's territory spread beneath them like an open wound someone had tried to stitch with roads.

"Bad," Ines said. "We lost the heat shielding on the left side during the debris run. I can't come down gentle. I can only come down *less fast*."

"Less fast is fine," Casper said. "Less fast is my favorite speed."

It was meant to be a joke. It came out as a prayer. Ines glanced at her—one second, maybe less—and whatever she saw in Casper's face made her reach across the console without looking, finding Casper's hand and squeezing it once, hard, before returning both hands to the stick.

"If we make it down," Ines said, "we're in Reclamation territory. Deep in it. They'll find us before we find anything else."

"I know."

"They'll shoot first. They've been shooting first for nine years."

"I know that too." Casper watched the ground swell beneath them. "But Ines—we're carrying half a message written in their language. Their founding tongue. Somebody down there might still remember what it sounds like."

"Somebody down there might remember we're Concord," Ines said grimly. "That memory tends to be more durable."

Then the atmosphere took the skiff fully into its hands, and there was no more time for talking.

* * *

Falling felt, absurdly, like the beacon room at Meridian: the same shaking, the same roar, the same sense of being a small thing inside a machine much larger than herself. Casper had spent nine years learning to be calm inside noise. She closed her eyes and did it now.

She thought about her grandmother's grammar primers, the ones with the burned edges, the ones she'd carried out of the vault in a canvas bag against direct orders. She thought about the phrase she'd translated in the relay station's flickering light—the word that had changed everything, the word that had gotten them shot at across half the system:

*ceasefire.*

Old Meridian didn't have a word for goodbye, her grandmother had written in the margin of one primer, in handwriting like bird tracks. It had a word for *until*, which was considered more honest.

The skiff hit atmosphere turbulence and dropped thirty meters like a stone let go of a sling. Casper's stomach arrived late to the rest of her body. Somewhere below them, a plain of ash-colored grass and shattered concrete rushed upward with terrible patience.

"Ines—" she started.

"I see it!" Ines hauled the stick hard over. The skiff groaned, rolled, and for one long moment they were flying sideways through the smoke layer, and Casper saw the ground through the side viewport: a river, silver-brown and sluggish; a ruined town with its roofs caved in like broken eggshells; and then, impossibly, green—a field of it, dense and deliberate, row upon row of crops planted in the bones of a city block.

Someone was farming here. Someone had planted things in the ruin.

Then the skiff's remaining wing caught a thermal shear, the nose swung, and the world became impact.

* * *

She woke to the smell of rain and burnt metal.

For a while she didn't move. She lay in the harness, upside down or nearly so, blood pooling warm in her cheeks, and listened. Dripping. Wind moving through grass. A ticking sound from the engine, cooling. And underneath all of it, distant but unmistakable: voices. Human voices, speaking a clipped, accented trade-tongue, getting closer.

Casper unclipped the harness and fell three feet onto the ceiling, which was now the floor. Pain bloomed along her ribs. She ignored it. "Ines?"

A groan from the cockpit. Then movement, and Ines Varga pulled herself into the wreckage of the cabin, hair loose, a cut above her eyebrow sheeting blood into her eye. She looked, Casper thought wildly, like a woman who had lost an argument with a planet and was considering a rematch.

"You're alive," Ines said.

"Barely. You?"

"Concord training has opinions about dying in crashes." Ines wiped her face with her sleeve, leaving a red smear. "Can you walk?"

"I can limp convincingly."

"Good. Because in about ninety seconds this wreck is going to have company, and we want to be *near* it, not in it. Wreckage is cover. Corpses are just corpses."

They got out through the torn cargo hatch, Casper clutching the canvas bag of primers to her chest like a child, and made it perhaps forty meters into the tall dry grass before the voices resolved into shapes. Six of them. Surface soldiers in mismatched armor, scavenged plate over woven ballistic cloth, carrying rifles that looked older than the war and better maintained than most Concord equipment. They fanned out around the smoking skiff with a competence that made Ines mutter something unflattering under her breath.

Casper raised her hands. After a moment's hesitation, Ines raised hers too—higher, slower, the gesture of someone who knew exactly how surrender worked and hated every part of it.

The soldiers saw them immediately. Rifles came up. Orders were barked in the trade-tongue, sharp and rhythmic, and Casper understood none of the words but all of the meaning: *down. Kneel. Hands where we can see them.*

They knelt. The grass scratched at Casper's knees. Her ribs screamed. One of the soldiers approached, a broad woman with a scarred jaw and eyes the flat gray of the sky, and went through Ines's pockets with rough efficiency. She found Ines's Concord identification chit, held it up to the light, and read it aloud. The other soldiers' expressions hardened into something older than anger. Something like grief wearing armor.

"Concord," the woman said, in heavily accented trade-tongue, spitting the word like a seed husk. "Orbital. You fell out of the sky onto our ground with their mark on you." She turned to Casper, rifled through the canvas bag before Casper could stop her, and pulled out the topmost primer.

And stopped.

The soldier stared at the page. At the dense, angular script covering it, the flowing Old Meridian characters that Casper had spent months learning to read by lamplight. The woman's thumb moved across the page—not reading, Casper realized. Tracing. The way you touch something you recognize from a dream.

"Where did you get this?" the soldier asked. Her voice had changed entirely. All the armor had gone out of it.

"It was my grandmother's," Casper said carefully. "I keep the Meridian Seed Vault. The beacon there received a signal in this language. I taught myself to read it from these books."

The soldiers exchanged looks. One of them, a young man barely old enough for the rifle he held, said something quickly in a language that was *not* trade-tongue—and Casper's whole body went cold, because she couldn't understand the words, but she understood the shape of them. The cadence. The falling endings. The rhythm of Old Meridian, spoken aloud, alive, in the mouth of a twenty-year-old with dirt under his fingernails.

Two hundred years dead. Except it wasn't. It had just gone underground. It had gone home.

The scarred woman crouched in front of Casper, rifle slung, and studied her face with unsettling directness. Up close Casper could see the age in her—fifty, maybe more, old enough to have buried people in this war, old enough to be tired of it in a way the young ones hadn't earned yet.

"The signal," the woman said. "From your beacon. You decoded it."

"Fragments. There's a key missing. Half of it." Casper's heart was hammering so hard she was sure the woman could hear it. "The message says ceasefire. It says coordinates. It says there's a countdown. And it's signed—" She hesitated. She had kept this close to her chest since the relay station, since Ines, since everything. But kneeling in the ash-grass with a rifle's shadow across her shoulders, lying suddenly seemed like the most expensive thing she could do.

"It's signed with a name," Casper said. "Mara Eastwick."

The silence that followed was total. Even the wind seemed to hold its breath.

The young soldier sat down in the grass. He didn't seem to decide to; his legs simply stopped holding him. The scarred woman went very still, and then Casper saw her lips move, silently, shaping the name the way you shape the name of the dead.

"Mara," another soldier whispered. Not Eastwick. Just Mara. First name only, the way you say the names of saints.

"She was born here," Casper said quietly. It wasn't a question anymore. It had never really been a question. "Wasn't she. Down on the surface. Before she went up."

The scarred woman looked at her for a long moment. "My grandmother sang songs about her," she said. "Every child here learns them. We thought they were only songs." She rose, and when she spoke again her voice carried the weight of command, though Casper would learn later she was no officer—just the eldest, which on the surface amounted to the same thing. "Marshal Oduya will want to see you. Both of you." Her eyes flicked to Ines, and hardened again, but only a little. "Even the orbital one. Especially the orbital one, if she helped you carry that book."

"She carried me," Casper said. "Out of orbit. Past her own people's guns. She's burned everything she had to get this message home."

"Home," the woman repeated, tasting the word. She looked out over the ruined town, the impossible green fields planted in its bones, the gray sky where the Concord's stations hung like knives. "You orbital people call everything home. Up there, home is wherever you put your furniture." She shouldered her rifle and jerked her chin toward the horizon. "Walk. It's a day's march to the Marshal's camp, and the rain is coming, and I would very much like to hear the rest of what Mara Eastwick's blood has to say for itself."

They walked. Ines stayed close enough that their shoulders touched, and once, when the young soldier wasn't watching, her hand found Casper's and held on.

Behind them, the skiff smoked against the sky like a fallen star, and ahead, somewhere past the hills, a marshal was waiting who could tell Casper who her grandmother had really been—and why she had fled the singing, the songs, the grieving kin, to die alone beside a silent beacon in the dark.

The rain began to fall, soft and gray, on ground that remembered everything.
