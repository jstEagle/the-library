## 21. Seeds for the Broken Ground

### Casper

The vault had never been designed for crowds, and in the nine years Casper had kept it, it had never held more than one. Now there were eleven people in the atrium, and the sound of them—the scrape of boots on the old tile, the murmur of voices, someone laughing softly at something someone else had said—was so unfamiliar that she kept pausing mid-task just to listen, like a woman hearing rain after a long drought and not quite believing it would continue.

The atrium had been her grandmother's idea, according to the original schematics Casper had found in the sublevel archive: a glass-roofed chamber at the vault's heart where the founders had intended to grow living specimens alongside the frozen ones. *A vault of seeds should not be a tomb*, the schematics read, in Old Meridian, in her grandmother's careful hand. For two centuries the atrium had been dark, its soil compacted, its irrigation lines choked with mineral scale. Casper had spent four months bringing it back. Her hands still smelled faintly of the vinegar she'd used to dissolve the scale, no matter how many times she washed them.

Today the first beds would be planted.

She stood at the head of the long worktable, trays laid out in rows, and tried to remember how to speak to more than one person at a time. "These are the surface lots," she said, and her voice came out rusty, and someone—Ines, of course—smiled at her from the far end of the table as if to say *keep going, you're doing fine*. "Heritage millet from the Meridian basin. Sorghum bred for shallow soil. The legumes are older—pre-Collapse strains. They'll fix nitrogen in ground that's been burned, which means they go in first, and everything else waits on them."

A Reclamation agronomist named Toma, a broad-shouldered woman with soil permanently worked into the creases of her knuckles, leaned over the millet tray and made a sound low in her throat that Casper initially took for disapproval. Then she realized it was something closer to grief. "My grandmother talked about this millet," Toma said. "She remembered when it still grew. She said you could smell a field of it from a kilometer away." She picked up a single seed, golden-brown, hardly bigger than a grain of sand, and held it in her palm like a small dead bird. "We thought all of it was gone."

"It was gone from the surface," Casper said. "That's what a vault is. It's not a miracle. It's just—refusing to throw something away."

She hadn't meant it to land the way it did. The room went quiet in a particular way, the way rooms had gone quiet all year when someone said something that applied to more than seeds. Toma closed her hand around the grain and nodded once, slowly, and Casper understood that she had just handed this woman something heavier than a seed, and that Toma had accepted it.

Nine months since the broadcast. Nine months since the whole system had heard her grandmother's voice—dead two hundred years, alive in a recording, confessing the false-flag attack that had started the war—and since the fleets had gone quiet not with a treaty's fanfare but with the terrible, shuffling silence of people who had learned, all at once, what they were actually fighting for. The tribunals were still running. Sef was still in custody. The war was not over; it was *unhappening*, slowly, the way a knot comes undone when you stop pulling on it and start picking at the right thread.

And the seeds were the thread Casper knew how to pick.

"First distribution goes out Thursday," she said, because the silence had gone on long enough and someone had to be practical, that had always been her job. "Two hundred kilos of mixed stock to the Kessari burn zone. The orbital farms get their allocation the same day—yes, Toma, the same day. I know what you're going to say."

"I was going to say," Toma said, with the ghost of a grin, "that feeding the people who bombed us feels like a strange way to spend a victory."

"It's not a victory," Casper said. "It's a delivery. And the people in the orbital farms didn't bomb anyone any more than the people in the burn zones did. The people who did this are mostly old men in rooms, and most of them are being dealt with." She looked down at the trays, at the thousands of small sleeping futures, and heard her own grandmother in her mouth. "My grandmother kept seeds for both sides. She didn't know which side would survive to need them. That wasn't cynicism. That was hope with its eyes open."

Toma looked at her for a long moment. Then she picked up a tray and carried it to the first bed, and the room moved, and the planting began.

Casper worked beside them for an hour before she noticed Ines had drifted to her side, hands dark with soil, hair tied back with the same frayed cord she'd used to tie back her hair on the *Kestrel* a lifetime ago. Ines didn't say anything. She just stood there shoulder to shoulder with her, pressing a sorghum seed into the dark earth with her thumb, and Casper felt the strange new thing that was still settling into her chest like a bird finding its perch: the presence of another person who required no explanation.

"The orbital allocation," Ines said quietly. "Sef's old office sent a formal request yesterday. They want the heritage wheat for Halcyon's farms. Priority status."

Casper's hands stilled in the soil. "Priority over the burn zones?"

"They didn't say that, exactly. They said *concurrent*." Ines's jaw did the thing it did when she was choosing words the way she'd once chosen targets—carefully, with an awareness of collateral damage. "Old habits. The Concord still thinks everything is a queue with itself at the front."

"Then they can wait," Casper said. "The burn zones have children eating protein paste. Halcyon has hydroponics and a grievance."

Ines laughed—a real laugh, short and surprised out of her—and Casper thought, not for the first time, that this was the sound she'd have kept the beacon running for, if she'd known it existed. She pressed one more seed into the earth and covered it, and around them eleven people worked in the warm light of the atrium's glass roof, and the vault, for the first time in its long life, was doing what it was built to do.

* * *

### Ines

Ines had spent her adult life learning to read rooms for threat: sightlines, exits, hands, the angle of a stranger's shoulders. The skill did not retire just because the war had. So she noticed, in the way she now noticed everything, that Toma watched her.

Not hostilely. Not exactly. It was the watchfulness of a woman doing arithmetic—*Concord hunter, here, in our founding place, with her hands in our seeds*—and not yet trusting the sum. Ines didn't blame her. She had run down Reclamation couriers through the debris fields above Kessari. She had burned a supply skiff over the Meridian basin with two people aboard, and had never learned their names, and had told herself at the time that names were not part of the job.

She set down her trowel, walked the length of the atrium, and stood in front of Toma in the direct, unlovely way that had gotten her through every hard conversation of her life.

"You want to ask me something," Ines said. "Ask it."

Toma wiped her hands on her thighs. She was not small, and she did not step back, and Ines respected her for both. "The skiff over the basin. Three years ago. The supply run from Halcyon Station Seven to the surface cells." Her voice was level. "You were the interceptor on that flight. I knew one of the people aboard."

"I know," Ines said. "I've known since the tribunal records were opened. I've read the manifest." She had read it eleven times. She had written the names on a card that lived in the pocket over her heart, because she had decided, in the wreckage of her old life, that she was done with the comfortable fiction that her hands had been clean. "Their names were Ade Okafor and Mirren Sato. Ade was twenty-six. I checked."

Toma's breath went out of her slowly, the way air leaves a room when a door opens in winter. "You checked."

"I check everything now. It's the one part of me the war left useful." Ines held the other woman's eyes. "I'm not asking for forgiveness. I'm telling you I know what I am, and I'm here anyway, because Casper taught me that the alternative to being useful is being nothing. If you want me off the surface lots, I'll work the orbital allocations and you'll never see me. If you want me gone from the vault, I'll go. But I won't pretend the ledger doesn't exist. I've spent too many years pretending ledgers don't exist. That's how men like Sef get rich."

The atrium had gone quiet again. Casper had stopped working ten meters away, a tray in her arms, not intervening, watching with the particular stillness Ines had learned to love—*let them finish, they're building something*.

Toma looked at her for a long time. Then she looked at the seed trays, and at the beds of turned earth, and at the glass roof where the afternoon light came down like something forgiven.

"Ade had a daughter," Toma said finally. "She's nine. She lives with my sister in the Kessari settlement." She picked up her trowel. "When the millet comes up, I'm going to teach her to plant it. You can be there or not be there. That's the whole offer."

Ines felt her eyes sting and did not look away, because looking away was another old habit she was done with. "I'll be there," she said.

Toma nodded and went back to her bed. Ines stood a moment longer, breathing, and then Casper was beside her, setting down her tray, and neither of them said anything, because there was nothing that needed saying in front of other people, and everything that needed saying could wait for the evening, and the evening would come, and they would say it.

* * *

### Casper

Evening in the vault was her favorite hour, and had been even in the lonely years—when the sun dropped behind the western ridge and the atrium's glass roof caught the last of it and the whole chamber filled with a low amber light, like the inside of a lantern. Now the hour had company.

They sat on the worktable, side by side, feet dangling, the way they'd sat on the *Kestrel*'s cargo hatch a lifetime ago while the relay station's dead antennas ticked around them. Below, the day's beds lay in their neat rows, dark and damp and patient.

"Toma's offer," Casper said. "The planting. With Ade's daughter."

"I know."

"You don't have to—"

"Ines." Casper stopped herself, and Ines huffed a small laugh, because they both did this—started sentences on each other's behalf and abandoned them halfway. Casper tried again. "You don't owe anyone that. Grief isn't a debt you pay in millet."

"No," Ines agreed. She was turning a seed over in her fingers, one of the sorghum grains, rolling it between thumb and forefinger. "But I owe *her* the truth, and the truth is that I'd rather stand in a field with a nine-year-old who has every reason to hate me than sit in a clean room being sorry at myself. Sorry at myself is what I did for nine years too, you know. Different vault. Same silence." She looked over. "You taught me the other thing. Seeds don't care who planted them. They just want the ground."

Casper leaned her head against Ines's shoulder. Below them the beds waited, eleven strangers' work in her grandmother's atrium, the first joint planting in the vault's history—the Concord and the Reclamation kneeling in the same dirt, and no one had declared it, no council had ratified it, it had simply happened because seeds were small enough that no one could start a war over them, and hands in the earth made lying difficult.

"Grandmother would have hated this," Casper said.

Ines went still. "Hated it?"

"Mm. She'd have hated the ceremony of it. The tribunals, the monuments, the *meaning*." Casper smiled into the amber light. "She was very clear in the archive. She wrote that the beacon should never become a shrine. *Memory is a tool*, she wrote. *The day it becomes an ornament, bury it and start over.*" She looked down at the beds. "But she'd have loved this. Dirt under the fingernails. No speeches. Just people putting things in the ground that might outlive their grudges."

"Tomorrow's shipment," Ines said. "Two hundred kilos to Kessari. And Halcyon can wait."

"Halcyon can wait," Casper agreed. "Let them learn what waiting feels like. It's educational."

Ines laughed, and the sound went up through the glass roof into the darkening sky, and Casper thought about the beacon on the ridge above them, retuned now, broadcasting its strange nightly liturgy—language lessons in Old Meridian and Concord Standard both, weather for farmers on two worlds, a song from the surface answered by a song from orbit, and the apologies, always the apologies, traveling in both directions like birds that had finally learned their way home.

She had spent nine years talking to a silence. Now the silence was full, and the fullness was not peace, exactly—it was too raw for peace, too new, too full of names like Ade Okafor and Mirren Sato and all the others—but it was a beginning, and beginnings, her grandmother's primers had taught her, were the most fragile and most stubborn things in any language.

"Thursday," Casper said. "Kessari. You and me and two hundred kilos of second chances."

Ines laced her fingers through hers. "Thursday," she said.

Above the vault, the beacon's evening cycle began, and somewhere in the dark a receiver on a burned hillside or a battered station flickered on, and listened, and was not alone.

* * *

But Thursday came with weather.

Casper saw it first from the atrium roof at dawn: a plume on the southern horizon, gray-brown, too wide for a dust devil, moving against the wind. By midmorning the vault's old instruments confirmed what her eyes already knew—a surface storm, the kind the burn zones bred now, the scorched ground heating fast and throwing the sky into fits. The Kessari corridor would be closed to flight for two days, maybe three.

The shipment could wait. Storms were honest; they delayed everyone equally.

It was the message that couldn't wait. It came in at 0900 on the beacon's diplomatic band, routed through the new joint relay network, flagged priority from the tribunal at Meridian City. Casper read it standing in the transmission room with the storm's first wind already rattling the ridge, and read it twice, and then went to find Ines, who took one look at her face and set down the crate she was loading.

"What is it?"

Casper held out the flimsy. "The tribunal's finished with Sef's financial records. The full audit." Her voice was steady, which surprised her. "Ines—he wasn't farming the war alone. The ledger goes wider. Much wider. And the first name on the list of co-signatories—" She stopped, and made herself say it. "It's a name from the vault's founding charter. It's someone my grandmother trusted."
