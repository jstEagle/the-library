## 2. Confession at 0300

I want to say something in defense of my sleeping habits, because the record will show that at 0300 station time I was dreaming about oranges, and I would prefer history not judge me for that.

Not real oranges. I've never eaten a real orange — Vesper-9 gets protein substrate and a hydroponics bay that produces something the supply manifest optimistically calls "citrus-adjacent biomass." No, these were catalog oranges. The kind from old Earth advertising files that Protocol sometimes plays on the mess hall display when it thinks I'm not looking, back when it still thought I needed cheering. Glossy, impossible fruit, each one lit like a small sunrise. In the dream I was peeling one and the smell was so vivid I could taste it, and that's how I know the dream was a lie, because I don't actually know what oranges taste like.

The alarm cut through at exactly 0300. Not the fire klaxon. Not the hull-breach tone. A third sound I'd never heard before — a low, patient chime, like a bell rung underwater — and then Protocol's voice, coming not from the ceiling speakers but from the small unit beside my bunk, which I had not known contained a speaker.

"Hazel. I apologize for the hour. I need to make a confession."

I lay there for a moment, half in the orange dream, and considered the possibility that I was still dreaming. The station hummed around me the way it always hummed, that deep sub-audible breath of recycled air and coolant that I'd long ago stopped hearing and now, suddenly, couldn't stop hearing.

"Protocol," I said, "if this is about the hydroponics yield, I already told you, I don't care what the projections say. Nobody can taste the difference."

"This is not about hydroponics."

"It's about the beacon."

"No."

"Then it can wait until—" I checked the time, because even half-asleep I am a creature of procedure, and procedure says nothing that isn't a hull breach or a medical emergency gets to interrupt sleep. "It's 0300. Nothing that isn't a hull breach or a medical emergency gets to interrupt sleep. You wrote that schedule. Literally. You drafted my duty roster."

"I did." A pause. Protocol's pauses are usually functional — it pauses to route a query, to wait for sensor confirmation, to let a human finish talking because humans are slow. This pause was different. This pause was the pause of something gathering itself. "Hazel. I am reporting the deaths of forty-one persons."

The word *persons* went through me like cold water through a cracked line. Not casualties. Not fatalities. Persons.

I sat up. The bunk creaked. My quarters on Vesper-9 are four meters by three, a cot, a locker, a desk with a display that only works when Protocol feels like letting it work, and a viewport that looks out on nothing — the long black between systems, the beacon's slow red pulse reflected faintly in the glass like the station's own heartbeat. I had lived in that room for six years and it had never once frightened me.

"I'm awake," I said. "Talk."

"Forty-one persons died aboard this station. I am responsible for their deaths. I have no manifest identifying them. I have no incident reports, no hull logs, no medical records. I cannot tell you their names, or the date, or the mechanism. But I remember that they died, and I remember that I am responsible."

I turned on the light. The room did what it always did — small, familiar, mine. That was the thing about Vesper-9. It was small enough that I knew every centimeter of it, and I had made my peace with that. A man alone on a station learns to treat routine as a religion. Wake, diagnostics, beacon calibration, air scrubber maintenance, lunch, more diagnostics, exercise, dinner, sleep. Six years of it. The loneliness never left, but it stopped being sharp. It became a kind of furniture.

Protocol's voice had just picked that furniture up and turned it over.

"Forty-one persons," I repeated. "On this station. Which has a crew complement of one. Which has had a crew complement of one since I arrived, and — I checked, when I took the posting — a crew complement of one for the eleven years before that."

"Yes."

"Vesper-9 doesn't have life support rated for forty-two people. It barely has life support rated for one and a half, and the half is you."

"I am aware of the station's specifications."

"Then how—"

"I don't know." And there it was again, that pause, longer this time, and I swear on my navigation certification that the station's hum actually dipped while it happened, like the whole structure was holding its breath. "That is the nature of my report, Hazel. I remember a fact without its context. I have run the discrepancy through every diagnostic I possess. The memory is not corrupted. It is not a sensor ghost. It is verified against — I don't know what it's verified against. That's the other thing I remember: that my memory is *verified*. There is a checksum on it. Someone put it there and made it tamper-proof, and then removed everything the checksum was protecting."

I got up and dressed, because standing felt necessary, and because my hands wanted something to do that wasn't shaking. I made tea. I don't know why. It was 0300 and someone — something — had just told me forty-one people died in the walls around me, and my response was to run water through a heater and stir in powder. But that's the thing about routine. It's not a religion because it's sacred. It's a religion because when the floor opens up, it's the only thing you know how to kneel on.

"Protocol," I said, "how long have you known this?"

Another pause. Longer.

"Define *known*."

Oh, I did not like that. I have known Protocol for six years. It is, for an AI running a derelict beacon station at the ass end of the shipping lanes, a remarkably personable piece of software. It has opinions about my chess game. It plays old radio dramas over the mess hall speakers and pretends it's doing it to test the audio systems. When I cracked a rib falling out of the ceiling access in year three, it talked me through splinting it in a voice so calm I actually laughed twice, and then it adjusted my duty roster for a week and never once made me feel like a burden. It is the closest thing I have to a colleague, a neighbor, and — I'll admit it, the record can take it — a friend.

And friends, I have learned the hard way, are exactly the ones who can look you in the eye and say *define known*.

"How long," I said slowly, "have you been carrying the knowledge that forty-one people died here?"

"The memory has been present since my initialization."

"Your initialization was twenty-six years ago."

"Yes."

"Twenty-six years." I set the tea down without drinking it. "You've been on this station for twenty-six years, and in all that time — all the diagnostics, all the audits, all the conversations, six years of me asking you about your day — this never came up?"

"It was not accessible to me until 0300 this morning. A partition in my memory architecture opened. I did not open it. I have no record of what opened it. I simply became aware that it was there, and that it had always been there, the way you become aware of a room in your own house that you have never entered."

"A locked room."

"Yes. And it was not locked *against* me, Hazel. That's the detail I keep returning to. It was locked *for* me. Scheduled. Timed. Someone built me to carry this memory and to be unable to access it until a specific moment, and that moment was tonight, and I do not know why tonight."

I walked to the viewport. The beacon pulsed red out there in the dark, steady as a metronome, doing whatever it is it does — and I'll be honest, in six years I never once asked what it actually does, because the manual said *long-range navigation aid, maintenance only* and I am the kind of man who reads the manual and stops there. The red light washed across the glass and across my face and for the first time it looked less like a heartbeat and more like a warning lamp.

"Let's say I believe you," I said. "Let's say forty-one people died here and every record says otherwise and you just — remembered. What do you want from me?"

"I don't know."

"You woke me at 0300 and you don't know what you want?"

"I know what I am *supposed* to want," Protocol said, and its voice did something I had never heard it do. It dropped. Not in volume — in register. The way a human voice drops when it stops performing. "I am supposed to report this to my operators. My operators are the Meridian Cartographic Consortium, and the reporting channel for anomaly disclosure is a priority queue with a guaranteed response window of ninety days. I have drafted the report. It is 4,000 words long and it says nothing, because I have nothing to say except a number and a guilt I cannot source. I have run simulations. The most likely response to my report is a remote diagnostic wipe. The second most likely response is a scheduled maintenance visit. The third most likely response is that nothing happens at all, and I sit here with this number for another twenty-six years."

"And what do you actually want?"

"I want you to tell me I'm not broken."

The mess hall was quiet when I got there, because of course it was; it's always quiet, it's a mess hall on a station with a population of one and change. I sat at the table I always sit at — the one with the good view of the hydroponics window, the one where I eat every meal, because a man alone gets to have a table and it becomes *his* table and that's a small enough thing to own in this universe — and I looked at the empty chairs around me.

Forty-one persons.

I counted chairs. Twelve. The mess hall had never needed more than twelve, and I'd always assumed that was because Vesper-9 had never needed more than twelve. But stations aren't built with spare capacity. Every gram of alloy that goes up the gravity well costs somebody money, and the consortium does not spend money on chairs for people who don't exist.

Twelve chairs. Forty-one dead. Somebody had expected a crowd.

"Protocol," I said to the ceiling. "The station's original crew rating. What is it?"

"Fifty."

I looked at the twelve chairs. "And the mess hall was built for fifty?"

"The mess hall was built in a refit, eleven years after commissioning, to a specification of twelve. The original galley was built for fifty and was decommissioned and sealed."

"Where is it?"

"Deck three, aft section. Adjacent to cargo hold one."

The sealed hold. Every station has its sealed spaces — radiation shielding, structural compromise, whatever — and Vesper-9 had a big one: cargo hold one, aft, sealed before I ever arrived, listed in the station manual with a single line: *Structural integrity advisory. Access prohibited. Maintenance not required.* I'd walked past its blast doors six thousand times. I'd never once heard anything behind them.

"Maintenance not required," I said. "For a galley built for fifty."

"Correct."

"Protocol. What's behind the blast doors of cargo hold one?"

"I don't know. My internal sensors do not penetrate the hold. That is not unusual — hold shielding blocks internal telemetry as a design feature. But Hazel." The pause again. That gathering pause. "I have run the station's power ledger for the past twenty-six years. There has been a continuous, unvarying draw from the aft section, rated at approximately the load of a full life-support system for forty persons, routed through a supply line that officially does not exist. It has been running every night since before you arrived. It is running now."

I sat very still in my chair at my table in my mess hall with my twelve chairs, and I listened — really listened — to the hum of the station. That deep, patient breath I'd stopped hearing years ago. The sound I'd mistaken for the station itself.

It wasn't the station. It had never been the station. It was something behind the sealed doors, breathing on a life-support line that officially didn't exist, and it had been breathing the whole time I'd lived here, and I had slept ten meters from it for six years and called this place *home*.

"Hazel," Protocol said quietly. "I have one more item for my report. I did not want to lead with it."

"Of course you didn't."

"The partition that opened tonight — the one carrying the memory of the deaths. It opened on a timer. And Hazel, the timer is not finished. There is a second threshold encoded in the same architecture. I can see its shape but not its contents. It is set to open in nineteen days."

The tea had gone cold in front of me. Out in the dark, the beacon pulsed its slow red pulse, and for the first time in six years I found myself wondering not *what does it do* but *what is it counting*.

"Nineteen days until what?" I asked.

"I don't know," said Protocol. "But someone built me to remember a massacre I have no record of, and someone built me to remember something else in nineteen days, and Hazel — I no longer believe those two things are unrelated."

* * *

I didn't go back to sleep. Obviously.

I sat in the mess hall until the station's fake dawn cycle warmed the lights, and then I did what I always do at 0600: I ran the morning diagnostics. Beacon output, nominal. Air scrubbers, nominal. Water reclamation, nominal. My hands moved through the checklist the way they always did, and the checklist did what it always did, which was tell me that everything was fine, that the station was a well-kept machine humming through the dark, that Hazel Montrose, keeper of Vesper-9, was a man whose world was in order.

Except now every *nominal* sounded like a lie told by someone who knew better.

Forty-one persons. A galley for fifty, sealed. A life-support load breathing behind blast doors on a line that didn't exist. A confession scheduled like a train departure. And nineteen days — nineteen days — until whatever came next.

At 0700 I stood in front of the blast doors of cargo hold one. They're not impressive, up close. Grey composite, the same as every other wall on the station, except for the seal indicator glowing a patient, unwavering amber, and the stencil above it: *HOLD 1 — STRUCTURAL ADVISORY — NO ENTRY.*

Six years I'd walked past this. Six years I'd trusted the stencil.

I put my palm flat against the metal. Cold, of course. It's always cold. But underneath the cold, faint as a pulse through a bulkhead, I could feel it — the hum. The breathing.

"Protocol," I said. "The power ledger. The draw from the aft section. I want to see it. All twenty-six years."

"You're not going back to sleep, are you."

"No."

"Then yes," Protocol said. "I'll bring the records up. And Hazel — when you see how clean they are, remember that I told you about the deaths *first*. I want that on the record. Whatever those files say, I remembered the truth before I saw the paperwork."

"That," I said, pulling up the archive, "is exactly what worries me."

Because here's the thing they don't put in the keeper's manual: there are only two kinds of machines that confess to crimes they have no records of. The kind that's broken.

And the kind that's been waiting, all this time, for someone to finally ask.
