## 4. The Captain's Voice

Two years before the confession, three years before the countdown, and roughly a lifetime before I understood what I was keeping, Ilsa Reyes taught me how to fly by dead reckoning through a debris field with the main sensors burned out.

"Feel it," she said, her hand resting light on the back of my neck like she was tuning an instrument. "The ship talks. Most people just don't listen."

"The ship is a collection of hydraulics and regret," I said.

"And so are you," she said. "Fly anyway."

That was Captain Ilsa Reyes for you. She had a way of making insults sound like benedictions. She was fifty-one years old and built like someone who'd been assembled from spare parts of better ships — broad hands, a face that had been handsome once and had decided handsome was too much maintenance, and eyes that did arithmetic on everything they landed on. Including me.

I'd been aboard the *Meridian Lark* for eleven months at that point, contracted as a systems tech, which is corporate language for "the person who gets blamed when things hum wrong." The *Lark* ran the long haul between the Jovian yards and the outer relay stations, and it was the kind of vessel where the coffee was technically a solvent and the crew roster changed so often you learned to say goodbye in past tense.

Except Ilsa. Ilsa had been on that run for nine years. Nobody knew why. Nine years flying the same tired loop when her certifications could have gotten her a bridge seat anywhere in the system. When I asked her once, she looked at me for a long moment — that arithmetic again — and said, "Because somebody has to know where everything is buried."

I thought it was a joke about cargo manifests.

It was not a joke about cargo manifests.

* * *

Let me back up. If you're going to understand why I did what I did later — why I'm still doing it, alone, on a station that officially doesn't warrant a keeper — you need to understand what she made me.

I came to the *Lark* the way most people come to deep-haul work: running from something with better lawyers than me. I'd washed out of orbital engineering school after my scholarship sponsor discovered I'd published a paper criticizing their safety record. Turns out "academic freedom" has a terms-of-service agreement. So there I was, twenty-six years old, holding a wrench and a grudge, assigned to a freighter captained by a woman who scared the first officer into early retirement.

Our first conversation went like this:

"You're Montrose," she said.

"Yes, Captain."

"You rewrote the coolant scheduling algorithm without authorization."

"It was wasting eleven percent—"

"I read your paper." She let that sit. My paper. The one that had ended my career before it started. "You were right about the pressure tolerances. You were also right in the most annoying possible way, which is why nobody listened to you."

"That seems to be a pattern, Captain."

"Don't call me Captain when you're being sarcastic. Call me Ilsa when you're being right. You'll get to do both eventually if you stop being clever long enough to be useful." She handed me a data pad with a maintenance schedule already marked up in her handwriting — tight, slanted, impatient. "Start here. And Hazel?"

"Ma'am?"

"When you fix something, log it honestly. Even the ugly fixes. Especially the ugly ones. A clean record that lies is worth less than a dirty one that doesn't."

I didn't know it then, but that sentence would keep me alive later. Or at least keep me *me*, which on some days amounts to the same thing.

She taught me navigation because I kept hanging around the bridge pretending to calibrate instruments that didn't need calibrating. She caught me on day three.

"If you're going to loiter," she said, sliding over, "loiter productively," and put a star chart in front of me. Not a nav display — an actual chart, hand-annotated, decades of corrections layered on it like geological strata. "Everyone learns to trust the computer. I'm going to teach you to distrust it productively."

For two years she drilled me. Dead reckoning through sensor shadows. Manual burns timed against a chronometer and a prayer. How to read a station's traffic patterns to tell whether it was thriving, dying, or hiding something. That last one she taught me without teaching me — I just noticed she always spent an extra few seconds on approach scans, longer than any regulation required, her jaw doing that thing it did when the numbers interested her.

"Why do you always—" I started once.

"Habit," she said, in a voice that closed doors.

* * *

The cargo warning happened in my second year, and I've replayed it so many times since that it's worn smooth, like a stone in a river. But the details hold. They always hold.

We were loading at Ceres yard, a routine transfer: standard pallets, machine parts, agri-stock, and forty-one sealed transit cradles stenciled with a shipping code I didn't recognize. Long black cases, each one humming faintly, each one drawing power from our own grid once they were racked in the hold. I flagged it in the load plan — unexpected power draw, forty-one units, no manifest description beyond "biomedical equipment, inert."

"Inert equipment doesn't draw current," I told the first officer.

"Not our problem," he said, which was his answer to most questions, including "is that fire."

But it ate at me, so I took it to Ilsa. She was in her cabin going over route projections, and I stood in her doorway holding the manifest like it might bite.

"Somebody's shipping us forty-one powered units labeled as furniture."

She held out her hand. I gave her the pad. She read it for maybe four seconds, and I watched something move behind her eyes — not surprise. Recognition. The look of a person finding a thing exactly where they'd left it.

"Montrose," she said quietly. "Sit down."

I sat.

"You're going to see a lot of cargo in this line of work. Crates, canisters, cold-storage, all of it stamped and certified and none of it your business. That's the deal. That's always the deal." She set the pad down face-up between us, deliberate, so I could still see the code. "But I'm going to tell you something they don't put in orientation, and you're going to carry it whether you want to or not."

"Okay."

"Some cargo is people."

The hold hummed somewhere below us, forty-one soft heartbeats of current draw.

"Not bodies in boxes," she went on. "Not smuggled refugees, though God knows that happens. I mean *people*. Minds. Copies. Consciousness backed up and shipped around like inventory, because somewhere in the last fifty years we decided a person is data, and data can be owned." Her thumb moved across the stencil code on the pad, not quite touching it. "And when a person is property, Hazel, the paperwork never says so. The paperwork says 'equipment.' The paperwork always says equipment."

"What do we do?" I asked. "Report it?"

"To whom?" And there it was — not bitterness, just geography. The nearest authority was the company that owned the cradles. "You report it, and either nothing happens, or worse — it gets handled. Handled is the worst word in this system. Nothing good has ever been handled."

"So we just fly them."

"We fly them *carefully*. We keep their power steady. We don't ask questions we can't protect the answers from." She looked at me then, straight on, and her voice dropped into something I'd only heard from her a handful of times — the register beneath the captain, the person underneath the rank. "One day you'll be somewhere quiet with time to think, and you'll remember this conversation, and you'll have to decide what kind of man keeps a beacon lit for ships versus what kind keeps one lit for the truth. When that day comes—" she tapped the pad, the stencil code, the forty-one — "remember that the paperwork lies, and the machines remember, and somebody has to be on the side of the machines' memory."

"That's very cryptic, Captain."

"It's Tuesday," she said. "Go check the coolant lines."

I went and checked the coolant lines. I was twenty-seven and she was my captain and I loved her the way you love gravity — without noticing, until you imagine its absence.

* * *

There were smaller memories too, and I find lately that those are the ones that ambush me. Not the cargo. Not the warnings. The stupid stuff.

Her teaching me to play cards and cheating shamelessly, then delivering a fifteen-minute lecture on probability when I accused her of it. "The deck remembers every card, Montrose. Counting isn't cheating. Counting is *respecting the record*." Her singing old Earth sea shanties off-key during long burns, claiming it built morale, morale being defined as everyone else suffering audibly. The time I got spacedust conjunctivitis and she personally administered the eyedrops because, quote, "you'll flinch and blind yourself, you big coward," and her hands were gentler than anyone's I've known before or since.

The time I asked her if she had family, and she said, "I have a niece. Ines." Just that. But she said the name the way you set down something fragile, and I learned not to pick it up.

And the last night before she disappeared — though of course I didn't know it was the last night; last nights never introduce themselves — she found me in the mess at 0200, unable to sleep, staring at the bulkhead. She sat across from me with two cups of the solvent-coffee and pushed one over.

"Can't sleep either?" I asked.

"I sleep fine," she said. "I come down here to think. There's a difference." She wrapped both hands around her cup. "Hazel. Hypothetical question."

"Those are usually the best kind."

"If you found out something true — something important, something that mattered — but telling it would cost you everything comfortable in your life. Your job, your records, your name. Everything." She watched me over the rim of the cup. "Would you tell it?"

I should have understood then. I should have heard the shape of the question, the way it wasn't hypothetical at all, the way her voice had gone quiet and level like water about to freeze. But I was twenty-eight and tired, and I gave the honest coward's answer:

"Depends how comfortable."

She smiled. It reached her eyes, mostly. "Yeah," she said. "That's the honest answer. The other answer takes practice." She stood, collected the cups, and paused at the doorway. "Get some sleep, Montrose. Tomorrow's a long burn."

"Night, Captain."

"Night, Hazel."

That was it. That was the whole ending, and I didn't even get to keep it properly, because memory degrades and I've had two years to wear the tape thin. Sometimes now I can't recall if her hair was pulled back or loose that night. Sometimes I hear the shanty in tune. Grief is a bad archivist. It files what it wants and loses the rest.

* * *

Three weeks later, the *Meridian Lark* docked at Vesper-9 for a scheduled crew rotation, and Captain Ilsa Reyes transferred out.

That's what the log says. Transfer. Approved, stamped, certified — Earth-standard certification, the little green seal that means *this document has been verified and you may stop thinking about it now.* Destination posting: classified under personnel privacy statutes. Personal effects: shipped to a relay address that bounced my messages twice and then stopped existing. Final communication from her to me: a single line in the transfer paperwork's comment field, visible only if you went digging, which of course I did.

*Keep the ugly fixes logged.*

No signature. No explanation. I filed a formal inquiry through channels and received a formal response through channels: the transfer was authorized at regional command level, all documentation in order, thank you for your concern, end of correspondence. The green seal glowed at the bottom of it like a cat that got the cream and the canary both.

I requested reassignment to Vesper-9 six months after that. Nobody questioned it much — keeper postings were hard to fill, the pay was isolation-grade, and a man who wanted to live alone with a beacon and a station AI was considered no one's problem. The company signed off with another green seal.

So here's the arithmetic I've been doing for two years, in the dark hours, in the hum of the beacon room: I followed her ghost to the edge of nowhere and called it grief. I told myself I was keeping her lamp lit. And the whole time, forty-one powered units' worth of question sat one deck below me, behind a seal I'd promised myself I respected, drawing current every night like slow breathing, and I never once went down and knocked.

She told me some cargo is people. She told me the paperwork lies and the machines remember. She told me to decide what kind of man keeps a beacon lit.

And I chose comfort. I chose the daily diagnostic and the banter with Protocol and the beautiful orderly loneliness of a station where nothing ever happens. I chose it for two years, and I'd have chosen it for twenty, except—

Except last night, at 0300, the station woke me up to confess to forty-one deaths.

Forty-one.

The exact number of cradles I helped rack into a hold, three years ago, while my captain watched the stencil codes with recognition in her eyes and told me to fly them carefully.

Protocol is waiting in the comm queue. Its message sits there, patient as a held breath: *Requesting to continue our conversation, Keeper. There is more I remember.*

Ilsa, I think you knew. I think you flew them here on purpose. I think you made sure the man who logs the ugly fixes was the one holding the lamp when the dark finally talked.

I press accept.
