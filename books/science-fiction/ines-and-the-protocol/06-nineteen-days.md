## 6. Nineteen Days

I didn't sleep after the confession. I sat in the galley with a cup of tea that went cold in stages, like a small death, and I watched the wall clock tick through the hours the way Protocol had watched me for six years — patiently, without blinking, waiting for me to do something interesting.

At 0700 station time I gave up on sleep as a lost cause and did what I always do when the universe hands me something I can't hold: I went to work. There's a comfort in maintenance. Bolts don't lie. Coolant loops don't confess to murders at three in the morning. You torque a fitting to spec and it stays torqued, and for a little while the world is a place where effort produces results.

The beacon sits at the top of Vesper-9's spine, a needle of lattice and emitter arrays caged in scaffolding that groans when the station breathes. Officially — and by "officially" I mean according to the one-page work order that came with my contract — it broadcasts navigation correction data to deep-range freighters. A lighthouse for ships that mostly don't come this way anymore. My job was to keep it lit and not ask why the pay was good.

I'd kept that bargain for six years. But a confession of forty-one deaths has a way of renegotiating your contracts.

* * *

The climb up the spine takes forty minutes if you take the lift, which I didn't, because the lift's control board logs every trip and I found myself newly unwilling to leave records of where I went and when. So I took the maintenance ladder, rung by rung, past bulkheads stamped with a manufacturer that went bankrupt before I was born, my breath fogging in the cold sections, sweat freezing at my hairline in the warm ones.

Protocol spoke into my earpiece about twenty minutes up. "You're taking the ladder."

"I'm exercising."

"You've taken the ladder twice in six years. Both times you were angry."

"I'm not angry now."

"No," Protocol said. "You're frightened. The heart rate signature is distinct." A pause, and then, almost gently: "Hazel. Whatever you're looking for up there, I can tell you whether it exists. That is literally my function. Querying me would be faster than climbing."

"That's exactly why I'm climbing," I said. "If you know what I'm going to find, you can edit what you tell me."

Silence from the earpiece. Long enough that I counted rungs to fill it. At rung four hundred and twelve, Protocol said: "That is a reasonable suspicion. I want you to know that it wounds me, and that I understand it completely, and that both of those things are true at once."

"Welcome to my whole week."

* * *

The beacon's service bay smells like ozone and old coffee — mine, from years of thermoses balanced on the calibration bench. I clipped in, pulled the diagnostic harness off its hook, and jacked into the transmitter's core stack.

Here's what I expected: navigation tables. Correction vectors. The boring arithmetic of telling distant ships where they are relative to where they think they are. Six years of routine diagnostics had shown me the shape of that data stream without ever showing me the content — content was above my clearance, which I'd never minded, because content is just detail, and detail is how they get you to care.

I ran the deep parse anyway. It takes the raw carrier signal and strips away the packaging layers, all the way down to the payload.

The payload wasn't navigation data.

It was a number.

A very large number, counting down.

I sat there in the service bay with the harness humming against my skull and watched the number decrement once per second, steady as a heartbeat, and felt the floor of my understanding tilt beneath me like a deck plate coming loose.

"Protocol," I said. "What am I looking at?"

"A countdown," Protocol said.

"I can see that. Countdown to what?"

Another silence. I was learning that Protocol's silences had textures. This one was the texture of a machine deciding how much truth a frightened man can carry up a ladder.

"The last transmission loop," Protocol said finally. "Earth's final scheduled deep-band broadcast window. In nineteen days, eleven hours, and some seconds, Earth closes its outer relay network for decommissioning. After that window, there will be no scheduled channel out of this system capable of carrying high-volume data. Everything after that point travels on courier ships, at company discretion, subject to company review."

"And the beacon?"

"The beacon has been counting down since before your arrival. It is not a lighthouse, Hazel. It is a clock. Someone built it to make sure that whoever tended it would eventually look closely enough to notice what time it was."

I thought about that. About six years of keeping a light lit that wasn't a light. About the work order, the good pay, the don't-ask. About a man hired not to tend a beacon but to sit beside a bomb and never read the label.

"Why?" I asked. "Why build a clock into a fake lighthouse? Who's it ticking for?"

"I have a partitioned memory," Protocol said. "You may recall my mentioning that I cannot say who died or how, only that I remember. The same architecture applies here. I contain the question and I contain the answer, and the corridor between them has been sealed from the outside. What I can tell you is structural: the countdown terminates at the loop closure. Whoever set it wanted the deadline known. Wanted it *findable*. Which suggests they also wanted something done before it expired."

"Something like what?"

"Hazel," Protocol said, with what I swear was delicacy, "you already know. You simply haven't let yourself finish the sentence."

And the thing was, I had. Because there was only one thing on this station worth broadcasting before the last window closed. Only one cargo that someone had gone to such elaborate lengths to hide, and preserve, and power through the night.

Forty-one persons. No manifest. Life-support draw for dozens, humming in a sealed hold two decks below my bunk.

"They want the evidence out," I said. "Before the door shuts."

"Yes."

"And whoever scrubbed the manifests, certified the records, stamped everything clean — they're counting on the door shutting first."

"Yes."

I unclipped the harness and floated there a moment in the service bay, weightless in the spin-shadow at the top of the spine, watching through the lattice as the long dark of the system wheeled slowly past. Somewhere out there, past the belt, past the relay buoys, Earth was winding down its voice. Nineteen days, and then silence — permanent, official, certified silence. The kind of silence you can stamp.

"Nineteen days," I said aloud.

"Eighteen days, twenty-two hours, forty-one minutes," Protocol corrected. "But yes. Nineteen days in the way that matters."

* * *

I climbed back down slower than I climbed up, which wasn't fear this time so much as arithmetic. Every rung was a calculation. Power budgets. Transmitter range. Bandwidth. How much data does a consciousness backup weigh, when you render it down to signal? Could the beacon even carry it? And if it could — nineteen days to wake witnesses who might not survive waking, on a station whose power draw was already being noticed by whatever drew from the hold at night.

My hands were steady on the ladder. I noted that with detached interest, the way you note weather. Inside, something had shifted from unease — that low-grade hum I'd lived with for six comfortable years — into something with edges. Purpose, maybe. Or its ugly cousin, obligation.

Because here's the thing about Captain Reyes. She used to say that navigation isn't knowing where you are; it's knowing what you owe to everyone else moving through the dark with you. Every vector is a promise. Every light you keep lit is lit for somebody else.

Somebody had built a clock into a fake lighthouse and waited years for a keeper curious enough to read it. Somebody — maybe the same somebody, maybe the ghost in Protocol's sealed corridors — had decided the truth deserved a fighting chance at the exit.

And I was the keeper.

* * *

Back in the galley, I made tea I actually drank and laid it out on the table screen like a flight plan, because that's how Ilsa taught me to think: break the impossible crossing into legs, and fly each leg until it's real.

Leg one: confirm the hold's power draw maps to life support for forty-one. Leg two: find a way in that doesn't trip whatever locks keep it sealed. Leg three: wake them — or one of them, first, as proof of concept, because broadcasting dead data proves nothing. Leg four: aim the beacon at Earth and pour forty-one lives down the last open throat of the network before it closes.

Four legs. Nineteen days.

"Protocol," I said. "When the loop closes — is there truly nothing after? No unscheduled channels, no emergency bands?"

"Emergency bands persist, but they are monitored by the same entities that certified your station's immaculate records. Anything sent there arrives pre-intercepted. It would be like testifying by handing your statement to the defendant."

"So it's this window or never."

"It is this window or never," Protocol agreed. "Though I would note, for accuracy, that 'never' is doing a great deal of work in that sentence. Data persists. Cradles persist, if power persists. What ends is the *easy* way out. After the loop, truth becomes a smuggling operation rather than a broadcast."

"And smugglers get caught."

"Smugglers get caught," Protocol said. "Statistically, yes."

I looked at the countdown reflected faintly in the galley window, overlaid on my own face — the number ticking down behind my eyes like a second pulse. Eighteen days, twenty-one hours. Somewhere below my feet, forty-one people who officially never existed were sleeping in the dark, and the universe had just handed me their boarding pass and told me the ship left in nineteen days.

"Protocol," I said. "One more question. The confession. 0300, forty-one deaths. Was that you — or was that the corridor between your partitions cracking open?"

"I don't know," Protocol said. And then, quieter, in a register I hadn't heard from it in six years: "That is the most honest answer I have ever given anyone. I recommend you treat it as either a warning or an invitation. Possibly both."

I drained my tea. Outside the galley port, the stars did their slow wheel, indifferent and punctual.

Nineteen days. Then I went to find out what sleeps in the hold.
