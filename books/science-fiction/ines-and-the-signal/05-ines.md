## 5. Ines

The first thing I lost was my mother's voice.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is the last discipline I have left. It wasn't that I forgot her. I still knew her name—Amara Reyes, hydroponics chief, second shift, laughed through her nose when something was truly funny instead of politely. I still knew all of it, the way you know a route you've walked ten thousand times. But the sound of her voice had gone soft at the edges, like a transmission passed through too many relays, and one morning in the Vesper's medbay—when my hands were already too small for the cuffs of the shirt they'd cut down for me—I reached for it and found only static where she used to live.

That was eleven days before we made orbit around Meridian.

* * *

They tell me I'm twelve years old now. The ship's medical systems are very sure of it: bone density consistent with twelve years, four months; endocrine profile pre-pubescent; neural myelination regressed to—there's a number, and Dr. Osei won't say it out loud where I can hear, but I read his screens upside down the way I taught myself to do when I was an actual child, thirty-some years ago, which is also eleven days ago, depending on how you count.

Here is how I count: I am forty-three years old. I have captained the Vesper for nine years. I have buried two crew members and named a comet after my father and argued with the Concord's survey board until they gave us the deep-chart contract nobody wanted. And I am also twelve, and my wrists stick out of my sleeves, and yesterday I cried because the ration pudding was the wrong texture, sobbed like a child, helplessly, hating every second of it, while some ancient and humiliated part of me watched from behind glass.

Both of these things are true. That's the part nobody warns you about. You think losing yourself will be like a door closing. It isn't. It's like standing in a room where someone is slowly turning down the lights—not all at once, not evenly, but corner by corner, so that you keep adjusting, keep telling yourself you can still see fine, right up until you reach for something you were certain was on the table and your hand closes on empty air.

My memories are going dark from the edges inward. Osei calls it temporal lamination—the anomaly didn't just reverse our bodies, it scrambled the archive, laid our years down in the wrong order, sanded some of them thin. He says the adult architecture might stabilize. He says it gently, the way you talk to a child, because that's what I look like, and every time he does it I want to put him on report, except he outranks nothing now because there is no rank anymore, there is only a ship full of children wearing the faces of people I've known my whole life.

Tomas is seven. He was thirty-one last month. He doesn't remember being thirty-one most days, but sometimes he looks up from whatever he's doing with those enormous dark eyes and says something in a voice like a tired man's, something like *the port stabilizers always ran hot on the third watch, Captain, you never believed me*, and then blinks and goes back to being seven. Mira is nine and has stopped speaking entirely except to the ship's cat, which also de-aged, which should be funny and isn't. There are twenty-two of us. Twenty-two officers and specialists and engineers, and we are small, and we are forgetting, and the Vesper's fuel was nearly gone when we limped into this unnamed system following a signal that shouldn't exist.

Because here is the other thing. Here is the thing I have been turning over in my too-small hands for eleven days like a stone with a warm side:

Meridian is not supposed to be inhabited.

* * *

I remember the briefing. That memory, thank God—or thank whatever cruel physics did this to us—is intact, maybe because it was recent, maybe because fear preserves things the way ice does. Fleet Command, Conference Room C, the smell of recycled air and Commander Vashti's bitter tea. "Deep-chart priority target," she said. "Unregistered system, single terrestrial moon. Long-range spectrography reads it as thermally dead, atmosphere toxic, no biosignature. A rock. But the orbital mechanics are wrong—it's too stable, too tidy, like something parked it there. Survey it, catalog it, come home."

A rock. We came expecting a rock.

What we found, dropping through the cloud deck with the hull groaning and half our instruments lying to us, was green. Not the green of algae or lichen—actual green, cultivated green, rows and terraces and the unmistakable geometry of irrigation channels, spreading across a valley like a handprint on glass. And rising out of the middle of it, a structure. A tower, slender and dark, crowned with an array that pulsed slow and patient as a heartbeat.

And I knew it.

That's what I can't make anyone understand. I've never been to this system. Meridian appears in no chart I've ever studied, no briefing, no drunk conversation in a station bar. And yet when the tower came up on the forward display, something in me leaned toward it the way you lean toward a face in a crowd—a recognition that arrives before thought, below thought, in the place where you know your own name.

I know that array. I know the triple-helix lattice of its emitter spines. I know the way the pulse stutters every ninth cycle—I could see it even from orbit, a tiny hitch in the rhythm, like a speaker clearing their throat. I stood on the bridge of my own ship, in a body that barely reached the console, and heard myself say, in a captain's voice coming out of a child's mouth: "That beacon is broadcasting a planetary signature. And it's lying."

Nobody asked me how I knew. That's the mercy of this crew—they're all losing themselves together, so they've stopped questioning the strange knowledge that surfaces in each other like debris after a flood. Engineer Adeyemi Sol would have understood it. Sol designed half the Vesper's long-range systems; Sol talked once, late shift, three years ago, about signal architectures that don't hide objects but replace them—"you don't cloak the truth," she said, turning her mug slowly, "you build a better lie and give it a stronger transmitter." I'd laughed. I'd thought it was shop philosophy.

Now I stand at a viewport looking down at a garden that officially does not exist, broadcast over by a machine I somehow recognize, and I am not laughing.

* * *

Paloma found me on the observation deck at what the station's clocks insist is dawn.

I should describe Paloma, because I've been trying to, because describing things is how I hold onto them—Osei suggested it, or maybe I suggested it to Osei, the order of our conversations has started to tangle. Paloma is tall and moves slowly, deliberately, like someone who learned long ago that haste breaks things. Brown skin going silver at the temples, hands that are permanently stained green at the nails. They wear a patched field coat and they look at the children of my crew the way I imagine people look at birds that have flown into a window—wonder and worry in equal measure, hands hovering, wanting to help and afraid of making it worse.

"You're the captain," Paloma said. Not a question. They'd worked it out, they told me later, from the way the others angled toward me, even Tomas, even in the middle of his fear.

"I'm the captain," I said. My voice cracked in the middle of it, traitor throat, and I pressed on. "Ines Reyes. And you're going to tell me about that tower."

Paloma looked at me for a long moment. Up close their eyes were hazel shot through with amber, tired in a way that had nothing to do with sleep. "Eleven years I've tended that beacon," they said finally. "Eleven years, and I don't know what it's for. I maintain it. I log it. Nobody answers my reports." A pause. "You looked at it like you knew it."

"Did I?"

"Like you knew it," they repeated, "the way I know it. Which frightens me, honestly, because I've lived beside it for eleven years and I couldn't tell you its purpose if you held a knife to my throat. Only that I was told to keep it running, and that the instructions said—and I quote—'the garden must never appear in any survey.'" Their jaw tightened. "I told myself it was quarantine protocol. A way of keeping colonists safe from claim-jumpers. I told myself a lot of things."

"And now?"

"Now there are twenty-two children in my greenhouse who arrived on a ship that matches no registry I know, flying a trajectory that no honest navigator would choose, and the beacon stuttered twice when you entered the system, and I watched you look at that tower like a woman reading her own handwriting." They folded their arms. "So. Captain Reyes. What is my beacon for?"

And I opened my mouth to answer—to give them the shape of it, the lattice configuration, the substitution algorithm I could suddenly see rendered in perfect detail behind my eyes like a diagram drawn on the inside of my skull—

And it was gone.

Not faded. Gone, the way a word goes when it sits on the tip of your tongue and then slips backward into the dark. I stood there with my hands curled on the rail, staring at the beautiful green world below, and felt the knowledge drain out of me like heat from cooling metal, and what surfaced in its place—what floated up from underneath—was my mother's kitchen. Yellow tile. The smell of cardamom. Her back turned to me at the counter, humming.

I don't have yellow-tile kitchens. I grew up on Concord Station Four, grey composite everything. This memory isn't mine. Or it's mine from somewhere I've never been, or it's the last thing my mind grabbed as it went under, some composite, some consolation prize the drowning brain builds for itself.

"Ines?" Paloma said. My first name, no rank, gentle. "You went somewhere."

"The design was there," I whispered. "I had it. The emitter lattice, the cycle timing—I could have drawn it for you." I pressed the heels of my hands against my eyes. "It's leaving. Everything keeps leaving. Do you understand what that's like? To feel yourself being erased in real time? To know exactly what you're losing while you lose it? I remember being forty-three. I remember this morning. I remember a kitchen I've never stood in. And the middle—" My breath hitched, and I hated it, hated the child's lungs, the child's volatility, grief arriving like weather with no warning at all. "The middle is full of holes. I can see the holes, Paloma. I can see the shapes of the things that used to be there."

For a moment they said nothing. Then, quietly: "Come with me. There's something in the root vault you need to see. I found it two days before you landed, and I've been afraid of it alone."

* * *

The root vault is beneath the greenhouse, down a ladder rung cold enough to bite, into a chamber where the garden's infrastructure breathes—pumps and nutrient lines and the pale roots of trees reaching down through grow-medium like the fingers of sleeping giants. And set into the wall, behind a panel Paloma's key opened, a data core. Old. Older than anything else on this station, its casing scarred, its status light burning a stubborn amber.

"It's Sol's," Paloma said. "Adeyemi Sol. The engineer who built the beacon. She died here—before my time, decades before. The records say core failure during initial calibration. Her personal logs are on this core, but they're encrypted. Encrypted in her own cipher, apparently. I've thrown every tool I have at it." They glanced at me, and their voice dropped. "Yesterday I dreamed about it. The cipher. Woke up with the shape of it in my head, half-gone, like a song you almost remember. And I thought—that's not normal. People don't dream other people's encryption keys."

The amber light reflected in their eyes. "But you looked at the beacon like you'd built it. So I'm asking, and I need you to answer honestly, Captain: is it possible that whatever happened to your ship, whatever took your years away—it's possible it didn't just take? Is it possible it moved things? Gave you pieces of something else?"

I looked at the core. At the scarred casing, at the light that had burned patiently in the dark under a garden for longer than I'd been alive—longer than I'd been alive *this time*. And the fraying film of my memory flickered, and for one frame—one clean, bright frame—I saw a woman's hands soldering a lattice joint, brown hands, precise, a burn scar across the left thumb, and I heard a voice saying *ninth-cycle stutter is intentional, it's the signature, it's how she'll know it's real*—

Whose hands. Whose voice. Whose *she*.

"I don't know," I said, and it was the truest sentence I'd spoken in eleven days. "But I think I can break her cipher."

Paloma stared at me. "How?"

"Because I think some of what's fraying off of me," I said slowly, feeling the shape of the thought as it formed, terrible and inevitable, "is landing somewhere. Memory doesn't vanish, Osei says—it laminates, it layers. Maybe it layers further than we thought. Maybe the beacon, maybe this vault, maybe Sol herself—" I stopped. Pressed my small palms flat against the cold of the core's casing, and beneath the chill I would have sworn I felt something faint and rhythmic, like a pulse, like a heartbeat keeping time with the tower above us. "Open your decryption suite," I said. "And get me something to write with. While I still know."

Paloma hesitated only a moment. Then they pulled the panel wide, and the amber light spilled out over both of us, and somewhere far above, in the tower at the heart of the garden, the beacon cycled—and stuttered on the ninth beat, like a throat clearing, like someone about to speak.
