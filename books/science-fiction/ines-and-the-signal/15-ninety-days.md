## 15. Ninety Days

The days after the survey schedules arrived had a texture to them, a grain, like wood planed thin enough to see light through. Ninety days. I counted them the way I once counted seedlings—by what they might become, not by how many were left.

I woke before dawn as I always did, but now there were small sounds in the station that hadn't been there for eleven years. Feet on the corridor grating. A giggle muffled badly behind a bulkhead door. The clatter of someone too short reaching for something on a shelf built for adults. The Vesper's children had woven themselves into Meridian's rhythms the way bindweed works into a fence—not invited, exactly, but impossible now to imagine the structure without them.

I lay in my bunk in the grey half-light and listened to the station breathe with more lungs than mine, and I thought: this is what it was all for. And then, because honesty is a habit even in solitude: this is what I will lose if the beacon fails.

Ninety days. I got up and made tea for nine.

* * *

The beacon's decay was not dramatic. That was the worst of it. It didn't spark or groan; it simply forgot, a little more each week, how to lie.

I spent the first ten days of our countdown in the lower chamber with the diagnostic harness draped over my shoulders like a shawl of cables, reading the signal's slow unraveling. The false signature Sol had built—the dead-rock broadcast, the radiation profile of a world scoured by its own sun—was fraying at the edges. Tiny inconsistencies. A thermal ghost here, an atmospheric whisper there. To any fleet sensor passing at range, nothing. But the Concord and the Halcyon would not pass at range. They would come close, and close was where lies went to die.

"Show me again," Ines said. She sat cross-legged on the deck beside the core housing, her chin propped on her fists, twelve years old and wearing concentration like a uniform two sizes too big.

I pulled up the waveform on the maintenance display. "This is what we're telling them." The line ran flat and ugly, a dead world's heartbeat. "And this is what we actually are." Beneath it, faint as breath on glass, the true signature pulsed—water, chlorophyll, oxygen in gentle excess, the warm electromagnetic hum of living soil. "The beacon has to keep the second one buried under the first."

"It's leaking."

"It's leaking," I agreed. "Slowly. Like a roof with bad flashing. You don't notice until the ceiling comes down."

Ines studied the display for a long moment. Then she said, in a voice that was older than her body, "Sol knew. That last entry—she wrote that the core would need re-keying within a decade. She left instructions she couldn't follow herself." Her jaw tightened. "She left them for a ghost."

"For you," I said quietly. "Or someone like you."

"I'm twelve." She laughed, and the laugh cracked in the middle. "I captained a ship for six years and now I need a stool to reach the coffee maker."

"You'll need more than a stool to re-key that core. So will I." I shut down the display before either of us could look too long at the numbers. "Which is why we have eighty days to become someone who died twenty years ago."

* * *

The neural archive lived in the root vault, beneath the oldest section of the garden, where the fig trees sent their roots through nutrient channels Sol herself had laid. I went down alone, mostly, in those early weeks—partly to study the archive's interface, partly because standing in the dark among the roots felt like standing inside a held breath. Sol's presence was everywhere down there without being anywhere. Her handwriting on the conduit labels. Her habit of coiling spare line in perfect figure-eights. A mug fossilized to a shelf by twenty years of mineral deposit, still bearing the faded print of some university I'd never heard of.

I began practicing with the interface harness in fifteen-minute sessions. Not syncing—not yet. Just learning the architecture of another woman's mind the way you learn the layout of a house whose owner has died: respectfully, sadly, taking mental notes.

Her memories were organized like a garden. Of course they were. Everything about Sol was gardens. There were beds of engineering knowledge, trellised procedures, whole seasons of colony history growing wild and unpruned. And at the center, gated, something dense and dark I chose not to open. Not yet. Eighty days gave me time to be a coward.

What unsettled me most, in those first sessions, was how familiar it all felt. Not the content—the shape. Her mind arranged information the way mine did, in rows, with attention to drainage and light. I told myself it was coincidence. Two people who loved growing things think alike.

I told myself a lot of things in the root vault.

* * *

Lessons happened in the greenhouse at mid-morning, when the light came through the polycarbonate panels at an angle that turned everything gold.

I taught them botany because it was the one subject I could teach honestly, and because hands in soil calm children in a way words cannot. Tomas, seven years old and carrying nightmares like stones in his pockets, took to propagation with startling gentleness—he could coax a cutting off a mother plant without tearing a single node. Amara, who had been the Vesper's navigator and was now nine, memorized planting calendars the way she'd once memorized star charts. Little Dev mostly ate things he shouldn't and had to be dissuaded from the chili peppers twice a week.

And Ines. Ines learned everything twice—once as a child encountering it new, and once, somewhere behind her eyes, as a woman who had already known it and forgotten knowing. I watched it happen over and over. I'd explain phototropism, and she'd nod along with the other kids, and then three days later she'd casually rotate a tray of seedlings a quarter-turn and say, "They lean if you don't," with the flat certainty of experience, and then look startled at her own hands.

"Do you remember your mother's garden?" I asked her once, while we thinned carrots. The children were off arguing about compost ratios at the far end of the bed.

"No." She worked the soil with small fingers, precise. "But my body remembers kneeling like this. Isn't that strange? The memory's gone and the knees kept it."

"Everything you've done is stored somewhere," I said. "Even the parts you can't find."

She looked up at me then, dirt on her cheek, and for a moment something moved across her face that wasn't twelve years old at all—an expression of such direct, unguarded regard that I had to turn back to the carrot bed and pretend the thinning required my complete attention.

My heart was doing something I had no botanical name for.

* * *

The seed vault became ours by accident.

It was the coolest room in the station, lined floor to ceiling with drawers of sealed heirloom stock—thousands of varieties collected by the original colonists, cataloged in Sol's handwriting, waiting eleven years for a future that kept not arriving. I went there to work on inventory. Ines started coming because it was quiet. Then the children came because Ines was there. Then, somehow, it was just the two of us again, most evenings, sorting and labeling by lamplight while the moon turned above us.

We invented a game, or she did. She'd pull a drawer at random and read the label, and we'd guess what the seeds would become. Scarlet runner beans. Moon-and-stars watermelon. Something called a Cherokee Purple tomato that Ines refused to believe was real until I showed her the photograph in the archive.

"That's the ugliest beautiful thing I've ever seen," she declared.

"They taste better than they look."

"Most true things do." She said it absently, sliding the drawer home, and I felt the sentence land somewhere under my ribs and stay there.

There was an evening—I mark it now as the evening—when we finished a long session of inventory and neither of us moved to leave. We sat on the floor between the drawer banks with a lamp between us, and she was telling me about the Vesper's voyage, the parts she still held clearly: the feel of the bridge under her boots, the sound of the long-range array singing, the specific weight of responsibility for forty sleeping crew. Her voice had slipped into its adult register without either of us noticing. For a few minutes she was thirty years old in a child's body, and I was listening to a captain tell me about the edge of explored space, and the lamplight was warm, and the vault smelled of paper and dry earth and time.

Then she stopped mid-sentence. "I lost it," she said. "The rest of that story. It was right here—" she pressed her fist to her sternum—"and it went out like a match."

"Ines—"

"Don't." She breathed. She was twelve again; I could hear it. "Don't do the face. Just—talk to me about something else. Tell me about the peach trees. You promised me peaches in year three and I intend to hold you to it."

So I told her about grafting, about how you take a piece of one tree and convince another tree to grow it as its own, and how the fruit that results belongs fully to neither and fully to both. She listened with her head tipped back against the drawer bank, eyes half closed, and when I finished she said, "That's the saddest beautiful thing I've ever heard," and fell asleep sitting up, mid-garden, trusting me completely.

I sat very still for a long time so as not to wake her, and understood, with the clarity of cold water, that I was in trouble of the best and worst kind.

* * *

The repairs filled whatever hours the lessons and the vault didn't claim.

The beacon's decay had physical symptoms we could treat, even if we couldn't yet cure the disease. I rebuilt the phase modulators one at a time, cannibalizing parts from systems that no longer needed to function—a colony that had never been colonized had left us a wealth of redundant machinery. Ines proved to have an engineer's hands despite everything; she could thread a waveguide coupling in the dark, and she remembered torque specifications she had no business remembering.

"Left-hand thread," she said once, stopping me before I stripped a fitting. "Sol marked all her nonstandard fittings with green tape. Look."

There was green tape, brittle and faded, on the housing. I looked at her. She looked at the tape.

"How did you know that?"

"I don't know." Her voice went small. "Paloma, sometimes I know things and I can feel that they're not mine. Like wearing someone else's coat. Is that—is that what happens when I get close to the core? Am I picking up Sol?"

I didn't have an answer I liked. The temporal field around the beacon behaved strangely—we'd measured that much—and Ines, de-aged by an anomaly, seemed to sit at some strange equilibrium near it, aging forward in increments too small to chart but too consistent to ignore. Every hour she spent in the lower chamber, she spent a little less as a child. I should have told her to stay away. Instead I found myself assigning her more work down there, and hating myself for it, and doing it anyway, because every hour she aged was an hour closer to the captain who could re-key the core, and ninety days is not generous with moral purity.

Seventy-eight days remained when I caught myself thinking, with a cold practicality that sounded nothing like me: *the faster she grows, the better our odds.*

I went out into the garden after that and stood among the beds until my hands stopped shaking, and I planted an entire row of lettuce I did not need, just to have something to do that was simple and honest and asked nothing of anyone's identity.

* * *

The stolen moments accumulated like coins in a jar.

A shared thermogram of tea on the observation deck, watching a meteor shower burn green in the upper atmosphere—Meridian's sky putting on a show no one had designed it for. Ines teaching me a hand-clap game the children played aboard the Vesper, both of us laughing so hard at my incompetence that Amara came to investigate and pronounced us "deeply weird." An argument about music that lasted three days and ended in a draw. The discovery that Ines snored, lightly, catastrophically, whenever she fell asleep in the vault, and my decision—selfish, tender—to never mention it.

Once, working late in the greenhouse, I found her standing at the long window looking out at the beacon tower against the stars, its warning lights pulsing their steady false heartbeat.

"Sixty-one days," she said, without turning.

"Fifty-nine. You're miscounting."

"I'm rounding up. It helps." She turned. Lamplight, leaf-shadow, her face half gold and half green. "Paloma. When this is over—if we pull it off, if the fleets never come, if we just... keep going—what happens then? To us?"

Us. She said it easily, the way you'd say *the garden* or *the station*, a thing that plainly existed and needed no definition. My chest did something complicated.

"Then we plant the orchard," I said. "Peaches. Year three, remember. You're holding me to it."

"And after the peaches?"

"After the peaches?" I shrugged, smiling. "Apples, I suppose. There's always something after the peaches. That's rather the point of a garden."

She nodded slowly, and I saw her file the answer away somewhere important, and I thought: *we are building something here out of almost-nothing, out of stolen evenings and shared labor, and it is the most fragile structure on this moon, and also the only one I would refuse to let fall.*

* * *

On day fifty-four, the beacon flickered.

It was brief—less than a second, a stutter in the false signature so small that if I hadn't been watching the monitor with tea halfway to my mouth I'd have missed it entirely. The waveform hiccupped. For 0.8 seconds, the true signature of Meridian—water, life, warmth—showed through the lie like a face at a window.

By the time I reached the lower chamber, it had stabilized. By the time Ines arrived, running, I had the diagnostics up and was lying to her with a straight face: routine fluctuation, nothing to worry about, the modulators run hot in the evening cycle.

She looked at me for a long moment. Twelve years old, old eyes, and I realized with a lurch that she knew I was lying—that she had learned my tells the way I'd learned hers, over weeks of lamplight and seed drawers, and that the intimacy I'd been treasuring had a price tag attached.

"The Halcyon probe launches in forty days," she said quietly. "Their survey ships reroute in sixty. If the beacon stutters even once while something's listening—"

"I know."

"We don't have fifty-four days, Paloma. We have however long it takes for that to happen again at the wrong moment." She put her hand flat against the core housing, and I watched her fingers whiten with pressure. "I need to start the syncs. With Sol's archive. Now."

"You're twelve."

"I'm the only key that fits." She turned to me, and her voice cracked straight down the middle, child and captain at once. "And you're the only one who knows how to hold the door open."

Outside, far above the garden, the beacon pulsed its steady lie into the dark, and for the first time in eleven years, I heard it as what it was becoming: a countdown.
