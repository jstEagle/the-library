## 35. The Price Paloma Paid

The collapse came in the greenhouse, between the tomato rows and the trellis where the Concord commander's daughter had planted a crooked row of beans.

Paloma had been showing her how to stake the vines—*loosely, Amara, the plant needs to move to grow strong, like anything else*—when the words simply stopped. Not faltered. Stopped, mid-syllable, as if someone had reached into their mouth and lifted the sentence out whole. Their hands went slack around the twine. The ball of jute dropped, rolled, unwound a lazy spiral across the flagstones, and Paloma followed it down.

They did not cry out. That was the worst of it, Ines would think later. No cry, no warning—just the slow toppling of a body that had been quietly emptying for months, the way a cistern empties through a hairline crack no one thinks to check until the garden goes thirsty.

Ines was across the compound, in the beacon chamber, standing witness while a Halcyon engineer and a Concord technician argued over calibration tolerances they had no right to argue over. She felt it before she knew it—the way you feel a change in pressure behind your ears, a door opening somewhere in a far room. The temporal field shivered. Every instrument on the wall flickered at once, and the anomaly's low hum, the one that had become the moon's second heartbeat, stuttered.

She was running before she understood why.

She ran through corridors that still smelled of solder and storm-dust from the repairs, past the med bay where Tomas slept off his nightmares, past the seed vault whose door she'd painted with the children's handprints in bioluminescent dye. She ran because some part of her—the part that had been a captain for thirty years and a child for two and something new entirely since—had learned that when the world shifted underfoot, you moved toward the person who anchored you.

She found them among the tomatoes.

Amara was kneeling beside Paloma, small hands pressed flat against their chest, saying *wake up, wake up* in the particular fierce whisper children use when they are trying not to be afraid. The jute lay coiled in the dirt. A bee—one of the first generation they'd raised from the cold-storage queens—circled the fallen watering can, confused by the absence of its route.

"Ines." Amara looked up, and her face was the face of every crew member Ines had ever lost, distilled to eight years old. "He just stopped talking."

"They," Ines said automatically, kneeling, and then the pronoun didn't matter anymore because nothing mattered except the weight of Paloma in her arms, lighter than it should have been, lighter than it had been even three weeks ago. "Paloma. Hey. Hey, I've got you."

Their eyes opened. And Ines felt the ground go out from under her a second time, because those eyes—warm brown, quick, always half-amused, always seeing her as both the child she'd been and the woman she'd become—looked at her with courtesy. With the polite blankness of a stranger at a funeral.

"Hello," Paloma said. Their voice was hoarse, as if they'd been shouting underwater for years. "I'm sorry—I don't—" They blinked, and Ines watched them search, watched the search come back empty, watched them try again. "You're the captain. Of the Vesper. You were a child. You're not now. I know that much."

"That's enough," Ines said, though her throat had closed to a wire. "That's plenty."

"It isn't, though." Paloma tried to sit up; she let them, bracing their shoulders against her own. Around them the greenhouse held its breath—tomato leaves trembling in the ventilation draft, the drip-irrigation ticking its patient count. "It isn't nearly enough. There's a hole here. A big one. Shaped like—" They pressed a hand to their sternum, pressing on the shape of the absence as if they could read it by touch. "Shaped like everything."

The medical scan, run twice in the med bay under the wary eyes of both fleets' physicians—who had, in these strange weeks of ceasefire, developed an uneasy habit of deferring to the colonists on all matters touching the anomaly—confirmed what Ines already knew in the marrow of her: the neural pathways braided with Sol's archive had burned through like fuse wire. Decades of Sol's memories remained, vivid and intrusive. But Paloma's own history had been consumed to feed the fire. Eleven years on Meridian: gone. The first landing: gone. The discovery of the root vault: gone. The long apprenticeship of loneliness, learning the garden's moods, the beacon's temper, the exact blue of dusk through the dome: gone.

And the weeks with Ines. The lessons in botany. The stolen hours in the seed vault. The falling, which had happened once and then been rebuilt deliberately, tenderly, after the last deep sync erased it the first time. Gone. Both times. All of it.

Ines sat beside the med cot through the afternoon and into the evening, holding Paloma's hand while the fleet doctors argued in low voices about neural grafts and memory scaffolds and prognoses delivered in the careful tones people use at bedsides. She let them talk. She already knew what she would do.

When the last of them left, she turned the lights down to the amber setting Paloma had once told her—had once told her—mimicked late sun on Earth, before either of their Earths had let them go.

"Paloma," she said. "I'm going to tell you a story. It might take a while. You don't have to believe any of it. You just have to listen."

Paloma's fingers twitched in hers. "Is it true?"

"Every word. I have witnesses." She almost smiled. "Most of them are seven years old."

* * *

She started at the beginning, because that was the only place stories could be trusted to start.

"You came to Meridian eleven years ago on a supply contract nobody wanted. The moon was listed as uninhabitable—that was the beacon's lie, though you didn't know it yet—and the pay was triple rate because no one else would take it. You'd left a life somewhere you never talked about, and you wanted a place so far from other people that other people couldn't find you. You got exactly what you asked for." She watched their face. Nothing. No flicker. She kept going. "For eleven years you tended a garden built for colonists who never came. Four hundred varieties in the seed vault. You knew every one of them by name. You talked to the plants because there was no one else to talk to, and you told yourself you didn't mind, and some days you even believed it."

"The loneliness," Paloma said slowly. Not remembering—reaching. "I can almost feel the shape of it. Like a coat I wore so long I forgot I had it on."

"Yes." Ines's chest ached. "And then a ship came. Ours. The Vesper, lost for six years, presumed dead. And when we landed, we were children. Every one of us. Forty-one crew aged backward by the anomaly our own charts had led us to. You should have turned us away. Any sane person would have. Instead you fed us. You taught us soil chemistry and composting and how to graft fruit trees, and you did it while terrified, because you'd found Adeyemi Sol's logs and you knew what the beacon was hiding and you knew the fleets were coming."

"I taught you?" Paloma looked down at their hands—hands that had, only this morning, staked tomato vines with unconscious grace. "These hands know things. They keep reaching for tools I can't name."

"They'll catch up," Ines said. "Your hands always learn faster than the rest of you. You told me that. You said the body keeps a garden even when the mind lets it go to weeds."

Something moved behind their eyes—not memory, but the ghost of memory's absence, grief for a thing they couldn't mourn properly. "Go on," they whispered.

* * *

She told them about the lessons. About how she, twelve years old with a captain's memories fraying like old film, had recognized the beacon's design because she'd studied Sol's work at the academy—because the Vesper's mission had never been exploration at all, but erasure, a survey to confirm the false signature and close the file forever. She told them about deciphering Sol's code together, hunched over the terminal in the root vault while the children slept, the two of them passing the same tea tin back and forth until dawn.

"You hated the tea," Paloma said suddenly. "No—you drank it anyway. You said—" Their brow furrowed, straining. "You said something about it tasting like punishment."

"Like penance," Ines corrected softly, heart hammering. "You said drinking my terrible tea was your penance for lying to forty-one children about the safety of their world. And then you kept making it. Every night. For me."

"I did?"

"For months." Her voice caught and she let it. There was no point hiding it; hiding was what had brought them here, all of them, the whole tangled architecture of concealment from Sol's first false signal to Paloma's final syncs. "You made it even after I grew up. Even after—" She stopped. Steadied. "After we fell in love. Which is the part I have to tell you next, and I need you to hear it without flinching, because you will want to flinch."

Paloma's hand tightened around hers. "Tell me."

"The first time, it happened slowly. Lessons in the greenhouse. Your patience with the children. The way you looked at me when I remembered something—like watching a photograph develop. Then I started aging forward near the beacon, and you blamed yourself, and I told you it was my choice, and neither of us believed the other, and somehow that was its own kind of intimacy." She laughed, wet-eyed. "Then the fleets scheduled their surveys, ninety days out, and you went into the root vault and found Sol's neural archive, and you started syncing with it to re-key the beacon. To buy us time. Each sync gave you what we needed and took what you were. I begged you to stop. You said, 'One of us has to be able to hold the signal, and you're the only other one who understands it, and I won't watch you burn.'" Her thumb traced circles on their knuckles. "So you burned instead. Quietly. A year of your memories, the first time. Then more. And then, after the sabotage and the storm—after we saved each other out on the ice plain with the field surging—you went deep one last time to stabilize the core before the fleets arrived. And you came back and didn't know me. The first time, I mean. You'd lost the falling-in-love. So I decided—if it was going to happen again, I wasn't going to grieve it. I was going to court you. Deliberately. Out loud. I brought you flowers from the orchard rows like an idiot. I read you Sol's logs in bad dramatic voices until you laughed. I kissed you in the seed vault on the night the accords were drafted, and you kissed me back, and you said—"

She stopped. Because Paloma was crying, silently, tears running into their collar, and their lips were moving as if following a text written somewhere behind their eyes.

"'You said,'" Paloma whispered, "'that loving someone isn't remembering them. It's choosing them again each morning. And you'd choose me every morning I had, however many mornings were left.'"

The words hung in the amber light. Ines realized she was shaking.

"You remember that."

"No." Paloma wiped their face with the heel of their hand, and smiled—a broken, blazing thing. "But my body does. Or my bones do. Whatever's left of me that isn't Sol. It heard you say it and it *agreed*. Ines—it agreed so hard I could taste it." They looked around the med bay, at the walls, the instruments, the small window showing the greenhouse lit soft green against the dark. "This is real. All of it. I can't see it but I can feel the weight of it, like furniture in a dark room. I know where everything is by bruising myself against it."

"Then let me be your lamp," Ines said. "For as long as it takes. I'll walk you through every room. We'll do it a hundred times if we have to. The tea, the logs, the storm, the vote, the trellis where the ceasefire started because the children wouldn't stop playing in the lettuce. I'll recite it all until it takes root."

"And if it doesn't take? If I sync again—if the core needs me—"

"Then we find another way. Together. No more burning alone. That's the deal. That's the *only* deal." She lifted their hand, pressed her lips to the calluses on their palm—gardener's calluses, earned twice over, once by a man they could no longer remember being. "You spent yourself keeping everyone safe. Let someone keep *you* now. Let it be me."

Outside, the beacon pulsed its steady invitation to the arriving ships, no longer a mask but a lighthouse, and its light came through the window and lay across the cot in long gold bars. Somewhere in the greenhouse, Amara was probably telling the others that the gardener had fallen down and the captain had run faster than anyone had ever seen, and embellishing it, and getting it wrong, and getting it perfectly right.

Paloma closed their eyes. "Tell me about the storm," they said. "Start with the storm. I want to know how you walked into it for me."

"Wrong way round," Ines murmured, settling closer, pulling the blanket over them both. "You went out first. Into a radiation storm, alone, to fix what a frightened seven-year-old broke, because you understood his fear better than his fear understood itself. I went out because I couldn't stand watching the door." She began, low and steady, the words worn smooth from the telling she'd already rehearsed in her head a hundred times, prepared for exactly this bedside. "The wind on the ice plain sounds like singing, if you let it. You taught me that..."

She talked. Paloma listened. And when they drifted toward sleep, their grip never loosened—as if some part of them understood, beneath the wreckage and the braid and the burn, that they were being held not just in arms but in a story, and that a self held in another's keeping is not lost. Only planted. Waiting for spring.

Ines stayed awake long after, memorizing the rhythm of their breathing, and did not notice—until the console by the door chimed softly, twice—that the beacon's pulse had changed.

Not failing. Not flickering.

*Answering.*
