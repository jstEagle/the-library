## 39. Ines Stays

The resignation letter was four sentences long, and it took Ines three days to write them.

She sat in the Vesper's wardroom—still called the wardroom, though the ship would never fly again, her hull opened like a seed pod on the plain below the garden, her corridors converted into dormitories and classrooms for the children who had once been her officers. The desk was the same one she'd commanded from at thirty-four, before the anomaly folded her backward through two decades. Her hands on its surface were the hands of a woman of nearly thirty now, steady, scarred at the knuckle from storm work, from beacon work, from the night she'd walked into the radiation wind to save a man who was half someone else.

Four sentences. She wrote them eleven times.

*I, Ines Reyes, captain of the deep-space vessel Vesper, do hereby resign my commission in the Concord Fleet, effective immediately.* Too formal. It sounded like a surrender.

*Effective immediately, I resign.* Too blunt. It sounded like a door slamming.

The problem, she understood finally, on the third evening, was that every version she tried made the resignation sound like an ending, and it wasn't an ending. It was a planting.

In the end she wrote:

*I, Ines Reyes, having been returned to myself by a moon that was never supposed to exist, hereby resign my commission in the Concord Fleet. I served because I believed the fleet kept people safe. I have since learned that safety is a thing that grows, and cannot be commanded. I am going where I can tend it.*

She read it aloud once, in the empty wardroom, and her voice cracked on *tend*, and she decided that was correct. Some words should cost you something to say.

* * *

The fleet's response came back in nine hours, which was fast, and Ines understood what the speed meant. Command had reviewed her file. Command knew what she was now: the captain who had been de-aged by an anomaly, restored by the same anomaly, witness to the confession of Adeyemi Sol, signatory to the Meridian Accords. She was not a person to them anymore. She was a jurisdictional complication.

The reply was signed by an admiral whose face Ines remembered from a briefing eleven years and one lifetime ago, a woman with a voice like a closing hatch. The message was courteous in the way that orbital mechanics are courteous—everything governed by forces too large to argue with. Her resignation was accepted with regret. Her pension would be computed from her *chronological* service record, a phrase that made Ines laugh out loud in the mess hall, because the fleet still could not decide how old she was, and had apparently decided the problem was unsolvable and therefore ignorable. She was invited—*invited*, the word doing the work of a drawn weapon—to make herself available for debriefing at the Concord's discretion. She was reminded that the temporal research sealed inside the beacon's core remained the property of the Concord Fleet under the Accords' Article Nine.

She was not thanked. She noticed that. Eleven years of service, a lost ship, a childhood spent twice, and the fleet's entire emotional register for her was *available for debriefing*.

She carried the message out onto the Vesper's hull, where the evening light was doing its slow amber work on the plain, and she read it again in the open air, and then she did the thing she had been wanting to do for three days. She deleted it. Not archived. Deleted. The fleet could keep its copies in its own vaults, in its own cold rooms. She was done being a document.

The garden was visible from the hull, terraced up the low hill like green handwriting on the moon's gray page. And in the middle terrace, small against the wide sky, a figure was moving down the rows with a water can, stopping, bending, touching leaves the way you'd touch the faces of sleeping children. Paloma. Who had burned most of their own memory to keep the lie alive long enough for the truth to be survivable. Who had woken in the medical bay knowing Ines only as a story someone had told them about themselves. Who had learned her again, deliberately, patiently, the way you learn a language—until the day Ines had said *do you remember the seed vault* and Paloma had said *yes* and meant it, meant all of it, and the word had landed in Ines's chest like a root finding water.

Ines stood on the hull of her dead ship, holding the ashes of her career in a deleted file, and felt the strangest thing, which was lightness.

* * *

She told the crew the next morning, in the greenhouse, because the greenhouse had become the place where the Vesper's people did their true talking. The children sat on the low benches among the seedling trays—her children, her officers, her lost decade made flesh—Tomas at nine now and steadier, Mara still collecting beetles, little Dev who had been her navigator and was now seven and could still, unnervingly, do orbital math in his head.

"I resigned," she said. "From the Concord."

She had expected questions. What she got was silence, and then Mara, without looking up from her beetle: "Good. Now you can stay."

That was all. That was the whole ceremony. The children went back to their seedlings, and Ines stood there with her speech unwritten and unneeded, and understood that she had spent three days composing a resignation for an audience that had never required one. The people who mattered had known she'd stay since the day she'd walked out into the storm.

Tomas caught her at the door. He was taller than he'd been at seven, and his nightmares had mostly gentled, though he still sometimes woke reaching for a railing that wasn't there—the railing of a ship that had burned, in a battle he'd seen at an age no one should see it.

"Captain," he said, and then corrected himself, the way they'd all agreed to: "Ines. Is it true the fleet wanted you to come back?"

"They wanted me to be *available*," Ines said. "That's different."

"Is it?"

She crouched to his height. Outside the greenhouse glass, the beacon stood on its ridge, no longer a mask but a lighthouse, its signal now the garden's true signature broadcast honest and open to anyone listening. "It's different," she said. "Availability is what you give people who own your time. Staying is what you give people who own your heart. I'm done being available."

Tomas considered this with the gravity of a nine-year-old who had once been a tactical officer and remembered enough of it to know when a sentence was load-bearing. "Okay," he said. "The apple seedlings are ready. Paloma said to tell you. They've been ready for two days, but Paloma said you'd want to do it when you were ready too, and not before."

Ines felt her throat tighten. "Of course they did," she said.

* * *

The orchard site had been chosen a year ago, in the way that Paloma chose everything—by walking the land until the land told them. It was a south-facing slope below the third terrace, sheltered from the storms by a spine of rock, with soil that Paloma had spent two seasons building: compost and crushed regolith and the slow black alchemy of the garden's own decay, turned and turned again until it smelled like the floor of an old forest, which on Meridian it technically was, the oldest forest the moon had, which was to say the only one, which was to say the beginning of one.

Forty seedlings waited in their trays. Apple, because the archive of Sol's had contained an apple—Ines had seen it in the memories Paloma still carried braided in with their own, Sol at twenty, biting into an apple on a station orbiting a world that no longer existed, and thinking *I will grow this again somewhere, someday, on something that's mine*. Sol had never gotten her orchard. She'd gotten a beacon and a death in a sealed core and forty years of a lie.

"We're planting her apples," Ines said, kneeling at the slope's edge, the trays fanned out before her.

"We're planting *our* apples," Paloma said. "She just carried them this far."

Paloma had changed since the collapse—since the day their memories had burned down to embers and Ines had held them and recited their history back like a rosary. Some of Paloma had come back on its own, the way a garden comes back after fire, from roots too deep to kill. Some had come back because Ines had planted it there, telling and retelling, until the telling became the memory. And some was Sol's, permanently, irreplaceably: Paloma sometimes paused mid-sentence with a far look and said things like *the regolith on Kestrel's Landing was redder* in a voice that wasn't quite their own. Ines had learned not to flinch. You didn't flinch at the weather. You gardened with it.

They dug together. The soil broke open dark and damp and breathing, and the smell of it rose up—that smell, the one Ines had no word for except *beginning*, wet mineral and living rot and something almost sweet, the smell of a world deciding to be one.

"You know," Paloma said, working the first hole wider with their hands, refusing the trowel, the way they always did, as if the soil could tell the difference between tools and fingers and would only forgive fingers, "I spent eleven years tending this garden for no one. I used to say it like a prayer. *For no one, for no one.* I thought devotion was the point. That it didn't matter if anyone ever came."

"And now?"

Paloma looked up. The light through the greenhouse glass caught the gray at their temple—the premature silver the syncs had paid for, the price still visible in their hair like frost that never melted. "Now I know the prayer was being answered the whole time. I just hadn't met the answer yet." They held out their hand. "Seedling."

Ines passed it over: a apple whip no taller than her forearm, its roots a pale tangle, its single bud-swelling already promising leaves. Paloma set it in the hole with the tenderness of someone setting down something breakable, and Ines packed the soil in around it, and they pressed the earth down together with their palms, four hands on one small tree.

"That's it," Paloma said quietly. "That's the first one."

Ines sat back on her heels. The slope stretched below them, forty holes waiting, the trays gleaming, the whole afternoon open in front of them like a road. Somewhere up the hill the children were shouting about something—Mara's beetle had escaped, by the sound of it—and the beacon hummed its honest signal at the sky, and two fleets' ships sat in parking orbits like large wary animals that had decided, for now, not to fight over the watering hole.

"Say something," Paloma said. "You've got the look."

"What look?"

"The look you got when you signed the Accords. Like you're holding a sentence too big for your mouth."

Ines laughed. She picked up the second seedling and turned it in her fingers, and found that the sentence was there after all, had been there for days, maybe for years, waiting for the ground to be ready.

"I spent my whole life," she said, "being made by other people. The fleet made me. The anomaly unmade me. Sol's archive, the Accords, the war, the lie—all of it, other people's hands on me, shaping. Even coming back to adulthood, even this—" she gestured at her own body, her own steady hands, "—that was the beacon's field, not my choice. I didn't get to choose anything. Not my age. Not my ship. Not my war." She set the seedling down in the second hole. "This is the first thing I've ever chosen with my whole self. This dirt. This tree. You." She looked up. "I'm not staying because I resigned, Paloma. I resigned so I could stay. There's a difference. The first is leaving something. The second is arriving."

Paloma was very still. Then they reached across the fresh-turned earth and took Ines's hand, soil and all, and pressed it, and neither of them said anything for a while, because some things are said better by pressure than by language, and they had both learned that in the medical bay, in the storm, in the long year of falling in love twice with the same person.

They planted all forty.

It took until the light went long and copper, and by the end they were working by the beacon's glow, and the children came down to help with the last row, small hands packing earth, Dev solemnly informing everyone that the trees would reach productive yield in approximately four to six standard years and Mara informing Dev that nobody asked him, and Tomas quietly, at the end, patting the last seedling's stem like the neck of an animal he was trusting with something.

When the children had gone up to supper, Ines and Paloma stayed on the slope with the forty small trees standing in the dusk, each one staked and watered and named—because Paloma insisted trees grew better with names, and Ines had stopped arguing about it, because the evidence was on Paloma's side. Sol got the first tree. The others got the names of the dead of the Vesper, and of the garden's forgotten colonists, and one, at Ines's insistence, got the name of the admiral with the voice like a closing hatch, "so that every year when it drops its apples, she can be useful for once."

Paloma laughed until they had to sit down in the dirt.

* * *

They walked back up through the garden in the dark, hand in hand, the path lit by the low amber lamps Paloma had strung through the terraces years ago for an audience of no one. The beacon pulsed softly on its ridge. In the seed vault, later, with the door closed and the smell of green all around them, Ines stood at the workbench and found Paloma already there, writing.

Not in the garden's logs. In the other book—the old one, the one they'd found in the root vault, Sol's log, the coded record of the lie. Except the code was broken now, had been broken for years, and the book had become something else. Paloma had been writing in it since the Accords. A new log. A true one. Addressed, Paloma said, to whoever tended the garden long after them, because someone always would, now; the garden was for someone, forever, and someone would always need to know where the bodies were buried and where the seeds were kept, and which was which.

"What are you writing?" Ines asked, coming up behind them, resting her chin on their shoulder.

"Log entry," Paloma said. "4,413." They turned their head and kissed her, unhurried, the way the garden did everything. "It's about the orchard."

"Read it to me."

Paloma smiled, and looked down at the page, and Ines watched their face as they read, and that was when she saw it—the small hitch, the flicker, the far look that was not Sol this time but something else, something new, something that had arrived in Paloma's eyes like a ship at the edge of a scan.

"What?" Ines said. "What is it?"

Paloma looked up from the page, and their voice, when it came, was quiet and strange and full.

"There's a ship coming," they said. "The beacon just flagged it. It's not a fleet ship." They turned the logbook toward her, and Ines saw the entry, the fresh ink, the last line still wet: *To whoever tends this garden after us: the first settlers arrive in spring. Plant them apples.*

"Paloma," Ines said slowly, "the Accords didn't schedule any settlers until—"

"I know," Paloma said. "I know. But someone read our signal. Someone out there read it years ago, maybe—someone who's been traveling toward this garden since before we ever told the truth about it." They looked at each other, and outside, far off, at the very edge of the dark, a new light was rising over the ridge line, steady and patient and getting brighter.

"Who is it?" Ines asked.

Paloma shook their head, and picked up the logbook, and held it the way you hold something that has just become heavier. "I don't know," they said. "But they're answering a signal we didn't know we sent."
