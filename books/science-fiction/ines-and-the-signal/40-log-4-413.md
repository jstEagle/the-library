## 40. Log 4,413

The garden had never been this loud.

Paloma stood at the edge of the eastern terrace with a cup of barley tea going cold in their hands and listened to it: the shriek and laughter of children running the drip-line rows, the clatter of the new hydroponic frames the Halcyon engineers had bolted together in a single feverish week, the low argument of two Concord agronomists debating nitrogen fixation with the passion other people reserved for theology. Somewhere near the orchard—*their* orchard, the one with forty saplings in the ground and names chalked on the tree guards—a Halcyon shuttle pilot was teaching a Vesper kid to whistle through a blade of grass, badly, and the kid was laughing like it was the finest music in the system.

Eleven years Paloma had tended this garden for no one. It had been quiet then. It had been quiet the way a held breath is quiet.

Now it exhaled.

They took a slow sip of the tea and let the noise wash over them, and beneath the noise, beneath everything, they felt the other sound—the one only they and a handful of others could hear anymore, the beacon's new voice, broadcasting not a lie but an invitation, a lighthouse pulse that said *we are here, we are growing, come see.*

The old log was waiting for them in the root vault, as it always was. Sol's log. Log 4,412, the last entry in a dead woman's hand, written in a code that had once taken Paloma and Ines three weeks of shared insomnia to crack. Paloma had read it a thousand times. They had read it so many times that the words had stopped being Sol's and become something the garden itself said, the way old trees creak in wind.

*If you are reading this, you are tending what I planted. I am sorry for the weight of it.*

Paloma had decided, three days ago, on a morning much like this one, that it was time to answer.

* * *

The root vault smelled of earth and old polymer and, faintly, of the neural archive's coolant—a cold mineral note beneath the warm dark, like a river running under a field. Paloma knelt at the vault's mouth and lifted the panel, and there it was: Sol's terminal, scarred and patient, its screen waking at their touch with the amber glow that had become, over the past year, as familiar as sunrise.

They had thought, once, that sitting at this terminal would feel like wearing a dead woman's coat. For a while it had. During the syncs—during those terrible, necessary weeks when Paloma had poured themselves into Sol's archive to re-key the beacon, trading their own memories for her expertise, their own years for her knowledge—it had felt like drowning in someone else's life. They had surfaced with her hands in their memory, her grief in their chest, her confession lodged in them like a seed that would not stop growing: *I broke the sky on purpose. I bought them time. I broke the Vesper doing it, and I could not make myself stop.*

But that was a year ago now. The braid had settled. Paloma had learned to live in the composite country of their own head—their mother's kitchen in the Lagos arcologies, and Sol's first frost on Meridian; the smell of Paloma's first seed flat, and the weight of Sol's hand on the beacon's core as she sealed herself out of it. Some memories had come back. Some never would. Some were Sol's forever now, and Paloma had stopped trying to sort them into piles. A garden was not a monument. You did not restore a garden; you tended what grew, and what grew was always partly what you planted and partly what the wind brought.

They opened a new file.

The cursor blinked. Paloma sat with it a long moment, listening to the vault's silence, and then they began to type—slowly, in plain language, in no code at all, because the whole point now was to be understood.

*Log 4,413. Meridian. Year twelve of the garden, year one of the peace.*

*My name is Paloma Okafor. I did not plant this garden, and I did not build the beacon, and I did not break the sky. I only kept the plants alive and the lie running, for eleven years, for no one, because someone had to. If you are reading this—if anyone is reading this, in ten years or a hundred or after whatever comes next comes and goes—then the keeping worked, or it failed in a way that still left something growing, which is the same thing.*

*You will find Engineer Adeyemi Sol's entries below mine. Read them all, even the hard ones. Especially the hard ones. She hid the truth of what she did inside her maintenance logs the way you'd graft a scion onto hardy rootstock—because the truth alone would not have survived, and the lie alone would have borne no fruit. She was a saboteur and a savior and she was both on purpose, and I have made my peace with that, most days. Some days I haven't. Tend the garden anyway.*

*Here is what I know that she did not live to write down: the lie was never the thing that held. The beacon bought us time, yes. The false signature turned away the Concord, the Halcyon, the probe, the survey wings, all of it. But what actually held—what actually stopped two fleets from tearing this moon apart—was a table under a trellis, and tomatoes on it, and commanders' children playing tag between the rows with children who had once been a starship's crew. Power came here looking for a lever and found a garden, and a garden is a terrible lever and a very good argument. Remember that. Whatever you are keeping, whatever mask you are maintaining, whatever impossible thing you are holding up alone: the mask is not the miracle. The miracle is whatever is still alive underneath it.*

*The beacon is re-keyed. It speaks the truth now—the real atmospheric readings, the real soil chemistry, the real coordinates of the orchard and the seed vault and the medical bay. It also carries the Accords on a repeating channel, all nine articles, in Concord Standard and Halcyon trade-script and the Vesper crew's own dialect, which Ines insists on because she says a peace that only speaks one language is just a longer war. Ships come now. Not many. A supply hauler last month with soil from three different worlds. A survey team that stayed two weeks and left behind a wind chime made of spent fuel cells, which is the ugliest and most beloved object on this moon.*

*The temporal anomaly is contained. Sol's containment holds, and my modifications hold, and the field still pools near the beacon the way heat pools near a stove, so be careful there—time runs a little fast, a little strange, and if you stand too long you will come back to the garden having missed a little of it, or gained some, we've never fully worked out which. Ines says that's not a flaw. Ines says a garden should have weather.*

*Ines. I should tell you about Ines.*

Paloma paused. Their hands hovered over the keys. Through the vault's open panel, faint and far, they could hear the children again, and one voice above the rest—low now, adult, steady, giving instructions about grafting angles with the same calm authority she had once used to give helm orders—and Paloma smiled at the screen in the dark.

*Ines Reyes was the captain of the Vesper, the ship my beacon's lie was partly built to protect and partly built to hide from. The anomaly aged her crew backward into children, and she came back to herself a twelve-year-old with thirty years of memory fraying off her like old paint. She remembered the beacon's design before she remembered her own surname. She cracked Sol's code in a week. She voted to reveal the garden when the vote tied, knowing what it might cost, and then she walked into a radiation storm to help a stubborn gardener hold the beacon together, and she nearly died in the temporal surge, and she would tell you herself that the nearly dying was the easy part.*

*She is thirty-one now. The field gave her back her years, all of them, and she resigned her commission with a straight spine and a steady voice and planted the first apple sapling with her own hands an hour later. She is teaching the children navigation using the orchard rows as a star chart. She is teaching me to rest, which is harder than botany. She is the reason the log you are reading exists in this form, because it was her idea that the next keeper deserve the truth in plain words.*

*If you ever meet her, bring coffee. She has opinions.*

Paloma sat back. The entry was long—longer than anything Sol had ever left, longer than anything Paloma had ever written that wasn't a maintenance report or a seed inventory. It felt right. Sol had written in code because she had to hide; Paloma wrote in the open because they had finally, finally earned the right to be understood.

They were reaching for the commit key when a shadow fell across the vault's mouth, and a voice said, dry as good soil, "You're writing about the coffee opinions, aren't you."

* * *

Ines Reyes stood at the top of the vault steps with her sleeves rolled and dirt to her elbows and a flat of tomato seedlings balanced on one hip, and Paloma thought—not for the first time, not for the thousandth time—that there was no getting used to it. A year since she'd stood in the storm. A year since the field had finished its slow work and given her back her height, her hands, her face, the gray thread at her temple that she claimed she'd earned at twenty-six and dared anyone to argue. A year, and still some mornings Paloma looked up and saw the captain of the Vesper standing in the garden and had to relearn, all over again, that this was the same person who had once fallen asleep mid-sentence in the seed vault, aged eleven, mid-argument about whether legumes could fix nitrogen in regolith.

"You're writing about the coffee opinions," Ines said again, descending the steps, setting the seedlings down with the care she gave to everything living. "I can tell by your face. You have a face you make when you're being sentimental in writing. It's the same face you make when you thin the carrots."

"I am not sentimental. I am historically accurate."

"You wrote *she has opinions* with a capital O. I felt it from up there."

Paloma turned the screen toward her. Ines read, and Paloma watched her read—the way her eyes moved, the way her mouth went soft at the parts about the storm and went softer at the parts about herself and went carefully, carefully neutral at the parts about Sol. She had made her own peace with Sol, in her own time, in her own way: long nights at this same terminal, reading the confession over and over, the woman who had broken the sky and broken the Vesper and bought them all their lives with the same act. Ines had come out of those nights saying only, "She owed us an apology and gave us a garden instead. I've decided to accept the exchange rate."

Now she reached past Paloma and, instead of taking the keyboard, pulled the spare stool from the wall and sat down beside them, shoulder to shoulder in the amber light.

"Add one thing," she said.

"Anything."

"Tell them about the vote." Her voice changed—captain's voice, but gentle with it. "Not the greenhouse vote. The one before. The one where it was just the two of us, and I wanted to broadcast, and you wanted to protect the children, and we fought about it for nine days and didn't speak for two of them." She looked at the screen. "Tell them the fight was the point. Tell them that when the vote tied, and I broke it toward revelation, you didn't speak to me for two days, and then you came to me at midnight with a draft of the broadcast in your hand, because you'd spent those two days writing the truth so carefully that it could survive being told. Tell them that's what a real argument between people who love each other looks like: not one of us winning. Both of us building."

Paloma looked at her for a long moment. "You've been thinking about this."

"I've been thinking about it since the Accords were signed. Since before." She touched the screen, just above the cursor, not typing. "Sol wrote to whoever came after her, and she wrote alone, in a code nobody was meant to crack, and it nearly broke everything. We're writing to whoever comes after us, and we're writing together, in plain words, and it's the only reason any of this worked." A small, crooked smile. "That's the whole lesson. That's the entire curriculum. Everything else is botany."

So Paloma typed it. *The fight was the point. Both of us building.* And Ines read it, and nodded once, the way she nodded over a successful burn, and then she stood and brushed off her knees and said the tomatoes weren't going to harden themselves off, and Paloma followed her up the steps into the noise and the light.

* * *

That evening, after the children were abed and the agronomists had argued themselves into agreement and the wind chime made of spent fuel cells was singing its ugly song off the eastern eave, Ines came back to the vault alone.

She had not told Paloma she was coming. This was her own errand, her own small ceremony, and she suspected—correctly—that Paloma would understand. The terminal still glowed. Log 4,413 sat open at the bottom, Paloma's last line still warm on the screen:

*The garden is for someone now. Tend it. Tend them. They are the same work.*

Ines pulled the stool close and sat, and thought about what a captain's log was for, and what a garden log was for, and how it had taken her two lifetimes—first one cut short, then one given back—to understand they had always been the same document.

She typed slowly, with dirt still in the creases of her knuckles.

*I was going to write something formal. I deleted it three times. So: this is Ines Reyes, formerly captain of the Vesper, currently the person who fixes the irrigation on Tuesdays. I have been a child and an adult on this moon, and I recommend both, in that order, if you can manage it. I have been lied to by a beacon and saved by a garden and I recommend the second one unreservedly.*

*Whoever you are: you will be tempted, one day, to keep something hidden. Something you're ashamed of, or afraid of, or protecting. Sol hid the truth and it cost her everything. Paloma kept the lie and it cost them a year of their own memory. I held my silence when I should have spoken and it cost me my crew's childhoods. Hiding is expensive. It always has been. The garden taught me the alternative: you plant the seed in the open ground, in the full light, where anyone can see it and anyone can help it grow, and that is the only way anything here has ever survived.*

*The beacon says what's true now. So does this log. Keep it that way.*

*The tomatoes are ready to go in the ground tomorrow. If you're reading this in season, put some in for us. If you're reading this out of season, put some in anyway. Under glass. Trust me.*

*The signal carries forward. So do we.*

She looked at the entry a long time—Paloma's words and hers, braided on the same page the way their memories were braided now, the way the fleets were braided into the Accords, the way everything on this moon had learned to grow into everything else. Then she committed the file to the archive, beside Sol's 4,412, and keyed the beacon's auxiliary channel—the small one, the one that didn't reach the fleets, that reached only the seed vault and the root vault and the little transmitter they'd built into the orchard's first tree, the one that would carry the log to whatever terminal, whatever keeper, whatever pair of hands came after theirs.

Outside, the beacon pulsed its honest pulse against the dark. Ines climbed the steps and stood a moment in the cool night, looking up at where the signal went, out past the orchard, past the fleets at their long peaceful anchor, past the system, past everything either of them would ever see.

Somewhere out there, someone was going to read this. Someone was going to kneel at a vault mouth and open a panel and find two voices instead of one.

She smiled, and went to find Paloma, and the tomatoes, and the morning.
