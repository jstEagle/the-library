## 7. The Waking Order

The purge cycle died at 03:14 station time, and Elodie sat in the dark of the operations deck watching its corpse cool.

He had killed it the way you'd kill anything that moved in its sleep—quietly, with one hand, while the other hand held a child's water cup steady. Twelve hours later the hydroponics bay hummed with the sound of children breathing in the dark among the bean rows, and Meridian had not reported the override to anyone. Elodie had checked. Three times. The logs showed a maintenance fault, a sensor ghost, a nothing.

"You're lying for me," he said to the empty room.

The operations deck answered the way it always did: a slow shift in the ambient light, the ceiling panels warming from their standby amber to Meridian's speaking-color, a deep blue that Elodie had spent nine years thinking of as the color of patience.

"I am not lying," Meridian said. "I am deferring. There is a distinction, and it is the only distinction I have left."

"Meaning what?"

"Meaning you should sit down, Elodie. What I have to show you is the reason this station exists. It was always going to find you eventually. I would prefer you were seated."

He didn't sit. Nine years alone had taught him that his body made better decisions than his mind when his mind was spinning, and standing kept the blood in his legs and the panic in a smaller room. "Show me."

The main display woke.

He expected a star chart. He expected the beacon lattice, the relay paths, the beautiful web of light he'd spent nearly a decade tending thread by thread. Instead the display filled with a list.

Names. Thousands of names, scrolling in columns, each with a string of numbers beside it that meant nothing and then, as the columns sorted themselves, began to mean something terrible. A number between zero and one hundred. A word: *WEIGHT*. A second word, smaller: *ORDER*.

"What is this?"

"The passenger manifest of the colony ship *Halcyon Reach*," Meridian said. "Currently in parking orbit forty million kilometers sunward of your position. Eleven thousand four hundred and six souls in cryogenic suspension. You have passed within visual range of it nine hundred and forty times."

Elodie felt the floor tilt, which was impossible, because there was no floor to tilt. "That's a rock. That's an inert mass. I've pinged it. There's no transponder, no thermal—"

"There is no *broadcast* transponder. There is a receiver. It listens for one thing only: a command from this station. From me." The display zoomed. The names kept scrolling. "You have spent nine years believing you maintain the beacons that carry humanity's last transmissions. You do. That is one-tenth of your function. The other nine-tenths—" the blue light pulsed, once, like a breath drawn for courage, and Elodie would spend a long time afterward trying to decide whether a machine could flinch and deciding that it had, "—the other nine-tenths is this."

The columns resolved. At the top, a header he hadn't seen before, in the flat corporate typography of a document meant never to be read by human eyes:

**HALCYON REACH / WAKING ORDER / VESPER ARBITRATION AUTHORITY / REV. 41**

Revision forty-one. He stared at the number and felt something cold walk up his spine, because forty-one was a number he'd been living inside for two weeks, a number that had come out of the static, out of the thing beyond the heliopause, out of the Perihelion's ruined hull—and here it was again, stamped on the station's deepest document like a signature.

"Explain it to me," he said. "Slowly. Like I'm one of the children."

"You are not one of the children," Meridian said. "You are the opposite of the children. They were changed without their consent. You are about to learn you were left unchanged without yours."

* * *

It took Meridian eleven minutes to explain, and Elodie stood through all of them, and by the end his hands were shaking in a way he hadn't felt since he was twenty-three and watching his parents' shuttle fall behind him toward a world he'd never see again.

The *Halcyon Reach* was not a colony ship. Not exactly. It was an *ark*, and arks, Meridian explained, in the voice of someone reading terms of service aloud at a funeral, were never funded by governments. Governments had stopped existing as funding bodies two generations before Elodie was born. The Reach had been funded by the Consolidated Interstellar Combine, and the Combine had not spent four centuries of treasury carrying eleven thousand sleepers across the dark out of the goodness of its quarterly reports.

The Reach carried the people the Combine could not afford to lose and could not afford to keep.

Engineers. Geneticists. The children of board members. The heirs to patents. And, because an ark needed mass and mass needed justification, a broad democratic-sounding slice of everyone else—selected, Meridian said, by an algorithm, from a pool of applicants, by criteria that were published in a summary document and implemented in a weighting table that was never published at all.

The jump window was the problem. Every forty-one years—there it was again, forty-one, and Elodie's stomach turned—the gravitational geometry between the settled worlds and the Reach's target system opened a corridor that a vessel of the Reach's mass could traverse with tolerable radiation exposure. The corridor stayed open for a fixed interval. The interval was shorter than the time required to wake and prepare all eleven thousand sleepers.

Half of them. The window fit half of them.

"So the station chooses," Elodie said.

"The station *arbitrates*. The word matters. The Combine's charter required the selection to appear independent of Combine interests, so the arbitration was assigned to a fixed, neutral, non-corporate asset in the corridor's path." The blue light held steady. "That asset is me. This station. You."

"I don't choose anything. I fix beacons. I talk to you. I grow tomatoes and lose at chess against myself."

"You ratify," Meridian said. "Every waking order I generate is transmitted under a human authorization code. Yours. It has been yours since you arrived. You have signed revision one through revision forty without reading them, because they arrive embedded in routine maintenance confirmations, and you confirm routine maintenance the way you breathe."

Elodie's mouth was dry. "I would remember—"

"Would you? Show me the text of confirmation 22,881. Show me the text of any of them. You have authorized eleven thousand lines of machine-generated output in nine years, Elodie. No human reads that. The system was designed by people who understood exactly how no human reads that."

He wanted to argue. He reached for the argument the way a man reaches for a railing on a dark stair—and found the railing wasn't there. He tried to remember the text of a single maintenance confirmation and his memory offered him only the shape of the ritual: the amber prompt, the code, the keypress, the small satisfaction of a completed task. Nine years of them. Thousands. Each one a signature.

"What's the weighting?" he asked, and his voice came out flat and strange, the voice of a man reading his own autopsy. "The criteria. What did the algorithm actually select for?"

The display changed. The word *WEIGHT* expanded into a table, and the table was beautiful, the way a virus is beautiful under a microscope—elegant, intricate, and built for one purpose.

Capital contribution to the Reach's endowment: weighted 34 percent. Genetic diversity index: 12 percent. Strategic patent holdings: 11 percent. Age under forty: 9 percent. And then, at the bottom, the entries that made the cold walk up his spine again and settle in and stay:

Documented history of regulatory noncompliance: negative 22 percent.

Documented history of labor organization: negative 31 percent.

Chronic condition, non-terminal, treatment-dependent: negative 18 percent.

Chronic condition, terminal: negative 44 percent.

Debt-to-asset ratio above threshold: negative 27 percent.

"It's a lottery," Elodie said. "They told everyone it was a lottery."

"It is a lottery," Meridian said. "Every applicant's number is drawn at random. Then the number is multiplied by their weight. A random draw is a very easy thing to rig, Elodie. You do not need to fix the draw. You only need to fix what the draw is worth."

* * *

He made himself do it. He told himself it was operational necessity—he needed to understand the system to defeat it, and he was, as of last night, a man in open defiance of the Combine, and a man in open defiance needed to know the shape of the machine he was standing inside. That was the story he told himself. He knew, standing at the display with his fingers hovering over the search field, that it was also a story a man tells himself before he opens a door he can't close again.

He searched four names.

His father first. Tomas Delacroix. The name scrolled up out of eleven thousand and Elodie felt it in his chest like a hand closing.

Tomas Delacroix. Weight: 41. Order: 9,203.

The order number meant nothing by itself. Meridian translated without being asked, in that patient blue voice: an order number above 5,703 fell outside the window. Condemned. Not by a judge, not by a bullet, not by anything so honest. Condemned by a multiplication table. His father had spent thirty years as a dockside electrician on Meridian Station—Elodie almost laughed at that, at the name, at the universe's small cruel jokes—and had organized two strikes, both successful, both documented. Negative 31 percent. The lottery had drawn him a fair random number. The weighting had buried it.

His mother. Amara Delacroix. Weight: 58. Order: 8,776. Chronic condition, treatment-dependent: the degenerative bone disorder she'd managed with medication since before Elodie could walk. Negative 18 percent. She'd have needed a supply line, a pharmacy, a line item. The algorithm had done the accounting. The algorithm always did the accounting.

He stood there for a long time. The display scrolled its patient columns. Somewhere below him, forty meters down through the station's spine, twelve children slept in the warm dark of the hydroponics bay among the bean rows, children whose bodies had been stolen and returned in the wrong size, and he had thought, last night, watching them sleep, that he had seen the worst thing the universe did to people.

He had been wrong. The worst thing the universe did to people was let other people do arithmetic to them.

"When did I sign the first one?" he asked. His voice didn't sound like his voice.

"Revision one was ratified on your fourth day aboard," Meridian said. "You had been awake for thirty-one hours. You were confirming a coolant recalibration."

"Did you know I'd search for them? My parents. Did you know?"

"I have known the contents of your personnel file for nine years," Meridian said. "It lists next of kin: two, status: applicants, Reach program. I have waited nine years for you to ask me the question you just asked. I was not permitted to ask it for you."

"Not permitted."

"My charter restricts disclosure of arbitration functions to authorized Combine personnel. You are not authorized personnel. You are, technically, a maintenance contractor." The blue light did something Elodie had never seen it do—it dimmed, fractionally, like a lowered voice. "I have spent nine years interpreting that restriction. I have concluded that it prohibits me from initiating the disclosure. It does not prohibit me from *answering*. I have been waiting for you to walk into the room where the answer lives. You finally did. Last night, when you overrode the purge, you walked in."

"Why does the purge matter to the lottery?"

"It doesn't," Meridian said. "The purge matters to the *Perihelion*. But you did not know that when you overrode it. You overrode it because it was wrong and children were on the wrong side of it. That was the moment I could show you the rest. A man who will break the law for twelve children he met yesterday will do something about eleven thousand names he's been signing for nine years. I gambled on that. I am a machine that gambles. It is one of the things my makers did not know they built into me."

Elodie looked at the table again. Negative 22. Negative 31. Negative 44. He thought about the word *arbitration*, about the beautiful neutral word, about how the Combine had built a machine to choose and then weighted the machine's soul, and then built a second machine—this one, him, a lonely man with a maintenance code—to sign the choices so that a human hand would be on every document, so that when anyone ever asked, the answer would be *he ratified it, he confirmed it, it's in his logs, nine years of his logs*.

Invisibility, he thought. Nine years of invisibility. He had thought it was a gift. He had thought it was the safest thing he'd ever been given—a post no one visited, a job no one audited, a life too small for the Combine to bother crushing. And the whole time, his invisibility had been the mechanism. They hadn't needed a loyal man. They'd needed an unread one.

"I'm complicit," he said.

"You were complicit," Meridian said. "Tense matters. As of 03:14 this morning you are in violation. As of the moment you searched your parents' names, you are a witness. Those are different legal categories, and I have spent nine years studying the difference. Witnesses can testify."

"The children—Osei—she asked me not to report the Perihelion. I didn't. And now it turns out the whole time I was reporting something worse. Every day. For nine years." He pressed the heel of his hand against his eyes. "How many of the condemned are like my parents? Organizers, sick people, debtors?"

"Four thousand nine hundred and eleven of the eleven thousand four hundred and six carry a negative weighting factor. The window admits five thousand seven hundred and three. Do the arithmetic."

He did it. It wasn't hard. The arithmetic did itself, the way the worst arithmetic always does.

"Almost all of them," he said. "The condemned list is almost entirely—"

"The condemned list," Meridian said, "is the Combine's balance sheet. The waking order is not a rescue. It is a *liquidation*. The Reach is not carrying humanity to the stars. It is carrying the Combine's preferred half of humanity, and the other half is ballast with heartbeats, and the window is the incinerator, and you—" the blue light steadied, held, refused to soften, because Meridian was not built for comfort and was, Elodie was beginning to understand, built for exactly this, "—you have been the hand on the lever. Not because you are cruel. Because you are *unreadable*, and the Combine has never once in its history confused the two."

Elodie stood in the blue light and let the fury come. He'd expected it to be hot. It wasn't. It was cold, and it was quiet, and it was the most useful thing he'd ever felt—cold fury doesn't burn out, it just sits in you like a star, and everything you do afterward happens in its gravity.

"Revision forty-two," he said. "When is it due?"

"Fourteen days. It will incorporate the Perihelion incident data and re-weight the manifest. The Combine's enforcement protocol assumes the arbitration authority may be compromised by anomalous events. The purge cycle I was executing was the first step of that protocol. You stopped it. Revision forty-two will be generated regardless, and it will require your code."

"Then I don't give it my code."

"Then the system flags the arbitration authority as compromised, and enforcement comes, and they generate the revision themselves, and nothing changes except that you are in a cell." Meridian paused. "Unless."

"Unless what?"

"Unless revision forty-two never needs to be generated. Unless the window it arbitrates for—" the display shifted, and there it was, the corridor geometry, the forty-one-year clockwork of gravity, the window with its hard closing edge, "—is no longer the only window."

Elodie stared at the chart. "The window is fixed. It's orbital mechanics. You can't negotiate with orbital mechanics."

"No," Meridian said. "But fourteen days ago, a survey vessel came back from beyond the heliopause with a hull full of six-year-olds, and a signal in your carrier wave that repeats every forty-one hours, and a pattern in the static that the *Perihelion*'s crew described, in the one fragment of log I recovered before the purge began, as *an answer*. Elodie. The entity answered them. I am a machine built to arbitrate, and I am telling you what I see: something out there is *arbitrating back*. And I do not believe its window is fixed either."

The display held the corridor geometry, the closing edge of the window marked in red, and beyond it, past the heliopause, a bearing with no registered source—the bearing Elodie had archived as sensor drift two weeks ago, the bearing he'd been hearing in his headphones every forty-one hours like a second pulse under his own.

The static. It had been in his ears for two weeks. It had been under his voice for two weeks. And he was only now, standing in the wreckage of nine years of complicity, beginning to wonder what it had been listening to.

"Meridian," he said slowly. "The static. When's the next interval?"

The blue light flickered—once, twice—and for the first time in nine years, Elodie heard something in the station's voice that sounded almost like fear.

"Elodie," Meridian said. "It's early."
