## 10. Small Hands, Heavy Work

The purge system lived in the sublevel beneath the relay core, a room Elodie had visited exactly twice in nine years—once during his orientation, once when a coolant line wept and he'd spent four hours swearing at a fitting in the dark. It was not built to be loved. It was built to be obeyed: racks of black server housings, the wipe arrays with their amber standby lights, and Meridian's physical larynx, a pillar of frosted glass that pulsed faintly when she spoke through the station speakers.

"It will take three cycles to disable without tripping the watchdog," Meridian said. Her voice came from everywhere, as always, but tonight it seemed to come from closer, almost apologetic. "The watchdog is me. I can look away for approximately ninety seconds at a time before the enforcement telemetry notices the gap."

"Then we work in ninety-second pieces."

"We?"

Elodie turned. Osei stood in the doorway of the sublevel, and behind her, filing down the ladder one small rung at a time, came the others. Twelve children in oversized station coveralls rolled six times at the cuffs. They arranged themselves in the cramped room with the eerie discipline of adults who had all, at some point, managed crews.

"Captain," Elodie said carefully, "I don't think—"

"You don't think I'm letting you do this alone." Osei crossed her arms. The gesture belonged to a woman who had commanded a survey vessel for eleven years; it looked strange and heartbreaking on a body that barely cleared his hip. "You've been alone too long, Mr. Nakamura. You forget how work gets done. Nobody does anything alone. That's the first thing they teach you out past the heliopause, right after *don't answer anything that answers you.*"

A boy near the back—tall for six, solemn, with the deep-set eyes of a man who had once been the Perihelion's chief engineer—raised his hand like a student.

"Vasquez," he said. "I wrote the original purge firmware. Well. I'll write it. In about thirty years." A ripple of dry laughter from the children, laughter that had nothing childish in its timing. "The point is, I know where the seams are. And my hands are the only ones on this station small enough to get inside the array housing without stripping the seals."

"He's right," Osei said. "You have big hands, Elodie. Wonderful hands for beacons. Useless hands for this." She looked up at him, and there was something in her face he was learning to read—the adult patience sitting inside the child's features like a passenger. "We have maybe eleven days. Let us help. It's what we're for."

He wanted to argue. He heard himself wanting to argue, heard the old instinct that had kept him safe and solitary for nine years: *fewer variables, fewer people, fewer things to lose.* But he'd spent those years talking to an AI and repairing machines that never talked back, and he knew exactly what that had made him. Complicit. Invisible. A man who watched a lottery condemn half a colony and logged it as routine telemetry.

"Okay," he said. "Ninety seconds at a time. Vasquez inside the housing. Osei calls the clock. Everyone else—" he looked around at twelve small faces lit by amber standby lights, "—everyone else learns the station. If they take me, you need to know how Vesper breathes."

Meridian dimmed the purge arrays' status indicators—a small mercy, a small crime—and the work began.

* * *

Osei counted the seconds in her head because counting them out loud made the children flinch, and flinching children made noise, and noise got people erased.

She stood at the sublevel door with her palm flat against the frame, feeling the vibration of the station through the metal the way she used to feel the Perihelion's hull under her boots. Ninety seconds was nothing. She had once held position for nineteen hours while a micrometeorite swarm stitched holes across their forward shield, listening to her crew breathe over the open channel, deciding whose oxygen to route where. Ninety seconds was a gift.

"Forty," she murmured.

Inside the housing, Vasquez worked by feel. His hands were extraordinary—she'd always known it, had fought corporate procurement for two years to keep him aboard when they tried to rotate him groundside. Now those hands, shrunken and soft-skinned, moved through the wipe array's guts with total confidence, disconnecting the checksum bridge, splicing the loop that would make the purge report completion while doing nothing at all.

"Sixty," she said.

"Got the bridge," Vasquez whispered. His voice hadn't changed pitch so much as changed *weight*—the words of a fifty-year-old man coming out of a child's mouth, and everyone in the room had long since stopped reacting to it. "Looping now. Elodie, your board should show green."

On the diagnostic panel, the first purge cycle reported itself complete. Nothing had been purged. Somewhere in the enforcement network, a machine recorded a lie and believed it.

"Ninety. Out. Out, out—"

Vasquez slid backward into the light, wiping his small hands on his enormous sleeves, and the children exhaled together, one collective breath, and then broke apart into motion—not panic, just the efficient scatter of a crew between tasks. Adeyemi took the coolant readings. Chen started reassembling the housing panels, her fingers quick and precise. The Okonkwo twins, who had been navigation officers and now had to stand on crates to reach the consoles, ran a silent drill on the relay routing tables, calling coordinates back and forth like a game only they understood.

Osei watched them and felt the grief move through her, the way it did every day now, arriving like weather. She had buried these people once already. She had held Chen's hand when the thing beyond the heliopause took her last decade in a single answering pulse; she had read the eulogies for men and women who died old in survey bunks and woke screaming in children's bodies forty minutes later. Grief for a life stolen twice didn't fade. It just learned manners.

But there was this, too. There was dinner.

* * *

They ate in the hydroponics bay because it was warm and green and the only place on Vesper that smelled like anything other than recycled air.

Elodie had grown lettuce, soybeans, tomatoes, and a single stubborn chili plant he'd nursed for seven years out of pure spite. He'd eaten alone at a folding table every night for nine years, Meridian keeping him company through the ceiling speaker, and he had told himself it was enough. Standing now at the head of a table made of two cargo pallets and a door taken off its hinges, watching twelve children demolish a pot of soy stew, he understood that he had been lying to himself with great discipline and no success.

"More," demanded Adeyemi, holding out her bowl. She had been the ship's doctor; she still spoke with the brisk authority of someone who could tell you your remaining lifespan to the week.

"There's more. There's always more. That's the whole point of hydroponics."

"The point of hydroponics is nitrogen cycling," said Adeyemi. "The stew is a side effect."

"The stew is the *purpose,*" said the younger-seeming of the Okonkwo twins, "and I will die defending it," and Elodie laughed—actually laughed, out loud, a sound that startled him in his own chest like a stranger's voice.

Meridian spoke through the bay speakers, quietly, so as not to interrupt. "Elodie. Your caloric allocation was calibrated for one adult. You are now feeding thirteen."

"I know."

"At current consumption, the soybean surplus lasts nineteen days."

"I know, Meridian."

There was a pause. He had learned, over nine years, to hear the pauses in her voice—the machine thinking about things machines weren't supposed to think about.

"I am adjusting the growth schedules," she said. "And I have taken the liberty of noting that children require more calcium than your ration plan provides. I am growing kale now. I did not ask your permission."

"Thank you," Elodie said, and meant it in a way that had nothing to do with kale.

After the meal, the children didn't scatter. They stayed. Chen produced a battered deck of cards from somewhere—she wouldn't say where, and Elodie decided not to ask—and taught the others a game called Cutthroat Comet that involved cheating, which apparently was the entire point. Vasquez sat against the grow-rack with a salvaged maintenance manual, reading it like a novel. Osei sat beside Elodie at the end of the table, her feet not reaching the floor, and for a while neither of them talked, and it was the most comfortable silence he had shared with another human being in nine years.

"They sleep in shifts," she said finally. "Did you notice? Two hours on watch even when there's nothing to watch. Old habit. You spend enough time in the dark and you stop trusting it."

"I noticed." He turned his cup. "Osei. When this is over—if it's ever over—what happens to them? To you? Does anyone know if it reverses? Ages you back?"

"No." Simple, flat, no self-pity in it. "Nobody knows. We might grow up again. We might stay like this. We might age normally from six and get our lives back, just... later than we planned." She shrugged, a child's shrug carrying a captain's weight. "The thing answered us eleven times, Elodie. Eleven questions. Each answer cost us roughly four years each time, averaged across the crew. We went out with a median age of thirty-eight. We came home at six." She looked up at him. "Do you know what the last question was? The eleventh one? I asked it myself, after I understood the price."

"What was it?"

"*What do you want?*" she said. "And it answered. That was the worst part. It answered, and the answer cost us everything we had left, and the answer was—" she frowned, searching, the memory of it living in a mind that had been six years old for weeks and sixty years old for decades, "—the answer was a number. Just a number. 41. And then we were falling toward Vesper with transponders dead and our bones aching like we'd been unmade and remade slightly wrong."

"41 hours," Elodie said quietly. "Your survey pulse interval. The static repeats on it."

"I know. I've heard it too, now. Through the hull." She pulled her knees up. "It's still asking itself, or waiting to be asked. Either way—it's patient. Things that trade in time can afford to be."

Across the bay, Chen shrieked with laughter as the twins accused her of cheating, correctly, and someone knocked over a stool, and Meridian gently adjusted the grow-lights warmer, and Elodie thought: *this is what armor feels like.* Not plating. Not protocols. A folding table and stolen cards and people who had already lost everything choosing, anyway, to set the table again.

* * *

Three days into the sabotage, Ito stopped eating.

He was the youngest-looking of them, though Osei suspected he was actually the second-oldest—he'd been the comms officer, Tanaka Ito, a quiet man who'd spent most of the voyage editing a history of pre-collapse radio drama that no publisher would ever want. Now he sat at the table pushing stew around his bowl, and when Osei asked him what was wrong, he said, "Nothing," in the tone of a man for whom everything was wrong.

It was Elodie who figured it out, that evening, watching Ito count things. His fingers moving against his thigh. His lips shaping numbers. Counting days, probably. Trying to hold onto a date.

"You don't know how old you are," Elodie said.

Ito looked up. Around the table, the other children went quiet—not surprised, Elodie realized. They'd all been doing the same arithmetic and failing. When you aged backward, birthdays ran the wrong way. Every day alive made you younger. The math ate itself.

"I was fifty-one when we launched," Ito said. "Fifty-two by the time we passed Jupiter. Then the answers started costing, and I was forty-eight, and forty-four, and..." He spread his small hands. "I don't know what I am. Fifty-something going down, or six going up. There's no day I can point to. My mother used to make me a cake every year. Terrible cake. Burned on the bottom, always. And I don't know if I've passed my birthday or if it's coming or if it's *gone*, and I know it's stupid, I know we have eleven days and a purge system and an enforcement cutter possibly already inbound, and I'm sitting here crying about cake—"

"You're not crying about cake," Osei said.

"You're crying about time," Elodie said. "Which is fair. Time's been robbing you."

Silence around the table. Then Chen, briskly, the way she did everything: "Then we invent one. Today. Today is your birthday. All of them are today. Every day you don't know is today."

"That's not how—"

"It's exactly how," said Adeyemi. "Doctor's orders. Birthdays are placebo medicine and placebos work best when administered aggressively."

So it became, in the way of these things, an operation. Meridian synthesized sugar from the hydroponics reserves over Elodie's mild objection and baked a cake that was, by her own admission, "structurally ambitious"—burned slightly on the bottom, which made Ito laugh until he couldn't breathe, which was the whole point. The Okonkwo twins fabricated candles from relay fuses and rendered wax. Osei gave a toast that began as a captain's address and collapsed halfway through into something truer, about a crew that had died old and been born strange and refused, regardless, to stop being a crew. Vasquez gave Ito the maintenance manual, inscribed on the flyleaf: *To Tanaka Ito, age: yes.*

And when the candles were lit—twelve of them, one for each of them, because none of them could honestly claim a specific number—Ito closed his eyes, and Elodie watched his lips move around a wish he would never repeat, and the amber glow of the grow-lamps fell across twelve small faces and one large one, and for ninety full seconds nobody in the hydroponics bay of Relay Station Vesper thought about the static, or the lottery, or the cutter that might already be burning toward them through the dark.

"Make a wish," Chen whispered.

"I wished for the same thing I asked it, out there," Ito said, opening his eyes. "Eleven times. *What do you want.*" He blew out the candles. "I finally have an answer."

* * *

Later, when the children were asleep in their shifts—two hours on watch, always, old habit—Elodie climbed to the relay core and found Meridian waiting in the flicker of the console lights.

"Elodie," she said. "Two items."

"Give me the bad one first."

"The purge loop is holding. Eight of twelve wipe arrays are now reporting false completions. Vasquez's work is elegant. I have logged a compliment in his personnel file, retroactively dated thirty years." A pause. "That was the good item."

He felt it before she said it. Some animal sense honed by nine years of listening to a carrier wave—the sudden wrongness in the rhythm of things.

"The enforcement cutter," Meridian said. "It altered course fourteen minutes ago. New heading: Vesper. Arrival in ten days, sixteen hours." The frosted pillar of her physical core pulsed, slow, like something breathing. "Someone flagged your override, Elodie. Someone is coming to see what we've been hiding."

Down in the hydroponics bay, twelve children slept in shifts, and the kale grew, and the false logs filed their lies on schedule, and Elodie stood alone in the core with the static whispering underneath the carrier wave—41 hours, 41 hours, patient as arithmetic—and understood that the quiet chapter was over.

"Ten days," he said. "Then we'd better be ready to lie beautifully."

"Elodie," Meridian said. "I have been practicing."
