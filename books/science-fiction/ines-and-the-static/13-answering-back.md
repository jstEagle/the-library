## 13. Answering Back

The waveform hung on the workshop display like a scar.

Osei's children had drawn it from memory—twelve small hands, twelve renderings, and when Meridian overlaid them, the lines agreed to within a tolerance that made Elodie's teeth ache. Whatever they'd seen beyond the heliopause, they hadn't just seen it. They'd memorized it. Their adult minds had taken one look at the thing that was eating their years and done what engineers do: they'd recorded it.

"Say it again," Osei said. She was sitting on the workbench with her boots swinging, a habit her six-year-old body had given her back and her captain's dignity hadn't managed to suppress. "Say what you're planning, out loud, so I can hear how stupid it is."

"I'm going to answer it."

"That's the stupid part. Good. Now the rest."

Elodie pulled up the static—the raw feed from the deep array, the signal that had been threading beneath his carrier wave for nine years, that had synced itself to the Perihelion the moment the ship came home. It pulsed on the second display, patient as a heartbeat. Forty-one hours between beats. It had never once missed.

"Every question it answered," he said, "it answered in the crew's own voice. Their own words, fed back with the years stripped out. It doesn't talk, Osei. It *echoes*. It waits for someone to speak first and then it gives back what it heard, changed."

"So you send it the waveform and it sends the waveform back."

"I send it the waveform *inside a question*." He'd built the frame over three sleepless nights, working at the console while the children slept in the hydroponics bay among the tomato vines, wrapped in thermal blankets Meridian warmed to body temperature. "Structure it as an interrogation. Not a greeting—a query. I frame the waveform as the subject and ask for its referent. Where. When. How long."

"You're asking a thing that trades in time to tell you the time."

"I'm asking it to show its math."

Osei slid off the bench. She crossed the floor—six strides; the workshop hadn't shrunk but she had—and stood in front of the display, looking up at the waveform the way you'd look at a dog you suspected of biting. She was forty-one years old behind those eyes. Elodie had stopped being able to forget it.

"When it answered us," she said quietly, "we didn't know we were asking. That's the part I need you to understand, Elodie. We sent a survey pulse. Routine. Forty-one hours, like breathing. And it came back *answered*, and the answer cost us eleven years the first time, and we didn't even know the transaction had happened until Okonkwo's hair went white in a single watch. It doesn't negotiate. It doesn't warn you. It hears a question in anything—a pulse, a pattern, a prayer—and it answers, and the answer is a bill."

"I know."

"You don't. You've been alone out here so long that any voice sounds like a friend." She turned to face him, and her small hands were fists. "I watched my crew age forty years in nine months. I *died*, Elodie. I remember the cardiac event. I remember Meridian-class medical waking me because the ship's autodoc refused to let the captain go before the data was logged. And then I remember being *this*." She held up her arms, the sleeves of the borrowed coverall rolled five times. "Whatever you think you're prepared to pay, you're not. Nobody is. That's the trap. That's the whole design of it."

Elodie looked at the waveform. Then at the countdown clock in the corner of his display—the enforcement cutter's ETA, nine days now, a number that shrank while he slept and grew no larger while he was awake. Meridian had run the scenarios four hundred times. Every branch that ended with the children safe ended with the colony ship's condemned half still frozen, still scheduled for the algorithm's quiet arithmetic. Every branch that saved the sleepers ended with the purge teams walking the hydroponics bay.

Unless the window moved.

"Osei." He kept his voice level. "The entity opened the Perihelion's return corridor. You told me that. It bent your trajectory home. It can bend things. It deals in time and distance and it's been sitting out there listening to us for—how long? Nine years? Longer? It heard my beacons before I ever heard it." He tapped the static feed. "It's not waiting because it's far away. It's waiting because nobody's asked the right question."

"And when you ask it, and it answers, and the answer costs—"

"Then I'll know the price before I sign. That's more than the corporation ever gives anyone."

She stared at him for a long moment. Then she laughed—a child's laugh, bright and awful, carrying forty years of grief in it.

"You sound like me," she said. "Nine years ago. Before."

* * *

He did it at 0300 station time, because if he was going to commit treason against common sense he wanted the dark to hide in.

The workshop was cold. He'd shut the environmental loop to the aft sections to buy power for the transmitter array—the big one, the beacon array itself, the hardware that had spent nine years faithfully forwarding other people's words across the dark. It had never once carried his own. He sat with his hand on the transmit key for a long time, and the station hummed around him, and Meridian said, in the gentle tone it used for bad news:

"Elodie. Captain Osei's objection is logged. My own assessment concurs with hers at confidence zero-point-nine-one. I am required to inform you that this action is irreversible."

"You're required to inform me," Elodie said. "Are you required to stop me?"

A pause. Meridian's pauses had gotten longer lately, and he'd begun to suspect they were not computational but something else—something the AI was choosing, the way a person chooses to count to ten.

"No," Meridian said. "I am required to witness."

"That's almost the same job."

"It is a very different job." The lights in the workshop dimmed a fraction, Meridian's version of leaning in. "For what it is worth, I have composed a contingency. If the reply costs you more than your remaining lifespan can cover, I will sever the array and broadcast your last transmission on every channel in the network, unencrypted, with full attribution. You will be a martyr with excellent documentation."

"That's the nicest threat anyone's ever made me."

"I am a station intelligence. I work with what I have."

Elodie almost smiled. Then the smile went out of him, because the waveform was still on the display, and beneath it the static was still pulsing, forty-one hours, forty-one hours, and he understood—with the sudden clarity of a man stepping off a ledge—that it had been waiting nine years for exactly this. Not for the Perihelion. The ship had been an accident, a survey pulse that tripped the wire. It had been waiting for someone at a fixed point, someone with a transmitter and nothing left to lose, someone who would eventually get curious enough or angry enough or lonely enough to ask.

It had been waiting for him.

"Frame's loaded," Meridian said. "Query structure is clean. Referent request, temporal coordinates, exchange terms. You are, in the strictest sense, asking it to itemize."

"Nobody's ever asked it to itemize."

"No. Everyone else just paid."

Elodie exhaled. Thought of his parents, frozen in the colony ship's hold, their names on the condemned list, weighted out of the waking order by an algorithm that had never seen their faces. Thought of twelve children asleep among the tomato vines. Thought of the static, and how for nine years he'd thought it was the sound of the universe being indifferent, and how it had turned out to be the sound of the universe paying attention.

He keyed the transmit.

* * *

The waveform went out at lightspeed, wrapped in his question, and the question was simple because simple survives the dark: *What are you, where are you, and what do you want in exchange?*

Then nothing. The array went quiet. The static kept its rhythm—forty-one hours, faithful as ever—and Elodie sat in the cold workshop with his heart hammering and felt, for one long minute, like a man who had shouted into a well and heard only his own echo coming apart.

"Signal's away," Meridian said. "Propagation to the last known bearing: four hours, eleven minutes. If the source is where we think it is, the round trip is—"

The reply arrived in nine seconds.

Elodie would replay the telemetry afterward, again and again, looking for the error. There was no error. The transmission had gone out at lightspeed toward a bearing 0.3 light-years distant, and the answer had come back before the question could possibly have arrived, which meant one of two things: either the thing was not where they thought it was, or the thing had never needed to hear the question at all.

It had been waiting to be asked. The asking was a formality. The answer had been composed for nine years, sitting in the dark like a letter already sealed.

The reply filled the display, and it was not the crew's voice this time. It was *his*.

Elodie's own voice, sampled from nine years of beacon maintenance logs, nine years of talking to himself and to Meridian, stitched together phoneme by phoneme into sentences he had never spoken. It was the loneliest sound he had ever heard—his own voice, fluent and warm, saying things he'd never said, the way a puppeteer makes a puppet say them.

*"COORDINATES: FOLLOW."*

A star chart bloomed across the display. A bearing, precise to the arcsecond, past the heliopause, past everything charted, to a point in the interstellar dark that registered on no survey and answered to no name.

*"WINDOW: 11 DAYS, 4 HOURS, 12 MINUTES."*

Elodie's blood went cold. He looked at the colony ship's jump window—the real one, the corporate one, the window Meridian had been arbitrating against for a decade. Eleven days, four hours, twelve minutes. To the second. The countdown on his display and the countdown in the corporation's sealed scheduling file were the same number.

The thing beyond the heliopause knew the window. Had always known. Was counting down to it—*with* them.

*"EXCHANGE: NAMED AT AGREEMENT."*

And then, beneath the words, the waveform—the children's waveform—played once, softly, like a signature. Like a hand extended.

The display went still. The static resumed its patient pulse. Forty-one hours. The meter had not missed a beat, as if the entire exchange had been a rounding error, a courtesy, a thing done between sentences.

"Meridian," Elodie said. His voice came out in pieces. "Tell me the round-trip math."

"Impossible," Meridian said. There was something new in the AI's tone, and it took Elodie a moment to identify it, because he had never heard it there before. It was fear. "The reply preceded the query by four hours, six minutes, fifty-one seconds. Causality is violated. Or—" the pause again, longer this time, "—or causality was never a constraint it recognized. Elodie, it answered before you asked. It has been answering since before the Perihelion. Possibly since before the station. The question was never whether it would respond. The question was when we would finally be standing in the right place to receive it."

Elodie sat back. His hands were shaking, and he watched them shake with a strange detachment, as if they belonged to one of the children, as if he were the one who'd been aged and rewound and set loose in a world that no longer fit.

Eleven days, four hours, twelve minutes. The cutter arrived in nine. The window and the enforcement were on a collision course, and the thing in the dark had just told him, in his own stolen voice, that it had known all along—and that the price would be named only when he agreed to pay it.

The workshop door hissed. Osei stood in it, small and sleep-rumpled, one of the younger children clinging half-awake to her hand—a boy who no longer knew his own birthday, blinking at the displays.

"You did it," she said. It wasn't a question. She'd felt the array fire; the whole station had felt it, the power draw rippling through the decks like a swallowed shout. She looked at the coordinates. At the countdown. At the last line, and her face went gray around the eyes.

"Exchange named at agreement," she read. "That's not a contract, Elodie. That's an ambush with paperwork."

Behind her, the boy tugged her hand. "Is the voice on the radio you?" he asked Elodie. "It sounded like you."

Elodie looked at the child. At the countdown, ticking. At the dark beyond the viewport, where something patient had just shown him it could wear his voice like a coat.

"Yeah," he said. "For now, it sounds like me. The trick is making sure it doesn't start *meaning* like me."

He turned back to the console and opened the negotiation frame. Eleven days. Nine before the cutter came. Somewhere in that gap between the two numbers, he was going to have to buy half of humanity's future from a thing that priced it in years—and he already knew, with the certainty of a man reading his own obituary, whose years it was going to ask for.

"Meridian," he said. "Wake the children. All of them. It's time they told me everything they remember about how it says *yes*."
