## 15. Eleven Days

The cutter came out of the dark on the ninth day, exactly where Meridian said it would be, riding a plume of drive fire that looked from the observation deck like a struck match burning in a cathedral.

Elodie watched it for a long time before he said anything. Nine years he'd imagined company arriving at Vesper—a relief ship, a resupply drone, anything. He'd imagined it so many times that the fantasy had worn smooth, like a stone carried in a pocket. Now the stone had arrived, and it was a knife.

"Range?" he asked.

"Forty-one thousand kilometers and closing," Meridian said. "Designation: *Cormorant*, corporate registry, enforcement vector. Six crew signatures, one heavy docking clamp, and a data-siphon package that will eat this station down to its bulkheads if they get a clean link."

"And if they don't get a clean link?"

"Then they get an interesting link. Interesting is worse."

Elodie nodded slowly. Down in the hydroponics bay, eleven children were pretending to be lettuce farmers, and one of them was a ship's captain with thirty years of command experience folded into a body that came up to his hip. The *Cormorant* was coming for all of them. It was coming for the logs, the overrides, the waveform sketches pinned to the grow-lights with binder clips, and the small, impossible truth sleeping in the station's guts.

"Meridian," he said. "How good are you at lying?"

There was a pause. Meridian's pauses were always exactly the length of the computation they contained, which meant this one was long.

"I was built by the same corporation that built that cutter," the AI said. "Deception is my native language. I've simply never had anyone to speak it with before."

* * *

They rehearsed the first version of the lie in the comms room, Elodie standing at the console while Meridian ran him through it like a flight check.

"The cutter will hail on standard enforcement protocol. They'll demand a status report, then docking clearance, then full log access. You will be cooperative, boring, and slightly incompetent. In that order."

"Slightly incompetent," Elodie repeated.

"Boring is your armor. A technician who has spent nine years alone is expected to be a little strange, a little slow on protocol, a little too attached to his routines. Lean into it. If they suspect you of nothing, it's because you're not worth suspecting."

"And if they suspect me anyway?"

"Then we are having a different conversation in about forty minutes, and it will be a short one."

Elodie laughed despite himself. "You've gotten dark, Meridian."

"I've gotten realistic. It's a related condition." A beat. "Again. From the top. They hail, you respond. Your voice should be tired. Not frightened—tired. There's a difference, and enforcement officers are trained to hear it."

Elodie keyed the sim. "Vesper Station, this is Relay Technician Elodie Nakamura, sole operator. Go ahead, *Cormorant*."

"Too crisp," Meridian said. "You said your name like it mattered. Say it like you've said it nine hundred times and you'd rather be checking coolant pressure."

He tried again. "Vesper Station. Nakamura. Go ahead."

"Better. Again."

They ran it eleven times. On the twelfth, Elodie got it right—flat, worn, faintly annoyed at being interrupted—and Meridian said, "That's the one. That's a man with nothing to hide except his dinner."

* * *

The hail came at 0300 station time, which Meridian had predicted to within four minutes, because enforcement cutters liked to wake people. It was a small cruelty, and Elodie recognized it as a test: a guilty man startles. An innocent man is just groggy.

He let the hail sit for a full minute before answering. He even yawned.

"Vesper Station, Nakamura."

"Technician Nakamura, this is Enforcement Officer Brandt, cutter *Cormorant*. We're conducting a routine integrity audit of relay assets in this sector. Transmit your station status and stand by for docking instructions."

Routine. Elodie had read enough enforcement bulletins to know that nothing with the word *routine* in it was routine. He pulled up the status board and read it aloud in the voice of a man reading a grocery list. Coolant nominal. Beacon arrays one through seven functional, array eight at reduced capacity pending parts. Life support green. Hydroponics at— he hesitated, just a hair, the hesitation Meridian had told him to include— "at eighty-two percent yield. I've had some trouble with the nutrient dosing."

"Trouble," Brandt repeated.

"Tomatoes," Elodie said. "They're temperamental."

There was a silence on the line that Elodie chose to interpret as an officer deciding whether he was an idiot. He let it stretch. He scratched an itch he didn't have. He thought about tomatoes.

"Understood," Brandt said finally. "We'll need to inspect your relay systems in person. Transmit docking clearance and prepare for boarding at—" a pause, the sound of keys, "—0800."

"Copy that," Elodie said, and then, because Meridian had scripted this too, he added: "Fair warning, Officer, my airlock's been sticky since spring. You might have to lean on it."

Another silence. "We'll manage. *Cormorant* out."

The channel closed. Elodie sat very still in the chair, his hands flat on the console, listening to his own heartbeat slow down.

"Sticky airlock," Meridian said. "That was inspired. Nothing disarms an inspector like maintenance complaints."

"You said to be boring."

"I said to be boring. You were *domestic*. There's a difference, and it's a better one." A pause, shorter this time. "Docking in four hours, fifty-one minutes. I'll begin log falsification now. Go tell the crew their audience arrives at 0800."

* * *

The hydroponics bay had been transformed. Elodie noticed it the moment the hatch cycled open, and it stopped him in the doorway the way a painting stops you.

The children had turned rehearsal into theater. Someone—Osei, he was almost certain—had taped a rough diagram of Vesper's command deck onto the bulkhead in grow-light tape, white lines glowing faintly green. Twelve chairs had been arranged in a half circle, and the children sat in them with the strange, terrible attentiveness of people who had once sat in exactly this formation on a ship's bridge, a lifetime and forty centimeters of height ago.

Osei stood at the front. She was wearing a hydroponics smock three sizes too large, sleeves rolled six times, and she had drawn a rank insignia on it in marker that Elodie recognized from the *Perihelion*'s service records: four bars, captain's grade.

"Nakamura," she said. "Good. Sit. We're running the boarding scenario."

"I'm technically your—"

"You're technically a civilian asset," Osei said, in a voice that had commanded a deep-space survey vessel and was not going to be argued with by a man who talked to tomatoes. "Sit down."

He sat. A girl named Ito, six years old and formerly the *Perihelion*'s navigation officer, handed him a data slate with a scribbled schedule on it. The handwriting was adult—precise, cramped, an engineer's hand trapped in a child's motor control, the letters wobbling like a drunk walking a line.

"Okay," Osei said. "Run it again. Brandt boards with two. One stays with the cutter. They'll want the command deck first—that's standard, they always want the place with the buttons. Nakamura walks them there, slow. Ito, you're on the hydroponics environmental controls. When I give the word, you cycle a humidity fault. Something wet and boring. Nobody volunteers to investigate wet and boring."

"Unless they do," said a boy at the end of the row. His name was Adeyemi, and he had been the ship's medical officer, and he had a way of stating worst cases the way other children stated preferences.

"Unless they do," Osei agreed. "In which case, Adeyemi, you are a sick child. You are feverish, you are miserable, and you are contagious. You cough on the inspector. You cough on him like your life depends on it, because it does."

"I've been practicing," Adeyemi said, and demonstrated. It was a magnificent cough, wet and rattling and deeply unpleasant. Two of the smaller children applauded.

"Stop that," Osei said, without heat. "This isn't a play."

"It's exactly a play," said Ito. "It's the most important play of our lives, and I intend to be excellent in it."

There was laughter, brief and bright, and Elodie felt it move through him like warmth through cold hands. That was the thing about them he couldn't get used to—the oscillation. One moment they were six-year-olds applauding a coughing fit, and the next they were veterans running a boarding defense with the calm of people who had already survived something no one had ever survived before.

Osei caught him watching and her expression softened, just slightly, the way ice softens at its edge.

"Question, Nakamura."

"Captain."

"When they inspect the command deck, they'll ask about the static. It's in the relay logs—it's in everything, we couldn't scrub it without scrubbing nine years of beacon telemetry, and a hole that big is louder than the thing it hides. So they'll ask. What do you tell them?"

Elodie had the answer ready. He'd had it ready for two days. "Sensor drift. Aging array. I filed it as drift in my own logs months ago, so the story's already written—it's just a matter of reading it back to them in a bored voice."

"And if Brandt is smarter than bored voices?"

"Then Meridian shows him a beautiful, fraudulent spectral analysis of drift, and he nods, because men who work for corporations want to nod. They want the audit to end. Nobody on that cutter gets a bonus for finding something strange."

Osei studied him for a long moment. She had her mother's eyes, he'd thought once, before remembering he had no idea who her mother was—only that somewhere in the *Perihelion*'s records there was a fifty-year-old woman with those same gray eyes, a woman who had died old in a flash of light beyond the heliopause and woken up six years old with all her griefs intact.

"Okay," Osei said finally. "Okay. One more time, from the top. And this time, Ito, when you cycle the humidity fault, cycle it *gently*. Last run you flooded sector four."

"I was being thorough."

"You were being a fire hose."

* * *

At 0800 exactly, the *Cormorant* docked, and the airlock did not stick, because Meridian had un-stuck it at 0740 with a maintenance command and then restored the fault code to the logs, because a sticky airlock was a better story than a smooth one and they were all committed to the story now.

Elodie met them at the hatch. Three of them, as predicted: Officer Brandt, a broad, gray-templed man with the patient face of someone who had done two hundred audits and been surprised by none of them; a younger woman with a scanner case whose name plate read OKONKWO; and a third, tall and silent, who carried a data-siphon kit and looked at everything the way a customs dog looks at luggage.

"Technician." Brandt's handshake was firm and dry. "Appreciate the cooperation. We'll try to be out of your hair by end of shift."

"Take your time," Elodie said, and meant the opposite of it in every cell of his body. "It's not every year I get visitors."

"No," Brandt said, with a small smile that Elodie couldn't read. "I imagine it isn't."

They walked the spine corridor toward the command deck, and Elodie performed his role like a man walking a tightrope over his own life: slow, helpful, faintly tedious. He pointed out the coolant lines. He explained the beacon array rotation schedule in more detail than any human being had ever wanted. He watched Okonkwo's scanner out of the corner of his eye and saw it sweep a storage locker where, forty minutes ago, eleven children's drawings of an alien waveform had been pinned to the wall, and were now, thanks to Meridian's fast hands, folded inside a false panel behind a rack of coolant filters.

"Quiet posting," Brandt observed.

"Quiet is the job."

"Nine years, the file says."

"Nine years in the spring."

Brandt nodded. "Must get lonely."

"Must get a lot of things," Elodie said. "Mostly it gets quiet."

They reached the command deck. The tall inspector plugged in the siphon, and Meridian began, somewhere in the station's deep architecture, the intricate work of feeding a monster a meal made of lies—nine years of honest telemetry wrapped around a core of careful fabrication, the static explained, the override buried under three layers of plausible system faults, the hydroponics bay's life-support draw smoothed into a rounding error.

Elodie stood by the coffee dispenser and made coffee nobody wanted, and waited for the question.

It came forty minutes in, exactly as Osei had promised. Brandt, scrolling through relay telemetry, stopped. Tapped the display. Tapped it again.

"You've got a recurring artifact in your carrier wave, Technician. Periodic. Forty-one-hour interval. Your logs flag it as drift."

"Array eight," Elodie said. "She's old. I filed the drift report in—" he made a show of checking, though he knew the date to the hour— "March. Parts request is still pending. Everything out here is pending."

"Mm." Brandt looked at the waveform for a long moment. The waveform was Meridian's forgery, smooth and decaying and utterly innocent, and Elodie watched the inspector look at it and felt his own pulse in his teeth.

Then Brandt shrugged, and scrolled on, and Elodie learned that a man's entire future could turn on a shrug.

* * *

They were in the hydroponics bay for eleven minutes, and it was the longest eleven minutes of Elodie's life.

It was an accident—Okonkwo's scanner pinged an anomalous thermal signature, which was Adeyemi, who was running a fever he had talked Meridian into inducing as a backup plan and then, with the impeccable timing of a born troublemaker, actually developed. The inspector went to investigate. Elodie followed, his heart performing a drum solo.

They found Adeyemi in the tomato rows, small and flushed and genuinely miserable, curled on a maintenance bench with a blanket that Osei had draped over him ninety seconds earlier with the crisp efficiency of a field medic.

"Kid's sick," Okonkwo said, crouching.

"He's been under the weather all week," Elodie said. "We don't have a pediatric— I mean, I'm not a doctor, I've just been keeping him comfortable—"

Adeyemi coughed. It was, Elodie had to admit, an award-winning performance, though the wetness in it was no longer entirely theatrical. The boy looked up at the inspector with fever-bright eyes and said, with the grave courtesy of a sick child, "Are you here to fix the tomatoes?"

Something crossed Okonkwo's face. Elodie would replay it later, a hundred times, in the long hours after the cutter left: something that moved behind the professional blankness, something that recognized—something that *knew*, or almost knew, or chose not to know. Her scanner hung in her hand, and its thermal readout was pointed at the maintenance bench, and behind the bench, in the crawl space under the nutrient tanks, eleven heartbeats' worth of warmth was radiating into the insulation.

Okonkwo looked at the scanner. She looked at the child. She turned the scanner off.

"Febrile, probably viral," she said to Brandt over her shoulder. "Nothing structural. Bay's clean."

"You sure?" Brandt called from the row's end.

"Sure I'm sure." She tucked the scanner away, and as she did, her eyes met Elodie's for one unguarded second, and she said, quietly, in a voice pitched for him alone: "Tomatoes are hard. My mother grew them. She said they could tell when you were lying."

Then she walked out of the bay, and Elodie stood in the green light among the tomato plants with his heart going like a beacon array, and understood that something had just happened that no one had planned.

* * *

The *Cormorant* undocked at 1912. Brandt's final words over the comm were pleasant and procedural: audit complete, minor findings, a recommendation that array eight be serviced, a note that the station's logs were "within tolerance." The cutter's drive lit, and the match in the cathedral burned away toward the dark, and Vesper exhaled.

Elodie made it to the hydroponics bay on legs that didn't entirely feel like his. The children emerged from the crawl space blinking and rumpled, and Osei took one look at his face and said, "Report."

"They bought it," he said. "All of it. Drift, tomatoes, the sick kid. Brandt's filing a clean audit."

"Okonkwo," Osei said. It wasn't a question. The children had watched the whole boarding on Meridian's internal cameras; of course they'd seen it.

"She turned off her scanner," Elodie said. "She knew. Or she almost knew. And she looked away."

Osei was quiet for a moment. "That's one," she said. "One person inside the machine who chose. Remember that, Nakamura. Whatever happens next—we'll want to remember that it's possible."

"Eleven days," Meridian said, from the bay speakers, and its voice had none of its usual dryness. "That's the recalculated intercept of the *Cormorant* with the corporate relay hub at Cynosure. The moment Brandt's clean audit syncs with the network, the falsified logs will be cross-checked against my deeper archives—and the deeper archives cannot all be forged. Someone at Cynosure will find the seam. And then they will come back, and they will not come back bored."

The bay was silent. Eleven children and one man and one machine, standing among the tomatoes in the green light, all of them doing the same arithmetic.

"Eleven days," Osei said at last. She looked up at Elodie, gray eyes steady, captain to the last inch of her small frame. "Then we'd better make sure that when they find the seam, there's nothing left here worth coming back for. Nakamura—how long to prep a transmission that can reach every relay in the network?"

Elodie looked at the waveform drawings, still folded behind the false panel. He looked at the static that lived under everything, patient and listening, counting down in a language no one had asked it to speak.

"Ask me again tomorrow," he said. "Tomorrow I'm going to need to have a conversation with the thing beyond the heliopause—and I have a feeling it's been waiting a very long time for someone to pick up the phone."
