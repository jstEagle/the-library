## 16. Boarding Party

The cutter docked at 0400 station time, which was a fiction Elodie had invented for himself in year two and now couldn't shake. He stood at the airlock in his cleanest jumpsuit, hair combed with water and fingers, wearing the expression he'd practiced in the dark of his bunk: bored, competent, slightly resentful of the interruption. A technician interrupted by paperwork. Nothing more.

The lock cycled. Three figures came through in corporate gray, helmets under their arms, breath fogging in the cold airlock vestibule. The lead was a woman in her fifties with a face like an audit—flat, thorough, unimpressed by anything it had ever seen. Her name tag read VASHTI OKONKWO, ENFORCEMENT VECTOR SEVEN. Behind her: a younger man whose badge said ILYES, and a third who hadn't given a name at all, just stood slightly back with the stillness of someone paid to watch.

"Elodie Nakamura," Okonkwo said. Not a question.

"That's me."

"You filed an override on purge cycle nine-oh-two."

"A sensor fault," Elodie said. "The system tried to wipe a data core that was flagged as active navigation memory. If it had gone through, my beacon alignment would have drifted and every ship in the corridor would have been navigating off a ghost." He shrugged. "You want to explain that to the traffic authority, or do you want to explain it to me?"

He'd rehearsed that too. It was good. It was almost true—the override *had* protected navigation memory, among other things—and the best lies were load-bearing.

Okonkwo looked at him for three full seconds. Then she handed him a slate. "Inspection order. Full systems audit, log verification, biological inventory. Standard for an unscheduled override." She stepped past him into the station proper. "Show us your server room first. Then we'll walk the ring."

Behind her back, the unnamed one's eyes moved across the corridor walls, the ceiling, the seams between panels. Counting exits, Elodie thought. Or counting hiding places.

*Meridian,* he subvocalized against the implant's pickup, *tell me you're ready.*

*I have been ready since before they were born,* Meridian answered, calm as deep water. *Their logs are already false. Try to breathe like a man with nothing to hide.*

Elodie breathed. It was harder than it sounded.

* * *

The server room took ninety minutes. Okonkwo worked through it in silence, cross-checking Meridian's fabricated logs against the enforcement vector's own telemetry, and Elodie sat with his hands folded and watched her not find what wasn't there. Twice she paused. Twice Meridian fed a correction into the display so smoothly that even Elodie couldn't see the seam. The younger one, Ilyes, drank three cups of the terrible coffee from the galley dispenser and asked questions about beacon maintenance that were almost friendly.

"Nine years out here alone," Ilyes said, leaning against the rack housing. "What's that like? Honestly."

"Loud," Elodie said. "Everything hums. You stop hearing it until something stops humming, and then you can't sleep until it's fixed."

Ilyes laughed. "My sister did a four-year rotation on Ceres gate. Came back talking to the vending machines."

"The machines talk back here. Station AI. Meridian."

"Must be nice." Something flickered across his face—not envy exactly. Loneliness recognizing loneliness, the way sailors' lights blink at each other across a black sea.

Okonkwo closed the last panel. "Logs are consistent," she said, and Elodie felt the floor tilt half a degree with relief before she added: "Now the biological inventory. Station's rated for one. Your life support shows consumption patterns for—" she checked the slate, though Elodie was certain she knew the number by heart— "six."

The hum of the station filled the silence.

"Hydroponics," Elodie said. His voice came out level, and he marveled at himself from a great distance. "I run the grow lights hot. Biomass respiration reads as metabolic load. You want me to show you?"

"I want you to show me everything," Okonkwo said. "In order. Ring section by ring section."

*Meridian.*

*They will reach hydroponics in forty minutes,* Meridian said. *The children are in place. Captain Osei has the plan.*

*Whose plan?*

*Hers. She made me promise not to tell you. She said you're a bad liar and shouldn't know the details.*

That sounded exactly like Osei.

* * *

They walked the ring. Okonkwo moved like a woman reading a document she'd written herself—air scrubbers, water reclamation, the comms suite where Elodie explained the static as solar weather with the confidence of a man describing his own heartbeat. Ilyes trailed, asking about the viewports, the exercise equipment, the small human archaeology of nine years: a chipped mug, a wall of hand-drawn star charts, a child's drawing taped above the galley table that Elodie had forgotten to take down.

It was a crayon sun. Twelve rays. Underneath, in careful six-year-old letters: *FOR ELODIE.*

Ilyes looked at it a beat too long.

"My niece," Elodie said. "Sends them every cargo run."

"Mm," said Ilyes, and said nothing else, but Elodie felt the moment pass over him like weather.

The unnamed inspector never spoke. He walked at the rear and touched things—door frames, conduit housings—with two fingers, and wherever he touched, Elodie later realized, a small gray tag appeared, a passive tracker no bigger than a fingernail. Meridian catalogued each one silently. *Let them,* the AI advised. *A watched station is a trusted station. We will peel them off after.*

Forty minutes became fifty-five because Okonkwo stopped twice—once at a coolant leak Elodie genuinely hadn't noticed, once at the viewport overlooking the Perihelion, still moored at the far arm with its radiation-scarred hull drinking the light.

"That ship isn't on any registry I have," she said.

"Deep survey. Dark running. They asked me not to log their approach—proprietary survey data." He held her gaze. "Corporate charter. You can pull it if you want to spend six hours on the uplink."

Something moved behind Okonkwo's flat face—calculation, or fatigue, or the particular weariness of an enforcer who has learned that most mysteries are paperwork wearing a costume. "Later," she said. "Hydroponics next."

* * *

The hydroponics bay door opened onto green.

Elodie had spent the previous night making it true. Rows of bean vines and dwarf wheat, nutrient mist drifting gold under the grow lamps, the air thick and wet and smelling of living things. He'd thinned the canopy deliberately along the main aisle so nothing blocked sightlines—nothing *obvious*—and behind the third tier of planters, in the service crawl space where the irrigation manifolds ran, twelve children waited in the dark with their adult minds and their small hands folded in their laps.

Osei had organized them like a watch rotation. Six-year-old bodies, forty-year-old discipline. The youngest, a boy named Tomas who had been the Perihelion's navigator, had cried quietly for ten minutes and then stopped, because crying used water and water was rationed.

Okonkwo walked the aisles. Ilyes sampled a strawberry with apologetic eyes. The unnamed inspector knelt at the end of the row and put two fingers on the irrigation manifold—

—and the manifold hissed.

Not a malfunction. Osei. She was in the crawl space with a wrench and nine years of remembered engineering she'd learned in a body twice this size, and she had chosen that exact second to open a pressure relief valve, because a hissing pipe draws eyes, and eyes that look down don't look back.

"What's that?" Okonkwo turned.

"Cycling," Elodie said. "Pressure differential between tiers. It does that."

"It does that on a schedule?"

"Plants don't keep schedules. That's the whole problem with plants."

Okonkwo stared at him. For one long second Elodie saw the audit behind her eyes begin to add numbers that didn't fit—consumption curves, the crayon sun, the pressure valve that had released precisely when a kneeling man touched a manifold—and then, from inside the crawl space, came a sound.

A child's voice. Singing.

It was thin and off-key and absolutely perfect. Some nursery song, muffled by the planter tiers, the kind of thing a small person might hum without thinking while playing where they shouldn't. And then a second voice joined it, giggling, and a small scuffle-sound, and then Osei's voice—pitched high, pitched young, pitched *guilty*: *"Tomas! Get out of there—you're not supposed to—"*

She erupted from the crawl space dragging a boy by the hand. Both of them were filthy with nutrient mist. Both of them were unmistakably, catastrophically, six years old.

Time stopped. Elodie heard his own heart. Beside him, Ilyes went very still. The unnamed inspector rose slowly to his feet, hand moving toward his hip.

Osei looked up at the inspectors with huge brown eyes brimming with staged tears, and Elodie—who knew her, who knew the captain underneath, who had watched this woman command a starship and stare down an entity beyond the heliopause—watched her become, entirely and convincingly, a frightened little girl caught hiding in the plants.

"I'm sorry," she whispered. "Please don't tell the company. Please. We're not supposed to be awake."

* * *

The silence lasted eleven seconds. Elodie counted.

It was Okonkwo who broke it, and she didn't speak to Elodie at all. She crouched. Down to the children's level, slate forgotten in one hand, and studied Osei's face the way she'd studied the logs—thoroughly, flatly, missing nothing.

"Names," she said.

"Ines," Osei said.

Elodie's blood went cold. *She's using the station name.* The name from his own transmissions, the name woven through nine years of logs, the name that existed nowhere in any corporate record except as a signal ghost. If Okonkwo checked—

"Tomas," said the boy, sniffling.

"Where are your parents?"

"Asleep." Osei pointed vaguely upward, toward the colony ship sleeping in its long orbit. "We woke up early. It was an accident. There was a man who said he'd help us but we're not supposed to be seen because the company says accidents get..." She faltered. She let the sentence die, and let the silence finish it, and the silence finished it perfectly: *accidents get erased.*

Okonkwo stood up. Turned to Elodie. Her face gave away absolutely nothing, and that was the most frightening thing Elodie had seen in nine years.

"These children are not in your crew manifest."

"They're not crew. They're stowaways. Sort of." He spread his hands. "What was I supposed to do, Okonkwo? Open a ticket? You know what the protocol is for unsanctioned wakes. Quarantine, data wipe, 'reintegration review.'" He put the corporate word in his mouth like spoiled meat. "They're *children.*"

"They're also evidence," the unnamed inspector said, speaking for the first time. His voice was soft and precise. "Unregistered minors aboard a relay station with an overridden purge cycle and an unlogged vessel at dock. Nakamura, you understand how this looks."

"I understand how it *is*," Elodie said. "It's two kids eating my strawberries and hiding from people like you."

Nobody moved. The grow lamps hummed. Somewhere in the mist, water dripped from a vine, patient as a clock.

And then Ilyes cleared his throat.

"So these are the consumption anomalies," he said. "Six bodies. That actually reconciles the life-support numbers—I was going to flag it as a sensor calibration issue, but if there are kids running around eating rations, the math closes." He glanced at Okonkwo. "Biological inventory matches observed personnel. Two minors, one technician. I can write that up clean."

"Ilyes." Okonkwo's voice was quiet.

"Ma'am, I've been on cutters for six years. You know what happens when we file a report like this. It doesn't come back as a rescue. It comes back as a *sanitization team*, and none of us are on it anymore." He looked at the children—at Osei, small and trembling and watching him with eyes too old for the trick she was running, though he couldn't know that, could only see a scared kid—and something in his face settled, like a decision being made in a place decisions aren't supposed to be made. "Sometimes the kindest report is the boring one."

The unnamed inspector's hand was still near his hip. He looked at Ilyes. He looked at Okonkwo. He looked, longest of all, at Tomas, who chose that moment to hide his face against Osei's shoulder with the flawless instinct of a real child, whether he meant to or not.

The inspector took his hand away from his hip.

"Consumption anomaly resolved," he said. "Sensor drift and dietary variance. I'll note it in the secondary log."

Okonkwo looked at her two inspectors for a long moment. Then at Elodie. Then, last, at the children—and Elodie saw it happen, whatever it was, behind the audit-flat face: a hairline crack, a memory surfacing and being pushed back down, a cost being calculated and found unacceptable.

"Here's what's going to happen," she said. "We complete the inspection. Logs verify. Biological inventory closes at three. The Perihelion gets logged as a proprietary survey vessel, which means it falls outside my jurisdiction, which means I never have to look at it again." She picked up her slate. "And if I ever come back to this station, Nakamura, there had better be nothing here worth finding. Do you understand me?"

"Completely," Elodie said.

"One more thing." She crouched again, briefly, to Osei's level. Whatever she saw in the girl's face, she looked at it directly, refusing to flinch. "Whatever accident you woke up from," Okonkwo said quietly, "grow up fast. People like me won't always be the ones who find you."

Then she straightened, and the audit-face returned, and she walked out of the hydroponics bay with her two inspectors trailing behind her—one relieved, one silent, both complicit now in the oldest conspiracy there is, which is mercy.

* * *

The cutter undocked at 1130. Elodie watched it shrink against the black until it was a spark, then a rumor, then nothing, and only then did his knees inform him they'd like to stop working. He sat down on the deck of the observation blister and laughed until it turned into something else.

*They knew,* Meridian said. *Okonkwo knew. The reconciliation was impossible—your life-support curve predates the children's arrival by weeks. She chose the boring report anyway.*

*I know.*

*Do you understand what that means? Inside Enforcement Vector Seven there is at least one officer willing to falsify a federal inspection to protect two children she met eleven minutes ago. That is not an anomaly, Elodie. That is a variable. Systems break along variables.*

Elodie looked at his hands. They were shaking, and he let them.

From the corridor behind him came small footsteps, and then Osei was standing in the doorway, still sticky with nutrient mist, the captain fully restored behind the child's face like a lighthouse behind fog.

"You used my name," Elodie said. "Ines. Why?"

"Because it's true," Osei said simply. "We heard it in the static before we ever heard your voice. All of us. It's the name the thing out there uses for this station—for the person in it." She climbed into the seat beside him, feet not reaching the floor. "Elodie, when Okonkwo crouched down and told me to grow up fast—she wasn't threatening us. She was warning us. She's going to spend the rest of her career wondering what happened to two kids on Relay Station Vesper, and someday that wondering is going to make her dangerous to the people who sent her."

"Or it'll get her reassigned to a fuel depot past Neptune."

"Maybe both." Osei swung her feet. "There's something else. While they were walking the ring, I was in the crawlspace with the wrench, and I could feel it."

"Feel what?"

"The static." She looked out the viewport, toward the dark past the Perihelion, toward the bearing with no registered source. "It changed when the cutter docked. It got... attentive. Like it was listening to them too." She turned to him, and her voice dropped into the register of a captain reporting contact. "Elodie—it knows they're here. And eleven days is what Meridian gave us, right?"

"Eleven days."

"Then we should assume the thing beyond the heliopause is counting them down with us." She smiled, and it was a child's smile and it was not a child's smile at all. "Come on. The others drew the waveform again while you were upstairs lying beautifully. This time Tomas got the amplitude right, and I think it's trying to tell us something new."
