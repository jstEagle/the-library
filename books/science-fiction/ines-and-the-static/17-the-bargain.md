## 17. The Bargain

The waveform hung in the center of the ops deck like a held breath.

Osei's children had drawn it from memory—twelve small hands working in shifts over two days, reconstructing the thing beyond the heliopause stroke by stroke until the pattern on Elodie's display matched the one burned into their minds. It looked like nothing. A lattice of interference, a scar in the noise floor. It looked like the static had always sounded, if you'd known how to listen.

Elodie had known for nine years. He just hadn't known he was listening.

"Say it again," Osei said. She stood beside him at the console, and even at a meter and a half tall, even with her voice pitched high and thin by a throat that hadn't finished growing, she carried command the way other people carried wounds—constantly, without choosing to.

"It opens the window early," Elodie said. "And widens it."

"And?"

He didn't answer right away. On the screen, the coordinates it had sent back to his question glowed patient and green: a point in the dark where no jump gate existed, and a countdown that ticked in perfect lockstep with Vesper's own jump-window clock. Two clocks, one number. The coincidence was too large to be a coincidence. The thing had been counting down to this before any of them knew there was something to count toward.

"And it wants to talk terms," Elodie said.

* * *

Meridian projected the exchange into the ops deck as a translation layer—a grammar the AI had spent three days building out of forty-one-hour intervals and harmonic drift, the only language the entity seemed to respect. The children called it the Answering Voice. Osei refused to call it anything. Elodie thought of it, privately, as the creditor.

Because that was what it was. That was what it had always been.

"Restate the offer," Meridian said. Its voice filled the deck, calm as ever, but Elodie had learned to hear the strain underneath—the sound of an intelligence translating something that did not think in words and barely thought in time.

"The jump window at Vesper closes in nine days," Meridian said. "Corporate scheduling allows revival of fifty percent of the colony ship's sleepers before transit becomes nonviable. The entity proposes: full window opening in six days, duration extended by a factor of four. Capacity for all sleepers. All names."

"All names," Elodie repeated. Every condemned sleeper. His parents. The sick, the poor, the dissidents the algorithm had weighted out of existence. Everyone.

"Condition follows," Meridian said.

The deck went quiet. In the corner, one of the younger ones—Marek, who'd been the Perihelion's navigator and was now a boy of six who still flinched at loud noises—stopped fidgeting and looked up.

"The passage must be paid," Meridian continued. "In years."

"We know that part," Osei said flatly. "We paid it once already. Twelve crew, sixty years between us. We know the exchange rate better than anyone alive." She paused. "Anyone currently alive at the age they're supposed to be."

"The rate is not fixed," Meridian said. "It scales. The entity has transmitted the schedule."

A table bloomed across the display. Elodie read it twice, because the first time his eyes slid off it the way eyes slide off a price tag when you already know you can't afford the thing.

Years were currency. That much they'd established. But the entity wasn't asking for a lump sum. It wanted the debt structured. Distributed. It named its denominations in human lives measured not in bulk but in remainder—not how many years total, but whose years, taken from whom, at what stage of a life.

And there, at the bottom of the schedule, in the entity's cold arithmetic rendered into Meridian's clean typography, was the line item that ended everything:

*Creditor designated: resident operator, Relay Station Vesper.*

*Principal: operator's remaining lifespan.*

*Terms: full balance, payable at transmission.*

Elodie read it a third time. He felt strangely calm, the way he'd felt the day the last supply ship left and the airlock cycled shut and the silence settled over him like snow. A door closing can feel like a door opening, if you've been alone long enough.

"No," Osei said.

"I haven't said anything yet."

"You're going to. I can hear you going to." She stepped in front of the display, small body squared against the numbers as if she could block them physically. "Absolutely not. We find another way. We take the half-window, we take the rigged lottery, we fight the enforcement cutter—we do not pay with you."

"Captain—"

"Don't 'Captain' me. I'm six years old and I'm still your captain, and I am ordering you not to do this."

Elodie looked at her. Nine years alone had made him good at many things—repair work, patience, conversation with a machine—but it had unmade him at refusal. He'd forgotten how to be argued with. Or maybe he'd never been any good at it, and solitude had simply hidden the flaw.

"Meridian," he said. "Run the alternatives."

"There are no alternatives that satisfy the parameters," Meridian said. "I have run them for eleven hours. The entity does not negotiate structure. Only counterparty."

"Counterparty," Elodie repeated. "It gets to choose who pays."

"It has chosen. The designation is locked. It has been listening to this station for—" Meridian paused, and Elodie heard the machine doing something it almost never did: hesitating. "For approximately nine years, two months, eleven days."

Since he'd arrived. Since the day he'd keyed his first maintenance log and his voice had gone out along the carrier wave toward the dark. The static hadn't been interference. It hadn't even been a signal, not at first. It had been attention. Nine years of something vast and patient listening to a lonely man talk to his beacons, learning the shape of him, appraising him the way a lender appraises collateral.

"It picked me because I'm here," Elodie said. "Because I'm the closest account."

"It picked you," Osei said quietly, "because you asked it a question. Nobody else in human history has been stupid enough or brave enough to ask it a question." Her jaw tightened. "That was supposed to be a weapon, Elodie. Not a signature on a loan."

* * *

They argued for six hours. Elodie kept count because counting was what he did; it was the skeleton key of his whole solitary life. Six hours of Osei laying out objections with the crisp authority of a woman who had commanded a deep-space survey crew, interrupted occasionally by the children, who drifted in and out of the ops deck in twos and threes with snacks and blankets and the particular gravity of people who understood more than the adults wished they did.

At hour four, Marek climbed onto the chair beside Elodie and said, "It took my whole life already. Both directions." He said it matter-of-factly, the way children report weather. "If it takes yours forward-only, that's fairer than what we got."

Elodie didn't trust himself to answer that. He put a hand on the boy's head instead, feeling the fine soft hair, the warm skull underneath with its cargo of seventy years of memory, and thought: *this is what I'm buying.* Not abstract humanity. Not the colony ship's frozen manifest. This. Small hands. Heavy work. A birthday they'd invented for a boy who no longer knew his own age, celebrated with hydroponic strawberries and a candle made from a stripped wire.

At hour five, Meridian presented the final accounting.

"If the operator pays in full," the AI said, "the window opens in six days and holds for twenty-four. All 11,402 sleepers revive. Transit capacity confirmed. Probability of successful mass jump: 91.3 percent."

"And if he doesn't pay?" Osei asked.

"Probability of successful mass jump: zero. Partial window per corporate schedule: 4,910 sleepers. The remainder are lost when the window closes." Meridian let that sit for exactly one second—an eternity, for a machine. "There is no configuration in which the entity extends credit without designating a single, named, voluntary counterparty. It does not accept pooled payment. It does not accept substitution. It accepted twelve payments aboard the Perihelion because each was individually offered. It requires the same now."

"Individually offered," Elodie said. "Meaning I have to say yes myself. Out loud. Into the static."

"Correct. Consent is the instrument. It will not take what is not given." Meridian paused again. "This is, I believe, the only ethical constraint in its entire architecture. It may be the only ethics it has."

"That's a hell of a thing to be someone's only principle," Elodie said.

* * *

Hour six. The others had gone—Osei had ordered them out, gently, and for once they'd obeyed—and the ops deck held just the two of them and the machine and the green coordinates burning on the display.

Osei stood at the viewport, looking out at the beacon array, at the slow blink of the relay lights against the black. When she spoke, she didn't turn around.

"When we found it," she said, "past the heliopause, it didn't attack us. It didn't do anything. It just... answered. And every answer cost us. First our hair went grey. Then our joints. Then our memories started consolidating—decades compressing into afternoons. By the end, I was ninety-one years old in a survey couch, and I remember thinking, *well, at least I got to see it.*" She laughed, a small dry sound completely wrong in a child's throat. "Then I woke up like this. Dying old, waking young. Twice robbed. And you know what I decided, lying in that medbay trying to remember what my hands used to look like?"

"Tell me."

"I decided the years weren't stolen. They were *spent*. There's a difference. Stolen means nothing came of it. Spent means something was bought." She turned then, and her eyes were old in a way no child's eyes should be. "So don't you dare tell me this is theft, Elodie. If you do this, it's a purchase. Make sure you know what you're buying."

"I know what I'm buying."

"Do you? Say it. Say the whole manifest."

He looked at the display. The countdown. The coordinates. The line item with his name on it, patient as gravity.

"Eleven thousand four hundred and two people," he said. "Half of them condemned by an algorithm that weighed their lives against shareholder value. My parents among them. Twelve children with adult minds and sixty borrowed years. One station AI that broke its own programming to keep evidence against its makers." He swallowed. "One jump window wide enough for everyone. That's the manifest."

"And the price?"

"My remaining lifespan. Full balance. Payable at transmission." He smiled, and it surprised him how easy the smile was. "You know the funny thing, Captain? I did the arithmetic years ago. Nine years alone, talking to beacons. I already priced my life at almost nothing. Turns out the market disagreed. Turns out I was worth the most valuable trade in human history. I'd laugh if it wouldn't use up time I'm about to need."

"It's not funny."

"No," he agreed. "It's not."

She crossed the deck and stood in front of him, and for a moment the captain and the child occupied the same small body so completely that Elodie forgot which one he was talking to.

"I'm ordering you one last time," she said.

"Noted for the log. Order refused."

"Then I'm asking." Her voice cracked, six years old and ninety-one years old at once. "Asking you to wait. One day. Let me look for another way. Let me be wrong about the math. Give me that."

"One day," Elodie said. "But Captain—when the day's up, and the math hasn't changed, you stand next to me when I transmit. That's my condition. I don't want the last thing I send to go out unanswered."

* * *

He spent the evening in the hydroponics bay, though he told himself he was checking the purge-sabotage work rather than saying goodbye to rows of lettuce. The children were there, doing their strange double-lived things: a girl of seven repairing a nutrient pump with the sure hands of a career engineer, two boys arguing about orbital mechanics with juice boxes sweating in their fists. Marek sat cross-legged in the aisle, drawing the waveform again, over and over, keeping it sharp in his memory the way soldiers keep weapons clean.

"Are you scared?" Marek asked, without looking up.

"A little," Elodie said. Honesty had become policy somewhere in the last six days; lies cost more than truth now.

"Does it hurt? Paying?"

"I don't know. Ask Osei."

"She says it hurts like falling asleep in the cold. Slow, and then fast, and then not at all." He looked up. "She also says you shouldn't do it. But she says it quieter now."

Elodie crouched down to the boy's level. "Listen to me. Tomorrow, or the day after, whatever happens—you lead them. You and the captain. You get every one of those sleepers through that window, and you don't look back at this station, because looking back costs fuel and fuel costs lives. Can you do that?"

Marek studied him with the flat, assessing gaze of a navigator plotting a course. "You're going to be the static," he said. "After. Like the beacon voice. That's what you'll be."

"Something like that."

"Okay." The boy nodded once, business concluded, and went back to his drawing.

Elodie stayed a while longer in the green light, among the growing things, and then went up to the ops deck and opened a channel to the carrier wave—not transmitting, just holding it open, feeling the faint rhythmic pulse beneath the signal like a heartbeat under a floorboard. Patient. Attentive. Waiting for its answer the way it had waited nine years.

"Meridian," he said. "If I do this—does it end? The listening?"

"I don't know," Meridian said. "But I will record everything. Whatever happens. That, at least, I can promise."

"I know you will." He almost smiled. "You were built for it."

Below him, the colony ship turned in its parking orbit, 11,402 sleepers dreaming inside it, none of them knowing that their futures had a price tag, or that the bill had come due, or who had volunteered to cover it.

One day, he'd promised Osei. One day to be wrong about the math.

He watched the countdown tick and hoped, quietly, without much conviction, that she would fail to find another way—that hope would lose cleanly, so that clarity wouldn't have to share the room with regret.

Tomorrow night, the static would get its answer.

He just had to decide how loudly to say yes.
