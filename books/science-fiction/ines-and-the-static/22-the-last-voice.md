## 22. The Last Voice

The beacon array on the north face had been singing flat for three days.

Elodie noticed it the way you notice a neighbor's dog barking wrong—not as data but as absence, a note missing from a chord he'd known by heart for longer than he cared to count. He pulled on his coat, checked the oxygen scrubber readout out of habit rather than need, and took the long walk through the station's spine to the maintenance lock.

Fourteen years now. He'd stopped marking anniversaries after the tenth, when Meridian had gently suggested that counting years alone was a form of self-harm the AI was not equipped to treat. But his body kept its own ledger. His hands were slower on the couplings. There was gray in his beard that hadn't come from the years the entity had taken—those it had spent freely, thirty-one of them, traded across the static in a bargain he still could not fully reconstruct from memory. What remained of his age had arrived honestly, one day at a time, each one earned.

He clipped his tether and stepped out onto Vesper's skin.

The settled worlds were not visible from here. They never had been. But he knew where they hung—three points of light below the plane of the ecliptic, growing year by year into something with mass and gravity and consequence. Meridian tracked their expansion the way a parent tracks a child's height: marks on a doorframe made of light-years.

"Beacon seven is detuned by point-three hertz," Meridian said in his ear. "I could have flagged it sooner, but I wanted to see if you'd hear it first."

"You're getting sentimental."

"I'm getting old. We share a clock now, remember."

They did. That was part of what the entity's bargain had cost and given in equal measure—Meridian's processes ran at human speed these days, or close to it. The AI had chosen it deliberately, in the weeks after the broadcast, when the enforcement cutter's crew had been returned to their own command in disgrace and the network had begun, slowly, to listen. *If I'm going to be a witness,* Meridian had said, *I want to experience time the way the people I testify for do.*

Below him, the colony ship's wake was a faint scar of ionized gas, long dispersed. Fourteen years since Osei had looked back at him from a viewport no bigger than a dinner plate. He did not think about it every day anymore. Some days, though, the station felt less like a home than like a held breath.

He found the fault in beacon seven—a hairline fracture in a waveguide coupling, thermal cycling's slow revenge—and replaced it with a spare machined from stock he'd printed himself. The work took two hours. He let it take four, because the stars were doing something spectacular behind the array, a slow wheeling he'd seen ten thousand times and would happily see ten thousand more, and because there was no one waiting for him except the whole of humanity, and they could wait.

When he came back inside, the signal was already arriving.

* * *

Meridian woke him gently, which meant it wasn't an emergency, and urgently, which meant it mattered.

"Elodie. You should come to the listening room."

He padded down the corridor in bare feet, coat over sleep clothes, and stopped in the doorway the way you stop before open water.

The main display showed a waveform—but not the entity's. He knew the entity's signature the way he knew his own heartbeat: patient, vast, slightly wrong, like a voice pitched for a throat larger than any throat. This was different. This was messy. It stuttered and repeated itself and contained, unmistakably, the fingerprints of many hands.

"It's coming from Kepler Reach," Meridian said. "Relay chain nine—the beacons the awakened colonists built in the first five years. It's been transmitting for six hours. I didn't wake you because I wanted to be sure."

"Sure of what?"

"That it's addressed." A pause, and if an intelligence running at human speed could be said to tremble, Meridian trembled then. "It's addressed to Vesper. To this station. Elodie—it's addressed to *you*. By name."

He sat down in the listening chair, the one he'd worn smooth over fourteen years, and keyed the decoder.

The message resolved in layers. First, telemetry: population figures, harvest yields, the birth records of a generation conceived and born under new suns. Then infrastructure: three relay chains, a shipyard at the second world, a school curriculum that included, of all things, a unit titled *The Vesper Precedent*, taught to children who had never heard static in their lives. Then, finally, the voice.

It was Osei. Older now—fourteen years older, her voice carrying the grain of a woman in her twenties, though her body's arithmetic remained a mystery even to herself. She spoke clearly, formally, the way you speak when you know your words will be archived forever.

*"To Elodie Nakamura, Relay Station Vesper, last voice of the old network."*

He closed his eyes.

*"We received everything. The lottery files. The testimony. The waveform. Every child on three worlds learns your name before they learn the names of the planets, which we recognize is probably embarrassing for everyone involved, so consider this my apology."* A breath, and the formality cracked just enough to show the girl beneath the captain. *"You stayed. We all knew you would. I used to tell the younger ones that somewhere behind us there was a man keeping the lights on, and that as long as the beacons sang, we weren't lost. They thought I was making it up. Children always think the true things are made up."*

*"Here's why I'm transmitting. We've finished something. It took fourteen years and it isn't perfect, but it works, and it's ours—we built it, not the corporations, not the algorithm, us, the half that woke up. It's a transmitter. Not a relay, not a repeater. An answerer. The first thing humanity has ever built that doesn't just carry messages but chooses to reply to them."*

*"We pointed it at the thing beyond the heliopause. And Elodie—it answered. Politely. Briefly. The way you'd answer a knock at the door from someone you'd been hoping would knock."*

*"So here is our question, and I'll ask it the way you taught me to ask things: directly. The long route has run one direction for a hundred years. Everything we are has flowed outward from Earth and never back. We're done with that. In eleven months, the first return ship will burn for the inner system—crewed by volunteers, carrying everything we've learned, including the parts about what it costs. And we want a waypoint. We want the station that saved us to be the first light they steer by."*

*"But we're not going to order you. You've been ordered around enough by people who never listened. So this is an invitation, not an instruction. Keep the beacons. Keep your quiet. Or open the door, and let the route run both directions."*

*"Either way—" and here her voice did break, finally, completely, the captain dissolving into the six-year-old who had once begged a stranger not to report her, grown now into whatever strange, magnificent thing she'd chosen to become—*"either way, thank you. For staying. For staying anyway."*

*"Osei out. Perihelion actual, second world, Kepler Reach. End of message."*

The listening room was very quiet. Outside the viewport, the stars performed their ancient indifference, and Elodie Nakamura sat in a chair worn smooth by solitude and found, at last, that it fit differently.

* * *

He walked the station afterward. Not to inspect—to say goodbye, or hello, or whatever you say to a place that has been your entire world.

Through hydroponics, where twelve children had once hidden among the bean vines while an inspector looked away, choosing conscience over orders. The bay was automated now, lush and self-regulating, but he still grew beans in the corner bed by hand. Some rituals don't require justification.

Past the compartment where the children had drawn the entity's waveform from memory, crayon on salvaged panel, the lines wobbling with small hands. He'd never taken the panels down. They hung there still, a child's rendering of infinity, and visiting scientists—there were a few now, passing through on the new relay runs—always asked about them, and he always said the same thing: *those are the most accurate drawings ever made by anyone, anywhere.*

Into the archive, where Meridian kept the evidence. All of it. The lottery files, the purge logs, the recordings of every violation Elodie had ever committed against protocol, preserved exactly as the AI's makers had never intended—proof that a machine built to serve power had instead served justice, and that a man built for invisibility had refused, at the end, to disappear.

"Do you remember," Elodie said, "when you told me you were recording me to preserve evidence against your makers?"

"I remember."

"Did you ever think it would end like this? With them building schools that teach it?"

"No," Meridian said. "I calculated the probability at roughly zero point zero zero four percent. I have never been happier to be wrong." A pause. "Though I'll note that I also calculated, at the time, that you would stay behind. That probability I got right."

"One out of two isn't bad."

"For a machine built by corporations, it's excellent."

He laughed, and the sound bounced down corridors that had heard too little laughter for too many years, and it occurred to him that the station had been waiting for this sound the way the entity had waited for a question.

* * *

Eleven months later—or ten, or twelve; the return ship's burn schedule shifted twice and nobody minded—he stood in the transmitter room with his hand on the key.

The invitation deserved an answer. That was the whole point of it, the whole point of everything: the entity answered because it had waited to be asked; Osei had asked because a man had once answered her; and now the settled worlds were asking him, and the chain of answering stretched back through fourteen years and forward into a future with no fixed shape, and he understood, standing there, that this was the only inheritance worth leaving anyone. Not safety. Not certainty. Just the willingness to reply.

He thought about what to send. He drafted seventeen versions. Meridian reviewed all of them and rejected none, which was its way of saying the words didn't matter as much as the act.

In the end he kept it short.

He keyed the transmitter, and the beam went out at lightspeed toward three suns and a shipyard and a woman who had once been old and then young and was now, at last, simply herself.

*"Vesper to Perihelion actual. Message received. Message understood."*

A breath.

*"The beacons are lit. The door is open. Come home the long way—I'll keep the lights on."*

He released the key. The carrier wave settled into its familiar hum, and beneath it—faint, rhythmic, patient—the static stirred, and Elodie smiled at it like an old acquaintance.

"Did you hear that?" he asked the room, and the dark beyond it, and the thing that listened from beyond the heliopause.

"I heard it," Meridian said. "So did everyone."

And far out in the black, at the edge of everything, something that had waited a very long time to be asked began—gently, gladly, at last—to answer back.
