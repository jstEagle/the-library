## 11. Meridian Wreck

**Dominic**

The coordinates Ines gave him didn't look like anything. That was the first thing he noticed — a string of numbers that resolved on the nav display to a stretch of empty dark between two dead gas giants, the kind of place star charts marked with nothing at all. No station. No debris field logged. No reason for anyone to have ever gone there.

"You're sure about this," he said.

Ines stood beside him in the cockpit with her arms folded tight across her chest, the way she did when she was holding something in that wanted badly to get out. The running lights painted her face in slow alternation of red and green, and in that shifting light she looked younger and older at once, like a photograph of someone taken across decades.

"I memorized those coordinates when I was nine years old," she said. "Before I memorized anything else. Before the language, even. My mother made me recite them every night like a prayer." A pause. "She said someday I'd need to find my way home."

"And home is a patch of nothing."

"Home is what they tell you is nothing." She unfolded her arms and touched the viewport, as if she could reach through the glass and the vacuum and forty years of silence all at once. "Take us in slow, Dominic. Slow enough to see."

He took them in slow.

The Vector's hull ticked around them as it cooled, that familiar creak of metal settling into the cold, and the two dead giants wheeled past the viewport like sleeping animals, their storm-banded flanks lit faintly by a sun too distant to warm anything. He watched the range counter fall. Ten thousand kilometers. Five. Two. Nothing on passive sensors but hydrogen haze and the whisper of the solar wind.

Then, at eight hundred kilometers, the Vector's shadow found it.

It came out of the dark the way shipwrecks do — not gradually but all at once, a shape resolving out of shapelessness, and Dominic felt his breath go shallow in his chest. It was enormous. A colony ship, one of the old deep-range hulls, the kind they'd stopped building before he was born: a kilometer and a half of spine and habitat rings, its drive cone dark, its skin scabbed with micrometeorite scars and the long oxidation blooms of decades in the hard vacuum. It tumbled with the patience of a dead thing, end over end, so slowly you had to watch for a full minute to see it move at all.

"That's not possible," he heard himself say. It was becoming a habit around this woman, saying that. "The Meridian was lost with all hands. It's in every history text. The Concord issued a memorial—"

"They issued a memorial because they needed somewhere to put the grief," Ines said, "so no one would ask where to put the blame."

The name came up off the hull as the Vector's floodlamps swept across it, letter by letter, in an alphabet he'd only ever seen in Ines's notebooks: MERIDIAN, and beneath it, smaller, worn nearly smooth, a line of the other language — the dead language, the impossible language — that he could not read yet but was beginning, against all his instincts, to recognize the shape of. Like recognizing a song from the rhythm alone.

"It's been scuttled," he said, and his voice came out flat with shock. "Look at the drive section. Those aren't battle damage. Someone opened the coolant lines and flooded the reactor housing deliberately. Someone killed this ship on purpose and walked away."

"Yes," Ines said.

He turned to look at her. She wasn't looking at the wreck anymore. She was looking at her own hands, turning them over as if checking whether they still belonged to her.

"Ines. Who scuttled it?"

"The people who told the galaxy it burned in a drive accident with eleven thousand souls aboard." She looked up, and there it was — the fury he'd been waiting weeks to see in her, banked so long it had gone hard and bright as slag iron. "The people who then spent thirty years making sure no one went looking. The people whose fleet is currently sitting three systems away, waiting for your forged transmissions to give them an excuse."

* * *

**Ines**

She had dreamed of this moment so many times that being inside it felt like a second dream, less vivid than the ones she'd built.

In the dreams she always wept. Standing here in the actual cold light of the actual wreck, she found she couldn't. There was a pressure behind her eyes like water against a sealed hatch, and it would not open, and she understood dimly that some part of her had decided grief was a luxury she hadn't earned yet — not until she knew everything, not until the whole shape of the crime lay exposed in the floodlights where she could count its teeth.

Dominic flew them along the Meridian's spine, close enough that the hull filled the viewport wall to wall. She read it the way she'd once read archival documents: the scorch pattern at the airlock where cutting torches had opened her from outside; the docking collars stripped away; the habitat windows darkened from within, painted over with sealant — someone had wanted no light escaping, no sign of life, even though there had been no life left to signal. Or so the record would say.

"There," she said. "Bay four. Port side."

"What am I looking at?"

"The maintenance shuttle bays are all empty. Every one except bay four. Look at the door."

He looked. She watched him work it out, watched his jaw tighten as the arithmetic completed itself. The bay door was closed but the seals were fresh — newer than the rest of the hull, a different alloy, a repair made after the scuttling. And bolted over it, almost invisible under grime and oxide, a transponder housing.

"A beacon," Dominic said slowly. "Someone left a beacon on a scuttled ship."

"Not a beacon. An archive node. Passive. It only answers to a handshake protocol." She pulled her slate from her jacket, and her hands were steady, which surprised her. "My mother's handshake. She helped design the archive system. They thought they'd erased everything." Her mouth curved, and it was not a kind expression. "They never asked the archivist's daughter."

She keyed the handshake into the short-range laser link, character by character, in a language extinct for two centuries according to every database in human space, and alive in her mouth the way her mother's voice was alive in her memory.

For a long moment, nothing. The Meridian turned its slow grave somersault against the stars. Then a green light bloomed on the port side of bay four, and her slate filled with data — a catalog index, a manifest, timestamps — and the pressure behind her eyes finally broke, though mercifully her back was to Dominic and the floodlights were harsh enough to hide it.

The archive was intact. Forty years of records. Ship's logs. Passenger manifests. And, flagged in her mother's private notation scheme, a folder labeled with a single word in the old tongue that translated, more or less, to *evidence*.

"Dominic," she said, and had to stop and start again. "Pull us alongside. I'm going over in the suit."

"Absolutely not. That hull has been tumbling unpressurized for forty years—"

"I don't need to go inside. Just to the node. I need physical access to pull the sealed cores — the sensitive files are air-gapped, they won't transmit." She was already moving toward the airlock, pulling her suit liner on. "And I need to stand on it. You wouldn't understand."

He caught her arm at the inner hatch, gently, the way he did everything — as if handling something that might shatter or might bite, still deciding which. "Eleven thousand people," he said quietly. "You knew them."

"I knew all of them," she said. "That's what an archivist's daughter is. I catalogued the dead before they were dead. I have their handwriting samples in my head. Their voices." She looked down at his hand on her arm and did not shake it off. "Let me go collect them."

* * *

**Dominic**

He held the Vector in a hover thirty meters off bay four while she crossed the gap on a tether line, and he discovered that watching her drift toward that vast dark hull was worse than any combat approach he'd ever flown. The suit comms carried her breathing, slow and even, and beneath it the static song of the Meridian's residual charge ticking against his antenna array.

"I've got movement on the tumble axis," he warned. "Thirty seconds of shadow in about ninety. Don't be on the hull when it comes."

"Copy," she said, distracted, already at the node housing.

He watched her work on the external feed — small gloved hands, methodical, unsealing a panel that had waited four decades for exactly these fingers. He found himself thinking about the word she'd used. *Home.* She'd called a derelict home, and the terrible thing was that standing here, looking at the kilometer of murdered steel turning in the dark, he was starting to understand her.

The first core came free. Then the second. On the third, her breathing changed.

"Dominic." Her voice had gone strange. Careful. "I'm reading the scuttle logs. They're in the clear — the crew filed them openly, right up to the end. They weren't hiding. They thought the truth would protect them."

"Read it to me."

A pause. Static. When she spoke again, each word came out like something pried loose with tools.

"Concord naval survey vessel *Halcyon* intercepted the Meridian eleven years into transit. Not an accident. Not distress. Intercepted, boarded, seized under emergency authority — statute citation follows, and Dominic, the statute they cite doesn't exist. It's fabricated. The whole boarding authority is forged." Another pause, longer. "The passengers were transferred to three transport hulls over six days. Eleven thousand and forty-two souls, listed by name. The transports' destinations are redacted — no. Not redacted. Encrypted, and the encryption key is registered to—" She stopped.

"To who?"

"To the Concord Office of Colonial Settlement. Which was dissolved two years later and folded into the Admiralty's strategic planning directorate." Her breath fogged the faceplate feed. "They didn't lose the Meridian. They took it. They took the people off it, scattered them to places the logs weren't allowed to say, blew the drives, wrote a memorial, and buried the file so deep that the only copy left in the universe was bolted to the corpse."

The shadow came. The Meridian's bulk swung between the Vector and the distant sun, and the floodlit hull went black, and for a few seconds Ines was just a suit-light drifting against darkness, a single small flame beside a drowned cathedral.

When the light came back she was hanging motionless, facing the hull, both hands pressed flat against the scarred plating over what he guessed had once been a garden deck. He let the comm stay open and said nothing, because there was nothing a man could say to that, and because he was busy feeling something rise in his own chest that he recognized, with unease, as fury — inherited, adopted, but his now. The Concord had written a memorial. His original had died — had maybe died, had maybe not — chasing exactly this. And somewhere above them both, three systems away, the same admiralty that had done this sat with weapons hot, waiting for a lie to give them permission.

"Come back to the ship," he said at last.

"One more minute."

"Take your minute," he said. "I'll keep the lights on."

* * *

**Ines**

Inside the Vector's small cargo bay, stripped of the suit, she laid the three archive cores out on the deck plates in a row, and the three of them sat over them like mourners over a coffin — which, she supposed, in a way they were.

"Play the last entry," she said. "Ship's log. Final day."

Dominic queued it. The speaker crackled, and then a voice filled the bay — a woman's voice, accented with the old language's vowels, tired but level, speaking standard trade-tongue for the record:

"*Final log of the colony vessel Meridian, date as stamped. This is First Archivist Sefa Voss, recording for whoever finds this ship. We have been ordered to disembark. We are told our destination cannot be disclosed for reasons of settlement security. We are told our ship will be disposed of. I have moved the archives to the exterior node and sealed them. To whoever comes after: we were not lost. We were taken. Say our names. We had names.*"

Silence. The recycler hummed. Somewhere aft, the Vector's aging drive coil ticked as it cooled.

"Sefa Voss," Ines said. Her voice came out sanded flat. "My mother."

Dominic looked at her sharply. "You never said—"

"There was never a *before* worth mentioning." She knelt and touched the nearest core, lightly, the way you'd touch a sleeping child. "She recorded that knowing she might never see me again. I was already off-ship — sent ahead on a supply run with my father. We waited at the rendezvous for a ship that the logs now show was rerouted by the same office. We waited eleven days. Then the Concord patrol boat found *us*, and told us the Meridian had burned, and offered resettlement assistance, and watched us for the next twenty years to make sure we stayed grateful and quiet."

"And the others? The eleven thousand?"

"Scattered. That's the elegance of it — no massacre to investigate, no mass graves. Just eleven thousand people dropped in small groups onto colonies that didn't want them, given new registries, new names where convenient. Told their language marked them. Told that speaking it invited attention." She laughed, and it was an awful sound, thin as wire. "They didn't kill the language, Dominic. They made it *dangerous*. Made the survivors bury it themselves. Two centuries of extinction — no. Four decades. Four decades of mothers teaching children to forget."

She stood. The fury was still there, banked and bright, but threaded through it now was something else, something that surprised her with its warmth: vindication, clean and cold as well-water. The archive was real. The logs were real. Her mother's voice was real, and it named the crime, and it named the criminals, and it had waited forty years in the dark for someone to come and carry it into the light.

"We have it," she said. "All of it. Names, dates, the forged statute, the transport registrations. Enough to burn the cover-up down to the waterline."

"Enough to start a war, you mean." Dominic was staring at the cores, and his face had gone careful in the way she'd learned meant fear wearing a workingman's expression. "Ines. Both fleets are converging on a fiction. If we drop the *truth* into the middle of it—"

"Then the fiction dies and the truth takes its place, and everyone has to decide what they're actually fighting about."

"Or everyone decides the evidence is a forgery planted by their enemy, and fires anyway."

She looked at him — this man built from a dead investigator's template, carrying another man's debts and another man's murder case, who had burned fuel he didn't have toward a voice in the dark and kept burning ever since. He was afraid. Good. So was she. Fear meant he understood the stakes.

"Then we make sure they can't call it a forgery," she said. "We take them to the source. We take them *here*."

His head came up. "The wreck is neutral ground. Nobody can dispute a hull they can touch—"

The proximity alarm cut him off.

They both froze. The Vector's threat board woke in amber, then red: a drive signature, inbound, decelerating hard from beyond the second gas giant — a profile the AI tagged, after a heartbeat of consultation with databases older than either of them, as Concord naval. A corvette, running dark until now, closing on the coordinates that Ines's forged transmissions had made the most interesting empty space in the sector.

"They followed the forgeries' timing drift," Dominic breathed. "Or they've been shadowing us since the parley." He was already moving for the cockpit, and she was already moving with him, and behind them in the cargo bay the three archive cores sat in their row of lamplight, full of names, waiting.

On the display, the contact resolved: one corvette, and behind it, at the edge of sensor range, two more.

"Ines," Dominic said quietly, hands moving across the boards, "whatever we were going to do next — we're going to do it with an audience."
