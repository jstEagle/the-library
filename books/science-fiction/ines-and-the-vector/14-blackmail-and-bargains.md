## 14. Blackmail and Bargains

The invitation came on cream-colored cardstock, which was absurd. Nobody used paper anymore except people who wanted you to know they could afford the affectation.

*Ms. Voss — drinks aboard the CSV Meridian's Wake, 1900 hours. Dress: whatever you like. — S. Iqbal, Concord Naval Intelligence.*

Ines read it three times in the galley of the Vector while the recycler hummed its tired hymn and Dominic slept off his shift in the narrow bunk behind her. The name of the ship was a joke so old it had calcified into tradition — every Concord vessel above a certain tonnage got a heritage name dredged from the fleet's founding myths, and someone with a sense of humor had let *Meridian's Wake* slip through. Or no one had noticed. Or everyone had noticed and no one had cared, because that was how erasure worked: not as a knife but as a shrug.

She turned the card over. Blank. She held it to the light, looking for microprinting, for a tracker, for anything. Just paper, faintly scented with something floral and expensive.

She should burn it. She should wake Dominic and tell him and they should burn fuel instead, run for open space, abandon the delicate architecture of lies they'd spent weeks building between two fleets.

Instead she found herself, at 1858 hours, drifting toward the Concord ship's guest berth in a borrowed coat, her heart beating the way it always did before she lied to someone new: slow, deliberate, like a diver conserving air.

* * *

The *Meridian's Wake* smelled different from the Vector. That was the first thing. The Vector smelled of recycled water and hot electronics and, lately, of the strange green effort of the hydroponics bay Dominic had coaxed back to life — a living smell, imperfect, human. The Concord ship smelled of nothing. Filtered air scrubbed down to a neutral absence, surfaces polished to a shine that reflected nothing back. A ship designed to leave no impression, because impressions were evidence.

Sera Iqbal met her at the wardroom door without an escort.

Ines had expected the intelligence officer to look a certain way — sharp, severe, uniform pressed into a weapon. Iqbal was none of that. She was small, round-faced, maybe fifty, with laugh lines that seemed genuinely earned and a civilian's cardigan over a uniform collar she'd clearly unbuttoned the moment she stepped off duty. Her hands were wrapped around a teapot, not a glass, and she poured two cups before Ines had finished taking in the room.

"You looked at the ship's name," Iqbal said, by way of greeting. "When your skiff docked. You looked at it for four seconds. Most people don't look at all."

"Most people don't care."

"No." Iqbal handed her a cup. "They don't."

The wardroom was small and warm and had a viewport that faced away from the Vector, out toward the dark where the Meridian wreck hung in its slow orbit of debris and silence. Ines sat where she was gestured and did not drink. Tea could be drugged; tea could be sampled later; tea was also, more often than anyone liked to admit, just tea. But caution had kept her alive through decades that should have killed her, and she held the cup for the warmth alone.

"You've been sending transmissions," Iqbal said pleasantly. "From a civilization that died two hundred years ago. In a language only a handful of living beings can speak. You've convinced a Concord picket commander, three analysts, and — I'm told — Vice Admiral Okonkwo herself that there is an extant non-human or pre-collapse culture occupying these coordinates, and that they are negotiating exclusively with us." She sipped her own tea. "It's very good work. The syntax reconstruction alone is doctoral-level. The ritual honorifics in the third transmission — those took real scholarship. Most archivists wouldn't have known them."

The word *archivist* landed in the room like a dropped glass.

Ines kept her face still. She had practiced stillness for so long it had become her native language, more fluent than any tongue she'd memorized. "I don't know what you're—"

"Ines Voss." Iqbal said the name gently, the way one handles a fragile thing. "Born aboard the colony vessel Meridian, registry year eleven of voyage. Listed among the dead when the ship was reported lost with all hands, forty-one years ago. Except you weren't dead. And neither were several thousand other people who were quietly scattered across the Rim under new names, new papers, and strict instructions never to speak their own language again." She set down her cup. "I've been reading files for thirty years, Ms. Voss. I know what a buried report looks like. This one was buried by my own admiralty, and I would very much like to dig it up."

The heater ticked somewhere in the bulkhead. Out in the corridor, boots passed and faded. Ines became aware of her own breathing, of the tea's steam curling upward and vanishing, of the enormous weight of everything she had carried for four decades suddenly balanced on the edge of a stranger's next sentence.

"Why?" she asked finally. It was the only question that mattered. Not *how do you know*, not *who else knows*. Why.

Iqbal's laugh lines deepened, but nothing about her eyes laughed. "Because I'm Concord intelligence, Ms. Voss, and I am very good at my job, and my job has recently consisted of helping my admiralty prepare to start a war over coordinates they already understood perfectly well. They knew what happened to the Meridian. Some of them signed the order. And now they're willing to spend thousands of lives — Compact lives, our own lives — to keep it buried, because the alternative is admitting that the great humanitarian fleet committed genocide by paperwork and called it resettlement." She refilled her own cup, unhurried. "I've spent my career believing the institution was worth the compromises. Lately I've been doing arithmetic I don't like."

"And so you invite me for tea."

"And so I invite you for tea." Iqbal smiled, and it was a real smile, tired and crooked. "You're sitting in the den of the organization that erased your people, drinking from our cups, and I haven't had you arrested. Consider what that costs me. Then consider what I'm about to ask, and what it will cost you."

* * *

They talked for two hours.

Iqbal laid it out with the clean economy of a professional: she knew the transmissions were forged because she had run the linguistic analysis herself and found the hand of a native speaker hiding inside a scholar's grammar — the tells were subtle, the kind only another speaker would catch, and she'd caught them because she'd spent six years stationed on a border world where Meridian refugees had settled before the last sweep. She knew the distress signal that started everything had been genuine, because she'd traced its power signature to a surviving relay buoy, one of a network the scattered colonists had maintained in secret for decades. She knew, though she didn't say how, that the original Dominic Lockridge had gotten close to the truth before he'd died — and she watched Ines very carefully when she said his name, cataloguing whatever flickered across Ines's face, filing it away.

And then she made her offer.

"I can bury this," she said. "Right now, tonight. I can flag the linguistic analysis as inconclusive, attribute the anomalies to signal drift, and the war machine rolls forward on schedule. Both fleets shoot at each other over a fiction, the survivors' settlement stays hidden, and you go on playing ghost-voice until someone less careful than me catches up. That's option one. It buys time. Time is not infinite."

"Option two?"

"I take the evidence — the wreck's location, the crew manifests, the resettlement orders, the names of the officers who signed them — and I put it in front of people inside Concord command who still have spines. There are more of them than you'd think. They've been waiting for something they could act on, something concrete enough to survive the political fallout. What they've lacked is proof, and a witness." She looked directly at Ines. "A living archivist of the Meridian, testifying to what was done. That's not a document that can be shredded. That's a person."

"And if your people with spines turn out to be fewer than you think? If it goes up the chain and gets buried again — this time with you attached to it?"

"Then I'll be court-martialed, and you'll be worse than dead, because they'll know exactly where the settlement is and exactly who's been lying to both fleets." Iqbal spread her hands. "Yes. That's the risk. I won't pretend otherwise. I'm asking you to trust the institution that destroyed your life, on the strength of my word, because I happen to believe some part of it is still worth saving." She paused. "I'm aware of how that sounds."

"It sounds like blackmail," Ines said quietly. "Dressed as an alliance."

"It is both. Everything worth doing is both." Iqbal leaned forward. "But understand the asymmetry, Ms. Voss. If you refuse, I lose a cause I believe in. If I betray you, you lose everything — your family, your language, the last forty years of hiding, all of it, burned in a week. You'd be a fool to trust me. I know that. So I'm not asking for trust." She slid a data chip across the table, small and dull as a seed. "That's a copy of my analysis, my service record, and a dead-man's switch keyed to my biometrics. If I'm arrested, detained, or killed within the next year, it releases to forty journalists, three judges, and one very persistent Compact intelligence officer I've been corresponding with against regulations. My insurance policy. Now you hold a copy. Trust, cautiously. As you said."

Ines stared at the chip. Her hands, she noticed distantly, were steady. Forty years of practice.

"You said option one buys time," she said. "What's option three?"

Iqbal's eyebrows rose. "There isn't one."

"There's always a third option. There's the one nobody says out loud." Ines turned the chip over in her fingers. "You go to your admiralty's enemies. Not the Compact fleets — the Compact *people*. The settlers along the Rim whose grandparents came from the same shipping lanes. The ones who already suspect. You don't expose the Meridian to save Concord's soul. You expose it to make the cost of the war unbearable on both sides at once. You make the lie so expensive that peace becomes the cheap option."

"That's—" Iqbal stopped. Ines watched her actually consider it, watch the calculation move behind her eyes like weather. "That's not how institutions work."

"No," Ines agreed. "It's how survivors work. We've had a lot of practice. Institutions are a luxury of people who've never had to scatter."

For a long moment the intelligence officer said nothing. The viewport framed the dark, and somewhere out in that dark hung a scuttled ship full of ghosts and a settlement full of children who had grown up forbidden their own mother tongue, and Ines thought: *this woman has no idea what she's asking to unleash.* None of them ever did. The admiralty hadn't, forty-one years ago, when they'd signed their tidy orders. Lockridge hadn't, when he'd started pulling threads. Even she hadn't, all those years ago in the archive stacks, when she'd copied the first file that didn't add up.

"What happened to you," Iqbal said at last, and it wasn't a question, it was almost a wound, "to make you talk like that?"

"I was archived," Ines said. "Filed under lost. It clarifies things."

* * *

She left the *Meridian's Wake* at 2130 hours with the chip in her boot and Iqbal's terms ringing in her ears: seventy-two hours to decide. The officer walked her to the airlock personally, and at the threshold — in full view of the dock cameras, deliberately, Ines realized — shook her hand and said, loudly enough for the record, "Thank you for your consultation regarding the translation work, Ms. Voss. Concord values independent scholarship."

Then, barely a breath, lips barely moving: *"Third option. Think harder about the third option."*

The Vector's airlock cycled around her with its familiar groan, and the ship's smell hit her like a tide — water and metal and growing things — and she stood in the entryway for a long moment just breathing it, letting it wash the neutral nothing of the Concord ship out of her lungs.

Dominic was awake. Of course he was. He sat at the galley table with two cups of the terrible synthetic coffee he pretended wasn't terrible, and he looked at her face for approximately one second before saying, "Whoever it was, they knew."

"Sera Iqbal. Concord intelligence." She sat, took the coffee, drank it. It tasted like burnt grain and honesty. "She knows everything, Dominic. The transmissions, the Meridian, the settlement. She's known since the second forged broadcast."

He went very still. She watched him do the math she'd already done — the exposure, the countdown, the fleets — and then do the other math, the one that took longer: *she came back. She's telling me. Why?*

"She made an offer," Ines said. "Her silence, her help, in exchange for the evidence. She wants to burn her own admiralty with it." She set the chip on the table between them, small and dull as a seed. "And she gave us her insurance policy to hold. Which means either she's the most sincere traitor Concord ever produced, or this is the most elegant trap I've seen in forty years of running from elegant traps."

"Which do you think?"

Ines looked at the chip. Thought of Iqbal's tired, crooked smile; of the ship named for a ghost no one aboard had bothered to notice; of the words *a person, not a document.*

"I think," she said slowly, "that she asked the right question. She asked what happened to me. Nobody asks that. The Compact wants my language, the Concord wants my silence, and this woman sat across a table from the woman who could end her career and asked what happened to me." She turned the chip once beneath her finger. "But there's something else. Something she said at the end, when she thought I wasn't listening."

Dominic waited.

"She said the original Lockridge's file crossed her desk three years ago. Flagged, sealed, ordered destroyed by the same office that buried the Meridian report." Ines lifted her eyes to his — his face, the face she'd recognized half-frozen in an airlock, the face that had haunted her long before she met it twice. "Dominic. Your murder wasn't just staged. It was *authorized*. By the same people. And Sera Iqbal knows why — she wouldn't say it aloud, but she knows — and in seventy-two hours we either hand her the evidence, or she buries us alongside everything else."

The coffee cooled between them. Somewhere aft, the hydroponics pump cycled on, patient and green and utterly unaware.

"So," Dominic said quietly. "We're trusting her."

"We're deciding whether trust is cheaper than the alternative." Ines smiled, and felt how thin it was. "Welcome to being a survivor, Dominic. First lesson: everybody offers you a bargain. The trick is figuring out which ones are real before the countdown ends."

Outside the viewport, far off in the dark, the running lights of a Concord picket blinked in a pattern that meant, in the old grammar of the fleet: *we see you. We are watching.*
