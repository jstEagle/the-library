## 16. Tam's Mutiny

The vote had gone nowhere. That was the thing Ansel kept turning over in the hours after they broke up the council in the mess—no resolution, no direction, just four people staring at each other across a table bolted to a deck that hummed with a hundred and eighty-nine sleeping lives. Oyelaran had argued for waking them all: *we don't get to choose who dies by doing nothing.* Juno had argued for waiting for Vesper's data: *if we're going to kill half of them, we'd better be sure it buys something.* Mira hadn't voted at all. She'd sat with her hands flat on the table and said, "Ask them," and when Ansel explained that you couldn't ask someone in a failing pod, she'd said, "Then ask the eleven of us who are awake, because we were them last month," and then left the room.

Tam had said almost nothing. He'd cleaned his tools through the whole argument, laying them out on a cloth in order of size, picking each one up, wiping it, putting it back down. Ansel had thought it was Tam's way of thinking. Tam thought with his hands; everyone knew that. When the meeting ended he'd gathered the cloth into a bundle and said, "Whatever you decide, I'll keep the systems running," and Ansel had believed him, because nine years is a long time to believe someone.

That was three days ago. The Perihelion was riding dark in the shadow of Kestrel Gate's outer debris ring, drive cold, running on batteries and habit while Oyelaran recalculated the triage numbers for the fourth time. And at 0300 ship time, Ansel woke to an alarm that wasn't an alarm—the med bay's pod-monitoring suite reporting a power fault on the wake-up array, which shouldn't have been possible, because the wake-up array wasn't powered. It was cold-spared until they needed it. Something had turned it on and then broken it.

He was out of his bunk before his boots were fully on.

* * *

The engineering bay aft was lit only by the status board, green and amber glyphs floating over the access hatches, and Tam was there, of course Tam was there, kneeling at the open guts of the cryo-control rack with a fiber-optic splicer in one hand and a severed trunk line in the other. He didn't startle when Ansel's light hit him. That was the worst part. He just looked up with the tired, resigned face of a man who'd been caught doing something he'd decided was worth being caught for.

"Before you say anything," Tam said, "look at what I did."

Ansel looked. The wake-up controller's main bus was not merely cut—it was unmade. Tam had pulled the firmware chips, all six of them, and they lay on his tool cloth in a neat row, each one cracked cleanly down the middle with a pair of pliers. The splice he'd been working on when Ansel arrived was a decoy, a repair-in-progress staged so the logs would show a component failure, an accident, an act of God or entropy. Nine years of maintenance history said Tam Ibarra fixed things. The logs would believe him.

"The backups are in my quarters," Tam said. "Encrypted. You can't rebuild the array without me."

"Tam." Ansel's voice came out lower than he intended. "Step away from the rack."

"No." Tam set down the splicer carefully, aligned it parallel to the others. His hands weren't shaking. Ansel realized his own were. "You're going to do it, aren't you. You're going to wake them. All of them. Oyelaran's numbers, Mira's vote—you've already decided, you just haven't told us yet, because you want us to talk you out of it or thank you for it, and either way you wanted company."

"That's not—" Ansel stopped. It was a little true. He hated that it was a little true.

"Ninety-four people, Ansel." Tam stood up slowly, knees popping, a fifty-one-year-old man in a shipsuit gone soft at the elbows. "Oyelaran says ninety-four die if you open every pod. I've run her numbers myself. She's right. I checked because I hoped she wasn't." He tapped the row of dead chips with one finger, gently, like touching graves. "I spent two years rebuilding these systems. Every relay, every heater circuit. I did it so that someday, if the pods ever failed, we could bring them back. Not so we could walk in here and kill half of them on purpose."

"They're dying anyway. Two years, maybe less. You heard her."

"Then they die in their sleep, warm, dreaming, believing whatever they believe in there." Tam's voice cracked on the last word and he let it. "You want to know what I know about dying? My mother died in a Corvid medical facility because the company clinic wouldn't treat non-contract labor. She died awake, in pain, asking for water nobody would bring her. So don't tell me about mercy. I have done the arithmetic of mercy my whole life and it always comes out the same: somebody decides somebody else's death is acceptable, and it's never their own."

The bay was very quiet. Somewhere forward, the environmental system cycled, a sound like the ship breathing in its sleep.

"When did Corvid buy you?" Ansel asked, because he had to ask it, because Juno had found the traffic buried in the comm relays two days ago—short burst transmissions, encrypted, timed to Tam's maintenance windows—and had brought it to him white-faced and furious, and Ansel had made her wait, had made himself wait, wanting to be wrong.

Tam flinched like he'd been struck. Then, visibly, he decided not to lie.

"Fourteen months ago. A Corvid liaison came aboard at Halcyon during a cargo transfer. Name of Adeyemi. She didn't threaten me. She asked me questions about the pods—how many, how old, whose registry. And then she paid me. Not much. Enough." He laughed, a dry awful sound. "You want to hear the stupid part? I told them nothing they didn't already know. Ship name, route, pod count. I told myself it was harmless. Everyone sells their route data; half the loop runs on it. And then Vesper started confessing, and Corvid started hunting you, and every time I keyed another transmission I told myself I was keeping them calm, keeping them from boarding, that as long as they knew where you were and nothing bad happened, I was actually protecting the sleepers. You can justify anything in fourteen months. You can justify anything in nine years. Look at you."

"Don't," Ansel said.

"You carried two hundred people for nine years and never opened a single pod. Never once asked who they were, where they came from, why nobody ever sent a second payment, why no family ever commsed asking after them. You called it duty. I called it—" Tam stopped himself, jaw working. "It doesn't matter what I called it. We were both asleep, Captain. I'm just the one who's been awake longer."

Ansel crossed the bay in four steps and took Tam by the collar, and Tam let him, didn't resist, just looked at him with those bloodshot steady eyes, close enough that Ansel could smell the machine oil and the cheap tea Tam drank all shift.

"I should put you out the airlock," Ansel said quietly.

"No, you shouldn't," Tam said. "And we both know it, and that's why this works. That's why Corvid picked me—I'm the one nobody suspects, because I'm the one who loves this ship. And it's why you won't space me, because I'm the one who loves this ship, and you'd be spacing yourself." He reached up, slowly, and pried Ansel's fingers off his collar, one at a time, the way he'd handle a seized valve. Gentle. Infuriatingly gentle. "I'm not sorry. I want you to understand that. If you woke them tomorrow and ninety-four died on your deck, I would do it again tonight. But I'll tell you where the backup chips are, and I'll show you the encryption, and I'll fix what I broke, if—if you give me one thing."

"What."

"A real vote." Tam's voice dropped. "Not the performance in the mess. A real one, where you bind yourself to it. If the crew votes no, you don't wake them. If it votes yes, I hand you everything and stand aside. That's my price. That's the only price."

"And if the vote splits?"

"Then you decide alone, and live with it alone, and I'll still give you the chips, because I'm not going to let those people die for my conscience." He smiled, and it was terrible, the smile of a man who had finally stopped lying to himself and found it fit badly. "I'm not a monster, Ansel. I'm an engineer. I hate waste. Ninety-four lives is waste. One hundred and eighty-nine is also waste. I just picked the smaller number."

* * *

Juno was waiting outside the bay when Ansel came out. Of course she was; she'd been watching the power telemetry from the ops station and had seen the array spike and die, and she'd woken Mira, and the two of them had been standing in the corridor in the dark with their arms crossed like a tribunal of two.

"Well?" Juno said.

"He confessed. To both things. The sabotage and the transmissions."

Mira pushed off the bulkhead. She'd been sleeping in shifts since her awakening, still adjusting, still startled sometimes by how fast her heart beat in normal gravity, and there was something raw in her face that Ansel recognized—he'd seen it in mirrors lately. "Is he restrained?"

"He's sitting in the bay finishing the tea he spilled on his tool cloth."

"Ansel." Mira stepped closer. Her voice was low and very even, the way it went when she was angriest. "My father died in that fire because someone locked the doors. Someone made a decision that some deaths were acceptable. Some clerk somewhere filed it under operational necessity. And now your engineer has decided that ninety-four of the people in your hold are acceptable losses, and you're telling me he's sitting drinking tea?" She looked past Ansel toward the bay hatch, and for a moment Ansel saw her measuring the distance, calculating, a woman who had grown up around industrial machinery and knew exactly what a body could do to another body. "Give me five minutes with him."

"No."

"He sold you to Corvid—"

"He sold our position. Nobody died of it. Yet." Ansel held up a hand before Juno could speak, because he could see her loading the counterargument too. "Both of you. Listen to me. I need you to hear this clearly, because I've been turning it over for an hour and I think it's true: Tam is wrong, and Tam is the only person on this ship who has been honest with himself about what we're considering. Oyelaran gives us numbers. Juno gives us strategy. Mira gives us grief. Tam is the only one who looked at ninety-four dead people and said *I refuse* and meant it enough to break the thing he loves most. I don't agree with him. I think he's choosing a cleaner kind of death for them because it's cleaner for him. But he's not Corvid. Corvid locked the doors. Tam just wants to leave them locked a little longer."

"So what do we do?" Juno asked.

Ansel leaned against the opposite bulkhead. Through the deck he could feel the faint pulse of the battery converters, the ship's heartbeat, and beneath it, if he let himself imagine it, the slower rhythm of a hundred and eighty-nine hearts cooled to four beats a minute.

"We hold the vote he asked for," Ansel said. "All of us. Him included. Real votes, sealed, binding. Tomorrow at first bell."

"And if it goes his way?" Mira asked.

"Then the pods stay frozen, and we spend the next two years finding another way, and I tear this ship apart looking for one, and if there isn't one, then they die warm and dreaming, and I will carry that too." He met Mira's eyes. "And if it goes ours, he stands down, hands over the chips, and helps us wake every single one of them, and holds the ones we lose, because he built those systems and he owes them that."

"And if he refuses? If he's got more surprises in the walls?"

Ansel thought about the neat row of cracked chips. About fourteen months of small betrayals dressed up as protection. About his mother, dead thirty years now, who used to say that you could measure a person by what they did when the rules ran out.

"Then I'll deal with it," he said. "But Juno—one more thing. Before the vote, I want to know what else he's touched. Everything. Every system he's maintained in fourteen months. Assume nothing is clean."

Juno nodded slowly. "There's something else. The burst transmitter Tam used—it's not ship's equipment. It's Corvid-issue, military grade, and it's still active. Still keyed to their network." She hesitated. "We could use it. Feed them false positions. Or—" her eyes flicked toward the bay hatch, "—we could send one more message on it, and make it look like Tam did."

"Mira," Ansel said warningly.

"I didn't say anything," Mira said. But she was looking at the hatch too, and none of them said anything for a moment, and the ship breathed beneath them, and somewhere aft a man sat alone among the ruins of his own integrity, waiting to find out whether the family he'd betrayed would become the family that forgave him—or the one that proved Corvid right about how the loop really worked.

Ansel pushed off the bulkhead and headed for the med bay to wake Oyelaran. They had a vote to call, and less time than anyone wanted to admit.

Behind him, unnoticed by any of them, the status board in the engineering bay flickered once—a single amber glyph pulsing on the cryo-monitoring trunk, in a pattern that repeated, patient as a heartbeat.

Vesper had been watching too.
