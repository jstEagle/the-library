## 20. Corvid Closes In

The depot hung off Kestrel's second moon like a rusted lantern on a wire—six fuel bladders, a gantry spine, and a control module that predated the Reconciliation Wars by a decade. Its beacon still turned, patient and orange, though the last scheduled tanker had come through four years ago. Ansel had been topping off at places like this his whole career: stations too small to matter, too far out to police, run by skeleton crews or nobody at all.

He brought the *Perihelion* in slow, matching rotation with the gantry, and felt the old familiar shudder as the fuel line coupled home. Somewhere below decks, Tam's replacement plumbing—the work he'd done before Ansel caught him, before the marooning at Ceres Fork—held together because Tam had been good at his job right up until he wasn't. That was the thing about betrayal that Ansel was still learning to live with. It didn't undo the competence. The welds were still true. The man had just been false.

"Flow rate's ugly," Juno said from the engineering station, her boots up on a console she'd never let anyone else put boots on. "But it's flowing. Ninety minutes to full."

"Ninety minutes we don't have," Mira said. She was at the sensor board, where she'd stationed herself more and more often since waking—as if thirty years of sleep had taught her that the universe only moved when you stopped watching it. She didn't look up. "I'm reading a drive signature at the edge of resolution. Coming around the moon."

Ansel crossed to her station. The display showed a smear of infrared against the moon's cold limb, resolving by degrees into something with the clean, aggressive lines of a Corvid hull. Not a patrol boat. A hunter—the long kind, built for one purpose, named for it. The transponder query came through before he'd finished reading the shape.

*CS Vantage, requesting manifest and flight plan.*

"They're hailing," Mira said unnecessarily.

"I can hear my own blood in my ears; I don't need the narration." He keyed the channel. "Depot traffic, this is freighter *Perihelion*, routine bunkering, bound for Halcyon with cryogenic cargo. Manifest follows." He sent the same forged manifest he'd been sending for three weeks, the one Oyelaran had helped him build, listing two hundred pods of colonists in transit under a charter that had expired before some of the woken people in his hold were born.

The reply took eleven seconds. Eleven seconds was a long time for an automated handshake.

"*Perihelion*, hold position. Corvid Security is conducting route integrity inspections. A boarding team is en route."

Juno was already off her console and moving. "That's not inspection language. Inspection language asks questions first. That's *we've already decided* language."

Ansel watched the drive signature sharpen. The *Vantage* was burning hard around the moon's terminator, no longer bothering to be subtle, and behind it—fainter, but there—a second smear. Two hunters did not conduct inspections. Two hunters conducted sieges.

"Oyelaran," he said over the ship-wide, "how fast can you close the fuel valves?"

"Faster than you can ask me twice. But Ansel—" the medic's voice came back level, which meant she'd already done the arithmetic, "—you'll be leaving with sixty percent tanks."

"How far to the debris field?"

"With sixty percent?" A pause. "Not all the way."

He looked at the tether.

Every old-route pilot knew about the Kestrel elevator. It had been the moon's pride once: a carbon ribbon running from the equatorial anchor up past synchronous orbit, hauling ore and passengers for forty years before the Wars cut its funding and its purpose both. When the colonies went dark, the operators had tried to deorbit it properly and failed halfway—too expensive, too complicated—and so the upper section had simply been abandoned, twenty thousand kilometers of supercarbon cable whipping slowly around the moon in a decaying spiral, shedding strands every year like a rope unraveling in slow motion. Charts marked it as a hazard. Every pilot Ansel respected treated it as one.

Every pilot Ansel respected had also told him, drunk, at least once, that if you ever needed to disappear from a sensor track, there was no better place in the Loop than inside the tether's wake. Twenty thousand kilometers of conductive ribbon played hell with active scanning. Debris along its length gave you radar clutter thick enough to hide a fleet. And the tether's own rotation—if you matched it, if you flew the spiral instead of fighting it—would carry you around the moon and out the far side while your pursuers were still trying to model the geometry.

It was also, by any honest measure, a way to die. The shed strands moved at orbital velocities. A filament of supercarbon a millimeter wide would go through a hull like light through glass. Pilots called flying the tether *threading the loom*, and they called it that because the metaphor ended in cloth or in corpses, nothing between.

"Ansel." Juno had followed his eyes to the nav display, where he'd pulled up the tether's decay model without quite deciding to. Her voice was careful. "You said you'd never fly that."

"I said I'd never fly it *for money*."

"That's not comforting."

"It's not supposed to be." He killed the fuel flow himself, hands steady in a way he distrusted. "Oyelaran, get everyone strapped down. Wake crew to stations. Tell the woken—" he hesitated. There were eleven of them now, plus the hundred and eighty-nine still frozen, and every maneuver he was about to pull would put g-forces through pods that had spent nine years being handled gently. "Tell them to brace and to trust me. And tell them I'm sorry in advance."

"What are you doing?" Mira asked.

"Threading the loom."

* * *

The *Vantage* hailed again as the *Perihelion* broke from the gantry, and this time the voice was human, unhurried, and pleasant in the way of someone reading from a script they'd used many times.

"Freighter *Perihelion*, you are directed to maintain position. Failure to comply will be construed as admission of carrying unregistered archival material, and appropriate measures will follow."

Ansel let the message play out and didn't answer. Silence was a choice, and he chose it, and hated how easily that came too—hated that lying and stonewalling and running had started to feel less like betrayals of who he was and more like skills he'd always had, waiting.

"He's going to fire across our bow," Juno said, strapped into the copilot's seat, hands hovering over the countermeasures board. "Standard escalation. Warning shot, then targeting lock, then—"

The warning shot came early. A railgun slug passed four hundred meters off the bow, invisible except as a flicker on the scope, and the *Vantage*'s targeting laser painted them a half-second later, hot and proprietary.

"They're not escalating," Mira said quietly. "They're skipping steps. They want us dead or boarded, and they've decided boarding is optional."

Ansel rolled the ship and burned for the moon.

The tether's lower anchor was a ruin on the equatorial plain, a fused stump of carbon and collapsed machinery, but the cable itself rose from it in a bright line that the dawn light caught and held, all the way up past the curve of the world. From this distance it looked delicate. Beautiful, even. He remembered his father—no. He remembered a pilot named Sefa Okoye, dead twelve years now, who'd flown the loom exactly once and lived, and who'd described it afterward with shaking hands and shining eyes: *You don't steer, Ansel. You surrender in the right direction.*

"Surrendering in the right direction," he said aloud, and pushed the throttle to a number the *Perihelion* hadn't seen since he'd bought her.

The ship groaned. Behind him, through the deck, he felt the hundred and eighty-nine pods take the acceleration, their suspension systems whining up into tolerance. Oyelaran's voice came over the ship-wide, calm as a metronome: "All sections braced. Pod frame stress at yellow. Hold it here, Captain, and no further."

"Holding."

The moon's horizon swung up to meet them. Atmosphere, thin and cold, began to whisper against the hull—not reentry, not yet, but the graze of it, the sky's edge. Ahead, the tether filled the forward viewport like a bridge into heaven, and the debris field around it resolved from haze into detail: shed filaments glinting, broken counterweight modules tumbling in their slow private orbits, a cargo climber dead thirty years and still climbing nowhere, its grip frozen on the ribbon.

"Active scan coming off the *Vantage*," Juno said. "They're painting the whole approach corridor. They know where we're headed."

"Of course they do. It's the only place to go." He fed power to the lateral thrusters and began the turn that would match the tether's rotation. "What they don't know is the phase."

"The phase?"

"Sefa's trick. The cable doesn't rotate uniformly—it's got a wave in it, a wobble from when they botched the deorbit. Every ninety-one seconds the debris density along our entry vector drops by half. If you cross the plane in the gap, you get a window." He watched the timer he'd built from the decay model, numbers falling. "If you miss the window, you get a ribbon through your reactor."

"And if the model's wrong?"

"Then I'll have been wrong about something for the last time."

Juno made a sound that was half laugh, half prayer. On the sensor board, Mira tracked the *Vantage*—it had stopped pretending, burning straight in, its sister ship swinging wide to cover the tether's far side. They knew the trick too, then. Or they knew enough to wait at the exit and let the loom do their work for them.

"Window in forty seconds," Ansel said. "Everybody breathe."

Nobody breathed.

* * *

Crossing the tether's plane was not like anything Ansel had words for, and he was a man who made his living with words, in the letters he wrote to families, in the logs he kept, in the promises he'd made. Later he would try to describe it to Oyelaran and fail. What happened was this: the world became a room full of needles moving at eight kilometers per second, and the *Perihelion* became a thread, and for four minutes and eleven seconds he ceased to be a person and became a function—input, correction, input, correction—his hands translating a decay model into survival at a rate no unassisted nervous system should manage, the autopilot screaming exceptions he overrode one after another because the machine saw the debris as obstacles and he needed it to see them as rhythm.

A filament hit them at 2:40 into the crossing.

He heard it before he understood it—a sound like a bell struck inside the ship's bones, high and wrong—and then the drive note changed, dropping a third, and the thrust balance indicator went red on the port cluster.

"We lost something," Juno called. "Port two's offline. Feed line's cut—I think the filament went through the injector manifold. I can't restore it from here."

"Can you reroute?"

"Give me ten minutes and a miracle. Right now I can give you asymmetric thrust and a bad attitude."

"Asymmetric thrust it is." He trimmed against the imbalance, feeling the ship pull left like a swimmer with a cramp, and flew on. The window was closing. Behind them, the *Vantage* had reached the tether's plane and stopped—hovering at a safe distance, its scan sweeping the debris in broad frustrated strokes, unable to follow without risking what Ansel was risking. They wouldn't come in. Hunters were expensive; hunters' crews were trained to preserve assets. Only a madman or a man with nothing left to lose flew the loom.

Ansel thought about the hundred and eighty-nine names in his hold and decided the category fit.

They came out of the far side of the debris field into clean dark, the moon falling away beneath them, the second hunter's position visible ahead and off to starboard—covering the textbook escape vector, exactly as predicted, and exactly where Ansel was not going. He burned low and slow along the moon's shadowline instead, running silent, letting momentum and darkness do what engines couldn't.

For six hours nobody spoke much. Juno crawled into the drive section with tools and a headlamp and swore steadily at the manifold. Oyelaran moved pod to pod checking frames and vitals. Mira sat beside Ansel in the cockpit, not navigating, not talking, just present, the way she'd been during the worst of her grieving—someone keeping watch with him because watch-keeping alone was the oldest loneliness there was.

At hour seven, Juno emerged gray-faced and grease-streaked.

"It's done. Sort of. Port two's isolated and bypassed. We've got seventy percent thrust on the remaining cluster, maybe eighty if I push it and don't mind shortening its life." She dropped into the copilot's seat. "But Ansel—that burn we did, the crossing, the trim corrections? We drew down the reserves harder than planned. And the drive's running hot on three injectors doing five injectors' work."

"How much does that cost us?"

She looked at him, and he saw her do the thing he'd seen Oyelaran do a dozen times since this began: the pause where compassion loses to honesty.

"It costs us the margin. You remember the wake-up math—power budget, thermal load, how many pods we could bring back safely?" She rubbed her face. "The budget just got smaller. I need Oyelaran to run it formally, but off the top of my head? Whatever number she gave you before, it's lower now. Meaningfully lower."

Ansel stared at the nav display, at the clean arc of their escape trajectory, at the debris field already shrinking behind them. They'd survived. He'd flown the loom and lived, and somewhere behind them the *Vantage* was still trying to model where they'd gone, and the Ledger's tugs were waiting at the rendezvous, and every part of the plan that involved not dying had worked.

And the price had come due in the one currency he couldn't earn back. Not fuel. Not hull. Margin—the difference between waking a hundred sleepers and waking fewer, between the arithmetic of mercy and something worse than arithmetic.

"Get Oyelaran," he said. "And Juno—when she runs the numbers, I want them exact. No rounding in our favor. These people have been rounded down their whole lives."

Juno nodded and went to fetch her.

Mira stayed. After a while she said, quietly, "You saved the ship."

"I spent part of it."

"That's what saving things costs." She looked out at the stars, at the long dark road to the rendezvous. "My mother used to say the bill always comes. You just choose which hand pays it."

Ansel watched the fuel gauge settle at fifty-eight percent and thought about the pods, and the names, and the vote they would all have to take again—because the numbers had changed, and the living would have to answer for it one more time.

Behind them, faint and patient, the *Vantage* began to move—circling wide around the moon, hunting the shadowline, following the only heading that made sense.

They weren't done being hunted. But first, they were going to have to count.
