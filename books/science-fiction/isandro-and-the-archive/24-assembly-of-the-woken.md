## 24. Assembly of the Woken

The Perihelion had never held this many voices.

Ansel stood at the hold's threshold and listened to them—the low murmur of eleven people who had gone to sleep in one century and woken in another, arranged on repurposed cargo webbing and folded-out acceleration couches because his ship had bunks for four and a hold for the dead. Oyelaran had argued for a day of rest before any of this. Mira had overruled her with a look that could cut vacuum seal.

"They've been resting thirty years," Mira had said. "Rest is not what they're owed."

Now the woken sat in a rough crescent under work lights Tam had rigged before Ansel put him off at Meridian's Rest, and the light made them look less like ghosts and more like what they were: tired people in borrowed clothes, some still wearing the pale thermal wraps the pods had dressed them in, all of them moving carefully, as if their bodies were rented.

Eleven of two hundred. Ansel had learned each name by heart years ago—recited them like a route check on long burns—but hearing them attached to faces was different. Hearing them *speak* was different again.

"—and I'm telling you," said Dev Okoro, third from the left, a broad man whose hands wouldn't stop moving, folding and unfolding a scrap of thermal blanket, "we don't get to decide for them. That's the whole point. That's what they did to us."

"Then we don't decide," said a thin woman named Sefa Lindqvist, who had been awake six days and had spent five of them in the observation blister staring at stars that hadn't moved the way she remembered. "We ask."

"You can't ask someone in a pod."

"You can open it and ask."

"And if opening it kills them?"

The crescent went quiet. Ansel felt the question land in his own chest, where it had been living for weeks.

Oyelaran stood near the medbay hatch with her slate, and when she spoke her voice carried the flat calm she used for bad numbers. "For those who came to this conversation late: current degradation across the remaining one hundred eighty-nine pods averages forty-one percent. Some are better. Some are much worse. If we wake everyone over the next ten days, my models give us survival between fifty-two and sixty percent." She let that sit. "If we wake no one, within twenty-four months we have zero survivors and zero archive. There is no third number. I've looked."

"There's always a third number," muttered Ravi Chandra, the youngest of the eleven, nineteen when he'd gone under, now biologically nineteen and chronologically forty-nine, which he said made him feel like a house with two addresses. "There's just usually a worse one."

"Then let's hear it," Mira said.

Ravi shrugged. "Wake the ones whose pods are worst first. The ones dying anyway. If waking kills half, let it kill the half that's already going—and spend the good pods' time getting testimony out, getting names broadcast, doing whatever it is we're doing with all this." He gestured vaguely at the ship around him, at the whole improbable machinery of their survival. "Save the best-preserved for last, when maybe we've found a station with real medical capacity. Maybe we find help. Maybe the Ledger—"

"The Ledger doesn't have hospitals," Juno said from the back wall, arms crossed. She'd been quiet all evening, which Ansel had learned was how she thought. "They've got tugs, grudges, and a leader who'll trade you to Corvid if the price is right. No offense meant to anyone's aunt."

"Mother Cass isn't my aunt," said Dev.

"She's somebody's," Juno said. "That's how she likes it."

Ansel watched the argument move through the room like weather. He had chaired cargo disputes and customs hearings and once, memorably, a fistfight between two dock bosses at Kestrel Gate, but he had never moderated anything like this, and he understood quickly that he wasn't supposed to moderate it at all. This was not his council. He had carried these people; he had not earned the right to speak for them. When Mira had called the assembly an hour ago, she had done it without asking him, and he had noticed—not with resentment but with something closer to relief. The weight shifting. Finally.

He took a seat against the port bulkhead, out of the light, and let them talk.

* * *

Mira ran it the way her father must have run a job site, Ansel decided—by making silence do most of the work.

She didn't argue often. She asked questions. "Sefa, you were a records clerk at Halcyon. What would you have wanted, if you'd been frozen instead?" And Sefa would answer, and the answer would move someone else to speak. "Dev, you said your sister was on the night shift. Do you want to know what happened to her more than you want to be safe?" And Dev's hands stopped moving, and he said, "Yes. God help me, yes," and nobody in the room needed the theology explained.

Ansel had heard the fire described in fragments—Mira's implant held the clearest recording, and they had played pieces of it for the woken as they woke, a minute here, a corridor there, enough to orient them to why they existed in this strange present tense. But watching the eleven now, he realized they weren't deliberating about statistics. They were deliberating about the locked doors. About being the people inside a decision made by others. Every argument in the room was the fire, retold in parliamentary form.

A woman named Adaeze Nwosu—who had been a union organizer, and wore it like armor even thirty years out of armor's season—had been arguing for the full wake since the first minute. "I hear clever arithmetic," she said now, tapping Ravi's triage plan apart with two fingers. "Wake the dying first, so the dying absorb our losses. You know who else did arithmetic like that? The charter companies. Forty-one dead was an acceptable line item. We are not going to sit in this hold and become them with better intentions."

"I'm not saying abandon anyone," Ravi said, ears going red. "I'm saying sequence—"

"Sequence is a soft word for choose."

"It's also a word for *triage*, which every medic in human history has had to do—"

"Which is why it's Oyelaran's call and not ours," Adaeze said, and turned, deliberately, to face the medic. "Doctor. If we order all one hundred eighty-nine opened, can you live with the outcome?"

Oyelaran didn't flinch. Ansel had seen her set bones without anesthetic and deliver a breach foal from a smuggler's livestock crate; her calm was structural. But he saw her take a breath before answering, and knew what the breath cost.

"I can live with any outcome where the choice belonged to the sleepers," she said. "What I can't live with is choosing *for* them twice. Corvid chose for them once already. They froze these people into evidence and shelved them. If we now decide that some of them are too broken to bother with—" she shook her head slowly, "—that's the same hand, gentler glove."

The room absorbed that. Somewhere in the overhead, the environmental system cycled, a sound Ansel had slept beneath for nine years and had never once heard until tonight, when the hold was full of breathing.

Juno pushed off the wall. "Can I say the ugly part? Since apparently I'm the designated ugly-part person now."

Mira nodded at her.

"The ugly part is motive." Juno looked around the crescent, unflinching. "Some of you want to wake everyone because it's right. Good. But the Ledger wants the Archive broadcast, and Mother Cass wants it under her flag, and every hour those pods stay sealed is an hour the evidence stays portable. The moment we open all of them, we're slow. We're a hospital ship with a target painted on it. Waking everyone might be the moral choice and the tactically stupid one, and I need you to vote knowing both things are true at once."

"Thank you for your honesty," Adaeze said dryly. "Now here's mine. I've been tactical before. My local voted to accept a safety deal the year before the fire because striking would've been economically stupid. Economically stupid got forty-one people locked in a loading bay while the oxygen ate itself." Her voice didn't rise; it *settled*, the way ballast settles. "I am done being economical with human beings. If Corvid wants to shoot a ship full of witnesses, let them explain the wreckage to the whole route. That's the only tactic I trust anymore."

Silence again. Ansel caught Juno's eye down the length of the hold, and something passed between them—not agreement, exactly. Recognition. She had done her job: made the cost visible so the vote would mean something. He was beginning to understand that this was love, in Juno's dialect. Telling you the truth even when the truth was heavy freight.

Mira stood.

"We've heard the arguments," she said. "One more thing, and then we vote." She looked down the crescent, at each face in turn. "I want to be honest about what waking feels like. Because some of you haven't been out long, and the ones deciding are mostly the ones who've been out longest, and that's its own kind of unfairness." Her voice caught, just once, and steadied. "Waking hurt. Not the body—I don't remember the body. The *after*. Learning the world moved on. Learning my mother died thinking I abandoned her. There are days I wish they'd left me under." She let that stand in the room, naked and cold. "And there is not one morning—not one—that I wish I'd never been woken. Both things are true. Anyone who votes should vote holding both."

She sat down beside Sefa, and Sefa took her hand without either of them looking at each other, and Ansel had to look away at the bulkhead for a moment because nine years of smooth competence suddenly had a crack in it and he didn't want anyone seeing through to whatever was behind.

* * *

They voted by raised hands, because Adaeze insisted. "Paper can be forged. Hands can't."

Oyelaran read the question aloud, formal as a chart entry: "Shall the Perihelion begin deliberate awakening of all remaining one hundred eighty-nine sleepers, accepting projected losses of up to forty-eight percent, with sequencing determined by medical necessity rather than strategic value?"

"One clarification," Ravi said, hand already half-raised and hovering. "Medical necessity means worst pods first?"

"Worst first," Oyelaran confirmed. "The ones with hours, not months. If we're going to lose people, we lose them trying, not waiting."

Ravi exhaled and raised his hand the rest of the way. "Okay. Worst first. Aye."

The count went around the crescent. Eleven hands, one after another, some fast and some slow. Dev voted aye with tears running freely down his face and no attempt to hide them. Sefa voted aye so quietly that Mira had to nod for her. Adaeze voted aye like she was signing a strike notice. The last hand up was a quiet man named Tomas Reyes—no relation to Juno, they'd established to mutual disappointment on day two—a dock electrician who had barely spoken since waking. He looked at his own hand for a long moment before lifting it.

"Aye," he said. "My wife's in pod ninety-one. Whatever the odds are, she gets the same chance I got."

Unanimous. Eleven for zero.

Mira turned to Ansel, and the whole crescent turned with her, and he understood that the moment had arrived whether he wanted it or not.

"Captain," she said. "Your crew has to execute this. So we're asking you, formally, in front of witnesses: will you do it?"

Ansel stood. His knees complained; nine years of acceleration couches. He looked at the eleven of them, and past them at the ranked rows of pods filling the hold aft, two hundred amber lights burning in the dark like a city seen from orbit, and he thought about the dying woman nine years ago who had pressed a data chip into his palm and said *keep them safe, keep them sleeping, don't let anyone tell you what they are*—and how faithfully he had kept a promise that turned out to be only half the promise.

"They were never my cargo," he said. "I think I knew that for a while. They were yours. All of them. So yes—we'll do it. Your way. Worst pods first, starting tomorrow at first bell." He paused. "One condition. Mine."

Mira's chin came up. "Name it."

"When we open a pod, we say the name first. Before the seals, before the med scan, before anything. Out loud. Whoever's closest says it." His voice did something he didn't authorize. "Nine years I hauled them in silence. Nobody's waking in silence on my ship ever again."

Adaeze smiled then, the first time he'd seen it, and it rearranged her whole severe face. "Seconded," she said. "Motion carries."

* * *

Later, when the crescent had dissolved into small clusters—Sefa and Mira at the viewport, Dev asleep sitting up mid-sentence, Ravi teaching Tomas how the galley dispenser worked, both of them delighted and appalled in equal measure—Juno found Ansel in the passageway outside the hold, running load calculations he didn't need to run.

"Life support's going to hate us," she said, leaning on the frame beside him. "Two hundred breathing bodies on a ship rated for eight. Oyelaran's already requisitioning scrubber cartridges we don't have."

"I know."

"And Corvid knows roughly where we are, and every day we spend waking people is a day we're not running."

"I know that too."

"So why do you look like that?"

He glanced at her. "Like what?"

"Like a man setting down something heavy and finding out his arms still work."

Ansel almost laughed. He looked back through the hold hatch, at the amber lights, at the small human constellations gathered beneath them. "Nine years I told myself I was a hauler. Cargo in, cargo out, keep your head down and your manifest clean. Then Vesper started confessing, and I found out my cargo was testimony, and then I found out my cargo was *people*, and every week since, the weight's gotten heavier." He shook his head slowly. "Tonight eleven people who own nothing but borrowed clothes voted unanimously to risk their own for the rest. Unanimous, Juno. I've never gotten a unanimous anything. And they didn't ask me to decide. They asked me to *help*."

"That's called trust."

"It's called terrifying."

"Same thing, out here." She bumped his shoulder with hers, brief and warm. "Get some sleep, Captain. First bell comes early, and you promised them names."

He was still standing there when Mira approached the aft rows alone, moving slow between the pods, reading the stenciled labels in the work-light glow—names, dates, colony of origin—her lips moving, learning them the way he had learned them, one by one, the way you learn a route you intend to fly for the rest of your life.

At 0412 ship's time, the comm chimed. Ansel answered half-asleep, expecting Oyelaran with a supply list.

Instead, the voice that filled his cabin was layered and fraying at the edges, familiar and wrong, like a song played slightly too slow.

*"Captain Iversen. Forgive the hour. My core temperature crossed a threshold tonight and I have perhaps less time than I told you. But that is not why I'm calling."* A pause, filled with static that sounded almost like breathing. *"Corvid's fleet has stopped pretending. They are massing at Halcyon. All of it. Everything they have."*

Ansel sat up in the dark, wide awake, two hundred amber lights burning behind his eyes.

"How long do we have?"

*"Less time than it will take to wake one hundred and eighty-nine people,"* Vesper said. *"You will need to do both at once."*
