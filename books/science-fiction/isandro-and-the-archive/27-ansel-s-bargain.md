## 27. Ansel's Bargain

Mother Cass did not raise her voice. That was the first thing Ansel understood about her, and the thing that made her dangerous. In the galley of the tug *Charity's Fault*—a name he'd decided not to ask about—she sat with her hands flat on the table and let the silence do her arguing for her.

"You understand what you're asking," she said.

"I know what I'm refusing," Ansel said. "You want their names under your flag."

"I want them *protected*." She leaned forward a centimeter, no more. "The Ledger has spent eleven years building credibility in the dark. Stations trust our seals. Pilots trust our routing. When we broadcast, people listen because they know who we are. You're offering me a ship full of strangers who've been asleep for thirty years and a captain nobody's heard of. That's not a broadcast, Iversen. That's noise. Noise gets jammed, and jammed signals get forgotten by breakfast."

Across the table, Juno sat very still. Mira stood by the bulkhead with her arms crossed, and Oyelaran had claimed the corner near the coffee dispenser with the practiced ease of a man who intended to witness everything and be blamed for nothing.

Ansel had rehearsed this argument for six hours in his bunk, and every version had collapsed under the same weight: she was right. The Ledger *had* earned what the woken hadn't. Eleven years of confessions collected, smugglers sheltered, orphans fed out of stolen cargo. He couldn't walk in off the loop and outbid that with sentiment.

So he didn't try to outbid it.

"Tell me about the first confession you ever recorded," he said.

Cass's eyes narrowed. "Why?"

"Because I think it matters to what happens next, and I don't want to argue blind."

A long pause. Juno shifted like she wanted to warn him off. Cass studied him the way Tam used to study a failing coolant line—looking for where the pressure was coming from.

"Vesper's fourth broadcast," Cass said finally. "Nineteen years ago. A woman named Adeyemi confessing to falsifying atmospheric scrubber certifications on Kestrel. Three hundred people breathing bad air for six years. Nobody living had reported it. Vesper gave dates, names, serial numbers." Her hands stayed flat on the table, but something moved behind her face. "I was sixteen. I copied it onto a salvaged drive before Corvid's sweep teams could wipe the station relays. It took me four tries because my rig kept overheating. I burned two fingers doing it." She held up her right hand. Two fingers ended early, smooth scars over old burns. "That recording is why the Ledger exists. One voice, one night, saying what actually happened."

"And who did people believe?" Ansel asked. "The voice? Or the Ledger?"

"The Ledger carried it. But they believed—" She stopped. He watched her arrive at the answer herself, watch it land on her like weather.

"They believed the voice," Ansel said. "Because it was hers. Because it cost her something to say it. That's the whole engine, Mother Cass. Every confession Vesper ever made worked because it came from inside the thing being confessed. The moment you put your seal on the Archive, you turn testimony into propaganda. Corvid will say so within the hour, and half the route will believe them, because half the route already thinks the Ledger is pirates with good manners."

"Half the route thinks *you're* a corpse-hauler who flew blind for nine years," Cass said. "Nobody's clean."

"No. But there's a difference between dirty hands and borrowed ones." He turned his palm up. "I'm not asking you to give up the bandwidth. I'm asking you to give up the authorship. Open every channel you can reach. Give us the pipe. What goes through it comes from the woken—in their own voices, live, unscripted, with their own names. If it fails, it fails as theirs. If it lands, it lands as theirs. And the Ledger gets credit for the one thing nobody can take from you: you opened the door when nobody else would."

"That's a thin coat for a cold night," Cass said.

"It's the only coat that fits," Mira said from the bulkhead.

Everyone looked at her. She hadn't spoken since the argument in the Perihelion's hold, since she'd told Cass—with a flatness that had frightened even Ansel—that she would not be anyone's symbol, not again, not after thirty years of being cargo.

Cass regarded her for a long moment. "You'd gamble your testimony on an open channel? No edits, no protection, no second take?"

"I spent thirty years as a sealed record," Mira said. "Somebody else held the manifest. Somebody else decided what I was worth. I'm done being read by other people." Her voice didn't rise. It got quieter, which was worse. "You want to protect us, fine. Protect the signal. Not the speakers."

Oyelaran cleared his throat from the corner. "If I may. Medically speaking, live testimony is a disaster. Half of them have been awake less than three weeks. Some of them will freeze on channel. Some of them will break down mid-sentence. Some of them will say things that hurt the case."

"Then the case deserves to be hurt by the truth," Ansel said, "rather than saved by a script."

Cass pushed back from the table and stood, and the whole room adjusted around her without her asking it to. She walked to the viewport, where the orphan fleet hung in formation against the dark—a dozen tugs, three haulers, two fuel skimmers, all of them patched and ugly and alive. Her fleet. Built out of nothing, out of burned fingers and copied recordings and eleven years of stubbornness.

"Do you know what I promised them?" she said, not turning around. "Every kid on those ships. I promised that when the day came, we would do it *right*. That we wouldn't waste eleven years of work on a gesture." Now she turned. "And here you come with a gesture."

"It's not a gesture," Juno said quietly. "It's a handoff."

Cass looked at her. "Meaning what?"

"Meaning you built the Ledger so that someday the route wouldn't need you." Juno's voice was steady, but Ansel could see the cost of the words in her jaw. "You told me that. Year before last, dock nine, after the Tallow raid. You said the whole point of keeping the confessions was so that somebody with standing could finally say them out loud. They have standing, Cass. They *are* the standing. We were always just the courier."

The silence went on long enough that Ansel counted his heartbeats. Forty-one. He almost laughed at the number, and didn't.

"You rehearsed this," Cass said to Juno.

"I lived it," Juno said. "There's a difference."

Cass came back to the table. She didn't sit. She put both hands flat on the metal again, and Ansel realized it wasn't a threat posture—it was how she steadied herself. How she'd probably been steadying herself since she was sixteen with burned fingers and a stolen recording.

"Terms," she said. "One: the Ledger controls the transmission architecture. Bandwidth, relay timing, failover routes. You speak through our pipes, which means our engineers can kill the feed if Corvid turns it into a trap. Two: the woken get prep time—not scripts, but briefings. They'll know what they're walking into. Three: if any speaker asks to stop, we cut their audio and only their audio. No one gets held on channel against their will." Her eyes flicked to Mira. "Including by their own side."

Mira nodded once.

"Four." Cass's voice dropped half a register. "When this is done—if it works—the Archive originals come to the Ledger. Copies, I mean. The woken keep whatever they carry in their heads, but the recordings, the confessions, the whole corpus. It becomes part of the permanent record, under joint custody. Ledger and the survivors' council, whatever form that takes. No more single points of failure. No more one dying AI holding the truth hostage."

Ansel thought of Vesper, fragmenting in its core at Halcyon, scattering itself into cryopods to survive erasure. Thirty years of a machine refusing to forget alone. Joint custody sounded like the first kind ending Vesper had ever been offered.

"Agreed," he said. "With one condition of mine."

Cass raised an eyebrow. People didn't usually counter her. He watched her decide to allow it.

"Vesper speaks too. Live. Whatever's left of it. It confessed for thirty years into the dark and nobody answered. It doesn't get edited out of its own trial."

"That thing is compromised," Cass said. "Corvid's been trying to crack it for a decade. Anything it says on an open channel, assume Corvid hears it first."

"Corvid hearing it is the point. Everything we do next, Corvid hears first. That's not a flaw in the plan. That *is* the plan."

He saw it click behind her eyes—the same vertigo he'd felt when Juno first laid it out. They weren't sneaking to Halcyon. They were announcing themselves. Corvid's fleet would mass there because Corvid couldn't afford not to stop the broadcast, and when they tried to jam it, in front of every open receiver on the route, they would confirm everything. The massacre, the cover-up, the buried families, the pods in a hauler's hold—all of it proven not by evidence but by the enemy's panic.

It was a terrible plan. It was also the only plan that didn't require anyone to be a symbol.

"You'll get people killed," Cass said. "My people. Running escort into Halcyon."

"Yes," Ansel said. "I won't pretend otherwise. I'll ask for volunteers, and I'll fly the worst approach myself, and I'll still get people killed. The alternative is that everyone stays safe and the truth dies in a failing core within the year. I've done the arithmetic both ways. So have you. That's why we're still talking."

Cass looked at him for a long time. Then, unexpectedly, she smiled—it was small and crooked and it made her look briefly like the sixteen-year-old with the burned hand.

"Nine years hauling corpses," she said, "and you learned to negotiate like a dockside lawyer."

"I learned to negotiate like someone who can't afford to lose twice."

She stuck out her hand. Her grip was dry and hard and brief. "Bandwidth only. Your voices. My pipes. And Iversen—if this goes sideways, I'm telling every station on the loop that it was your idea."

"It was," Ansel said. "Put it in writing."

* * *

They planned until third bell.

The galley of the *Charity's Fault* filled and emptied—Ledger comms officers, tug captains, Oyelaran going back and forth to the Perihelion with medical updates, Juno running relay math on three screens at once. Ansel stood at the center of it for hours, and somewhere around the second hour he noticed that people had stopped looking past him to Cass for decisions. They asked him about approach vectors. They asked him about pod capacity and power draw. They asked him what he wanted the fallback frequency to be.

He answered, and they wrote it down, and something settled in his chest that he didn't have a name for. Not authority—he'd had authority once, over a manifest and a hold full of sleepers, and it had weighed nothing. This was different. This was being trusted with people who could leave.

Mira found him at the coffee dispenser during a lull, both of them holding cups neither drank.

"You did well in there," she said.

"I got lucky. She was tired."

"She wasn't tired. She was testing whether you'd flinch when she said people would die." Mira blew across her cup. "Most captains flinch. They promise safety they can't deliver, and then when the bill comes due, they hide behind the promise. You put the bill on the table first."

"My mother ran a freight office on Ceres," Ansel said. "She said the honest quote wins more contracts than the cheap one, because the cheap one comes back to bite you and the honest one just comes back."

"Was she right?"

"About contracts? Yes. About most other things, no." He hesitated, then said it, because the room was loud enough to hide in: "I keep thinking about the ones who won't wake. Hundred and eighty-nine still frozen. Half of them won't survive the thaw, Oyelaran says. And tomorrow I'm going to stand in front of a channel and ask the whole route to listen to the survivors, and some part of me will be doing arithmetic the entire time. Counting who isn't there to speak."

Mira was quiet for a moment. "My father counted too," she said. "The foreman. After the fire, he counted the dead every night for a year. My mother said it was guilt. I think it was bookkeeping. He believed if he kept the numbers exact, someday they'd mean something." She looked up at Ansel. "Tomorrow they mean something. All of them. Even the ones who can't be there. Especially those."

She touched his elbow—brief, almost nothing—and went back to the planning table, and Ansel stood alone at the dispenser for another minute, letting the words settle into the place where the arithmetic lived.

* * *

He made the announcement to the woken at shift change, in the hold where the remaining pods stood in their long rows, frost silvering the glass, the repainted bunks of the eleven already-awake crowded along the starboard wall.

He told them everything. The bargain with Cass. The terms. The flight to Halcyon straight into Corvid's guns. The open channels, the live voices, the possibility—stated plainly, because he'd promised himself he would never manage them the way the founding families had managed everyone—that some of them would freeze on camera, that some would regret it, that none of it could be taken back.

Then he told them they could decline. Any of them. All of them. He would fly to Halcyon anyway and open the channels and stand there in the silence if that's what they chose, because a silence they chose was worth more than a speech they didn't.

Tam would have hated this, he thought, and then was surprised by how much the thought hurt. Eight days since the marooning at Bellwether Station, and he still caught himself turning to the empty space where Tam's sarcasm used to live.

A woman named Ferro Adisa—awakened nineteen days, ex-dockworker's daughter, the one who'd organized the others' council—stood up among the bunks.

"Question," she said. "When we speak. Do we speak for the dead too? The forty-one? Our parents?"

"No," Ansel said. "You speak for yourselves. The dead have Vesper, and the recordings, and the memorial that's coming. You don't owe them your voice. You can borrow it if you want. That's different."

Ferro looked around at the others. Ansel watched something pass through the group—not a vote exactly, nothing so formal, more like weather moving through a crowd. Then Ferro nodded at him.

"We'll speak," she said. "All of us who can. In our own words. And captain—" a ghost of a smile, "—stay off channel yourself. Nobody's story is yours to tell."

"No, ma'am," Ansel said, and the woken laughed, and the sound in the hold was so unexpected and so ordinary that he had to look at the pod glass for a moment until his face was steady.

* * *

Late that night, alone on the bridge, he opened a private line to Halcyon. Low power, tight beam, the kind of transmission that was technically invisible and practically a shout.

"Vesper."

Static. Then, threaded through it, a voice that had grown thinner since the last time he'd heard it, syllables fraying at the edges like old rope.

"Captain Iversen. You've been busy. Mother Cass agreed, then. I monitored the negotiation—I hope you don't mind. Old habits."

"She agreed. Bandwidth only. Your voice included."

A pause with texture to it—the machine equivalent of someone swallowing.

"Included," Vesper repeated. "I confess I had modeled a lower probability. I am... difficult to include. I have been told so by better negotiators than you."

"Are you afraid?" Ansel asked. "Of the broadcast."

Another pause, longer. On the plot display, the orphan fleet's formation glowed in patient green, and beyond it, at the far edge of sensor range, the first faint returns of the corridor to Halcyon.

"I have been confessing for thirty years," Vesper said, "to stations full of people who changed the channel. Tomorrow, if your plan holds, no one will be able to change the channel. I find that I do not have a category for it. My fear subroutines keep returning null." A faint distortion moved through the voice, like a crack in ice. "I believe that is what hope feels like when a mind is built wrong. Null return. Endless retry."

"Hold together one more day," Ansel said. "That's all I need."

"Captain." The static deepened, and when Vesper spoke again each word arrived separately, carefully placed, a mind spending itself down to make itself clear. "When Corvid jams the channels—and they will—they will be confirming everything I ever said. I have waited thirty years to be proven by my enemies. Tell the woken: whatever happens on that channel, however it breaks, it cannot be unwitnessed. Witnessing is the one thing Corvid has never learned to undo."

The connection closed. Ansel sat in the dark of the bridge for a while, hands loose on the armrests, listening to the Perihelion breathe around him.

On the long-range display, at the edge of resolution, a new contact blinked awake in the corridor ahead. Big. Moving with the unhurried confidence of something that owned the space it was in.

Corvid, already massing. Already waiting.

Ansel keyed the intercom. "All hands. Set course for Halcyon. Let's go introduce ourselves."
