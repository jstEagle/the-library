## 40. Epilogue: The Keeper of Names

The girl's name was Sefa Okonjo-Iversen, and she was eleven years old the first time she asked him about the wall.

She had been born aboard the *Perihelion* — the third child to carry that distinction, after her brother and a boy named Dov Reyes-Vasquez who had since gone planetside to study engines on Halcyon. She had grown up with the hold's painted bunks and the galley's squealing door and the observation blister's cracked sealant patch that nobody ever fixed because Tam had touched it last and they didn't say his name in front of new crew. She had grown up, in other words, inside the story without ever being told it whole.

The wall stood at the aft end of what used to be the cargo hold. Two hundred names, hand-painted in white on the grey composite, arranged not alphabetically but by pod number, because Mira had insisted — *they were numbers for thirty years, they don't get to be alphabetical now, they get to be exactly what they were.* Below each name, smaller, in Juno's steadier hand: a date of waking or a date of failure. A handful of names had a small circle beside them instead of a date, which meant the person had walked off the ship alive and chosen not to come back, and that too was honored, because leaving was also a kind of testimony.

Sefa stood in front of it one watch-cycle while the ship rode the long quiet between Kestrel Gate and Halcyon, and she said, "Captain Iversen. Why do we keep these?"

Ansel was fifty-eight years old and his hands ached in cold weather and he had stopped correcting children who used his title like it was real. He set down the cargo strap he was re-racking — some things never changed; the *Perihelion* still hauled freight, though now it was mail and seed stock and passengers who slept in bunks, not pods — and he looked at the wall for a while before answering.

"Sit down," he said. "It takes a while."

* * *

He started where he always started, because starting anywhere else felt like lying.

"Nine years before you were born, this hold held two hundred cryopods. People paid good money to be frozen and shipped to colonies that had already closed their gates. The companies knew. The families paying didn't. And the man flying the ship — me, back when my knees worked — didn't know either. He thought he was a courier. Turns out he was a librarian who never read the books."

"Vesper," Sefa said. She knew fragments. Everyone aboard knew fragments; Vesper's surviving shards lived in the ship's comm array and told terrible jokes at second bell.

"Vesper," Ansel agreed. "A station intelligence that watched a massacre happen and was ordered to forget it. So instead it hid the memory — broke itself into pieces and scattered them into the sleepers' implants, one confession at a time, for thirty years. My hold wasn't cargo. It was a witness box. Two hundred witnesses, asleep."

"And the fire?"

"The fire came first." He told it plainly, the way he'd learned to. Forty-one dockworkers at Waystation Halcyon, locked behind doors that were ordered sealed, burned so that a safety inspection would die with them and a charter company could dissolve itself clean. The founding families buried it. Corvid Security buried the burying. For thirty years the only mourner on the entire route was a machine having a nervous breakdown in public, one confession per quarter, broadcast to stations full of people who thought their AI was malfunctioning.

"It wasn't malfunctioning," Sefa said. It wasn't a question. Children raised by the Ledger's grandchildren learned early that machines could grieve.

"No. It was testifying. Slowly. In the only courtroom it had."

He told her about Juno, then — how a salvage tech with grease under her nails and a ledger of confessions hidden in decommissioned sections had warned him that the last person to ask questions had vanished. How she'd come aboard as a friend and an agent both, and confessed it, and how the confession had made them closer, not further apart, because that was the whole lesson of those years: truth costs something up front and pays forever after.

He told her about Tam, and he did not soften it. "He sabotaged the wake-up systems because he couldn't stand the arithmetic. Half of them might die if we woke them. All of them would die if we didn't. He wanted the choice taken out of our hands." Ansel had marooned him at Ceres Fork with a month's pay and no references, and Tam had later sent a message that said only *you were right and I hate it*, and Ansel had kept it, and that was all anyone needed to know about mercy.

He told her about the vote — eleven woken sleepers choosing to wake the rest, knowing the cost. About the long days of the Long Wake, names read aloud over the intercom whether the pod opened onto a living face or a still one. About Mira Okonjo-Vasquez, foreman's daughter, walking out of thirty years of ice with her father's last recording in her skull and rage enough to power the drive.

"She's your mother," Sefa said. Not a question either. The girl had done her arithmetic.

"She is." Ansel let that sit. Some parts of the story he told to everyone, and some parts he kept for the wall, and the part where a grieving woman and a guilty courier had found each other in an observation blister over a wreck field — saying nothing right, saying nothing at all — that part belonged to the two of them and to the silence they'd built a life inside.

* * *

"So we ran the blockade," he went on, "and we flew into the trap at Halcyon on purpose, and the Ledger's tugs opened a corridor with their hulls. Good people died so a signal could live. And then we broadcast everything. Every confession, every recording, every name spoken in its own voice. Corvid jammed us, and jamming a confession is just confessing louder. By the time we docked, three charter companies had dissolved themselves — properly, this time, with reparations — and the colonies opened their gates."

"And justice?"

"That's the part people always want neat." He tapped the wall. "There was no court. No verdict. No gavel. What happened instead was that forty thousand people along the route watched the broadcast and simply stopped doing business with the guilty. Contracts lapsed. Fleets got sold for scrap. One director died poor and alone in a rented room on a station whose doors wouldn't open for him. That's not a courtroom. But I've sat in courtrooms, and I'll tell you — sometimes the docket closes and nothing has actually changed. This way, everything changed, and there was no docket at all."

Sefa frowned, eleven years old and unconvinced. "So the bad guys just... lost money."

"They lost the route," Ansel said. "And the route was the only world they had. Meanwhile the ones they hurt got their names back." He gestured at the wall. "That's the trade. That's what this is. Not a trophy. A return of property."

* * *

He walked her down the line of names, the way he did once a year on the anniversary of the broadcast, and gave her the ones he always gave.

"Mira's mother, Adaeze — pod 114, woke day six, first one to hug me, which I did not deserve. Pod 31, Elio Brandt, woke day nine, walked off at Halcyon and started the memorial fund. Pod 87—" his voice did the thing it always did, the small hitch he'd never sanded smooth, "—pod 87 was Tomas Reyes. Juno's father. He woke on day four and got nine months with his daughter before his heart finished what the ice had started. Juno says it was nine months more than the companies ever intended to give anyone, and she's right, and it still isn't fair, and both of those are true at once. You'll want to pick one. Don't."

"Pod 12," Sefa read. "No date. Just a circle."

"Selene Marchetti. Woke blind in one eye, deaf in one ear, furious in both. Took her settlement money and bought a tug and flew relief runs during the Kestrel famine. Sent us a card every year until she didn't. Leaving is allowed here. Coming back is allowed too. She came back twice."

"And the ones with the failure dates?" Sefa's finger hovered over a cluster of them — day three, day five, day seven — the hard arithmetic made visible, ninety-one names that never drew breath again.

Ansel crouched down to her level, knees cracking like docking clamps.

"Those are the ones who trusted us with their lives and lost the bet," he said. "We knew the odds. They voted anyway — the ones who were awake voted for the ones who weren't, which is the heaviest thing a person can carry on someone else's behalf. We read every name aloud. We keep every name up here. Because the alternative to remembering them isn't peace. It's just the fire again, from the other direction — doors closing, records sealed, everyone agreeing to move on. Moving on is what the founding families called it. We call it something else."

"What?"

"Keeping the books."

* * *

Second bell rang through the deck, and Sefa glanced toward the galley, torn between hunger and history. Ansel recognized the expression; he'd worn it for nine years in a quieter key.

"Go eat," he said. "But take this with you." He pressed his old manifest tablet into her hands — the original, the one with two hundred passenger names that had turned out to be two hundred next-of-kin notifications waiting to happen. "First assignment. Learn ten names tonight. Tomorrow, ten more. In three weeks you'll have the wall by heart, and then you'll be crew the way that counts — not hired, inherited."

She hugged him, quick and fierce the way children do when they've been given something heavier than they can yet lift, and ran for the galley, tablet clutched to her chest.

Ansel stayed a moment longer. From the comm array overhead, a voice like dry paper and warm static said, "You tell it better every year, Captain."

"You were there for most of it, Vesper."

"I was there for all of it. I just wasn't *all there* for any of it." A pause, the machine's idea of a shrug. "The girl will do. She asked why before she asked what. That's the correct order."

From the corridor, Mira's voice: "Ansel? Juno says if you're going to make the girl memorize the wall, the least you can do is fix the blister seal before we hit Halcyon atmosphere."

"I'm retired," he called back, already reaching for the sealant.

"You're a captain," she said, appearing in the hatchway with grey in her hair and her mother's stubbornness in her spine. "Captains don't retire. They just change cargo."

He looked past her, down the length of the hold — bunks where pods stood, a table where the wake-up rig had been, children's drawings taped above the wall of names, mail sacks bound for five open colonies stacked and waiting. Somewhere ahead, Halcyon's lights were turning on for evening shift, and Vesper's oldest fragment was already composing its quarterly report, which was no longer a confession, just a letter home.

"Change of heading," Ansel said, and keyed the intercom. "All hands, this is Iversen. First bell in twenty minutes. New route papers are stamped, the Loop's open gate to gate, and we're carrying mail, passengers, and one very old ghost who complains about the coffee. Let's go be ordinary for a while. We've earned it."

The *Perihelion* turned her bow toward the light of the inner stations, and the wall of names rode quietly at her back — not a grave, not a monument, but a hand closed around two hundred stories, carried forward by everyone who had ever been told them whole.
