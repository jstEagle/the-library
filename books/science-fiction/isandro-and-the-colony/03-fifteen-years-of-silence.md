## 3. Fifteen Years of Silence

Captain Adaeze Oyelaran had commanded the *Meridian* for eleven years, and in all that time she had never once been asked to justify her height. Now she stood on a crate in the cargo bay of Ceres Station, nine years old and forty-one inside, addressing a terraformer who kept looking at her hands.

"Stop that," she said.

Edmund Ravensworth blinked. "Sorry, Captain."

"You're not sorry. You're recalibrating." She hopped down from the crate — undignified, necessary; her legs were half their former length and she refused to pretend otherwise — and walked the length of the bay with her arms folded behind her back, because that was how she thought best, and her body still remembered it even if her knees ached differently now. "You looked at our drive signature when we docked. You looked at the hull scarring. And then you said the word *classified* like a man swallowing glass. So either you tell me what you know, or I put you off my ship."

"It's your ship," Edmund said carefully, "that's docked at my station."

"Everything is negotiable in contested orbit. Talk."

He talked. He told her about his military years — six of them, before he'd walked away from a supply posting above Ganymede and let the record show him dead in a docking accident. He told her about a research division whose funding lines he'd audited by accident, a division with no name and no port of call, drawing power enough to run a small moon. He told her he'd seen the word *temporal* exactly once, in a requisition for exotic-matter containment, and that he'd spent fifteen years telling himself he'd misread it.

"And now you're standing here," Oyelaran said, "aged backward by eleven years, and you've stopped telling yourself that."

"I stopped the moment I saw you." He rubbed his face. In the low light of the bay he looked older than his records claimed, which was its own kind of evidence. "Captain, what happened to you out there?"

She had been waiting for someone to ask. She had rehearsed the answer for the whole of the return voyage, four months alone in the dark with a crew of children who remembered being adults, and now that the moment came she found the rehearsal useless. Some things could only be said forward.

"Sit down," she said. "It's a long story, and I'm going to tell it standing up anyway, because if I sit I'll feel nine, and right now I need to feel like a captain."

* * *

They had been fourteen months into the survey of the Kuiper fringe when the *Meridian*'s long-range array caught something impossible: a distress beacon broadcasting on a naval frequency that hadn't been issued yet. Not an old code. A *future* one. Chief Okonkwo — now a gangly twelve-year-old who wouldn't stop fidgeting — had spent three days proving the signal wasn't spoofed before Oyelaran authorized a course change.

The station hung in the dark beyond Sedna's aphelion like a splinter of bone. No registry. No running lights except a maintenance flicker. Docking clamps designed for ships twice the *Meridian*'s class.

"We went in expecting survivors," Oyelaran told Edmund. "We found laboratories."

The station's logs were intact. Its people were gone — evacuated, or worse; they found containment cells with restraint harnesses sized for human bodies and scorch marks that suggested the restraints had been needed. The research was elegant and obscene in equal measure: temporal displacement fields, tuned to wrap around a target settlement and drag it decades out of phase with the rest of the system. Not a bomb. A siege weapon. You didn't destroy a colony that defied Earth; you sealed it behind a wall of years, let it watch the universe age without it, let its children grow old while their grandparents' war went on without them. The tests had been conducted on uninhabited rocks first. Then, quietly, in the footnotes of logistics reports, on rocks that weren't uninhabited at all.

"The outer colonies think those settlements died of reactor failures," Oyelaran said. Her voice stayed level. It cost her. "Three of them. Four thousand people, give or take. My grandmother was born on one of them."

Edmund said nothing. His hands had gone very still in his lap, and she understood that stillness — it was the stillness of a man holding something down.

"The navy will deny it," he said finally.

"The navy already denied it. That's what the future-dated codes were for — a cover program, so any inquiry would conclude the station was a fabrication." She let out a breath. "I ordered the archive copied. All of it. Every log, every test result, every name. Okonkwo cracked their encryption in nineteen hours while I sat in the captain's chair doing arithmetic — how fast could we run, how far could we get, how many of my crew would I spend to carry this home. Then their automated defenses woke up, and the arithmetic stopped mattering."

"What happened?"

"They fired the weapon." For the first time her composure slipped, just a millimeter, the way ice slips before it becomes water. "Not at us. At the station. They collapsed the whole facility back through its own timeline — thirty years of displacement in four seconds. We watched it happen on sensors. One moment it existed. The next it was a ruin that had been a ruin since before I was born." She looked down at her own small hands. "The next morning I was eight. By the time we cleared the fringe, I was nine. The field caught our stern as we ran. It's decaying — the doctors can probably stop it, maybe reverse some of it, but the point is they did this to us to keep us quiet, and we are the only living proof that any of it happened."

The bay was silent except for the hum of Ceres Station breathing around them.

"So you ran home," Edmund said.

"I ran home," she agreed. "And now I'm looking at a man who also ran, fifteen years ago, and I want to know where."

* * *

He didn't answer at first. He got up instead, crossed to the viewport, and stood looking out at the belt — the slow wheeling scatter of rock and ice, the distant patient sun. When he finally spoke, it was to the glass.

"Ganymede. Supply command, third fleet. I processed freight manifests. It was the dullest posting in the fleet, which is why nobody noticed when I started reading past the columns." A dry almost-laugh. "I found a shipping lane that didn't exist. Tonnage moving to coordinates with no destination, funded by budget lines with no oversight. I flagged it. My commanding officer told me to lose the flag. I lost it. And then I couldn't sleep, so I went back and un-lost it, and this time I copied everything before I filed it again."

"That's desertion with aggravating factors," Oyelaran said.

"That's what the tribunal would have called it, yes." He turned. In the starlight his face was hard to read, but his voice wasn't. "I didn't plan it. I want to be honest with you about that, Captain, because you're going to have to decide whether to trust me, and I'd rather you decide based on the truth. I planned nothing. I loaded my hauler with medical supplies bound for a colonial outpost that the war had cut off — supplies Earth had impounded, civilians starving two jumps away, and every regulation said let them starve. I flew them there instead. And when I came back to face the music, I found out the music included a quiet order to disappear me, because I'd seen too many numbers. So I disappeared myself first. Faked my death. Started hauling cargo under a dead man's name."

"Fifteen years."

"Fifteen years." He nodded toward the corridor beyond the bay. "There are two hundred people on this station who did roughly what I did. Deserters, refugees, families that fled the conscription sweeps. They've spent fifteen years being no one, and it worked, and it kept them alive. Your ship just ended that."

Oyelaran studied him. Here was the thing she had learned about command, the thing no academy taught: everyone aboard was carrying something, and the captain's real job was knowing what each person carried and never making them set it down before they chose to. This man had been carrying his guilt for fifteen years, and he had just set it down in front of a stranger, and that told her more than any service record could.

"You could have lied to me," she said.

"You'd have known."

"Yes. But most people lie anyway." She climbed back onto her crate, because her legs were tired and pride was a luxury she was learning to ration. "Here's my problem, Ravensworth. I have an archive that proves Earth murdered four thousand colonists and maimed my crew, and I have nowhere safe to put it. Every port in the inner system is Earth's. Every colonial authority is compromised or terrified. And you're telling me there's a hidden station full of fugitives two hundred meters from where I'm standing."

"I'm telling you there's a family," Edmund said. "It's messier than a station."

"Families usually are." She was quiet a moment. Out the viewport, the belt turned, indifferent and ancient. "When I ordered the copy made, Okonkwo asked me — he was thirty-four then, remember — he asked me what I thought the evidence would do. Whether anyone would act on it. And I told him the truth, which is that I didn't know. I told him we weren't carrying justice. We were carrying a mirror, and mirrors don't do anything but show people what they are. What happens next depends on who looks."

"And if the people who look are cowards?"

"Then we find better people." She met his eyes. "You deserted to feed starving strangers. I stole a war crime from the people who committed it. Neither of us ever expected company." She extended her hand — small, child-sized, steady. "So. Do we wake up your family, Mr. Ravensworth? Because I promise you, mine is already awake, and they're scared, and scared people with proof are dangerous to everyone including themselves."

Edmund looked at her hand for a long moment. Whatever he saw in it — the eleven missing years, the grandmother born on a dead colony, the arithmetic of lives weighed against a truth — he took it.

"There's something you should understand first," he said. "About the people out there. They didn't hide to make a point. They hid to survive. And the thing about people who've survived by staying invisible—" He exhaled slowly. "Some of them are going to want to burn that archive rather than be seen."

"Then I suppose," Oyelaran said, "we'd better go convince them otherwise. Take me to your family, Mr. Ravensworth. Let's see how much of it survives the morning."
