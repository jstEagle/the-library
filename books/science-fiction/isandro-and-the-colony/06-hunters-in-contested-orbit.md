## 6. Hunters in Contested Orbit

The word came down from the listening post at 0400 station time, and by 0415 Edmund was standing in the cold dark of the colony's comm shack, watching two fleets wake up on opposite sides of the Belt.

Priya had flagged it first — a burst of encrypted traffic, too much of it, too fast. Now the tactical display painted the picture in cold amber: Earth's navy sortied three frigates and a tender from the Ceres blockade station they'd pretended for fifteen years wasn't a blockade station, while out past Jupiter's shadow the colonial fleet detached a hunter-killer pair from its picket line. Both converging. Both aimed, with the blunt certainty of institutions that had never once doubted themselves, at a single derelict beacon where a ship full of children lay docked to a hulk that officially did not exist.

"They know," Priya said. She said it without drama, the way she reported reactor pressures and air scrubber failures. "Maybe not everything. But they know the *Meridian* came back, and they know something on her is worth killing for."

Edmund watched the projected intercept lines crawl toward each other. Fifteen years he'd kept this place invisible — filed false manifests, burned transponder logs, taught smugglers how to lie with radar shadows. Fifteen years of patience, and it had lasted exactly nine days after the *Meridian* opened her airlock.

"Get everyone up," he said. "All hands. We're leaving."

* * *

They gathered in the old hydroponics bay because it was the only room big enough to hold all ninety-one of them, and because Oyelaran had asked — in that voice that still carried a captain's authority even though it came out of a body that barely reached the table's edge — to see the plants before they went.

She stood among the tomato vines now, small hands folded behind her back, and told them what Edmund already knew.

"Both fleets will reach us within thirty-six hours. Whichever arrives first takes the evidence, and whichever takes it wins the war." She looked around the bay, at faces weathered and young, scarred and soft. "I've spent eleven years in deep space learning what governments do with weapons. I would rather burn this archive than hand it to either of you intact."

A murmur ran through the crowd. Old Marta Vasquez, who'd been hauling ore since before Edmund could legally fly solo, called out from the back: "Then why haven't you?"

"Because burning it makes us nothing," Oyelaran said simply. "And being nothing is how thousands died out there while we gardened."

Silence, then. The kind that has weight.

It was Priya who broke it, as Edmund had known she would. "We run," she said. "We take the *Meridian* and every hauler that can make thrust, and we go through the Halcyon debris field. Neither fleet will follow us into that graveyard — their hulls are worth too much to risk."

"And ours aren't?" someone asked bitterly.

"Our hulls are the only ones whose crews get a vote," Priya answered.

The vote itself took less than an hour. That was the thing about found family, Edmund thought, watching hands rise one by one in the green light of the grow-lamps: it argued like any family, but when the moment came, it moved like a crew. Eighty-three for running. Four for destroying the archive. Four abstained, including, he noticed, himself. He hadn't been able to lift his hand either way. Some part of him was still standing at that derelict beacon, fifteen years ago, choosing desertion over duty and telling himself it was the last hard choice he'd ever have to make.

* * *

The convoy assembled in the dark side of Ceres's orbit like a rumor taking physical form.

Seven ships. The *Meridian* herself, patched and re-armed with salvaged point-defense guns that three different governments would have recognized and none could claim. Two ore haulers, the *Long Patience* and the *Second Harvest*, riding high and empty. A water tanker converted to carry fuel bladders. A medical skiff. And at the rear, slowest and oldest and proudest of them all, the *Marta's Pride* — forty years of hauling history welded into a hull that predated the war, piloted by the woman she'd been named for, because Marta Vasquez refused, absolutely refused, to let anyone else fly her.

"You're too slow," Edmund told her over the comm, in the last hour before departure. "The formation can't wait for you in the field. You know that."

"I know my ship, boy." Her voice was gravel and engine grease. "I've been through Halcyon eleven times. I know which corridors are real and which are ghosts. Your fancy pilots with their fancy charts will pile up against a tumbling girder inside ten minutes. I'll be your eyes."

"And if we have to run?"

A pause. When she spoke again, her voice had gone quiet in a way he'd never heard from her. "Then you run, and I do what old ships are for. Edmund. Look at me." He switched to visual. Her face filled his screen, seamed and brown and utterly unafraid. "Fifteen years ago you carried me and mine out of Jovian space when the navy was burning habitats. You didn't ask what it would cost. Don't you dare start doing arithmetic now."

He wanted to argue. He found he couldn't. Some debts are only ever paid forward, and she had decided this was hers.

"Formation in twenty minutes," was all he said.

"Aye," she said, and cut the channel, and Edmund sat alone in the cockpit of the *Long Patience* feeling the truth settle over him like ballast: he had never once believed she'd survive this, and neither had she, and they had both agreed anyway, because the alternative was letting the truth die in the dark with them.

* * *

The Halcyon debris field had been a shipyard once, before the war's first year, before Earth's navy had cracked it open with kinetic strikes to deny its use to the colonies. Eleven thousand people had worked there. The wreckage stretched across four hundred thousand kilometers — hull plates the size of city blocks, frozen coolant clouds that glittered like auroras, the slow tumbling skeletons of dry docks, and everywhere, everywhere, the small unidentifiable fragments that had been people.

No charts survived. Every transit was a negotiation with the dead.

They entered at 1900, running dark, engines at minimum, the convoy strung out in a line behind the *Marta's Pride* like ducklings behind a mother who had raised far worse broods in far worse weather. Edmund flew second position, close enough to see her running lights blink the corridor changes: green for hold course, amber for shift, red for everyone stop breathing.

For six hours it was almost peaceful. The field at night, running silent, was the closest thing to sacred Edmund had ever known — flying through a cathedral of the dead, carrying proof of how they'd died. He caught himself thinking about the greenhouse, the seedlings they'd planted before evacuation, whether Priya's assistant had remembered to reset the irrigation timers. Absurd, the things the mind clings to.

At 0113, the *Meridian*'s sensors caught the first active ping.

"Contact bearing zero-four-seven," Oyelaran reported, her child's voice flat and professional over the comm. "Frigate-class. They're not in the field yet — they're sweeping the approaches. They know we came in here."

"They won't follow," Priya said. "Their hulls—"

"They don't need to follow." Oyelaran again. "They need only wait at every exit. We have fuel for one transit. They have fuel for a siege."

The math was brutal and simple, and everyone on the convoy did it in silence. Then Marta's voice came through, calm as a lullaby.

"There's an exit they haven't charted. Old tug corridor, northeast quadrant. Narrow. Takes six hours we don't have, unless—" another pause, and Edmund knew, knew with sick certainty, what was coming next— "unless somebody draws the frigate's attention elsewhere. Somebody loud. Somebody slow."

"No," Edmund said. It came out of him before thought, raw. "Marta, no."

"Boy, I'm seventy-one years old. My hips are shot, my eyes are failing, and my ship burns fuel like a politician spends promises. What exactly am I saving myself for?" A dry chuckle. "But a decoy needs to look like a convoy. Needs transponders, chatter, a big fat signature. Needs to be *believable*. There's exactly one ship in this fleet that qualifies."

"We can find another way—"

"You can't, and we both know it, and we've wasted ninety seconds arguing about it." Her voice softened. "You gave me fifteen years I wasn't owed, Edmund Ravensworth. You gave my grandchildren a place to grow up where nobody shot at them. Let an old woman settle her account."

He flew then, for a while, unable to speak, unable to do anything but hold his course through the glittering dark while the woman who had named her ship after herself made ready to die in it.

"Marta," he finally managed. "The garden. The tomatoes. You promised the kids you'd—"

"I know what I promised." Gentle. Final. "That's why. Grow them something sweet, Edmund. That's the whole point of everything, isn't it? Grow them something sweet."

* * *

The *Marta's Pride* broke from formation at 0147, transponders blazing, broadcasting a convoy signature on every band, chattering cheerfully on open channels about cargo tonnage and route checkpoints — a ghost convoy of one, loud enough to be a lie the frigate wanted to hear.

The frigate took the bait. Of course it did; navies are built to believe in signatures. It turned from its picket station and burned hard for the noise, and the rest of the convoy slipped northeast through the narrow corridors Marta had mapped from memory, through gaps between dead ships that no living pilot had ever flown, trusting entirely to the skill and the sacrifice of a woman who had spent four decades learning where the ghosts were.

They heard it happen at 0322. Everyone heard it. The frigate, closing on its prey, had opened fire at extreme range — not to disable, Edmund understood later, but to scatter, to force the decoy into evasive patterns that would reveal whether there were more ships hiding in the clutter. And the *Marta's Pride*, instead of running, turned *toward* the fire, driving straight down the frigate's throat, filling every sensor the warship owned with one impossible, glorious, suicidal target.

The last transmission from the *Pride* was Marta's voice, laughing, actually laughing, over the sound of her hull coming apart:

"—told you I knew these corridors, you bastards! Nothing in Halcyon dies quiet!"

Then a bloom of light on the tactical display, expanding, fading. Then static.

Then, forty seconds later, a secondary flash — the *Pride*'s remaining fuel detonating as she tumbled into the path of the frigate's own point-defense fire, crippling the warship's sensor array in a final act of spite that bought the convoy another hour of clean running.

Nobody spoke for a long time. In the cockpit of the *Long Patience*, Edmund flew with tears freezing on his cheeks in the recycled air, and did the arithmetic he had sworn never to do.

One life. One old, tired, magnificent life, traded for eighty-three others and a data archive and the chance — only the chance — that the truth might end the killing before it ended everyone.

Was it worth it?

He genuinely did not know. He suspected the question had no honest answer, that *worth* was a currency only the survivors got to spend, and that Marta had understood this better than any of them. He suspected that somewhere in the wreck of the *Pride*, drifting now among the eleven thousand dead of Halcyon, the old woman would have laughed at him for asking.

What he knew was this: the convoy cleared the debris field at 0940, undetected, intact, carrying its terrible cargo toward the transmission window and the end of the war or the end of everything.

And what he knew was this: somewhere behind them, in the dark of the field, an old woman's promise hung unfulfilled among the stars, and he was the one who would have to keep it.

* * *

They held a funeral at 1200, because Priya insisted that some things could not wait for peace.

Ninety voices, one empty suit laid out on a cargo pallet, the black of open space beyond the observation blister. Oyelaran spoke — short, military, precise. Priya spoke — fierce, grieving, promising. And then, because no one else could bear to, Edmund stepped forward, and found that the words were simpler than he'd feared.

"She flew us out of the fire fifteen years ago," he said. "Yesterday she flew into one so the rest of us wouldn't burn. I don't know if the truth is worth what it costs. I only know she decided it was, and she never once asked any of us to agree with her." He looked at the empty suit, at the stars beyond it. "So we'll carry it for her. All the way. Both halves."

He said *both halves* without quite meaning to, and felt the phrase land in the room like a seed dropped into soil — felt heads turn, felt Oyelaran's sharp eyes fix on him, felt the idea take root even before he understood it fully himself.

Both halves. Neither fleet whole. Neither side armed with the entire truth.

He stood there in the green-lit dark with ninety grieving people watching him, and felt the shape of the plan assemble itself in his mind — desperate, half-formed, possibly insane — and knew that in the morning he would have to stand in front of these same people and ask them to trust everything they had left to couriers who might never come home.

Behind him, the stars wheeled slowly onward, indifferent and patient, full of dead ships and living children and one window of time that was closing faster than any of them could say.

The transmission loop expired in nine days.
