## 7. The Transmission Loop

The announcement came at 0300 station time, which was to say it came in the hour when the colony's corridors were quietest, when the only sounds were the tick of cooling pipes and the distant hum of the hydroponics pumps, and Adeyemi Oyelaran sat cross-legged on a crate in the comms alcove because she could not sleep and no one had yet thought to give her a bed that fit.

She heard it first as static. Then the static resolved into a voice — clipped, official, wearing the flat accent of Earth Command like a uniform — reading from what was obviously a prepared statement.

*"...in accordance with wartime security directive 114-C, all open-band transmission protocols will be permanently retired following the final scheduled loop. Effective 1900 hours, standard encryption keys will rotate fleet-wide. Independent broadcasts will be unable to authenticate against either command structure. This is not a communications blackout. This is a renewal of trust."*

Adeyemi laughed. It came out of her nine-year-old throat as something between a cough and a sob, and she pressed both hands over her mouth, but the laugh kept going, shaking her small shoulders in the dark of the alcove.

*A renewal of trust.* She had spent eleven months in deep space watching Earth's navy test temporal displacement on its own colonies. She had watched a research station fold time like paper and crush three thousand lives inside the crease, and then she had watched the same navy file the incident as a reactor failure. And now they were retiring the last open channel through which anyone outside their encryption lattice might ever speak to them again.

They weren't renewing trust. They were sealing the tomb.

She keyed the intercom before she could talk herself out of it. Her voice, when it came out, was still the captain's voice — low, precise, carrying the weight of eleven months of command — even though it issued from a body that hadn't yet grown into it. She had stopped apologizing for the mismatch weeks ago. The colony had stopped noticing.

"All hands. Comms alcove. Now."

* * *

They came in ones and twos, pulling on jackets over sleep clothes, blinking against the alcove's harsh light. Edmund arrived first, hair uncombed, eyes already awake in the way of a man who had never fully slept since the Meridian docked. Priya Kaur came behind him with her slate already running, fingers moving before she'd fully stopped walking. Old Marta Vasquez leaned in the doorway with a mug of something steaming. Behind them, the rest of the colony — forty-one souls who did not exist on any registry — crowded into the corridor, and behind them, absurdly, solemnly, the de-aged crew of the Meridian stood in a ragged line, children in borrowed clothes with veterans' faces looking out of them.

Adeyemi played the announcement twice. On the second pass, she watched Edmund's face instead of listening, and what she saw there frightened her more than the announcement itself: not shock. Recognition. He had known this deadline was coming. He had been counting toward it the way she had once counted down burn windows.

"1900 hours," Priya said quietly. "That's sixteen hours."

"After that," Edmund said, "the keys rotate. Every independent broadcast in the system becomes noise. We could stand on Ceres and shout the truth into the dark until we die of old age, and neither fleet would hear a word of it. They'd hear *something* — an unauthenticated signal — and they'd treat it as a hostile spoof. That's if they bother to listen at all."

"So we broadcast before 1900," someone said from the corridor. "We have the archive. We push it out wide-band, every frequency, let them choke on it."

"And they shoot the source," Marta said without turning around. "Both fleets have standing orders on unauthenticated data bursts. You want to know how I know? Because I wrote half of them, back when I wore a different flag." She sipped her drink. "Wide-band truth looks exactly like an attack. It *is* an attack, the way they'll see it. Information warfare. First response is kinetic."

Silence settled over the alcove like frost.

Adeyemi looked at the faces around her. Forty-one colonists who had spent years perfecting the art of being nobody. A crew of children who had crossed eleven light-months to bring home proof of atrocity. And one window, sixteen hours wide, closing on all of it.

"We tried hiding," she said. "We tried running. Both of those are over — the moment either fleet finds this station, and they will find it, our invisibility stops being armor and becomes a death sentence. So the question isn't whether we act. The question is what acting looks like." She turned to Edmund. "You've been quiet. You've been quiet since the announcement started. Say what you're thinking."

Edmund looked at his hands for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice was rough, like something dragged up from a depth.

"Fifteen years ago I deserted because I saw what Earth's command structure does with information. It doesn't share it. It *spends* it. Any piece of truth that reaches only one side becomes ammunition — leverage, blackmail, justification. If we hand the whole archive to the colonial fleet, they use it to justify escalation, and Earth calls it fabrication and doubles down. If we hand it to Earth, they bury it so deep no light reaches it, and the colonies never learn why their people died." He lifted his head. "So we don't hand it to either one. We split it."

Priya's fingers stopped moving on her slate. "Split it how?"

"Half the archive to each fleet. Interleaved. Neither copy is complete without the other. Each contains enough to prove the weapon exists and the cover-up began — but the full kill records, the test logs, the names of the dead, the chain of authorization — those are split across both transmissions. To reconstruct the whole truth, the two fleets have to *talk to each other*. They have to compare copies. They have to sit at the same table." He exhaled. "Neither government wants an armistice. But neither can refuse one if refusing means the other side holds half the evidence and they hold nothing. We don't give them the truth. We give them a reason to need each other."

"That's insane," said Tomas Reyes, the colony's medic, from the back. "That's — you're proposing we broker a peace treaty with a data packet."

"I'm proposing we make peace *cheaper* than war," Edmund said. "For about ninety minutes. Long enough for cooler heads to grab the wheel."

"And if it doesn't work?" Marta asked. "If they just take their halves and go back to shooting?"

"Then we've lost nothing but the transmission window we were going to lose anyway." He paused, and Adeyemi saw him choose his next words carefully, the way he chose everything. "There's one more problem. Broadcasts can be jammed, spoofed, buried. A signal arriving from a smuggler's relay gets flagged and deleted by some junior analyst before it ever reaches an admiral. For this to work, the evidence has to arrive in *person*. Hand-carried. With witnesses. With testimony."

"You're talking about couriers," Priya said slowly.

"I'm talking about messengers." Edmund's jaw tightened. "And yes. I'm talking about the very real possibility that both sides shoot the messengers. That's the honest math. Anyone who carries this walks into a fleet flagship carrying proof of war crimes, and the first instinct of every flag officer in history is to kill the bearer and control the story."

The silence that followed was different from the last one. Heavier. Adeyemi felt it settle onto her small chest like ballast.

"No," she said.

Every head turned to her.

"You are not volunteering yourself alone, Edmund Ravensworth. I've watched you do that for fifteen years — carry everything yourself, decide everything yourself, protect everyone by deciding for them. Not this time." She stood up on the crate so she could see over the crowd, and her voice cracked once, and she let it. "This is my evidence. My crew found it. My crew bled for it. And my crew—" she gestured at the ragged line of children behind her, "—my crew has the right to finish what we started. Whatever we do, we decide it together. All of us. Colony and crew. That's the vote."

* * *

The vote took place in the greenhouse, because Marta refused to hold it anywhere else. "People think straighter around growing things," she said, and no one argued with Marta.

Forty-one colonists and twenty-three children stood among the tomato vines and the young apple trees Edmund had planted years ago, trees whose fruit would ripen long after anyone in this room might live to taste it. Adeyemi thought there was something fitting about that. Everything they were about to do was planting for a harvest they might not survive to see.

The split-transmission plan passed, 58 to 6. The courier plan passed narrower — 41 to 23 — and Adeyemi noticed that most of the nays were the de-aged crew, voting against sending *anyone*, themselves included, into the guns. When the tally was read, a girl of perhaps seven — Lieutenant Okonkwo, formerly sixty-two, formerly the Meridian's navigator — raised her hand and said, in a voice like a bell, "Then we should pick couriers who've already died once. We're less afraid than you are."

It took another hour to choose. In the end it came down to two names, and everyone in the greenhouse understood why before anyone said it aloud.

Edmund, because he knew Earth Command's protocols, its frequencies, its paranoia; because Admiral Chen's flagship had once been his posting; because a deserter walking voluntarily into the fleet that wants him is either the bravest man alive or the most useful liar, and Chen would have to look into his face to learn which.

And Adeyemi herself, with the Meridian and her crew, toward the colonial fleet — because the colonial sailors had mothers and fathers and sisters out there, and no colonial gunner would fire on a ship full of children without asking questions first. Children were the one cargo in the system that made trigger fingers hesitate.

"The window closes at 1900," Priya said, when it was done. She stood under the grow-lights with the archive drive held against her chest like something living. "Transmissions launch at 1845. Couriers depart immediately after. If either ship is late — if either signal arrives after key rotation —" She didn't finish. She didn't have to.

Adeyemi caught Edmund's eye across the greenhouse, across the vines and the sleeping colony and the children who used to be her officers. Fifteen years ago he had run from Earth's navy in the dark, alone, leaving everything behind. Tomorrow — in twelve hours — he would walk back into it in broad daylight, carrying the thing he'd run from, trusting strangers with half the truth and strangers' hands with the other half.

He nodded to her, once, captain to captain.

Outside the greenhouse glass, Ceres turned slowly against the stars, and somewhere beyond them, two fleets drifted in contested orbit, loaded, aimed, and blind — waiting, though they didn't know it yet, for a message carried by a deserter and a child.
