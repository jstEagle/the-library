## 8. Two Couriers

The vote happened in the hydroponics bay, because it was the only room on Ceres Station big enough to hold everyone and warm enough to make them feel human. Priya Kaur stood at the front with her back against the nutrient tanks, feeling their slow mechanical pulse through her spine, and watched two hundred people decide the shape of the rest of their lives.

Edmund had laid it out plainly, because that was the only way he knew how to say anything. Split the archive. Two copies, two couriers, two fleets. Neither government gets the whole truth. Neither can build the weapon from half a blueprint. The armistice becomes arithmetic — each side needs the other's piece to understand what was done to them, and so they have to sit down at the same table to do it.

"It's not a plan," someone had said. "It's a gamble."

"It's the only plan where we're not choosing a winner," Edmund had answered.

The vote wasn't even close. What surprised Priya was not the number of hands raised for splitting the message but the number raised for something she hadn't expected: a third option, scrawled on a scrap of paper and passed forward by old Marta Osei, who had fled Ganymede with nothing but her granddaughter and a grudge. *Send both copies with people this war owes something to.* Not smugglers looking for leverage. Not mercenaries looking for payment. People with accounts to settle.

That was how Priya ended up standing in Docking Bay Three at 0400 station time, staring up at the Meridian and trying to remember when she had last slept.

* * *

They had spent nine days refitting her. Nine days of scavenged plating and cannibalized drives, of Priya crawling through conduits with a fusion torch while the de-aged crew of the Meridian worked alongside her — children's hands doing adult work with adult precision, which never stopped being unsettling no matter how many times she saw it.

Captain Oyelaran stood at the base of the boarding ramp now, nine years old and wearing a flight suit sized for a woman, the sleeves rolled six times. She'd refused the child-sized suits the station fabricators offered. "I didn't earn this body," she'd said. "I'm not going to dress like I did."

"You could stay," Priya told her. It was the last time she would say it, and she said it knowing the answer. "Ceres is hidden again, after this. You could grow up somewhere safe."

Oyelaran looked at her with an expression far too old for the face carrying it. "Commander Kaur, I spent eleven years in deep space watching my crew age backward one day at a time, wondering if I'd live long enough to forget my own name. Safe stopped being a thing I recognize." She adjusted the rolled sleeve. "Besides. My ship. My mission. The Navy taught me that much before I taught myself the rest."

"The Navy also taught you to follow orders."

"The Navy taught me to know which orders are worth following." Something flickered across the small face — grief, maybe, for the woman she'd been. "Yours are worth following. That's why I'm here."

Priya crouched down to the girl's eye level, an instinct she'd learned from her nieces before the war took them, and found she couldn't say anything clever. "Bring my ship back," she finally managed. "Both of them. The Meridian and the captain."

"No promises," Oyelaran said. Then, quieter, in a voice that was suddenly all nine years old: "But I'll try. Nobody's ever asked me to come back before. Everyone always just assumed I would." She turned and went up the ramp, small boots ringing on the metal, and Priya watched her go and understood that she had just been trusted with something enormous by someone very small, and that the trust ran both directions whether she wanted it to or not.

* * *

Edmund was loading his own ship in silence two bays over. The *Long Patience* — fifteen years of hauling cargo through contested orbits had given him a hauler as battered and stubborn as he was, its hull patched with plates from four different wrecks, its engines tuned by hand until they hummed almost in tune.

He worked alone. That was the thing about Edmund Ravensworth that Priya had never quite forgiven and never quite stopped admiring: he carried everything alone, even now, even here, even when the whole colony had voted to carry it with him.

She walked over and picked up a crate without asking, and he let her, and they loaded in silence until there was nothing left to load.

"Chen's flagship will be at the rally point in six days," he said, checking the tie-downs a fourth time. "My window into their perimeter is narrow. If I miss it—"

"You won't miss it. You've been running blockades since before I could weld."

"Before you could weld," he repeated, and smiled faintly. "That's a strange sentence. Fifteen years ago you were—" He stopped himself, but not fast enough.

"Eighteen and hiding in a shipping container on Titan," Priya finished for him. "Yes. We've all done our time being young and terrified. Some of us just got to do it in the right order."

He laughed — short, surprised, real. It was rare enough that she catalogued it.

"Edmund." She made herself say the rest. "If Chen decides you're a spy — and he will, for about ten minutes — you need to know that nobody here will think less of you for turning back. And nobody here will ever forgive you for dying stupidly. Those are different things. Try to keep them straight under fire."

"I'll try." He closed the cargo hatch. His hand lingered on the metal. "You know what I planted in the greenhouse this morning? Before all this. Last row of apple stock. Thirty years to fruit. I'll be seventy-eight."

"You'll be seventy-eight and insufferable about it."

"Maybe." He looked at her then, and behind the tiredness there was something she'd only seen once or twice in fifteen years — the thing that had made him plant those trees in the first place. "If it works. If the fleets stop. Come back and help me prune. I don't actually know how to prune. I just plant things and believe at them."

"That's not how orchards work."

"It's how everything works," Edmund said. "You'll see."

They didn't shake hands. They didn't hug. Fifteen years of shared silence and shared guilt had burned away every gesture that wasn't load-bearing. Instead Priya put her hand flat against the hull of the *Long Patience*, over the patch plate from the wreck of the *Sunder*, over the place where Vasquez's name was stenciled beneath the newer paint, and left it there for three seconds, and Edmund watched her do it and nodded once, and that was the whole farewell.

It was enough. It had to be.

* * *

At 0517 station time, two ships left Ceres Station on divergent burn.

Priya sat in the Meridian's command chair — Oyelaran's chair, though the captain had insisted she take it for departure — and watched the *Long Patience* shrink on the display until it was a spark, then a rumor, then nothing. Somewhere out there, Edmund Ravensworth was flying alone toward the fleet that had written him off as a deserter fifteen years ago, carrying half a truth in his hold and no backup worth the name.

"Course laid in for the colonial fleet rendezvous," said Okonkwo — twelve years old now, voice steady, hands moving across the helm with the economy of three decades' practice trapped in a child's reach. "Eleven days at current burn."

"Then we have eleven days," Priya said, "to become the most convincing witnesses in human history."

Oyelaran climbed into the seat beside hers, too small for it, feet not reaching the floor, and pulled the harness snug anyway. On the main display, the colonial fleet's beacon region glowed faint and far — thousands of ships that had broken with Earth, waiting for proof of what Earth had done to justify every choice they'd made.

"They'll want to verify the archive independently," Oyelaran said. "They'll want to interrogate us. Some of them will want to seize it and finish the weapon themselves." She said it without drama, the way she reported reactor status. "We should be ready for that."

"We will be."

"And Commander?" The girl looked up at her. "When they ask why we came — why children came, in this ship — what do we tell them?"

Priya thought about the greenhouse full of trees that wouldn't fruit for thirty years. About Vasquez staying behind in a dying hauler so the rest of them could run. About two hundred people voting to send their truth out in other people's hands because keeping it would have been another kind of lie.

"We tell them the truth," she said. "All of it. Even the parts that cost us."

The Meridian burned on toward the fleet, small and patched and unafraid, and eleven days out, at the edge of the debris field where old Vasquez had died buying them their chance, Priya's console lit with a priority intercept neither of them wanted to read:

**EARTH FLEET VESSEL INTERCEPTED IN RESTRICTED SPACE. ONE SURVIVOR IN CUSTODY. FLAGSHIP *VIGILANT* REQUESTING VERIFICATION OF CLAIMED EVIDENCE.**

Edmund had made it. Edmund had been caught. And now everything depended on what a nine-year-old captain and a deserter's half-truth could prove to two armed camps in the next few hours.

"Helm," Oyelaran said, in her captain's voice, in her child's body, "all ahead full."
