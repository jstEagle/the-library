## 1. The Long Route

The skiff *Meridian's Daughter* had been carrying forty tons of deuterium slurry and eleven souls between Vesper's Reach and the mining platforms at Cael Anchorage when it simply stopped existing. That was the phrase the initial report used—*stopped existing*—and Ines Pemberton had spent nine years teaching junior adjusters never to accept such language. Things did not stop existing. They burned, or they depressurized, or they were stolen, or they fell into a gravity well with their transponders dutifully screaming all the way down. But they did not stop.

She stood in the claims archive on the station ring above Vesper's Reach, the room kept cold enough that her breath should have fogged, except the air scrubbers pulled even moisture from breath. The light came up amber through the floor panels, the color of old paper, of things already archived. Around her, the racks of data-slates hummed at a frequency she felt in her back teeth more than heard.

The *Daughter*'s flight recorder had been recovered—or rather, its casing had, scorched and empty, dredged out of the transit corridor by a salvage tug six weeks after the fact. Empty. Which meant either the core had been destroyed in whatever event unmade the ship, or someone had removed it first, and Ines had learned long ago that the second explanation was always cheaper to arrange.

So she did what she always did when the primary evidence declined to speak: she interrogated everything around it.

The manifest was clean. The crew rosters were clean. The insurance bond, underwritten by her own consortium—the Meridian Compact, though no one outside the colonies ever used the name, because officially the Compact insured nothing beyond Earth's licensed sphere—was clean down to its notarized seals. Everything about the *Meridian's Daughter* was so clean it squeaked, and Ines had come to distrust cleanliness the way other investigators distrusted blood.

It was nearly midnight by station clock when she pulled the ancillary file: not the skiff's own records but the routine incident logs from Vesper's Reach itself, the colony below, filed as context for the route risk assessment. Docking collisions. Atmospheric processor faults. A greenhouse fire. A pressure breach in a residential module, three years back, that had killed two people whose names she now read twice because something in them snagged like a loose thread against a nail.

She ran the timestamps out of habit. It was the first thing they taught you at the Compact's academy on Luna: *time is the one witness that cannot be coached*. Every event leaves a temporal signature—the interval between alarm and response, between fault and failure, between the last logged heartbeat and the first logged silence. Liars could forge contents. Timestamps required an entire infrastructure to forge, and infrastructures left shadows.

The greenhouse fire: 03:14:07 colony standard.

The pressure breach: 03:14:07.

She sat back. The archive hummed. She told herself it was coincidence, the way you tell yourself a sound in the dark is the building settling. Two events, years apart, sharing a timestamp to the second—it happened. Random distributions produced patterns; that was the whole reason humans saw faces in clouds and conspiracies in noise. She was a professional skeptic. Skepticism was not the belief that nothing was true; it was the discipline of believing only what survived examination.

So she examined.

She widened the query. Every incident report filed from Vesper's Reach in the last eleven years, every anomaly, every fault, every death classified accidental—and there were more than there should have been, she noted, for a colony of sixty thousand, more than the actuarial tables liked, though never so many that anyone upstairs would raise a flag. She stripped the dates, kept the times, and let the sorting engine do its dumb, honest work.

They clustered. Not all of them—maybe a third—but a third was obscene. Fires, breaches, mechanical failures, a cargo lift that dropped nine meters with a man inside, all stamped 03:14:07, as if the colony's misfortunes kept an appointment. As if something down there woke at that hour and went to work.

Ines pressed her palms flat against the cold of the console and made herself think like an adjuster and not like the child she had once been, the one who'd believed the dark was full of intentions. Instrument error? No—the timestamps came from independent systems, dock clocks, medical logs, fire suppression controllers, each synced to the colony's master chronometer but recorded locally. To falsify all of them would require touching every subsystem on the station ring and half the ones planet-side, and such an operation would leave procurement trails wide enough to drive a hauler through.

Unless, said a voice in her head that sounded uncomfortably like her own at twenty-three, idealistic and newly badged—you unless you built the systems that way from the beginning. Unless the clock itself was the lie, and everyone downstream inherited it faithfully.

She thought about the *Meridian's Daughter*, empty-cored, immaculate, vanished between worlds. She thought about how the Compact's charter defined an uninsurable event, and how the definition had been amended eleven years ago, quietly, in a rider no one discussed: *temporal anomalies of provenance*.

Provenance. As if time itself had a birthplace, and some events were born somewhere else.

Her comm chimed. Handler priority. Of course it was; Halvorsen had a nose for when she found things, the way sharks had a nose for blood in water at concentrations no instrument could detect.

"Pemberton." His voice came through flattened by encryption, all warmth squeezed out of it, which was his default and therefore told her nothing. "You're burning cycles on a total-loss cargo claim. The *Daughter* is written off, the payout is processed, close the file."

"The flight recorder was empty before impact trajectory analysis says any fire reached it."

"Salvage cores fail. It happens."

"Eleven years of incident reports from this colony share a timestamp to the second. That doesn't happen."

A pause. Through the port behind her, Vesper's Reach turned below, a brown-and-green coin wrapped in cloud, its night side pricked with settlement lights. Somewhere down there, at 03:14:07, something had a schedule.

"Pemberton." Now his voice changed, and she recognized the register—he used it for witnesses he intended to discredit and adjusters he intended to protect, and after nine years she still couldn't tell the difference. "You're tired. You've been in the field four months. Come home, take decompression, let the machine sorters handle the pattern-matching. That's what they're for."

"The sorters flagged it. I'm the one asking why."

"And I'm the one telling you to stop." A breath, static-softened. "Some signatures are artifacts. Clock drift, sync cascades, boring engineering reasons. You'll find one if you keep digging, and you'll have spent a month finding a rounding error. Close the file. That's an instruction, not advice."

The channel closed. Ines stood a while in the amber light, listening to the racks hum, feeling the particular loneliness of a person holding a number no one else wants to hold. 03:14:07. She said it aloud just to hear it in the empty archive, and the room gave it back to her in its own voice, a faint sympathetic buzz from the nearest slate rack, and she decided she had imagined that.

* * *

She dreamed of fire.

Not the abstract fire of an adjuster's imagination—not the tidy diagrams of burn propagation she drew for tribunals, arrows and angles and estimated temperatures. This fire had a smell, and the smell was wrong for a station: woodsmoke and hot dust and something sweeter underneath, like scorched hair, like sugar left on a burner. This fire had a sound—a roaring that was also somehow a singing, dozens of voices thinned by smoke into one long note.

And this fire had a building, and the building was a dormitory.

She knew it was a dormitory the way you know things in dreams, without evidence, without chain of custody: rows of narrow bunks, blankets printed with faded rockets, a night-light shaped like a moon still glowing absurdly on the wall while the corridor outside filled with orange. Small shoes under the bunks. A window too high to reach. Hands—her own hands, she understood with dream-certainty—pressed against glass gone hot, looking out at a sky she did not recognize, a sky with two moons, one of them cracked like dropped porcelain.

She was trying to count the bunks. That was the task, the urgent task her sleeping mind had assigned itself: count the bunks, because each bunk was a name, and the names were being taken away faster than she could read them, ink lifting off the bed-tags and swirling up into the smoke. She got to thirty-one before the heat drove her back from the glass, and she woke—

—woke in her berth on the station ring with her hand flat against the cool wall panel and her heart going like a failing pump, and the station clock read 03:14, and seven seconds later the ventilation system cycled with a soft pneumatic sigh, right on schedule, right on time.

Ines lay in the dark and did not sleep again. She was a professional skeptic. She knew what dreams were: memory consolidation, sensory noise, the brain's overnight janitorial staff hauling out the day's refuse. She had never been near a burning dormitory. She had never seen a sky with two moons. Vesper's Reach had one moon, intact, catalogued, boring.

But the timestamp was real. The timestamp was in the archive, witnessed by machines that did not dream.

In the morning she pulled the personnel files for the two dead in the residential breach, and then, on an impulse she would later be unable to explain, the manifests for every worker transferred off Kestrel Station—because Kestrel Station appeared exactly once in the entire Compact database, in a decommissioning footnote dated eleven years ago, reactor failure, total loss, three hundred souls, records sealed—and cross-referenced it against the colony below.

Three hundred people did not simply evaporate. Some of them, statistically, inevitably, had ended up here, on Vesper's Reach, carrying their habits and their griefs and perhaps their suspicions to new bunks under a single cautious moon.

And somewhere down there, in a maintenance hatch or a housing or a wall, someone had scratched a name the archives refused to say aloud.

She booked a shuttle to the surface for 06:00, and as she sealed her berth she caught herself checking the clock, and hating herself for checking, and checking anyway.

03:14:07 was coming around again tomorrow. It came around every night.

Something down there was keeping count.
