## 1. The Beacon Nobody Ordered

The beacon at Station Vesper-9 was older than Ines Oyelaran, which she supposed wasn't saying much, since Ines Oyelaran was four years old — or thirty-one, depending on how you counted, and nobody had ever given her a straight answer about which counting method was official.

She counted it in wrench turns, mostly. The beacon's primary emitter housing required one hundred and forty of them to service properly, and she had done it every ninety days for four years, and today was day eighty-nine, and she was putting it off because the housing bay was cold and because she'd been having an argument with a dead woman all morning and wanted to finish it first.

"You can't just leave a case note like that," Ines said to the room. "That's not how notes work."

The room, being a station office built for a war that had ended without records, did not answer. It never did. But it had good acoustics for arguing anyway — high ceilings, bare alloy walls, the faint hum of life support doing its patient work — and so Ines argued with Detective Inspector Adaeze Oyelaran the way she did most mornings: out loud, over tea, with the dead woman's own handwriting spread across the desk between them like a third participant who kept interrupting.

*Subject's financials don't reconcile,* the note read, in Adaeze's cramped left-slanting script. *Follow the shipping manifests. If I'm right about this, it goes all the way up.*

And then nothing. No follow-up. No *all the way up to where.* Just that, and then eleven years of silence, and then a cloning contract funded through a shell trust with instructions that read, in their entirety: *Continue.*

Ines turned her mug a quarter-turn on the desk. It was a habit she'd inherited without knowing from whom — possibly from Adaeze, possibly from some lab technician whose coffee habits had contaminated the memory templates. That was the thing about being a clone that nobody warned you about: you couldn't tell which parts of yourself were yours. The way she hummed when she soldered. Her dislike of cilantro. The quarter-turn of the mug. All of it arrived pre-installed, secondhand, like furniture from a deceased relative's estate.

"Follow the shipping manifests," she muttered. "Great. Wonderful. Follow them where, Ada? You could have written *where.*"

She called her original *Ada* sometimes, in private, the way you'd call a difficult older sister by a nickname. It felt disrespectful on some days and necessary on others. Today it felt necessary.

Her terminal chimed — the soft two-tone chime that meant routine traffic, the chime she heard maybe six times a day, and she got up to log it because logging routine traffic was, as far as anyone in the fleet cared, the entire purpose of Station Vesper-9 and therefore of Ines Oyelaran.

The traffic display showed a Meridian-bound ore hauler, transponder squawking its registration, running the long loop around the Reach. Ines typed the entry into the station log with the ease of long practice:

*Day 1,462. 0640 station time. One (1) ore hauler, registered Meridian Freehold, course steady, no anomalies. Beacon functioning within parameters. Nothing happened.*

She'd been adding *nothing happened* to the logs for about a year now. Technically it wasn't standard format. Technically nobody read the Vesper-9 logs, so technically didn't matter, and the phrase made her feel like the log was honest. A log that said only *one ore hauler, no anomalies* implied that something else might have been expected to happen. A log that said *nothing happened* told the truth: this was a lighthouse at the edge of nowhere, blinking at an ocean of dark that had stopped needing it decades ago.

Vesper-9 had been built during the last year of the war — a relay and early-warning post strung along the Calypso Reach like a bead on a very long, very cold string. There had been nine stations once. She knew there had been nine because the construction schematics said so, and she knew where the other eight were because they weren't anywhere; they'd been decommissioned, stripped, and scuttled after the ceasefire, their records folded into archives so deep that even her queries — and she had Adaeze's credentials, real ones, valid ones — came back with polite error messages. *Record sealed under Truce Provision 12. Record sealed under Truce Provision 12. Record does not exist.*

That last one was her favorite. Not *sealed.* *Does not exist.* Somewhere, a fleet archivist had looked at a station the size of a small city and typed *does not exist* with a straight face, and Ines thought about that sometimes the way other people thought about unsolved crossword clues.

Vesper-9 alone had survived, and she'd pieced together why from maintenance requisitions and a single memo she'd found laminated inside a wall panel, presumably hidden there by some technician with a sense of history: the beacon's frequency band was grandfathered into the truce communications accords. Decommissioning it would require a treaty amendment, and amending treaties was expensive, and so the fleet had done what fleets do with problems too small to matter and too annoying to solve. They'd assigned it to someone nobody would miss.

Which was how a cloned homicide detective had ended up as a lighthouse keeper.

Ines finished her tea, pulled on her thermal jacket, and went to argue with the beacon instead. At least the beacon fought back.

* * *

The housing bay was cold the way space is cold — patiently, thoroughly, with no interest in your feelings. Ines clipped her tether, popped the access panel, and began the hundred and forty wrench turns, breathing fog, talking to herself because the alternative was listening to the station creak.

"Turn forty-one," she announced. "You're doing great. We're both doing great."

The emitter housing did not agree. Turn forty-two met resistance — the old, gritty resistance of a bearing that had decided, after forty years, to develop opinions. Ines braced her boots against the mount and leaned.

"Come on. You've done this ninety times. I've done this sixteen times. Between us we have centuries of experience and zero excuses."

The bearing gave with a shriek and then a smoothness so sudden she nearly headbutted the housing. She hung there in the tether, heart thumping, and laughed at herself — a short, embarrassed laugh in the empty cold, the kind of laugh you only make when absolutely no one can hear you.

That was the arithmetic of Vesper-9, really. Sixteen months of solitude per stretch, resupply shuttle every ninety days if the weather on Ceres Relay cooperated, which it regarded as a personal insult. She talked to the station. She talked to the beacon. She talked to Ada. Once, memorably, she'd had a full conversation with a malfunctioning door sensor named Gerald, and Gerald had won.

And here was the thing she'd never admitted to anyone, mostly because there was no one: she was *good* at this. Not competent — good. She liked the rhythm of it, the daily rounds, the small repairs that stayed repaired, the log entries stacking up like rings in a tree. She liked knowing that somewhere out in the dark, ships passed safely because a light she maintained was burning. It was purpose, even if it was borrowed purpose, even if the fleet had handed it to her the way you hand a stray animal to the neighbor nobody likes.

Contentment, she suspected, was exactly this: work that needed doing, done well, in a place that needed you, more or less.

The doubt came later. The doubt always came later, usually around hour twenty of a shift, when the work ran out and there was nothing left between her and the question.

*Who are you when the wrench is down?*

Adaeze Oyelaran had been a detective. Twenty-two years of casework, commendations, a reputation that still surfaced occasionally in the true-crime feeds Ines pretended not to binge. Adaeze had died — officially in a transit accident, though the accident report had the same flavor as the station records, that *does not exist* smoothness — and someone with money and patience had taken her genetic template and grown a new woman with her face, her instincts, and a four-year education consisting of case files, station manuals, and whatever else the trust's curriculum designers had thought relevant.

They hadn't included *how to be a person when the case is closed.* Ines had checked. It wasn't in the modules.

She finished turn one hundred and forty, sealed the panel, and floated back down the spine of the station toward warmth, her breath still fogging, her hands aching in the good way that meant honest work. On the way she passed the memorial wall — her own invention, fleet regulations permitting approximately nothing and her caring approximately less. Photographs printed on foil stock, salvaged from Adaeze's effects: Adaeze at the academy, young and sharp-faced. Adaeze with a squad of investigators, all of them laughing at something outside the frame. Adaeze holding a medal she clearly thought was ridiculous.

And beneath them, taped up with deliberate care, the thing Ines had built the whole wall around: a single page of case notes, the last coherent one, dated eleven days before the transit accident.

*If anything happens to me,* Adaeze had written, *the work matters more than the record. Find the manifests. Count the names. Someone has to count the names.*

Count the names. Ines had run that sentence through every analytical module she owned. Names of what? Suspects? Informants? It matched nothing in the accessible case files, because the accessible case files were, she was increasingly sure, the wrong files — the tidy ones, the ones meant to be found. Somewhere under the seals and the provisions and the polite errors was the real case, and Ada had seen its edge, and eleven days later she was dead, and four years ago a stranger with a funding stream had pressed *continue* like a key on a keyboard.

Ines smoothed the page flat with her thumb, a gesture she'd performed so many times the foil had gone soft at the corners.

"I'm working on it," she told her. "Slowly. The tools here are me and Gerald, and Gerald is a door sensor."

Gerald, sensing himself discussed, blinked his little amber light in the corridor, which Ines chose to interpret as solidarity.

She was heading back to the office to start the afternoon round — filter flush, antenna check, the eternal negotiation with the water reclaimer — when the proximity alarm sang.

Not the routine chime. The *other* one. The one with a rising third that the manual described as *unscheduled approach advisory* and that Ines's body interpreted, before her brain caught up, as *something is coming.*

She stood in the corridor, cold hands warming, and watched the plot resolve on the wall display. A contact, inbound from the deep Reach, decelerating hard. No scheduled traffic. No filed flight plan. The transponder handshake request sat in her queue like a raised hand in a quiet classroom.

Ines pulled up the identification feed and read the registry line twice, because the first time she assumed she'd misread it.

*CORIANDER. Hull registry CV-7731-K.*

She knew that hull number. Not from traffic lists — from a case file, one of the tidy ones, the kind meant to be found. CV-7731-K, freighter, lost with all hands eleven years ago. Destroyed. Investigated, closed, archived. She'd read the incident summary a dozen times, mostly because it was one of the few files in Adaeze's collection with actual texture to it, and because the summary had that same *does not exist* smell underneath its tidy language.

The ship that had burned eleven years ago was decelerating toward her dock, alive and asking permission.

Ines stood very still in the corridor of a station built for a war that ended without records, and felt two things happen in her chest at once, in the same place, almost in the same instant.

The first was fear — clean, animal, the fear of a keeper watching something impossible come out of the dark toward her light.

The second was harder to name. It felt like the moment in a case file when the detail that doesn't fit stops being noise and starts being the whole story. It felt like *count the names.* It felt, absurdly, thrillingly, like the first day of work.

The handshake request blinked, patient, waiting.

Ines Oyelaran did not answer it.

Instead, she killed the corridor lights, checked that the Coriander's approach vector put her station's bulk between her heat signature and their sensors, and went to find out why a dead ship was coming home.
