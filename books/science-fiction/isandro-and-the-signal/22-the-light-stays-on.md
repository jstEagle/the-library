## 22. The Light Stays On

The shuttle traffic started in the third month, and by the sixth it had become a weather system.

Ines stood at the observation blister with a mug of tea going cold in her hand and counted four approach vectors stacked up like cars outside a popular restaurant. Two were registered to historical societies. One was a Meridian Compact diplomatic courier, which still made her teeth itch on principle, though less than it used to. The fourth was a battered family hauler out of the Reach, its hull patched in six different colors of salvage metal, and that one she recognized — the Okonkwo line, Amaia's grandchildren aboard, coming for their quarterly visit.

"Four more," she said to the empty room, because some habits survived even when their reasons didn't.

From somewhere below, Bao's voice floated up through the station's ancient ductwork, carrying the particular aggrieved warmth of a man who had complained about something every day for eight months and would have been lost without it. "Ines! The east docking clamp is doing the thing again!"

"Have you tried hitting it?"

"I have tried hitting it *extensively.*"

"And?"

A pause, then a reluctant, muffled clang, followed by the hydraulic sigh of a clamp accepting its fate. "I hate that this works."

She smiled into her tea. Eight months ago, Bao had stood in this same corridor with plasma burns wrapped in emergency gauze and told her he was staying only until the Coriander was spaceworthy again. The Coriander had been spaceworthy since month two. He had stopped mentioning leaving around month three, and neither of them had ever acknowledged the arithmetic of that, because acknowledging it would have required him to admit he'd been wrong, and Bao would rather rewire a fusion plant barehanded.

Junie, at least, had never pretended otherwise. She'd announced her decision the way she announced everything — at volume, mid-crisis, over an open comm channel. *"I'm seventeen, I have a pilot's cert, a criminal record that got expunged, and the best story anyone my age has ever had. Where exactly am I supposed to go that tops this?"* Then she'd landed the Coriander in Vesper-9's shadow bay and started calling the old crew quarters "my room" without asking anyone's permission, which, Ines had come to understand, was simply how Junie did everything.

The petitioners came first, back before the traffic became weather. Survivors' kin, mostly, clutching printed manifests like relics — and they were relics now, Ines supposed, the restored record listing all three hundred names of Calypso Reach in letters that could no longer be edited away while someone watched. They came to stand in the beacon chamber and look at the transmitter that had carried their dead back into history, and they wept, and they thanked her, and she never quite knew what to do with the thanking. She'd turned a switch. Other people had bled for what the switch transmitted.

Then came the historians, who were worse, in a way, because they asked questions.

"You have to understand," she'd told a round-faced doctoral candidate from the Meridian archives two weeks ago, "I wasn't making a grand statement about institutional memory. I was trying not to die."

"But you chose justice over—"

"I chose Isandro over the archive," she said. "The philosophy came later, the way regret comes after a bad haircut. You can dress it up afterward all you like."

He'd written it down anyway. She suspected it was in his book. Junie had bought three copies and read the worst passages aloud at dinner for maximum embarrassment.

* * *

The beacon itself had changed, or rather its meaning had, which amounted to the same thing at a station where meaning was the only cargo.

Once, it had pulsed its navigation pattern into the dark on schedule, faithful and pointless, a lighthouse for a shipping lane nobody sailed. Now it still did that — Bao insisted the original function stay intact, out of some engineer's superstition about machines knowing their own names — but layered over it, on a quiet subcarrier that fleet engineers had overlooked because nobody had ever bothered to look, ran the full record. The massacre. The cover-up. The names. All three hundred, cycling endlessly, uneditable, mirrored across forty relay points that the restored archive now maintained as a matter of law.

Voss's trial had made the subcarrier famous. The signal had made it sacred. Ines thought of it as the difference between a signature and a scar: one you choose to make, the other chooses you.

She climbed down to the beacon chamber most mornings anyway, out of ritual more than necessity. The room hummed. The great transmitter coils breathed their slow warm breath, and the light — actual light, the original rotating lamp, kept polished because Amaia had once touched its housing with both hands and said her husband would have liked to know it still worked — swept its patient circle.

"Good morning," she told it. She talked to the ghost of her original here too, but differently now. Less like case notes. More like letters.

*I found the last note,* she said, the way she always did, though the words had long since stopped needing saying. *You knew. You knew you'd lose, and you built me anyway, and you trusted me with the losing.* She rested her palm against the housing, warm under her hand. *I'm not angry anymore. I don't think I ever really was. It's strange, being someone's answer to a question they died before hearing asked. But it's a good question. I've decided to keep it.*

The lamp swept past. Somewhere in the dark beyond the port, four shuttles waited their turn to dock, carrying people who had heard the question too.

* * *

Amaia arrived with the afternoon wave, leaning on her grandson Tomás's arm and pretending, as always, that she didn't need it. She was smaller every visit, Ines thought. Sharper, too, the way old wood gets dense.

"You're feeding these children properly?" Amaia demanded, before hello.

"We have a medic-trained cook and a teenager who considers instant noodles a food group. Define 'properly.'"

"That is a no." She pressed a parcel into Ines's hands — bread, dense and dark, baked in a hauler galley by someone who treated ovens the way Bao treated engines. "For the table tonight. Everyone sits. That includes your historian friend if he's still lurking."

"He left Tuesday."

"He'll be back. They always come back. Sit him down anyway; the bread keeps."

Dinner at Vesper-9 had become an institution without anyone founding it. The mess hall, built for a wartime complement of sixty and used for years by an audience of one, now held a shifting population of twelve to thirty: crew, petitioners stranded between shuttles, the occasional Compact observer who'd learned to eat quietly and wash their own dishes. Bao cooked on alternate nights with the fierce precision he brought to everything, and Sefa's recipe book — recovered from her effects, annotated in her cramped kind handwriting — had become something close to scripture. Tonight was her spiced root stew. Nobody spoke during the first bowl. That was tradition too.

Tomás sat at the far end, quiet, as he always was. The boy who had sold their route to fleet security, coerced with threats against Amaia, had been pardoned under the restoration statutes — officially, completely, the record scrubbed clean. The pardon had changed nothing inside him. He still flinched when Isandro's name came up, still took the dish duty nobody assigned him, still ate last. Ines had stopped trying to tell him it was forgiven. Some things you couldn't transmit; people had to receive them on their own frequency, in their own time. She set aside a second helping for him anyway, unlabeled, the way you leave a light on.

"So," Junie said, into the lull after bowls were refilled. "The Coriander's refit is done done. New clamps, new comms array, and I finally fixed whatever Bao did to the number-three thruster."

"What I *did*—"

"The point," Junie said, with the gravity of a senator, "is that she flies. And there's a colony run organizing out of the Reach — supply ships, settlement charters, the whole rebirth-of-the-frontier thing. Somebody should fly lead on the first convoy. Somebody with experience being officially dead."

The table went still in the way tables do when everyone realizes a goodbye has entered the room ahead of schedule.

Bao put down his spoon. "When?"

"Next month. Maybe six weeks." Junie looked at her stew. For once, she wasn't performing. "I keep telling myself it's temporary. A run, maybe two. But I also keep packing."

Ines felt it land — the specific ache of a door opening in a wall you'd gotten comfortable leaning on. She thought about saying something wise. What came out instead was true: "You'd better send flight plans. I log everything. It's the whole reason I exist."

Junie laughed, wet-eyed, and threw a noodle at her, and the moment broke open into the ordinary noise of a family arguing about thruster maintenance.

* * *

Isandro's letter arrived on the weekly packet, handwritten, because he'd decided eleven years of running had earned him the right to be slow about things.

*Ines —*

*Third colony world. The hearings here drew four hundred people, standing room, and half of them stayed after to just — talk. To each other. About the ones they lost. I think that's the real work, honestly. The broadcast opened the record; the record is finished. What isn't finished is everyone learning how to say the names out loud without the sky falling. That takes longer than a war.*

*Amaia writes that you're feeding historians. God help you. Feed them double; guilt makes them thin.*

*The shard you gave me — the burned one, the first proof — sits on my desk. I used to carry it like a wound. Now it's just a paperweight that remembers. I recommend this transformation to everyone.*

*Tell Bao the converter coupling worked. Tell Junie the Reach pilots ask about "the kid who cracked Voss's cipher" and I say nothing, because she'd want me to charge them for the story.*

*And tell yourself — no. I'll tell you myself next time I dock. There's a thing about your original's case notes I finally understand, and it needs saying face to face, the way the important ones do.*

*— Isandro*

P.S. The beacon light. From orbit, coming in on the standard approach, you can see it sweep. Eleven years I flew past stations looking for anything that meant what it said. Yours does. Keep it burning.

She read the postscript twice, then folded the letter along its worn creases and put it in the drawer with the others — beside the old case notes, the sealed effects, the last decrypted message from a dead woman who had gambled everything on a copy of herself. The drawer had stopped feeling like an archive months ago. It felt like correspondence.

* * *

That night she made her rounds, because the station deserved it and because the walking helped her think.

Past the mess hall, where Bao was showing Tomás how to calibrate the galley's temperamental heating element, two heads bent together over the open panel, the boy's hands steady for once. Past the shadow bay, where the Coriander slept under work lights, already wearing the faint patina of a ship that belonged where it was. Past the visitor quarters, where the Okonkwo grandchildren were meant to be asleep and were audibly not, trading whispers about the famous transmitter two decks down.

Up, at last, to the beacon chamber.

The lamp turned. The coils hummed. On the subcarrier, invisible and tireless, three hundred names rode the dark toward everywhere.

Ines settled onto the old maintenance stool — hers now, worn to the shape of her — and let herself feel the whole strange weight of it. She had been built to finish a dead woman's question, and the question was answered, and by every logic she'd been raised on, she should have come apart at the seams when the work ended. Who was she when the case closed? She'd asked the dark that, once, in the early days, talking to ghost-notes in an empty station.

The dark had taken its time answering. But the answer had arrived, shuttle-load by shuttle-load, bowl by bowl of stew, in Bao's complaints and Junie's flight plans and Isandro's slow handwriting and Tomás learning to fix things instead of break them. She was not the end of a question. She was the place people came to ask new ones.

The lamp swept past her face, warm as a hand.

Below, faintly, through the deck: Junie's voice, running pre-flight checks she didn't need to run yet, and Bao's answering grumble, and the high bright laughter of children who were supposed to be asleep. The found family, assembled around a signal that finally meant what it said.

Ines pulled up the maintenance queue on her wrist display. Fourteen items. Two of them urgent. One of them, per Bao, was "the thing with the clamp," which was not a diagnosis but was somehow always accurate.

"Tomorrow," she told the beacon, and the beacon, being a machine that knew its own name, kept shining anyway.

Outside, the next shuttle made its approach, running lights blinking, carrying someone new toward the light — and Ines Oyelaran, keeper of the station nobody ordered, stayed exactly where she was.
