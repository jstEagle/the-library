## 5. The Truce Clock

Captain Ives drank her tea like it was a hostage negotiation.

She'd brought her own leaves — actual dried camellia sinensis, hoarded from some Concord supply run — and she brewed it in a battered steel flask with the ceremony of a woman performing normalcy. Sable watched her pour two cups in his cramped quarters on Meridian, where the gravity had held steady for six days now and everyone was pretending that meant something.

"Chamomile's for civilians," she said, handing him a cup. "This is for people who are about to have a difficult conversation."

"You could just have the conversation."

"I could." She sat down across from him, uninvited, which by now was practically a friendship ritual. "But tea buys me thirty seconds of you thinking I'm kind."

"You're not kind?"

"I'm *fair*," Ives said. "There's a difference. Kind people lie to you gently. Fair people tell you the ceasefire expires in eleven days."

The cup was warm in his hands. Sable made himself sip it. Made himself not react. Eleven days. He'd known the truce was fraying — everyone on Meridian knew; the station had been forgotten precisely because both fleets had better things to shoot at — but hearing the number spoken aloud made it solid, made it a thing with weight and edges.

"Eleven days," he repeated. "And then?"

"And then the Concord and the Veyra Compact resume hostilities over a border dispute neither side can actually explain anymore, and Meridian Station becomes either collateral damage or a very convenient place to stage an ambush." She smiled without warmth. "My superiors have expressed interest in avoiding the second option."

"How?"

"They want what's in your head, Dravenmoor."

There it was. Sable kept his face still, the way he'd learned to over three years of recording decay — the recyclers coughing rust into the water supply, the colonists leaving one shuttle at a time until only forty-one remained, the slow patient erosion of a place nobody above remembered existed. You got good at stillness when your whole job was watching things fall apart.

"What's in my head," he said carefully, "is three years of maintenance logs and a growing collection of complaints about the coffee synthesizer."

"Your head contains the complete inherited memory lattice of Isandro Dravenmoor, founder of this colony, architect of the original fleet charters, and — according to our intelligence — the last person to transmit something called the Meridian Accord before he was murdered for it." Ives set down her cup. "Nine seconds ago — nine seconds, six days ago — every loose object on this station floated, and both fleets' instruments lit up like festival lanterns. That wasn't a malfunction. That was a memory-lattice detonation, and the resonance signature matched *you*, specifically. So yes. They want what's in your head."

"And by 'want,' you mean—"

"Extract. Formally, they're calling it 'forensic retrieval.' It involves opening the lattice while the host is—" She paused, choosing the word with visible care. "—non-essential."

"Dead," Sable said. "You can say dead. I've been dead-adjacent my whole life. I was grown out of a corpse."

"I know. I read your file." Something flickered across her face — not quite guilt, but its cousin. "For what it's worth, I argued against it. Extraction kills the lattice integrity anyway. Whatever message the original encoded degrades past recovery in roughly sixty percent of cases. My superiors don't care. They want the *trigger*, not the truth. Whoever holds the Accord controls the post-war settlement."

Sable looked into his tea. The surface trembled faintly — the recyclers cycling, or his hands, hard to say.

Here was the arithmetic, then, laid out neat as a ledger: eleven days until the truce died. Two fleets circling. A message in his skull that he had already heard — *coordinates, a voice like his own but older, saying* go to the relay, go to the heart, finish it — and that he had already partially decoded during long sleepless nights in the flooded dark of the lower decks. He knew what the Accord said. He knew it named the corporation that had seeded both fleets, built Meridian, and started the war as a revenue stream. He knew it implicated the founding heroes of *both* sides equally, which meant neither fleet would ever willingly let it broadcast.

And Captain Ives was sitting in his quarters drinking good tea, telling him the truth about her orders because she thought honesty would buy compliance, and the terrible thing — the corrupting, arithmetic thing — was that it might.

He needed her ship. She needed his cooperation. Neither of them needed to know how much he already knew.

"So let me propose an alternative," Sable said.

Ives raised an eyebrow.

"You don't extract anything. Instead, you give me transport — toward the neutral relay at the system's heart — and I deliver the Accord myself, intact, through proper authentication channels. The message gets where it needs to go. Your superiors get their post-war leverage. Nobody has to open my skull like a salvage hull."

"The relay requires verification from Dravenmoor, *original*. Dead men don't authenticate."

"Then we'll find out what counts as the original," Sable said, "won't we? Because I've got his memories, his voice, his stubbornness, and apparently his enemies. If that doesn't add up to the man, nothing does."

Ives studied him for a long moment. He watched her do the same arithmetic he was doing — the corrupting kind, the kind where you use people who are using you and call it alliance. He felt a flicker of shame and set it aside. Shame was a luxury for people with more than eleven days.

"You already know what the message says," she said suddenly. Not a question.

Sable sipped his tea. "I know what my dreams say. Dreams aren't evidence."

"That's exactly what someone who knows what the message says would say."

"And that's exactly what someone who wants plausible deniability would want me to say." He smiled at her, gentle as chamomile. "See? We understand each other."

Ives laughed — short, reluctant, real. "God help me, you even argue like him. The historical record says the original Dravenmoor once talked his way past a Veyra customs blockade by complimenting the commander's hat for twenty minutes."

"It was probably a very good hat."

"It was, allegedly, *magnificent*." She stood, collecting the flask, and paused at the door. "Transport to the relay. One ship, minimal crew, eleven days. But Dravenmoor — if I find out you're carrying something that burns both fleets' founders, my orders stop mattering and my survival instincts start driving. Understand?"

"Perfectly," Sable said, which was true, and also a lie, because the Accord burned *everyone's* founders and he intended to broadcast it anyway.

Her boots faded down the corridor. Sable sat alone with two cold cups of tea and did the final sum: eleven days, one borrowed ship, one dying engineer named Oyelaran somewhere in the flooded decks who owed him answers, and a message that required him to be a man he had never been in order to prove he was.

He began recording.

"Archive entry, day one thousand ninety-two. Today I lied well enough to get a ride to the center of everything. Tomorrow I have to convince a dead man's ghost that I'm worth authenticating."

Outside the viewport, far off, a Veyra corvette adjusted its orbit — closer than it had been yesterday.

Eleven days. Ten, if you counted honestly.

Sable drained the cold tea, grimaced, and went to pack.

* * *

That night, the memories came again — uninvited, involuntary, the original's last evening rising up through Sable's sleep like water through a cracked seal. Not the murder this time. Before it. The original standing in this same quarters, packing the data core into a courier case, murmuring to no one:

*"If they offer you tea, they've already decided. Take the ride anyway."*

Sable woke with his heart hammering and the absurd, grief-stricken certainty that somewhere, somehow, the original Dravenmoor was laughing at him.

Ten days. He'd need a magnificent hat.
