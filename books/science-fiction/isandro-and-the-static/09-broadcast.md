## 9. Broadcast

The relay's verification prompt hung in the air like a held breath: **AUTHENTICATE — DRAVENMOOR, ORIGINAL.**

Sable stood in the relay's core chamber, a sphere of old brass and newer desperation, and laughed. Not because anything was funny, but because of course. Of course the last lock on the door was the one thing he could never be.

"I'm not him," he said to the relay, to Ives, to whatever gods kept ledgers on dead founders. "I'm what's left of him. I'm the receipt."

"Then be a very persuasive receipt," said Captain Ives.

She had come aboard alone — or alone in the way that meant four marines waited in her shuttle with their rifles set to *regretful*. Her extraction orders were still open on her wrist display. Sable had seen them. He'd also seen her close them twice, then reopen them, like someone checking whether the milk had gone off yet.

"The Accord needs his voiceprint," she said. "His biometric lattice. His—" She stopped. "You know what it actually needs."

"It needs him," Sable said. "And he's been dead for three years, and I've spent those three years being haunted by his homework."

The relay chimed again, patient as a bureaucrat. **VERIFICATION REQUIRED.**

Sable closed his eyes. Somewhere behind them, in the wet architecture of borrowed memory, Isandro Dravenmoor was waiting — had always been waiting — for exactly this moment. Not the coordinates. Not the core. This. The part where someone had to stand in front of a machine and argue that a copy deserved the original's name.

*Say it like you mean it,* the dead man's voice murmured, warm and dry, the voice of a man who had once told a corporate board that their station would outlive their company and been laughed at by everyone who later died proving him right.

"I am Isandro Dravenmoor," Sable said.

The relay did not respond.

"No, wait." He opened his eyes. "That's not true, and you'd catch the lie in a heartbeat, wouldn't you? You're built by the same people who built me. You know what a clone sounds like when he's pretending." He put his hand flat against the relay's casing, felt its warmth, its ancient patient hum. "I'm Sable Dravenmoor. Grown from his body, raised on his memories, stuck with his enemies and his errands. I didn't ask for any of it. But I carried it. Three years of watching this station rot while the whole system pretended we'd already died. Nine seconds of falling. A flooded deck where a good man bled out holding data instead of a weapon. And every step of the way, I chose to keep going. So no — I'm not the original. I'm the continuation. And if your verification can't tell the difference between a man and everything he finished, then the corporation won, and I'd rather die here than let that be the last word."

Silence.

Then, softly, the relay spoke in a voice Sable recognized from a hundred inherited evenings: Isandro's own voice, recorded decades ago, young and amused.

*"About time somebody said it properly. Verification accepted — all claimants, all continuations, all stubborn inheritors. Broadcasting."*

Ives exhaled something that might have been a laugh or a prayer. "You argued with a door."

"It's a family trait. Apparently."

* * *

On the Concord flagship *Perpetual Dawn*, eleven minutes before the truce expired, Ensign Rhee watched the launch countdown freeze at 00:11:04 and turn, impossibly, into text.

Not an order. Not a priority alert. A document. It scrolled up her display in plain, unencrypted language — the kind of language nobody used anymore, because plain language couldn't be walked back later. It named Meridian Consolidated. It named the seeding contracts, identical, filed under two different shells, one for each fleet. It named the first shot of the war, and who had paid for it, and why, and it included the purchase orders.

Rhee read it twice. Then she looked at the countdown, which had resumed, and at the launch bay status board, where forty pilots were supposed to be strapping in.

Only nine were.

In the ready room, Flight Chief Okafor had turned off the briefing screen and was reading the broadcast aloud from his own tablet, slowly, the way you read a will. Nobody stopped him. The squadron commander stood in the doorway with his helmet under his arm, listening to the part about the seeded colonies, and did not give the order he was being paid to give.

"Chief," Rhee said quietly, "if we don't launch—"

"Then somebody else has to be the first fleet in history to admit they read the fine print," Okafor said. "Might as well be us. We've got the best eyes on the ship."

Across the *Dawn*, in corridors and galleys and the engine sections where the real decisions always got made, crew members were doing the same arithmetic at the same time. Not mutiny — nobody raised a flag, nobody seized a bridge. They simply, politely, collectively declined. Weapons officers logged maintenance faults. Navigation reported sensor ghosts. An entire destroyer's gunnery department developed, within four minutes, a mysterious and unanimous case of food poisoning.

Admiral Voss stared at the readiness board going amber section by section and understood, with the particular clarity of a person watching weather arrive, that there was nothing to shoot. You cannot court-martial eight thousand people for hearing the truth simultaneously. There weren't enough brigs in the system.

* * *

Aboard the Veyra cruiser *Iron Mercy*, Sergeant Davit Kesh heard the broadcast while cleaning a coil rifle he had no intention of firing.

He'd grown up on a Veyra seed colony — one of the ones the Accord's attachments listed by contract number. His grandmother's village had been relocated twice for "resource optimization," and he'd enlisted because the recruiters promised the war would make the colonies safe. Reading the founding lie scroll past his display, he felt less anger than a strange, vertiginous relief, like setting down a weight he'd forgotten he was carrying.

The order came through to prepare for final assault. Kesh looked around the compartment. Twelve soldiers. Every one of them reading the same document on private displays, every one of them pretending not to see the others doing it.

Corporal Amaya broke first. She always did; it was her best quality. "So are we all just going to sit here, or—"

"We're maintaining the rifle," Kesh said. "Very thoroughly. Could take hours."

"Hours," Amaya agreed solemnly. "Maybe days. These things are complicated."

The *Mercy*'s captain locked herself on the bridge and threatened the compartment speakers for twenty minutes. Nobody disobeyed her, exactly. They simply continued to exist in a state of profound technical difficulty. By the time the truce clock expired, the Veyra fleet's combat readiness had evaporated so gently that no single log entry anywhere recorded a refusal. The war didn't end with a treaty or a surrender. It ended the way a fever breaks — thousands of small decisions arriving at once, invisible individually, undeniable together.

* * *

In the relay chamber, Sable watched the system-wide traffic counters climb into numbers that made no sense — billions of reads, millions of replies, a civilization talking to itself in a register it hadn't used in thirty years.

"They're not fighting," Ives said, staring at her display. Her voice had gone strange. "My ship just went dark on weapons. All of them. My own gunners." She laughed, unsteadily. "I spent eleven days trying to get you to the relay alive, and my superiors spent eleven days trying to get you extracted dead, and it turns out neither of us mattered. All anybody had to do was *listen*."

"That's the trick of it," Sable said. "Disclosure doesn't need force. It just needs to arrive. Everything else is logistics."

"You sound like him again."

"I'm starting to mind less."

The relay chimed — a new tone, softer. Incoming transmission, source: Meridian Station. Sable's chest tightened. He keyed it, and Oyelaran's replacement, a round-faced tech named Bex whom Sable had never met, filled the chamber with the particular cheerfulness of someone who has survived something and intends to enjoy it.

"Relay, this is Meridian actual — well, Meridian acting-actual, long story, the recyclers came back online an hour ago and honestly we're treating it as an omen. Confirming receipt of the Accord. Confirming the whole system received it. Also confirming that approximately six hundred messages have arrived asking whether the founder's clone is single, which I'm choosing to interpret as a sign of cultural recovery."

"Bex," Sable said, "tell them the clone is flattered and very tired."

"He says he's flattered and very tired! Meridian out."

Ives was looking at him with an expression Sable couldn't parse until she said, "Your extraction orders are void. Officially, I never received them. Unofficially—" she tapped her wrist display, and the order dissolved into static under her finger, "—I find that some documents are better left unread. Congratulations, Dravenmoor. Both of you."

"Thank you, Captain. For standing down."

"Don't thank me. Thank the eight thousand sailors who decided to develop food poisoning at the exact same moment." She paused at the chamber's threshold, then turned back. "For what it's worth — the argument with the door. He'd have liked it. I read his early letters, during the investigation. He argued with everything. Elevators. Weather. Once, allegedly, a vending machine, for eleven minutes, and won."

Sable smiled, and found that the smile belonged entirely to himself. "Family trait," he said again, and meant it differently now.

The relay hummed beneath his palm, broadcasting to a system that was, minute by minute, deciding to stop killing itself. Somewhere in the noise, Sable knew, the corporation's lawyers were already drafting responses, and admirals were already composing resignations, and historians were already arguing about what to call today. That was fine. That was tomorrow's work.

He settled against the relay's warm casing, opened his recorder, and began the archive's next entry: *"Day one thousand ninety-six. The war ended today. Nobody signed anything. Here's how it sounded from inside the room where the truth was kept—"*

He got four words in before the proximity alarm sang out — a single courier pod, cold-tracing toward the relay from the direction of the corporation's abandoned vaults, transponder dark, origin registry sealed.

Registered owner: **Dravenmoor, I.**

Sable stared at the contact plot, and the recorder kept running, capturing the exact sound of a man realizing his inheritance had a sequel.
