## 14. Amara's Translation

The Kestrel drifted in the shadow of a dead moon while Amara Okafor did not sleep.

Paloma found her on the third morning, or what passed for morning in the ship's slow rotation of the lights — hunched over the translation rig in the cargo bay they'd converted into her workshop, surrounded by the delicate architecture of recovered wafers. Each one sat in its cradle like an egg, filament-thin leads running from their shells to the reader array, and above them hung the holographic bloom of the message itself: a slowly turning lattice of light that looked less like language than like weather.

She hadn't changed her clothes. There was a cup of tea beside her that had gone cold enough to grow a skin, and another behind it, and another behind that, a little archaeological record of her vigil. Her eyes were red-rimmed and ferocious.

"You look," Paloma said, "like a woman who's about to tell me something I don't want to hear."

Amara laughed — a short, cracked sound with no humor in it. "I've been telling myself I don't want to hear it for two days. Sit down, Paloma."

He sat. The cargo bay smelled of solder and recycled air and, faintly, of the orange-blossom scent that clung to everything the wafers touched now, as if Ashfall were leaking into the ship one memory at a time. Sefi had named the smell before anyone else could. *That's home,* she'd said, matter-of-fact, the way children say things that rearrange the furniture of your soul.

"Show me," he said.

* * *

Amara pulled up the lattice. "Here's what we knew. The message is a plea — terraforming collapse, population failing, a people rationing their own memories to stay alive. Warden Essu asking the other colonies to remember them. That part took me weeks, and it was beautiful work, honestly. Whoever built this message understood compression the way poets understand grief." She turned the lattice with one finger. "But there was always residue. Signal underneath the signal. Harmonics that didn't fit any dialectal pattern I could find. I assumed degradation. Corrupted sectors. Forty years in transit through Anselm's smuggling route — you'd expect rot."

"But it wasn't rot."

"It wasn't rot." She zoomed in, and the lattice resolved into something denser, a second structure threaded through the first like veins through a leaf. "It was a second message. Encrypted inside the first, using the memory-rationing records themselves as the key."

Paloma felt the temperature of the room change. "Explain that slowly."

"Ashfall was dying, so they rationed memory. Every citizen got an allocation — which memories they could keep, which had to be surrendered to the archive. It sounds monstrous until you understand it was mercy. A mind holding too much burns too much water, too much oxygen, too much self. So they chose what to forget." Her voice went quiet. "And here's the thing, Paloma. The rationing records are complete. Forty years of them. Every surrender logged, every allocation justified. And buried in those justifications — in the bureaucratic language of who was allowed to keep what — is a pattern. Someone at the top of that archive used the rationing system as a vault. They hid the records nobody was supposed to survive having inside the ledger of everything everyone else was forced to lose."

The lattice turned. The vein-light pulsed, patient as a heartbeat.

"What records?" Paloma asked, though some cold and ancient part of him already knew.

"Transmission logs from the war years. Shipping manifests. And testimony — sworn testimony, timestamped, authenticated by Ashfall's own chain of custody protocols because whoever built this vault knew no one would ever believe a smuggled wafer without provenance." Amara finally looked up at him, and her eyes were wet and blazing at once. "Paloma. The war wasn't a failure of diplomacy. It was a product. Earth's cartels engineered the embargo, manufactured the incident at Meridian Station, and let three worlds bleed each other white because a divided market is a captive market. The proof names names. Dates. Contracts. It shows which families funded both sides' munitions. It shows the order that cut Ashfall off — not lost, not destroyed. *Abandoned.* Deliberately, with paperwork, with signatures."

The silence afterward had weight. Paloma heard the Kestrel breathing around them — coolant cycling, hull ticking as it shed the day's heat. Somewhere aft, Dev was singing badly to himself while he patched the fuel lines, oblivious, and the ordinary sound of it made the extraordinary thing in the room feel even more impossible.

"They didn't just abandon us," Paloma said. His voice came out strange, flat, distant, as if someone else were speaking through him. "They wrote it down. They kept receipts."

"Governments always do. They never imagine the archive will outlive the regime." Amara gestured at the lattice, and something in her face shifted — awe curdling into dread, dread hardening into resolve. "This isn't a message anymore. This is a detonator. If this reaches a public channel — if one child on Earth reads these manifests — the Concordat falls. The Councils fall. Half the enforcer command structure goes to prison and the other half goes into hiding. Everything the last forty years was built on comes down."

"And if it doesn't reach a public channel—"

"Then Ashfall dies quietly, the lie holds for another generation, and every child who dreams that dream keeps waking up screaming with nowhere to put it." She killed the hologram. The dark rushed in, soft and total. "There's no version of this where we go back to being smugglers, Paloma. I need you to understand that. The moment Reyne flagged the Kestrel, the moment Anselm died, the moment Sefi dreamed — we stopped being cargo runners and became witnesses. And witnesses only have two options."

"Testify or disappear."

"Testify or disappear."

* * *

He called the crew together in the mess, because some things should not be decided alone, and because he had learned — slowly, painfully, over nine years of carrying other people's memories and none of his own — that a secret held by one person is a stone; a secret carried together can become a keel.

Dev came in wiping grease from his hands, still humming. Sefi arrived last, trailing the particular gravity she always carried now, the dream clinging to her like static. She climbed onto the bench across from Paloma and looked at him with those steady eyes and said nothing, because she already knew why they were gathered. Children always know.

Amara laid it out. All of it. The second message, the vault, the manifests, the names. She spoke for twenty minutes without embellishment, and Paloma watched his crew the way he watched customs officers: reading the small tells, the micro-movements of fear and want.

Dev went still somewhere around the word *abandoned*. He stopped humming. He stopped moving entirely, and when Amara finished, he said, very quietly, "My brother was born on Meridian during the embargo year. Rationed protein. He came out small and stayed small." He rubbed his hands together as if the grease were suddenly unbearable. "You're telling me somebody signed off on that. Somebody billed for it."

"I'm telling you there are invoices," Amara said.

Sefi raised her hand, an absurd schoolroom gesture in the middle of the end of the world, and Paloma almost smiled. "The dream says bring it home," she said. "Warden Essu says the truth is the only seed that survives the winter. That's in the message. You translated it, Dr. Okafor. It's not just proof. It's instructions."

"It's a plea," Amara corrected gently.

"It's both," Sefi said, with the terrible certainty of eleven-year-olds and prophets. "Things that matter are always both."

Paloma stood. He walked the length of the mess and back, boots loud on the deck plating, buying time the way he'd once bought customs officers — with motion, with misdirection, with anything but stillness. Because stillness would let him feel what he was actually feeling, and what he was feeling was terror wearing the mask of purpose.

"Let me name the cost," he said finally. "Not the abstract cost. Ours. If we deliver this, Earth doesn't send Captain Reyne after us again. Reyne is a hook; hooks are for fishermen who want the fish alive. What comes next is extermination orders. They will burn Ashfall rather than let this surface — Amara, you know it, say it."

"I know it," Amara said simply. "Sterilization protocols exist. They've existed since the war. I've seen fragments of them in academic channels and told myself they were theoretical."

"They'll declare the Kestrel a biohazard. They'll quarantine every port we've ever docked at. Dev, your debts stop being financial and start being mortal. Sefi—" and here his voice caught, and he hated it, and he let it catch because hiding it would be its own kind of lie, "—Sefi, they will call you a symptom. A vector. They have words for children who carry things they shouldn't, and none of the words are kind."

The mess was silent. The recycler hummed. Outside the viewport, the dead moon turned its scarred face toward them, gray and patient, a world that had already survived its own ending.

"So," Paloma said. "We vote. Not because I outrank anyone — I don't, I'm a smuggler with a ship held together by prayer and spite. But because whatever we choose, we choose it together, and no one gets to wake up in six months and say they weren't asked." He looked around the table. "All in favor of delivering the message — the whole message, the plea and the proof, all the way to Ashfall, all the way to every ear in three systems — raise your hand."

Dev raised his first. Fast, no hesitation, jaw set. "For my brother," he said. "And because I'm tired of running cargo for people who sign things."

Amara raised hers second, slower, the way a scholar signs her name to something she knows will be disputed for centuries. "For the record," she said. "Someone has to be the record."

Two faces turned to Sefi.

"You don't have to," Paloma started.

"Yes I do." She raised her hand high, arm straight, the way you salute, the way you swear an oath. "I dreamed it first. It's mine to carry too." Then, softer, with a flicker of the child still alive under the mission: "Also I want to see the orange fields. I've been dreaming them my whole life and I've never once smelled them for real."

Paloma raised his own hand last. It felt heavier than any wafer he'd ever carried, and lighter than anything he'd held in nine years.

"Unanimous," he said. "Then we're no longer smugglers. We're couriers. And couriers deliver."

* * *

Afterward, he found Amara in the corridor outside the cargo bay, standing at the viewport with her arms crossed, watching the dark between stars.

"You hesitated," she said, without turning. "When you listed the costs. Right at Sefi's name."

"I'm allowed to hesitate."

"You're allowed. But that's not what it was." She turned then, and her expression was the one she wore when a translation refused to yield, when the dialect fought back. "You weren't afraid for her, exactly. You were afraid *of* something. Like you recognized the shape of what's coming."

Paloma leaned against the bulkhead. The metal was cool through his shirt. Nine years of sealed silence pressed against the inside of his skull, the way it always did when the conversation got close to the center of him — the locked room he'd paid a fortune in Ceres Gate back-alley clinics to wall off, the reason he'd fled Earth with a head full of fog and a heart full of running.

"The message has seven more pieces," he said instead. "Anselm's ledger says the rest are scattered along the old route. We get them all, we get them whole, we get them to Essu. That's the job. That's all the job is."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only answer I have right now, Amara." He pushed off the bulkhead and headed for the cockpit, where Dev was already plotting the jump to the next drop-point, where the work was simple and the hands had something to hold. But at the corner he stopped, because lying to a linguist is a fool's errand — they read the pauses better than the words.

"When we get to the last wafer," he said, not turning around, "there's something I have to tell you all. About me. About why I really left Earth."

Behind him, Amara was quiet for a long moment. Then: "I know. The message told me."

He turned. "What?"

"The encryption key, Paloma. The rationing records — they're keyed to a genetic marker. Ashfall lineage. The vault won't open fully for anyone else." Her eyes were gentle and merciless at once. "Whoever built that message built it for one specific courier. Someone Ashfall-born. Someone whose testimony was already in the archive, already erased, already waiting." She let that land, let it sit in the corridor between them like a third passenger. "I haven't decoded the final layer yet. But the header fields are clear enough. The message isn't addressed to the colonies, Paloma."

The lights cycled toward evening. Somewhere aft, Sefi was laughing at one of Dev's terrible jokes, and the sound carried down the corridor like something rescued from a fire.

"It's addressed to you."
