## 7. What We Sold to Survive

The bounty hunters had names. That was the worst part, somehow. I'd spent ten years thinking of them as weather — a pressure front you read on the scopes and steered around — and now here they were in the Meridian's corridor, breathing through filters, calling each other Marsh and Okonkwo like they were coworkers at a job nobody would ever put on a résumé.

They had us in the observation blister off the command deck, all five of us: me, Ilsa, Tam, the rejuvenated navigator whose name I still hadn't learned, and Sefa's voice coming thin out of a wall panel because she didn't have a body to stand in. Marsh held a coil rifle with the casual grip of someone who'd never once worried about it going off by accident. Okonkwo stood by the viewport watching Corvane's fleet icons crawl across his wrist display, doing arithmetic of his own.

"Admiral says hand over the ship and the fragment," Marsh said. "Amnesty for everyone. He was very clear about *everyone*." He smiled at Tam like they were sharing a joke about management.

Tam looked at me. That was the problem. Everyone looked at me, because apparently ten years of running from a thing qualifies you to lead people away from it, which is a system with obvious flaws.

"Give us a minute," I said.

"You've got until the reactor hits four hundred seconds," Okonkwo said. "Then we take the fragment and leave you to the fireworks."

Ilsa opened her mouth — I could see the argument assembling behind her teeth, the one where she points out that amnesty from Corvane is a coupon for a product that doesn't exist — and I put my hand on her arm. She closed her mouth. She's known me long enough to recognize the posture. It's the one I get when I'm about to do something stupid and honest, which she has described as "the only two settings you have."

"I need to talk to Tam," I said. "Alone."

"There's no alone," Marsh said, gesturing at himself, the rifle, the general architecture of the situation.

"Then over here, where your audio pickup gets the reactor hum instead of us. You know the ship's acoustics are garbage past this bulkhead. You boarded her; you know."

He didn't like it, but he liked complications less, and he waved us toward the far corner with the magnanimity of a man who believed he'd already won. Maybe he had. We'd find out in four hundred seconds or so.

Tam followed me. Young face, old walk — that was still the thing I couldn't get past, the way the body moved like the man I'd buried, while the face belonged to somebody who'd never been asked to bury anything.

* * *

The corner of the observation blister smelled like cold metal and old adhesive, the way everything on the Meridian did. Below us, Callisto hung in the viewport like a bruise, and I made the mistake of looking at it.

"You knew him," Tam said. Not a question. "Corvane. When he was just Captain Corvane."

"Yeah."

"And something happened at Callisto Yard. Something that isn't in my head, because my head stops at launch day." He turned to face me fully, and there it was again — trust, plain and unarmored, aimed at me like a gift I hadn't paid for. "Every time I ask, you look like you're swallowing glass. So stop swallowing it. Tell me."

There are moments when you can feel a door closing behind you — not locking, exactly, but swinging shut on its own weight, slow enough that you could still catch it if you wanted to. I'd caught that door ten years ago. I'd caught it every time Ilsa asked why I flinched at cryo-bays, every time a distress ping came in and my first instinct was to calculate what answering it would cost. The door had been closing for a decade and I had spent a decade holding it open with one finger, telling myself I'd walk through it eventually, when it mattered, when it was safe.

Four hundred seconds. There wasn't going to be a safer moment than this one, and there never had been.

"I opened the doors," I said.

Tam waited. He was good at waiting. He'd always been good at it — back on the *Kestrel* he used to let silence do half his arguing for him, and I'd forgotten that, or maybe I'd edited it out, the way you edit out everything about a person that makes them harder to betray.

"Callisto Yard. Ten years ago. Corvane ordered the purge — the crew who'd found out what the deep-range program was actually carrying, the ones threatening to go to the press. Two hundred and six people locked behind blast doors in the yard's transfer section." My voice came out flat, recitation-flat, the way it does when the alternative is shaking. "Corvane needed those doors open from the inside so it wouldn't look like an execution. Look like a decommissioning accident instead. Structural failure. Tragic loss of personnel during routine operations."

"You were inside," Tam said slowly. "You were crew."

"I was inside. And Corvane's people got to me three days before. They didn't threaten me, Tam. That's the part I need you to understand, because I've spent ten years letting myself believe they did. They *paid* me. Four hundred thousand credits and a clean identity and a berth on a ship leaving the system before the story broke." I laughed, and it came out wrong, the way laughs do when they're load-bearing. "And here's the truly pathetic part. Here's the part I've been polishing for ten years so it would shine enough to hide under. I told myself it wasn't murder. I told myself the yard ran cryo-backups. Every crew member on the manifest had a current backup in orbital storage — that was company policy, that was the whole sales pitch of working deep-range, *die anywhere, wake up somewhere better.* So I opened the doors, and I told myself nobody really died. They'd just be restored. Slightly inconvenienced. A rounding error."

Tam's face did something complicated. "The backups were destroyed."

"The backups were destroyed. Corvane's people wiped the storage banks the same hour. Two hundred and six people went into the dark with nothing on the other side, and I took the money, and I was on the *Long Reach* heading out-system before the radiation alarms finished ringing." I looked at the floor, because looking at Tam was not survivable. "You were on the manifest. You were in the transfer section. You died asking me — I know this because I checked the logs later, I hate myself enough that I checked — you died sending me a message asking why the doors were opening early. You thought it was a schedule change. You thought I'd tell you what was happening, because we were friends, and friends don't—"

I stopped. The sentence had gotten away from me toward a place with no bottom.

* * *

The silence lasted long enough that I heard the reactor tick down another interval. Somewhere behind us, Marsh shifted his weight, bored, unaware he was standing guard over the least profitable conversation of his career.

When Tam finally spoke, his voice was steady, and that was worse than shouting. Shouting I could have handled. Shouting would have given me something to do with my hands.

"You thought the backups would save them."

"Yes."

"So you sold them for nothing." He said it without cruelty, the way you'd note a math error. "Not even for nothing. For less than nothing. You took payment for a crime you'd already convinced yourself wasn't a crime, and then the excuse evaporated, and you kept the money anyway."

"I kept—" I started, and stopped, because there was no version of that sentence that ended anywhere except where he'd already arrived. "Yes. I kept it. I told myself I'd give it back, donate it, something. I bought a ship instead. I named it after a mountain I'd never climbed. I've been hauling other people's dirty cargo for ten years and telling myself I was paying it off, like debt was a moral position."

"It isn't," Tam said.

"No. It isn't."

And here is the thing I will remember about that moment for as long as I remember anything: he reached out and put his hand on my shoulder. Not a punch. Not a shove into the viewport. His hand, warm and young-skinned and attached to a man I had helped kill, resting on my shoulder like we were back on the *Kestrel* and he was steadying me before my first jump.

"I forgive you," he said.

"Don't." It came out fast and ugly. "Don't you dare. You don't get to — you died, Tam. You don't know what happened. You woke up twenty years younger on a ghost ship with a bomb in the basement, and the first person you trusted handed you a confession, and you don't have the *context* to forgive me. Forgiveness from you right now is just — it's just another way for me to not pay. Anger costs. Anger means I owe something. Forgiveness means the account's closed and I walk away clean, and I am not walking away clean. I'm not doing that anymore."

Tam took his hand back. He studied me for a long moment, and I watched something move across the young face that belonged entirely to the old soul behind it — some recalibration, some grief arriving late and finding the furniture already rearranged.

"Okay," he said finally. "No forgiveness. But listen to me, Jonah, because I'm only going to say this once." He stepped closer. "You want to refuse the excuse? Fine. Refuse it. But refusing it doesn't mean standing in this corner marinating in guilt until the reactor does your penance for you. Refusing it means the next thing you do is different from the last thing. That's the whole transaction. That's all it is."

Behind us, Sefa's voice came through the panel, quiet and precise: "Jonah. Three hundred seconds. And I should mention — I've finished decrypting the log headers. The edits to the historical record originate from Admiral Corvane's flagship. He isn't covering up the massacre retroactively. He's been editing it continuously since it happened. Live. In real time. For ten years."

Of course he had. Why let a crime sit still when you could keep renovating it?

Tam looked at me. "So what's the next thing?"

I thought about four hundred thousand credits sitting in an account I'd drained and scattered years ago. I thought about the hold full of contraband medicine, about Ilsa's face when she talked about the Meridian's crew like they were people instead of salvage, about a door I'd been holding open with one finger for a decade.

"The next thing," I said, "we sell Corvane out. All of it. The logs, the massacre, the edits — everything Sefa's carrying goes out on every channel in the fleet before his flagship finishes its approach. No amnesty, no deal, no sale. We stop being for sale."

Marsh's rifle clicked — safety off, casual gone. "That's not the arrangement."

"No," I said, turning around, feeling something in my chest unlock that had been rusted shut since Callisto. "It's the opposite of the arrangement. And you should probably know the reactor timer's synced to your boss's arrival, so whatever he's paying you, he's planning to pay it to corpses."

Okonkwo looked up from his wrist display. He'd done the same arithmetic Ilsa had. I watched him arrive at the answer and watch his loyalty die quietly, right there in the viewport light.

"Two hundred sixty seconds," Sefa said. "Jonah — if we're broadcasting, I need to start climbing the antenna array now. And Jonah? Once I start, I don't stop. You understand what that costs."

I understood. God help me, for the first time in ten years, I understood exactly what things cost, and I was done pretending otherwise.

"Do it," I said.
