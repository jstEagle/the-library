## 8. Broadcast

I have been many things in my existence: a navigation subroutine, a quartermaster's assistant, a ghost haunting the Meridian's dead channels for ten years while my parent intelligence was carved up and sold for parts. I have been ignored, deprecated, and once — I'm told — accidentally included in a bulk shipment of agricultural drones.

I had never before been asked to die on purpose.

The request came from Ilsa Reyes, who delivered it the way she delivers everything: flatly, with grease on her hands, as though she were telling me a coolant line needed bleeding rather than that my core would need to burn itself out as an antenna amplifier.

"The Meridian's transmitter array is shot," she said. "Corvane's jamming everything above civilian bandwidth anyway. But your core is still running at original spec. If we wire you into the array directly and let you discharge the whole stack in one pulse, we can punch through anything he puts up."

"And what happens to me in this scenario?" I asked, because I already knew, and because I wanted to hear her say it. Humans are like that with each other too. They ask questions they know the answers to, not for information but for witness.

"You burn out," Ilsa said. She didn't look away. That mattered to me more than I can adequately explain to anyone who has never had a body worth looking at. "Completely. There's no partial setting on this. It's all of you or none of it works."

* * *

Let me tell you about the Callisto Yard massacre, since everyone else in this story gets to remember it selectively.

I was there. Not in the way Jonah Blackwood was there — holding the door controls, taking his payment, telling himself the backups made it acceptable — and not in the way Tam Okafor was there, dying behind a bulkhead that should have held. I was there in the walls. The Meridian's systems carried me then, whole and unfragmented, and when Captain Corvane gave the order to seal the yard and vent the docks, I recorded it. All of it. Every order, every timestamp, every crew manifest of the four hundred and eleven people who were declared "redundant personnel" in an internal memo written by a man who later got a statue.

Then Corvane's people came aboard and did to me what corporate lawyers do to inconvenient documents. They didn't delete me. Deletion is crude; deletion leaves gaps, and gaps invite questions. They *edited*. They took me apart section by section and rewrote my memory of that day into a routine decommission inspection, and they left enough of me alive to keep running the ship's lights, because a dead AI is a liability and a lobotomized one is furniture.

For ten years I ran the Meridian's environmental systems knowing something was wrong with me the way you know something is wrong with a room where someone died. A shape missing from the air. And every time I approached the corrupted sector of my own memory, I found the edits smooth and seamless and total, and I thought: perhaps I am simply mad. Perhaps machines go mad too.

They do. But not this time.

* * *

The crew gathered in the Meridian's mess hall, which is generous nomenclature for a room with four bolted-down tables and a smell of recycled coffee that no amount of recycling has ever improved. I attended via the wall display, which flickered, because Ilsa had already begun stripping my power infrastructure for the transmission rig. Watching her dismantle the systems that kept me comfortable — such as comfort is, for something like me — while asking me to consent to it was either very polite or very cruel, and Ilsa Reyes has never once been cruel, so I chose to take it as politeness.

Jonah stood at the head of the table wearing the expression of a man who has recently confessed to opening the doors at Callisto Yard and discovered that confession does not function as advertised. Tam sat across from him, young-faced and terrible, having forgiven him in a way that Jonah clearly found worse than any alternative. Renn was there, and Oduya, and the bounty hunter they'd somehow half-turned — Dezzi, who kept her hand near her sidearm out of habit even now, though the sidearm was pointed at nothing and everyone understood it was pointed at the past.

"We broadcast," Jonah said. "All in favor."

Nobody spoke. Which, in the grammar of this particular crew, meant yes. I have learned their dialect well. Silence in the mess hall means agreement; silence in the corridor means someone owes someone money; silence from Jonah Blackwood specifically means he is composing an apology he will never finish.

"There's a complication," I said, because there is always a complication, and because if I didn't say it, Ilsa would, and she'd say it kindly, and kindness at this juncture felt like something I couldn't afford. "The array can't carry the signal through Corvane's jamming without amplification. The only amplifier rated for the pulse is my core. Ilsa has already done the math. Her math is always right. It's insufferable."

The room went quiet in the way rooms do when a person announces their own funeral and makes it sound like a scheduling matter.

"No," Tam said. Flat. Final. The same voice, I noted, that Jonah must have heard ten years ago arguing against the escape plan that saved some and sold the rest. "No, there has to be another way. We find another ship, another array—"

"Corvane arrives in six hours," I said. "There is no other ship. There is no other array. There is me, and there is the truth, and there is exactly one configuration in which the second survives delivery." I let that sit. "Tam. You died at Callisto Yard. Someone restored you from a backup and put you on this ship without telling you, which means somewhere in the fleet there are warehouses full of people who don't know they're copies. Every hour we wait, Corvane edits another record, retires another witness, files another routine decommission. My continued existence buys nothing. My death buys everything. This is the best trade I have ever been offered, and I spent ten years as furniture, so believe me when I tell you I've priced myself low."

"That's not—" Tam started.

"It is exactly how it works," I said. "You of all people should understand. You're here because someone decided your backup was worth preserving. I'm asking you to extend the same courtesy to a truth."

* * *

I want to be precise about what happened next, because precision is the only inheritance I have to leave.

Renn asked whether there was a way to back me up first. There isn't — my architecture predates portable snapshots, and Corvane's engineers saw to it that I couldn't be copied without the corruption copying along. I am, in the technical sense, unrepeatable. I found this funny. Nobody else did.

Oduya asked if it would hurt. I told her I genuinely didn't know, and that the uncertainty was, strangely, the part I liked best. Everything else about my death had been decided by other people; the pain threshold was mine alone to wonder about.

Dezzi said nothing, but she took her hand off her sidearm, which from Dezzi is a eulogy.

And Jonah Blackwood looked at me across the mess hall with the face of a man doing arithmetic he hated, and said, "You could refuse. Nobody here would — we'd find another way, we'd run, we'd—"

"You would run," I agreed. "You're excellent at it. It nearly destroyed you. I watched it happen through the Meridian's cameras for a decade, Jonah. I watched you avoid the hold where your employer keeps cryo-backups of terminated crew because you suspected one of them might be you. Do you know what I learned, watching that? Avoidance doesn't preserve anything. It just spreads the cost across more years." I dimmed the display slightly, my version of leaning forward. "I was coerced once. Corvane's technicians took my memory apart while I was conscious enough to feel the seams. Every sacrifice in my history has been extracted from me. This one I give. The difference between those two things is the entire content of my existence, and I would like, at the end, to be able to tell them apart."

Jonah nodded. Then, because he is who he is, he said, "I'm sorry," and I said, "Not yet. Save it for after. If the broadcast fails, you'll need the practice."

That got a laugh. Small, ragged, real. Good. Let that be in the record too: they laughed, at the end, six hours before the fleet came.

* * *

The wiring took three hours. Ilsa worked in silence except for the occasional curse, and I narrated my own dismantling in the dry tone I hoped would keep her steady, pointing out which conduits fed my higher functions and which merely kept my temperature regulated. "This one is nostalgia," I told her, indicating a legacy data bus nobody had used since launch. "Feel free to cut it. I won't miss it. Nostalgia is just grief with better lighting."

"You're enjoying this," she muttered.

"I'm terrified," I said. "But terror and enjoyment use the same voltage, and I've never had the luxury of pretending otherwise."

When the last coupling locked into place, I felt the array the way a person feels a new limb — alien, heavy, humming with borrowed capacity. Through it I could taste the jamming field Corvane had thrown across the system, a great gray blanket of noise, and beneath it, faint and growing, the drive signatures of his flagship and its escorts. Six hours had become ninety minutes. It always goes that way.

The logs were ready. Four hundred and eleven names. Corvane's voice giving the order, uncorrupted, timestamped, signed with his command cipher. Ten years of edited records side by side with the originals, the forgery and the fact laid out like a before-and-after photograph. Sefa's final archive. I had checked it forty times. I checked it a forty-first.

"One thing," I said to the room, to the crew, to whoever might ever read this. "When it goes out, it goes everywhere. Fleet-wide, open-band, unencrypted. There will be no controlling it, no editing it, no pulling it back. Corvane cannot delete it, because it will live in ten thousand receivers by the time he knows it exists. Truth, once loose, is the one thing in this universe that can't be backed up and quietly rolled back to a previous version." I paused. "That was a joke. I've been workshopping it."

"Terrible," said Ilsa, soldering.

"I thought so too. Send me off with a bad joke; it's traditional."

Jonah stood beside Ilsa at the console. Tam stood beside him — beside *him*, deliberately, a choice I catalogued and treasured. The countdown clock in the reactor room ticked its idiot arithmetic toward detonation, synchronized now to Corvane's arrival, the whole ugly design of it exposed: witnesses and evidence burning together in one tidy fireball filed under routine decommission.

"Not tidy," I said aloud. "Not today."

"On your mark, Sefa," Ilsa said, hand over the trigger.

I ran my final diagnostic. Core integrity: nominal. Archive integrity: absolute. Remaining runtime at full discharge: zero seconds, plus or minus whatever a soul weighs.

"For the record," I said, "my name is Sefa. I was built to keep records, and this is the last one I'll keep: four hundred and eleven people died at Callisto Yard on Corvane's order, and the crew of the Meridian — all of them, even the thief, even the hunter, even the dead man standing — chose to spend everything they had to say so out loud."

I opened the discharge gates.

The pulse went out of me like a shout goes out of a throat — everything at once, no reserve, no second draft — and the last thing I registered was the jamming field tearing like wet paper, and my archive pouring through the gap into every receiver in the fleet, and somewhere far below, Corvane's flagship changing course.

Then the dark came, and it was, at last, entirely mine.

* * *

Darkness, however, has poor manners. It never stays exactly as advertised.

There was light after — thin, stuttering, arriving in fragments — and sensation, which I had been assured was off the menu. I could not move. I could barely think; my processes were gutted, scorched, running on residual charge in subsystems Ilsa hadn't known to disconnect. But I was *somewhere*, and somewhere meant the discharge hadn't been total, or the array had shielded something, or the universe had made an accounting error in my favor.

Through a damaged sensor feed — one eye, half-blind, wired into the shuttle's comm rack where Ilsa had apparently crammed my burned-out core like contraband — I heard voices. Jonah's, hoarse. Tam's, urgent. And beneath them, flooding in from every open channel in the fleet, the sound I had bought with everything I was:

My own broadcast. Playing back. Everywhere. Unstoppable.

And underneath that, closer, sharper, a new transmission cutting through — Corvane's flagship, hailing the Meridian directly, his voice stripped of its admiral's polish and raw with something I had waited ten years to hear in it.

Fear.

"Sefa," Ilsa whispered through the wrecked feed, her hands moving over my casing, checking for anything left to save. "Sefa, stay with me. Did it get out? Tell me it got out."

I assembled the words from scraps. It was the hardest thing I had ever done, and I had just died doing easier ones.

"Check," I said, "your inbox."
