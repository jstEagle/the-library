## 6. What Sefa Buried

There are two kinds of liars in this world, and I have been both.

The first kind lies to get something. The second kind lies to keep something from being taken away. The second kind is worse, because they can do it for forty years and call it love.

I was sorting seed stock when the shuttle came down over the eastern ridge — military grey, no colony markings, the kind of polite violence that descends on places like ours maybe once a generation. I watched it through the archive window with a cup of tea going cold in my hand, and I thought: *so it's finally come to collect.*

But I'm getting ahead of myself. Old people do that. We live so much in the past that the present has to shout to be heard over it, and lately the past has been shouting back.

Let me tell you about the night we loaded the Meridian.

* * *

Forty-one years ago, give or take a season, I was thirty years old and the most important thing about me was that I could drive a loader without killing anyone. Kepler's Rest wasn't a colony yet. It was a staging ground — a place where things were moved from one silence to another, and nobody asked what the things were. That was the whole genius of it. A port too small to matter, staffed by people too tired to care, running on a charter so vague you could drive a warship through its loopholes. Which, as it turned out, was the plan.

The Meridian came in on a Tuesday. I remember it was Tuesday because the mess hall served protein loaf on Tuesdays and Ivo Ren complained about it, loudly, to anyone within earshot, which at that hour was me and a forklift.

She didn't look like a courier. Couriers, in my imagination, were sleek and fast and full of themselves. She looked like someone's tired aunt — mid-forties, cropped grey hair, a jacket with too many pockets, and eyes that did arithmetic on everything they landed on. Including me. Especially me.

"You're the loader," she said. Not a question.

"I'm the loader."

"Good. Loaders don't ask questions." She handed me a crate, light for its size, warm along one edge like something inside had been running all day. "This one goes aboard last. It goes in the cabin, not the hold. If anyone official asks, it's agricultural samples."

"It *is* agricultural samples," I said, reading the manifest chip she'd slapped on it. "That's clever. You made it true."

"I make everything true," she said. "That's my job. Making true things survive long enough to matter — that's the hard part."

I should have asked questions then. I want that on the record, wherever records go when everyone who kept them is dead: I *should have asked.* But the war was three years old by then, and we'd all learned the survival arithmetic of the outer colonies. Questions cost. Silence paid. Silence paid in rations, in permits, in the continued good health of everyone whose name you knew.

So I drove the crate to the Meridian's cabin, and Ivo Ren walked beside it the whole way, and we talked about protein loaf.

* * *

Here is what I know now, assembled across four decades from fragments I pretended not to collect: Ivo Ren was a courier of evidence. Not contraband, not weapons, not people — though she'd carried all three at various points, she told me once, in the tone of someone listing old jobs. Evidence. Testimony. Recordings. The raw, unglamorous documentation of what powerful people do when they think no one is keeping receipts.

And there was one document she cared about more than the rest. One she'd nearly died for twice.

You've heard of the Callas Station atrocity. Everyone has. It's the sentence the war begins with, in every schoolbook on both sides: *the enemy burned Callas Station, and so the war began.* Four thousand dead. Children among them. I was twenty-seven when it happened, and I remember exactly where I was standing, and I remember deciding, right there, that the other side was no longer made of people.

Except it hadn't been the other side.

It had been us. Our government. Our fleet. A station full of our own colonists, sacrificed on schedule, filmed from three angles, blamed on the enemy before the smoke cleared. Because a war needs a beginning, and beginnings need to be unbearable, and nothing is more unbearable than your own children burning.

Ivo had the footage. Ivo had the internal transmissions, the timing orders, the names. And Ivo had a plan to get it off-world and into the hands of every neutral broadcaster in the system, and the only ship leaving quietly enough was the Meridian, out of a port too small to matter.

They found out, of course. Powerful people always find out. The question was never whether they'd stop her. The question was how loudly.

* * *

Launch night. I was on the pad crew, which meant I was one of eleven people close enough to watch the Meridian die.

I want to describe it honestly, because honesty is the only currency I have left and I've been hoarding it like a miser. The launch was clean. Beautiful, even — that column of fire, the shudder in the ground, the crowd of us squinting up like idiots at a miracle. The Meridian cleared the tower. Cleared the atmosphere gate. Made it ninety seconds into open sky.

Then the secondary burn fired wrong. Not malfunctioned — *fired wrong*, in a pattern I would spend years telling myself I'd imagined, until I finally admitted I hadn't. A scuttle charge dressed up as an engine failure. The ship came apart in a bloom of white, and the debris fell for a long time, glittering, and the port director took off his cap, and we stood in silence for the seven souls officially listed aboard.

Seven souls. Officially.

Ivo Ren was not on the manifest. Her name existed nowhere in our records — I checked, later, obsessively, the way you check a wound to see if it's still bleeding. She'd boarded under a freight designation. The woman I'd talked to about protein loaf, the woman who'd trusted a tired loader with the last copy of the truth, went up with the ship or didn't, and either way the world was told nothing, because the world was told there was nothing to tell.

The crate of agricultural samples burned with her. So I believed, for a very long time.

* * *

Do you know what guilt looks like after forty years? It doesn't look like anything dramatic. It looks like diligence. It looks like volunteering for every archive shift, cataloguing every record, sealing every file so tightly that even you can't reopen it without ceremony. It looks like becoming the colony's memory-keeper precisely so you can control what gets remembered. People call me the archivist. They say it warmly. *Old Sefa remembers everything.*

Yes. That's the problem.

I built this colony's paper history the way you build a wall around a grave — stone by stone, sincerely, telling yourself it's a monument. Every year on the anniversary of the Meridian's loss, I entered a maintenance note in the beacon log. Routine systems check. Nothing anomalous. Forty-one entries, each one a small flat lie, each one signed with my own hand. It was penance of a sort. It was also camouflage. If I wrote the silence often enough, I'd never have to hear what was underneath it.

Because here's the thing I've never said aloud, not to anyone, not in forty-one years:

The beacon received a transmission the night the Meridian died. Before the debris finished falling. A short burst, tight-beam, from somewhere above the crash corridor — automated, compressed, addressed to the beacon core with a handshake code I did not have and was not given. The system flagged it. I read the flag. And I buried the flag so deep in the archive that I convinced myself, over time, that I had dreamed it.

She got off the ship. That's what the transmission meant. Somehow — escape pod, transfer, a trick I'll never know — Ivo Ren survived the scuttling of the Meridian, and her first act in the wreckage of her own murder was to send her cargo home to the one place they'd never look: a forgotten beacon on a colony that didn't exist yet, guarded by a loader who didn't ask questions.

She trusted me. That's the part that calcifies. Not the fear, not even the shame — the *trust*. She looked at a thirty-year-old with protein loaf on their shirt and decided I was worth betting the truth on. And I repaid it by spending my whole life standing guard over her message while pretending it wasn't there, because opening it meant admitting I'd helped load the coffin.

Duty, I called it. Keeping the seal intact. Protecting the colony. There are very good words for cowardice. I've used all of them.

* * *

The girl came by yesterday. Orsen — the gardener, the beacon-tender, the one person on this rock who visits me without wanting anything except my terrible tea. They sat in the archive doorway with that look they get, the one that means a thought has been growing in them like one of their seedlings, patient and unstoppable.

"Sefa," they said. "The beacon printed something. A manifest. From the Meridian."

The tea cup in my hand was ceramic and forty years old and I am proud to say I did not drop it. "Old systems hiccup," I said. "Corrupted buffers. Ghost data."

"It had a passenger list." Their eyes stayed on me, steady, the way you look at someone when you already know the answer and are giving them one last chance to say it themselves. "One name isn't in any registry. Ivo Ren."

And there it was. Forty-one years of stone walls, and a name walked straight through them wearing a gardener's coveralls.

I told them to forget it. I heard my own voice do it — gentle, reasonable, the voice of a tired old friend — and I despised every syllable, because it was the same voice the port director used on us the week after the crash, the voice that says *there's nothing here, go home, be small.* I have spent my life hating that voice and here I was, wearing it like a borrowed coat.

Orsen left. Politely. Which is worse than arguing, with Orsen. Politeness means they're going to go do it anyway.

And now the military shuttle sits on the eastern pad, and men with clean boots ask polite questions about beacon transmissions, and I sit in my archive surrounded by forty years of carefully buried truth, doing the arithmetic I've been avoiding since Tuesday, forty-one years ago.

If the beacon spoke, then the seal is broken. If the seal is broken, then Ivo's message is awake. And if Ivo's message is awake, then everything I buried — the ship, the lie, the war's first sentence rewritten in our own handwriting — is coming up through the soil like something planted long ago by a woman who complained about protein loaf and trusted the wrong tired loader.

I know where the handshake code is. I've known for forty-one years. She gave it to me the night we loaded the crate, folded into a scrap of manifest paper I have never once allowed myself to reread.

I think it's time I opened it.
