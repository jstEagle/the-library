## 12. Sabotage in the Greenhouse

The alarm woke me at 0340 with the gentle insistence of a fire drill conducted by someone who hated me personally. Not the reactor countdown alarm — that one had its own voice now, a low pulse beneath everything like a second heartbeat I couldn't get rid of. This was the humidity sensor in Bay Four, screaming because the humidity had stopped existing.

I was out of my bunk and down the root-tunnel stairs before my brain finished booting. That's the thing about a garden you've raised for eleven years: you don't think when it cries out. You just go.

Bay Four's outer door stood open to the night air of Kepler's Rest — thin, cold, wrong, the atmosphere we'd spent a generation thickening still too hungry to forgive a mistake like this. Frost glittered on the first three rows of seedling trays. Beyond them, where the warmth had held, the plants were already wilting in that terrible soft way, leaves folding like hands giving up.

A third of the garden. Maybe more. The cold had come through the open door in a slow tide while everyone slept, and the trays nearest the breach were dead or dying — the lumen-rice, the frost-ferns Sefa had smuggled from the old research station, the whole east bank of nitrogen-fixers we'd been coaxing toward soil that could someday hold an orchard.

I stood there in my thermal underlayer, breath fogging, and did the arithmetic every gardener does at moments like this. What can be saved. What can't. What was taken.

That last column was short. One item.

The core's backup key — a palm-sized cylinder of etched crystal that Dara and I had split custody of after the council vote, her half in the archive safe, mine taped under the germination bench where I told myself no one would ever look because no one would ever be that kind of monster.

My half was gone. The tape was still there, neatly peeled back and re-stuck, which somehow made it worse. Whoever took it hadn't torn through. They'd been careful. Careful is what family looks like when it's lying to you.

* * *

By dawn the whole colony was in Bay Four, and I want to tell you they were angry, but anger requires a target and we didn't have one yet. So instead there was that particular silence of people counting losses together — Sefa moving down the rows with her sleeves pushed up, pressing her thumb to stems the way she used to check the Meridian's cargo seals back when, according to her, she loaded grain and spare parts and nothing else, nothing else, don't ask again, Orsen.

Dara arrived last, hair uncombed, boots mismatched, and the sight of her hit me somewhere below the ribs in a way I wasn't ready to examine.

"Three forty," she said. "The alarm went off at three forty?"

"Three thirty-eight."

"And you came straight down?"

"I always come straight down."

"I know." She looked at the frost line, the wilted east bank, the open door. Her face did something complicated and then settled into the version of itself she showed the world, which was competent and slightly amused and hiding exactly nothing, which is what I'd believed about her for nineteen years. "Whoever it was knew the door code."

"The door code is six people," I said. "Me, you, Sefa, Councilor Voss, Tam, and the Okafor twins, who are nine."

"So it's one of four adults, a nine-year-old prodigy, or somebody who tortured it out of one of us." She crouched by the germination bench, and I watched her look at the spot where the key had lived. Watched her look at it maybe two seconds longer than shock requires. "Orsen. Your half's gone."

"Yes."

"Where's mine? In the archive—"

"In the archive. I checked it on my way past. Untouched."

She nodded slowly. "So they wanted one key. Just enough to—" She stopped. Everyone stops mid-sentence these days; the countdown has taught us all to hear our own sentences as things that might not finish. "Enough to read the message without us. Or enough to hand Adeyemi the whole thing wrapped in a bow."

"That would be my guess."

Sefa appeared at my shoulder, quiet as compost. "The door log," she said. "It'll show whose code opened it."

"It'll show whatever the person who wiped it wants it to show," Dara said. "You think someone smart enough to do this left their name in the log?"

"You'd be surprised how often guilt forgets to clean up after itself," Sefa said, and looked at Dara when she said it, just for a moment, just long enough, and I told myself I'd imagined the angle of it.

* * *

We spent the day triaging. There's a mercy in triage: it gives your hands a job so your mind has to stand in line. I moved the salvageable trays into Bay Two, wrapped the borderline cases in emergency foil, and let the dead stay dead for now because grief needs a scheduled appointment or it takes over the calendar.

Tam found the tool marks around the door's manual override at noon. Pried, not forced — eased open with something flat and patient, then resealed. The frost damage pattern told its own story too: the door had been opened for hours, not minutes. Someone had come in, taken the key, and *left the door open*, which meant either cruelty or cover. A broken window gets blamed on burglars. An open door gets blamed on carelessness, on the colony itself, on *me* — the gardener who forgot to latch his own greenhouse. It was almost elegant.

Almost. Because whoever did it also had to know I checked the door seal every night at 2300, part of the same ritual as talking to the seedlings. Which meant they either didn't know me as well as they thought, or they knew me perfectly and wanted me standing in this exact spot, holding this exact suspicion, aiming it at this exact person.

I thought about that while watering trays that no longer needed water.

Here's the ugly ledger I kept in my head all afternoon:

Item one: Dara voted against sending the message. Loudly. At the council meeting she'd stood up with her fists on the table and said, "We are not a monument, we're four hundred people who breathe filtered air," and the room had split down the middle like ice on a pond, and I had looked at my best friend across that crack and felt the first cold draft of a world where she and I stood on different sides of it.

Item two: Dara's brother, Pell, has been a POW for six years. POW status in a war that just got offered a ceasefire it hasn't decided to accept yet. And Commander Adeyemi does not strike me as a woman who wastes leverage.

Item three: When Dara looked at the empty spot under the germination bench, she looked at it like a person confirming something she already knew.

Item four — and this is the one I keep trying to delete and it keeps restoring itself — item four is that in nineteen years, Dara Okonkwo has never once lied to me, and I have built entire seasons on that foundation. But I'd never given her a reason to lie before. People are only as honest as their circumstances allow. I know that now. I learned it from a government that staged an atrocity and called it history.

* * *

I found her at the water reclaimers at dusk, doing the evening flush, and I didn't plan what I said next, which is unlike me, because planning is most of what I am.

"Pell," I said.

Her hands stilled on the valve. Didn't slip. Stilled — the controlled stop of someone who'd rehearsed this. "What about him?"

"When did you last hear from him?"

"Eight months ago. Standard POW letter, three lines, censored to confetti. You know this. You sat with me when it came."

"I know." I turned the valve myself, gently, taking the task from her hands the way you take a lit match from a child. "Dara. If someone promised you he'd come home — if someone with a warship and a uniform and a very reasonable voice told you that one small crystal could buy your brother's life — what would you say to them?"

The silence lasted four seconds. On Kepler's Rest, four seconds of silence between Dara and me is a geological era.

"I'd say," she said finally, and her voice was level in the way a frozen lake is level, "that I'd want to know why my best friend was asking. And then I'd ask him directly, to his face, instead of constructing little interrogations at the water reclaimers like some amateur detective in one of Sefa's awful novels."

"Dara—"

"Did I take the key, Orsen?" She stepped close. Close enough that I could see the exhaustion underneath the anger, the six-years-deep bruise of Pell-shaped absence. "Look at me and ask me that. Straight. No valves, no frost patterns, no door codes. Ask me."

And here is the confession I'll carry longer than any other scar from this whole affair: I couldn't. I stood in front of my closest friend, the woman who taught me to graft cuttings, who held my head during the fever year, who named half the cultivars in Bay Three after jokes only the two of us remember — and I could not make my mouth form the question, because some traitorous chamber of my heart had already answered it.

She saw that. Of course she saw that. She's known me since we were both nobody, and you can't hide a withheld question from someone who's catalogued every face you own.

"Okay," she said quietly. "Okay. You didn't ask. Noted." She pulled her hands free of the valve, wiped them on her thighs, and walked away down the tunnel without looking back, and the sound of her boots on the metal grating was the loneliest sound the colony has ever made, and I have heard the beacon transmit a dead courier's voice into a war.

* * *

Sefa found me later in Bay Four, sitting on the floor among the foil-wrapped survivors like a man in a lifeboat counting the living.

"You suspect her," Sefa said. Not a question.

"I suspect the situation. There's a difference."

"There isn't, at your age. At mine there barely is either." She lowered herself down beside me with the complaint of joints that predate the colony charter, which, I now understood, they literally do. "Let me tell you something about suspicion, gardener. It's a seed like anything else. Plant it in fear, water it daily, and it grows whether or not it's true. I spent forty years growing one. Want to see the harvest? The harvest is a scuttled ship and a name nobody says."

"Sefa—"

"Adeyemi doesn't need your key," Sefa said. "Think. She has soldiers, scanners, a siege of this tower that grows tighter by the hour. If she wanted the backup key she'd have taken both halves the first night. One key, taken gently, blamed on the colony's own division — that's not theft. That's *gardening*. Someone is planting suspicion in you and letting the cold in through the door."

I stared at the frost-killed east bank. "Then who?"

"Someone who knows you check the door at 2300. Someone who knows you'd never ask Dara to her face. Someone who watched the council vote and mapped exactly which crack to widen." Sefa's old eyes were steady. "Someone who's been inside our walls long enough to know that the fastest way to destroy a family isn't to break its door. It's to make each member certain another one opened it."

The countdown pulsed beneath us — fifty-one hours now, the number arriving in my head unbidden, the way it does, the way it will until it doesn't. Fifty-one hours, a third of my garden dead, my best friend walking away from me in the dark, and a thief among six names who may not exist at all.

And then Tam came running down the root tunnel with a slate in her hand and her nine-year-old face gone grey, and said the words that ended the argument forever:

"The archive safe's been opened. Dara's half of the key is gone too. And the log says—" she swallowed, "—the log says it was opened with *your* code, Orsen. Timestamped while you were sitting right here."

I looked at Sefa. Sefa looked at me.

Somebody wasn't just stealing the truth anymore. Somebody was building a frame around it, plank by plank, and I had just handed them the nails.
