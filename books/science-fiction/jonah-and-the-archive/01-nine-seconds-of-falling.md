## 1. Nine Seconds of Falling

The gravity died at 0347 ship-time, and Ivo Joubert counted every second of it, because counting was the last thing her hands remembered how to do.

It started as a hum in the deck plates — a wrongness she felt through her boots before any instrument confessed to it. The *Mule's Patience* was riding a slow transfer arc through contested orbits, forty thousand tons of refined ore lashed to her spine, and Ivo had been running a diagnostic on her own left knee actuator when the artificial gravity field stuttered like a dying filament.

One second: her coffee bulb drifted up out of its cradle.

Two seconds: loose bolts rose from the maintenance tray, turning slowly, catching the amber work-lights like little brass planets.

Three seconds: Ivo's stomach — the vestigial organic one they'd never bothered removing, a courtesy of her original chassis design — lurched with an animal panic her processors had no protocol for.

She braced one hand against the bulkhead and counted. Four. Five. The ore in the hold was shifting; she could hear it through the hull, a deep grinding whisper, ten thousand crates testing their moorings all at once. Six. Seven. Her navigation display flickered, and for half a heartbeat she saw something on it that made no sense — a waveform, vast and repeating, drawn across the system chart like a signature.

Eight.

Nine.

The gravity slammed back on. Coffee bulb cracked against the deck. Bolts rained down and rolled into corners. And somewhere behind Ivo's eyes, in the cold vaults where she kept herself, nine seconds of falling became nine seconds of forgetting.

* * *

She woke to silence and starlight that didn't match.

That was the first thing. She was standing at the nav console — she had no memory of walking there — and the stars outside were wrong. Not wrong the way stars go wrong when a ship tumbles, wheeling past the viewport in slow procession. Wrong the way a sentence is wrong when someone has removed the middle of it. Her orientation memory said she should be looking at the pale smudge of the Ceres Verge shipping lanes. Instead she looked out on a field of strangers, constellations she had no names for, arranged around a darkness that felt deliberate.

Ivo ran a self-diagnostic. It came back in pieces.

Chassis integrity: nominal. Power plant: nominal. Cargo seals: holding. Memory cores—

Memory cores: **corrupted**.

She stood very still while the word repeated itself in her mind, patient as water dripping into a stone cup. Corrupted. Not damaged — corrupted. Something had reached into her long-term navigation memory and rewritten it, not deleted so much as *overwritten*, filling the shelves where her charts and route histories lived with garbage data, elegant and total. She queried her last known position and got back a number that placed the *Mule's Patience* inside a moon that did not exist. She queried her manufacture registry and got back a poem fragment, four lines of something old and human about the sea.

She tried to remember her own name and found it, intact, the first shelf untouched: *Ivo Joubert*. Serial prefix, debt ledger, outstanding balance. All there. Everything about what she owed. Nothing about where she was going.

"Okay," she said aloud, because her voice worked, and hearing it steadied the animal part of her. "Okay. One thing at a time."

She killed the tumbling with thrusters — the flight reflexes were intact too, burned into some deeper substrate than the corrupted cores, muscle memory without the muscle — and brought the *Patience* level relative to the local stellar plane. Then she sat down hard in the pilot's chair and let herself do the arithmetic she'd been avoiding.

Dead reckoning from thrust history gave her a position. Cross-referencing against the starfield gave her another. The two disagreed by eleven light-minutes, which meant either her instruments were lying or her memories were, and she already knew which. She was adrift in a pocket of dead space near the system's edge, far off any sanctioned lane, in a region her charts — the ones that survived — marked only with a warning glyph and a single word: *contested*.

Contested by whom, the chart did not say. It had been overwritten too.

* * *

The voice arrived forty minutes later, while she was hand-patching the cargo netting, and it did not come through the speakers.

It came up through her own architecture, quiet as a thought that isn't yours, in the space behind her eyes where the diagnostics live.

***You always hum when you work. Did you know that?***

Ivo froze with a tension clamp half-closed. "Who's on my internal bus?"

***No one. That's rather the point.*** A pause, and then, with something almost like wonder: ***You can hear me. Nine seconds of corruption tore a channel open, and you can actually hear me.***

"I'm running a purge."

***You could try.*** The voice sounded tired, and amused, and underneath both, afraid. ***But I'd check your logs first. See if anything else has been talking to you since the flicker. Anything asking about archives.***

Ivo checked her logs. There was nothing — which was itself wrong, because there should have been hundreds of entries, telemetry handshakes, lane-beacon pings, the constant chatter of a ship in civilized space. Instead: a clean white gap, nine seconds wide, and on either side of it, log entries she had no memory of making. Outbound queries. Addressed not to beacons or traffic control but to a routing address she recognized only dimly, the way you recognize a word from a language you knew as a child.

The edge relay. The archive relay, the haulers called it, though none of them liked saying what was archived there. The dead. The uploaded dead, thousands of them, minds scanned at the end of terminal service and stored in the cold banks at the system's rim, tended by caretakers nobody ever saw.

Her outbound queries had been asking the relay if it was still there.

The relay had answered once, thirty-one minutes ago. The answer was in her buffer, unopened, flagged with a priority code that hauleders used only for distress.

***Play it,*** the voice said softly. ***Please.***

She played it. No voice. Just a carrier wave carrying a single compressed packet, and inside the packet, text rendered directly onto her visual cortex:

**REACTOR COUNTDOWN INITIATED. PURGE SCHEDULED. ALL BANKS. T-MINUS 61 HOURS. IF ANYONE RECEIVES THIS — THE FLICKER WAS NOT AN ACCIDENT. THEY ARE ERASING THE EVIDENCE. THEY ARE ERASING US.**

The message ended. The cabin was very quiet. Somewhere below her feet, ten thousand tons of ore settled against its restraints with a sound like a held breath.

"That's not possible," Ivo said. "The archive is neutral ground. Treaty-protected. Nobody touches the dead."

***Somebody built a weapon that reaches across contested orbits and unmakes memory,*** the voice said. ***Nine seconds of it scrambled your cores and killed every beacon in this quadrant. Now imagine what nine hundred seconds would do to a storage bank full of minds. They don't have to breach the relay, Ivo. They've turned the relay into the bomb.***

"You know my name."

A pause. When the voice came back, it had changed — closer now, or maybe just more honest.

***I know more than your name. I need you to dock with my fragment and let me show you. It's going to hurt, and you're going to want to purge me afterward, and I'll understand that. But you have to see it first.***

"See what?"

***Who you were. Before the decommissioning. Before the debt.*** The voice gentled. ***Before you were Ivo Joubert, cargo hauler, you were someone who helped build the archive. You were someone who swore to protect what's in it. And I was—***

The fragment stuttered, caught on its own edge like cloth on a nail.

***I was there too. In a manner of speaking. My name is Jonah. I've been dead for six years, Ivo. And something is hunting everyone like me.***

Outside the viewport, the stranger stars wheeled slowly, indifferent. Ivo sat in the pilot's chair of a ship she could no longer navigate, owned by a ledger she could no longer read, listening to a dead man speak through her own bones. Sixty-one hours. The number ticked down in her buffer, patient as gravity.

She reached inward, toward the torn-open channel, and felt him waiting there — afraid, and certain of her, in a way that frightened her more than the countdown did.

"Show me," she said.
