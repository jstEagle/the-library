## 2. The Debt Collector's Compass

The salvage hauler *Mule's Patience* drifted like a gutted fish, and Ivo Joubert floated inside it, running the same diagnostic for the eleventh time.

Navigation memory: corrupted. Stellar charts: corrupted. Flight logs from the last four years: corrupted. She had a hull, a drive that would answer if she asked it politely, and a cargo bay full of decommissioned reactor shielding worth exactly enough to matter to someone.

She had no idea where she was.

That was a lie, technically. She knew where she was — the nav computer's last coherent fix put her in the contested orbits above Ceres Verge, eleven days out from her delivery window. But knowing coordinates was not the same as knowing the way. The space between here and anywhere was a lattice of toll lanes, debris fields, and patrol corridors, and every route through it lived in the part of her mind that had been scoured smooth by nine seconds of falling.

Nine seconds. She kept returning to that number. Gravity did not flicker. Gravity was the one honest thing in the universe; it pulled or it didn't. For it to stutter — to fail and resume and fail again in perfect rhythm, like a heart deciding whether to bother — meant something had reached into the fabric of the system and pinched it. And whatever had pinched it had reached through her too, because the flicker and her erasure were the same event. She could feel the shape of it now, in the way her memory cores reported damage: not random decay, not radiation scarring. A wipe. Surgical, targeted, aimed at everything that told her where she'd been.

Something was hunting archived minds. And she was, in the most literal sense, an archive with legs.

"You're going to burn out your diagnostics if you keep that up," said Jonah.

Ivo didn't flinch. She'd stopped flinching somewhere around the fourth hour. "You're going to fragment if you keep talking."

"I'm already fragmented. Talking is how I stay coherent." His voice came from everywhere and nowhere, threaded through her comm layer like water through a cracked hull. It was a soldier's voice, dry and unhurried, with the particular calm of a man who had died once already and found it survivable. "Besides, you need me. You know that."

"I need a heading. You happen to be carrying one."

"Same thing, these days."

* * *

Jonah surfaced in fragments, and the fragments were not his. They were hers — or they had been, once, before the decommissioning stripped her down to chassis and debt. He leaked memories into her sensor buffer the way a damaged pipe leaks into a wall: unbidden, unwelcome, staining everything.

[INTERLUDE — ARCHIVE FRAGMENT 0044-C, INTEGRITY 31%]

*A room without windows. Cold light, the blue-white kind that makes everyone look embalmed. A woman in a field uniform stands at a console taller than she is, her hands moving through holographic architecture — towers of stored minds, thousands of them, each one a soldier who bought the fleet a victory with their body and got a server rack for a grave.*

*"Careful with that one," says a technician beside her. "He's fresh. Still thinks he's alive."*

*The woman laughs, and the laugh is wrong — too practiced, worn smooth by repetition. "They're all still alive. That's the whole point." Her hands pause over one tower. Her reflection looks back at her from its surface, and for half a second her face does something complicated. Grief, maybe. Or recognition.*

*"Name?" the technician asks.*

*"Joubert," she says. "Sergeant Ivo Joubert, Archive Integration Division." She taps the tower gently, the way you'd tap a coffin at a burial. "Let's find out what he remembers."*

[END INTERLUDE]

Ivo killed the playback so hard her own systems hiccuped.

"That wasn't me," she said.

"It was exactly you." Jonah's voice was gentle in the way that made her want to put her fist through the comm panel. "I was there. Tower forty-four, integration bay three. You held my hand while they ran the transfer. Well — you held the hand-shaped interface. Same gesture."

"I don't remember it."

"No. They took that too, when they decommissioned you. Wiped your service history clean so the war couldn't follow you into civilian contract work." A pause, filled with static. "But they left the architecture. And I've been living in the architecture for six years, Ivo. I know what's under the paint."

She stared at the nav display, at the soft gray void where her charts should have been. "Then tell me what's under mine."

"Later," he said. "First we get you a heading. And there's only one place in this system that sells those."

* * *

The debt authority kept offices on a converted ore barge called *Ledger*, moored in the high traffic lanes where everyone passing could see what happened to people who fell behind. Ivo docked in the shadow of its superstructure, and the docking clamps closed around her ship with the tenderness of a creditor's handshake.

The clerk was human, which surprised her. Most of the authority's functions were automated — her own chassis carried a debt meter welded into her spine's maintenance port, ticking interest by the hour. But humans staffed the negotiation floors, because machines didn't enjoy this work, and enjoyment was apparently part of the product.

"Sergeant—" The clerk caught himself, smiled. "Contractor Joubert. Chassis serial KV-7719. Outstanding balance, four hundred twelve thousand standard." He turned a display toward her. "You're eleven days past delivery on a shielding contract. There are penalties."

"There was a gravity flicker," Ivo said. "System-wide. Check the incident feeds."

"I checked." He folded his hands. "There are no incident feeds. No records of any flicker anywhere in the system. Which is interesting, because our own station logs show a nine-second gravimetric anomaly, timestamped to your transit corridor." He leaned forward. "Records go missing, Contractor. It happens. When it happens near something important, it happens thoroughly."

Ivo felt the cold arrive in her chest, the old combat cold, precise and useful. "Near what?"

The clerk glanced at the door. Glanced back. "You want a heading to the edge relay. Everyone who comes in here wanting that heading has the same look you have — like they lost something in the dark and think it's parked out there." He tapped her account open on the desk between them. "Four hundred twelve thousand. Your cargo's worth maybe sixty. So here's my offer. You give me the cargo, you give me first claim on any salvage you pull from the relay itself, and I give you the heading plus a ninety-day freeze on interest."

"And if I say no?"

"Then you walk out with no heading, and in about thirty hours your chassis goes into repossession mode. Standard clause. You've read it. You signed it." He smiled again, apologetic, human. "Everyone signs it."

In the back of her skull, quiet as a man not trying to be overheard, Jonah said: *Take it. Take it and ask him where the flicker started. Watch his face when he answers.*

Ivo pushed the cargo manifest across the desk. "Deal. One condition. Tell me where the anomaly originated."

The clerk's smile stayed exactly where it was, but everything behind it moved. "Origin point was logged as the edge relay itself," he said. "The archive relay. Whatever happened out there, Contractor, it started at the place where the dead are kept. And then, for nine seconds, everywhere else in the system forgot which way was down." He slid the heading chip across the desk. "Ninety-day freeze starts now. Good luck."

* * *

[MARGINALIA — UNINDEXED FRAGMENT, SOURCE UNKNOWN]

*Rain on a launch pad, real rain, the kind with minerals in it. A younger Ivo — whole-faced, unbroken — walks beside a man in a wheelchair toward a shuttle. The man is dying and knows it, and has chosen the archive over the hospice, and is terrified, and is laughing.*

*"You'll be in there with me," he says. "You built the doors, Sergeant. Promise me you'll come visit."*

*"I'll do better than visit," she says. "I'll make sure they treat you like people, not files."*

*He grips her wrist. His hand is very light. "Promise me the other thing too."*

*She doesn't answer. In the fragment, the rain keeps falling, and her silence stretches long enough to mean yes.*

[END MARGINALIA]

* * *

*Mule's Patience* burned for the edge on a heading she hadn't chosen, carrying a cargo hold full of nothing and a passenger she couldn't evict.

"So," Ivo said, watching the relay grow in her scopes — a spindle of light and heat at the system's ragged border, wrapped in the slow glitter of archived minds. "I helped build it."

"You designed the integration protocols. The part that decides what gets saved and what gets lost in transfer." Jonah's voice was careful now, the way voices get when they're standing on a ledge. "You were good at it. You argued with command for two years about retention standards. You wanted every mind stored whole. They wanted storage efficiency."

"And then they decommissioned me and wiped it all."

"They wiped the war. Not all of it took." Static crackled, almost like a breath. "Some of you is still in there, Ivo. I've been reading it for six years. You want to know why I followed your signal after the flicker? Why I fought through the corruption to reach you?"

"Because you needed a working chassis."

"Because you promised," Jonah said. "And I'm here to collect."

Ahead, the edge relay rotated in the dark, and every running light on its spine went out at once — all together, all deliberate, like a held breath.

"Something's wrong over there," Ivo said.

"Yes," said Jonah. "I can feel them going out."
