## 12. The Testimony Fragment

Dax worked the way other men prayed—hunched, murmuring, hands moving over the console like it might answer back.

Gideon had watched him for six hours. The arbitration recording had come off the black box in pieces, a corrupted lattice of audio and biometric data that no commercial decoder would touch. Dax had built his own from salvage: three generations of cracked firmware stitched together with prayer and solder. He called it *the loom*. He never explained why.

"Say something," he said without looking up. "Anything. I need to calibrate against your voiceprint."

"What do you want me to say?"

"Doesn't matter. Something true."

Gideon thought about it. "I'm afraid of what's on this recording."

Dax's fingers paused for half a second, then resumed. "Good enough." He fed her sentence into the loom, let it map the frequencies of fear, and turned back to the wreckage of forty-year-old audio.

Sefa sat on the deck plating with her back against the reactor housing, turning a memory-pearl over in her calloused hands like a stone she was deciding whether to skip. Amara stood at the viewport with her arms crossed, watching Meridian's traffic lanes crawl past, not seeing any of it.

They had been at this since the raid on the dock. Since Latch took the mother-of-all key and Gideon escaped with one pearl sewn into the socket where her own memories used to live. Since Sefa gutted half their ship's systems to build a decryption rig that could crack Calloway's proprietary encoding—one pearl at a time, slow as distilling.

The child's pearl had been first. They'd all listened to it twice. Nobody had spoken after the second time.

Now this. The last recoverable fragment from the black box. Not a colonist's final day—the archive pearls were still locked—but something older. A record that had been riding inside the Halcyon's own systems when she burned.

"Gideon," Dax said quietly. "You should sit down."

"I've been sitting down for six hours."

"You should sit down *closer*."

She crossed the cramped cabin and pulled a crate up beside his console. On the screen, the loom had assembled a waveform out of static—a voice, rising and falling, almost whole. Beneath it, a second track: room tone, the hum of a tribunal chamber, the dry cough of someone important being bored.

"Arbitration proceedings are recorded on triple-redundant local storage," Dax said. "Corporate standard. They keep the originals for ten years, then wipe. This one didn't get wiped because it wasn't supposed to exist—it was the Halcyon's internal mirror, logging everything that happened aboard her, including remote feeds. Somebody at Kestrel's Landing patched the tribunal feed into the ship's network. Probably so the colonists could listen at home."

"Did they?"

"The timestamp says the hearing happened while the Halcyon was still in dock at Meridian. Four months before launch." He looked at her. "The colony was already dying before anyone boarded."

Amara turned from the viewport. "Play it."

Dax played it.

* * *

The voice came through first as texture, then as words, then—horribly—as something familiar.

"—submitted into evidence, Exhibit Nine, the pressure-tolerance certificates for dome seal lots KL-4401 through KL-4419, manufactured by Calloway Biologics, Orbital Division, in the fiscal year preceding settlement charter approval—"

A man's voice. Flat, procedural, reading. Then another voice, older, drier, with the particular patience of someone who has already decided how this ends:

"The tribunal has reviewed Exhibit Nine. Proceed to testimony."

And then her own voice.

Gideon did not move. Could not. She heard herself speaking from forty years ago, from inside a body she had lived in and lost, and every cadence was hers—the slight drop at the end of sentences, the habit of pausing before hard facts as if giving them room to land.

"My name is Jonah Iversen. I am the colony liaison for the Kestrel's Landing settlement project, appointed by the Colonial Compact Office. I'm here to enter testimony regarding the structural integrity of the atmospheric containment systems."

"You're aware," the dry voice said, "that Calloway Biologics certified those systems."

"I'm aware that Calloway Biologics certified those systems. I'm also aware of what certification costs, because I reviewed the invoices. Certification ran eleven thousand credits per lot. Independent testing runs sixty thousand. Calloway paid for the cheaper option and stamped the results themselves."

"That is an accusation of fraud."

"It's an arithmetic problem." Her old voice was steady. Steadier than Gideon had ever managed to be in this life. "The seals in question were rated for forty kilopascals of differential pressure. The Kestrel's Landing environment requires sustained tolerance of fifty-two. Every engineer who looked at the numbers flagged it. Every flag was resolved by a memo. I have the memos. I have the engineers' names. Two of them resigned rather than sign off. One of them is in this room, if the tribunal would like to hear from him."

"He has signed a nondisclosure agreement."

"He signed it under threat of blacklisting. Which is itself a matter I'd like entered into—"

"The tribunal notes your concern. Continue."

And Jonah Iversen continued. For eleven minutes of recovered audio, she laid it out piece by piece: the defective seal lots, the internal emails marked *ship anyway*, the cost-benefit analysis in which four hundred twelve lives had been assigned a remediation value of zero because the colony's insurance liability capped at less than the price of a recall. She read the analysis aloud. She read the name of the executive who approved it.

Gideon sat very still and listened to herself be brave.

It was the worst thing she had ever heard.

* * *

The recording degraded near the end. Dax leaned into the loom, coaxing, whispering to it. The tribunal's voices came apart into grain and hiss, but Jonah's held—encoded deeper somehow, or maybe just spoken louder, the way people speak when they know they're running out of time.

"Let the record show," her old self said, "that I asked this tribunal to halt the launch of the Halcyon pending independent inspection. Let the record show that the tribunal declined. Let the record show that I asked why, and was told the question was out of order. Let the record show—" a breath, and here the voice finally shook, just once, "—let the record show that four hundred twelve people are going to board that ship believing they are safe, and that I told them they were safe, because I believed the process meant something. And let the record show that when they die—and they will die, if these seals fail at pressure, and these seals will fail at pressure—I want whoever finds this recording to know that it was not an accident. It was a decision. Someone decided. I am asking this tribunal to write down who."

Static. Long, seething static.

Then the dry voice again, distant now, dissolving:

"—finds the testimony of the colony liaison to be speculative and prejudicial to the commercial interests—"

"—remanded to Calloway internal review—"

"—standard remediation protocols to be applied—"

Dax killed the playback. The cabin went quiet except for the reactor's low heartbeat and, after a moment, the sound of Amara Okonkwo sitting down very heavily on a crate that was not designed for sitting.

"Remediation protocols," Sefa said. She said it carefully, like handling something sharp. "That's the clinic word. That's what they call it when they cut you open and take the years out."

"I know what it means," Gideon said.

Her voice came out strange. Level. She noticed that about herself the way you notice weather—as a fact occurring outside you.

"They scheduled it," she said. "After the verdict. After they buried the testimony. They scheduled me for erasure, and instead of waiting for them to do it, I—" She stopped. Started again. "There was a message at the clinic. *If you came back, you already know why.* I thought it meant I ran from trauma. From watching them die. That I couldn't live with the grief, so I bought myself empty."

"And now?" Dax asked gently.

"Now I know better." She looked at the dead screen, at the ghost of her own waveform still hanging there. "I didn't run from the massacre. I ran from the verdict. There's a difference. The massacre was done to me. The verdict—" her jaw tightened, "—the verdict I fought. I stood up in front of a bought tribunal and told the truth with my whole chest, knowing exactly what it would cost, and when they came to take my memory of it, I got there first. I chose the knife. I picked which parts of me died so the rest could live."

"That's not cowardice," Amara said.

"No," Gideon agreed. "It's not."

She had expected, somewhere underneath, to feel shame. That had been the shape of her dread for weeks—that Jonah would turn out to be complicit, that Latch's hint had been true in the cruelest way, that she had been part of the machine that killed them. Latch had wanted her to believe that. It was his best weapon: make her doubt whether she deserved her own name.

But the recording said otherwise. The recording said she had been the only person in that room telling the truth, and that she had known, going in, exactly how it would end, and had gone in anyway.

Not innocence. Courage. The kind that gets punished.

"I testified," she said slowly, testing each word like a hull plate under pressure. "I named names. I put it all on the record. And the record survived—in the black box, in the ship, in the one place they didn't think to look, because who audits a graveyard?" She turned to Dax. "How much of it can you clean up? Enough to hold up? Enough that a stranger could listen and know it's real?"

Dax rubbed his eyes behind his spectacles. "Authentication headers are intact. Voiceprint matches station medical records for a Jonah Iversen, Colonial Compact appointment, Kestrel's Landing project—which, by the way, is a file that officially does not exist, but Okafor's fragments restored enough of it to cross-reference." He allowed himself the smallest smile. "It'll hold. It'll hold in any court in the system that isn't already bought. And maybe one or two that are."

"Then we don't stop at the archive pearls." Gideon stood. The fear was still there—she could feel it in her hands, a fine tremor she made no attempt to hide—but it had changed polarity. It pointed forward now. "Latch offered to tell me what Jonah did. He wanted me to buy my own history back one humiliating piece at a time. Fine. Here's the exchange rate: we take the testimony, we take the four hundred twelve names, and we give them to everyone at once. No negotiation. No deal. The truth goes out free or it doesn't go out at all."

Sefa looked up from the pearl in her hands. "The broadcast rig isn't finished."

"Then finish it."

"The Founder's Day lattice window is nine days out. You're asking me to bet the boat."

"I'm asking you to bet it on something worth the pot."

Sefa held her gaze for a long moment. Then she grinned—not happily; grimly, the grin of an engineer who has just been given permission to do something magnificent and possibly fatal—and went aft toward the gutted engine room, already talking specs to herself.

Amara rose more slowly. At the hatch she paused, her hand on the frame, and did not turn around.

"I flew the shuttle away from that hearing," she said. "Metaphorically. All those years, I flew away, and I sold the memory of the flight, and I kept the guilt because I thought guilt was all I deserved to keep." Her voice roughened. "You kept the courage and threw away everything else. We were carrying the same cargo, Jonah. Just packed different."

She left. Dax began packing up the loom with the tenderness of a man putting away a musical instrument.

Gideon stayed at the console a moment longer. She pulled up the fragment one more time—not the audio, just the metadata, the cold little string of identifiers that the Halcyon had logged along with the feed. Witness designation. Seat assignment. And a field the tribunal software reserved for post-hearing disposition, filled in by some clerk's keystroke forty years ago:

*REMEDIATION SCHEDULED — PRIORITY — DO NOT RELEASE.*

Priority. They had wanted her emptied fast, before the testimony could grow roots anywhere else.

Instead she had grown roots in the dark for four decades, nameless, careful, invisible—and every rule she'd ever lived by, every wall she'd built, had been load-bearing structure around this one buried fact: *she had already told the truth once, and survived the price.*

She closed the file.

"Dax," she said. "When we broadcast—when the whole station hears it—I want my name attached. Not Gideon Iversen. Not the smuggler. Jonah. The liaison who testified. Let them hear both voices if they can tell the difference."

Dax zipped the loom's case shut and looked at her over his spectacles for a long, unreadable moment.

"There's something you should know before you decide that," he said. "Something I found in the authentication headers while I was cleaning the audio. The tribunal feed wasn't patched into the Halcyon's network automatically." He set the case down. "Someone routed it manually. From a terminal inside the colony dome. The access signature belongs to a colonial administrative account."

Whose?

Dax met her eyes. "Jonah Iversen's. She recorded her own testimony and hid it where they'd never burn it. Which means somewhere in that archive of yours—" he nodded toward the sealed cache of colonist pearls, "—there may be more than final days. She may have left you instructions."

Gideon felt the tremor in her hands go still.

"Get me the next pearl decrypted," she said. "Tonight."
