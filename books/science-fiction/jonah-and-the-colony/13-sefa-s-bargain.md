## 13. Sefa's Bargain

The archive sat in Gideon's berth like a sleeping animal—forty-one pearls in a padded case, each one holding the last recorded day of a colonist at Kestrel's Landing. Encrypted with Calloway Biologics' industrial-grade cipher, the kind that laughed at commercial keys.

Sefa turned one over in her calloused fingers, the way she handled anything she was thinking about taking apart.

"This is military lattice encryption," she said. "Not corporate. Corporate is a lock. This is a vault inside a vault inside a vault."

"Can you open it?"

Sefa set the pearl down gently, which from Sefa meant no—or not cheaply. "With what? My rig tops out at brute-forcing door codes. This would take my hardware four hundred years." She looked up, and there was something in her face Gideon had learned to read as the beginning of an idea she didn't like. "Unless we stop trying to be clever about it."

"I don't follow."

"The Halcyon's black box." Sefa nodded toward the hold, where the salvaged box from the colony ship sat wrapped in shock-webbing like a relic. "Calloway built the dome seals. Calloway built the ship. Same security division, same cipher family. The black box has a hardware decryption core baked into its flight recorder—it's how they authenticated telemetry back to Meridian without anyone spoofing it. If I gut it, pull the core, and graft it into my decrypt array..."

"You'd have a key that fits the lock."

"I'd have a key that *is* the lock," Sefa said. "The only one left in existence, probably. And to graft it, I need the processing head from our nav computer, both of our comm relays for bandwidth, and the power couplings off the drive regulator." She ticked them off on her fingers like she was reading a grocery list instead of a eulogy. "Which means while I'm doing this, we are a barge. No nav, no comms worth the name, and if the regulator coupling goes wrong, no engines ever again."

Gideon felt the shape of it settle over the room. "And if you pull all that and the core doesn't work?"

"Then we've got a very expensive paperweight and no way home." Sefa smiled, and it wasn't a happy smile; it was the smile of someone who had already decided. "So. Family means betting the boat, right?"

Nobody had said that. Not in those words. But it had been said, in pieces, across late meals and shared watches and the night they'd all listened to strangers die. Gideon looked at her—at Sefa, who owed her a life-debt she'd never once mentioned since the day she'd paid the first installment by wiring Gideon's berth against Latch's scanners—and understood that the debt had quietly become something else. Something neither of them had a word for yet.

"If it fails," Gideon said, "we're stranded on Meridian. With Latch looking for us."

"If it fails, we were always going to be stranded eventually." Sefa rolled up her sleeves. "You can't run forever on a ship that can't fight. Better to spend the ship on something that matters than keep it safe for nothing." She paused at the hatch. "Get Amara to park us somewhere quiet. I'll need thirty hours, maybe forty. And Gideon—" she tapped the case of pearls, "—don't let anyone touch these but me. If Latch gets his hands on even one, everything I'm about to tear out of this ship was for nothing."

* * *

Amara chose Docking Bay 114, on the ring's underside, where the station's oldest berths huddled under failing heat exchangers and the rent was paid in cash and silence. A place for ships that didn't want to be seen: salvage haulers, unregistered ferries, one ancient tug that had been "under repair" for six years.

"It smells like someone died in here," Dax said, hauling crates of Sefa's tools down the ramp.

"Someone probably did," Amara said. She ran her palm along the bay's structural rib with something close to affection. "But look at that truss work. They built Meridian when people still expected things to last." She glanced at Gideon. "I've flown out of worse. I've flown out of better-looking places that killed me slower."

They worked through the first watch in a rhythm none of them named. Sefa disappeared into the engineering spine with a cutting torch and a vocabulary that made Dax blush. Dax set up his spoofing kit at the bay's access terminal, layering false transponder chatter so the berth looked occupied by a routine freighter running late on paperwork. Amara pre-flighted the ship twice, then a third time, hands moving over controls she'd known for forty years, checking systems she was about to lose.

Gideon stood watch at the bay doors and tried not to count the hours.

It was Okafor who found them—not in person, but as a message routed through three dead drops to Dax's anonymous relay. No signature. Just a line of text:

**Latch filed a dock seizure warrant this morning. Judge signed it. He doesn't know your berth yet. You have maybe two days before he narrows the grid. Move or finish fast.**

Dax read it aloud, and the bay went quiet except for the distant groan of the station's bones.

"He's using warrants now," Amara said. "That's new. That means he wants this legal-shaped. Means he's worried about witnesses."

"Means he's losing patience," Gideon said. "Keep working, Sefa."

From somewhere deep in the ship, muffled by bulkheads, came the whine of a plasma cutter, steady as a heartbeat.

* * *

Hour nineteen. Sefa emerged looking like she'd lost a fight with her own ship, soot on her jaw, a burn dressing on her left forearm she refused to discuss. She carried the black box's decryption core cradled in both hands—a dull silver slab the size of a hymnal, trailing fiber-optic roots.

"Got it out clean," she said. "Mostly. Now comes the part where I marry it to my array, and if the handshake fails, we find out how good Calloway's engineers really were."

"And if it succeeds?"

"Then we own every pearl in that case." She looked past Gideon at the others—Dax pretending to work, Amara watching from the cockpit stairs, all of them gathered without being called. Sefa's voice dropped. "I want to say something before I go back in, because if the regulator coupling blows during the graft, I might not get another chance."

"Sefa—"

"Let me." She took a breath. "Three years ago you pulled me out of a decompression breach on the *Kestrel's Wake*—yeah, I know, funny name, I've thought about that a lot lately—and you didn't ask me who I was or what I owed. You just held the patch on my suit until the rescue skiff came, and then you vanished, because that's what you did back then. Vanished." Her eyes were wet and furious about being wet. "I spent three years figuring out how to repay a life-debt to someone who wouldn't stay still long enough to collect. And now I finally get it. It was never a debt. Debts end. This—" she gestured at the bay, the ship, the ragged assembly of them, "—this doesn't end. So I'm spending the boat. All of it. Because a ship is just metal, and you people aren't."

Nobody spoke. Dax blew his nose loudly and blamed the lubricant fumes.

Then Sefa went back into the dark with her torch, and the cutting began again.

* * *

Hour twenty-six. The graft held. Sefa's voice over the ship's internal line, thin with exhaustion and triumph: "Handshake confirmed. Core's talking to my array. Decryption cycle started—call it eight hours for the full archive. Go to sleep, all of you, before you fall down."

For the first time in days, Gideon slept without dreaming of green fields.

She woke to alarms.

Not the ship's—those were silent, stripped as they were—but the bay's external klaxon, a flat industrial honk that meant unauthorized approach. She was out of her bunk and moving before her mind caught up, bare feet cold on deck plating, and found Dax already at the access terminal, face lit gray.

"Two vehicles at the bay perimeter," he said. "Station-security livery. It's fake—I checked the assignment logs, no patrol scheduled for this ring tonight." His hands were shaking as he typed. "And there's a third contact on approach vector. Private shuttle, transponder reads *Meridian Port Authority*, which is adorable, because the Port Authority doesn't fly at 0300."

"Latch."

"Latch."

Down in the engineering spine, the decryption cycle ticked along: sixty-one percent complete. Four hours remaining. Sefa's voice crackled up the line, calm in the way of people who go calm precisely when things break. "I heard. Nobody panic. How long do we have?"

"Six minutes before they cut the bay doors," Dax said. "Maybe less. He'll have brought a breacher."

Amara was already in the pilot's seat, strapping in, and the sight of her there—seventy years old, silver-braided, hands steady as bedrock—did more for Gideon's fear than any reassurance could have. "We're not going anywhere," Amara said. "We're a barge, remember? No nav, no comms, half our power routed into a decryption array. I can lift us, but I can't steer us anywhere smart."

"Unlicensed gates," Gideon said.

Everyone turned. The phrase hung in the bay air like smoke.

"There are maintenance gates in the old ring structure," Gideon went on, pulling the memory forward from her first year on Meridian, from her smuggling days, from routes she'd flown before she knew why flying away felt familiar. "Pre-expansion service tunnels. They're not on any current chart—they were sealed when the station doubled its diameter twenty years ago. But they're sealed, not destroyed. A ship this size can fit through if the pilot is insane."

"I flew those gates," Amara said slowly. "Before the expansion. Before they told me to forget the routes." Something moved behind her eyes—the ghost of a memory she'd sold and never fully stopped grieving. "I don't have the charts anymore. They took them when they took everything else."

"But your hands remember," Gideon said. "You told me once. Muscle memory survives erasure. It's the one thing they couldn't reach."

Amara stared at her. Then she laughed—one short, wild bark—and started flipping switches. "My hands remember. God help us all. Dax, kill our transponder, full blackout. Sefa, strap that array down or we lose sixty percent of the archive to a hard turn. We lift in ninety seconds."

"Gideon." Sefa's voice on the line, urgent now. "The cycle hits seventy percent in four minutes. If we move before it completes, the transfer buffer corrupts. We lose everything we've decrypted so far."

Gideon closed her eyes. Outside, the sound of the bay doors beginning to complain under a breaching charge—metal shrieking, the high whine of plasma.

"How long to complete?"

"Eight minutes."

"We wait seven," Gideon said. "Amara, can you hold us here for seven minutes with their shuttle coming in?"

Amara's answer was to bring the drive regulators—what was left of them—to a low growl. "I can hold us here for seven minutes. Whether *they* can hold themselves together for seven minutes is their problem."

* * *

The bay doors came down in a bloom of orange sparks at minute two.

They came through in formation, six of them, armored in matte black with no insignia, weapons low and professional. And behind them, unhurried, came Mr. Latch, coat immaculate, hands folded, stepping over the ruined door as though it were a threshold he'd been invited across.

He stopped ten meters from the ship's ramp and raised a small device—not a weapon, a speaker—and his voice filled the bay, courteous as a dinner invitation.

"Gideon Iversen. Or should I say Jonah?" He tilted his head, savoring it. "You've been busy. I confess I admire the ambition—gutting your own ship to read other people's mail. Very romantic. Very doomed." He gestured, and his team spread into a loose arc, covering the ramp. "Here is the arithmetic. Your ship cannot fly—you've amputated it yourselves, which saved me the trouble. Your archive is incomplete. And my colleagues are standing between you and the only exit." He smiled. "Surrender the pearls and the core, and I promise you'll walk out of here. Eventually. After some conversations."

In the engineering spine, the counter climbed. Sixty-eight percent. Seventy.

On the ramp, Gideon stood alone, hands visible, heart hammering a rhythm she recognized from a lifetime ago—from a tribunal room, from a whisper saying *run*. She had spent years building a self designed never to stand on ramps facing men like this. Here she was anyway.

"You're stalling," Latch observed pleasantly. "Why are you stalling?"

Seventy-four percent. The shuttle outside was docking clamps-down now; she could feel it through the deck, a second heartbeat joining theirs.

"Because," Gideon said, "you made a mistake."

"Did I?"

"You came in through the main doors." She smiled, and it felt strange on her face, unfamiliar, like a name she was learning to wear. "There's another way out of this bay. There always was. You'd know that if you hadn't bought your maps from people who sell the same map twice."

Eighty-one percent. Latch's smile thinned by a degree. Behind him, one of his team murmured into a collar mic, and Latch's eyes flicked upward—toward the ceiling, toward the old ring structure above and beyond—

"Ninety-two," Sefa whispered on the line. "Ninety-five. Done. Archive complete. Get us out of here."

Gideon stepped backward up the ramp. "Mr. Latch. It's been educational."

"Doors," Latch said, and the world became noise.

* * *

Amara flew like a woman remembering a language she'd been forbidden to speak.

She brought the ship off the deck sideways, hull screaming past the bay's structural ribs with centimeters to spare, and put them through the breach in the ceiling panel—a maintenance hatch Sefa had unbolted during hour twelve, "just in case," because Sefa believed in just-in-case the way other people believed in gods. The ship rose through it into the dark throat of the old ring structure, and behind them the bay erupted as Latch's team fired at a target already gone.

"Contact astern!" Dax shouted. "Their shuttle's in the tunnel behind us!"

"Hold on to something," Amara said, and banked hard left into a junction that no chart had shown for twenty years.

The old gates were exactly as Gideon remembered and worse: unlit, unmapped, ribbed with dead machinery, clearance margins measured in hope. Amara flew them through by touch and instinct, calling turns before they happened—"brace, brace, *left*"—her body executing a route her conscious mind had sold, her hands fluent in a language the clinic had never managed to fully repossess. Twice the hull kissed the tunnel wall with a shriek that Sefa, strapped beside her gutted array, swore at fluently in three languages.

The shuttle behind them was bigger, faster, newer. It was also flown by someone with a map and no memory of the route, and at Gate Nine—a hairpin around a collapsed coolant conduit that Amara took at a speed that should have torn the wings off—the pursuit fell back, then broke off entirely, its running lights shrinking in the dark behind them.

Silence, then, except for breathing. Except for the hum of a wounded ship carrying a stolen vault of the dead.

They came out of the old ring structure through a decommissioned cargo port on the station's far side, into open vacuum and the slow wheeling stars, and Amara leveled off with hands that were, at last, trembling.

"Course?" she asked, voice rough.

Gideon looked back through the cabin at her crew—Dax clutching his terminal like a talisman, Sefa with her arms wrapped around the decryption array and the forty-one pearls locked inside it, all of them battered, breathless, alive.

"Somewhere quiet," Gideon said. "We've got four hundred twelve voices to listen to."

But even as she said it, her hand was already reaching for the archive readout—and the first file, the top of the queue, the one the core had decrypted before any other, glowed on the display with a label that stopped her breath:

**IVERSEN, J. — TESTIMONY FRAGMENT — PARTIAL RECOVERY — 3%**

Three percent. Ninety-seven percent corrupted. Three percent of her own voice, waiting.

"Amara," she said quietly. "Play it."
