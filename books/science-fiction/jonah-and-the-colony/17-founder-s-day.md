## 17. Founder's Day

Meridian Station smelled like sugar and ozone.

That was the first thing Jonah noticed stepping out of the dock district at 0600 station time: the recycler vents had been flushed with a cinnamon compound that the Founders' Trust imported every year at obscene expense, so that for one day the station's permanent smell of hot metal and recycled air would be disguised as something festive. Underneath it, if you knew what to sniff for, the metal was still there. The ozone was still there.

She thought that was about right for Meridian. A scent laid over a truth nobody wanted to breathe directly.

The promenade was already filling. Banners in the Trust's blue-and-gold hung from the atrium gantries, and work crews were bolting down the last of the stage platforms around the central lattice spire—the great black column of transmitter nodes that rose through all nine decks of the public concourse, carrying ceremony broadcasts, market feeds, government addresses, and once a year, on Founder's Day, the communal memory-lattice sync when every registered resident on the station jacked in for the traditional Sharing of the Ledger. Four hundred thousand people, plugged into the same feed at the same moment, watching the same carefully edited history of the colony fleet.

Four hundred thousand people who were about to watch something else.

Jonah kept her hood up and her gait bored. She'd spent eleven years perfecting the walk of a woman with nothing interesting in her pockets, and today she was carrying the most dangerous cargo on the station: forty-one memory-pearls in a padded courier case against her ribs, plus the Halcyon's salvaged black box riding in a freight sled disguised as catering equipment, plus Sefa's broadcast rig distributed across three innocent-looking toolbags carried by three people who were not looking at each other.

Dax fell into step beside her near the noodle stalls, close enough to talk, far enough to be strangers.

"Uplink control says the lattice goes live for the sync at 1100," he murmured. "Authentication handshake runs through the Trust's ceremonial key—Sefa has the spoof ready. Okafor confirmed her rotation. She's got the north inspection team until 1115."

"And Latch?"

"That," Dax said, "is the question, isn't it."

They passed the memorial wall where the names of Meridian's founding crews were etched in brass. Jonah let her eyes flick across them without stopping. Somewhere in the Trust archives there was supposed to be a wall like this for Kestrel's Landing. There wasn't. There was a purchase order and a purge receipt.

"Latch doesn't make mistakes," she said quietly. "He made one letting us get this far, or he wanted us to get this far."

"You've said that every day for a week."

"Because it's been true every day for a week."

Dax was quiet for a few steps. Then: "My grandmother used to say you can't cheat an honest man and you can't trap a paranoid one. So pick which one you're going to be for the next five hours."

"I'm going to be both," Jonah said. "It's a special occasion."

* * *

Sefa had turned the maintenance crawlspace behind the lattice spire's third-deck junction into something between a workshop and a shrine. Cables ran everywhere in neat, color-coded looms—she'd insisted on color-coding even while cannibalizing their own ship's navigation core to build the relay, because chaos was for people with backup ships. The spoof module sat in the center of it like a heart waiting for a body: a matte-black wedge of salvaged hardware that would impersonate the Trust's ceremonial authentication key well enough to fool the lattice for exactly as long as it took anyone to look closely.

"How long is that?" Jonah had asked her, two nights ago.

"Depends how fast they look," Sefa said. "Fast? Ninety seconds. Slow? Eleven minutes."

Eleven minutes. That was the number everything hung on now. Okafor could hold her inspectors off for eleven minutes. The spoof could hold the authentication for eleven minutes. Amara had the tug positioned at gate seven with its transponder singing a garbage-hauler's song, and she could keep them airborne—well, spaceborne—for eleven minutes before Meridian traffic control got genuinely annoyed instead of performatively annoyed.

Eleven minutes to push four hundred twelve lives into four hundred thousand heads.

Jonah found Sefa flat on her back under the junction housing, a torch in her teeth, solder smoke curling up past her face.

"We're live in four hours," Jonah said.

"I'm aware." Sefa slid out, pushed her goggles up, and looked at Jonah with the flat patience of someone who had been asked variations of this question all morning. "You want to know if it'll work."

"I want to know what worries you."

Sefa wiped her hands on a rag that was more solder than rag. "Two things. One: the uplink. The lattice can receive anything we push, but pushing it station-wide means riding the main trunk, and the main trunk has a physical cutoff in Uplink Control on deck one. If Calloway's people get to that cutoff before we're done, they don't have to beat our spoof—they just pull a lever and we're broadcasting to a dead line."

"And two?"

Sefa hesitated. That was unlike her. Sefa did not hesitate; she calculated, announced the result, and dared you to argue.

"The black box," she said finally. "I told you I could use it as a physical relay if the spoof failed. What I didn't tell you is that using it that way means cracking its casing and hard-splicing it into the lattice core. It works. I've tested it on sim. But it's a one-way door. Once it's spliced in, the box is part of the station. We don't get it back, and neither does anyone else."

Jonah looked at the freight sled, at the scuffed black rectangle inside that had crossed half the system to get here, that had carried the manifest with her name on it out of a fire she couldn't remember.

"It already did its job," she said. "It got us here. Let it finish."

* * *

Inspector Vey Okafor put on her dress uniform at 0800 and hated it.

Founder's Day meant parade inspections, which meant the whole customs division walking the promenade in formal grays while tourists took holo-snaps of them, which meant Vey—senior enough to lead a team, honest enough that her superiors liked her visible and stupid enough, they assumed, to be proud of it—marching four junior inspectors along the vendor rows checking permits that everyone knew were forged.

Her team today: Reyes, who was ambitious; Chen, who was competent; Okoye and Brandt, who were new. None of them knew anything. That was the point.

At 0830 her comm buzzed with a priority flag from Division: supplemental inspection order, effective immediately. All north-concourse access points to Uplink Control to be swept and secured for the duration of the ceremony, by order of the Founders' Trust liaison office.

Vey read it twice. Inspection orders came from Division. They did not come from corporate liaison offices wearing Division letterhead. And Uplink Control was already locked down for Founder's Day—it had been locked down since Tuesday, by a security contract awarded to a private firm whose name she'd run last week and whose registration traced, through three shell layers, to a biotech holding company.

Calloway was locking the door before the thieves arrived. Which meant Calloway knew the door needed locking.

She thought about dropping it. She'd thought about dropping it every day since she'd copied those half-restored registry fragments and sent them to a burner address she wasn't supposed to have. Every day she'd woken up certain that today would be the day Internal Affairs knocked, and every day they hadn't, and the not-knowing had become its own kind of sentence.

But there was a difference between looking away and standing guard for the people doing the burning.

She keyed her team channel. "Reyes, Chen—with me. We're sweeping the north concourse access points." A pause she made sound like paperwork. "Per the supplemental order."

Reyes brightened. Corporate liaison duty meant visibility. Visibility meant promotion.

Vey clipped her comm to her collar and thought: eleven minutes. Give me eleven minutes and I'll give you a door that opens slow.

* * *

By 1000 the promenade was a river.

Jonah stood at the edge of it with a tray of festival dumplings she'd bought purely for camouflage, watching the crowd flow around the lattice spire. Families in their best clothes. Kids with paper lanterns. Old-timers from the first docking generation wearing the blue sashes of the original crew families, telling anyone who'd listen that the real Founder's Day had better dumplings.

Four hundred thousand people about to plug in and be told, again, that everything was fine, that the ledger balanced, that the dead were accounted for.

Somewhere in that crowd, she was nearly sure, Mr. Latch was also watching.

She'd turned the problem over so many times it had grooves worn in it. Latch had searched her berth. Latch had taken her mother-of-all decryption key. Latch had let her walk away with one pearl hidden in the socket where her own memories used to live—a violation she still felt like a draft under a door—and he'd done it all with the courtesy of a man dealing cards he'd already counted. He knew what she was. He'd practically told her: *you weren't a victim, Ms. Iversen, you were a witness.* He knew she'd come back for the name. People who bought erasure always came back for the name; he'd probably seen it a dozen times.

So either he was complacent, which contradicted everything else about him—

—or the ceremony plan was his.

She'd voiced it to the crew two nights ago, in the cramped galley of Amara's tug, over food no one tasted. Amara had argued they had no choice; the Founder's Day sync was the only window when a single injection could reach every resident at once. Dax had argued that if it was a trap, it had been a trap from the moment the black box surfaced, and they'd been swimming in it ever since, so they might as well swim toward the shark. Sefa had said nothing for a long time, then said: "Then we need a way in that isn't the way he expects."

Which was why the black box was in the sled, and why Sefa had spent a week building a second splice path, and why Jonah now watched the crowd and waited for the trap to spring so they could find out what shape it was.

Her comm ticked once. Dax's signal: *In position. Authentication window opens 1058.*

She took a dumpling she didn't want and ate it anyway. Her mother—no. Not her mother. The woman in the pearl, the whisperer, the voice that was hers: *Jonah, run.* She wondered if that woman had eaten dumplings on this station, in another life, planning something just as impossible.

Probably. People like them were always planning something impossible. It was practically the family business.

* * *

At 1054, everything went wrong on schedule.

Jonah felt it before she saw it: a change in the crowd's texture near the spire. Blue-and-gold Trust stewards moving with purpose instead of festivity. And cutting through them, unhurried, immaculate in a charcoal suit that no one on Meridian could afford, came Mr. Latch.

He didn't look at the crowd. He looked at the spire. And then, with the mild precision of a man confirming a reservation, he raised one hand, and six of his people peeled off toward the deck-one access points to Uplink Control.

Jonah's comm ticked twice. Dax: *He's early. He's cutting the trunk.*

Then a third tick, and a voice she hadn't expected: Okafor, on the burner channel, low and fast. "Supplemental order came down an hour ago—Calloway security has joint authority at Uplink Control. My hands are tied at the doors. I'm sorry. I'm working the edges but I can't stop this."

The lever. Sefa's first worry, arriving exactly on time. If Latch cut the main trunk before the sync, the lattice would go dark, the ceremony would proceed on cached local feeds, and four hundred thousand people would share their beautiful lie none the wiser. Everything they'd built—the spoof, the pearls, the eleven minutes—would die in a locked room on deck one.

"He leaked the plan," Jonah said aloud, to no one, to herself. "He leaked it weeks ago and we walked straight down the middle of it."

Amara's voice came through, calm as ballast: "Jonah. Say the word."

She stood in the river of festival-goers, a woman with a tray of dumplings and a dead plan, and she thought about the green field in the first pearl she'd ever peeked at, the one that had started all of this. Grass that grew under a dome that was built to fail. Children singing in air that was already being sold out from under them. Somebody had recorded that field knowing what was coming, because recording it was the only weapon left.

The word was yes. The word had always been yes. The only question left was which door they went through.

"Sefa," she said. "Plan B. Crack the box."

A pause on the channel—one heartbeat, two—long enough for Jonah to understand that Sefa had been waiting for exactly this, had maybe been hoping for it, because Sefa did not believe in plans that survived contact and had never once bet on the boat she wasn't willing to lose.

"Copy," Sefa said, and there was a grin in it. "Splicing now. Amara—get me to the core."

Across the promenade, above the crowd, the great screens flickered to life with the Trust's opening montage: blue skies, golden wheat, the founding fleets rising in formation. The lie, in high resolution, right on schedule.

And deep beneath the third-deck junction, in a crawlspace full of color-coded cable, a salvaged black box from a ship that never launched began, at last, to speak.

* * *

The splice took ninety seconds and cost Sefa a burned thumb and most of her composure.

"Core is live," she reported, voice tight over the noise of Amara pushing the tug through maintenance corridors no garbage hauler had any business flying. "I've got physical layer access to the lattice. But Jonah—the trunk's cut. Latch pulled the master cutoff at 1056. Everything south of deck one is isolated. I can broadcast to the core, but the core can't reach the people."

"Then we don't use the trunk," Jonah said. She was moving now, shoving through the festival crowd toward the spire, courier case banging against her ribs, past banners and lanterns and children. "The lattice isn't just the trunk. It's every node. Every screen. Every jack-in point on the station. Where do the nodes get power?"

A pause while Sefa's brain visibly rewired itself. "Local feeds. Each node has its own supply loop for failover—that's regulation, post the Dock Nine blackout. They're designed to fall back to local broadcast if the trunk dies."

"So when Latch cut the trunk—"

"—he didn't kill the lattice," Sefa breathed. "He switched it to failover. To local. And I'm spliced into the core, which is—" a wild laugh, quickly strangled "—which is local to everything. He handed us the station. Jonah, he handed us the whole station."

"Don't celebrate yet," Amara said. "We're sixty seconds out and there's a Calloway skiff shadowing us through the corridors. He'll figure it out the moment the first node wakes up wrong."

"Then we wake them all up at once," Jonah said. "Dax—authentication?"

"Spoof is warm. The Trust's ceremonial key accepts us in twenty seconds, on my mark, and after that they'll know in—" he checked something "—about ninety seconds. Maybe less, if Latch is as good as you think he is."

"He's better," Jonah said. "Do it anyway."

She reached the base of the spire and stopped, chest heaving, in the exact center of Meridian's founding square, under the biggest screen, surrounded by the biggest crowd of the year. Above her, the montage played: wheat, sky, ships. Below her, through the deck plating, she could feel the hum of the lattice waking to a new voice.

On the burner channel, Okafor's voice came through one last time, steady as a handrail: "North team's stalled at a permit dispute I invented. You've got your eleven minutes. Make them count."

"Dax," Jonah said. "Mark."

"Mark."

Twenty seconds of nothing. The crowd laughed at something on the screen. A child on her father's shoulders waved a paper lantern.

Then every node on Meridian Station stuttered, re-authenticated against a key that had died with a colony, and the screens went black—all of them, all at once, in the middle of the Trust's proudest morning.

Into that sudden silence, Jonah opened the courier case, pressed her palm to the transmit plate, and said the words she'd buried eleven years ago:

"My name is Jonah Iversen. Colony liaison, Kestrel's Landing. And I have four hundred twelve things to show you."

The first pearl woke.

And on deck one, in a room full of cut levers and dead switches, Mr. Latch looked at the master cutoff that had done exactly what he'd intended—and understood, with the first genuine emotion he'd shown in years, that he had built the trap, sprung the trap, and locked himself inside it.

"Get me that core," he said quietly. "Now."
