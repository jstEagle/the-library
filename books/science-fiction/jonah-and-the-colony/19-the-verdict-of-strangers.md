## 19. The Verdict of Strangers

The screens went dark at 14:07 station time, and Meridian did not go back to its party.

I stood in the shadow of the lattice relay housing with Sefa's hand clamped around my wrist and watched eleven minutes of the dead play across every public surface on the station. The Founder's Day banners still hung from the atrium gantries. The orchestra had stopped mid-phrase. Somewhere above us a child was crying, and somewhere below us a man was shouting *turn it off*, and nobody turned it off.

Four hundred twelve lives, ending in real time. The dome seals failing one by one. The air thinning. A woman named Priya Castellanos recording her daughter's heartbeat until the recorder itself died. A hydroponics tech named Osei breaking a window to let the last of the outside in, as if Kestrel's sky could be borrowed for a few more breaths. My own voice, younger and steadier than I remembered it, whispering *Jonah, run* — and then running myself, and living, which was its own kind of wound.

When the feed finally guttered out, the silence it left behind was louder than anything Amara had ever flown through.

"They're not moving," Sefa said. "Look. Nobody's moving."

She was right. Ten thousand people stood in the atriums and concourses of Meridian Station, still holding drinks they'd forgotten, still wearing paper crowns from the Founder's Day games, staring at dark screens as if the truth might come back if they waited politely enough. I knew that look. I'd worn it for six years without knowing what it was for.

Then someone in the crowd began to say a name. Just one name, over and over, getting louder: "Castellanos. Castellanos. Castellanos."

And then another voice picked up a different name. And another. And by the time the station security drones arrived to restore order, there was nothing left to restore, because order had already changed hands.

* * *

They came for Calloway House within the hour.

Not the enforcers — those had scattered or been scattered, Latch cuffed on the concourse floor with Okafor's knee in his back and her stunner against his spine, choosing sides out loud where everyone could hear it. No, what came for Calloway House was the crowd itself, ten thousand strong, carrying the names like torches they didn't need to light because every screen on the station was already burning.

Dax found me in the maintenance crawlway and pulled me up by the arm. "You have to see this," he said, and his voice had a crack in it I'd never heard before. Dax, who sold other people's secrets for a living and never cracked. "You have to see what you did."

What we did, I almost said. But he'd have shoved me back down the crawlway, so I kept it behind my teeth and followed him up into the light.

Calloway Biologics' local charter office occupied the most expensive real estate on the station's third ring: white stone imported at ruinous cost, a lobby fountain that ran on recycled water while the lower decks queued at ration taps. The crowd had not broken the glass. That was the thing that stayed with me afterward, when I told this story and people refused to believe it. They had not broken the glass. They had stood outside it, shoulder to shoulder, thousands deep, and read the names aloud through it — all four hundred twelve, taking turns, passing them down the line like buckets in a fire chain — until the people inside the building could not hear their own thoughts.

Inside, Calloway's station director was already gone. So were the lawyers, the accountants, the security liaisons. What remained was a skeleton crew of clerks who opened the doors themselves, because staying shut had become more dangerous than opening, and began handing out whatever documents the mob demanded. Charters. Shipping manifests. The internal memos about the dome seals — *known defect, batch 7-C, ship anyway, arbitration exposure acceptable* — printed off and passed hand to hand until the words were literally in everyone's mouths.

"Exposure acceptable," Sefa read over my shoulder, and spat on the marble floor. In Sefa's dialect, I would learn later, that was a curse with real weight. It meant: may your house fall and no one remember to mourn it.

By end of shift, the station council — five administrators who had spent the morning insisting they needed time, process, deliberation — convened an emergency session in the open atrium, in front of everyone, because the alternative was being convened by the crowd instead. They voted unanimously to suspend Calloway's operating charter pending investigation. The vote took four minutes. It had taken Calloway nine years to buy the charter in the first place.

And Justice Merrin Hale — Arbitrator Hale, who had presided over the tribunal where a colony liaison named Jonah Iversen testified about defective seals and was bought and dismissed and scheduled for remediation — Justice Hale packed two bags and boarded a private shuttle bound for the inner system before the vote finished counting.

Amara told me that part later, with a pilot's flat satisfaction. "He didn't even file a flight plan. Twenty years I've known that man's departure schedules down to the second — he used to make the whole third ring wait for his commute. Tonight he ran like a thief." She turned her cup in her hands. "You know what the worst part is? He'll land somewhere warm and tell himself he was always innocent. Memory's a forgiving country. I lived there long enough to know."

"We could broadcast his face," Dax said. "Name the shuttle. Let the inner system know what's arriving."

"No," I said, and they all looked at me. "Not yet. He ran. Running is its own confession, and it'll follow him longer than any broadcast would. We keep the evidence. We don't become the thing we're broadcasting against." I heard my own voice and was surprised by it — steady, older than the woman in the memory-pearl, older than Gideon the smuggler. It sounded like someone who had testified before. Because she had. Because I had.

* * *

The summons came at 03:00, delivered not by drone or courier but by Inspector Vey Okafor herself, standing in the doorway of our battered berth with her badge held slightly away from her body, as if she wasn't sure anymore whose authority it carried.

"You're wanted," she said. "Tomorrow, 10:00, central hearing room. Public session."

"Wanted by whom?"

Okafor's mouth did something complicated. "Officially? The station council's investigative tribunal, exercising emergency powers under the charter suspension. Unofficially?" She glanced past me into the berth, where Sefa was asleep with her boots on and Dax was pretending not to listen. "Unofficially, by ten thousand people who spent last night learning your name. Ms. Iversen. Both of your names. They want to hear it from you."

"I'm not a witness," I said. "I'm contraband. That's what I've been my whole life since Kestrel's Landing — something smuggled past inspection. You don't put contraband on the stand."

"You put it wherever it does the most damage," Okafor said. "That's what I've learned this week." She hesitated, and then, quieter: "For what it's worth — I flagged your shipments three months ago because your record was too clean. Perfect compliance is a kind of lie, and I hate lies even the honest ones. I was going to break you, Ms. Iversen. Instead you broke everything else open." She straightened. "10:00. Wear something that isn't smuggler's gray. Half the station will be watching, and the other half will be watching the half that's watching."

After she left, Dax sat down across from me at the little table where we'd built the decryption rig out of cannibalized ship parts. "You don't have to do it their way," he said. "We could refuse. Make them subpoena you. Make it look like coercion — sympathy plays better."

"I'm done playing better," I said. "I played better for six years. Better got four hundred twelve people a mass grave and a purged registry." I looked at my hands. Smuggler's hands, pearl-handler's hands, and underneath them, somewhere, a liaison's hands that had once held testimony like a weapon and lost. "They get me tomorrow. All of me. Whatever's left after the erasure, whatever Dax reconstructed, whatever I can't prove — all of it, out loud, under my own name."

Dax nodded slowly. Then he reached across the table and did something he'd never done: he took my hand, briefly, and let go. "Jonah," he said, testing the name like a key in a lock. "Yeah. It fits."

* * *

The central hearing room had been built for commerce disputes — tariff appeals, contract arbitrations, the polite knife-fighting of station business. It seated two hundred. By 09:30 there were four thousand people in the atrium outside, and the council had ordered the doors propped open and screens mounted in the corridor, because the alternative was a riot, and everyone including the rioters preferred a hearing.

I walked in wearing Amara's old flight jacket, union patches faded on both shoulders, because it was the only honest clothing I owned. Sefa had tried to give me a pressed collar. I'd refused. "I'm not testifying as someone respectable," I told her. "I'm testifying as what they made me. The jacket tells the truth faster than I can."

The tribunal sat five: three council members, an independent ombudsman hauled out of retirement, and — to my genuine surprise — Inspector Okafor, appointed that morning as the enforcement seat. She wouldn't meet my eye as I approached. Later she'd tell me it was because if she had, she'd have smiled, and smiling at a witness compromises a hearing. Okafor believed in procedure the way some people believe in gods, and after this week, I understood why. When everything else is bought, the rules are the last thing standing that can't be bribed — only broken, publicly, by people willing to be seen breaking them.

A clerk swore me in. Then the ombudsman, a dry old woman named Ferro with eyes like audit software, said: "State your name for the record."

And here is the thing I had not anticipated, though I should have. Six years I had answered to Gideon. Six years the name Jonah had been a splinter under my skin, a manifest entry, a ghost's signature. I had rehearsed saying it aloud in the dark of the berth, and it had come out fine in rehearsal.

But with four thousand people listening, and the lattice relays carrying my voice to every deck of Meridian Station, I opened my mouth and what came out first was the truth under the truth:

"My name is Jonah Iversen. For the last six years I've also been called Gideon, which is the name I bought along with my amnesia, and I answer to it, but it isn't mine. The woman who testified before Arbitrator Hale on the twelfth of Thaw-Month, nine years ago — that was me. I'm here to finish what she started."

The room made a sound I'd never heard a room make. Not gasping, exactly. More like a great inhale held by ten thousand lungs at once.

Ferro let it run out. Then: "Proceed."

So I proceeded. I told them about the dome seals — batch 7-C, the stress fractures documented in Calloway's own quality reports, the memo ordering shipment anyway because delay would cost more than the settlement ceiling. I told them how I'd found the reports as colony liaison, how I'd filed testimony, how the tribunal had adjourned for forty minutes and reconvened with new language in the ruling, language I hadn't written, dismissing my findings as *unsubstantiated vendor criticism*. I told them about the man in the gray suit who visited my quarters that evening and explained, courteously, the way Mr. Latch explains things, that I had two options: remediation and resettlement assistance, or a long stay somewhere with poor ventilation.

"And you chose to forget," Ferro said. Not accusing. Establishing.

"I chose to forget." My voice held. I was almost proud of it. "I want that in the record clearly, because people will want to make it either heroism or cowardice, and it was neither. It was arithmetic. A dead witness testifies to nothing; an erased one at least stays alive, and alive meant maybe someday—" I stopped. Started again. "Alive meant maybe someday I'd come back. The technician at the clinic told me I left a message for myself. *If you came back, you already know why.* I didn't understand it for six years. I understand it now. I came back because four hundred twelve people deserved a witness who couldn't be disappeared twice."

"Ms. Iversen." This was one of the council members, a smooth man named Aldous Rennick whose district had taken Calloway sponsorship money for a decade. I watched him choose his words like a man walking on ice he'd personally weakened. "You're asking this tribunal to accept extraordinary claims based substantially on recovered memories — memories you yourself purchased to destroy. How can the tribunal verify testimony from a mind that admits it was altered?"

It was a good question. It was the question. And I had spent the whole night before knowing it would come, which is why Dax was sitting in the third row with a case at his feet.

"Chain of custody," I said. "The same standard you'd apply to cargo. The memory-pearls recovered from Kestrel's Landing are original recordings, unedited, timestamped by the colony's own network — you have Sefa Okonjo's forensic certification and Inspector Okafor's independent verification. My erased testimony exists as a corrupted arbitration recording that Dax Meridian reconstructed; the reconstruction methodology is published and reproducible. And the physical evidence — the defective seal samples, the shipping logs, the batch records your own clerks handed to the crowd last night — none of it depends on my memory at all. My memory is just the map. The territory's been sitting in your archives the whole time, waiting for someone to look."

Rennick tried again — questions about my smuggling record, my criminal history, whether a confessed trafficker in contraband memories could be a credible witness. I gave him the only answer I had.

"Everything I ever smuggled was grief," I said. "Recordings of funerals, of last conversations, of children grown and parents dead — things the customs code calls unauthorized emotional data and I call the only inheritance some people have left. Yes, I broke the law. Thousands of times. I'd do it again tomorrow, and if this tribunal suspends my license for saying so, then suspend it. But understand what you'd be punishing: for six years the only crime I committed consistently was refusing to let this station's laws decide whose love counted as legitimate freight." I leaned toward the pickup. "You want to assess my credibility? Ask yourselves why the law I broke was written to protect Calloway's market, and the law Calloway broke was written to protect no one at all."

The inhale again, ten thousand lungs. And this time, when it released, it released as sound — not applause, Meridian crowds don't applaud tribunals, but something better: the murmur of consensus forming, the audible noise of a verdict assembling itself out of raw strangers.

* * *

The tribunal deliberated for six hours, which Ferro later admitted was theatrical — the outcome was never in doubt after the seal samples were passed hand to hand among the five seats. But theater matters. Procedure matters. If they'd decided in ten minutes it would have looked like the mob had simply replaced Calloway as the authority, and Meridian would have learned the wrong lesson: that truth is whatever enough angry people insist on. Instead they took six hours, reviewed the certifications, cross-examined Dax on his reconstruction methods until he was sweating through his shirt, and issued findings in writing, in public, read aloud by Ferro in a voice like a closing ledger:

Callaway Biologics' local charter: suspended permanently, assets frozen, parent company referred to system authorities with a formal request for interstellar investigation. Arbitrator Merrin Hale: stripped of office in absentia, his rulings from the tainted tribunal voided and re-opened. The Kestrel's Landing massacre: reclassified from *industrial accident, sealed record* to *mass homicide, active investigation*. And the names — all four hundred twelve — entered into the permanent public record of Meridian Station, to be read aloud annually on the anniversary, on every screen, by law.

"By law," Ferro repeated, looking directly into the pickups. "Not by charity. Charity gets revoked when it becomes inconvenient. Law doesn't. That is the difference between what happened nine years ago and what happens today."

Outside, the crowd did not cheer. They did something I'll carry longer than the fear or the grief. They read the names again — all of them, four hundred twelve voices passing the list down the atrium like a flame — and when they finished, they said my name once, together, not as praise but as inclusion. *Iversen.* Like I'd been added to the manifest.

Okafor found me at the edge of it, off-duty now, out of uniform, holding two cups of terrible station coffee. She handed me one.

"For the record," she said, "I arrested a corporate enforcer, defied my superiors, fed classified fragments to a smuggler, and perjured myself exactly once — I told the tribunal I had no personal stake in your testimony." She sipped her coffee. "I'd do it again."

"That's not perjury," I said. "You do have a stake. You just paid for it honestly."

She almost smiled. Then her comm chimed, and her face changed, and the day tilted.

"Jonah." She said my name carefully, like handling live ordnance. "Custody transfer request just came through for Latch. Inner-system jurisdiction — Calloway's parent company claims him as a corporate asset, wants him moved off-station tonight." Her jaw tightened. "Standard procedure says I comply. But standard procedure also said I should drop the Kestrel file, and we both remember how that went." She looked at me. "There's a transport prepping at dock nine right now. And Latch has been asking for you. By name. Both of them."

I looked out at the atrium, at the crowd still murmuring four hundred twelve names into the bones of the station, at the screens where Ferro's findings scrolled on loop. We had won today. I understood, with the particular clarity of someone who has been robbed before, that winning is when they come to take it back.

"Dock nine," I said. "Let's go hear what he's selling."
