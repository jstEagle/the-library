## 2. Eleven Winters

The archive room sat at the heart of the vault like a heart indeed—a small warm chamber in a body of cold, its walls lined with data-slates and paper, actual paper, brittle as moth wings. Cyrus went there on the last day of every winter cycle, when the inventory was done and the vault hummed its long note of preservation and there was nothing left to do but remember.

The caretakers' logs were kept in strict order. Forty-one years of them, bound by regulation into numbered sequences: *Caretaker's Log, Svalgard Seed Vault, Annual Record.* The first entries were typed. The middle ones were handwritten in a slanting, hurried script that belonged to a woman named Odile Ferrant. The last eleven were Cyrus's own voice, spoken aloud into a recorder because typing had begun to feel too much like talking to no one, and speaking at least made the silence answerable.

Cyrus poured tea from the dented kettle—real tea, hoarded, rationed to one cup a week and spent gladly on this night—and set the oldest slate on the reader.

*Annual Record One. Caretaker: E. Vasquez.*

The voice that came out was dry and brisk, a man reading from a checklist because a checklist was all he trusted himself with.

*"Supply ship Meridian's Promise arrived 14 March, standard orbit, full resupply. Crew of six. Cargo manifest attached. Note for my successor: the cook, Anselm, makes a bread you will dream about. Be kind to him. He is far from his daughter."*

Cyrus smiled at that, the way one smiles at a photograph of strangers dancing. Vasquez had served nine years. His final log was shorter.

*"Ship arrived late. Crew of four this time. They said the colonies are voting on something called the Charter of Separation. Politics is not my department. The seeds are fine. The ice is fine. I am going home to see the sea before it changes."*

Then Odile Ferrant, whose handwriting looked like wind through wheat. Her first year overlapped the war's beginning, though nobody called it a war yet. They called it *the disagreement*, then *the crisis*, then, after the colony ship *Halcyon Reach* went silent with eleven thousand souls aboard, they stopped calling it anything at all and simply called it the Meridian War, as if naming it had been the last courtesy anyone could afford.

*"Supply ship arrived four months late,"* Odile recorded in her sixth year. *"Half cargo. They took back our medical stores—requisitioned, the officer said, for the war effort. I asked him which side he meant. He didn't laugh. Nobody laughs anymore."*

Her seventh year: *"No ship."*

Her eighth year: *"No ship. I have begun keeping bees again. There is a tradition of it here, from the old polar stations. If no one comes, I will at least have honey."*

Her ninth year, the last: *"I am leaving on the final evacuation charter. My replacement has been selected but cannot arrive before the window closes. To whoever finds this: the vault does not need you. That is the secret of it. It needs almost nothing—power, cold, an hour of your morning. What it needs is someone willing to be needed by almost nothing. I hope that sentence makes sense to whoever you are now. I hope you chose this. I did not, exactly. But I stayed anyway, and I do not regret it, and that is the same thing in the end."*

Cyrus touched the slate's edge, where Odile's fingerprint still ghosted the surface, oils preserved by cold like everything else in the vault.

"I chose it," Cyrus said aloud, to the empty room, to the recorders, to the forty-one years of dead voices. "You can put that in the file."

Outside, the aurora moved over the ice sheet in slow green curtains, and the vault breathed its refrigerated breath, and somewhere below, in darkness and deep cold, three hundred thousand seeds waited out a war their keepers had started without asking them.

* * *

Sleep came hard on inventory nights, and Cyrus had learned not to fight it. Instead there was the ritual of the cot, the thermal blanket pulled to the chin, and the ritual of the memory—the one memory that had any right to be replayed, because it was the reason for all of this, the root system under everything.

Her name was Wren. She had been twenty-four and fearless in the specific way of people who have decided the universe owes them a fair hearing. When the colonial recruitment posters went up—*SEED THE STARS, CARRY EARTH'S GARDENS TO NEW SOILS*—Wren had taped one to the refrigerator door of their shared apartment and stood in front of it with her arms crossed like a lawyer presenting evidence.

"Look at it," she'd said. "Look how happy everyone is. Nobody looks that happy unless they're lying or telling the truth so hard it hurts."

"You believe a poster?"

"I believe tomatoes don't grow on ships, Cy. Somebody has to bring the gardens. Why not me?" And then, softer, the way she said things that mattered: "Why not come with me?"

Cyrus had been twenty-six, mid-degree, terrified of vacuum and committed to a life measured in seasons rather than light-years. "Somebody has to stay and mind the store."

"Somebody," Wren agreed, and kissed the top of their head the way she had since they were children, and that was the last ordinary thing.

The *Halcyon Reach* departed on a Tuesday. For eight months it sent back routine telemetry—course true, systems nominal, Wren sending messages that got shorter as the novelty wore off and funnier as her real self reasserted itself. *Day 61: I have named the seed bay after you. It's full of beans. Make of that what you will.* *Day 118: Saw a nebula today. Looked like spilled milk. Wren says hi.* *Day 190: Ask me about the hydroponics scandal sometime. Actually don't. Miss you. Beans are thriving.*

On day 214, the telemetry stopped.

Not failed—stopped. Clean, simultaneous, across every channel, which engineers later said was impossible for any natural cause. The official finding was a drive cascade, a catastrophic malfunction, tragic, unavoidable, our hearts go out. The colonies blamed a Directorate weapons test. The Directorate blamed colonial saboteurs. Eleven thousand people became a dispute about jurisdiction before their bodies had even finished drifting.

Cyrus remembered the exact texture of those weeks: the apartment that would not stop being quiet, the refrigerator door with the poster still taped to it, the message thread Cyrus could not delete and could not open. Remembered applying to the caretaker program eight months later, writing in the motivation field a single sentence that the selection board presumably found either moving or alarming: *Someone should be minding the store.*

The vault was the opposite of a ship. A ship went; the vault stayed. A ship carried seeds toward futures; the vault held them against futures that might never want them. Cyrus had told the interview panel it was about stewardship. It was about standing still. It was about being the person who stays by the phone.

Eleven years now. Wren would be thirty-five. In Cyrus's private arithmetic—which no log recorded, which no audit would ever find—Wren was thirty-five, and probably laughing at something, wherever the drift had taken her, some cold bright orbit where spilled-milk nebulas turned forever.

"Beans are thriving," Cyrus said to the dark ceiling. "I checked today. Yours especially. I keep your bay running at optimum. Don't tell the others."

The others being three hundred thousand seeds. The joke worked better than most jokes had a right to, alone at the bottom of the world, and Cyrus slept.

* * *

Morning brought the supply review, which was less a task than a wake. Every caretaker maintained a projected-resupply ledger, updated quarterly, and Cyrus had kept the habit long past the point of function. The ledger was a grid: expected tonnage against received tonnage, year over year, a seismograph of abandonment.

Year one of Cyrus's tenure: sixty-two percent of projected supplies delivered. Enough. More than enough, really—the projections had always been padded.

Year two: forty percent. The ships came smaller and the crews came tired, and one dock officer, a woman with a colonial accent she didn't bother hiding anymore, had stood in the airlock and said, "Eat everything they send you. I mean it. Eat it fast. There won't be a next time worth planning around."

Year three: eighteen percent. Spare parts only. No food beyond calories-by-formula, no luxuries, no mail. Mail had ended quietly, without announcement, somewhere in year two, and Cyrus had never mentioned it in a log because mentioning it would make it true.

Year four: a single automated drone, pre-programmed, no crew, delivering fuel cells and a printed directive: *CONSOLIDATE CONSUMPTION. EXTEND AUTONOMY INDEFINITELY.* Indefinitely. As if indefinitely were a plan and not a dare.

Year five onward: nothing. Not even the drone. The Directorate's last communication was a form letter acknowledging receipt of Cyrus's annual report—an annual report describing a facility holding the agricultural heritage of two worlds—and wishing the caretaker continued success in their vital mission. The letter was signed by a sub-administrator's office. The signature was a font.

Cyrus closed the ledger and sat with it a while, the way one sits with a grave.

Here was the strange arithmetic of it, the part that never made it into the official reports: the vault did not care. The cold held. The power flowed, fed by geothermal taps drilled a century ago by optimists who assumed maintenance crews forever. The seeds slept their patient sleep, indifferent to politics, to borders, to the slow withdrawal of the human attention that had built their tomb-cradle. If Cyrus walked out tomorrow—if Cyrus died tonight—the vault would run on for decades, maybe a century, a library with no librarian, a garden with no gardener, until some failure cascaded or some future hand opened the door.

So why stay? The question arrived on schedule, as it did every winter's end, and Cyrus let it sit in the room like a guest who meant well.

Because the seeds couldn't choose. Because Wren had gone out carrying Earth's gardens and the going had killed her, and somebody owed those gardens a keeper who stayed. Because Odile Ferrant had written *I hope you chose this* and Cyrus had chosen it, stubbornly, spitefully almost, chosen it the way you plant a tree whose shade you'll never sit in. Because if the war ever ended—if anyone ever came looking—they would find the vault intact, the logs complete, the work faithful, and they would know that at least one person on either side of the Meridian line had refused to stop tending things while the shooting was on.

That was the duty. Not orders—there had been no orders in seven years. Duty as a private religion, with rituals of tea and inventory and one candle burned each winter for a ship full of gardeners.

Cyrus rose, dressed, and began the morning round: reactor readouts, coolant levels, humidity seals, the long walk down Corridor Nine where the bean bays lived. At Bay 114—the bay with Wren's name stenciled inside the access panel, invisible to any inspector, legible only to love—Cyrus ran the diagnostic twice, logged the results, and stood a moment in the blue-white grow-light glow with a hand flat against the glass.

"Still here," Cyrus told the seeds. "Both of us."

It was on the walk back, passing the communications alcove with its array of aging receivers—the equipment maintained out of protocol rather than expectation, a telephone installed in a house where everyone who might call was dead—that the console chimed.

Not the weekly time-sync ping. Not the ionospheric static that passed for weather up here. A chime. An incoming-carrier alert, the kind the manual described in a section titled *Historical Function*.

Cyrus stopped in the corridor. The chime sounded again, patient, deliberate, like breathing.

Nobody had transmitted to the Svalgard Vault in seven years. Nobody alive, as far as the ledgers knew, even remembered it was listening.

Cyrus stood very still in the cold corridor, hand still warm from the glass of Bay 114, and watched the receiver's signal-strength meter climb, and thought—with the sudden vertigo of a person whose long vigil has just been answered—*someone knows we're here.*
