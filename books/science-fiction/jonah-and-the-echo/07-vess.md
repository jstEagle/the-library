## 7. Vess

The Perennial smelled of wet iron and old bread.

Vess Anandar had stopped noticing it years ago—the way you stop noticing your own heartbeat—but tonight, crouched in the maintenance crawlspace behind the ship's tertiary comms array, the smell was all she could think about. Wet iron. Old bread. The smell of eleven thousand people living inside a tin can that had forgotten where it was going.

She pressed her cheek against the conduit housing and listened. Not to the ship—to the silence underneath it. Somewhere above her, two decks up and forty meters aft, Enforcer Korren was making his rounds, his boots ringing on the deck plates in the slow, bored rhythm of a man who had done this walk four thousand times and would do it four thousand more. She'd memorized his cadence the way sailors once memorized weather. *Step-step-pause. Step-step-pause.* He lingered at the hydroponics bay because the grow-lamps were warm and the air there tasted of green things. He hurried past the memorial wall because nobody liked standing next to nine hundred names.

He would pass the tertiary array without stopping. He always did. Nobody guarded the dead frequencies.

That was the whole idea.

* * *

The Reformation of Tongues had happened two hundred years before either of them was born, back when Earth still believed it could remake itself by decree. The story they taught in the Perennial's schoolrooms was gentle about it: languages had grown tangled, communication across the colonies had become inefficient, so the Directorate had standardized. One tongue for administration. One for commerce. One for the fleet. Clean as a surgeon's table.

What the schoolrooms didn't say—or said only in the flat voice reserved for uncomfortable facts—was what standardization required. It required that someone decide which words were safe. And once someone decides which words are safe, someone else has to decide which words are not. And once a word is decided against, everything ever written in it becomes contraband by inheritance. Two centuries of poetry, law, confession, and grief—filed under *obsolete*, sealed in archives with Directorate locks, and slowly, quietly, allowed to rot.

Old Terran had been the first to go. The mother tongue. The language of the launch manifests and the founding charters and the early distress calls. It died officially in the year the last native speaker's clearance expired, and unofficially long before that, strangled by policy and embarrassment.

Which meant, Vess thought, wedging herself deeper into the crawlspace as Korren's boots passed overhead, that Old Terran was the one language left in human space that no censor knew how to read.

Dead tongues can't be censored. You can't redact what no one alive is authorized to understand.

Her grandmother had taught her that. Her grandmother, who had been a philologist in the old sense—a person who loved languages the way other people loved children—and who had spent her retirement teaching a girl of seven to conjugate verbs from a civilization that no longer existed. *"They took our words, little bird,"* she used to say, *"but they couldn't take all of them. They weren't fast enough. Nothing is ever fast enough."*

Vess had thought it was sentimentality then. The rambling of an old woman with too many books.

Now it was tradecraft.

* * *

The transmitter was hers in the way that stolen things are yours—with guilt and tenderness at once. She'd built it from salvage over three years: a decommissioned relay coil from the water reclamation plant, a signal amplifier cannibalized from a defunct nursery monitor, capacitors bought piecemeal from a supply clerk who asked no questions because she paid him in coffee real enough to have come from actual beans. The whole assembly weighed less than her mother's sewing machine and drew power like a night-light, which was the point. A transmission that couldn't be felt on the ship's power grid was a transmission that didn't exist.

She connected the final lead, waited through three full cycles of Korren's footsteps, and keyed the array live.

The Perennial was, officially, a seed-ship. That was the joke none of them laughed at anymore. Eleven thousand colonists aboard a vessel designed to carry genetic stock to a world that had never been surveyed, launched during the last optimistic year before the Meridian War swallowed the sky. They were supposed to arrive at a green planet. Instead they arrived at a war, at a blockade, at orders rerouting them into deep patrol space where they now drifted in slow ellipses, ostensibly conducting "long-duration agricultural viability studies."

In truth, they were hostages. The Directorate called it protective custody. The colonial assembly, such as it remained, called it nothing at all, because calling it anything would have required admitting they couldn't do a single thing about it.

Eleven thousand people. Growing food in trays. Waiting.

Vess had spent the first years waiting too. It was the archive discovery that ended her patience—the day she'd found, buried in the ship's legacy database, a fragment of pre-Reformation fleet correspondence that should not have existed. A routing header. A timestamp. Three seconds of telemetry from the Meridian approach corridor dated six hours *before* the attack everyone blamed on the colonies.

Six hours. The discrepancy was small. Discrepancies always are. But she was a linguist, and linguists know that meaning lives in the smallest units—that a single phoneme can turn blessing into curse, that a misplaced pause can start a war or confess one.

Someone had moved those six hours. Someone had edited the record. And whoever held the unedited version held the war's throat in their hand.

She'd been transmitting for fourteen months since.

* * *

The protocol was simple and maddeningly slow. She compressed her messages into Old Terran phonetic structures, wrapped them in the mathematical padding of seed-catalog cipher—an old agricultural encoding scheme, obsolete and therefore invisible—aimed the array at nothing in particular, and broadcast blind. No handshake. No return address. No expectation.

Blind transmission, her grandmother's books called it. The practice of speaking into the dark without confirmation, the way lighthouse keepers once kept their lamps lit through fog so thick the lamp itself couldn't be seen from the water. You didn't light the lamp for the ships you could see. You lit it for the ships that might be there.

Fourteen months of lighting lamps.

Tonight's message was the same as the last forty: coordinates, encrypted in the old syllabary, pointing toward a place she had found in a corrupted manifest—a place called Svalgard. A seed vault on a frozen world, flagged in the records with a Directorate seal class she'd never seen before and couldn't decode. *Restricted heritage asset. Custodial access only.*

A vault that grew seeds did not need custodial access restrictions. A vault that grew seeds did not need its location stripped from public charts. Whatever Svalgard held, it wasn't only seeds.

She finished the transmission block, watched the power draw flicker and settle, and began the wait. Ninety seconds of transmit, then silence, then the long hours of listening on frequencies no one monitored, in case the universe bothered to answer.

It never had.

* * *

Afterward she climbed down through the access trunk to Deck Nine, where her family's quarters were, and stood for a moment in the corridor breathing the smell of other people's dinners. Lentils again. The hydroponics yield had dropped eight percent this quarter and the menu planners had responded with the culinary equivalent of a shrug.

Home was compartment 9-C, four bunks and a fold-out table, where her mother sat mending a pressure suit liner by lamplight and her younger brother Dov was assembling a model of a ship neither of them would ever fly.

"You're late," her mother said, not looking up.

"Array inspection. Tertiary systems." The lie came out smooth now, worn soft as river stone. Fourteen months of lying will do that. "Condensation in the waveguide housings again."

"Mm." Her mother bit her thread. "Korren came by. Asking whether you'd been spending time in the upper decks."

The needle in Vess's chest went in deep and came out clean. "I inspect the arrays, Ma. That's my rotation."

"He's Enforcer rotation," her mother said. "You're life-support rotation. Those aren't the same decks." She looked up then, and her eyes in the lamplight were tired and sharp at once, the eyes of a woman who had raised three children through a war that happened entirely out the window. "Be careful, little bird."

The old name landed like a hand on the back of the neck. Her grandmother's name for her, inherited like everything else—like the books, like the languages, like the stubbornness. Her mother knew. Vess understood suddenly, completely, the way you understand a thing you've known for years: her mother had always known. Had perhaps known since the first month, since the nights Vess came home smelling of ozone and dust from the crawlspaces, since the coffee that vanished from the ration lockers a gram at a time.

She didn't say it. Saying it would make it real, and real things could be arrested.

"I'm always careful," Vess said.

"That's what your grandmother said," her mother replied, bending back to her mending. "Right up until they came for her books."

* * *

She slept badly, which was normal, and woke before shift-bell, which was also normal, and made her way to the listening post—which was not normal, because the listening post was a storage locker off the coolant galleries that she'd claimed under the fiction of archiving thermal logs.

She put on the headphones. She let the static pour in.

This was the part no one warned you about, she thought. Not the danger—danger was simple, danger was boots in corridors and questions from enforcers. What no one warned you about was the faith. The daily, grinding, unglamorous act of believing that anyone was out there. That the signals crossed the dark and found anything warmer than vacuum. That somewhere, in some frozen place or drifting hull, another human being was doing exactly what she was doing: pressing an ear to the wall of the universe and hoping.

Fourteen months. Five hundred and eleven transmissions. She had built each one carefully, addressed them to no one, signed them with nothing but a string of cipher that meant, if anyone ever decoded it, *I am here. I am listening. Tell me I'm not alone.*

There were nights she lay awake composing rebuttals to her own hope. *No one hears you. The arrays are jammed. The vaults are empty. Everyone who might have cared is dead, and the dark doesn't care about your grandmother's languages.* There were mornings she nearly dismantled the transmitter, nearly told herself the war was too big and she was too small and a girl with a salvaged relay coil could not argue with fleets.

But then she thought of the six hours. The missing six hours, sitting in the record like a pulled tooth. Eleven thousand people on this ship alone, drifting in custody, raising children in a lie so large they lived inside it without seeing its walls. And somewhere out there, presumably, other ships—other thousands, tens of thousands—all of them fed the same story, all of them forbidden the same words.

You couldn't fight a fleet. But you could refuse to let a lie finish eating the record. That was a thing one person could do. That was a thing a linguist could do better than almost anyone.

Her grandmother had a saying for it, one of the few she'd left untranslated, in a dialect dead even by Old Terran standards. Vess whispered it now to the static, a private ritual, a key turned in an empty lock:

*The lamp does not ask the sea's permission.*

She settled in for the morning listen. Static, static, the long hiss of a universe indifferent to small courage. She logged the bands, checked the drift, recalibrated. Routine. Ritual. The same as every day for fourteen months.

She was reaching to shut the array down when the waveform stuttered.

Not interference. Interference had a texture she knew intimately—solar hash, engine wash, the rhythmic stammer of the Perennial's own aging systems. This was different. This was structured. A carrier tone rising out of the noise floor like a shape surfacing through fog, weak, wavering, half-swallowed by distance—

—and repeating.

Repeating, she realized with a jolt that went through her like cold water, in a pattern she recognized. Syllabic stress groups. Old Terran prosody. Someone, somewhere, was broadcasting in a language two centuries dead, using the same cipher padding she used, aimed—at her? at anyone? at the dark?—with the same desperate, blind persistence she had taught herself.

Her hands were shaking. She locked the frequency, captured the raw feed, and stared at the scrolling analysis while her heart tried to climb out through her ribs.

The source vector resolved slowly, degree by degree, out of the noise.

It pointed toward the cold. Toward the edge of the old survey charts. Toward a coordinate she had spoken into the dark five hundred times without answer.

Toward Svalgard.

Someone had heard her. Someone was answering.

Vess sat very still in the storage locker, headphones pressed to her ears, listening to a stranger's voice arrive across light-years in the tongue of the dead—and for the first time in fourteen months, the lamp saw a light coming back.
