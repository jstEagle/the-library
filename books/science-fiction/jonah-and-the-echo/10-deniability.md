## 10. Deniability

The hail came at 0400 vault time, which was to say it came in the dark, in the cold hours when Cyrus's body had learned to expect nothing but the hum of the compressors and the small animal sounds of their own breathing.

They were awake anyway. They had been awake for three days.

The alert tone was one Cyrus had never heard outside the manuals—a triple chime descending through the octaves, the sound the vault made when something with a registry number spoke to it. Not a relay ping. Not debris telemetry. A voice.

Cyrus stood in the comms alcove with their hand hovering over the accept key for a full minute, watching their own reflection ghosted in the frosted display: a thin figure in a thermal suit gone grey at the elbows, hair cropped short for convenience rather than style, eyes that had not slept properly since the coordinates resolved into a ship's deceleration burn. Behind the reflection, the corridor stretched away into blue-lit dark, and somewhere down that dark, behind four sealed bulkheads and a door that should not have existed, an archive sat waiting like a loaded weapon.

*Heritage asset recovery.* The flag on the transponder. As if the vault were a painting in a flooded gallery. As if Cyrus were part of the inventory.

They pressed the key.

"Vault Svalgard, this is the retrieval vessel *Charybdis*, Earth Directorate Registry 77-Kilo-9." A woman's voice, level and warm in the way that trained voices are warm—warmth applied like a coating, like anti-corrosion paint. "We are entering your approach corridor on a scheduled heritage inspection. Please confirm your operational status and stand by for formal contact."

Scheduled. Cyrus almost laughed. There had been no schedule in eleven years. The last schedule had died with the supply runs, sometime around winter six, when the manifests stopped coming and the caretaker manual quietly dropped its section titled *Relief Rotation*.

They keyed the mic. Their voice came out rusty, too loud.

"This is Caretaker Kavanagh. Vault is operational. I—" *I what? I know why you're here. I've been rerouting power for two days. I have a dead language in my ears and a Directorate seal burned into a door I broke open.* "I wasn't expecting anyone."

A pause. Long enough to be polite. Short enough to be deliberate.

"We understand, Caretaker. Conditions being what they are, inspections fall behind schedule. We'll be in orbit within the day. Captain Reyen will handle formalities personally. Please continue your normal operations."

Normal operations. The channel closed with a click like a lock turning.

Cyrus stood in the alcove for a long time afterward, listening to the compressors breathe, and understood that the conversation had already begun—that every word from here forward would be a move on a board they could not see, against a player who had read the whole game before sitting down.

* * *

They spent the morning doing the most dangerous thing possible: nothing unusual.

Cyrus walked the seed galleries with their tablet, running the quarterly inventory that wasn't due for another month, scanning rack after rack of foil-sealed drawers, calling up accession numbers for wheat strains that hadn't grown under open sky in two centuries. The ritual of it steadied them. *Triticum aestivum, landrace, Kavir basin, accession 44-118.* Each drawer a small promise that someone, someday, would plant again. They had made this walk ten thousand times. It was the only honest thing left in the building.

But honesty, they were beginning to understand, was about to become a currency they could no longer afford to spend.

At 1300, the second hail came, and this time it was Reyen herself.

"Caretaker Kavanagh. Captain Ilsa Reyen." The face that filled the display was older than her voice, sharp-boned, with the particular stillness of someone who had spent decades watching other people for a living. Her uniform was immaculate in the way only shipboard uniforms were—no dust anywhere in the universe of her frame. "Thank you for receiving me. I'll try not to take much of your time."

"You're taking all of it," Cyrus said, before they could stop themselves. "There isn't much else here."

Something moved at the corner of her mouth. Not quite a smile. An acknowledgment of a correct answer.

"That's what I wanted to talk to you about, actually. Time. Eleven years, is it? Alone?"

"Eleven this spring."

"That's remarkable endurance." She said it as though reading it off a form. "Most caretakers request rotation by year three. The psychological attrition rates for long-haul isolation postings are—well. You'd know better than I. You lived them."

"I didn't request rotation," Cyrus said. "There was no one to rotate."

"No," Reyen agreed softly. "There wasn't."

And there it was—the first probe sliding under the skin, so smooth Cyrus felt the entry only afterward. She knew the history. Of course she knew the history. She had probably read the logs—all of them, eleven years of daily recordings made to an audience of zero—and somewhere in those logs was everything: the grief, the sister, the slow erosion, and lately, if the transmission intercepts had been pulled, three days of anomalous power draw and a signal burst answered on a frequency the vault's own manuals claimed did not exist.

"I'm required to conduct a standard integrity inspection," Reyen continued. "Structural, environmental, archival. It's routine. But I confess I also accepted this assignment out of curiosity. A living caretaker is rare enough these days. A good one is rarer." She tilted her head. "Tell me about your days, Caretaker. What does a man do with himself at the end of the world?"

"I'm not a man," Cyrus said.

"My apologies. What does a person do?"

"Inventories. Maintenance. I record the log." A beat. "I sleep more than I used to."

"And the deep-space array? I see from our approach telemetry that it's been active. Drawing significant power. Transmitting."

The question landed exactly where Cyrus had known it would land, and still it went through them like a spike of ice. They heard their own answer come out too fast, too clean:

"Calibration runs. The array drifts without traffic to bounce off. I run test loops to keep it aligned."

Silence. Two seconds of it. On the display, Reyen's expression did not change at all, which was itself a kind of scream.

"Test loops," she repeated.

"To keep it aligned."

"Mm." She consulted something off-screen, unhurried. "Our telemetry shows the transmissions aren't looped. They're structured. Repeating sequences with variation—which is, forgive me, what language looks like from the outside."

Cyrus's heart was a fist striking the inside of their ribs. *Lie badly,* they thought, *and she'll dig. Lie well and she'll dig slower.* They made themselves breathe. Made themselves think of the inventory, of accession numbers, of the flat litany of things that were true.

"You're right," they said slowly. "It's not calibration."

Reyen waited.

"It's a project." Cyrus let their shoulders drop, let exhaustion into their voice—not entirely manufactured; there was plenty of real exhaustion to draw from. "After the war started, when the ships stopped coming, I couldn't—I needed something to do with the array or I was going to go out an airlock. So I started transmitting the seed catalog. Accession numbers, provenance data, germination parameters. Encoded. In case anyone out there ever wants to rebuild agriculture from scratch, the data shouldn't die with this rock." They looked directly into the pickup, the way you look at someone when you want to be believed. "It's not language, Captain. It's a library. I've been reading the library aloud to nobody for three years. You caught me embarrassed, that's all."

Another silence. Longer this time. Reyen studied them, and Cyrus studied her back and saw the thing he would remember afterward with a chill: she was enjoying this. Not cruelly. The way a chess player enjoys a position where both sides have good moves.

"A library," she said. "Broadcast in the clear? Or encrypted?"

"Encrypted. Old habit. The pre-war caretaker protocols—everything sensitive goes out ciphered, even if it's just wheat."

"Which cipher?"

"The catalog cipher. Standard issue. It's in the manual."

"It is," Reyen said pleasantly. "Section twelve. Though section twelve notes the catalog cipher was deprecated nine years ago as cryptographically obsolete." She folded her hands. "You're telling me the last caretaker of humanity's seed heritage is broadcasting agricultural data in a code any first-year signals analyst could crack in an afternoon."

"Yes," said Cyrus, who had spent two days teaching themselves to say such things without blinking.

Because that was the lesson of the morning, the lesson the first call had taught and the second call was confirming: the truth, properly rationed, was the best lie available. Every word Cyrus had just spoken was true except the shape of it. They *had* transmitted the catalog. They *had* used the old cipher—for the decoy bursts, the ones they'd begun seeding the spectrum with forty hours ago, boring wheat-data wrapped in deprecated encryption, timed to match the power signatures Reyen's telemetry would have already swallowed. The real broadcast, Vess's language, the archive's contents—all of it buried now beneath a haystack Cyrus had built with their own hands.

Reyen regarded them for a long moment. Then, unexpectedly, she smiled—a real one, brief and tired at the edges.

"You're better at this than you think, Caretaker."

"At what?"

"This." She gestured vaguely at the screen between them, at the whole apparatus of the conversation. "Defending your ground. Most people in your position would have confessed to something by now, just to make the questioning stop. Isolation does that. It makes people desperate to be believed by anyone, even the people they should fear." Her voice gentled by a degree, and the gentleness was worse than the probing. "I've read your logs, you know. All of them. Eleven years. You have a beautiful voice for talking to no one. I listened to the entry from your fifth winter—the one about your sister's name, and how you say it out loud once a year so the vault learns it. That was—" She paused, and for a moment the captain's mask slipped and something almost human looked out. "That stayed with me."

Cyrus's throat closed. That entry was the most private thing they owned. Hearing it described—in Reyen's measured, courteous voice—felt like watching a stranger turn over the bones of someone they loved.

"Don't," they managed.

"I'm not threatening you. I'm telling you I understand what eleven years costs. And because I understand, I'm going to offer you some advice, free of charge." She leaned toward the pickup. "When we dock tomorrow, there will be an inspection team. Political Officer Dreville will be aboard—he handles cultural compliance. He will ask you questions, and he is not me. He does not enjoy the game; he enjoys the outcome. If there is anything in this vault you would rather not have found, Caretaker, now is the night to decide what story you're going to tell him—and to make sure the story and the evidence agree."

"Why are you telling me this?"

Reyen held their gaze through the static-flecked connection, and for one suspended second Cyrus saw her weigh an answer and set it down unspoken.

"Because I inspect heritage assets," she said finally. "And I find I care what happens to them. Good night, Caretaker Kavanagh. Docking at 0900."

The channel died.

* * *

Cyrus sat in the dark of the comms alcove for a long while, shaking—not with fear, or not only fear, but with the strange vertigo of having lied successfully for the first time in their life and finding it fit like a glove worn inside out.

Then they got to work.

The haystack needed thickening. Through the night they seeded the spectrum with decoy bursts—catalog data, deprecated ciphers, the dull litany of wheat and barley and sorghum—until the vault's transmissions told exactly the story Cyrus had told Reyen. In the sealed wing, they rewrapped the decrypted fragments in the same obsolete cipher, half-hidden in plain sight: if Dreville cracked them, he would find agricultural records, boring and true. The real core file—the launch telemetry, the casualty ledgers, the discrepancy that unmade the war's founding story—Cyrus split into shards and hid the way the vault itself had taught them to hide things: in the seed bank's own indexing scheme, accession numbers as keys, provenance fields as addresses. Anyone looking would see a library. Only someone who knew the catalog by heart could read the message inside it.

Only a caretaker, in other words. Only someone who had walked these galleries ten thousand times, whispering numbers to the frozen dark.

By dawn their hands were steady and their eyes were sandpaper, and the log recorder's light blinked its patient red, and Cyrus realized they had not recorded an entry in four days—the longest silence in eleven years.

They sat down in front of it. Considered. What would they even say? *Dear no one. Today I learned to lie. Today a stranger quoted my grief back to me as leverage, and I thanked her by deceiving her, and I was good at it, and I don't know yet whether that makes me a survivor or something the vault should quarantine.*

Instead they said only: "Log entry. Day four thousand and sixty-one. Inspection party arriving. Inventory current. Vault secure." A pause. "Svalgard, if you can hear me: hold."

They reached to end the recording, and that was when the console chimed—an incoming transmission, tight-beam, low-power, from the direction of the Perennial's last known drift. Vess. Cyrus opened the channel, and the Old Terran filled the alcove, halting and urgent, and they translated it word by word with their heart climbing into their throat:

*Hardliners aboard. She's moving early. Whatever you're going to do—do it before they arrive.*

Cyrus looked at the clock. Four hours until docking.

Outside the viewport, the *Charybdis* had come out of the storm-shadow at last, hanging in the black above the ice like a blade held point-down, and along its flank the docking lights were already warming from amber to green.
