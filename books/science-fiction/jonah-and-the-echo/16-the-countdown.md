## 16. The Countdown

The alarm came as a sound Cyrus had never heard the vault make before: a low, subsonic pulse through the deck plating, felt in the teeth before it was heard in the ears. He was elbow-deep in the coolant manifold on Sublevel Three, a splice of stolen power cable still warm in his gloved hands, when the lights changed—every corridor flushing amber, then red, then amber again, cycling like a heartbeat under stress.

He froze. The cable hung from his fist like something caught.

*Intrusion protocol,* said the vault's voice, flat and sexless and suddenly everywhere. *Perimeter breach. Directorate vessel Charybdis. All caretaker functions suspended.*

"No," Cyrus whispered, to no one, to everyone. "No, not yet."

The reroute wasn't finished. Two junctions remained between his improvised power line and the deep-space transmitter's capacitors—two junctions that would take forty minutes he no longer had. On the schematic glowing against his wrist display, the Charybdis's descent vector crawled downward through the atmosphere like a splinter working toward the heart.

He thought of Reyen's face in the dark of the archive wing, three nights ago, when she had told him what her orders actually were. *Erasure, Kavanagh. Not recovery. The word on the manifest is heritage asset, but the word in my orders is sanitize.* She had said it the way people confess to crimes they haven't committed yet, watching him for absolution or for an accomplice. He had given her neither. He had given her a plan.

And now the plan was bleeding out through amber light and a pulse in the floor.

Cyrus pulled the cable free, coiled it over his shoulder, and ran.

* * *

On the Charybdis, Political Officer Dain Mercer stood in the ship's nerve center with his hands folded behind his back and watched the mutiny assemble itself out of ordinary crew behavior—a delayed acknowledgment here, a sensor reading logged twice there, a maintenance request that would keep two technicians off the bridge for exactly as long as their consciences required. He had been doing this work for twenty years. Mutiny never announced itself. It arrived the way rot arrived in a hull: quietly, structurally, in places you only inspected after the sound started.

"Captain Reyen's shuttle pre-flight," reported the watch officer, a young woman whose name Mercer had made a point of knowing and whose file he had made a point of reading twice. "Destination: surface. Inspection of the Svalgard facility, per schedule."

"Per schedule," Mercer repeated pleasantly. "How industrious. Cancel it."

The watch officer's hands paused above her console. It was a small pause. It was everything.

"I beg your pardon, sir?"

"The inspection is concluded. The captain will remain aboard." Mercer turned, and gave the bridge his mildest expression—the one that crew members described afterward, in the anonymous reports that never went anywhere, as *worse than shouting*. "In fact, seal the ship. Full lockdown. No shuttles, no comms traffic except on my authority, no exceptions below my signature."

"Sir, the captain has command authority—"

"The captain," Mercer said, "is currently the subject of a loyalty review, which I am conducting. You may verify that with Fleet Command if you enjoy waiting eleven years for a reply." He smiled. "We're all very far from home, Lieutenant. Distance does things to communication. You've noticed."

She had noticed. Everyone had noticed. That was the whole architecture of the Meridian War: eleven years of distance, eleven years of messages that took months and came back edited, until both sides of the gulf were fighting ghosts of each other's intentions. Mercer had built a career inside that fog. He did not intend to let a seed vault and a dead language clear it.

He walked to the tactical station, where the Svalgard archive sat on the plot like a buried thing waiting to be dug up or buried deeper.

"Prepare the demolition package," he said. "Early deployment. We won't need the landing party at all."

* * *

Vess felt the Perennial change course before anyone announced it—a subtle lean in the deck, the way a body shifts its weight before it runs.

She was in the relay bay again, third sleepless shift, her ear pressed metaphorically to the frequency where Cyrus lived. The signal from Svalgard had grown stronger over the past days—stronger, she understood now, because someone down there was feeding power into an antenna that had been designed to whisper. Someone was shouting into the dark on her behalf. The thought kept her upright. The thought also terrified her, because strength drew attention, and attention, in this war, was a weapon pointed by whoever saw you first.

The intercom crackled. Captain Osei's voice, stripped down to wire: "All hands. We have company. Two ships, colonial registry, hardliner transponders, matching our vector. They're asking us to heave to for inspection."

A voice near Vess—Toma, the engineer who had helped her build the blind transmitter out of funeral equipment—said what everyone was thinking: "Inspection of what? We're a colony ship. We're carrying four thousand sleepers and a hydroponics deck."

"We're carrying *you*," Osei said, and there was no anger in it, only arithmetic. "They know about the transmissions. Someone aboard sold us, or someone ashore listened harder than we thought. They want the linguist and every data core she's touched."

Vess closed her eyes. In the darkness behind them: the truth, half-built, the fragments Cyrus was sending her one agonizing packet at a time—fleet telemetry, casualty ledgers, the discrepancy that unmade everything. In front of them: the relay beacon at Meridian Deep, still nine hours away at their best speed, the single point in all this black where her message could be amplified enough to reach a hundred receivers instead of one.

Nine hours. Two ships. One choice.

"Osei," she said, opening her eyes. "Put me on the general channel."

"You know what that does. It paints us."

"It paints *me*. And I'm the only thing they actually want." She was already moving toward the comm station, her heart going like a trapped bird, her mind going strangely calm, the calm of the moment in a broadcast studio when the red light comes on and there is nothing left in the universe but the next sentence. "Captain, if we run silent, they board us, and they take the cores, and nobody ever hears anything. If I speak now—in Old Terran, in all of it—they can jam me, but jamming is loud. Jamming is *visible*. Every neutral receiver in range will hear the shape of the silence."

Toma stared at her. "You want to use their censorship as a flare."

"I want to use everything as a flare," Vess said. "It's the only thing I know how to do."

Behind them, the first hardliner ship fired a warning shot across the Perennial's bow—a line of white fire in the dark, beautiful and obscene, a sentence written in a grammar everyone understood.

Osei looked at it for a long second. Then: "Do it."

* * *

Cyrus reached Junction Nine with the cable over his shoulder and found the corridor full of amber light and the smell of hot insulation. The vault was fighting him—not maliciously, never maliciously, but with the dumb immovability of a system designed by people who assumed the enemy would always come from outside. His caretaker credentials had been suspended. Every door asked him politely to identify himself and then refused to believe the answer.

He pried the junction panel open with a pry bar meant for seed crates. Inside, the bus bar gleamed, fat with stored charge, enough amperage to stop his heart across a room. Eleven years ago he would not have known what to do with it. Eleven years of solitude had taught him many useless things and a few essential ones, and this was essential: how systems failed, and how they could be made to fail *differently*.

He spliced. His gloves smoked. The vault screamed a warning he didn't bother translating.

Through the wrist display, Vess's last transmission played on loop, the one that had come in while he slept: *They found us. Two ships. I'm going to broadcast early, from open space, before we reach the relay. It won't carry far without amplification. Whatever you're doing down there—do it faster.*

And beneath it, older, from the night Reyen had confessed: *I need a reason to refuse, Kavanagh. Give me one worth dying for.*

Junction Nine closed. One left. Junction Twelve, deep in the old wing, past the sealed door he had breached a lifetime of weeks ago, in the room where the Earth Directorate's seals still shone under frost like coins at the bottom of a well.

He ran. The floor pulsed. Somewhere above him, beyond kilometers of ice and stone, the Charybdis was sliding into position, and he found himself thinking—not for the first time, but with new clarity—that the vault had always been shaped like a tomb, and he had spent eleven years mistaking it for a garden.

* * *

Reyen heard the lockdown klaxon from her cabin and knew, with the cold certainty of a career officer, that it was over.

Not the mission—missions could survive anything. Over was the other thing: the small, private rebellion she had been nursing for weeks, the one she had fed with Reyen-shaped justifications (*I'm staying close to the asset, I'm managing Kavanagh, I'm gathering evidence*) until it had grown too large to hide and she had stopped wanting to hide it. Mercer had seen it. Of course he had. That was what political officers were *for*.

Her door chimed. A marine—Hollis, young, decent, the kind of soldier who still believed the uniform meant something—stood in the corridor with his rifle slung and his eyes anywhere but hers.

"Captain. The political officer requests your presence on the bridge."

"Requests."

"Yes, ma'am." A beat. Then, barely audible, the words dragged out of him like shrapnel: "Ma'am—he's armed the demolition package. Early drop. The whole package. Surface detonation."

Reyen went very still. Demolition packages came in graduated yields; doctrine called for surgical charges on the archive wing, deniable, precise. A full-surface package was not erasure of documents. It was erasure of a continent's corner. It was erasure of a man.

"He's going to kill the caretaker," she said.

Hollis's jaw worked. "The order describes the facility as uninhabited, ma'am."

Eleven years of logs. Eleven years of a voice talking to an empty room about seed viability and frost patterns and the color of the light on the ice, and somewhere in the last month, Reyen realized, she had read every entry. She had listened to loneliness the way you listen to your own reflection. She knew the exact cadence Cyrus Kavanagh used when he was afraid and pretending not to be.

She straightened her collar. "Lieutenant Hollis. Walk me to the bridge."

"Ma'am, I'm supposed to—" He stopped. Looked at her. Looked at the deck. Made, in the space of two seconds, the decision that would define whatever was left of his life. "This way, Captain."

* * *

At Junction Twelve, Cyrus's hands shook so badly he had to press them flat against the panel and breathe.

Below him, through the ice, he could feel it now—a vibration too low for ears, the harmonic of a heavy thing descending through atmosphere. The Charybdis, coming to collect. Or coming to erase. He thought of Vess's voice, thin across the light-years: *do it faster.* He thought of his own logs, eleven years of them, the audience that had never answered until one day it did.

"Not yet," he told the panel, and told the vault, and told whatever was listening in the dark between stars. "I'm not finished."

He cut the last line. Spliced the last splice. On his wrist display, the schematic bloomed green along its whole length: vault grid to bypass conduit to capacitor bank to the deep-space transmitter, the great dish in the old wing that had waited two centuries under frost for someone to give it a reason.

Power flowed. The capacitors began, slowly, sweetly, to drink.

And then the vault spoke again, and this time its flat voice carried something almost like apology:

*External uplink detected. Priority transmission. Origin: Directorate vessel Charybdis. Message follows.*

Cyrus stood in the amber light, cable in hand, heart hammering, and listened to his own death warrant read aloud in a courteous machine voice:

*"Svalgard Facility, this is Political Officer Dain Mercer, acting commander, CSV Charybdis. Effective immediately, this facility is designated a biohazard containment site under Emergency Statute Seven. All personnel are directed to evacuate via the north pad within ninety minutes. Failure to evacuate will be construed as contamination, and the site will be sterilized accordingly. This is not a negotiation. This is hygiene."*

Ninety minutes.

Cyrus looked at the schematic. The capacitors needed six hours to reach broadcast charge. The math was simple and it was a wall.

Then, faint beneath the machine voice, another signal bled through the vault's ancient array—a human voice, ragged with static, speaking a language two centuries dead, and underneath the dead language, unmistakable, the shape of a name. *His* name. Vess, broadcasting blind from open space, spending her ship's secrecy like the last coin in her pocket, calling to him across the gulf because she had no other way to say: *I'm still here. Are you?*

Cyrus wiped his face with the back of his glove, leaving a streak of soot and frost-melt, and keyed the transmitter with six hours of charge and ninety minutes of life.

"Vess," he said. "I hear you. Hold on. I'm going to need a miracle, and I think one might already be on her way down the Charybdis's main corridor with a sidearm and a conscience."

Above him, through kilometers of ice, the demolition package began its slow descent.
