## 17. Every Dead Language

The vault had a heartbeat, and Cyrus Kavanagh had spent eleven years learning to hear it.

It lived in the coolant lines—a slow, tidal pulse beneath the floor plates, glycol and nitrogen cycling through the deep shelves like blood through a body too vast to fully know. On ordinary days it was comfort. Tonight it was a metronome.

Cyrus stood in the sealed wing with the archive's core file open across three screens, the launch records glowing pale blue against the dark: *Meridian Station telemetry, 0417 hours. First launch signature: Earth Directorate cruiser Vantage. First casualty projection: colonial mining fleet, 11,204 souls.* The truth, laid out in numbers so clean they looked almost gentle.

Behind them, the deep-space transmitter hummed at forty percent power—every ampere they'd stolen from the containment systems over six days of sabotage rerouted into its capacitors, stored like breath before a shout.

The wall clock read 03:12 vault time. The Charybdis hung in orbit overhead, and somewhere on her bridge, Captain Ilsa Reyen was holding her own crew together with bare hands and borrowed minutes.

Cyrus pressed record.

"Log entry—" A pause. Eleven years of entries had begun this way, always addressed to an audience that never answered. This one was different. "This is the last entry. If you're hearing this, then it worked. And if you're hearing this, I need you to understand what you're about to receive isn't mine. It belongs to eleven thousand people who were blamed for their own deaths. It belongs to every colony that starved believing a lie. I'm just the hand that opens the door."

They stopped the recording there. Their voice had gone thin. There would be other recordings tonight—the real ones, the ones that mattered—and Cyrus needed everything the throat had left.

* * *

The plan had been Vess's, in the end.

*Dead tongues can't be censored,* she'd said, three nights ago, her voice arriving across four light-years in fragments compressed to almost nothing. *That's why I broadcast in Old Terran. The Directorate's filters are trained on modern Standard—they parse intent, flag dissent, rewrite in transit. But Old Terran predates their semantic architecture by two centuries. To their systems it's noise. Beautiful, meaningless noise.*

*So we don't send one message,* Cyrus had said, understanding blooming slow and cold. *We send a chord.*

*We send a chord,* she'd agreed, and he could hear her smiling through the static. *Old Terran for anyone who remembers. Pre-Reformation dialects—there are nine still archived in your own linguistic wing, did you know that? Meridian Creole, High Antarctic, the trade-pidgin from the first orbital generation. And underneath all of it—*

*Seed-catalog cipher.* Cyrus had laughed then, actually laughed, alone in the frozen dark. *The Directorate made me catalog every accession code in the vault. They wrote the key themselves.*

*Their own bureaucracy becomes our encryption,* Vess said. *Every censor who tries to strip the message apart will find only seed lot numbers. CV-4471, durum wheat, drought-adapted. Except the numbers aren't lot numbers. They're coordinates, timestamps, casualty figures. The truth hidden in the most boring language ever invented.*

*No one censors a seed inventory,* Cyrus said.

*No one even reads one,* Vess agreed. *That's the point.*

Now the encoding stack filled the sealed wing's main console, nine parallel channels braided into one carrier wave. Cyrus had spent two sleepless days building it, teaching the vault's ancient translation matrices to speak in chorus. The core file—fleet telemetry, launch records, the Directorate's own internal casualty memos, stamped and signed—had been split into fragments and scattered across all nine languages like seeds sown across a field. Any single channel, intercepted alone, would look like gibberish or grain statistics. Together, reassembled by anyone with patience and the public linguistic archives both sides still pretended to maintain, they would become undeniable.

The message would repeat. That was the final mercy of the design. Not once—not a shot fired into the dark that could be swallowed—but continuously, for as long as the transmitter drew breath, cycling through every dead tongue Earth had ever abandoned, so that no jamming window, however wide, could catch it all.

*How long can you keep it running?* Vess had asked.

*As long as I'm alive to keep it running,* Cyrus had said, and had not let himself think about what that sentence promised.

* * *

At 03:40, the vault's external array chimed: traffic in high orbit.

Cyrus pulled the feed with hands that had learned steadiness the way old sailors learn sea-legs. Two objects detaching from the Charybdis's ventral bay—launches, small ones, descending toward the surface on trajectories that would bring them within relay-interdiction range of the transmitter dish inside the hour. Jamming platforms. Reyen had warned him this might come: *Vossik has his own orders, and his orders end with silence. When he moves, we move. Don't wait for my signal. Wait for theirs.*

Reyen's voice came through on the tight-beam channel they'd established through the vault's maintenance band, low and clipped, the connection degrading as the Charybdis's political officer tightened his grip on ship communications.

"Cyrus. Status."

"Encoding complete. Capacitors at ninety-six percent. Ready on your word or without it."

"Without it, if you have to." Static chewed the edges of her words. "Listen carefully. Vossik just ordered engineering to run a full diagnostic on the main drive. Unscheduled. He's looking for an excuse to come down early—or to fire from orbit and call it an accident. My chief engineer is about to give him a very interesting excuse instead."

"What kind?"

"The kind that takes four hours to diagnose and requires the whole ship's attention." A pause, and when Reyen spoke again her voice had dropped into something rawer. "A staged coolant breach in the drive housing. Real alarms, real evacuations, real confusion. My people are going to walk through fire to buy you minutes, caretaker. I want you to spend them well."

"How many minutes?"

"If we're good? Forty. If we're lucky, sixty. After that, either Vossik figures out the breach is theater, or he stops caring whether the ship burns so long as the vault does." Another pause, longer. "Cyrus. Whatever happens up here—"

"Don't," Cyrus said gently. "Save it. Say it after. You're going to need something to say after."

A rough sound that might have been a laugh. "Arrogant gardener."

"Optimistic gardener. There's a difference."

"Barely." The static surged. "Reyen out. Broadcast when ready. God help us all—we stopped asking permission a long time ago."

* * *

At 03:51 vault time, the Charybdis began to scream.

Even from the surface, even filtered through the vault's seismographs and the thin atmosphere above Svalgard's ice, Cyrus felt it: a shudder in the planetary crust as the warship's drive housing vented superheated plasma in a controlled, choreographed catastrophe. Emergency beacons bloomed across the tactical display like fever blisters. Distress chatter flooded every open frequency—*breach in engineering, casualties unknown, all hands to damage control stations*—and the two jamming platforms descending toward the vault hesitated, wavered, turned back toward their wounded mother ship like children summoned home.

Cyrus watched the chaos bloom and thought: *those are people up there, burning themselves to buy me time*, and then thought: *no. They're not burning. They're pretending to burn. That's the miracle of it. For once, the fire is a lie told in service of the truth.*

Forty minutes. Maybe sixty.

Cyrus sat down at the console, cracked their knuckles—an old habit, useless, human—and opened the broadcast interface.

Nine channels waited. Nine dead languages, each one a coffin the Directorate believed sealed. Old Terran, the mother tongue, the language of the first colonies' founding charters. Meridian Creole, born in the mining tunnels where the war's first victims had lived and died. High Antarctic, the research stations' private poetry. The orbital-generation pidgin, half mathematics, half lullaby. Five more, older and stranger, dredged from the vault's linguistic wing where they'd been preserved like seeds—because that was what the vault did, Cyrus realized now, what it had always done. It didn't preserve crops. It preserved *possibility*. Every language was a seed. Every seed was a memory waiting for soil.

And threaded through them all, invisible until assembled: seed-catalog cipher. The Directorate's own accession codes, their own bureaucratic handwriting, carrying the launch records of the Vantage, the casualty projections, the signed memoranda ordering the cover-up. *CV-4471, durum wheat, drought-adapted* becoming *0417 hours, first launch, Earth Directorate cruiser*. Truth dressed as inventory. History disguised as homework.

Cyrus loaded the core file. The system asked for confirmation, patient and stupid: *BROADCAST WILL BE UNENCRYPTED AND UNRECALLABLE. PROCEED?*

Unrecallable. Cyrus let the word sit in their chest for a moment. Eleven years of careful, reversible, deniable routine, and here at last was the thing that could not be taken back. No archive to seal. No log to delete. Once this wave left the dish, it would outrun every gun in the system, spreading at lightspeed toward a hundred receivers, and no power in existence—not the Directorate, not the colonial hardliners, not God himself—could call it home.

Their finger hovered.

Somewhere four light-years away, Vess was watching her own receiver banks, waiting. Somewhere in orbit, Reyen was standing on a bridge full of smoke and theater, buying seconds with her career and probably her life. Somewhere below the ice, eleven thousand dead colonists waited to stop being liars in the history books.

"You were right," Cyrus said aloud, to the empty wing, to the sister lost between stars, to the audience that had finally, finally arrived. "Somebody had to keep the records."

They pressed the key.

* * *

The sound the transmitter made was not a sound. It was pressure—a physical shove against the chest, a tremor in the fillings of the teeth—as forty kilowatts of stolen power converted themselves into voice. On the monitoring display, the waveform climbed like a living thing, nine braided frequencies rising in parallel, and Cyrus watched the first cycle of the message unspool into the dark:

*In Old Terran:* We were not the ones who fired first.

*In Meridian Creole, the miners' tongue:* Your dead were murdered twice—once by the missile, once by the lie.

*In High Antarctic:* Here is the proof. Here is the proof. Here is the proof.

*In seed-catalog cipher:* CV-0417-VNTG-DIR-111204. Launch confirmed. Origin confirmed. Blame reassigned.

Cycle time: ninety seconds. Then it began again, and again, tireless, a heart that would not stop beating as long as there was power in the lines.

Cyrus stood in the humming dark of the sealed wing and felt something crack open in their chest—not grief, not fear, though both were there—but *exhilaration*, pure and terrifying, the feeling of a held breath finally released after eleven years. They laughed. They couldn't help it. They laughed alone in the frozen vault at three in the morning, broadcasting the founding crime of a war across the whole listening sky in every language anyone had tried to bury, and it was the first honest sound they'd made since they'd volunteered for this posting.

"They can't jam all of it," they whispered. "They can't jam *any* of it. It's already gone."

Because it was. The message was moving at lightspeed now, and lightspeed forgives nothing. Every second of transmission was another three hundred thousand kilometers of truth that no fleet could chase down. Even if Vossik burned the vault to slag this instant, the first cycles were already beyond reach, already washing over the outer relay buoys, already falling toward the Perennial and a hundred colonial receivers and—who knew—who else. Sympathetic ears inside Earth itself. Archivists. Teachers. Anyone who had ever been told a story that didn't sit right in their stomach.

Vess's voice broke through on the tight-beam, ragged with static and something else entirely.

"Cyrus. Cyrus, I'm receiving—I've got the Old Terran channel clean, the cipher's resolving, oh stars, it's *resolving*—" A sob, quickly mastered. "The launch records. I can see the launch records. Everyone on this ship is going to see the launch records."

"Hold it," Cyrus said, grinning like a fool at a console, at the ice, at the dark. "Hold it and relay it. Wide as you can. Don't explain it—just pass it on. Let it speak for itself."

"It's speaking in nine languages."

"Then everyone gets to hear it in their own."

For a moment neither of them spoke, and the distance between them—four light-years of vacuum and war—felt, for the first time, like a hallway rather than an ocean. Two voices on one frequency. One message, multiplying.

Then the vault's threat board screamed.

* * *

Cyrus saw it in the same instant Vess went silent: the tactical overlay flaring red as the Charybdis's forward batteries swiveled, tracking, acquiring. On the ship-feed, a figure had appeared on the bridge—Vossik, the political officer, his face gray with fury, standing over a diagnostics console that clearly showed no breach at all. His mouth was moving. Orders. And around him, Reyen's crew was moving too—slowly, deliberately, bodies drifting between Vossik and the fire-control station like a barricade made of people.

"They know," Vess breathed. "Cyrus, they've figured out the malfunction is staged—"

The tactical display updated. Target lock: surface installation, transmitter array, grid reference that Cyrus knew by heart because they had walked its shadow every day for eleven years.

Forty minutes hadn't been enough. It was never going to be enough. But the message was still transmitting—cycle fourteen, cycle fifteen, the dead languages pouring out into the storm—and every second the dish stayed alive was another three hundred thousand kilometers of truth that would outlive them all.

Cyrus grabbed the emergency toolkit off the wall and started for the airlock, toward the backup dish on the surface ridge, toward the storm, toward whatever came next.

"Vess," they said, shouldering through the inner door as the first warning klaxons woke the vault's long-sleeping bones. "Keep relaying. Whatever you hear next—keep relaying."

Above the ice, the Charybdis armed her guns.
