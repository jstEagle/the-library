## 1. The Long Route

The corridor between the Meridian Fleet and the Ashfall Compact was eleven days wide and nobody's, which made it the only honest piece of space Dominic Okafor had ever traveled through.

He said as much to Vesper every morning. Vesper was his ship—a Cetus-class freighter older than either fleet's current admiralty, held together by prayer, duct tape of three incompatible standards, and a flight computer that had developed opinions over the decades. She didn't answer, of course. But she hummed in a particular way when he talked to her, a low harmonic through the deck plating that he chose to interpret as agreement.

"Eleven days," he told her, watching the corridor stretch out on the nav display: a ribbon of nothing between two walls of everything. To port, the Meridian Fleet sat in its slow orbital carousel around the gas giant Canticle, ten thousand ships burning formation lights like a city that had decided to be angry forever. To starboard, the Ashfall Compact hung in the shadow of the brown dwarf Cinder, denser, quieter, its ships running dark out of what their doctrine called *discipline* and what Meridian propaganda called *cowardice*. Both descriptions were wrong. Dominic had been inside both fleets' heads—literally, in one sense—and knew that what they shared wasn't cowardice or discipline but fear, worn so long it had hardened into something that looked like policy.

Nobody flew the corridor. That was the point of it. The Armistice of Halcyon Reach had drawn two lines down the dark and declared the space between them demilitarized, de-populated, and de facto forbidden, which meant that anyone who did fly it was either a smuggler, a spy, or a fool.

Dominic was all three, depending on the contract.

Today he was none of them. Today he was hauling forty tons of desalination membranes from a Meridian orbital foundry to an Ashfall mining station that officially did not exist, paid through four shell companies and a blind escrow, because the war everyone was preparing for had not yet been declared, and until it was, both sides still needed water filters and someone willing to carry them.

"Vesper," he said, "run the silence check."

The silence check was his own ritual, invented in his first year on this route: sweep for transponders, sweep for active sensors, sweep for the faint electromagnetic breathing of any ship pretending not to be there. The corridor was supposed to be empty. It never quite was. There were always other ghosts—smugglers running cold, couriers running quiet, once, memorably, a derelict colony tender with a single flickering beacon that had taken him six hours to decide not to investigate, a decision he still thought about at 0300.

"Sweep complete," Vesper said, in her flat, patient voice. "No contacts. No emissions. No witnesses."

"No witnesses," Dominic repeated. "That's me, too."

He hadn't meant to say it out loud. It just came out, the way things do after eleven days alone, and he winced at it—not because it wasn't true, but because it was true in more ways than the obvious one.

* * *

Here is what the shipping manifests said about Dominic Okafor: master and commander of the freighter *Vesper*, licensed out of Halcyon Reach, bonded, insured, tax-compliant in two jurisdictions and invisible in a third. Here is what the manifests did not say: that the man signing them had been alive for thirty-one years, while the Dominic Okafor whose name appeared above his signature had been dead for twelve.

They grew you in a vat on Meridian Station Kestrel, in the ninth year of the war-that-wasn't. That was the official term everyone used—the war-that-wasn't, the conflict-that-couldn't-be-named, the situation—because officially there was no war between Meridian and Ashfall, only a regrettable state of heightened non-cooperation. But they had needed soldiers and diplomats and specialists, and cloning was faster than raising them, and so there had been a program, and Dominic had been one of its products: a copy of a promising young diplomat, decanted at physical age twenty-two with twenty-two years of someone else's memories pressed into him like flowers in a book.

The original Dominic Okafor had been a rising star in the Meridian diplomatic corps. The original had negotiated three corridor incidents back from the brink. The original had been murdered twelve years ago in a way that the investigation called *unresolved* and everyone else called *obvious*.

The clone had been decommissioned when the program was quietly shelved—too expensive, too controversial, and worst of all, too *useful*, which made it politically dangerous—and turned loose with a stipend, a legal identity that existed mostly on paper, and a face that got him recognized in every port he'd ever wanted to drink a quiet beer in.

"Hey," a dockhand at Kestrel had said once, grabbing his arm, eyes wide and wet. "You're dead. I carried your coffin."

"I get that a lot," Dominic had said, which was true, and gently removed his arm, which was also true.

He took contracts under his own name because legally it was his own name—he'd fought a four-year bureaucratic war to establish that, and won on a technicality involving an inheritance tax loophole—but every signature felt like forgery. Every time he wrote *Dominic Okafor* on a manifest, he imagined the original's ghost looking over his shoulder, checking his penmanship.

The ghost would have found the penmanship wanting. The original had beautiful handwriting. The clone had learned to write in a vat-grown hurry, all his motor memory secondhand, like a man who'd read about swimming but only ever touched water at age twenty-two.

* * *

Day four of eleven. He celebrated by fixing the recycler.

The recycler had been making a sound like a cat being asked to do mathematics, and Dominic had been ignoring it for two weeks on the theory that acknowledging a problem gave it power. This theory had served him well in most areas of his life and catastrophically in the area of ship maintenance, so he finally pulled the access panel, wedged himself into the crawl space behind the galley, and spent ninety minutes negotiating with a impeller bearing that had opinions about its purpose in life.

"You're not a cat," he told it. "You're a bearing. Bearings rotate. It's in the name."

The bearing declined to comment. He replaced it with a spare he'd machined himself out of a component that had originally been part of a Meridian torpedo guidance system, which was the sort of thing that happened when you bought salvage blind, and which meant that somewhere in Vesper's guts, a piece of a weapon that had been built to kill Ashfall ships was now peacefully filtering drinking water for Ashfall miners. Dominic found this genuinely funny. He'd told the story at a bar on Halcyon Reach once, to a table of neutral-station long-haulers, and gotten the biggest laugh of his life. For one evening he'd belonged to something—a small, damp, smoky something, but something.

Then a woman at the next table had looked up from her drink, gone pale, and whispered, "That's Okafor. The dead diplomat." And the belonging had evaporated like spillage on a hot plate, and he'd paid his tab and walked back to the ship through streets that suddenly seemed full of people who knew his face better than he did.

He finished the bearing, closed the panel, and treated himself to the good coffee—the real beans, hoarded, rationed to one cup per major repair—and sat in the cockpit watching the nothing go by.

The corridor's nothing was actually quite beautiful, if you let it be. Dust motes caught the distant light of Canticle and Cinder both, so the dark ahead shimmered faintly gold on one side and rust-red on the other, like a road lit by two rival streetlamps that hated each other. Somewhere out there, invisible, were the picket lines—automated listening posts, minefields that both sides denied having, sensor buoys disguised as asteroids. He threaded between them on routes he'd mapped over six years, routes that existed nowhere except in his head and in Vesper's increasingly eccentric memory banks.

"Course correction in forty minutes," Vesper announced. "Also, your coffee is getting cold."

"My coffee is fine."

"Your coffee has been fine for eleven minutes. Statistically, it is now cold."

"You're a freighter, not a mother."

"One of us has to look out for you, and it isn't going to be the bearing."

He laughed, alone in the cockpit, and the laugh echoed in a way that reminded him exactly how alone he was, and then he drank the cold coffee anyway, because she was right, and because arguing with your own ship and losing is one of the small honest pleasures available to a man with no witnesses.

* * *

On day seven, he passed within half a light-second of a Meridian patrol running the corridor's edge, and spent four hours motionless with everything powered down except life support, drifting on momentum, watching the patrol's drive flare crawl across his viewport like a slow orange wound.

This was the part of the job nobody romanticized: the waiting. Smugglers in stories always seemed to be doing something—dodging, bluffing, leaping into hyperspace with witty one-liners. In practice, smuggling was ninety percent stillness. You became a rock. You thought about your breathing. You watched the enemy's searchlight sweep past your hull close enough to read the serial numbers, and you did not sneeze.

His face, he reflected, was wanted in nineteen ports. Not his crimes—his face. There was no warrant. There was just the fact that Dominic Okafor, dead hero of the diplomatic corps, kept turning up alive, and various intelligence services on both sides found this either fascinating or intolerable. Meridian Intelligence had interviewed him twice, politely, in rooms without clocks. Compact Security had detained him once, less politely, in a room with a clock that ticked very loudly, which he understood was a technique. Each time they'd let him go, eventually, unsatisfied—not because they believed he was harmless, but because they couldn't decide what he was, and undecided things are more dangerous to detain than release.

He was, as far as he could tell, the only person in two fleets who benefited from being a mystery.

The patrol's flare faded. He brought Vesper back to life system by system, feeling the old familiar relief, and set course for the mining station where forty tons of membranes would become someone's clean water, and where he would be paid in scrip he could only spend at ports that didn't want him.

* * *

The mining station—call it Site Nine, because that was what its operators called it when anyone official was listening, and *the Rock* when they weren't—received him with the warmth of people who hadn't seen a new face in eight months.

Which was, of course, the problem.

"New face!" bellowed the station master, a barrel-shaped woman named Okonkwo-Bright who ran the place with the serene tyranny of someone whose nearest neighbor was forty million kilometers away. "Well, semi-new. You've been here before. Twice. But you're new-adjacent, and we'll take it."

"Good to see you too, Marta."

"Marta! He remembers my name. Dom, you beautiful ghost, come eat something that isn't a ration cube."

They ate in the mess: actual vegetables grown under grow-lights, tasting of effort and pride, and a protein stew that tasted of enthusiasm. Eight miners crowded around, delighted, asking about the outside world, the fleets, the price of things, whether the war was coming. Dominic answered what he could and lied gracefully about the rest, because the truth—that nobody knew, that both sides were arming for a war neither could afford, that the whole corridor was a fuse waiting for someone careless with a match—was not dinner conversation.

"You should stay," Marta said, later, walking him back to the docking arm. "We need a pilot with sense. Pay's bad but the air's free and nobody here cares whose face you have."

"It's not my face that's the problem."

"No?"

"It's that it's somebody else's."

Marta looked at him for a long moment, in the way of people who live far from anywhere and therefore have time to actually look. "Whose?"

"A dead man's. Mine, technically. It's complicated."

"Everything's complicated. Air scrubbers are complicated. Doesn't mean you can't breathe." She clapped him on the shoulder hard enough to require structural assessment. "Offer stands. Door's always open. Well—door's always locked, but open if you knock the right sequence, which I'll tell you before you leave, because that's how friendship works out here."

He flew out the next morning with the knock sequence written on his hand, and something warm in his chest that lasted almost two days before the dark wore it smooth again.

* * *

Day ten. Eleven hours from the corridor's far mouth, where Ashfall customs would scan him, find nothing interesting, and wave him through with the bored contempt that was, in its way, the closest thing to trust the war allowed.

He was logging the trip—Vesper insisted; the flight computer had developed a passion for documentation that he suspected was her way of keeping him company—when the array pinged.

Not the silence check. Something else. A signal, faint, buried in the static floor of the corridor like a heartbeat under snow.

"Source?" he asked.

"Unknown. Bearing relative zero-three-one. Narrowband, repeating. Pattern suggests artificial origin." A pause, the length of a machine consulting itself. "Dominic, the encoding is Old Meridian."

He stopped logging.

Old Meridian was extinct. Officially, formally, ceremonially extinct—retired two centuries ago when the Fleet standardized on Common Tongue after the Succession Schisms, preserved only in academic archives and the ceremonial oaths of two admiralty families who liked to sound important. Nobody spoke it. Nobody broadcast in it. It was a language the way a fossil was an animal.

"Play it," he said.

Vesper played it. Through the cockpit speakers came a thin, patient voice, repeating the same phrase over and over across frequencies that had been silent since his great-great-grandparents' grandparents:

*"...anyone... hearing... this... the ship is... Genesis... we are... still..."*

Static chewed the rest. Then the loop began again.

Dominic sat very still. The coffee—fresh, this time, he'd earned it—went cold beside him again, and this time he didn't argue with the ship about it, because he wasn't listening to her anymore.

Genesis. He knew the name the way everyone in both fleets knew the name: the colony ship, the first ship, the one that had carried their ancestors out from Earth or wherever Earth was now, the one both fleets claimed as heritage and neither fleet claimed as responsibility. Lost. Derelict. A story mothers told children, a hulk that appeared in propaganda paintings on both sides, always depicted flying toward opposite horizons depending on which side was commissioning the painting.

And something aboard it was calling for help. In a dead language. On a frequency nobody used. In the middle of the empty corridor, where nobody was supposed to be, broadcasting to nobody, for who knew how long.

"Signal strength?" he asked quietly.

"Weak. Degrading. Estimated viable transmission window—" Vesper paused, and for a freighter's flight computer she managed to make a pause feel ominous— "measured in months. Possibly weeks."

He looked at the nav display. His route lay ahead: customs, delivery, payment, the long anonymous loop back. Safe. Sensible. The kind of route a man takes when he has learned that curiosity gets clones detained in rooms with loud clocks.

But the voice kept repeating. *Anyone hearing this.* Anyone. As if whoever it was had been saying it into the dark for years, maybe decades, addressing an emptiness that never once said anything back.

Nobody was supposed to hear it. That was the thing. The corridor was empty by design. Whoever was sending this signal was sending it to no one—which meant either they didn't know that, or they knew it perfectly well and were sending it anyway, on the chance, the wild astronomical chance, that one stubborn ghost of a man in a patched-together freighter might be passing through the nothing at exactly the wrong—or right—moment.

"Vesper," he said. "Triangulate it. Precisely."

"Coordinates acquired." She displayed them. His stomach dropped, slowly, the way it does in a lift that's begun to fall.

The signal originated from the Genesis.

He sat with that for a long time while the gold-and-rust dark slid past the viewport, and the dead language repeated its patient plea, and somewhere behind his ribs an old hunger—the one he kept fed on cold coffee and one-sided conversations and the memory of a single warm evening in a smoky bar—turned over and opened one eye.

Eleven hours to customs. A lifetime of reasons to log the anomaly, file the report, and let the authorities handle it, the way sensible ghosts did.

He reached over and switched the array to record.

"Keep listening," he told Vesper. "Don't tell anyone. Not yet."

"Not yet," she agreed, humming low through the deck plates, and if that wasn't agreement, it was close enough.
