## 6. Contract of Convenience

The contract arrived at 0400, which was the first thing wrong with it.

Dominic knew this because he'd spent eleven years learning the rhythms of legitimate work. Legitimate contracts came in during the day cycle, when clerks were awake and bored and wanted to go home. They came padded with boilerplate, forty pages of liability language and customs declarations. This one was three paragraphs long, arrived in the dead hours, and paid four times his usual rate for a run he could do in his sleep.

*Ferry diplomatic materials to Meridian forward station Kestrel, corridor's edge. Discretion expected. Compensation generous.*

He read it three times while Vesper hummed around him, her old systems ticking through their night routines like a ship-sized cat settling into its sleep. Then he pulled up the sender's routing and laughed out loud, alone, the way he did most things.

Meridian Fleet Command. Not a procurement office. Not a shipping consortium. *Command.* The diplomatic corps of a fleet that hadn't held diplomacy talks in nine years was hiring an independent freighter with no diplomatic credentials, no security clearance, and — if their records were as thorough as he suspected — a face that matched a murder victim.

"Bait," he told the cabin. "Or a leash."

Vesper's environmental system cycled, which was as close as she came to disagreeing with him.

Here was the problem, and he turned it over slowly, like a stone with something underneath: he needed exactly what they were offering. A Meridian contract meant Meridian transponder codes, Meridian clearance corridors, Meridian permission to be anywhere near the deep corridor where Genesis drifted like a drowned cathedral. The data core sat in his quarters under a false panel, warm with secrets, and every day he didn't act on it felt like carrying a lit flare through a fuel depot.

And here was the other problem, the one he kept trying not to look at directly: they might know who he was. Not Dominic Okafor, clone, ghost-freighter. But *the* Dominic Okafor's face, walking around on a living man who'd just bought passage into their jurisdiction. Either they were careless — unlikely, from an organization that had murdered a diplomat and gotten away with it for a decade — or they wanted him close enough to watch.

"Either way," he said, "you're going."

He drafted his acceptance in careful, boring language. He asked no questions. He specified no terms beyond the fee. A man with nothing to hide asks questions; a man playing dumb signs quickly and complains about the coffee. He'd learned that from watching actual smugglers at Halcyon Reach, back when he still thought his original's death was somebody else's story.

He signed it with the name on his registry — *D. Okafor, independent* — and felt the small familiar vertigo of writing a dead man's name in his own hand.

Then he set course for Kestrel and tried to sleep, which went about as well as expected.

* * *

Captain Ilsa Reyes had been awake for thirty hours when the flag order came through, and she was on her fourth cup of the swill the mess hall called coffee, so her first reaction was not professional. Her first reaction was, *"Oh, you have got to be kidding me."*

Her second reaction was to read the order twice, because it got worse.

*Shadow and observe vessel VESPER, registry independent, operator D. Okafor. Do not engage. Do not make contact. Report observations through channel seven only.*

Channel seven was the one nobody used because it wasn't logged in any system a junior officer could find. Which meant this order came from above Captain level. Which meant someone upstairs cared about a cargo hauler the way you only care about things that scare you.

"He's a freighter pilot," she said to Lieutenant Okonkwo, who was pretending to review fuel manifests across the briefing table. "He hauls salvage and machine parts. Last quarter his biggest shipment was agricultural drones. I looked. There were *pictures*. Little drones with little sprayers."

"And yet," Okonkwo said, "Command wants him followed by a corvette with a full sensor suite."

"And yet." She rubbed her eyes. Thirty hours showed in everything; she could feel her own pulse in her teeth. "What do we actually know about him?"

Okonkwo had the file open before she finished asking, because Okonkwo was the kind of officer who heard questions as work orders, and Reyes privately considered him the best hire she'd ever made. "Okafor, Dominic. Age thirty-four standard. Licensed eleven years. Clean record — suspiciously clean, honestly. No violations, no debts, no vices on record. Pays his port fees early."

"Nobody pays port fees early."

"He does. It's in here. There's a note from a Halcyon Reach harbormaster calling him 'aggressively punctual.'"

Reyes almost smiled. Almost. "Clone?"

"Registry doesn't say. But there's a biometric flag from six years ago — facial match to a deceased person, flagged and then unflagged within a day. The unflagging authorization is—" Okonkwo scrolled, frowned. "Redacted. All the way up."

She sat with that. A dead man's face, walking around hauling agricultural drones and paying port fees early. And now Command wanted him watched in secret, in the same week Fleet announced 'diplomatic initiatives' for the first time in years, in the same week her own anomaly desk flagged a repeating signal from dead space that nobody would explain to her.

Everything was connected. She just couldn't see the string yet.

"All right," she said. "We shadow him. Passively. We're a rock in his sky, nothing more. And Okonkwo—"

"Ma'am?"

"If anyone asks why the *Kestrel's Watch* is loitering at the corridor edge, we're conducting navigation calibration exercises."

"We've done navigation calibration exercises for three weeks straight. Someone's going to notice we're either the worst navigators in the Fleet or up to something."

"Then we'll be up to something *competently*." She stood, drained the cup, and immediately regretted it. "Set the intercept course. Gentle intercept. If our friend Okafor spots us, I want him to think we're lost, not hunting."

* * *

Dominic spotted them ninety minutes after clearing the last buoy, because of course he did.

It wasn't hard. The Meridian corvette was running dark the way Navy ships always ran dark — which was to say, with her drive signature damped but her hull still radiating heat like a stove, and holding a course that made no sense unless the point was to be exactly where Vesper was going. He watched her drift through his passive array, patient as a heron, and felt something complicated settle in his chest.

Not fear, exactly. Or not only fear. There was a strange comfort in it, too, and he examined that comfort with suspicion, the way you'd examine a gift from someone who owed you money.

*They sent someone. An actual ship, an actual crew, to follow me.* In eleven years of hauling cargo along this corridor, he had been invisible by design — the route chosen precisely because neither fleet bothered with it, the anonymity worn like armor. Being followed meant being seen. Being seen meant mattering, even a little, even as prey.

"That's pathetic," he told himself, and put coffee on.

While it brewed he studied the corvette's handling. That was the interesting part. A lazy tail would have lagged, corrected, lagged again — the telltale stutter of a crew following orders they resented. This one moved like water. Every adjustment smooth, anticipatory, *elegant*. Whoever flew her had real hands.

He keyed the short-range band, open channel, and said, "Corvette off my port quarter, your attitude thrusters are running rich on the yaw axis. Might want to get that looked at."

Silence. Long, dignified, military silence.

Then, finally, a voice — clipped, female, doing a very poor job of sounding bored: "*Vesper*, this is Meridian corvette *Kestrel's Watch*, conducting navigation calibration exercises. Please maintain course and speed."

"Calibration exercises. Sure." He sipped his coffee. "You've been calibrating since the buoy line. Your instructor must be very thorough."

Another silence, longer. When the voice came back, something in it had shifted — the boredom abandoned, though the formality stayed. "Please maintain course and speed, *Vesper*."

"Maintaining," he said cheerfully. "Nice flying, by the way. Genuinely. Who taught you to correct before the drift instead of after?"

A pause. Then, almost against her will: "Flight academy. And then twenty years of being told I overcorrect."

"They lied to you. You don't overcorrect. You pre-correct. It's rarer than people think."

"*Vesper*, this channel is for official traffic."

"Understood. Maintaining course and speed." He clicked off, grinning at his own empty cabin like an idiot, and decided that whoever was flying that corvette, he liked them. Which was probably exactly how they got you.

* * *

Reyes stared at the comm log for a full minute.

"He complimented my flying," she said.

"He complimented your flying," Okonkwo confirmed.

"On an open channel. While being tailed by a warship. He critiqued our thruster balance and then gave me a flight lesson." She pulled up the recording again, listened to her own voice crack slightly on *overcorrect*, and closed her eyes. "I want it noted that I hate this assignment."

"Noted, ma'am."

"What do we know about his ship?"

"Old long-haul frame, heavily modified. Sensors are better than registry claims — he saw us way outside any legal detection envelope. Drive's been rebuilt; efficiency's off the charts for civilian work. And—" Okonkwo hesitated.

"And."

"There's a gap in his route history. Eleven days, eight months ago, unaccounted. His transponder shows him parked at a depot the whole time, but the depot's own logs show no berth issued."

Eight days ago she'd found a signal in Old Meridian coming from coordinates in the deep corridor. Eight months ago, this freighter pilot had gone missing for eleven days. She didn't believe in coincidence; she believed in paperwork, and paperwork was how coincidences hid.

"Keep watching," she said. "And pull everything on the Genesis. The colony ship. Quietly."

"The derelict? Why?"

"Because everyone suddenly cares about quiet little things this month, and quiet little things are never little." She looked at the plot, at the small bright mote that was Vesper, steady on her course toward the corridor's edge, and felt the unfamiliar itch of respect. "He knows we're here. He's not running. Either he's innocent, or he's very good, and I genuinely can't tell which worries me more."

* * *

Three days out from Kestrel, the diplomatic materials turned out to be two sealed cases and a courier named Pell, who boarded at a relay station with the cheerful menace of a man who enjoyed his job too much.

"Morning!" Pell said, ducking through Vesper's airlock and looking around at the cramped, clean, aggressively tidy interior with the expression of a health inspector. "Lovely ship. Cozy. Very... organized."

"I'm a very organized criminal," Dominic said pleasantly. "Cases go there. They stay sealed. I don't ask what's in them, per the contract."

"Oh, I'd hope not." Pell smiled with all his teeth. "Though between us? It's mostly paper. Actual paper. Treaties and position papers and such. The Fleet's old-fashioned about anything that might get leaked — paper doesn't broadcast." He leaned in, confiding. "There's talk, you know. Real talks. First time in years. Everyone's very excited and very nervous, which, in my experience, is the same emotion wearing different hats."

"Talks," Dominic repeated carefully. His heart was doing something complicated against his ribs. Talks meant movement. Movement meant attention on the corridor. Attention was cover, and danger, both at once. "Between whom?"

"Between the right people, eventually. These materials are the groundwork. Positioning." Pell tapped the cases. "You'd be amazed what gets decided by what gets carried, and by whom. Anyway! Where do you want me? I'm told I'm riding to Kestrel and back."

"You're told correctly. Bunk's aft. Don't touch the coolant housing, it bites."

Pell laughed and didn't touch the coolant housing, inside of which — wrapped in thermal sheeting behind the pump assembly — the data core rode in cold darkness, going exactly where Dominic needed it to go.

That evening, Pell ate dinner with him in the tiny galley and talked the entire time, a river of fleet gossip and station rumors, and Dominic let it wash over him while cataloguing it. Halfway through, Pell said, casually, mid-sip: "Funny thing, your name. Okafor. There was a diplomat by that name, years back. Big deal, back before the freeze got total. You related?"

"No," Dominic said, and the lie came out smooth and easy and tasted like ash. "Common name, where I'm from."

"Mm," said Pell, smiling, and changed the subject to the price of fuel at Kestrel, and Dominic understood that the question had been the whole point of dinner, and that his answer was already traveling home in whatever quiet transmitter lived in the man's smile.

After Pell turned in, Dominic sat in the cockpit with the stars crawling past and let himself feel the shape of the trap honestly. They suspected. Maybe not everything — maybe not the core, maybe not Genesis — but enough to leash him. The contract wasn't bait. It was a collar with a very comfortable lining, and he'd put it on willingly, because the alternative was staying free and blind.

But collars ran both ways, if you were clever. A leashed dog got taken places. And somewhere past Kestrel, past the edge of Meridian's maps, a dead woman's voice was still repeating itself in a dead language, and a hulk older than both fleets hung in the dark waiting for him.

He checked the passive feed one last time. Far behind, patient as ever, the corvette held her station — and for a moment he could have sworn she adjusted course a half-second before he did, anticipating him again, that rare pre-correction, as if the pilot were reading his mind or his habits and finding them worth studying.

"Who are you?" he murmured at the distant light.

In the corvette's cockpit, forty thousand kilometers back, Captain Ilsa Reyes was looking at the same plot, at the small bright mote of Vesper, and asking her lieutenant the exact same question.

Neither of them slept well. Both of them, without admitting it, was looking forward to morning.
