## 11. Amara's Last Light

Amara Chen had spent forty-one years learning to die slowly, and she resented being rushed at the end.

She felt the raiders before Vesper's sensors confirmed them—three hard burns out of the dark, deceleration profiles too clean to be salvagers and too aggressive to be anyone with honest business. The deck of the Genesis habitat hummed under her slippers, that old familiar tremor, like a cat purring in its sleep. She had lived inside this ship so long it had stopped feeling like a ship. It was a body. Hers. And now something was coming to kill it.

"Dr. Chen." Dominic's voice crackled through the corridor speakers, tight with the particular strain of a man trying very hard not to shout at an old woman. "Three contacts on approach vector. Compact transponders, spoofed registry, but I know Ashfall drive signatures when I see them. You need to be on this dock in four minutes."

"Then you'd better come help me carry things," she said pleasantly, and cut the channel.

* * *

Dominic stood in the airlock between the Vesper and the Genesis spine, one hand flat against the hull, and for three full seconds he considered simply leaving without her. The thought arrived uninvited, cold and practical: the archives were copied, Reyes was aboard, the tracker was ticking down, and every second he waited was a second the Compact raiders got closer. The original Dominic—the ambitious, compromised man whose face he wore—would have done the math and walked away.

He did the math anyway. Then he went back inside.

"You're insane," Reyes said behind him, jogging to keep up. She had her sidearm drawn and her jacket zipped wrong, which he chose not to mention. "We have copies. Copies are the mission. She is not the mission."

"She's the reason there is a mission," Dominic said. "Also, if you say 'she knew the risks,' I will put you out an airlock, and I'll feel bad about it, but I'll do it."

"I wasn't going to say that."

"You were composing it. I could hear the commas."

Reyes made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth, and they ran.

The habitat's central ring smelled the way it always did—wet green things, old metal, the faint mineral sweetness of recycled water that had been recycled so many times it had achieved a kind of seniority. Amara was in the hydroponics bay, of all places, calmly harvesting tomatoes into a canvas bag while alarms she herself had triggered strobed amber across the ceiling.

"Tomatoes," Dominic said.

"They'll rot otherwise." She snapped another fruit free and dropped it in the bag. "Forty-one years of watering these plants, Dominic. I am not going to let the last generation go to waste because some director's thugs have no appreciation for agriculture."

"We don't have time—"

"There is always time for tomatoes." But her hands were steady in a way that frightened him more than shaking would have. Steady hands meant a decision already made. "Help me with the decoy instead. That's a better use of your remaining minutes."

* * *

The decoy transponder lived in a maintenance crawlspace beneath the habitat's command module, and Amara had built it herself over eleven years, one salvaged component at a time—a second voice for the Genesis, a ghost signal that could broadcast from anywhere in the hulk while the real archive uplink went silent. It was, Dominic thought as he crawled through the duct beside her, exactly the kind of paranoid, patient, magnificent engineering that only a person expecting to be hunted would build.

"It triggers from here?" he asked, wedging his shoulders past a junction.

"From here, or from the command chair, or—" she patted a small brick of circuitry wired into the local net, "—from anywhere within thirty meters of a relay node. I put nodes everywhere. I've been putting nodes everywhere since before you were decanted, dear."

"That's a horrifying sentence."

"Thank you. I worked on it." She reached the trigger housing and paused, both hands resting on it, and in the amber emergency light her face looked carved out of something older than bone. "Now. Listen to me, because I'm only going to say this once and then I'm going to pretend I didn't say it, which will make it easier for both of us."

"Amara—"

"The decoy needs a live operator to sequence the burst pattern. Automated signals get fingerprinted; they taught your Compact friends that trick decades ago. A human hand on the key changes cadence every cycle, and their analysts will chase the ghost for hours believing someone real is aboard." She smiled at him, and it was the same smile she'd used when telling him his tea was terrible, when correcting his Old Meridian pronunciation, when tucking a blanket around his shoulders after he'd fallen asleep in her archive room. "I'm the fastest operator on this ship. I'm also the only operator on this ship."

"No," Dominic said. It came out flat and stupid and absolutely certain. "No. We rig the chair, we run the pattern remotely from Vesper, we—"

"You can't. Latency through the dock umbilical breaks the cadence. I tested it. Twice." She said it gently, the way she explained everything, as though the universe were merely a student who hadn't done the reading. "Dominic. Look at me."

He looked at her. Eighty-some years old, oxygen-starved lungs, hands spotted and thin—and eyes that had watched two centuries of history get buried alive and had spent four decades digging it back up with a trowel and a tomato garden.

"My heart has perhaps a year in it even if we all skip away happily," she said. "My oxygen scrubbers have weeks. My ship has minutes. Everything I am is already in those copies in your pocket—all except my handwriting on a signal burst, which is the one thing I can still give you from right here." Her voice didn't waver. "You are not going to steal my death from me. I've earned it. I've scheduled it. I'm very punctual."

* * *

Later—much later, in the comet-shield dark, when he could finally think about it—he would understand that what broke him wasn't the dying. It was how little ceremony she allowed it. No speeches. She simply took his face in both hands, kissed his forehead the way you kiss a child you're sending off to school, and said, in Old Meridian, the language of a colony nobody remembered:

*"Go be somebody. Not him. Somebody."*

Then she pushed him toward the dock and turned back to her console, and the last thing he saw of her was her shoulders, straight as a keel, and her fingers beginning to move.

* * *

"Umbilical's retracted. Docking clamps released. Go, go, *go*—" Reyes had the Vesper's helm and flew it the way she apparently did everything, with the controlled fury of a woman who had been lied to by an institution for twenty years and had recently decided to stop being polite about it. The freighter peeled away from the Genesis hulk and dove for the debris shoal below its orbital plane, engines cold, momentum doing the work.

On the display, the derelict grew small. And then it lit up.

Not with weapons fire—with *voice*. The decoy transponder bloomed across every band at once, chattering in Old Meridian, in Fleet Standard, in Compact trade-cant, a bright confused chorus of a ship that sounded very much alive and very much occupied and very much *elsewhere*, its signal source hopping between relay nodes like light between mirrors. On the tactical plot, all three raider contacts hesitated. Conferred. Then two of them broke formation and burned toward the phantom.

"They bought it," Reyes breathed.

"Of course they bought it." Dominic found he was gripping the console hard enough to whiten his knuckles. "She wrote the lie. She's had forty years of practice writing lies for people who want to believe them."

The third raider held position near the hulk—the boarding bird, he realized. They were going to put troops aboard. His stomach clenched. "Amara, they're docking, you need to move to the aft relays, do you copy—"

Static. Then her voice, unhurried, faintly amused, threaded through interference: *"I copy, dear. I'm aware. I've read their boarding doctrine; it's very dull. Keep flying, keep quiet, and whatever happens next, remember: the signal dies so the truth lives. That was always the trade. I'm just the one holding the receipt."*

"Amara—"

*"Tomatoes are in the galley cooler. Take them when you land somewhere honest. Goodbye, Dominic."*

And the channel closed—not cut off, but closed, deliberately, the way you close a door on a room you've finished cleaning.

* * *

They ran silent for six hours through the debris shoal while the battle happened behind them, heard but unseen: the sharp staccato of breaching charges, then gunfire echoing hollowly through the hulk's bones, picked up by passive arrays and rendered as ghost-rattles in the Vesper's quiet. Reyes sat watch at the sensor board. Dominic sat across from her and did not sleep, though his body begged.

At 0430 ship-time, the decoy signal died. Not faded—died, mid-burst, chopped clean, followed nine seconds later by a single detonation deep in the hulk's command section as the decoy's power core blew per its final instruction, taking the relay network and any evidence of the archive uplink with it. On the tactical plot, the raiders' chatter dissolved into confusion, then frustration, then the flat professional disappointment of men who had stormed a museum and found it empty except for a dead woman and a burned-out transmitter.

"They'll report success," Reyes said quietly. "'Derelict secured, no survivors, no archives.' Adeyemi will believe it because he wants to." She turned from the board. "It worked. You know that, right? It actually worked."

Dominic nodded. He couldn't speak yet. In his lap, wrapped in a scrap of hydroponic cloth, sat seven tomatoes, slightly bruised, absurdly red, still smelling of green growing things and forty-one years of stubborn care. He held them the way you hold an urn.

"She made me practice my vowels," he said finally. "Every morning. She said if I was going to carry a dead language, I should at least pronounce it properly." His voice cracked on the last word and he let it. "She was the first person who ever talked to me like I was going to *be* anything. Not a copy. Not a contract clause. Just... a student. Somebody worth teaching."

Reyes was quiet for a long moment. Then she unbuckled, crossed the cabin, and sat down beside him—not touching, but near, in the specific geometry of people sharing a grief neither knows how to hold alone.

"When we get clear of the shoal," she said, "we need to talk about what comes next. Because the raiders reporting 'success' means Adeyemi thinks the problem is solved, which means he'll move fast—which means the summit, the treaty, everything, is coming sooner than we thought. And we have exactly one set of archives, one stolen officer, one freighter, and no plan."

"We have more than that," Dominic said.

"Oh?"

He looked down at the tomatoes. Then up, and something in his face had changed—grief still, but grief with a spine in it now, the way Amara's voice had always sounded.

"We have her signal. The distress call that started all this—it's still in Vesper's buffers, every cycle of it. And Amara told me once, months ago, half-asleep, that she never sent anything without hiding a key in it. 'A good broadcaster,' she said, 'always leaves a lockpick in the song.'" He exhaled slowly. "I thought she was rambling. She was never once, in forty-one years, rambling."

Reyes stared at him. "The decryption key. For the full archive. You're saying it's been sitting in that distress loop the whole time?"

"Sitting in plain sight, in a language only two living people could read." He almost smiled. "One of them taught me my vowels."

Outside the viewport, the debris shoal thinned, and beyond it the dark of no-man's-space opened wide—the long road toward the corridor, toward Corvus Ring, toward a summit of liars who believed their secret was safe in a burned-out hulk. Dominic keyed the helm and turned the Vesper's bow toward home he didn't have yet.

Behind them, the Genesis drifted silent and dark, its last light gone out.

Ahead, in his buffers, her voice was still singing.
