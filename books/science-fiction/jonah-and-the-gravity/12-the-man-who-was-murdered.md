## 12. The Man Who Was Murdered

The comet had no name that anyone bothered to keep. In the old survey charts it was listed as K-1147/9, a dirty snowball on a long elliptical errand through the corridor's edge, trailing a shield of ice and gravel and frozen gas wide enough to swallow a freighter whole. Dominic had wedged the *Vesper* into a pocket of the drift three days ago, killed everything but life support, and let the comet's own debris field do what no stealth coating could: make him boring. Invisible by clutter.

It was, he reflected, not so different from how he'd spent most of his life.

He floated in the galley with a bulb of reconstituted tea cooling against his palm and the data core's contents spread across the main display in a slow, patient waterfall. The flight logs he'd already read twice. The genetic archives he'd read four times, because each time his mind kept snagging on the same impossible sentence: *Meridian clade and Ashfall clade share 99.7% genomic identity; divergence is cultural, not biological.* One colony. One people. Two fleets built out of its wreckage, pointing guns at their own reflection for two hundred years.

But tonight he'd opened the folder he'd been avoiding. The one labeled, in plain unencrypted text, **PERSONAL — D.O.** No encryption at all. As if the original had wanted whoever found it to read it. Or as if he'd never imagined anyone would.

Dominic took a breath he didn't need—clones breathed fine, thank you, whatever the port rumors said—and pressed play.

* * *

The first log was dated eleven years before the murder. The original was thirty-one, already decorated, already rising.

*"Log one. I've been asked by Command to draft the assessment of the Ashfall incursion at Threshold Station. The truth is there wasn't an incursion. There was a fueling dispute and two hotheads with itchy trigger fingers. But Voss wants language about 'escalating Compact aggression' for the council briefing, and Voss signs my promotions. So: escalating aggression it is. I'm telling myself everyone does this. I'm telling myself I'll fix it later, from higher up."*

A pause in the recording. Then, quieter:

*"Later. Right. Log one, end."*

Dominic stared at the display. He'd built a shrine in his head over ten years—the murdered diplomat, the martyr, the man whose face Dominic wore like a borrowed coat. Shrines, it turned out, did not survive contact with primary sources.

He queued the next log.

* * *

*"Log forty-something—I've lost count. Amara says I should number them properly but numbering them makes it feel like a ledger and this isn't supposed to be a ledger. Amara Chen, if anyone ever hears these: hydroponicist aboard Genesis, keeper of a dead language, and the only person alive who tells me the truth, which she does mostly by being disappointed in me in Old Meridian. There are tones in that language specifically engineered for disappointment. She has mastered all of them.*

*Today I signed off on the corridor interdiction schedule. You know what it is—a tax on every ship trying to cross between the fleets, dressed up as security. Half those crews are descendants of the same colony we all came from. We're charging our cousins tolls to visit.*

*I told myself when I was young that I'd change the system from inside. What nobody tells you about 'inside' is how comfortable it is in here. The chairs are excellent. The wine is excellent. And every compromise comes pre-wrapped in a flag so it feels like duty instead of appetite.*

*I'm not confessing yet. Confession requires wanting to stop."*

Dominic laughed—one short, startled bark that echoed strangely in the darkened galley. He hadn't expected humor in the dead man's logs. He especially hadn't expected *self-awareness*. The original had watched himself rot in real time and taken notes, like a naturalist embedded among politicians.

He refilled the tea bulb and kept going.

* * *

The middle years were hard reading. Not because they were dramatic—they weren't—but because they were *familiar*. The original covering for a friend's smuggling operation because the friend knew where bodies were buried. The original accepting a quiet apartment upgrade from a Meridian quartermaster whose contracts he'd steered. The original sitting through a memorial service for colonists killed in an "Ashfall raid" that his own analysis suggested had never happened, and delivering the eulogy anyway, beautifully, and hating himself in the shuttle afterward, and then doing it again eight months later.

*"Log ninety,"* the recording said, the voice older now, rougher. *"I gave another eulogy today. Eleven names. I looked out at the families and I thought: I know something that would matter more to you than my eloquence. I know your dead were killed by arithmetic, not enemies. And I said nothing, because saying it ends my career and possibly my life, and I have discovered that I like my life. That's the whole ugly confession. I like my life.*

*Amara says the archives can't stay hidden forever—that truth is like water, it finds the crack eventually. I told her cracks can be sealed. She said, in that tone, 'So can men, Dominic. It's called dying.'*

*I hate her a little. I keep coming back."*

Dominic set down the bulb. His hands were steady, which surprised him. Inside, something less steady was happening—the slow, vertiginous realization that he had spent a decade grieving a man who didn't exist. The martyr was a composite: the official record's polished surface laid over this—this *accountant of compromises*, this man who measured his soul in log entries and always, always found a reason to defer the reckoning until after the next promotion.

And underneath that vertigo, unwelcome and warm, something else: recognition.

Because Dominic knew that man. Had *been* that man, in miniature. Every contract signed under another name, every port entered with his face turned from the cameras, every year of *later, from higher up*—he'd told himself the same story. Stay small, stay safe, stay alive, and someday. Someday what? Someday he'd belong somewhere. Someday the life would start.

The original had died with his someday still in escrow.

"Not me," Dominic said aloud, to the empty galley, and was immediately embarrassed, because the empty galley had heard him say similar things before.

* * *

The turn came in the last year of the original's life. Dominic could pinpoint the log almost to the entry—the moment the tone changed, the way weather changes before a storm you can't yet see.

*"Log one-forty-one. I did a terrible thing today, or rather, I declined to do a good thing, which may be the same thing wearing better clothes. Adeyemi came to me directly—not through channels, directly—and asked me to certify the casualty figures from the Meridian retaliation at Calloway Drift. The real figures are perhaps sixty. The figures he wants certified are four hundred. Four hundred martyrs buy a decade of budget increases, a new escort wing, and public support for the corridor blockade. Sixty buys a footnote.*

*I certified four hundred.*

*I want to be clear about why, because I've gotten very good at lying to this recorder and I'd like, just once, to fail at it. I certified it because he offered me the Genesis file. The real one. Colony-of-origin, full genetic archive, the thing both governments have spent two centuries burying. He said: sign this, and you'll finally see what you've been digging toward for six years. He knew. He's known for years that I've been pulling threads. He let me pull because a curious, ambitious, compromised man is useful—you can aim him like a tool and cut him loose like one.*

*So I signed. And he gave me the file. And I sat in my office and read it and understood that I had sold four hundred strangers for the truth, and that the truth was: there are no strangers. There never were.*

*Amara, if you ever hear this: I'm ready now. I know you've been waiting eleven years for me to be ready and I know you'll say ready is a word for people who've run out of excuses. Fine. Guilty. I'm ready because I've run out of excuses.*

*Let's burn it all down."*

Dominic stopped playback. The galley was very quiet. Somewhere in the drift outside, a shard of ice ticked against the hull like a finger tapping on glass.

He had expected, walking into this folder, to find either a saint or a stranger. Instead he'd found a mirror with better tailoring. The original hadn't been destroyed by the cabal because he was too good—he'd been destroyed because he'd finally become *useful*, and then *dangerous*, in that order. Ambition had carried him into the room; conscience had only arrived late, carrying the bill.

And yet.

Dominic thought about Amara Chen, dying slowly in a sealed garden two hundred years off any chart, keeping a signal alive in a dead tongue. The original had spent eleven years failing to be brave, and then, at the end, he'd chosen the one act of bravery that cost everything. Whatever ledger he'd racked up—and it was long—he'd closed it with a single enormous credit. He'd tried to give two fleets back their shared name, and been murdered for the attempt, and the attempt had *worked*, in the way seeds work: invisibly, underground, waiting.

"You were a coward," Dominic said to the display, conversationally, "right up until you weren't."

The display, naturally, said nothing. But he felt lighter saying it than he'd felt in ten years. Grief, he was discovering, had been the wrong word all along. What he'd carried since the effects release wasn't grief for the original. It was *aspiration*—the exhausting, impossible project of living up to a ghost. And ghosts, it turned out, made terrible role models, because you only ever got shown the highlight reel.

The logs were the outtakes. And the outtakes were where the actual man lived.

* * *

He slept, or something adjacent to sleep, and dreamed of Halcyon Reach's cargo bays and the note in his own handwriting: *If you're reading this, don't go to Genesis.* He'd gone to Genesis anyway. He was beginning to suspect that instruction had never really been a warning. It had been a test, and the original—whoever he'd truly been, coward, collaborator, late-blooming arsonist—had known exactly who would find the core. A clone. A copy. A man built from the same raw material, running the same route, hungry for the same belonging.

*Don't go to Genesis* meant: *don't become me. Don't inherit my delays.* Go, and skip straight to the part where you stop making excuses.

Dominic woke, made tea, and pulled up the final log. Dated nine days before the murder.

*"Last one, or near enough. The plan is set. Amara has the archive copies distributed across three dead drops I won't write down even here—old habits. Reyes is a risk I haven't solved; she's sharp and loyal and those are the same quality viewed from different angles. Voss suspects nothing, which frightens me more than suspicion would. Adeyemi suspects everything, which is worse.*

*If this goes wrong—if I go wrong—someone will come along behind me. Maybe not soon. Maybe someone like... well. Let's not flatter ourselves with prophecy.*

*To whoever you are: I've left you a mess. Compromises, debts, a career's worth of small betrayals with my signature on them. Don't clean it up. Don't redeem me. I don't need a second chance; I need a first witness. Look at what I did—all of it, the good and the shameful—and then do differently. That's the whole inheritance. That's everything I own.*

*One more thing. Whoever you are: pick a name. Not mine. Mine's worn through, like a coat passed down too many times. Pick a name and wear it in daylight. That's not advice. That's the mission."*

End of log.

Dominic sat for a long time while the tea went cold and the comet's slow shadow wheeled past the viewport. Then he did something he'd never done in ten years of hauling other people's cargo under a dead man's face.

He opened the ship's registry file—the one that had read **Okafor, D. (orig.)** since the day he'd inherited it—and added a line beneath it. Just a working draft. Nothing legal. Nothing binding.

**Okafor, Dominic. Second of the name. First of the person.**

It wasn't much. It wasn't a name yet. But it was a direction, and directions were more than he'd had yesterday.

* * *

He was still smiling faintly at his own sentimentality—it was either that or cry, and crying used hydration—when the *Vesper*'s passive array chimed with a priority intercept. Not addressed to him. Addressed to *everyone*: a fleet-wide diplomatic bulletin, dual-signed, broadcast simultaneously on Meridian and Compact command bands.

**EMERGENCY SUMMIT CONVENED — CORVUS RING STATION — FULL DELEGATIONS REQUIRED — AGENDA: PERMANENT CORRIDOR SETTLEMENT AND PARTITION ACCORD.**

Dual-signed. Both fleets agreeing on something, instantly, eagerly, after two centuries of manufactured frost. Which meant the cabal wasn't reacting to pressure anymore. It was moving to make the lie permanent—partition, sealed archives, joint rule of the corridor—with the whole weight of both governments as the stamp.

Dominic looked at the bulletin. Then at the registry file with its single unfinished line. Then at the data core, humming softly beside the tea bulb, holding the truth that was about to be entombed forever behind a treaty seal.

"Okay," he said—to the galley, to the comet, to the dead man whose outtakes he'd finally read. "You got your witness."

He fed course coordinates to the helm. Corvus Ring lay four days out, and he'd need credentials he didn't have, a cover story that shouldn't exist, and help from a Meridian intelligence officer who had every professional reason to arrest him instead.

The *Vesper* eased out of the comet's shield like a fish slipping a net, engines waking, and turned toward the summit of liars.
