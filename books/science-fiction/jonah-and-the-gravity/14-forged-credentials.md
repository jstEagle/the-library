## 14. Forged Credentials

The message arrived at 0300, ship-time, and Dominic read it four times before his hands stopped shaking enough to hold the coffee bulb.

**PRIORITY — MERIDIAN FLEET COMMAND**
*To: Okafor, D. (Cmdr., ret.)*
*Re: Convocation at Corvus Ring*

*In light of your reported survival and recovery from long-term memory disruption sustained during your disappearance eleven years ago, Fleet Command extends formal invitation to attend the Corvus Ring Convocation as accredited observer of the Office of Interfleet Affairs. Biometric verification will be conducted on arrival. Congratulations on your return to service.*

"Congratulations," Reyes said, reading over his shoulder in Vesper's cramped galley. "They killed you and now they're congratulating you. That's almost poetic."

"It's a trap with a bow on it."

"It's a door." She tapped the screen. "You wanted into that summit. Adeyemi's going to be there. Voss is going to be there. The treaty that buries the archives forever gets signed there. This is the only way in that doesn't involve boarding torpedoes."

"And if their biometric scan notices I'm not the man they murdered?"

Reyes pulled out the chair across from him and sat down, which she only did when things were serious. She had a way of occupying a chair like a tribunal.

"Then we're both dead, but honestly, Dominic, I've been dead since I burned my clearance for you. I'd rather be dead doing something than dead waiting for it." She slid a data chip across the table. "So let's make sure they don't notice."

* * *

The forgery took three days, and it was the strangest work of Dominic's life: he was, in effect, forging himself.

Reyes handled the technical side—eleven years as an intelligence officer meant she could build a personnel file so dense with authentic-looking bureaucratic scar tissue that no auditor would dare open it. Medical records showing cryo-stasis trauma consistent with "memory disruption." A fictional recovery facility on a Meridian station that had, conveniently, been decommissioned last year, destroying all corroborating evidence. A commendation for surviving, posthumously awarded and then quietly rescinded when he turned out to be alive, because paperwork hates a resurrection.

"The beauty of it," Reyes said, welding the file together at Vesper's little workbench, "is that bureaucracy always prefers a miracle it can file over a scandal it has to investigate. If you show up with papers saying you're Dominic Okafor, recovered amnesia survivor, then either you are—which is fine—or you're not, and admitting that means admitting someone faked his death, which means reopening the murder case, which means everyone who signed off on this war having a very bad decade. They'll wave you through just to keep the coffin shut."

"And if someone looks too closely?"

"Then I remind them I'm still technically a captain, my arrest would be irregular, and I have a very loud voice." She paused. "Also I stole a shuttle once. I can be persuasive."

But the documents were the easy part. The hard part was the man.

Every night for those three days, Dominic sat with the original's personal logs—the ones he'd found in the comet drift, the ones that had taught him the dead man was ambitious, compromised, half-complicit before his conscience turned—and studied him like a language he was failing to learn.

"How did he take his coffee?" Reyes quizzed him, playing the role of hostile examiner with relish. She'd made flashcards. Actual flashcards, cut from ration packaging.

"Bitter. He complained about sweeteners. Called them 'cowardice in liquid form.'"

"His mother's name?"

"He didn't have one. Colony-born, crèche-raised on Meridian Prime. He lied about it at the academy—said her name was Beatrice. Used the lie so long it became a fact. If anyone asks about Beatrice, he'd smile and change the subject."

"Why?"

"Because the lie embarrassed him, and he covered embarrassment with charm."

"Good." Reyes flipped the card. "Who was his closest friend at the academy?"

Dominic hesitated. "Tomas Iwu. Who is now—" He felt the vertigo rise again. "Who is now Marshal Voss's chief of staff. Who will be at the summit. Who believes his friend died eleven years ago."

"Yes."

"And I'm going to sit across a dinner table from him wearing his dead friend's face."

"You're going to do more than that. You're going to remember his birthday."

Dominic put his head in his hands. "I'm a cargo hauler. I memorize shipping lanes and fuel prices. I don't memorize dead men's birthdays."

"March ninth," Reyes said gently. "He sent Tomas a bottle of Ashfall plum wine every year, even during the embargo, especially during the embargo, because Tomas thought it was hilarious and dangerous. You got it through a smuggler named Kess who worked out of Halcyon Reach. Kess retired eight years ago and opened a noodle shop."

"How do you know all this?"

"I spent eleven years investigating his murder, Dominic. I know what he ate for breakfast the morning he died." Her voice went quiet. "Kasha with dried cherries. He was in a good mood. Whatever he'd found, that morning he still thought he could fix it."

The galley hummed around them. Somewhere behind the bulkhead, Vesper's drive ticked as it cooled, patient as a sleeping animal.

"I never met him," Dominic said finally. "My original. I was decanted two years after he died. Everything I know about being him, I learned secondhand, from files, from people who hated him or loved him or both. And now I have to walk into a room full of those people and be convincing." He looked up. "What if I'm not? What if I'm just a bad copy giving a bad performance?"

Reyes considered this with the seriousness she gave everything.

"Here's what I know about grief," she said. "People don't remember the dead accurately. They remember a version of them shaped by missing them. Eleven years is a long time. The man Tomas Iwu remembers isn't the man who died—it's the man softened by absence, smoothed down, made kind by distance. You don't have to be Dominic Okafor. You have to be the memory of Dominic Okafor. And memories, thank God, are forgiving."

"That's the most comforting thing anyone's ever said to me, and it's also horrifying."

"Welcome to intelligence work."

* * *

Corvus Ring hung in the black like a crown thrown away by a careless king—a great broken circle of docking spars and conference halls, built back when someone still believed the two fleets might share things. It had been neutral ground for sixty years and used exactly twice, both times to exchange prisoners and insults.

As Vesper approached, traffic control's voice came through, clipped and suspicious. "Freighter Vesper, you are cleared to berth fourteen. Be advised, biometric verification required on disembark. Welcome to Corvus Ring."

That last word carried no welcome whatsoever.

Dominic stood at the airlock in a borrowed Meridian dress uniform that fit him perfectly, because of course it did—it had been fitted for his face, his shoulders, his height. Eleven years in climate-controlled storage, waiting for a corpse that never came to claim it. The collar was stiff. The medals were real. One of them, he'd learned from the logs, the original had privately admitted he didn't deserve.

"You'll do great," Reyes murmured, adjusting a collar tab she didn't need to adjust, buying herself three seconds of touching something familiar. "Remember: charm covers embarrassment. Bitterness covers coffee. March ninth."

"Plum wine. Kess. Noodle shop."

"If Tomas hugs you, let him. Men like that hug once a decade and it costs them something."

"And if Voss tests me?"

"Then you're charming, vague, and slightly fragile. Amnesia survivors get enormous latitude. Forget things. Forgetting is your entire medical excuse for existing." She stepped back and looked at him—really looked, the way she'd looked at the archives, hunting for flaws. "God. Even I forget sometimes. Standing here, I almost think—"

"Don't." His voice came out harder than he intended. "Please don't finish that sentence."

She didn't. She touched his shoulder once, briefly, and went to play her own role: Captain Ilsa Reyes, reassigned to escort duty, career smoldering quietly behind her eyes.

The airlock cycled. Dominic walked out onto Corvus Ring, wearing a dead man's body and a living man's fear, and the first person he saw was a woman with silver-streaked hair and a delegate's sash who stopped dead in the concourse, pressed a hand to her mouth, and whispered:

"Dominic? *Dominic.* They told us you were dead."

And there was nothing to do but open his arms and let a stranger grieve into his chest, while over her bowed head he saw, at the far end of the hall, Director Sefu Adeyemi turn around slowly, deliberately, like a man who has just heard a sound in an empty house—

—and start walking toward him with a smile that had murder in it, polished smooth by eleven years of practice.

"*Commander Okafor,*" Adeyemi called warmly, closing the distance. "Of all people. We simply *must* talk about where you've been."

* * *

The reception that evening was held in the Ring's Grand Rotunda, under a dome painted with the constellations of Earth—a world neither fleet had seen in three hundred years, rendered by some long-dead optimist who'd clearly believed someone would go back.

Dominic moved through it like a man wading through warm water, smiling, nodding, being embraced by ghosts.

It was worse than he'd imagined and funnier than he'd feared. A Meridian admiral toasted his "miraculous return" and immediately tried to recruit him for a committee. An Ashfall minister pinched his cheek and told him he hadn't aged a day, which was, in fairness, medically accurate. Someone's aunt—weeping, delighted—insisted he'd promised her son a job on his staff eleven years ago and demanded he honor it, and Dominic heard himself say, "First thing after the treaty, Marta, I swear on my mother's grave," and then had to stand there smiling while internally screaming *he doesn't have a mother, I don't have a mother, why did I say that, oh God, Beatrice, use Beatrice—*

"Beatrice sends her love," he added smoothly, and the aunt beamed and moved on, and Dominic took a long swallow of wine and decided that impersonating the dead was ninety percent improvisation and ten percent terror, and that the ratio reversed without warning.

"You look well," said a voice beside him, "for a dead man."

Tomas Iwu stood there with two glasses of plum wine, and his eyes were wet and fierce and searching, and Dominic's heart performed a maneuver not approved by any flight manual.

"Tomas." He took the glass. Ashfall plum, smuggled, probably by the noodle man's successors. Some traditions survived everything.

"They told me it was amnesia." Tomas searched his face. "Eleven years, Dom. Eleven years I lit a candle on your birthday. March ninth. Every year. And now you walk in here looking like—" His voice caught. "Looking like nothing ever happened to you."

*Let him,* Reyes had said. *Men like that hug once a decade.*

But Tomas didn't hug him. He gripped Dominic's forearm instead, hard, the old academy grip, and leaned close, and for one terrible second his eyes weren't wet anymore—they were sharp, and quick, and intelligent, and something flickered across them that might have been doubt.

"You'll come to dinner tomorrow," Tomas said. "Just us. Like old times. There's so much to catch up on." A pause, delicate as a blade laid flat on a table. "There's so much I want to ask you."

"I'd like that," Dominic said, and heard the original's warmth in his own voice, borrowed and worn in, and wondered whose heartbeat was making that much noise.

Across the rotunda, Adeyemi watched them over the rim of his glass, unhurried, pleasant, absolutely certain.

And near the great doors, Captain Ilsa Reyes caught Dominic's eye for half a second and gave him the smallest shake of her head—not *don't*, but *careful*—because she had seen it too.

Tomorrow there would be dinner with a grieving best friend who might already suspect. Tonight there was only the wine, bitter as the original liked it, and the long walk to a cabin with a dead man's name stenciled on the door, and somewhere above them all, in the painted constellations of Earth, a long-dead optimist's stars wheeling silently over a room full of liars raising glasses to peace.
