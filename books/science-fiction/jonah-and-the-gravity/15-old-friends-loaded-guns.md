## 15. Old Friends, Loaded Guns

The champagne at Corvus Ring was terrible.

Dominic had learned this within the first hour of the summit's opening reception, when a steward in Meridian dress grays pressed a flute into his hand and he'd taken a sip out of politeness. It tasted like someone had dissolved a coin in vinegar and then apologized for it. He'd smiled anyway—the dead man's smile, easy and diplomatic—and held the glass for the next three hours without drinking again, which was, he was beginning to understand, the single most authentic thing about his performance so far.

*Dominic Okafor, recovered amnesia survivor,* the credentials read. *Rescued from a derelict transport near the Genesis corridor. Identity confirmed by biometric match.*

Biometric match. Of course it matched. He was the only man in two armadas whose DNA came pre-notarized.

He worked the reception the way he imagined the original would have—three sentences to a person, remember the name, ask about their homeworld, exit gracefully. It was strange how well the body remembered things the mind had never learned. His hands found the right gestures. His laugh landed in the right register. Somewhere in the architecture of him, ten years of a murdered diplomat's muscle memory were still folded away like letters in a drawer, and tonight he was rifling through all of them.

"You look like a man enjoying himself," Reyes murmured, materializing at his elbow with two fresh flutes. She handed him one as cover and spoke without moving her lips much, a skill he was starting to envy. "You look like a man who enjoys vinegar."

"It's an acquired taste," Dominic said pleasantly, for the benefit of the Compact delegate drifting past. "I acquired it before my accident, apparently."

"Apparently." Her eyes swept the room. "Marshal Voss has been watching you for twenty minutes. Not the polite kind of watching. The kind where she's counting your blinks."

"I don't blink much."

"That's what worries her."

Across the ballroom, under a chandelier of salvaged crystal that had once, according to the plaque, hung in the Genesis's first-class lounge—a detail that made Dominic's stomach tighten every time he looked at it—Marshal Elin Voss stood in a small constellation of aides and did not drink either. She was a tall woman with silver-shot hair cut short as a verdict, and she had known the original Dominic Okafor for eleven years. She had dined with him. She had, if Amara Chen's fragments were to be believed, signed off on at least two of his intelligence briefings before deciding he knew too much.

She raised her glass to him across the room. A small salute. A smile that reached exactly halfway up her face and stopped, like a lift between floors.

Dominic raised his own untouched flute in return and thought: *one wrong word.* One wrong word and the summit became a morgue with better lighting.

* * *

Captain Ilsa Reyes had been followed before. It was practically a professional courtesy in Meridian intelligence—you surveilled your colleagues the way sailors checked each other's rigging, not out of malice but because everyone eventually frayed. But there was a difference between being watched by your own service and being watched by Director Sefu Adeyemi's people, and the difference was that Adeyemi's people didn't file reports. They filed disappearances.

She'd counted them twice on the walk from her quarters to the conference wing: two men, gray suits, Compact-cut lapels, moving with that particular unhurried gait of professionals who wanted to be seen. A message, then. Not a tail. An announcement.

*Adeyemi knows I vouched for you,* she'd told Dominic the night before, in the supply alcove they'd turned into a war room, surrounded by crates of vacuum-rated rations and one very confused maintenance drone. *He let me bring you here anyway. Which means either he believes the amnesia story—*

"—or he wants you inside the room when the treaty signs," Dominic had finished. "So he can watch what you do."

"So he can watch what *you* do," she'd corrected. "I'm just the leash."

Now she sat in the observers' gallery above the main hall, second row, hands folded, watching the two men take turns pretending to read their tablets while watching her pretend to read hers. She gave them nothing. Twenty years of poker face, purchased at considerable personal cost.

Her comm buzzed against her wrist. A priority flag from Fleet Command, routed through channels that made her jaw tighten: *Capt. Reyes — supplemental orders attached. Acknowledge.*

She opened them in the privacy of her retinal feed.

*At Director Adeyemi's request, Capt. Reyes is relieved of observer duties effective 0900 tomorrow. Capt. Reyes will instead serve as liaison to the Compact security delegation for the duration of the summit.*

Liaison. Which meant embedded. Which meant her every movement logged, her quarters subject to inspection, her access codes mirrored on Adeyemi's desk by morning. He wasn't going to wait for Dominic to slip. He was going to shrink the world around him until slipping was impossible.

And buried in the boilerplate, one line that made her blood go cold and hot at once:

*Liaison will surrender all personal data devices to Compact security upon assignment.*

All personal data devices. Including the encrypted shard in her breast pocket holding a copy of the shared-origin archives—the real ones, the ones Amara Chen had died protecting, the reason any of them were still breathing.

Reyes acknowledged receipt with a single crisp *Understood*, then walked calmly to the gallery rail, looked down at the hall below, and found Dominic Okafor laughing at something a Compact admiral had said, wearing a dead man's face and a live man's terror underneath it.

*We have until 0900,* she thought. *Then we're both naked in the snow.*

* * *

Voss caught him at the end of the second session, in the corridor outside the main hall, while the delegations broke for what the schedule optimistically called "informal refreshment" and what everyone understood to be espionage with canapés.

"Dominic." Just his name, in a voice like a hand on the back of the neck. "Walk with me."

There was no graceful way to refuse a marshal of the Meridian Fleet, so he walked. She led him along the observation gallery, past windows that looked out onto Corvus Ring's slow carousel of stars, and stopped at a display case he clearly was meant to notice. Inside, behind old-fashioned glass, lay a fragment of hull plating, scorched black, mounted on a stand.

"Do you know what this is?" Voss asked.

"The Genesis," Dominic said. "First contact with the wreck, forty years ago. Meridian salvage team took the fragment as proof of—" He stopped himself, recalibrated. The original would know this cold. Would he find it interesting? Tragic? "Proof of how far our people fell before the Fleets rose. You gave a speech about it. At the Academy, the year I—" *the year I died*, his brain supplied helpfully, "—the year of my accident."

Something flickered in Voss's eyes. Satisfaction? Or the pleasure of a fisherman feeling weight on the line?

"Close," she said. "It was the *second* expedition. The first found nothing but vacuum and bad air, and Command wanted the whole search quietly ended. I argued against ending it." She turned from the case to face him fully. "You argued with me. For six hours, over brandy neither of us drank. You said—" she paused, delicately, "—do you remember what you said?"

The corridor was very quiet. Somewhere below, a string quartet was massacring a Meridian folk air.

*Say something wrong and die. Say nothing and die slower.*

"I said," Dominic heard himself answer, "that a people who forget where they crashed will crash there again."

Voss studied him for a long moment. Then she laughed—a short, genuine bark that seemed to surprise even her—and shook her head.

"Word for word. Ten years, and it comes back word for word." She touched his arm, briefly, almost tenderly. "They told me you might be diminished. Fragmented. I'm glad they were wrong. Though—" and here her voice went light as frost forming, "—it's odd, isn't it? The doctors' files say retrograde amnesia. Total loss. And yet the brandy argument survives intact."

"Memory is a strange country," Dominic said. "I've been lost in it for a decade. Some districts survived the fire. Others didn't." He met her eyes and let a little of the truth leak through, because the best lies are load-bearing truths. "There are whole years I couldn't tell you anything about, Elin. Ask me about them and you'll get silence, not comfort. Is that what you want to hear?"

It was a gamble. He watched her weigh it—the marshal who had loved or used or merely tolerated the original, searching his face for the forgery beneath.

"No," Voss said finally. "What I want to hear is what you remember about the last briefing you gave me. Before the accident." Her grip on his arm firmed, pleasant as a manacle. "You sent it encrypted. Personal channel. You never explained why. Then you died, and the message was sealed pending your recovery." A smile, halfway up. "Well. You've recovered. Tomorrow morning, you'll come to my office and unseal it yourself. Biometric lock. Your voiceprint, your retina, your handwriting on the release waiver."

*The archives,* Dominic thought, with the distant clarity of a man watching the ice crack under his boots. *Whatever the original sent her—it's still sitting in her vault, and she's been waiting ten years to open it.*

"I look forward to it," he said.

"I know you do." She released him and stepped back into the flow of delegates, then paused, glancing over her shoulder. "Oh—one more thing. The steward tells me you haven't touched your champagne all evening. Two nights running." Her eyes glittered. "Our Dominic used to drink like a man putting out fires. Funny, the things amnesia takes."

She left him standing alone in front of the scorched hull fragment, smiling the dead man's smile, doing arithmetic with his remaining hours.

* * *

Reyes found him at 2200 in the supply alcove, which was becoming, against all regulations and several laws of physics, the most important room on Corvus Ring.

"She set a trap," he said, before the door finished cycling shut. "A biometric seal on a message my original sent her before he died. Tomorrow morning I open it myself—or I'm a fraud in front of the woman most likely to shoot me personally rather than delegate the job."

"What's in the message?"

"If I knew that, I'd sleep." He dragged both hands down his face. "It could be nothing. A resignation letter. It could be the names of the cabal. It could be evidence that would hang us both. I won't know until I'm standing in her office with her sidearm approximately nine inches from my spine."

Reyes leaned against the ration crates and, despite everything, felt the corner of her mouth twitch. "You know, most impostors worry about table manners."

"My table manners are flawless. It's my murder logistics that need work." He dropped onto an overturned crate. "And Adeyemi?"

"Relieved me of observer duty. Effective 0900. I'm now 'liaison' to his security team—which means watched, searched, and mirrored." She pulled the archive shard from her breast pocket and held it up between two fingers, a sliver of dull metal no bigger than a thumbnail, containing the history of two civilizations. "They'll take this off me at 0900 too. Unless it stops existing between now and then."

Dominic looked at the shard, then at her. "You're not suggesting—"

"I'm suggesting we stop being clever and start being desperate. There's a maintenance conduit off the hydroponics ring. If the shard has an unfortunate accident—falls into a coolant recycler—I report the loss, eat the disciplinary hearing, and Adeyemi gets a very sincere apology from a careless officer." She turned the shard in the light. "But we keep the copy you carry. And we accept that after tomorrow, I'm inside Adeyemi's pocket with no way to pass you anything except through sheer theatrical talent."

"And the seal?" Dominic asked. "Voss's trap?"

Reyes was quiet for a moment. Then she sat down beside him on the crate, shoulder to shoulder, two conspirators in a room that smelled of recycled air and vacuum rations, and said, "Tell me honestly. When she tested you today—when she asked if you remembered—what did you feel?"

"Like a man tap-dancing over a minefield."

"No. Before that. When you got the words right. Word for word." She watched his profile. "Did it feel like acting?"

He didn't answer immediately. Out beyond the window, the stars wheeled their slow indifferent wheel, and somewhere out there the Genesis drifted with its gardens and its grave, and a dead woman's signal had taught him a language officially extinct.

"No," he admitted. "It felt like remembering."

"Then maybe that's the answer to the seal." Reyes stood, tucked the shard away, and allowed herself the luxury of one grim smile. "Maybe whatever Okafor sent her, the man who remembers it is standing in this room. Go open your own mail, Dominic. And if it's a death warrant—" she shrugged, "—at least you'll die knowing your table manners were impeccable."

He laughed despite himself, and it echoed strangely off the ration crates—warm, human, entirely his own.

Neither of them saw the maintenance drone in the corner, its optical sensor glinting, quietly recording everything.

Neither of them noticed that its serial number matched the Compact security inventory.

And neither of them could have known that in an office two decks above, Director Sefu Adeyemi was already watching the playback, glass of genuine pre-Fall whisky in hand, murmuring to the empty room:

"Well, well. Hello again, Dominic."
