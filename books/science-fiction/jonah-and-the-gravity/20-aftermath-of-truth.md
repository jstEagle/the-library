## 20. Aftermath of Truth

The fleets had stopped pretending to fight.

That was the strangest part, Ilsa Reyes thought as she stood on the observation deck of the Meridian cruiser *Halberd's Wake* and watched two armadas hang in the black like guests at a funeral who weren't sure whose funeral it was. For eleven days since Corvus Ring, the Meridian Fleet and the Ashfall Compact had maintained their formations—thousands of ships in their centuries-old postures of mutual menace—but nobody had fired. Nobody had moved. The war had been a machine, and the broadcast had cut its power, and now everyone was standing around the dead thing wondering what they were supposed to do with their hands.

She'd been doing the same herself.

Her career was over. She'd known it would be when she chose truth over loyalty, and she'd made her peace with it somewhere between the broadcast and the gunfire. What she hadn't expected was the summons—not to a brig, not to a tribunal, but to a conference room aboard *Halberd's Wake*, where three officers of the Provisional Council sat behind a table that had clearly been borrowed from the mess hall because nobody could agree on whose flag should be on the wall.

"Captain Reyes." The senior officer, a gray-haired woman named Okonkwo with the tired eyes of someone who'd spent the last week reading archives she'd been ordered to forget, gestured to a chair. "Please sit."

Reyes sat. She kept her hands visible on the table, an old habit from interrogations, and waited.

"You understand your status," said the second officer, a Compact liaison—Reyes noted, with distant amusement, that the Compact had sent a liaison to a Meridian ship for the first time in anyone's living memory, and he looked like a man who kept pinching himself to check. "You're relieved of command pending review. Your access is revoked. Technically, you're here as a courtesy."

"I'm aware."

"And yet," Okonkwo said, "you are also the only intelligence officer in either fleet who saw the truth coming and did something about it. Which makes you, in the current climate, either a traitor or the most valuable witness we have." She slid a slate across the table. "We'd like you to testify. Formally. On the record. About everything: the manufactured incidents, the corridor traffic patterns, Director Adeyemi's network, Marshal Voss's orders. And about him."

"Him," Reyes repeated.

"The clone. Okafor."

"He has a name," Reyes said, before she could stop herself.

Okonkwo's eyebrows rose a fraction. "Does he? And what would that be?"

"That's his to give," Reyes said. "Not mine. Not yours. That's rather the point of all this, isn't it?"

There was a silence. The Compact liaison coughed into his fist, which might have been hiding a smile.

"The council will extend him the same offer," Okonkwo said finally. "Testimony, immunity, and—" she hesitated, choosing words like someone defusing ordnance, "—recognition. As himself. Whatever name he chooses to testify under. But he won't come unless you ask him. He trusts exactly one person in either fleet, Captain, and as of eleven days ago, that person is no longer employed by anyone."

Reyes looked down at the slate. It was blank except for a comm frequency and a single line: *Your call.*

It was, she thought, the first honest piece of paperwork the Meridian Fleet had ever handed her.

* * *

Dominic found the message waiting when Vesper finished her slow drift back toward the corridor's midpoint. He read it three times, sitting in the pilot's chair with his boots up on the console, while the two armadas turned slowly on the display like weather systems deciding whether to become storms.

They wanted him to testify. Not as Dominic Okafor, diplomat, murdered ten years ago and resurrected last week in a stolen suit and forged credentials. As himself. Under whatever name he chose.

He laughed out loud, alone in the cockpit, and it came out slightly unhinged.

"What name?" he asked the empty cabin. "I've had one name my whole life and it belonged to a dead man with bad judgment and excellent tailoring."

Vesper's cabin lights hummed. Outside, a Meridian patrol cutter passed at a respectful distance—the first Meridian ship in a decade that hadn't tried to scan him, board him, or shoot at him—and flashed a navigation light pattern that was either a greeting or a bureaucratic error.

He thought about Amara. He thought about how she'd called him by his original's name right up until the end, and then, in her last hour, had caught herself and said, *No—you're not him, are you? You're what he was trying to become.* He thought about the note in his own handwriting: *don't go to Genesis,* written by a man who had gone anyway, because conscience is a terrible listener.

He keyed the frequency.

"Reyes."

"Dominic." Her voice was careful, professional, and underneath it, tired in a way that made something in his chest ache sympathetically. "You got the council's message."

"I got it. They want me to testify under my own name." He leaned back. "Small problem. I checked the registry. There's no such person. I'm a legal ghost with a face that's wanted in four jurisdictions—well, three now, I suppose, if we count Adeyemi's jurisdiction as dissolved."

"It's dissolved," Reyes said dryly. "Adeyemi fled toward deep space two days ago with three loyalist ships and whatever he could loot from the Compact treasury. Last tracking put him heading for the Oort fringe, running dark. Half the fleet wants him brought back for trial. The other half wants to pretend he never existed so they don't have to explain how he got so powerful."

"And which half are you?"

"I'm currently unemployed, so I get to want whatever I want." A pause. "I want him found. He killed your original. He'd have killed both of us at Corvus Ring if Voss's crew hadn't mutinied first."

Dominic watched the patrol cutter recede. "Why did you call? Really. The council could have sent anyone."

Another pause, longer. When she spoke again, the professionalism had thinned. "Because I know what they're offering sounds like a trap. Because you'll walk into that hearing expecting handcuffs, and you'll be braced for it, and you won't hear anything else they say. And because—" she exhaled, "—because somebody should tell you that when they asked me to reach out, I said yes before they finished the sentence. And I don't do that. I've never done that. So."

So. Dominic smiled at the ceiling.

"They offered you your commission back," he said. "In exchange for delivering me."

"They offered. I declined to accept conditionally. I told them I'd deliver you regardless, and they could sort out my employment afterward." He could almost see her shrug. "Turns out unemployment suits me. I sleep better."

"You slept at all?"

"Four hours. Continuous. It was decadent." Now he definitely heard the smile. "Come to the hearing, Dominic. Testify. Not for them—for the record. Amara kept those archives alive for forty years in a tin can with failing oxygen so that someday there'd be a record. You're the record now. You're the only living person who heard her say it."

He was quiet for a long moment.

"There's one more problem," he said. "The name."

"You'll think of something."

"How do you know?"

"Because," Reyes said, "you already stopped being him. The rest is just paperwork."

* * *

The hearing was held aboard Genesis itself.

That had been the Compact delegation's suggestion, apparently—a gesture nobody quite trusted and everybody found themselves unable to refuse. The old colony ship hung in no-man's-space where she'd drifted for two centuries, scarred, dark along most of her length, but with one habitat ring glowing faintly amber where emergency power still held. Delegates from both fleets docked at her ancient ports in shuttles stenciled with rival insignias, and walked the same corroded corridors, and pretended not to notice that they were all breathing the same recycled air.

Dominic arrived last, in Vesper, because he insisted on arriving last. If this was a trap, he wanted to see it spring on someone else first.

It wasn't a trap. It was worse: it was sincere.

They'd set the hearing in the hydroponics bay—the restored one, the ring Amara had kept alive for forty years. Someone had cleaned it. Someone had replanted it; there were rows of green shoots in the old growth trays, soybeans and dwarf wheat and something flowering white that no botanist present could identify, which meant it might have been growing in the sealed sections for two hundred years. Delegates sat on folding chairs among the planters, and the air smelled like wet soil and chlorophyll, and every few minutes someone would look around at the green and the old hull plating stamped with the colony markings—the shared markings, the ones from before the schism—and go quiet in a way that had nothing to do with protocol.

Dominic stood at the front, in a plain civilian jacket, and felt four hundred pairs of eyes cataloguing his face. Half of them had known his original. Some of them had probably attended the memorial service. One elderly Meridian admiral kept staring at him with an expression of profound indigestion, as though the universe had served him a course he'd specifically ordered removed.

The presiding officer—a joint panel, one voice from each fleet plus a neutral arbiter scraped from some station civil service—read the charges into the record. Impersonation of a deceased official. Forgery of credentials. Unauthorized transmission on fleet channels. Hijacking of summit communications. Dominic listened to the list with the strange detachment of a man hearing his own obituary read aloud, and noticed that the gentle humor of it didn't quite reach his hands, which had gone cold.

"Do you understand these charges?" the arbiter asked.

"I committed all of them," Dominic said. "Some twice."

A ripple through the room. Somewhere in the Compact section, someone snorted.

"Before testimony begins," the arbiter continued, "the record requires a name for the witness. The council has extended recognition of identity on the witness's own terms. State your name for the record."

And there it was. The whole chapter of his life balanced on it. He thought of contracts signed under another man's name, of ports where his own face was contraband, of a note in his own handwriting warning him away from the truth. He thought of Amara's white flowers, growing in the dark for two hundred years without anyone to name them.

"My original was Dominic Okafor," he said. "He was ambitious, and compromised, and then he was brave, in that order, and he died for it. I'm not him. I finished his work, but I'm not him, and I'd like the record to stop confusing us." He paused. "My name is Jonah Okafor. Dominic gave me the name without knowing it—it was in his logs, the story he used to tell himself about why he did what he did. Jonah and the gravity. The man who ran from the call until the call got bigger than his fear." He looked out at the room, at the green shoots and the old hull and the faces of two fleets. "I ran for ten years. I'm done. Jonah Okafor. That's the name. It's mine. I just grew into it."

Silence. Then the elderly admiral with the indigestion stood up, cleared his throat, and said, in a voice roughened by decades of command, "I knew Dominic Okafor. Dined with him twice. He once lied to my face about convoy schedules with tremendous charm." A pause. "You're not him. He never would have admitted to anything." He sat back down, and that, somehow, was the moment the room believed.

* * *

The testimony took six hours. Reyes watched all of it from the third row, unemployed and uninvited and utterly certain that no one would dare remove her, and by the fourth hour she noticed that the stenographers from both fleets had stopped typing and started simply listening, which was its own kind of verdict.

He told it all: the distress call in Old Meridian, the data core, the flight logs, Amara. When he described her—forty years alone in a dying habitat, keeping the archives alive, broadcasting into the dark in a language both fleets had let die—he had to stop for a moment, and nobody rushed him, and in the silence the ventilation hummed and the plants breathed, and Reyes understood that something was happening in this room that none of the charges on the record could contain.

When it ended, the panel withdrew for deliberation and came back in twenty minutes, which anyone who had ever dealt with a military tribunal knew was either very good or catastrophic.

It was very good. Immunity, full and joint, signed by both councils. Recognition of identity. And then, awkwardly, the thing nobody had put on the agenda: the Compact delegate stood, adjusted his collar, and said, "The Provisional Councils would also like to ask the witness—Mr. Okafor—whether he would consider... remaining available. In a consultative capacity. Regarding the Genesis site. And the archives. And..." he faltered, "...matters of shared heritage."

"You want to hire me," Jonah said.

"We want to *not lose you*," the delegate admitted, with the pained honesty of a man saying something his briefing documents had forbidden.

From the Meridian side, Admiral Okonkwo added, "Both delegations. Jointly. We're told that's unprecedented. Everything is unprecedented now. We're learning to live with it."

Jonah looked at Reyes. She raised her eyebrows: *your call.* His call. That was the whole shape of the new world, she thought—everything was somebody's call now, instead of everybody's orders.

"I'll stay," he said. "On conditions. Genesis becomes shared ground—memorial, archive, school. Old Meridian gets taught again, properly, with native speakers' recordings—Amara left hours of them. And the white flowers in hydroponics get named after her." He paused. "Dr. Amara Chen. Look it up; it's in the archives you sealed for a century. She earned a flower."

Motion carried. Unanimous. Two fleets agreeing on something for the first time in two hundred years, and the item was flowers.

Afterward, in the corridor outside the hydroponics bay, with delegates streaming past them toward their shuttles and the amber light of the old ship falling through the viewports, Reyes fell into step beside him.

"Jonah Okafor," she said, testing it.

"Still getting used to it myself."

"It suits you." She glanced sideways. "Though I notice you kept the surname."

"He had good taste in surnames. Terrible judgment otherwise." Jonah stopped at a viewport where Genesis's scarred hull curved away into the dark, the habitat ring glowing like a coal that refused to die. "One more thing came in while I was testifying. Priority channel, both fleets, flagged jointly." He handed her the slate.

She read it, and the warmth of the afternoon went out of her like a snuffed lamp.

Deep-space tracking array, long-range passive. Three contacts running dark on an outbound vector past the Oort fringe, consistent with the looted Compact ships. And attached, a fragmentary signal intercept—brief, garbled, but unmistakable in its grammar and cadence.

Old Meridian.

Someone fleeing into the deep dark was broadcasting in a language only two living people were supposed to know—one of whom was dead, and the other of whom was standing right here holding the intercept.

"Adeyemi," Reyes said quietly.

"Or someone Adeyemi took with him," Jonah said. "Either way—he knows the signal. He knows what Amara taught the original. Which means he knows things about the archives that we haven't even found yet." He looked out at the dark beyond the hull, where three cold contacts were falling away from every human home there was. "The councils want to send a ship. Fast one. Small crew."

Reyes looked at him. He was already smiling, that slightly unhinged smile she was beginning to suspect was permanent.

"Vesper's fast," he said.

"Vesper's a cargo hauler with delusions."

"Vesper," Jonah said, "is the only ship in either fleet whose captain speaks Old Meridian." He tapped the intercept against his palm. "Well. Almost the only one. Want to find out who the other is?"

Reyes sighed, and felt—despite everything, despite the mutiny and the gunfire and the career she'd burned—something that felt suspiciously like being glad.

"When do we launch?"
