## 21. First Contact, Second Chance

The Genesis had been dying for two hundred years, and now she was being born.

Dominic stood at the edge of the grand atrium—the same chamber where, three months earlier, he had crouched behind a collapsed bulkhead while Amara Chen's decoy transponder sang its last song—and watched two hundred workers from two fleets argue about lighting.

That was the argument. Lighting.

The Meridian contingent wanted warm amber, "for dignity." The Ashfall contingent wanted cool white, "for clarity." The argument had been going on for eleven days, and it had escalated through formal memoranda, a near-incident involving a stolen lighting rig, and one memorable shouting match in which a Meridian engineer had called an Ashfall engineer's color theory "a crime against photons."

They had settled, in the end, on both. Amber above, white below, meeting in a soft gradient at the middle of the atrium where the two delegations would sit facing each other across a table made from the ship's original hull plating.

"It's a compromise," Reyes had said, squinting up at it. "It's also the ugliest thing I've ever seen."

"It's the most beautiful thing I've ever seen," Dominic had answered, and meant it, because nobody had ever compromised over lighting before in his presence. Wars were fought over lighting. Compromises over lighting were new, and new things deserved reverence.

Now the delegations were arriving, and the atrium smelled like fresh sealant and cut flowers—actual cut flowers, grown in the restored hydroponics bay, in the very trays Amara had kept alive for forty years with scavenged parts and stubbornness. Someone had thought to harvest from her garden for the table. Dominic had stood in that bay that morning, alone, and touched a tomato still warm from the grow-lamps, and had to excuse himself for a moment to a maintenance alcove where nobody could see a grown man's face do what it was doing.

"Delegate Okafor." A young Meridian aide, nervous, holding a tablet like it might bite. "They're ready for you. Whenever you're—whenever."

"Thank you." He straightened his collar. He was wearing his own name today, stitched into the delegate's sash: *O. OKAFOR, WITNESS*. Not *D. Okafor, Diplomat, Meridian Fleet*. Just Okafor. It had taken a provisional council, three hearings, and a truly absurd amount of paperwork to make that small change, and he treasured it more than anything he had ever owned.

* * *

The testimony chamber was the old colony records hall, which felt right. The last time anyone had spoken official words in this room, they had been the founders of both fleets, and the words had been the same words for both of them, in the same language, before the language split and the fleets split and everything split.

The table was long and the room was full. Meridian on the left, Ashfall on the right, neutral observers from Halcyon Reach and the corridor stations filling the back rows. Marshal Voss's empty chair had been removed; Adeyemi's had been removed too, though the Ashfall delegation kept glancing at the space where it had been, the way you glance at a missing tooth.

Dominic stood at the witness station, and the room went quiet in a way that rooms rarely do—two hundred people deciding, all at once, to stop breathing.

He had written a speech. He had rewritten it nine times. He had practiced it in Vesper's cockpit, to an audience of cargo manifests. And now, standing there, he abandoned all of it.

"My name is Dominic Okafor," he said. "I want to be very clear about which Dominic Okafor, because for most of my life, that wasn't clear even to me."

A ripple. Somewhere in the Meridian section, an older delegate folded her hands and waited with the patience of someone who had sat through many speeches and expected to sit through one more.

"I'm a clone. I was grown in a facility on Halcyon Reach from tissue samples sold by a company that doesn't exist anymore, and for thirty-one years I signed contracts under my original's name because it was the only name anyone would honor. I hauled cargo through the corridor because the corridor was the only place where my face wasn't wanted or feared, depending on which side of it you stood on." He paused. "I was invisible by design. That was the whole point of me. A spare part, walking around in a man's shape, useful precisely because no one had to think about what I was."

He had expected the room to be hostile. It wasn't. It was attentive, which was worse, or better—he still hadn't decided.

"Ten years ago, the other Dominic Okafor found something in this ship. He found that the Meridian Fleet and the Ashfall Compact came from the same colony. One ship. One people. One language—Old Meridian, the language your grandparents were punished for speaking, the language they told you died out. It didn't die out. It was killed. And a handful of people on both sides killed it, and killed the records, and killed anyone who remembered, because a shared origin is a terrible thing when your power depends on a war."

He gestured, and the archive projection bloomed above the table—the genetic ledgers, the flight logs, the founding manifests, the same faces appearing in both fleets' honor rolls. It was the fourth time the room had seen it. It still made people look away, or lean closer. There was no neutral way to see your own history proven false.

"I'm not here to tell you how to feel about that. I've watched both fleets try to feel about it for three months and it's been like watching two people learn they're siblings at a funeral. It's messy. It's loud. Someone always breaks a chair." A ripple of laughter—startled, real. "What I'm here to tell you is what I know about one woman who refused to forget."

He told them about Amara. Not the martyr version—the real one. The woman who had hidden in a dead ship for forty years, who had kept tomatoes alive in the dark, who had sworn at him in Old Meridian when he tripped over her hydroponics line, who had made him tea so bitter it qualified as a controlled substance, who had looked at him—*him*, the copy, the spare part—with no pity at all, only recognition.

"She told me I wasn't my original," he said. "I was furious. I'd spent my whole life trying to be him—better than him, even, to finish what he started. And she said—" he had to stop, because his voice did something unauthorized, and he let it, because Amara would have mocked him gently for hiding it, "—she said, *the original was a man running from himself. You might be a man running toward something. Those are different men.*"

Silence. Then, from the Ashfall section, an elderly delegate—Admiral Okonkwo, who had lost two sons to the corridor skirmishes—rose, and said, in careful, accented, beautiful Old Meridian: *"We hear you."*

The Meridian section, a heartbeat later, answered in the same tongue. And then the whole room, haltingly, in a language half of them had learned in the last three months from smuggled recordings and Amara's decoded signal, said it together: *we hear you, we hear you, we hear you.*

Dominic stood in the middle of it and thought: *this is what home sounds like.* Not a place. An echo that comes back.

* * *

The proposal took longer, because proposals always do, and because the Ashfall delegation's chief counsel had the soul of a man who believed every sentence should be negotiated individually, including the sentences containing the word "the."

But by the fourth day, it held: the Genesis would be restored—not as Meridian territory, not as Ashfall territory, but as common ground. A memorial to the shared colony, to Amara Chen, to the eleven thousand people who had died in the corridor over sixty years of a manufactured war. Joint administration. Joint garrison, small, symbolic. Joint school.

The school had been Dominic's addition, and it was the part he'd fought for hardest, standing in front of the council with the data core in his hand like a stone he'd carried up a mountain.

"You can sign a treaty and mean it," he'd said, "and in twenty years your grandchildren will sign it away, because treaties are paper and grievance is gravity. But if a Meridian child and an Ashfall child learn the same songs in the same room—if Old Meridian is the first language either of them learns to argue in—then the war doesn't just end. It becomes *unthinkable*. That's the only security that lasts. Not walls. A classroom."

The counsel had objected that the curriculum would be a battleground. Dominic had said yes, obviously, everything is a battleground, that's what children are *for*, and the council had laughed, and the school had passed.

Now he walked through it, on the fifth day, while the delegations argued about jurisdictional language in a chamber he was gratefully absent from.

The first class had nine students. Four Meridian, four Ashfall, and one girl from Halcyon Reach whose parents were station-born and claimed no fleet at all, which the teacher—a former Ashfall signals officer named Wren, who had once been ordered to jam Amara's signal and had instead recorded it—said made her the most important student in the room.

They were learning the alphabet. On the wall, in chalk, in characters two centuries dead and three months resurrected, someone had written: *The sea was not our home. We carried it with us.* The old colony's first sentence, the one that opened every archive, the one Amara had whispered into her transmitter for forty years like a prayer with a frequency.

The girl from Halcyon Reach was sounding it out, slowly, her finger under each mark, and when she got to the end she looked up with the ferocious triumph of the newly literate and said, "Again."

Dominic stood in the doorway and did not go in. Some things you shouldn't be present for. Some things are better witnessed the way Amara had witnessed the corridor for forty years: from a distance, through a wall, trusting that the sound meant what you hoped it meant.

* * *

On the seventh day, the anthem happened by accident, which is the only way anthems should happen.

There was to be a ceremony—of course there was to be a ceremony, both fleets were constitutionally incapable of ending a week without a ceremony—and the planning committee had deadlocked on music. The Meridian anthem was, in retrospect, an embarrassment: it named the Ashfall as "the ash that chokes the starlight," which nobody had noticed for sixty years because nobody listened to the words. The Ashfall anthem was worse. It named the Meridian as "the cold that eats the harvest." The committee had discovered, in the same afternoon, that both sides had been singing insults at each other's funerals for three generations, and had adjourned in a state of collective horror.

It was Wren, the signals officer, who solved it. She came to Dominic's quarters aboard the Genesis guest ring—Vesper was docked alongside now, in a berth of honor, which still made him check the seals twice a night—and she didn't sit down.

"Play me her signal," she said. "The full recording. The forty years of it. Not the decoded parts. The whole thing."

"Why?"

"Because I've been listening to it for three months and I think it's not a distress call. I think it's a song, and she was singing it to herself, and we've been decoding a woman humming in the dark."

They played it in his quarters, the raw signal, the repeating pulse that had pulled Vesper off its route and into the rest of his life. And Wren was right. Once you heard it that way—the rhythm, the rise and fall, the way the pattern shifted slightly every few years as Amara aged, as her hands slowed, as she added harmonics to keep herself company—it wasn't a distress call at all. It was a lullaby. It was a woman in a dying ship, singing to a fleet of children who didn't know they were her children, saying *I'm here, I'm here, I'm still here.*

The committee adopted it unanimously in under an hour, a speed record for any body containing both fleets. They called it *Amara's Signal*, because no one could agree on a grander name and no one wanted one. At the ceremony, when the delegations stood, the atrium played it—amber light and white light meeting in the middle, nine children in the front row singing the decoded words in a language their grandparents had been forbidden, and Dominic watched two admirals who had spent their careers trying to kill each other stand side by side with their hands over their hearts, both of them crying, neither of them pretending not to.

He caught Reyes's eye across the room. She raised an imaginary glass. He raised one back. Neither of them had a real glass, because the ceremony's champagne had been vetoed by the joint garrison commander on grounds of "operational sobriety," which everyone understood to mean *someone always says something true when they drink, and we can't afford that yet.*

* * *

The last evening, before the delegations departed and the real work—the unglamorous, decades-long, committee-infested work of peace—began, Dominic walked the Genesis alone.

He had the run of her now. That was the strange gift of the restoration: a ship that had been his crime scene, his refuge, his near-grave, was now his responsibility. He walked the hydroponics bay and checked the tomatoes, because someone had to and because it made him feel close to her. He walked the records hall and straightened a chair. He walked to the outer observation blister, where the hull was original, two centuries old, scarred by micrometeors, and he put his hand flat against the cold of it.

Below him, in the memorial garden they were building in the old cargo ring, Amara's grave lay under a slab of the same hull plating, with her name in three scripts: Old Meridian, Meridian Standard, and Compact Common. The first person in either fleet's history to be honored in all three. The stonecutter had cried while carving it, and had apologized, and Dominic had said don't, that's the correct amount of crying.

"I kept them alive," he said to the ship, to the grave, to the dark beyond the glass. "The tomatoes. And the school. And the signal. You'd find something to criticize. You'd say my pruning is barbaric. You'd be right."

The ship hummed around him—new systems in old bones, the sound of a body remembering what health felt like.

He thought about the note in his own handwriting. *If you're reading this, don't go to Genesis.* The original had been wrong, in the end, the way frightened men are wrong: the answer had been at Genesis all along. But Dominic understood it now, better than he had in the comet drift, better than he had in the crossfire. The original hadn't been warning him away from a place. He'd been warning him away from becoming him—another man who found the truth and hesitated, who weighed his ambition against his conscience and let the scales sit too long. *Don't go to Genesis* had really meant *don't be the man who knows and waits.*

Dominic had gone. And he hadn't waited. Whatever else was true of him—clone, copy, spare part, ghost—that much was his. Not inherited. *Chosen.*

His comm chimed. Reyes.

"You're going to want to see this," she said, and her voice had the particular flat calm she reserved for things that were either wonderful or catastrophic, and after everything, he still couldn't always tell which.

"Good news or bad news?"

"Both. The provisional councils just ratified the corridor mail contract. Civilian traffic, both fleets, starting next month." A pause. "They want a carrier with corridor experience and a captain both sides will actually let through the lines. There was exactly one name on the list."

Dominic looked out at the dark, at the distant running lights of the two fleets holding station around the old ship like a held breath finally being let out.

"And the bad news?"

"The bad news," Reyes said, and he could hear her grin through the static, "is that the first contract run is a diplomatic priority shipment to a station out past the Ashfall deep pickets, and the manifest is sealed, and the last time a sealed manifest crossed my desk, we ended up here."

He laughed, tired and real, and looked at the berth where Vesper waited, fuelled, loaded, and—for the first time in her existence—flying under a captain whose name was his own.

"When do we leave?"

"Dawn," said Reyes. "Old Meridian for it, if you've been studying. There's a word I keep seeing in the children's primers. It means *the going-toward*, not the going-from. The founders used it for the launch."

Dominic closed his eyes and tried to remember it. He almost had it. He'd have it by dawn.

"Then we'd better be worth the word," he said, and turned off the light, and went home to his ship.
