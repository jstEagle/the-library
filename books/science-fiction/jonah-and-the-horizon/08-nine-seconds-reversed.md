## 8. Nine Seconds, Reversed

The relay chamber smelled of dust and ozone and the particular cold of machines that had been dreaming alone for thirty years. Juniper stood beneath the great antenna's root—the trunk of it, thick as a cathedral pillar, running up through the shattered dome into a sky the color of old pewter—and felt the loop ticking through the floor like a pulse in a wrist.

*Four days. Maybe less now.*

Ovid Rell stood on the far side of the console, his coat immaculate even here, at the end of the world. Behind him, the Earth relay's main screen cycled its endless nine-second stutter: the timestamp, the timestamp, the timestamp, a wound in time that the whole system had been pressing its thumb against for weeks.

"You could end the waiting," he said. "Inner Belt first. They built the vaults. They built *her*—" he tapped the console, the relay, the whole dying apparatus of Earth's last voice, "—and they have been asleep for thirty years while the Outer Reach squatted in the dark and called it freedom. Wake them. Let them sort the rest."

"And the Reach sleeps forever."

"The Reach chose the dark. You'd just be honoring the choice."

Juniper laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "You rehearsed that. You've been rehearsing it the whole way out here, haven't you? On the long burn, in the quiet, telling yourself you were the reasonable one."

Ovid's jaw tightened. He was, she had learned, a man who could not bear to be seen rehearsing.

But she wasn't looking at him anymore. She was looking at the loop.

Nine seconds. It had bothered her since Ceres Hollow—the *neatness* of it, the way every screen in the port had shown the same nine-second timestamp, the way gravity had failed not randomly but *precisely*, like a hand letting go and catching. A countdown signal would count *down*. This didn't. It repeated. It repeated the way a doorbell repeats when the person outside isn't sure you heard them the first time.

Or the way a handshake repeats when neither party has answered yet.

"Oh," Juniper said, very quietly. "Oh, you beautiful, stupid machine."

Ovid turned. "What?"

"It's not a countdown. It's a handshake." She was moving now, hands flying across the console, pulling up the loop's architecture, the deep structure she'd spent a lifetime learning to read in other people's memories and had never once thought to read in her own. "Earth's relay wasn't announcing anything. It was *knocking*. Nine seconds of gravity-drop—that's the authentication pulse. The system-wide flicker. It sent the knock through every gravity generator in the Meridian at once, because the vaults are keyed to the gravity lattice, and it's been waiting—" her voice caught, "—it's been waiting thirty years for something to knock *back*."

"The key," Ovid said slowly. "The key is the answer."

"The key is the *knock back*. And it has to come from the lattice itself, broadcast to both vaults at once, because the handshake protocol doesn't take sides. It was built before the Sundering. It doesn't know there *are* two halves." She looked up at him, and she saw the understanding land in his face like a falling ship. "You can't wake one side alone, Ovid. Not with this. Not with *any* of it. The protocol was never broken. We broke everything *around* it."

For a long moment the only sound was the loop, ticking, ticking, patient as snowfall.

Then the floor shuddered.

Not the loop. Something docking—hard, graceless, military. Ovid's head came up like a hound's. "They followed me. I led them here thinking I'd deliver you." He wasn't even pretending anymore; there was a strange relief in it, a man setting down a weight he'd carried so long it had grown into his spine. "Juniper. Whatever you're going to do. Do it now."

She was already doing it.

The interface cradle was old Earth tech, a memory-diver's rig bolted beside the console by some engineer who'd died before Juniper was born. She'd used cradles a thousand times, but always to carry someone else's past, always as a courier, never as cargo. She settled into it and felt the clamps take her temples like cold hands.

"Juniper." Ovid stood over her. Enforcers were hammering at the outer airlock now; she could feel it through the floor, a heartbeat faster than the loop's. "The fragment in the loop plus the fragment in your head. If you merge them in the lattice—"

"Then everyone gets it at once. Both vaults. Simultaneous." She met his eyes. "You could still stop me. You've got a gun, I assume. You people always do."

He looked at her for a long three seconds—she counted them, the way she'd learned to count everything in nines—and then he turned and walked toward the airlock, drawing the gun, pointing it *inward*. At the door the enforcers would come through.

"I was Inner Belt authority for twenty years," he said. "I'd like, just once, to be something else."

* * *

The cradle took her down into herself like a diver into black water.

Her memories rose to meet her, and this time she didn't fight them. The snowfall first—always the snowfall—ash-grey and warm-smelling, drifting past the relay antenna while a woman's hands moved over a console much like this one. Her mother's hands. She could see them now, could see the fine burn scar across the left wrist, and the grief that hit her was so total it had no edges, no shape, just weight.

*They took this from me. They took her from me. They took the whole day and left me a snowfall with no sky to hang it in.*

But grief was a luxury with a countdown attached. Juniper gathered the day of the Sundering around her like an armful of stolen goods—the key fragment, half of a sequence that looked like a lullaby's skeleton, like the bones of the song she'd been smuggling memories inside for years without ever knowing why the melody felt familiar—and she turned toward the surface, toward the lattice, toward the knock.

And her own past reached up and held her.

It wasn't malice. That was the terrible thing. It was just *everything at once*—thirty years of restored self flooding in through the cradle, every wiped hour arriving in a single tide. She was a child at the relay. She was a girl on a smuggler's first run, terrified, invisible, *practicing* invisibility like scales on a piano. She was every memory she'd ever carried for other people, all of them still in her, filed in the deep places, the lullabies and the last suppers and the weddings and the funerals of a thousand strangers who had paid her to remember what they couldn't afford to feel. She was drowning in the one thing she'd spent her whole life pretending not to have: a past.

*Let go,* something in her said. *Let go of the surface. Let it have you. It's easier.*

And it was. That was the horror of it. To stop being the courier and become the cargo. To sink into the snowfall and stay there, warm, complete, a whole person at last—

The loop ticked. Nine seconds. The knock, still waiting, patient as ever.

*No,* Juniper thought. *You don't get to keep me. None of you get to keep me—not the Authority, not the Reach, not my own goddamned childhood. I was never cargo. I was the courier.*

She took the key fragment in both hands, the way she'd taken every shipment she'd ever run, and she *threw* it.

* * *

The lattice answered.

She felt it happen from inside the gravity itself—a shudder running through the Meridian's bones, every generator in the system catching the knock and returning it, the handshake completing in a cascade of light no one would see and everyone would feel. The key, whole, split deliberately down its middle, flying outward along the old trade routes she knew like her own heartbeat: one half inward, to the Inner Belt's sleep-vaults; one half outward, to the Reach. Both at once. Neither first. The protocol didn't know how to choose, and so, at last, neither would anyone.

The airlock blew behind her. She heard Ovid's gun—three shots, precise, buying seconds—and then boots, shouting, the crash of the console. The cradle was ripped away from her temples and the tide of her own past came down on her like the dome coming in, and Juniper Brightwater, the invisible woman, the courier of everyone's hearts, felt herself coming apart into the snowfall—

—and held, at the very last, to one thought, small and stubborn as a smuggler's oath:

*Tamsin will come. Tamsin always comes.*

Then the nine seconds took her, and the world went white, and somewhere very far away a door that had been locked for thirty years began, at last, to open.
