## 9. Waking Is a Choice We Make Together

The silence after the broadcast was not silence at all.

Juniper had expected the relay to go dark when the loop ended—had braced for it, the way you brace for a door closing. Instead, the old antenna kept singing, but the song had changed. The nine-second stutter repeated now, over and over, no longer a countdown but a heartbeat, and threaded through it she could hear the completed key cycling outward in all directions at once, toward the Inner Belt's vaults, toward the Outer Reach's frozen cities, toward every sleeping half of a broken species.

She heard it from the floor.

That was the first thing she understood about herself: she was on the floor of the relay station, on her back, staring up at a ceiling of corroded panels and dead lights, and she could not remember how she had come to be lying there. She could not remember her name. Not immediately—not the way you reach for a cup or a familiar handle. It came back slowly, like water finding its level: *Juniper*. *Brightwater*. Smuggler. Carrier of other people's pasts.

And then, worse than the missing name, came the gaps.

They were everywhere. She reached for the snowfall and found only the shape of it, an impression like a footprint left in melting ice. She reached for Tamsin's face and got the laugh without the features, the warmth without the person. She reached for the lullaby—the first thing she'd ever smuggled, the thing that had started everything—and the melody dissolved halfway through, trailing off into static where the rest should have been.

*Price still being counted,* she thought, and did not know if the phrase was hers or something she'd read once, somewhere, in a life that might have belonged to someone else.

Above her, the relay hummed its new heartbeat. Nine seconds. Nine seconds. Nine seconds.

She laughed—a dry, cracked sound with nothing behind it—and let her eyes close.

* * *

Tamsin found her eleven hours later.

They came aboard the relay in a salvage skiff borrowed from no one in particular—Tamsin had never been particular about ownership, and after losing their ship to the enforcers' blockade they had lost the last thing that made them careful. They floated down the relay's central spine with a hand-torch and a medical kit strapped to their thigh, calling her name into the dark in a voice gone hoarse from days of shouting into comm channels nobody answered.

"Juniper. Juniper Brightwater. If you're dead I'm going to be so unbelievably angry with you."

They found her in the transmission chamber, curled beneath the main console like a child hiding under a bed, and for one terrible moment Tamsin thought they were too late. Then she stirred, and her eyes opened, and they were her eyes—tired, wry, still faintly amused by the absurdity of everything—but looking at Tamsin the way you look at a stranger whose name you're trying to place.

"Tamsin," she said at last, testing it. "Your ship burned at Ceres Hollow."

"My ship burned at Ceres Hollow," Tamsin agreed, and their voice broke on the second word, and they didn't try to hide it. "You remember that?"

"I remember that it mattered." She tried to sit up and failed, and Tamsin caught her, and held her, and felt how light she had become—as if parts of her had been paid out as ballast somewhere along the long route. "I remember that I'm sorry. I don't remember your face."

"You will," Tamsin said, because saying anything else was unthinkable. "It's a very memorable face. People have written songs about it."

"They haven't."

"One song. It's terrible. You'll hate it. That's how you'll know it's sincere."

She laughed, and then stopped laughing, because laughing cost something she didn't have much of, and Tamsin ran the scanner over her while she drifted in and out, and the readings made them sit very still for a long time.

The neural map looked like a coastline after a storm. Whole provinces of her memory simply gone—not damaged, not suppressed, *gone*, the tissue repurposed, overwritten, spent. The key hadn't been copied out of her. It had been *built* out of her, brick by brick, and what remained was the foundation showing through.

"She gave herself away," said a voice at the chamber's edge. "The whole key. Both halves."

Ovid Rell stood in the airlock frame, gaunt, unshaven, his authority uniform scorched along one shoulder. Behind him, filling the corridor beyond, stood a dozen figures in mismatched armor and patched thermal gear—Outer Reach separatists, their weapons lowered but present, the way weather is present.

"You followed us," Tamsin said flatly.

"I followed the signal. Everyone followed the signal." Ovid's eyes never left Juniper. "Both vaults received it. Do you understand what that means? Both vaults are asking for confirmation codes. Neither side can wake alone. The protocol won't accept it." He laughed, and it was not a kind sound. "She didn't just send the key. She sent terms."

* * *

The argument began within the hour and did not stop for two days.

It happened in fragments, in the spaces between Juniper's waking hours, conducted across open channels because neither side trusted a closed one anymore. The Inner Belt authority demanded custody of the relay, of the key's origin point, of the woman who carried it. The Outer Reach medics who had arrived with the separatists—who had come, in truth, because the Outer Reach vaults had begun transmitting readiness signals for the first time in sixty years—refused to surrender her.

"She's Outer Reach born," said Dr. Ilsa Vey, the senior medic, a broad-shouldered woman with frost-scars on her knuckles and a voice like a hull breach. "Her bone density markers, her atmospheric adaptation profile. She's ours."

"She's a citizen of the Meridian Authority," Ovid countered, "and she committed crimes against—"

"She *saved* both of you, you absolute—" Tamsin, from the corner, arms crossed, done with diplomacy forever. "She built the key out of her own head. She has maybe sixty percent of a memory left. And you're standing here arguing about *paperwork*."

Juniper listened to all of it from the med-bay cot they'd rigged inside the relay, drifting, exhausted, strangely calm. Once, when the arguing reached a particular pitch, she raised a hand, and the room fell silent, because everyone in it understood exactly what she had paid and none of them wanted to look at the bill.

"Neither," she said. Her voice was thin but steady. "I'm not yours. Either of you. I'm the crossing between. That's the whole point. That's what I *made* the point be." She looked at Vey, then at Ovid. "The vaults wake together or not at all. That's my term. It stays my term. And I stay on the route between you, until you learn to talk to each other without me holding the wire."

Vey's jaw worked. "You need treatment. Real treatment. Our facilities—"

"Then bring your facilities to the route, Doctor. Or send them. I'll be moving."

Ovid stared at her for a long moment, and something in his face shifted—not softening exactly, but a recalculation, the look of a man realizing the cargo he'd been chasing had become the courier. "The Authority will never agree to joint custody."

"The Authority doesn't have to agree," Juniper said. "The protocol does. Ask your vaults who holds the confirmation codes." She almost smiled. "You do. Both of you. Equally. I checked before I sent it. I made sure."

* * *

On the third day, the first response came back from the sleep-vaults.

It came to every screen in the system simultaneously, the way the nine seconds had, and this time no one flinched. Two lines of text, transmitted in tandem from opposite ends of the Meridian System, synchronized to the millisecond:

**INNER BELT VAULT NETWORK: AWAKENING SEQUENCE PENDING — AWAITING COUNTERPART CONFIRMATION.**

**OUTER REACH VAULT NETWORK: AWAKENING SEQUENCE PENDING — AWAITING COUNTERPART CONFIRMATION.**

Two halves of humanity, frozen for sixty years, each waiting politely at the door for the other to knock first.

In the relay's med-bay, Tamsin read the message aloud twice, and then sat down heavily on the floor and cried, which they would later deny with great conviction. Vey watched the screens with her frost-scarred hands pressed over her mouth. Even Ovid said nothing, which from Ovid was a kind of prayer.

Juniper watched from her cot, propped on one elbow, and felt something loosen in her chest that she suspected was hope, arriving late and battered but arriving.

"Do you think they'll do it?" Tamsin asked quietly. "Talk? Actually talk?"

"I think they'll have to," Juniper said. "I took away every other option." She paused. The static where the lullaby used to be hummed faintly at the back of her mind, and she found, examining it, that she didn't mind the gap as much as she'd feared. Some things, maybe, weren't hers to keep. But this—this she intended to see.

Below them, Earth turned, dark and patient, its last transmission finally looping to silence. Above them, two vaults waited on each other's word.

And in the deep channels between, already, the first messages were moving—clumsy, formal, mistrustful, alive. A negotiation had begun. Someone on the Inner Belt side had opened with a formal apology. Someone on the Outer Reach side had answered with a list of grievances six hundred entries long.

"It's going to take years," Vey murmured.

"Good," said Juniper Brightwater, and closed her eyes, and slept without dreaming—or dreaming, perhaps, of snowfall, in a place where snowfall belonged to everyone.
