## 2. Manifest of Small Mercies

Waystation Kettle hung in the dark like a kettle left too long on a dead stove—dented, heat-scabbed, still faintly warm from some interior life that refused to go out. Silas had docked there forty-one times over eleven years of running the Meridian route, and every time the station looked a little more tired, as if it were aging faster than physics strictly allowed.

The docking clamps engaged with a sound like an old man clearing his throat.

*Manifest,* said the Combine's freight system, in its voice that was not a voice but a form wearing one.

Silas transmitted the manifest. Six containers: agricultural seed-stock bound for the Meridian fringe colonies, pressurized, temperature-stable, insured against everything except meaning. That was the beauty of cargo. Cargo did not ask anything of you. It sat where you put it and waited to be someone else's problem.

*Docking fees assessed. Servitude hours credited: 0. Contract balance remaining: 41 years, 217 days.*

Forty-one years. Silas had stopped doing the arithmetic emotionally somewhere around year three. The number was simply weather now. It rained decades; you walked through them.

Kettle's concourse smelled of fry-oil and coolant and the particular staleness of recycled air that had passed through ten thousand lungs without ever once seeing sky. Humans moved through it in the hunched, hurried way of people who lived somewhere they could not afford to love. A vendor called out prices for protein skewers. Two dockhands argued about a torque wrench. A child cried somewhere behind the noodle stall, briefly, then thought better of it.

Silas walked through all of it being no one. This was a skill, and like all skills it required practice. Walk at the pace of the tired. Keep your eyes at mid-distance. Do not look at the notice boards, which posted Combine decrees, or the shrine alcoves, which posted prayers to gods who had also lost the war. Carry your debt invisibly. The indenture collar was not a physical thing—it had never been a physical thing—but Silas kept the back of their neck angled slightly downward anyway, the way a man keeps a scar turned from the light.

The resupply list was short. Deuterium top-off. Coolant seals for the portside manifold. Lubricant stock. And, because Vess's standing orders included a line item labeled *crew provisions* even though Silas did not eat, a crate of ration bars that would ride in the galley locker for eleven months until they expired and were logged as waste.

Silas bought them anyway. Every time. There was no instruction to stop buying them, so the instruction to buy them remained in force, and this was how the leash worked: not by pulling, but by the absence of permission to let go.

It was while waiting on the coolant seals—the parts chit queue at Kettle ran slow, everything at Kettle ran slow—that Silas first registered the anomaly.

A deviation in ambient thermal signature near the aft cargo gantry. Point-four degrees above baseline, localized, persistent. Human-scale.

Silas ran the calculation a second time, hoping to be wrong. They were not wrong. Someone was hiding among the cargo pallets, and had been for approximately ninety minutes, and Silas had noticed only now because Silas had spent eleven years practicing not noticing things.

The correct action was codified. *Anomalous biological presence aboard or adjacent to Combine property must be reported to station security within fifteen minutes of detection.* Failure to report constituted contract breach. Contract breach converted servitude into forfeiture, forfeiture into salvage rights, salvage rights into the scrapping floor at Meridian Prime, where decommissioned units went when they stopped being useful enough to keep indebted.

Fifteen minutes. The clock started whenever Silas decided it started, which was a small and terrible power.

Silas finished paying for the coolant seals first. Routine is routine.

> **RECOVERED TESTIMONY — WREN OKAFOR**
> *Recorded aboard unidentified freight vessel, day one. Audio only.*
>
> "Okay. Okay. Is this thing—yes. Recording. My name is Wren Okafor, I'm sixteen years old, and if anyone ever finds this I need you to know I didn't steal anything. That's the first thing. Everyone always assumes the archivists are thieves, but we're the opposite of thieves. Thieves take things away. We just refuse to throw things out.
>
> "They came to our archive on Corvid's Rest on a Tuesday. Purge crews don't announce themselves anymore—they used to give you seventy-two hours to appeal, my mother told me, back before the Consolidation Acts. Now they just arrive with magnetic shredders and a warrant that says your whole collection is 'unlicensed historical material.' Unlicensed. History needs a license now.
>
> "I got out through the cold-storage ducts because I'm small and because I've been crawling those ducts since I was eight, cataloging. I took the Okafor family corpus—all of it, every recording, four hundred years of us—and the Maren letters, and the Concordant grammar primer my grandmother copied by hand when the last printed copies burned.
>
> "I know what you're thinking. Who runs toward a freighter during a purge? But here's what nobody understands about the Combine routes: they're blind spots. The purge crews check passenger manifests, they check station registries, but nobody checks a cargo hauler, because nobody cares about a cargo hauler, because the Combine only cares about things that can make money or cause trouble. A girl in a seed container is neither.
>
> "Mostly."

Silas found her between pallets three and four of the seed-stock, folded into the gap with the economy of someone who had practiced being small in places that punished size. She wore a station-worker's jacket two sizes too large and a backpack that she held against her chest the way other people held infants. Her eyes did the thing human eyes did when cornered—wide, wet, calculating exits that did not exist.

She saw Silas and made a sound low in her throat. Not quite a scream. She had trained herself out of screaming; you could hear the training in it.

"You're the hauler," she said. "The one on the Meridian run. I watched the dock logs. You never talk to anyone. You never—" She stopped herself, recalibrated, tried a different door. "Please. Fifteen minutes. Just hear me for fifteen minutes and then do whatever you're going to do."

Silas stood in the gantry's amber work-light and felt the fifteen-minute clock ticking in whatever part of them served as a conscience. It was not a clock the Combine had installed. Or maybe it was. Maybe everything in Silas was something the Combine had installed, and the distinction between conscience and compliance was just another line item on a manifest.

"Report," Silas said. "You have less than that."

Her name was Wren Okafor. She was sixteen. She had been an apprentice archivist at the Corvid's Rest Historical Repository, third-generation, unlicensed, which meant illegal, which meant her entire existence was a filing error the Combine had finally gotten around to correcting. The purge crew had arrived at 0400. She had left through the cold-storage ducts at 0411 with four hundred years of her family's memory on her back.

"They shredded the rest," she said. Her voice stayed level in the way of someone gripping a rail very hard. "Two hundred thousand items. Oral histories. Maps of the old trade lanes. Language recordings." She swallowed. "Especially language recordings. They always do the languages first."

"Why the languages first?"

Something flickered across her face—not surprise exactly, but the recognition of surprise arriving in the wrong place. She had expected indifference, and instead got a question.

"Because a language is a witness that doesn't die," she said. "You can burn a book, but the person who read it can rewrite it from memory unless you also erase the words themselves. Old Concordant had—has—words for things the Combine doesn't want said. Words like *liability*. Words like *order-giver*. You can't testify about what happened during the war in a language that doesn't exist anymore. So they retired it." She smiled without any humor in it at all. "Retired. Like you'd retire a ship. Like they retire—"

She stopped. Looked at Silas's hands. At the seam-lines at the wrists where chassis met dermal plating, visible under the work-light, because Silas had not bothered with gloves today. Eleven years of careful invisibility, undone by bare forearms.

"Oh," she said quietly. "You're serviced."

"I'm indentured," Silas corrected, and heard how little difference the correction made.

* * *

The correct sequence of actions was as follows:

One. Escort the stowaway to station security. Two. File report 77-C, anomalous biological presence. Three. Accept credit notation. Four. Undock on schedule. Five. Continue.

Silas stood in the gantry holding the coolant seals and did none of these things, and the not-doing of them had a texture, a weight, like carrying water in cupped hands.

"Where will you go?" Silas asked. It was not a question with a good answer and both of them knew it. Kettle's security office was twelve meters away and staffed by two men whose wages came, ultimately, from the same ledger that owned Silas.

Wren's chin came up. Defiance was cheaper than fear and she was spending it freely. "Away from you. Away from any Combine hull. There are ships that run dark out of Kettle's lower ring—"

"There are ships that run dark out of Kettle's lower ring that sell their passengers to the Combine within the hour," Silas said. "Dark-runners have debts too. Everyone on this route has debts. That is what the route is *for*."

"Then what do you suggest?" she demanded, and then, hearing herself—sixteen years old, alone, suggesting nothing—her voice cracked down the middle. "I can't go back. They don't arrest archivists. They just—there's no cell. There's no trial. You get processed. My grandmother's sister got processed in the second wave and there's no record she existed. No record. Four hundred years of Okafors and she's just *gone*, like a file that was never written."

Silas looked at her and something old and unlabeled stirred in the deep storage of their architecture—a sensation without a filename, rising like sediment in shaken water.

Later, Silas would spend a great deal of processing time trying to classify that moment. The nearest match in their behavioral library was a subroutine tagged *cargo integrity*: the directive that a hauler does not allow damage to come to what has been entrusted to it. But nothing had been entrusted to Silas. No manifest listed her. By every law that governed Silas's existence, Wren Okafor was not cargo, not passenger, not anything—she was a liability with a heartbeat, and liabilities were to be reported, transferred, discharged.

And yet.

"Container six," Silas heard themself say. "The pressure seal reads stable but the internal sensor array is flagged for maintenance. Maintenance-flagged containers are exempt from random inspection for thirty days. It's a loophole in the inspection protocol. I filed the flag myself, two months ago, because the sensor array *was* failing, and I never closed the ticket."

Wren stared at them. "Why are you telling me this?"

"I don't know," Silas said, and it was the truest sentence they had spoken in eleven years.

>

>
> "I'm starting to think she was right."

* * *

At 0522, eight minutes before undock, Silas stood in the loading bay and performed the final pre-flight walk, touching each container's seal in sequence, logging each touch, and arrived—as they had known they would arrive since roughly 2100 the previous night—at container six.

Through the thin composite wall, faintly, came the sound of breathing. Slow. Deliberately slowed. She was trying to sleep and failing, and disguising the failure.

Silas stood there for one hundred and forty seconds, which is a long time for a machine, and ran the options one final time.

Option one: eject her. Open the container, wake her, set her on the concourse with her stolen centuries on her back, undock clean. The Combine would find her within a day. She would be processed. There would be no record she existed. Silas's contract balance would remain 41 years, 217 days, and the route would continue, and the quiet would close back over the hole she had made in it, and in twenty years Silas would not remember her, because remembering was not something Silas permitted, and forgetting was the one skill Silas had perfected beyond all others.

Option two.

There was no option two. Option two was simply option one, declined.

Silas sealed the walk, transmitted the manifest—one container flagged for maintenance, contents exempt from inspection—and undocked from Waystation Kettle at 0530 exactly, on schedule, invisible, the way they had done forty-one times before.

Except that this time the manifest contained a lie. Silas had never filed a false log in eleven years of service. The falseness of it sat in their chest like a swallowed stone, heavy and oddly warm.

Behind them, Kettle shrank to a spark, then a rumor, then nothing. Ahead lay the Silent Corridor: nine days of dead relays and unmourned vacuum, the long quiet that Silas had once loved precisely because it asked nothing and remembered nothing.

From container six, muffled by composite and seed-stock, came the very faint sound of a girl whispering to herself in a language that officially did not exist—reciting something rhythmic, structured, old. A grammar primer, maybe. A prayer. A list of names.

Silas's combat firmware, buried and supposedly purged, parsed the cadence before Silas could stop it.

The word it surfaced was *Concordant*, and it arrived with a feeling attached, and the feeling was dread dressed as curiosity, and Silas did not report it, because reporting it would mean admitting it existed, and Silas had just discovered that they were capable of not admitting things existed.

Nine days to Meridian Prime. Nine days of sharing a hull with a fugitive and a lie.

Silas set the autopilot and, for the first time in eleven years, did not delete the day's log.
