## 3. Children of the Quiet Planet

Iyo had counted the dreams eleven times now, and they were always the same down to the smallest detail, which was the part that convinced her. Dreams were supposed to be sloppy things, full of doors that led into other doors and grandmothers who turned out to be fish. This one was tidy. This one was *rehearsed*.

She sat on the low wall outside the schoolroom, which was also the granary office, which was also the place where Ama kept the colony's dwindling stock of actual paper, because Meridian had run out of reasons to waste power on screens it didn't need. The morning was grey in the way Meridian mornings were always grey — not stormy, just tired, like the sky had been asked to do too much for too long.

Ama came out carrying two cups of chicory substitute, which everyone called coffee because lying to yourself was a survival skill on a dying colony.

"You've got your counting face on," Ama said, handing her a cup.

"I don't have a counting face."

"You absolutely do. Your mouth goes flat and your eyes go somewhere else. It's the same face your mother used to make when she was doing supply math." Ama sat down beside her, joints cracking in the cold. "So. Eleven?"

Iyo nearly dropped her cup. "How did you—"

"Kess told me at breakfast. Kess tells me everything. Kess has no concept of secrets." Ama blew on her drink. "Eleven nights, the same dream."

"Twelve children," Iyo said. "All of us. Same woman, same field, same everything. That's not possible, Ama. Dreams aren't supposed to work like that."

"No," Ama agreed. "They aren't."

The schoolroom behind them was quiet, because half the children had stopped coming. Not out of laziness — out of a kind of embarrassed grief. When a settlement runs out of fuel, then medicine, then certain foods, and finally starts talking openly about the evacuation that will never be funded, children stop raising their hands in class. What was the point of learning long division for a future with no numbers in it?

But last week, something had changed. The empty seats had started filling again. Because of the dream.

"Ama." Iyo turned to face her teacher properly. "Describe what you see when you close your eyes. Just try."

"Iyo—"

"Humor me. You always say imagination is a muscle."

"That was before I knew you'd use it against me." But Ama closed her eyes, and Iyo watched her face go through several small weather systems. "Grey sky. A field. Ash, or maybe pale grass — it's hard to tell. And a woman standing in the middle of it, facing us. She's not moving. Her mouth is—" Ama's eyes opened. "Her mouth is moving. Like she's saying something important, and we can't hear it."

"Brown skin," Iyo said. "Short hair, black, with grey starting at the temples. A scar over her left eyebrow, thin, like a paper cut that never quite healed. Fleet-issue coat, dark blue, but old style, with the collar worn soft."

Ama stared at her. "You know her."

"We all know her. That's the whole point. That's why we're scared." Iyo hugged her cup against her chest. "Except it doesn't feel like scared. It feels like waiting."

* * *

The dream had started with Kess, who was seven and therefore constitutionally incapable of keeping anything to himself. He'd described it at breakfast with his mouth full: *a lady standing in a burnt field, and she looked right at me, and she wasn't scary, she was sad.* Their mother had told him it was just his brain processing the abandonment broadcasts, and Kess had said, *then why did Dara dream it too?* and their mother had said, *coincidence,* and Kess had said, *then why did Tam dream it?* and their mother had run out of coincidences around child number four.

By the ninth night, every child under fourteen in New Halcyon Settlement had stood in that ash field. By the eleventh, they'd started comparing notes the way other children compared trading cards.

The details never wavered. The scar. The coat. The way the woman held her hands — loose at her sides, palms slightly forward, like someone who wanted to reach out but was waiting for permission. And the mouth, always moving, shaping words that arrived as pressure instead of sound, like a voice heard through a hull.

"She's saying *wait,*" Tam insisted. Tam was nine and had strong opinions about everything, including metaphysics.

"She's saying *coming,*" said Dara.

"She's saying our names," Iyo said quietly, and the others went still, because that was the detail none of them had admitted out loud yet. In the dream, when the woman's mouth moved, each of them somehow knew — the way you know things in dreams — that the words were meant for them alone.

The adults had theories. Dr. Sefa thought it was a shared environmental toxin affecting developing brains; he'd tested the water twice and found nothing, but testing the water gave him something to do, so nobody stopped him. Old Wren said it was mass hysteria, the psychological equivalent of yawning. Iyo's mother said it was grief wearing a costume, and she wasn't wrong about grief — she'd been wearing her own since the last fleet ship had passed overhead without stopping, three years ago.

But the adults' theories all had a hole in them, and the hole was this: the adults didn't dream her. Only the children. Only the ones who'd been born on Meridian or brought here young enough that Meridian's red-grey dust was in their earliest memories.

"It's like she's looking for something," Dara said once, "and only we have it."

"What could we possibly have?" Tam demanded. "We don't have anything. We don't even have real coffee."

"We have her face," Iyo said. "Whoever she is, she looks like somebody. Somebody specific. Somebody who exists somewhere, or existed." She'd thought about this a lot. "Dreams can't invent faces from nothing. Ama told me that. Every face you dream is a face you've seen, or a mix of faces you've seen. So either all twelve of us have seen the same woman—"

"—or she's seen us," Kess finished, delighted and horrified in equal measure.

* * *

That afternoon, Ama tried to teach fractions to nine students and got as far as thirds before Tam raised his hand and asked, not rudely, just with the terrible directness of children, whether learning fractions mattered if the ships never came back.

The room went very quiet. Outside, the wind moved through the dead hydroponic frames, making a sound like slow applause.

"Everything matters," Ama said. "Fractions matter. Knowing how to divide what little you have fairly — that might be the most important thing anyone ever teaches you."

"But the ships," Tam said.

"The ships will come when they come."

"They're not coming, though." Tam wasn't being cruel. He was being accurate, which was worse. "My dad says the Concord wrote us off. My mom says the Verity did too. Everybody says it, they just say it quietly, like if it's quiet enough it isn't true."

Iyo watched Ama take that hit. Teachers on Meridian had developed a particular endurance — the ability to absorb the truth from children and keep standing. But something flickered across her face that wasn't just endurance. It was recognition. As if Tam had said something Ama had been thinking herself, in the dark, for years.

"Tell me about the woman again," Ama said. "In the dream. What does she do besides stand there?"

It was the first time an adult had asked with genuine curiosity instead of clinical concern. The children exchanged glances — the quick, silent parliament of kids deciding how much truth to release.

"She waits," Iyo said. "That's the thing. She doesn't beckon or call or anything dramatic. She just stands there like she's promised to be there, and she's keeping the promise. Even though the field is burnt. Even though there's nothing left. She keeps showing up."

"And it doesn't feel like a warning?" Ama asked carefully. "Nothing about it feels wrong?"

Iyo considered. Fear would have made sense. Grief would have made sense. But the feeling the dream left behind, every morning, was stranger than either.

"It feels like being remembered," she said. "Like somebody far away is holding us in their mind on purpose. Not praying for us. *Remembering* us. There's a difference."

Ama looked at her for a long moment. Then she laughed — a short, startled sound, quickly smothered, but real.

"What?"

"Nothing. It's just — my grandmother used to say that. About people worth trusting. *Not the ones who pray for you, child. The ones who remember you.*" She shook her head. "I haven't thought about that in twenty years, and you just handed it back to me out of a dream."

* * *

That evening, Iyo walked home past the reactor housing, its hum gone slightly sour in the last month, a note off-key that only the children seemed to notice. Adults had stopped hearing it, the way you stop hearing any sound that's been there forever. But Iyo had begun to suspect that the children of Meridian heard everything now — every failing system, every silent sky, every promise that had expired unwitnessed.

Kess caught up to her at the corner where the road split, breath steaming.

"Iyo. Iyo. Did you have it again?"

"Twelfth time. Yes."

"Me too." He fell into step beside her, scuffing the ash-grey dirt. "I figured something out. In the dream tonight, I tried to talk to her. I never tried before, I just watched. But tonight I walked right up and I said, *who are you?*"

Iyo stopped walking. "What happened?"

"Nothing. She kept mouthing whatever she mouths. But—" Kess's face did something complicated, seven years old trying to hold a big idea. "Right before I woke up, I swear her mouth changed. Like the words changed. Like she heard me asking and answered a different question."

"What did it look like?"

"Like—" He squinted, hunting for it. "Like a name. Two words. First word short. Second word longer." He shrugged. "Then I woke up and Mom was burning the porridge again, so."

They walked on. Above them, Meridian's permanent cloud cover shifted just enough to show a single star, hard and white, before sealing over again.

Iyo didn't tell Kess that she'd had the same experience. That last night, for the first time, she'd read the woman's lips — really read them, syllable by syllable, the way you read a friend across a noisy room. That the name had two parts, and the first part was short.

*Mara.*

And the second word, the longer one, she hadn't been able to catch — except that it had felt less like a name and more like a title. Something like *Montrose*. Something that sounded, impossibly, like a word from fleet registration files, from the old broadcasts, from the world of ships and authority that had forgotten them.

A woman with a name and a rank, standing in a burnt field, mouthing promises to forty-one children on a planet everyone had abandoned.

At home, her mother was doing supply math by lamplight, mouth flat, eyes elsewhere. Iyo kissed her cheek and went to her bunk and lay awake, listening to the reactor's off-key hum.

Tomorrow, she decided, she would make Ama write it all down. Everything. Every detail, every drawing, every name the dream-woman might be mouthing. Ama kept the colony's records; Ama had a transmitter key, old and weak, but a transmitter nonetheless. The adults would say it was pointless. The adults said a lot of things were pointless lately.

But somewhere out past the grey ceiling of the sky, a woman with a scar over her eyebrow was remembering them on purpose.

Somebody ought to answer.

* * *

Two systems away, in a briefing room aboard station Corvus, a clone opened her eyes for the first time — but Iyo wouldn't learn that for weeks. All she knew, lying in the dark of a dying world, was that the dream had changed tonight.

For the first time, the woman in the ash field had not been alone.

There had been a second figure beside her, blurred, unfinished, like a photograph still developing. And the woman — Mara, Montrose, whoever she was — had turned to look at that second figure with an expression Iyo recognized from her own mother's face:

Hope, terrified of itself.

The dream was changing. Someone was coming.
