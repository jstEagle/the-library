## 9. The Cache Beneath

Iyo woke me at what the colony's failing chronometer insisted was five in the morning, which on Meridian meant the sky was the color of a bruise healing and everything smelled faintly of cold iron.

"Get up," she said. She was standing at the foot of my cot in the guest quarters of Ama's house, fully dressed, hair braided with the ruthless efficiency of a child who had learned to do it herself because there was no one else to do it for her. "You wanted to see it. Mara wanted to see it. Same thing."

"It is not the same thing," I said, sitting up. My mouth tasted like recycled air and regret. "Iyo, we talked about this—"

"We talked about a lot of things." She tossed something at me. It was a thermal jacket, three sizes too big, patched at both elbows with fabric that had once been a tarp. "Wear that. The tunnels are cold, and if you die down there I'll have to explain it to forty other children, and none of them will believe me, because you're supposed to be magic."

"I'm not magic."

"Then prove it by not dying." She turned on her heel and left, which I was beginning to understand was her signature move — exit before anyone could negotiate.

I sat on the edge of the cot for a moment, holding the jacket. Somewhere in the walls, the reactor ticked. Not hummed — *ticked*, like a clock counting down something it refused to name. Ama had shown me the readouts last night over a dinner of protein mash and reconstituted greens: containment margins thinning season by season, a maintenance schedule maintained mostly by prayer and one very stubborn engineer named Dosun who was older than the colony itself and had opinions about the reactor the way other men had opinions about weather.

*Weeks,* I'd thought, looking at the numbers. Maybe months. The kind of arithmetic where every answer was bad.

And now a twelve-year-old was taking me to the reason a woman with my face had been murdered.

I dressed and followed her out into the ash-light morning.

* * *

The settlement of New Halcyon — named, Ama had told me, by colonists who arrived expecting halcyon days and got permafrost and disappointment — clung to the side of a shallow valley like something that had tried to land gently and given up halfway. Forty-one people. Eleven of them children. Solar arrays furred with gray dust. Hydroponic domes sagging like tired shoulders. And everywhere, everywhere, the ash: fine and pale, drifting off the old burn scar to the north where, decades ago, an orbital accident had scoured a hundred kilometers of ground down to mineral bone.

The children didn't play in the ash field. But they dreamed in it, apparently. Every single one of them, every night, standing in that pale expanse watching a woman who looked exactly like me mouth words they couldn't hear.

I had spent two days among them trying not to flinch every time one of them looked at me with that particular expression — recognition without acquaintance, grief without loss. Yesterday a boy of about six had handed me a drawing of my own face rendered in charcoal with startling accuracy, then wandered off without a word, as though delivering mail.

"They knew you were coming," Ama had said, "before any of us did. Before *you* did, maybe. I don't know what to do with that except feed you and keep you close."

Now Iyo was keeping me closer than that.

She led me past the eastern dome, past the water reclamation sheds, up a switchback trail of compacted ash toward a ridge of dark rock that broke through the pale ground like a whale surfacing. The wind up here had teeth. She walked fast, and I noticed she kept glancing back — not checking on me, exactly. Checking whether I was still following. Whether I was still real.

"How far?" I asked.

"Two kilometers. Then down." She pointed at a seam in the rock face, barely visible — a door, or the memory of one, its edges grown over with lichen the color of old pennies. "Mara found it. Four years ago. She came up here with a survey kit and didn't tell anyone why, and then she came back down and started writing letters, and then—" Iyo stopped. Her jaw did something complicated. "And then she left. And then she died. That's the story we got. Ama tells it better."

"You don't have to tell it at all," I said gently.

"Yes I do." She pulled a hand terminal from her pocket — a battered civilian model, its case held together with adhesive strips gone gray with age. "Because nobody else remembers it right. The adults remember it sad. I remember it *important*. There's a difference."

There was, I thought. I had inherited a dead woman's memories, and even those had come to me secondhand — imprinted, compressed, annotated by technicians who had never met her. What I remembered of Mara Montrose was a highlight reel with gaps in it. What Iyo remembered was a woman who had knelt down to her level and listened. I wasn't sure which version was truer. I suspected the twelve-year-old's.

At the seam in the rock, Iyo pressed her palm against a recessed panel. Nothing happened. She pressed again, harder, and made a small frustrated noise.

"It sticks," she said. "Dosun greases it but he also complains about it, so it's basically a hobby for him." She braced her shoulder against the door and shoved. With a groan of protesting seals, it swung inward onto darkness and a breath of air so cold and clean it felt almost sacrilegious after the settlement's recycled atmosphere.

"You've been down here before?" I asked.

"Twice. Once with Mara, once after." A pause. "Once after, alone. I wanted to check it was still real."

"And?"

"It was still real." She clicked on a hand lamp. "Come on. Mind the third step. It lies."

* * *

The tunnel descended in a long spiral, cut into the ridge's bedrock with a precision that predated the colony — this was old infrastructure, pre-Verity, pre-Concord, from the first wave of expansion when governments still built things to last longer than press cycles. Emergency lighting flickered along the walls at intervals, running off some deep battery that refused, out of sheer institutional stubbornness, to die.

"The survey records call it Depot Seven," Iyo said, her voice echoing strangely in the spiral. "There were supposed to be more. Twelve depots across the system, fuel and parts, for the second wave of ships that never came. This is the only one that got filled before the funding died." She glanced back at me, lamp-light carving shadows under her eyes. "Ama says the whole colony is a receipt for promises nobody kept. This is just the biggest one."

The tunnel ended at a bulkhead door, and beyond the bulkhead —

I stopped walking.

The chamber was vast, cathedral-vast, carved from living rock and lined floor to ceiling in matte-gray containment cradles. And in each cradle, glowing with a soft blue-white luminescence that turned the whole space into something between a chapel and an aquarium, sat refined isotope cells. Hundreds of them. Thousands. Rows receding into the dark like organ pipes, each one humming at a frequency I felt in my sternum rather than heard.

I am, whatever else is true of me, a Concord investigator. I know what refined heptazide looks like in quantity. I have seen the fleet's strategic reserve manifests; I have audited them. The entire Concord mobile fleet — ninety-one ships, eleven thousand crew — draws on a reserve roughly half this size.

"By the stars," I whispered.

"Yeah," Iyo said, with the flat satisfaction of someone who has waited a long time for an adult to say exactly that. "That's what Mara said. Except she said a lot of other words first, and most of them Ama wouldn't repeat."

I walked forward slowly. My reflection ghosted across the nearest cell, blue-white and wavering, and for a moment I looked like a woman standing inside a lantern. Enough isotope here to power either fleet for a decade. Enough to make the difference between a civilization that contracted and one that endured. Enough, I thought with a coldness rising up through me like groundwater, to start a war over. To *be* a war over.

Both fleets had agreed to abandon Meridian. Both fleets had signed a ceasefire whose only load-bearing wall was the promise that no one would ever come back here. And all the while, beneath a dying settlement of forty-one souls, lay the single most valuable object in the system — unlogged, unclaimed, and known, until now, to exactly four living people: me, a twelve-year-old girl, a teacher, and a dead woman.

"Mara found this," I said. "Four years ago."

"She surveyed it for six days." Iyo hopped up onto a maintenance ledge, legs swinging, entirely at home. "She ran the assays herself. She told me it was enough to run the colony's reactor for two hundred years. She said—" Iyo's voice caught, just slightly, and she covered it by swinging her legs harder. "She said nobody would ever have to be cold again. Nobody would ever have to choose who eats. She said she was going to get us *out*. Ships, supplies, a real colony somewhere green. She wrote it all down. Letters. Codes. Ama has them."

Codes. I thought of the files aboard Corvus — Mara's coded letters to Meridian, promising rescue no fleet had authorized. I had read them in a cold archive room and thought: *poor deluded woman, promising what she couldn't deliver.* Standing here, in the blue-white glow of a decade of power, the letters rearranged themselves in my mind. They hadn't been delusion. They'd been a plan. A real one, built on a real discovery.

Which raised the question that had been circling me since I decanted: how does a fleet investigator die in a station corridor with no witnesses, no weapon recovered, and no motive on record?

Unless the motive was standing right in front of me, humming.

"Iyo," I said carefully. "Who else knows about this place?"

"Dosun. Ama. Me. Kess, a little — she helped carry assay samples, she's sixteen, she doesn't count as a kid anymore even though she acts like one." Iyo counted on her fingers. "And whoever killed Mara, probably. If she reported it up the chain like she said she was going to."

The cold clarity arrived then, all at once, the way ice forms — not gradually but in a sudden silent bloom. Mara Montrose had found a treasure worth fleets. She had done the diligent, honorable, catastrophic thing: she had reported it. She had filed her findings through official channels, encrypted, confident that the institution she served would do the right thing.

And the institution had done the efficient thing instead. Because there are only a few possible responses to a discovery like this, and every one of them requires either sharing it, fighting over it, or burying it. Sharing was against human nature. Fighting risked the ceasefire. So: burial. And the cleanest way to bury a report is to bury the reporter.

*I was not murdered despite my discovery,* Mara's memory seemed to say, standing beside me in the glow. *I was murdered because of it.*

I put my hand against a containment cradle. The metal was cool and steady and utterly indifferent to the fact that it had cost a woman her life and might yet cost a great deal more.

"Rafael?" Iyo said. She was watching me with those too-old eyes. "Your face went weird."

"My face often goes weird," I managed. "It's a feature."

"You look like you figured something out."

"I look like that a lot. It's less impressive than it sounds." I crouched down to her level — Mara's move, I realized, borrowed without permission. "Iyo. Listen to me. This place stays secret. You don't talk about it, Kess doesn't talk about it, Dosun keeps complaining about the door and nothing else. Can you do that?"

"Why? What happens if people find out?"

What happens is that two armadas currently pretending this planet doesn't exist remember very quickly that it does. What happens is that forty-one colonists become a rounding error in a territorial dispute. What happens is that the ceasefire — the fragile, exhausted, load-bearing ceasefire — comes down on top of everyone you love.

"What happens," I said, "is that adults start making decisions about your home without asking you. And I'd rather they didn't."

She studied me for a long moment. Then she nodded once, sharp, and jumped down off the ledge. "Okay. But you're going to fix it, right? Like Mara couldn't?"

The question landed somewhere under my ribs and stayed there.

"I'm going to try," I said.

"That's what she said too." Iyo clicked off her lamp and stood in the isotope-glow, small and unbowed. "But you're not her. So maybe you'll be better at it."

* * *

We climbed back into the ash-light, and I sealed the door behind us with hands that were not quite steady. Iyo walked ahead, chattering now — released, I think, by the secrecy, by having finally told the story to someone who understood its weight. She was describing Dosun's feud with the door hinge in loving detail when she stopped mid-sentence.

"Rafael." All the chatter gone from her voice. "Look up."

Above the ridge, high and hard and wrong against the pale sky, a point of light was moving. Not falling. Not burning up. Moving *sideways* — the patient, deliberate traverse of a ship in low orbit, running a scan pattern.

No ship was scheduled. No ship should be. The fleets had agreed: no one returns to Meridian.

The light slid across the sky, paused at the horizon's edge, and began its next pass — tighter this time, lower, methodical as a searchlight sweeping a cell block.

Someone had come looking. And the first thing I thought, with that same terrible ice-bloom clarity, was: *they're not scanning for survivors.*

They're scanning for the depot.

Behind me, Iyo took my hand. Her fingers were small and cold and absolutely certain.

"Don't worry," she said, in the tone of a child comforting an adult, which should have been funny and was instead the most frightening thing I'd heard in two lifetimes. "You'll think of something. You always do. It's in the dream."
