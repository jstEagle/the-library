## 14. The Children's Council

Iyo had called the meeting the way all important meetings on Meridian were called: by standing in the middle of the common hall and shouting "Council! Now!" until every child under fourteen came running, and then going to find the ones who didn't.

The council met in the old seed vault, because it was the only room in the settlement that still locked from the inside. It was small and cold and smelled like dust and the ghost of potatoes, and it fit eleven children if they sat close, which they did, knees knocking, breath fogging. Iyo sat on an upturned crate at the front. Beside her, on a slightly taller crate — because she was twelve and this was her council, but Petri was thirteen and insisted on protocol — sat Petri, who had brought a clipboard. Nobody knew where he'd gotten a clipboard. He guarded its provenance like a state secret.

"Order," said Petri, though nobody had been talking.

"You say order," said Sefa, who was nine and considered herself the council's conscience, "but you're the one who was talking."

"I was calling order."

"That's still talking."

Iyo let them go for exactly ten seconds, because letting them burn off the first layer of noise made everything after easier. Then she knocked twice on the crate, the way Ama did when class got loud, and the room settled the way rooms did when they remembered who she was.

"This is about Rafael," she said.

The name landed in the seed vault the way names of important things always landed: quietly, heavily, like snow off a roof.

"She's not Mara," Iyo went on. "We all know that. We've known since the second day."

"We've known since the first day," said Petri. "She held the wrench wrong."

"She held the wrench *differently*," said Sefa. "That's not the same as wrong."

"It is when you're trying to fix a coolant line."

"The point," Iyo said, "is not the wrench." She waited. Petri lowered the clipboard two centimeters, which from Petri was a concession the size of a treaty. "The point is what we do about it. Because here's the thing nobody's saying out loud. The dream told us someone was coming. And someone came. But Mara promised us rescue, and Mara died, and now there's a woman wearing her face walking around our settlement fixing coolant lines with the wrong grip, and none of us have asked her the one question that matters."

There were eleven children in the seed vault, and for a moment all of them thought the same thought at the same time, which was a thing that happened to Meridian children more than it happened to other people, on account of the dream. The thought was: *Is she going to leave too?*

"So I'm calling a vote," Iyo said. "Not a vote about whether she stays. We don't get to vote on that. She's a grown-up; grown-ups don't work that way, even the good ones." A ripple of grim agreement went around the room; everyone present had extensive experience with grown-ups not working the way you needed them to. "It's a vote about what we ask her. Tonight. All of us together, so she can't think it's just me being difficult."

"You're always just being difficult," said Petri.

"Thank you, Secretary Petri. Motion on the floor: we ask Rafael Montrose to stay until the last shuttle leaves Meridian. Not to be Mara. To be what Mara couldn't. Discussion?"

The discussion lasted forty minutes, which was long for a council where the average age was ten and a half, and during it the following points were raised:

One, by Sefa: that asking someone to stay until the last shuttle was asking them to stay for the scariest part, and it wasn't fair to ask unless you were also offering something back.

Two, by Petri: that the something back should be organized, and he had drafted a rotation schedule for who would bring her tea, because "you can't ask a person to watch a planet die without tea infrastructure."

Three, by twins Ode and Oda, age seven, speaking in their usual unison and then, when they disagreed, in furious alternation: that the dream-woman had mouthed words they couldn't hear, and maybe Rafael would hear them, and maybe that was worth staying for all by itself.

Four, by little Wren, who was six and mostly silent these days, and whose single contribution stopped the whole council cold: "If she stays till the end, then nobody's last." Nobody asked her to explain. Everybody understood.

Five, by Petri again, raising the objection that had been sitting in his chest like a stone: "What if she says no? What if we ask, and she looks at us the way the fleet people looked at us in the transmissions, like we're a line item?" And Iyo had answered, because this was the part she'd thought about longest, lying awake in her bunk doing the arithmetic of trust:

"Then we'll know. And knowing is better than hoping wrong. That's the whole lesson of Mara. Hope isn't the problem. Hope that won't look at itself is the problem."

Petri wrote that down. On the clipboard. Underlined twice.

They voted. Eleven hands went up out of eleven children, and Petri recorded it as unanimous, and then Sefa pointed out that Wren couldn't reach the tea urn on the high shelf so the schedule needed revising, and the meeting dissolved into logistics, which was how you could tell a meeting had gone well.

* * *

Rafael was in the pump house when they came for her, up to her elbows in the secondary coolant loop, wearing Mara's old coveralls with the sleeves rolled four times over, because the original had apparently been built on a smaller scale or with less regard for future generations. She had grease on one cheekbone and a flashlight clenched in her teeth, and when eleven children filed into the pump house in single file, oldest to youngest, she took the flashlight out of her mouth and said, with the wariness of a woman who had learned that children traveling in formation meant trouble:

"Okay. Either the reactor's fine and this is a surprise party, or the reactor's fine and this is an intervention. Those are the only two options where you're all calm enough to walk in a line."

"It's a council," said Iyo.

"A council."

"We have a government. It's been operating since before you came. You just haven't been informed, because you weren't a matter before."

Rafael looked at her for a moment, and Iyo watched her do the thing she did — the quick flicker behind the eyes, checking a memory that wasn't hers against a reality that was — and then Rafael wiped her hands on a rag, sat down on an empty coolant drum, and said, "All right. I'm listening. Is there an agenda?"

"There's an agenda," said Petri, producing the clipboard.

"Of course there is."

"Item one," Petri read, "acknowledgment of prior intelligence failures regarding wrench usage—"

"We're skipping item one," said Iyo.

"I move we don't skip item one."

"Overruled. Item two." Iyo stepped forward. Around her, the other children arranged themselves, not in a line anymore but in a loose crescent, the way they stood when they sang the settlement song on Founding Day, except nobody had sung anything on Founding Day for three years because there wasn't much to found anymore. "We know you're not Mara."

Rafael didn't flinch. That was one of the things Iyo had decided she liked about her, catalogued carefully, the way you catalogued things you might lose. "I know you know," Rafael said.

"We know you know we know. This isn't a trap. It's just where we're starting, because councils start with facts." Iyo took a breath. Somewhere in the pipes overhead, the failing heart of Meridian ticked through its cycle, patient as a clock counting down something nobody wanted to name. "Mara promised us rescue. She wrote letters. She meant them. And then somebody killed her for meaning them, and we got nothing, and we spent three years waiting for a woman who was already dead. So we're not going to do that again. We're not going to wait for anybody. We're going to ask straight out, and you're going to answer straight out, and either way we'll believe you, because you haven't lied to us yet, and we've been watching."

"Have you," said Rafael, and there was something in her voice that wasn't quite a smile but lived next door to one.

"Extensively. Sefa has notes."

"I have notes," confirmed Sefa.

"So here's the ask." Iyo made herself stand still and say it plainly, no drama, because drama was for people who hadn't watched shuttles never come. "When the evacuation starts — and it's starting, we heard you and Ama planning, don't make the face, the vents carry — when the shuttles start lifting, we want you to be on the last one. Not the first. Not somewhere safe in the middle. The last one. We want the person who tells us it's going to be okay to be the person who leaves last. Mara couldn't do that. Mara died first and left us holding her promise. We're asking you to be what she couldn't. Somebody who stays until the end, so that nobody here is ever the last one alone."

The pump house was very quiet. Even the pipes seemed to hold their breath.

Rafael set the rag down very carefully, the way people handle things when their hands aren't steady and they don't want anyone to notice. "You understand what you're asking," she said. "The last shuttle is the dangerous one. The fleets are circling. If anything goes wrong—"

"We know," said Iyo. "We did the math. Petri did the math twice."

"The math doesn't include what it does to forty-one people if the person they trusted most lifts off early 'for operational reasons,'" said Petri, reading from the clipboard with the gravity of a magistrate. "We've had operational reasons. Operational reasons are why we're down to one working greenhouse."

"And what do I get," Rafael said, and her voice had gone rough in a way that made Wren lean slightly closer, "if I say yes? What's in it for the woman who stays till the end?"

And that was the question Iyo had prepared for least, because she'd expected fear, or refusal, or the careful adult voice that explained why children's requests couldn't be accommodated. She hadn't expected to be negotiated with like a person.

Sefa answered first, because Sefa was nine and had no sense of pacing. "Tea. There's a rotation. Petri has a chart."

"There's a chart," Rafael repeated faintly.

"Every evening, one of us brings you tea in whatever room you're working in, and you have to drink it even if it's bad, and some of it will be bad, because Ode and Oda are on Thursdays and they put pine needles in it once."

"It was medicinal," said Ode.

"It was *experimental*," said Oda.

"And that's not the real thing," Iyo said. "The real thing is—" and here she faltered, because the real thing was hard to say in front of everyone, so she said it anyway, the way you jump into cold water. "The real thing is you keep saying you inherited her life. Her memories, her case, her face, her murder. Fine. But her family died with her, because she never got to have one out here, she only got to promise one. So if you stay — if you're the last one off this rock — then you don't inherit her life anymore. You get your own. With us in it. That's the trade. That's what's in it for you."

Rafael looked at the crescent of children for a long time. Iyo watched her count them — she saw her lips move slightly, *forty-one minus thirty, eleven* — and watched her arrive at the number and understand that every single one of them had voted on this, in a locked seed vault, about her.

"My handler would call this compromised judgment," Rafael said finally.

"Your handler isn't here."

"No," Rafael agreed. "My handler is very far away, and getting farther." She stood up off the coolant drum, and she stuck out her hand, grease and all, toward Iyo, formal as a treaty signing. "Terms accepted. I stay until the last shuttle. I fly the last shuttle, actually, because nobody else knows the approach corridor I mapped, and also because if I'm going to break approximately every regulation the Concord ever wrote, I'd like to break the last one personally."

Iyo shook the hand. It was warm and greasy and it gripped like a promise.

"For the record," Rafael added, "the wrench thing was a deliberate test. I wanted to see who'd correct me."

"Liar," said Petri.

"Absolutely," said Rafael, cheerfully, and the children laughed, all eleven of them, and the sound bounced around the pump house and up into the pipes, and for a moment the failing heart of Meridian sounded almost like laughter too.

* * *

They were still there an hour later — the council having devolved, as councils did, into Rafael showing them the coolant loop diagram and Ode asking increasingly unhinged questions about pressure differentials — when the settlement's comm panel chimed the three-note priority tone that nobody had heard in months.

Ama's voice came through, tight and fast: "Rafael. Reactor telemetry, section four. You need to see this. Now."

Rafael was moving before the sentence finished. At the door she paused and looked back at the eleven of them, and Iyo saw that the warmth in her face had been replaced by something older and harder, the face of the woman who had woken up in a cloning vat with a dead stranger's ghosts.

"Go home," Rafael said. "All of you. Lock your doors tonight."

"Why?" asked Wren, small and clear.

Rafael hesitated. Iyo watched her decide — watched the exact moment she chose, again, to tell them the truth instead of the comfortable thing.

"Because the reactor just started dying faster," she said. "And I need to find out how much time we have left."

She ran. The children stood in the pump house in their crescent, and above them the pipes ticked, and Iyo found she was holding Petri's clipboard without remembering taking it, gripping it like a railing.

"Update the agenda," she said. Her voice didn't shake, which surprised her. "Item one just changed."
