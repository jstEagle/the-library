## 22. Signals Still Answered

The night shift on Meridian Station — the name had stuck, over every objection and three formal petitions — began the way all Rafael's shifts began: with a cup of tea gone lukewarm beside the console, a blanket over her knees that Okafor had knitted and pretended he hadn't, and the long patient silence of ships that did not want to talk to each other.

She had come to think of the communications desk as a lighthouse with the light turned down low. Not a beacon, exactly. More of a porch lamp. *We're here,* it said. *If you need us.*

Most nights, nobody needed them.

The station sat in neutral space between the Concord lanes and the Verity lanes, close enough to both to be useful, far enough from either to be safe-ish, which was the most anyone could promise anymore. Forty-one colonists lived aboard now, plus the crew who'd chosen to stay after the tribunal, plus a slow trickle of drifters and salvagers and one extremely opinionated botanist named Ferro who had converted two cargo bays into greenhouses and guarded them like a dragon.

Rafael worked the late shift because she liked it. Because the children were asleep, because the traffic was thin, because there was something about the small hours that made even static feel companionable.

"Anything?" Ama asked, drifting through at midnight with her own tea. Ama still couldn't sleep past midnight; eleven years on a dying colony had wired her that way, and no amount of safety seemed to unwind it.

"Freighter *Kestrel's Lament* wants a weather update for the Verity corridor. A Concord patrol asked me to remind them they're overdue on their transponder fees. And somebody out past the buoy line is playing music at us."

"Music?"

Rafael tilted her head toward the speakers. Faintly, under the hiss, something orchestral and mournful looped every ninety seconds. "Third night in a row. No ID, no message. Just the symphony."

"Maybe they're lonely."

"Maybe they're *dramatic*." She logged it anyway. You logged everything. That was the whole religion of the comms desk: someone, somewhere, was always trying to say something, and your job was to be listening when they managed it.

Ama settled into the second chair, the one that had become hers without discussion. For a while they just sat, two women watching numbers crawl across screens, and it occurred to Rafael — not for the first time — that this was what peace actually looked like. Not treaties signed under floodlights. Just tea, and company, and nobody shooting at anybody.

"Iyo left her essay on your desk," Ama said. "Don't grade it gently. She'll know."

"She always knows."

"She gets it from you."

"I've known her fourteen months. Whatever she gets, she got it surviving me, which is different."

Ama smiled into her tea and didn't argue, which was its own kind of agreement.

* * *

The essay was about the ash field.

That was the assignment Iyo had given herself — the teachers offered a menu of topics and Iyo had walked past all of them to the blank space at the bottom and written her own name in. *What We Left Behind and Why It Wasn't Nothing.* She was fifteen now, tall and sharp-elbowed and unimpressed by authority in a way that made Rafael privately proud and publicly exhausted.

Rafael read it at 0300, when the traffic board went quiet.

Iyo wrote the way she spoke: plainly, without decoration, landing every sentence like a stone set in a wall. She wrote about the settlement's last winter. About the reactor's cough. About the day the shuttles lifted and Meridian went dark behind them, and how she had watched it through a port window until it was just another star, indistinguishable, ordinary, gone.

*"People say we lost everything,"* she wrote. *"But the dream ended because Mara let us go. That means it was real enough to end. I don't know what you call a thing that was only ever a dream but still saved forty-one lives. I think maybe you call it a promise. Even the broken kind is proof somebody tried."*

Rafael read that paragraph four times.

Then she took off her glasses — an affectation she'd adopted in month three because the original Mara had needed them and some habits arrived with the memories whether you wanted them or not — and pressed her fingers against her eyes until the colors bloomed.

Grief for Mara came less often now. When it came, it came sideways, through essays and tea temperatures and the way Iyo said *obviously* with exactly Mara's intonation. Rafael had stopped fighting it. You didn't get to inherit a woman's face, her case files, her enemies, and her children, and then decline the grief. It was all part of the estate.

At 0347, the board chimed.

Not the routine chime — the soft tick of a transponder handshake or a weather request. This was the old alert, the deep-priority tone Okafor had restored himself, arguing that some signals deserved to arrive with ceremony. Rafael sat up so fast the blanket slid to the floor.

Origin: unregistered. Deep field, well past the buoy line, past anything with business being there. Signal strength weak and getting weaker, as though the sender were running out of power or courage or both.

She routed it to audio.

Static first. Then, threading through it, a voice. Young. Very young. The kind of young that hadn't finished growing into its own echo.

"*Is anyone—*" A burst of interference swallowed the rest. The loop reset. "*Is anyone there? Does anyone remember Meridian? My grandmother says there was a colony called Meridian. She says a woman kept a promise. Please. Is anyone listening?*"

The loop reset again. And again. The same words, worn smooth by repetition, by hours or days of broadcasting into nothing.

Rafael's hands had gone completely still on the console.

*Does anyone remember Meridian.*

She thought of the ash field. Of twelve-year-old Iyo standing in it with the other children, all of them looking at a spot where no one stood, seeing a woman none of them had ever met. She thought of the dream ending — Mara's hand opening, the children released, the sky going clean. She thought of the tribunal, of Serrano in cuffs, of the cache signed over to civilian trustees, of the word *Meridian* spoken aloud in official rooms until it stopped sounding like a wound and started sounding like a place.

But out here, past the buoys, past the lanes, apparently it still sounded like a question.

"*Does anyone remember Meridian?*"

"Yes," Rafael said aloud, to an empty room, to a signal light-years from arriving. Her voice cracked on it. "Yes, we do."

Then she did her job.

* * *

She spent twenty minutes confirming what her heart already knew: the signal wasn't a ghost, wasn't an echo, wasn't a Verity prank or a Concord probe. It originated from a jury-rigged transmitter — the power signature was cobbled together from at least three incompatible systems, the kind of engineering children do when adults aren't looking or adults are gone. The source was moving. Slowly. Drifting, more than traveling. A ship, probably. A small one. Running on fumes and hope.

Running toward the one place a broadcast about Meridian might find an answer.

Rafael opened a channel. Then closed it. Then opened it again.

This was the moment, she understood, where the old life and the new one shook hands. There was protocol for this — there was always protocol — verify, escalate, notify the fleets, file the report, wait for permission. She could see the whole bureaucratic staircase laid out before her, every step labeled DUTY.

She also remembered what the fleets had done the last time someone promised rescue to people nobody official wanted rescued.

She keyed the channel.

"Unregistered vessel, this is Meridian Station, communications desk." Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "We hear you. We remember Meridian. Say again your position and status. We have medical, fuel, and hot food, and I am personally authorized to promise all three, which is a sentence I never expected to enjoy saying."

Silence. Long enough that she checked the board twice.

Then, crackling, disbelieving: "*You're real? You're actually real? Grandmother said if I sent it long enough—she said there was a station now, named after—she said a woman kept the promise.*"

"What's your ship's condition? How many aboard?"

"*Four. Grandmother, my brother, me, and Biscuit. Biscuit's a cat. The engine's mostly dead. We've been drifting nine days.*"

Nine days. Rafael did the arithmetic and didn't like any of it. "Hold tight. Don't touch the transmitter again except to answer me. I'm going to get you home."

"*Home?*"

And there it was — the word doing exactly what it had done for forty-one colonists once upon a time. Rafael looked at it hanging in the air of the quiet room, at the tea going cold, at the blanket on the floor, at the station around her full of sleeping people who called themselves the Meridian orphans even though half of them were older than she was.

"Yeah," she said. "Home. Stand by."

* * *

Okafor arrived in his robe at 0430, hair wild, reading the alert on his slate before he was fully through the door. "Deep-field distress. Unregistered. You answered it."

"You were listening."

"I'm always listening. It's my entire personality." He dropped into the third chair — his chair, though he claimed otherwise. "Fleets will want to be notified."

"The fleets can be notified *after* the tow tender launches. They lost their vote on who deserves rescuing at a tribunal eighteen months ago. It's in the minutes."

Okafor looked at her for a long moment. He had gotten older in the way good men do — softer at the edges, harder at the center. "Mara would've liked that answer," he said quietly.

"Mara *gave* me that answer. It was in the estate." She stood, pulling on her jacket. "Wake Ferro, tell him we need the tender fueled and the med bay warm. Wake Iyo."

"It's four in the morning."

"She'll want to be there. Trust me." Rafael paused at the door. "It's a kid, Okafor. Broadcasting into the dark, asking if anyone remembers. Somebody has to answer. That's the whole job. That's been the whole job since the beginning."

Okafor smiled and reached for the intercom. "Go," he said. "I've got the desk."

* * *

The tow tender cleared the dock at dawn, station-time, which on the observation deck meant the artificial lights warming from blue to gold while the real stars wheeled indifferent overhead. Iyo stood at the rail with her arms crossed, hair uncombed, radiating the specific fury of a teenager woken before five who was nonetheless extremely glad to have been woken before five.

"A kid," she said. "Alone on a dead ship. Nine days."

"With her grandmother and brother. And a cat."

"Biscuit," Iyo repeated, with the gravity of a judge. "Okay. Biscuit gets priority boarding."

They found the ship six hours later: a scavenged hauler barely bigger than a shuttle, hull patched with mismatched plating, running lights flickering like a candle in wind. Rafael brought her alongside herself, hands easy on the controls, and watched through the viewport as an airlock cycled open and three figures emerged into the tender's lights — two children squinting, an old woman leaning on a cane, and a cat in a carrier giving everyone the deeply unimpressed stare of its species.

The girl — she couldn't have been more than ten — looked up at Rafael and went very still.

"You're her," the girl whispered. "Grandmother showed me the picture. From the news. The woman who told the truth."

Rafael crouched to her level. Fourteen months ago this moment would have broken something in her. Now it just opened something, the way a door opens onto a room you didn't know your house had.

"No," she said gently. "I'm not her. Her name was Mara Montrose, and she was braver than me, and she'd want you to know that keeping a promise isn't the same as living to see it kept. I'm Rafael. I finished the job."

The girl considered this with the thoroughness of the very young. "Did you stay till the last shuttle?"

"I flew it."

"Okay," the girl said, satisfied, and took her hand.

Behind her, Iyo was already talking to the brother, already assigning him a bunk and a greenhouse shift and approximately nine hundred facts about the station, and Ama had the grandmother's arm, and somewhere in the noise Okafor was saying something about paperwork that absolutely no one was listening to.

Rafael stood in the tender's warm light, holding a stranger's hand, surrounded by the loud, imperfect, stubborn machinery of people taking care of people.

The stars outside were cold and far away and full of other voices, other signals, other questions still waiting in the dark.

She'd be listening.

That was the whole job.
