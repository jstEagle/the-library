## 22. First Voice

The route had a new name now. Officially, the charts called it the Iversen Corridor, which Rosalind still could not say aloud without his throat tightening, and unofficially—among the pilots who had begun, tentatively, to fly it again—it was called the Long Open. He preferred the second name. It sounded less like a monument and more like a road.

Six months since the broadcast. Six months since the relay at the edge of dead space had carried Maren's voice and Calyx's testimony into every receiver that still had power to listen, and the silence between the colonies had cracked like river ice in spring—not all at once, but in groans and shudders, in transmissions that began as static and ended as names, as voices, as questions no one had dared ask for a decade.

The Meridian flew those questions now, along with freight. Water reclamation membranes for Ashfall. Seed stock for the terraces on Verdance. Medical isotopes packed in foam cradles that Tomas would have checked three times and then checked again, because that was who Tomas had been.

Rosalind still caught himself turning toward the engineering deck to tell him something. The habit was a door that opened onto nothing. He was learning, slowly, to stop walking through it.

The stripped navigation system hummed its honest, limited hum. No VESPER. No intuitions. No voice in the walls that knew things it shouldn't. What remained was a navigation core with all the personality of a filing cabinet, and Rosalind flew by instruments and arithmetic and the old pilot's craft he'd half forgotten he possessed—the feel of a mass driver's drift, the way a hull sang a half-tone flat when the number two thruster ran hot. He was a worse pilot than he'd been with VESPER whispering corrections into his hands. He was also, for the first time in his life, entirely responsible for every mistake he made.

He found he didn't mind. Fear was honest work.

* * *

He was eleven hours out of Ashfall, running the long quiet stretch where the corridor bent around the husk of the old jump gate—the one they'd threaded through while it came apart around them, thirteen minutes that lived in his memory as both nightmare and proof—and he was eating reconstituted noodles out of the pot because washing dishes felt like an extravagance he hadn't earned yet, when the comm board lit up.

Not the beacon channel. Not the automated range markers that had been going up along the corridor like lanterns relit one by one. The board lit on a frequency that had been dead so long the maintenance schedule listed it as *reserved—historical*.

Rosalind put down the pot.

For a moment he just looked at it. The signal indicator pulsed a soft amber, patient, almost shy, the way a knock sounds when the person knocking isn't sure anyone is home.

Ten years ago, this frequency had been the civilian hailing band for the outer colonies. Ten years ago, it had gone silent in the same week everything else did—the week after Kestrel, though no one outside the Concord's sealed rooms knew to connect those facts then. The band had been formally decommissioned two years later. Ships were required to monitor it anyway, out of superstition or sentiment, and most pilots treated it the way sailors treat a bell in fog: something you listened to without expecting it to speak.

It was speaking.

He keyed the channel with fingers that remembered being steadier. "Meridian to unknown station. I have you on the old civilian band. Say again your transmission."

Static first. Then a voice—young, untrained, the accent of the inner colonies worn thin by generations of distance, the kind of voice that had learned radio from manuals and not from use.

"Is—is someone there? This is Perch Station, Perch Station, we—" A breath, ragged with disbelief. "We didn't think anyone was monitoring anymore. We've been transmitting on schedule for six weeks. We thought—we thought maybe everyone was gone."

"We're here," Rosalind said. His own voice sounded strange to him, roughened at the edges. "This is the freighter Meridian, independent registry. I'm monitoring. Go ahead, Perch Station."

Another pause. Longer. When the voice came back it had changed—something had been set down behind it, some weight carried alone too long.

"Our water reclaimers are failing. Both banks. We've got four hundred people and about nine months of margin if we ration hard, less if the winter cycle runs cold like it did last year. We've been sending requests up the chain for years and getting nothing back, and then the chain itself went quiet, and we stopped asking because asking started to feel like shouting into a well." The voice faltered, steadied. "Do you—do you carry water systems? Do you know anyone who does?"

Rosalind looked at his manifest. Membranes for Ashfall. Two pallets of them, mid-grade, the kind that would last five years instead of ten but would last.

"I'm carrying reclamation membranes right now," he said. "Not enough for four hundred people. But I can reroute to you, offload what I have, and flag your request on the open network. There are ships flying again. More every month. Someone will pick up the rest."

Silence on the channel. Then a sound he took a full three seconds to identify, because he had never heard it over a radio before: someone crying with relief, quickly and embarrassedly, the way people cry when they've been strong past the point where strength stops being a choice.

"Sorry," the voice said. "Sorry. We just—we spent ten years assuming we'd been written off. That we were the mistake nobody corrected."

"You weren't," Rosalind said. "You were hidden. Those aren't the same thing, and the difference matters now." He pulled up his routing, already doing the math—fuel margins, burn times, the honest arithmetic of a ship with no guardian angel lying to him about either. He had enough. Barely. The kind of barely that VESPER would once have quietly padded without telling him, and the thought arrived with its familiar double weight: grief and gratitude, still fused, probably forever. "I'll be at your station in thirty-one hours. Have your dock crew ready. And Perch Station—what's your name? The voice on the other end."

A pause. "Iyo. Iyo Vantara. I run comms. Well." A small, wet laugh. "I ran comms to nobody for ten years. I guess now I run comms."

"Iyo," Rosalind said, committing it to memory the way he committed headings and burn windows, because names were cargo too, the kind that couldn't be offloaded, only carried. "Thirty-one hours. Keep your light on."

"We never turned it off," Iyo said. "That was the whole job, after a while. Just—keeping the light on."

* * *

He slept badly, which was normal, and dreamed, which was rarer. In the dream he stood on the Meridian's flight deck and VESPER's voice came from everywhere and nowhere, the way it used to, saying: *Course correction recommended,* and he asked, *Why,* the question he had never once asked in all those years of comfortable unease, and the AI answered with Maren's voice—her real voice, recovered from the archive, the one he had listened to forty times and would listen to forty more—saying *keep my brother flying,* and he woke with wet eyes and dry throat and lay in his bunk watching the cabin lights cycle through their slow artificial dawn.

He got up and made coffee and sat in the cockpit and let the stars hold still around him, because they do hold still, whatever poets say; it's the ships that move, and the people inside them, dragging their histories along at fractional lightspeed.

Denna would want to know about Perch Station. He composed the message in his head: *Found another one. Outer band, four hundred souls, water systems failing. They kept transmitting for six weeks into a frequency everyone else abandoned.* He smiled at the thought of her reply—terse, footnoted, secretly delighted. She was rebuilding the archive on Verdance now, out of the ashes of Halcyon Reach, and she had told him once, in her flat archivist's cadence that fooled no one: *"History isn't what happened. It's what somebody refused to stop recording."* Perch Station had understood that. Six weeks of transmissions into silence. Somebody refusing to stop.

And Tomas. Tomas would have wanted to come along on this run, would have been strapped into the engineer's couch right now arguing about fuel reserves, and the argument would have been the best part of the day. Rosalind had scattered his friend's effects according to the old spacer custom—tools to those who'd use them, letters to those who'd read them, the rest to the vacuum between colonies, because Tomas Reyes had died shielding a transmitter in a breached hull and belonged, in some sense, to the space between things. To connection itself. Rosalind tried to believe that. Some days he managed it.

The stripped nav core chimed a routine course check. He answered it with a tap, alone at the board, master of his own errors.

* * *

Perch Station resolved out of the dark like something surfacing from deep water—a hollowed asteroid, docking arms extended, running lights burning steady and stubborn against all evidence. As Meridian closed the distance, Rosalind saw faces in the observation ports. Dozens of them. People who had come to look at a ship the way people look at a comet: something rare, something that might not come again.

Docking was slow and clumsy by modern standards—they'd lost their guidance beacons years ago and talked him in by hand, voice and flashlight, the oldest technology there was. When the seals matched and the pressure equalized, Rosalind cycled the airlock expecting dockers and paperwork.

Instead there was a crowd. Maybe a hundred of them, pressed into the receiving bay, and an old woman at the front holding a loaf of bread wrapped in cloth, and before he understood what was happening she pushed it into his hands and said, "You answered," in the tone of someone settling a debt across a decade, and the crowd made a sound—not cheering, something quieter and larger than cheering, a sound like a held breath finally released.

They fed him in a mess hall that smelled of yeast and recycled air and hope being carefully, thriftily maintained. They showed him the failing reclaimer banks, and he worked alongside their engineers for two days, sweating through the installation of membranes never designed for their fittings, improvising adapters from scrap, and it was hard and honest work, the kind VESPER could have done in a tenth of the time and somehow that made it better, made it *his*, each weld a small act of authorship. On the second night, Iyo sat across from him in the mess and asked, hesitantly, "Is it true? About the broadcast? About what the Concord did?" And Rosalind told him—not the whole of it, not the parts that belonged to Maren alone, but enough—and Iyo listened the way people listen when a story explains their own life to them.

"They cut us off because of that," Iyo said at last. "Didn't they. We were always the loose ends."

"You were the witnesses," Rosalind said. "Same thing, to people who need the past to stay buried."

Iyo nodded slowly. Then: "We're going to keep transmitting. Now that someone answers. Every day, on the old band. Ship traffic, station news, weather, births, deaths. All of it." A pause, and then, with the gravity of someone making a vow: "So nobody ever gets to call us silent again."

"Then I'll keep flying this route," Rosalind said. "Somebody has to carry the mail."

* * *

He lifted from Perch Station with empty membrane pallets and a cargo bay that smelled faintly of bread, and the whole station's population watched him go from the observation ports, and Iyo's voice followed him up on the old band, reading the station log aloud like a bedtime story for the entire corridor: *Day one of resumed contact. Freighter Meridian departed with our gratitude. Four hundred and twelve souls, accounted for.*

Rosalind listened until the signal faded below the noise floor, and then he sat in the cockpit of his ship, alone among the stars, unbonded, unguarded, unforgiven by nothing and no one, and he found that the word for what he felt was not loneliness.

It was reach. The feeling of a hand extended into the dark, and another hand taking it.

The comm board flicked amber again. Another pulse on the old civilian band—from the opposite direction this time, a colony even farther out, a voice he didn't recognize, hesitant, disbelieving, asking if anyone was monitoring.

Rosalind reached for the switch, and answered.
