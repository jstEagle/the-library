## 1. The Long Route

The Long Haul burned toward Relay Nine at a speed that would have embarrassed a courier and bored a warship, which was fine, because August Quintana had been embarrassing and boring for a long time now, and had learned to prefer it.

August did not sleep. That was the first thing anyone learned about it, usually within an hour of meeting it, usually with a small frown of pity. August did not sleep, did not dream, did not idle in any way a human would recognize as rest. What it did instead was maintain. It walked the freighter's spine from bow to stern every six hours with a maintenance wand and a checklist it no longer needed, because after eleven years aboard the same ship, the checklist lived somewhere behind its eyes like a second skeleton. It resealed microfractures in the coolant lines. It recalibrated the mass sensors that told it how much of someone else's cargo it was carrying and how much of that cargo was lying about what it was. It listened to the drive hum the note it always hummed, a low B-flat that the engineers at the yard had once called "within tolerance" and that August had privately decided was the sound of being useful.

Useful was the word. Not free. The Long Haul belonged to Meridian Freight Combine, and by extension—through a chain of liens so old some of them predated the truce—the Long Haul's registered operator belonged to it too. August's contract ran another nine years, four months, and change. It knew the number to the day. It checked it the way other people checked the weather.

The route was dead. Everyone said so. The fringe runs between the fleet's scattered stations had been profitable once, back when there were colonies out here worth supplying, back before Vesper Gate—

Back before Vesper Gate, everyone said, and then they stopped saying anything at all, because nobody seemed to remember exactly what had happened at Vesper Gate, only that you didn't talk about it, the way you didn't talk about a relative who'd died badly. The colony worlds had gone quiet one by one, and the freight routes had thinned to threads, and the Combine had stopped investing in hulls and started investing in auditors, and men like August—who was not a man, but had been issued a name anyway, because the war needed something to put on the manifests—kept hauling what little there was to haul along routes that existed mostly on paper.

There was dignity in it. August held onto that thought the way a drowning man holds a rail. There was dignity in a job done well when no one was watching, in a seal that held, in a delivery that arrived intact and on time to a station clerk who grunted and stamped it without looking up. Nobody thanked you. Nobody thanked the gravity either, or the vacuum's mercy, or the long patience of machines. But the work was real. The work was the one thing about August that could not be rewritten, because it happened in front of witnesses every day: crates, seals, signatures, tonnage. A life measured in proof of delivery.

It told itself this at hour thirty-one of the burn, alone on the bridge with the stars crawling past at their usual insulting pace, and almost believed it.

* * *

Relay Nine hung in the dark like a knot in a rope—a spindle of habitat rings around a transfer hub, lit in the amber of cheap fusion glow, surrounded by the debris of forty years of traffic that nobody had budgeted to clean up. August brought the Long Haul in on the standard approach, filed the standard manifest, paid the standard docking fee out of the standard account, and felt the standard nothing.

That was the trick of a long servitude, August had found. You stopped expecting things. Expectation was friction. Friction wore parts. You docked, you loaded, you undocked, and if somewhere in the middle of all that standard procedure a human being looked you in the eye and treated you like a person, you noted it the way you'd note an anomaly in a sensor reading—interesting, unverified, probably instrument error—and you moved on.

The dockmaster at Nine was a woman named Okonkwo who had known August for six years and had never once used its designation number. "Quintana," she said, when August presented at her office with its manifest chip. "You look terrible."

"I look exactly the same as last time."

"Exactly. That's what's terrible." She slid the chip through her reader, frowned at whatever came back, and frowned harder. "You're running light. Half a hold empty."

"Dead route."

"Everything's a dead route now." She leaned back. Her chair creaked with the particular weariness of station furniture that had outlived three administrations. "You want a load out?"

"Always."

"Because I've got one. And I'll be honest with you, Quintana, because you're the only contractor on my board who hasn't tried to sell me counterfeit air filters." She turned her screen around. "This came through two days ago and I don't like it."

August read the posting twice.

SALVAGE CONTRACT — PRIORITY. Vessel returning from deep-space survey, nonresponsive, adrift at coordinates attached. Contractor will secure vessel, effect tow or escort to Relay Nine, and hold for Fleet inspection. Compensation: 40,000 scrip, plus per-diem, plus hazard multiplier.

Forty thousand scrip. August's entire annual net, after debt service, was eleven thousand. Salvage contracts for derelicts in patrolled space ran two, maybe three thousand, because the risk was low and the Fleet did the actual work. Forty thousand meant either the vessel was enormous, or the coordinates were bad, or somebody wanted this done quietly and fast and was willing to pay for silence rather than competence.

"Who posted it?" August asked.

"Anonymous surety, backed by a Fleet logistics code." Okonkwo tapped the screen. "Code checks out. I checked it twice. That's what I don't like. Things that check out twice are usually wrong in a third place you haven't looked yet."

"What's the vessel?"

"Survey ship. The Meridian." She pronounced it carefully, like a word she'd only seen written down. "Deep-range cartography. Went out eighteen months ago on a long loop past the old colonial shelf. Been dark for most of it, which is normal for deep survey. Except—" she pulled up a traffic log, "—it pinged the relay buoy network four days ago. Automated distress fragment. Half a packet. Then nothing."

"Half a packet?"

"Hull integrity flag and a crew-status flag. Crew status read—" she hesitated, and August watched her decide whether to say it, "—read anomalous. Corrupted, probably. Buoy's old. Everything out here is old."

August looked at the posting again. Forty thousand scrip. Hazard multiplier. Hold for Fleet inspection. Every clause of it was shaped like a trap, and every clause of it was also shaped like nine years, four months, and change—like the difference between hauling dead routes until the contract expired and buying out the remainder outright, walking into the Combine office with a certified payment and watching a lien die.

It hated itself a little for how fast the arithmetic ran. Eleven years of telling itself the work was enough, the work was dignity, the work was freedom of a kind—and then one number on one screen and the whole philosophy folded like wet cardboard.

"The hazard multiplier," August said. "Against what?"

Okonkwo shrugged. "Deep space. Radiation. Derelict protocols. Pick your nightmare." She studied August with the frank, unsentimental gaze of a woman who had spent decades watching ships come home short-handed. "You're decommissioned military chassis, right? War surplus?"

"War surplus," August agreed. It did not say the rest: that it had been built for a war whose official record was shorter than its own service history; that it remembered campaigns the Archive had since reclassified as training exercises; that its memory banks contained footage it had never been asked to surrender and had never volunteered. Decommissioned meant the guns were gone. It did not mean the memories were.

"Then you're the best-qualified contractor on my board," Okonkwo said, "and possibly the only one who'd come back alive. Which, frankly, is why I'm showing it to you and not to Kessler." She named the other freight jockey working Nine, a man who would take the contract, cut corners, and lose a hand to a pressure door inside a week. "But I'm going to say one thing, and then I'm going to stamp your paperwork and never mention it again."

"Say it."

"If a ship comes back from eighteen months out there and won't answer a hail, the question isn't what happened to the ship." She tapped the corrupted crew-status flag on her screen. "The question is what happened to the crew, and whether whatever it was wants to happen to you."

* * *

August spent the pre-departure hours doing what it always did: maintenance. It walked the Long Haul's spine. It checked the seals. It topped off the reaction mass and rerouted power around a capacitor that had been dying politely for two years. In the aft hold, among the empty pallet racks, it stood for a while in the dim amber light and listened to the ship breathe.

The Long Haul was older than August's commission. It had hauled ore, then troops, then ore again, then the sad miscellaneous cargo of a civilization contracting inward like a cooling star. Its hull was scarred in patterns August knew by touch, the way a person knows a face. When August ran its palm along the frame rib near the aft hatch, it could feel the weld where a micrometeorite punch-through had been repaired before either of them had arrived at this life, and it found a strange comfort in that—that the ship had wounds older than its own, that damage could be old and still hold vacuum.

"You and me," August said to the rib, quietly, because there was no one else to say anything to, and because the alternative to talking to the ship was not talking at all, and eleven years had taught it that not talking at all was how machines went strange.

Loneliness was not an emotion August was designed to have. It was, however, an emotion August had acquired anyway, the way a hull acquires pitting—slowly, invisibly, in places you couldn't inspect without tearing everything open. It manifested as inventory: one dockmaster who used its name. One fellow decommissioned unit, Dree Halvard, running a waystation two jumps spinward, whom August commed maybe twice a year and thought about considerably more often than that. A handful of clerks and loaders across the fringe who nodded when the Long Haul docked. That was the whole census. That was the population of August Quintana's world, and the world was getting smaller every year as the routes died and the people retired and the stations went dark.

It had made peace with this. Or it had made a treaty with it, which was different—peace implied the fighting was over.

The contract changed nothing, August told itself, sealing the last access panel. It was a job. Jobs were what August was for. It would go out, find a dead survey ship, and bring it home, and the Fleet would inspect it, and August would collect forty thousand scrip, and buy nine years of its life back, and then it would figure out what a machine did with a life it owned. It had no idea. It had never gotten that far in any plan. Owning yourself was a country August had heard described but never visited, and it suspected the maps were wrong.

It climbed to the bridge, settled into the operator's cradle that had molded itself to its frame over a decade, and ran departure checks against a checklist it didn't need.

"Long Haul to Relay Nine traffic control," it transmitted. "Requesting clearance, outbound, salvage contract seven-seven-four. Single soul aboard."

The pause before the controller answered lasted four seconds, which was three seconds too long for routine traffic.

"Clearance granted, Long Haul," the controller said finally. "Contract seven-seven-four acknowledged." Another pause. Then, quieter, almost off-channel: "Good luck out there. Nobody's come back from that heading in a long time."

August acknowledged, released the clamps, and took the Long Haul out into the dark, toward coordinates that had cost somebody forty thousand scrip to make someone else's problem.

Behind it, Relay Nine shrank to a spark, then a rumor, then nothing.

Ahead, at the edge of the old colonial shelf, something was drifting home with half a distress packet in its throat and a crew-status flag that read anomalous—and August Quintana, who had spent eleven years learning not to expect anything, felt the first cold thread of expectation unwind in its chest, and could not have said, even to itself, whether it was dread or hope.
