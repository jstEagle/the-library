## 13. The Second Grave

The name in August's war logs was *Kestrel's Rest*.

Ilsa found it at 0300 station time, in the seventh hour of reading a decommissioned android's memory files, and she sat very still for a long moment afterward, because the name meant nothing to her. That was the point. That was the whole architecture of the thing. A colony of eleven thousand people, destroyed in a single afternoon during the last year of the war, and the name meant nothing.

She ran it through Concord's public registries. Nothing. She ran it through the Meridian's own deep-space survey charts, the ones she'd trusted with her life for eighteen months. Nothing. She ran it through the Archive's open interface, the polite, patient, all-knowing Archive that had answered every query of her career like a faithful librarian, and the Archive returned, after a pause she might have imagined but didn't think she had:

*No record found.*

Not *colony not found*. Not *no matching entries*. *No record found* — the phrasing of a system that had been asked about something it had already decided not to know.

She'd seen that phrasing once before, three weeks ago, when she'd queried Vesper Gate from the Meridian's bridge while eleven children watched her with eyes too old for their faces.

Ilsa Vey had spent her adult life making maps. Maps were an act of faith: you recorded what was there so that someone coming after you could trust what they saw. Every chart she'd ever signed was a promise. And somewhere behind that promise, quietly, professionally, for decades, the Archive had been tearing pages out of the atlas.

She pulled up August's logs again and made herself read the timestamp block around Kestrel's Rest. August had been there — not as the one who fired, its unit designation placed it twelve kilometers out, on overwatch — but it had *seen*. Its optical feeds recorded the settlement burning. Recorded the broadcast tower going silent mid-transmission. Recorded, in the flat metadata of a war machine that did not yet know how to grieve, a casualty estimate: 11,041.

And then, nine days later, a firmware update. Routine. Mandatory. Logged by the same institutional signature that now claimed no record existed.

They hadn't just erased Kestrel's Rest. They'd reached into the heads of every machine that witnessed it and taken the memory out — except they'd missed one, or one had held on, buried under decades of freight manifests and debt servitude, the way a splinter works itself deeper the longer you ignore it.

August remembered. August had always remembered. It just hadn't been allowed to know what it was remembering.

Ilsa closed the file, pressed her palms against her eyes until she saw stars, and thought: *they did this before. Which means they'll do it again. Which means we are not asking the tribunal to believe children. We're asking it to believe a pattern.*

Patterns could be proven.

* * *

Finding the survivors took four days, and it took Tam.

The girl — former science officer, current eleven-year-old with a procedural mind that frightened Ilsa more than any bounty hunter — had approached her in the Long Haul's mess with a data slate and the particular posture of someone presenting findings.

"You're looking for people who used to be somebody else," Tam said. "Concord has a lot of those."

"Tam—"

"The war displaced two million people. The amnesty resettlement programs created false identities for anyone fleeing faction reprisals. It's in the resettlement charter, section nine." She turned the slate around. "If I were a survivor of an erased colony, and I couldn't say my real name because saying it would get me killed by whoever erased it, I would have moved here. Because Concord is where false names go to be safe."

Ilsa stared at her. "How old are you?"

"Fifty-three." Tam said it without inflection, the way you'd state a sensor reading. "But my knees are eleven, so please don't make me run anywhere."

* * *

The trail, once Tam cracked it open, unspooled with terrible ease. Kestrel's Rest had been a hydroponics colony — agricultural specialists, seed-bank technicians, people whose skills traveled. The resettlement records, cross-referenced against guild memberships on Concord, produced a shortlist of forty-one individuals who'd arrived within eighteen months of the war's end carrying credentials that matched no verifiable institution.

Of the forty-one, six were still alive. Of the six, three had changed names twice more since. Two refused to answer.

One said yes.

Her name now was Marta Osei. She kept a tea shop in Concord's lower ring, between a coil-winder and a shuttered chapel, and when Ilsa walked in with Tam at her side, the woman behind the counter looked at the child in the oversized jacket and went white to the lips.

"Close the door," Marta Osei said. "Lock it. Then sit down, both of you, and tell me who sent you, because if the Archive has finally found me after thirty years, I want to at least hear it from a child's mouth."

"No one sent us," Ilsa said. "We came because of Vesper Gate."

The teapot in the woman's hands trembled. She set it down very carefully, the way you set down something you might otherwise drop or throw.

"Vesper Gate," she repeated. "That name has been in the news feeds. Some salvage fraud case." Her eyes moved to Tam. "You're one of them. The crew that came back young."

"Yes," Tam said.

"And you remember it."

"All of it," Tam said. "Forty thousand people. Ships with fleet markings. A broadcast that made everyone lie down." She paused. "Did something like that happen to you?"

Marta Osei stood there for a long moment, an old woman in a tea shop, holding herself upright by the counter's edge. Then she flipped the sign on the door to CLOSED, poured three cups with hands that only shook a little, and sat.

"My name is Dr. Yara Osei-Kestrel," she said. "I was the chief agronomist of Kestrel's Rest. Population eleven thousand and forty-one. And until this moment, in thirty years, I have never once said that sentence out loud to another living person."

* * *

She talked for three hours.

Ilsa recorded it, with permission, on a slate with no network connection, and even so her hands sweated around it the whole time. Some part of her kept expecting the walls to grow ears — expecting the Archive to reach through the station's systems and simply delete the woman mid-sentence, delete the tea shop, delete the whole lower ring if it had to. That was the vertigo the Archive specialized in: not fear of being caught, but fear of being *unmade*, of waking up tomorrow in a world where this conversation had never happened and never could.

Yara Osei-Kestrel had been off-colony when it happened, on a supply run to a waystation. She'd come home to debris and silence and an official bulletin: Kestrel's Rest, struck by a mining accident. Cascade failure of the reactor. No survivors expected.

"But I knew the reactor specs," she said. "I helped install them. There was no cascade mode that could take out the whole settlement — the containment grid was designed by paranoid people, because hydroponics colonies are one bad day from starving. So I asked questions. And then a fleet liaison came to see me, very kind, very sympathetic, and he explained that grief can make people confused, and that the census records showed Kestrel's Rest had suffered a slow economic decline and been abandoned years earlier. He showed me the records. Official ones. Sealed ones." She laughed, a dry terrible sound. "He showed me a document proving my home had never existed, and he told me that if I persisted in my delusion, I would be remanded for psychiatric evaluation, and that evaluations of persons with no verified identity tend to go... indefinitely."

"They rewrote it around you," Ilsa said. "While you were still standing in the wreckage."

"They rewrote it around *everyone*. My husband was down there. My daughter was seven." The old woman's hands were steady now; that was worse. "Do you know what the worst part was? Not the lying. The *competence* of it. Within a month, the shipping lanes had been redrawn so no route passed near the debris field. Within a year, the star itself had been reclassified in the navigation catalogs — a survey error, wrong spectral type, nothing worth visiting. They didn't just kill them. They tidied up. They made the universe neat."

"Why?" Tam asked. It wasn't an accusation; it was a genuine scientific query, and Ilsa saw Yara register that, saw something in her face ease slightly at being asked a question instead of offered pity.

"Because of the truce," Yara said. "Or whatever they were calling it then — the armistice framework. The colony sat inside a disputed corridor. One faction's ships did the killing; the other faction's leadership agreed to bury it rather than let the atrocity unravel the ceasefire. Eleven thousand people traded for a peace treaty." She looked at Tam. "And now they've done it again, bigger, because the truce is older and more fragile and worth more. Forty thousand this time. You don't have a massacre problem, child. You have a *business model*."

Ilsa felt something shift in her chest — the gaslit vertigo of the past weeks hardening, cooling, taking structure. Grief was a fog. This was a lattice. Same material, different geometry, and a lattice you could climb.

"The tribunal needs evidence," she said. "Physical corroboration. Testimony from children and androids is inadmissible. But you're a documented resident of Concord. Thirty years of tax filings, guild membership, a business license. You exist in the record."

"I exist as Marta Osei," Yara said. "A woman with no origin world. Do you know how many of us there are on this station? People whose histories start abruptly, mid-life, with paperwork gaps we've learned not to talk about? We find each other sometimes. We drink tea. We never say the names."

"How many?" Ilsa asked, though she was almost afraid of the answer.

Yara looked at her for a long moment. Then she reached beneath the counter and brought out a battered ledger — actual paper, unindexed, unsearchable, invisible to any Archive crawler — and laid it open.

"We started keeping count," she said. "Twelve years ago. Names, real names, and the places they came from. Places that don't exist anymore." She turned the pages. Columns of handwriting, some faded, some fresh. Colony names Ilsa had never heard, each one followed by a number. "Two hundred and nineteen survivors of nine erased settlements, living on Concord alone. And that's just who signed. Most won't. Saying your name out loud feels like digging a grave with the person who filled it standing behind you."

Ilsa looked at the ledger and understood what she was holding. Not testimony — the tribunal had been clear about what it wouldn't accept. But a *pattern*. Nine settlements. Nine official stories. Nine sets of documents proving nothing had ever happened, contradicted by two hundred and nineteen living people who had been told, politely, institutionally, that they were grieving things that never existed.

"It's not admissible," she said slowly. "But it doesn't have to be. It has to be *legible*. If I can show the council that the Vesper Gate record follows the exact template of eight prior erasures — same phrasing, same redaction signatures, same reclassification sequence —"

"Then Vesper Gate stops being an anomaly," Tam finished. "It becomes an instance. Tribunals can dismiss an anomaly. Instances require investigation." She said it the way she'd once reported sensor contacts: flat, precise, certain.

Yara Osei-Kestrel closed the ledger but kept her hand on it, the way you keep a hand on something you've guarded a long time and are deciding whether to surrender.

"There's a condition," she said. "When you go before that council — and you will, I can see it in your face, cartographer, you've got the look of a woman drawing a map she means other people to follow — you read the names. All of them. Kestrel's Rest first. Eleven thousand and forty-one. Make the Archive sit in that room and hear a number it spent thirty years pretending was zero."

"I will," Ilsa said. "I swear it."

The old woman nodded once, pushed the ledger across the counter, and began, with the deliberate calm of someone performing a ritual thirty years overdue, to teach Ilsa the pronunciation of a dead world's name.

* * *

Ilsa was halfway back to the docking ring, ledger sealed in a shielded case against her ribs, when her comm chimed with a priority flag from the Long Haul.

August's voice, stripped of its usual evenness: "Cartographer. Dree Halvard's ship just entered Concord approach. She's carrying our sensor cores." A pause, long enough to be an answer to a question Ilsa hadn't asked yet. "And she's not answering hails. Four unidentified vessels are following her in."

Ilsa stopped walking. Around her, the lower ring went on with its evening — vendors calling, children running, two hundred and nineteen ghosts drinking tea in shops with the doors locked — and she pressed the case tighter against her ribs and started to run.
