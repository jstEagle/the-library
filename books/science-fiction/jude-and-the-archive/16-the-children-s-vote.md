## 16. The Children's Vote

The Meridian's mess hall had been built for forty adults. It held eleven children and one woman the way a cupped hand holds water—carefully, aware of everything that could spill.

Tam sat at the head of the long table because no one else wanted it. She was nine years old now, or her body was; her service record said thirty-one years, science officer, third rotation, Vesper Gate survey complement. Both of these were true. That was the whole problem with them.

Ilsa stood at the far end with the tribunal's message open on the wall display, though everyone in the room had already read it twice. The words hadn't changed by being looked at again. Tam checked anyway. Good scientists re-verify.

*Physical evidence only. Testimony from minors and non-persons will not be entered into record.*

Non-persons meant August. Minors meant all of them. Which meant that unless something changed, the only witnesses to the burning of Vesper Gate were legally equivalent to weather.

"We could run," said Okafor, who was six and had been the ship's chief engineer for eleven years before his body caught up to being small. He was turning a torque driver over and over in his hands, the way he did when he was thinking about engines he couldn't fix. "Concord isn't the only station with an independent council. We could go further out. Past the fringe relays."

"And do what?" asked Ndeye, who had been their medic and was now seven and missing two teeth. "Grow up again on some rock nobody's ever heard of? Wait twenty years until we're tall enough to be believed?"

"That's exactly what I'm saying."

"Twenty years." Ndeye said it flat. "Okafor. Twenty *years*. You know what the Archive does in twenty years?"

Nobody answered, because everybody knew. The Archive unremembered things in twenty years. It had already started on Vesper Gate—the official census still showed forty thousand souls thriving on a world that was glass and ash when the Meridian's sensors swept it. Give the Archive two decades and there would be nothing left to testify about even if they grew into bodies the tribunal would listen to. There might not be a tribunal.

"There's another way," Ilsa said.

She'd been holding it back since the message came in. Tam had watched her hold it—watched the cartographer stand very straight with her hands flat on the table like a woman bracing against weather. Ilsa was the only adult among them, technically, which was a strange thing to say about someone who was younger than you. She'd been mission specialist, seconded late, off the ship during the worst of it. She carried her guilt like cargo she refused to set down.

"The Concord medical college has an experimental program," Ilsa said. "Reversal treatment. The same process that made you—" she stopped, restarted. "It can be run forward. Partially. They think. They've never done it on anyone affected by whatever happened out there. The success rate on standard cases is maybe sixty percent. On you, they don't know. And the failures—"

"Ilsa." Tam kept her voice level. Science officer's voice. "Say what the failures are."

Ilsa's hands pressed flatter against the table.

"Cognitive loss. Memory fragmentation. In the worst cases, full dissolution of continuity. You wouldn't die exactly. But the person standing up after the procedure wouldn't be the person who lay down before it."

The mess hall went quiet in the particular way ships go quiet—no silence at all, really, just the ventilation and the recyclers and eleven children not breathing.

Tam looked down the table at her crew. Her crew. She'd signed their duty rosters for six years. She'd logged their sleep cycles and their radiation exposure and once, memorably, the time Okafor had welded his own sleeve to a coolant line and laughed about it for a week. She knew every one of them in two registers: the children they were and the officers they had been, and the terrible overlap between.

Vashti, eight years old, helmsman, who steered them through the debris field at Vesper Gate while the sky came apart. Chen, ten, comms officer, who had tried for six hours to raise anyone, anyone at all, and whose log of those attempts was now evidence nobody would hear. The Adeyemi twins, five and five, life support, who held hands during votes.

They were looking at her. They always looked at her first. She'd never decided whether that was rank or age or just the accident of where she sat.

"All right," Tam said. "Procedural question. Then we vote."

* * *

They voted the way the Meridian had always voted, because Captain Reyes had built it into the ship's charter: any decision that touched the whole crew required a quorum and a recorded division, and no officer—not the captain, not the science officer—could vote on behalf of another. Tam had helped draft the language herself, back when her hands were big enough to reach across a keyboard. She remembered writing it. She remembered meaning it.

"Motion," she said formally, "that the crew of the Meridian submit to reversal treatment under Concord medical authority, for the purpose of qualifying our testimony before the tribunal. Discussion period, ten minutes. Objections heard in order of seniority, which tonight means—" she almost smiled, "—in order of height."

That got a small laugh, which was the point. Fear that can't laugh is fear that curdles.

Chen objected first, as comms officer, on procedural grounds: the sensor cores. "We have physical evidence. The cores recorded everything. Why risk us at all?"

"Because," Tam said, "the Archive will claim the cores are forged. They'll bring experts. They'll bring a hundred years of institutional credibility. Cores can be disputed, Chen. A living witness who says *I saw it, here is my name, here is my service record*—that's harder to unremember. The Archive doesn't have a tool for a face."

"They'll get one," Vashti muttered.

"Probably. But not by Thursday."

Ndeye objected on medical grounds, listing the failure modes with a medic's precision, and then withdrew her objection herself, saying, "For the record, I object, and for the record, I'm voting yes anyway, and both entries are true," which Tam thought was the most honest thing anyone had said aboard the ship in eighteen months.

Okafor didn't object. He just asked, quietly, "If it works—if we're grown up again—do we remember this? Being small? Being scared? Do we remember August carrying us through the debris field in a cargo pod?"

"I don't know," Tam said. "No data."

"Then I want it minuted," Okafor said, "that whatever happens, this part was real. Somebody should keep the record of it even if we can't."

Tam wrote it into the minutes verbatim. Her hand was too small to write fast and the letters came out crooked, and she let them stay crooked. Some records shouldn't be tidy.

* * *

Ilsa found her afterward in the survey bay, which had become Tam's office because it was the only room with a chair her feet reached the floor from. The star charts were still up—Vey's original charts, the deep-survey work that had brought the Meridian out past the fringe in the first place, before the orders changed, before the fleet markings appeared on the ships that burned the colony. Tam liked to look at them. Maps were promises that places existed. Someone owed Vesper Gate its place back.

"You're going to ask me to stop them," Tam said, without turning around.

"I'm going to ask you to stop *yourself*."

Tam turned. Ilsa stood in the doorway with her arms crossed, and behind the crossed arms she was shaking, and Tam pretended not to see because pretending was a courtesy adults extended each other constantly and she was, after all, trained in adult protocols.

"Sixty percent," Ilsa said. "On *standard* cases. You're not standard. None of you are. Whatever they did to you out there—it wasn't medicine, it wasn't an accident, it was a weapon or an experiment or a punishment, and nobody knows how reversal chemistry interacts with it. Four of you could walk out of that clinic as adults. Four of you could walk out as nobody at all." Her voice cracked on the last word and she pushed through it. "You're children, Tam. Whatever else happened, whatever you remember—you're children *now*, and children aren't supposed to—"

"To what?" Tam said. "To choose?"

"To carry this."

"We're not carrying it." Tam slid off the chair and stood, which put the top of her head at Ilsa's sternum, and she made herself look up. It was important, she'd learned, to make people look down at you when you said true things. It disarmed them. "Ilsa. Listen to me carefully, because I'm going to say this once and then I'm going to say it in front of the tribunal if I have to."

She took a breath. Small lungs. She'd forgotten how little air they held.

"We already lived whole lives. All of us. I was thirty-one years old. I calibrated instruments and filed reports and argued with Captain Reyes about sensor drift and taught Okafor to play cards and mourned my mother from four light-years away, and then I woke up in this body and the world started treating me like I'd never happened. Like the person I was was a rounding error." Her voice stayed level. She was proud of that. "The Archive says we're dead. The tribunal says we're not credible. Everyone keeps telling us what we are. This is the first decision anybody's offered us where we get to say what we are ourselves."

"If it fails—"

"Then it fails the way the mission failed. With the record intact." Tam held out her hand, palm up, the way you did for an oath in the old fleet style, the style Captain Reyes used. "You were off the ship when Vesper Gate burned. You've spent eighteen months apologizing for surviving. Don't spend eighteen more apologizing for us. We're not your penance, Ilsa. We're your crew."

Ilsa looked at the small open hand for a long moment. Then she knelt—down to Tam's level, which cost her something, Tam could see it cost her—and took the hand, and shook it once, formally, the way you sealed a duty roster.

"Aye," she said, barely audible. "Crew."

* * *

The vote itself took ninety seconds.

Eleven names called in order of seniority. Eleven responses of *aye*, clear and unhesitating, including the Adeyemi twins, who answered together in unison the way they did everything, and Vashti, who said *aye* so hard it echoed, and Chen, who had raised no one for six hours outside Vesper Gate and said, when his name was called, "Aye. Log it loud."

Unanimous. Tam entered it into the ship's record with the date and the ship's position and the full text of the motion, and then she did something no regulation required: she appended the minutes of the discussion, all of it, Okafor's request about remembering included, because a record that only held the outcome was half a lie.

August was waiting outside the mess hall when they finished. The old android filled the corridor the way bulkheads fill corridors—patiently, permanently—and it had been standing there, Tam suspected, for the entire hour, close enough to hear, far enough to make clear it wasn't part of the crew's deliberations. It was very careful about that. It had spent its whole existence being owned, and it never presumed.

"Well?" August asked.

"Unanimous," Tam said.

The android was quiet for a moment. Its optical shutters did the slow double-blink that Tam had catalogued, early on, as its version of being moved. "I fought a war," August said finally, "beside soldiers who volunteered for things worse than this. I never understood it. I was built to follow orders, and they chose, and I could never model why." It looked down the row of them—eleven small figures in oversized borrowed clothes, standing in the corridor light. "I think I'm starting to."

"Good," said Vashti. "Because somebody has to carry us to the clinic tomorrow, and you're the strongest one aboard."

There was laughter then, thin and real, bouncing down the corridor of a salvage freighter on the fringe of a fractured fleet, and Tam let herself have three seconds of it before she turned back toward the survey bay, because there was one more entry to make in the record.

She sat at the console and opened a new file and titled it the way she'd title any observation: *Anticipated outcomes, reversal cohort, Meridian crew.* Under it she wrote the honest numbers. Sixty percent, adjusted downward for unknown interaction effects. Probability that all eleven survived with continuity intact: low. Probability that the attempt was worth making regardless:

She stopped. Deleted the formula. Wrote instead, in plain language, in her small crooked hand:

*We were the Meridian's crew when we were grown, and we are the Meridian's crew now, and whichever way the bodies come out of the clinic, the testimony stands. Some things you don't calculate.*

She sealed the file, flagged it for the tribunal, and sent it.

The acknowledgment came back in under a minute—which meant someone on Concord was awake, and watching, and had read it. Not the tribunal clerk. The header was wrong for that. It was a routing tag Tam didn't recognize, tagged to a relay she'd never seen in any registry, and attached to it was a single line of text:

*Received. Advise: clinic intake schedule has changed. Your appointment may not be private.*

Tam stared at it, and felt the old officer's reflex come up through the small body like cold water: *someone is listening.*
