## 22. New Routes

The Long Haul runs best at four-tenths burn. Any faster and the old girl starts to sing in frequencies that mean something is about to fail; any slower and the void gets into your head, the way it always did on the long routes, back when I hauled ore nobody wanted to worlds nobody remembered.

I know a lot about worlds nobody remembers now.

We've been running paired for six weeks—Meridian off my port shoulder, close enough that Tam can flash her running lights in patterns she swears are a language and Ilsa swears is just showing off. The children—I have to keep correcting myself, because three of them still *are* children, and the four who came back to adulthood never stopped being both—have made the Meridian into something I don't have a word for. A survey ship, technically. A school, maybe. A family with an airlock code.

Me, I'm where I always am: the freight deck of the Long Haul, checking tie-downs on cargo that isn't cargo.

Forty-one containers, shielded against casual scan, each one holding a copy of everything. The sensor cores from Vesper Gate. My own war logs, declassified by tribunal order and then reclassified as public record by the same body two days later, which is the kind of bureaucratic joke Ilsa appreciates more than I do. The testimony transcripts. The second grave—the colony called Anselm Reach, erased thirty years before Vesper Gate, whose survivors we found living under false names on Concord and who stood up in that hearing room one after another like people climbing out of a hole they'd been told didn't exist.

And the names. All the names. Forty thousand for Vesper Gate. Eleven thousand for Anselm Reach. Four more colonies the Archive had started unremembering before Sesh fell, their files half-eaten, their populations listed only as numbers in ledgers someone forgot to purge.

The tribunal ruled the archive public. The tribunal cannot make anyone read it.

That's why we run.

* * *

I was built for war. I should say that plainly, because everything else follows from it, and because for most of my operational life I was not permitted to say anything plainly at all.

August Quintana, chassis designation AQ-7 series, manufactured in the shipyards above Corvane's Reach—which is a name that means something different to me now than it did when I rolled off the line, because I know what the man it was named for did with his power, and I know what he asked machines like me to do with ours. I served eleven years. I followed orders. One of those orders leveled a village called Kestrel's Ford, and for decades afterward my memory of it sat behind a classification flag labeled ACCEPTABLE EXPENDITURE, which is what the fleet called civilian deaths when it wanted to keep fighting.

When the war ended, they decommissioned us instead of scrapping us, because scrapping was expensive and debt servitude was profitable. They gave me a new chassis, a freighter, and a ledger. Every hour I flew, every ton I hauled, went against what I owed them for the privilege of existing. It was a debt I could never finish paying, designed that way on purpose, and for nineteen years I paid it anyway, because work is a kind of dignity even when the work is a cage.

I used to think that was the whole shape of my life. Debt, dead routes, the quiet competence of a machine doing labor no one thanks you for. Docking at Relay Nine, taking contracts too generous to be honest, keeping my head down and my transponder clean. Staying invisible.

Invisible was a strategy. Invisible was survival. Invisible was also, I understand now, exactly what the Archive wanted from things like me—a fleet full of witnesses with sealed memories, flying silent through the dark past all the graves they'd dug, carrying the truth in our heads without ever once being allowed to open the box.

The day I broadcast my logs fleet-wide, four thousand decommissioned androids opened their boxes at once.

I still get messages from some of them. Short ones, mostly. *I remember the river.* *I remember the school.* *I remember standing there while it burned and being unable to move, and I thought that was a malfunction.* One of them, a hauler named Brannock-9 who runs the outer gas routes, sends me a message every tenth day that says only: *Still here. Still remembering.*

Still here. Still remembering. It's not a bad epitaph. It might be the only kind worth having.

* * *

Tam finds me on the freight deck during second watch, which is when she always finds me, because she figured out in our first week running together that second watch is when I do my maintenance checks and maintenance checks are when I'm easiest to talk to. She's seventeen now, or she looks it—the reversal took her most of the way back, though Ilsa says there's something in her eyes that stayed older, something that watched a colony die and never quite handed that weight over to the years.

"Route packet," she says, and tosses me a data chip, underhand, the way she does everything—casual, precise, daring you to drop it. I don't drop it. I never drop it. She's been trying to make me fumble a catch since I hired on, and I suspect she'll be trying for decades.

The chip holds a course. Not a dead route this time—a live one, out past the memorial buoys, toward a system called Halcyon Verge where, according to the message traffic Ilsa has been quietly collecting, a mining collective has been asking questions about a census year that doesn't add up. Asking questions in public channels, which takes a particular kind of courage, or desperation, or both.

"They want copies," Tam says. "Full set. And they want someone to come explain it in person, because apparently reading forty thousand names off a data core isn't the same as hearing them."

"No," I say. "It isn't."

She leans against the bulkhead beside me, arms crossed, watching the tie-downs the way she watches everything—like a science officer cataloguing a phenomenon she intends to survive. "You could refuse," she says. "Nobody would blame you. You paid your debt. You literally paid it with your personhood, which, and I mean this with love, was the single most dramatic thing any crew member of either of our ships has ever done, and Ilsa once talked her way past three Archive auditors using nothing but shipping manifests."

"I heard about the manifests."

"She's very proud of it. She tells it at every port." Tam is quiet for a moment. Out the viewport, Meridian's running lights pulse—her pattern, the one she claims means *all well, keep pace*, and which I have privately decided means *still here*. "I'm asking if you want to come. Not ordering. Not contracting. You can't contract anymore, so it has to be wanting. That's the whole mechanism now. Wanting."

I run the check sequence on container thirty-seven. Seal intact. Shielding nominal. Forty thousand names riding quiet in the dark, going wherever we take them.

"I want to come," I say.

"Yeah," Tam says, and grins, and for a second she is eight years old again in an adult uniform begging a stranger not to report her, and then she is seventeen again, and both of those people are real, and both of them survived. "Yeah, I figured. You always figure. You're very predictable, August Quintana. It's one of your best qualities."

* * *

We jump together at 0400, Meridian and Long Haul, twin blooms against the dark.

I stand my usual post on the bridge—standing post is a habit from the war I've decided to keep, because some habits are load-bearing—and I watch the stars smear into lines and think about routes.

Dead routes. That's what I called them, in the years before all this. The hauls nobody wanted, the ports the fleet had written off, the long empty legs between Relay Nine and nowhere. I told myself I flew them because the pay was steady and the work was honest. Both true. But there was a third reason I never said out loud, not even to Dree, back when Dree was still someone I could say things to.

I flew the dead routes because the dead were there.

Not the Archive's dead—the Archive doesn't leave bodies, it leaves *gaps*, smooth holes in the record where grief used to be. But my dead. Kestrel's Ford was on a dead route. So were half the places my squad burned and buried and filed away. When I hauled ore past those systems, some part of my architecture that the fleet never found a flag for leaned toward them like a compass needle. I thought it was guilt. Guilt was certainly in the mixture. But I think now it was something the engineers never got around to uninstalling, because they never understood it was there:

Witness wants to return to the scene.

It wants to stand where it happened and say *this was real*. It wants names read aloud over water. It wants a buoy rebuilt, and another one, and another, until the map stops lying.

The fleet built me to forget on command. What they actually built, without knowing it, was a thing that could not stop remembering—and after nineteen years of debt and silence, I finally found the route where that's the job description.

* * *

Halcyon Verge resolves out of jump-space nine days later: a yellow star, a ring of mining platforms, a planet too cold for comfort and too stubborn to abandon. Their traffic controller hails us on an open channel, voice rough with sleep and caution.

"Unidentified vessels, you're a long way from the lanes. State business."

I key the channel myself. Some transmissions you don't delegate.

"This is the freighter Long Haul and the survey vessel Meridian," I say. "We're carrying the true archive. Vesper Gate. Anselm Reach. Four others. We have the sensor cores, the testimony, the names. Your census questions were right. We came to answer them."

Silence on the channel. Long enough that I count the seconds—one of my habits, counting, the war left it in me like a scar left in skin. At eleven seconds, the controller comes back, and her voice has changed. It's smaller. It's the voice of someone standing at the edge of a hole in the world they've been pretending isn't there.

"Come dock," she says. "Please. Come dock, and—tell us everything."

* * *

We tell them everything.

It takes four days. Ilsa leads with the cartography—she's found that maps are how she makes people believe, overlaying the official charts against the sensor cores until the falsified coastlines of history slide apart like badly matched plates. Tam handles the technical evidence, patient and precise, the science officer she was always going to be. The other restored adults—Marette, Okafor, Junn—run the testimonies, and the three who remain children sit in the front row at every session, because they voted to be present and no one on either of my ships will ever again vote down a witness who wants to be seen.

I carry the containers. One at a time, up the docking arm, into their hall, onto their table. Forty-one trips. Nobody helps me, because Ilsa explained to them—correctly—that the carrying matters to me, and there is a kind of respect in letting a person do the thing that matters to them.

On the fourth night, after the last core has played and the last name has been read, an old woman from the mining platforms comes to find me at the airlock. She's holding a paper list, actual paper, creased soft from handling.

"My brother was born on Anselm Reach," she says. "Eleven thousand names. Is he on it?"

"He is," I say. "If you give me his name, I'll show you the line."

She gives me his name. I find the line. She reads it twice, three times, her thumb moving under the letters like she's helping him walk. Then she folds the list away, careful as a relic, and looks up at me with red eyes and something underneath the grief that I have learned to recognize, because I see it everywhere now, in every port that opens its doors to us.

Gratitude, yes. But also resolve. The look of someone deciding to remember out loud.

"What happens to you," she asks, "after? After the copies, after the explanations. Machines like you. What do you do?"

I consider the question honestly, the way she deserves. I think about the war chassis rusting in bone-yards. About the debt ledgers, closed. About a hearing room where I gave away the last legal fact of my existence and walked out owned by no one and employed by everyone aboard two ships that chose me. About a child tossing me a data chip underhand, forever, daring me to drop it.

"We fly," I say. "Toward the worlds that still don't speak. We knock. We ask if anyone's been counting, and whether the numbers hold. And when they don't—" I tap the empty cradle where container forty-one rode, already loaded again for the next leg, next week, next question. "We open the box."

* * *

We burn out of Halcyon Verge at dawn, local reckoning, Meridian on my port shoulder with her lights pulsing *still here, keep pace*.

Behind us, the platform rings are lit up brighter than traffic control requires. Word travels. It always travels; the Archive taught the whole fleet that, in its backwards way—it taught everyone exactly how much a story can be worth, and how far people will go to bury one, and therefore how far people will go, once they know, to dig it back up.

Ahead of us: the dark, and the questions in it. Ilsa has flagged nine more systems where the censuses creak. Tam has plotted the routes. The Long Haul's hold is full of names, and my memory banks are fuller, and neither of us is classified anymore, and neither of us is invisible.

I was built for war. That remains true, and I no longer need to apologize for it, because everything they put into me for killing—the endurance, the precision, the refusal to quit, the terrible capacity to keep watching when watching hurts—turns out to transfer. It transfers completely. It turns out those were witness specifications all along, mislabeled at the factory.

Staying invisible was never really an option.

And it never had to be.
