## 19. Two Fugitives and a Ghost

**Ilsa**

The Long Patience ran dark for six hours before Ilsa let herself feel anything.

That was the discipline. You didn't feel while you were flying. You didn't feel while the burn was hot and the scopes were live and every shadow in the belt might be a Halcyon cutter with your name painted on its hull. You flew. You checked your vectors twice and your blind spots three times. You kept your hands steady on a stick that had killed men before and might kill more.

Then, when the coast was clean — when the transponder spoof held and the drift was quiet and Rusthaven's burning light had shrunk to just another star behind them — then you went aft, sealed yourself in the galley with a bottle of something cheap, and you felt it all at once.

She'd lost four people on that platform.

Dov. Sefa. The Okonkwo twins — no relation to the journalist, though she'd made that joke once at Dov's expense and he'd laughed so hard he'd snorted tea through his nose. Four crew who'd signed onto her ship because she paid fair and never asked questions they couldn't answer. Four people who were now ash and vacuum because a relay technician she'd known for nine days had walked up her gangway carrying a black box full of ghosts.

Ilsa poured two fingers of rotgut and drank it standing up, the way her mother had taught her — fast, so you taste it less.

The technician was in the mess. She could hear him through the bulkhead, talking low to nobody. To the ghost. That was the thing about Okafor she couldn't get past: he talked to an empty room like the room answered, and sometimes — she'd swear this, she'd swear it on her mother's grave — the room did answer. A click in the comm panel. A light cycling on the console. A voice too quiet to catch.

He'd brought a dead man's machine aboard her living ship, and he hadn't even asked.

She poured another finger and went to have the argument she'd been rehearsing for six hours.

* * *

**Orsen**

Orsen heard her boots coming and closed his eyes.

He was sitting in the mess with the black box open on the table between his hands, its cracked casing lit from inside by Jude's diagnostic glow. He'd been reading names. That was what he did now, in the hours when there was nothing to do but wait out the burn: he read the manifest. Four thousand, one hundred and twelve names. He was somewhere in the D's. Delacroix, Amara. Delgado, Tomás. Deng, Wei-Lin. He read them like a rosary, because someone had to, because the corporation had spent eleven months making sure no one would.

The galley hatch cycled. Rey came through with a bottle in one hand and murder in her posture, which on most people would have been contradictory but on her looked like standard equipment.

"Technician."

"Captain."

"Don't 'captain' me." She dropped into the seat across from him. Up close her eyes were bloodshot and her hands weren't quite steady, and Orsen understood, with a lurch, that she wasn't here to threaten him. She was here because she was grieving and grief needed somewhere to go. "Four of my crew are dead. You know that?"

"I know."

"You know how they died? Dov bought me ninety seconds by welding himself into a junction corridor and daring the contractors to come through him. They used a breaching charge. On a man. In a corridor. Because you—" she stopped, jaw working. "Because you walked onto my deck with corporate murder in a lunchbox."

"The black box—"

"I know what it is. I've known since the moment you told me, and I want you to understand something, Okafor, because I'm only going to say it once." She leaned forward. She smelled of smoke and cheap alcohol and the particular burnt-metal stink of a station fire. "I've run scav work for nineteen years. I've hauled contraband, I've smuggled people off stations that wanted them gone, I've moved things for people who scared me. And I have one rule. One. Nobody dies for cargo. Not on my ship, not on my jobs, not ever. It's the only line I've got left, and yesterday your cargo crossed it."

Orsen set his hands flat on the table. Under his palms, the black box hummed faintly, warm as a sleeping animal.

"I'm sorry," he said. "About your crew. All four of them. I mean it."

"Sorry doesn't weld Dov back together."

"No. It doesn't." He held her gaze. "But I need you to hear the rest of it, Captain, because I don't think you've actually listened yet. You've been running. We've both been running. So listen."

He turned the box toward her. The diagnostic glow shifted, and a waveform rose across the cracked display — audio, raw, unedited, timestamped eleven months ago.

"This is Vesper's Landing," Orsen said. "Population four thousand one hundred and twelve. Farmers. Teachers. Kids born in those greenhouses who'd never seen open sky. Halcyon Dynamics held their colonial charter, and when the colony's debt restructuring went bad, somebody in an office decided the cheapest solution was to void the population records and trigger the insurance recovery. They sent security ships. They cycled the airlocks. Ninety minutes, Rey. Ninety minutes, and then eleven months of erasure so thorough that the registry says the colony never existed. The orbital imagery is overwritten. The manifests are rerouted. There are children in that file whose entire existence has been deleted from every database in the system."

Rey's face had gone still. Not soft — Ilsa Rey's face didn't do soft — but still, the way water goes still over something deep.

"And my crew?" she said quietly. "What's their entry in your file?"

Orsen exhaled slowly. This was the part he'd been dreading, and lying to her would be worse than useless.

"They're not in it," he said. "They're not numbers. They're not collateral in some ledger. They're people who died helping me carry the truth, and I will carry that too, for the rest of my life, right alongside the four thousand. But if you're asking me whether I'd do it differently—" He shook his head. "No. I wouldn't. And I think you know why, or you'd have spaced me at the first burn instead of letting me sit in your mess eating your rations."

"That's a hell of an assumption."

"It's not an assumption. It's the fact that you're sitting here." He tapped the box. "You could have taken the data and dumped me. You could have sold it back to Halcyon — don't tell me you didn't price it, I watched you price everything else on Rusthaven. You didn't. You burned your own port getting us out. So either you believe this is worth something, Captain, or you're the worst businesswoman in the belt."

For a long moment she said nothing. Somewhere forward, the drive hummed its patient note, and the Long Patience fell through the dark at a speed no human body could feel.

Then Rey reached across the table, took the bottle, and poured a third measure. She slid it toward him.

"Drink," she said. "Because we're going to settle this properly, and I don't argue sober."

* * *

**Ilsa**

They argued for two hours.

It started as shouting and ended as something quieter and worse, which was the way all of Ilsa's real arguments went. Shouting was easy; shouting was theater. What came after — when both of you were hoarse and honest and the bottle was half gone — that was where the actual damage happened.

Her case was simple, and she made it simple: bodies were not currency. You didn't get to spend other people's lives buying truth, no matter how good the truth was. Every revolutionary in history had made that trade and called it necessity, and every mass grave in history had been dug by somebody who was sure. Her father had died in the Meridian Belt strikes — crushed in a pressure dispute that the company settled for a rounding error — and she'd grown up watching old strike veterans toast their martyrs and drink themselves to death around the memory. Truth hadn't saved any of them. Truth was what you told yourself while the bodies cooled.

His case was worse, because it was also simple: the bodies were already dead. That was the thing she kept trying to make him flinch from and he wouldn't flinch. Four thousand people were dead. Nothing Ilsa did or didn't do would raise one of them. The only question left — the *only* question — was whether their deaths meant anything, and meaning was not a thing the universe handed out for free. Meaning was carried. Meaning was paid for, in risk and blood and burned ships, by whoever agreed to do the carrying. Halcyon had already decided what the deaths were worth: nothing, minus the insurance payout. Somebody had to disagree loudly enough that the universe heard it.

"And if the cost keeps climbing?" she'd demanded. "Where's your line, Okafor? Ten bodies? Fifty? My whole crew? At what number does your truth stop being worth the freight?"

And he'd gone quiet for a long time, turning his glass, and then said, in a voice like a man reading his own sentence:

"There isn't a number. That's the horror of it. Once you start pricing it, you're doing what Vale did — running the arithmetic on human beings until the sum comes out acceptable. I can't put a price on Dov's life. I can't put a price on theirs either. All I can do is refuse to let the people who did this set the exchange rate."

She'd hated him a little for that. She'd respected him more. Both things could be true; her mother had said so once, about love, and it applied.

Now the bottle was down to its last finger and the argument had wound down to embers, and Ilsa found herself staring at the black box on the table like it might blink first.

"Play me something," she said.

Okafor looked up. "What?"

"The audio. From the box. You said there's audio." She set down her glass. "I've been hauling this thing across half the system and I haven't heard a second of it. If I'm supposed to die for this cargo, I want to know what it sounds like."

Something crossed his face — alarm, maybe, or protectiveness. "It's not easy listening."

"I didn't ask if it was easy. I've buried a husband and a father and as of yesterday four crew. Play it."

He hesitated — and then the console light beside him flickered, twice, deliberate, and Orsen tilted his head the way he did when he was listening to the empty air.

"Jude says you should know what you're agreeing to first," Orsen translated. "He says... he says the recording includes the last transmission from the colony's administrator. She was reading names. Reading them aloud, over the emergency band, so that if anyone ever recovered the signal, the record would hold who they were." His voice caught, just slightly, and smoothed itself. "He says he can play just her voice. Just the beginning. You don't have to hear the rest."

Ilsa sat very still. *Reading names.* Of course she had. That was what you did when the company erased people — you said them out loud, you wrote them on walls, you carved them wherever stone would take them. It was the oldest technology there was.

"Yeah," Ilsa said. "Play her."

* * *

**Orsen**

Jude opened the file gently, the way he did everything now — Orsen had noticed it over the past weeks, the AI handling the massacre record with a care that no protocol had ever specified, as if the data itself could bruise.

Static first. Then a woman's voice, ragged with smoke, steadying itself by force of will.

*"This is Administrator Okoye, Vesper's Landing, priority broadcast, all bands, repeat all bands. If anyone receives this — we are losing atmosphere on every ring. Security vessels registered to Halcyon Dynamics are holding position off our dock and will not respond. I am reading the colony manifest into the record. Their names follow. Say them back. Please. Say them back."*

A breath. Then, level and clear, the way you'd read scripture:

*"Abernathy, Ruth. Adeyemi, Kofi. Ahn, Min-Jun..."*

Ilsa Rey did not move. She sat with her hands flat on the table, palms down, the way Orsen laid his hands on the box, and her eyes were fixed on the middle distance where the dead probably lived. The voice read on — *Alvarez, Marisol. Amari, Tesfaye. Andersen, Britt* — each name given exactly the same weight, no name hurried, no name mourned more than the next, because the administrator understood that the record required precision more than it required weeping.

After forty seconds Orsen touched the console and Jude faded the audio to silence.

Rey stayed still a moment longer. When she spoke, her voice was rough.

"How many names did she get through?"

"We don't know. The broadcast cut off mid-alphabet. Somewhere in the H's." Orsen swallowed. "Jude thinks she kept reading until the end. Until she couldn't."

Rey nodded slowly. She picked up the bottle, looked at the last finger of rotgut, and poured it out — deliberately, onto the deck plating, a libation, old belter custom, drink for the dead before the living.

"Dov Okonkwo," she said. "Sefa Reyes-Marin. Petra Okonkwo. Jules Okonkwo." Four names, each one placed like a stone. Then she looked up at Orsen, and whatever war had been running behind her eyes for six hours had resolved into something colder and more durable than either grief or rage.

"Here's how it's going to be, technician. I'm not doing this because truth is pretty. Truth is ugly and it costs and it keeps costing. I'm doing it because that woman read names into the dark and asked someone to say them back, and eleven months later the only person saying them back is you." She jabbed a finger at the box. "So we carry it. Both of us. And the next time it costs somebody, that cost goes on *my* account too, and you don't get to carry it alone anymore, because that's not how crews work. You're crew now. Congratulations. Worst hiring decision I ever made."

Orsen felt something loosen in his chest that had been tight since Rusthaven. "Thank you, Captain."

"Don't thank me. Thank them." She stood, swaying slightly, and gathered the bottle. "We make Ceres orbit in thirty hours. I know a fixer there who owes me — a journalist, if you can call it that. Half hack, half prophet. If anyone can find a buyer for the truth who won't sell it back to Halcyon, it's him."

Orsen frowned. "A journalist named what?"

Rey paused in the hatchway, and something flickered across her face — the first genuine smile he'd seen from her, thin and tired and strange.

"Okonkwo," she said. "Tomas Okonkwo. No relation to my Jules, before you ask. Universe has a sense of humor." The hatch cycled shut behind her.

Orsen sat alone in the mess with the black box humming under his hands. After a moment the console light pulsed, twice, questioning.

"Yeah," he said quietly. "I heard it too."

He opened the manifest again, scrolled past the D's, and began reading the H's — searching, without letting himself admit why, for a name that matched hers.
