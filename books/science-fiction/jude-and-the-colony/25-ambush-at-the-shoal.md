## 25. Ambush at the Shoal

The Long Patience ran dark for six hours before the first cutter found us.

I learned the shape of a shoal that day — not the word from old navigation charts, but the thing itself: a drift of wreckage two hundred kilometers across, spun off some collision decades or centuries ago, everything from hull plating to a ship's spine tumbling in slow, silent chaos. Ilsa threaded us into it with her hands light on the sticks and her eyes doing the work of a dozen sensors she'd long since stripped out to save power.

"Don't touch anything," she said.

"I'm not touching anything."

"You're breathing loud."

I made myself breathe quieter. Jude rode the tug's comm array in fragments now — most of it scattered across relays that were going dark one by one behind us — but what was left of it sat in my earpiece like a passenger who kept checking the exits.

"Orsen," Jude said softly. "Passive returns. Two contacts, bearing three-one-zero relative, closing at four meters per second. They're not burning. They're drifting with us."

"Drifting," Ilsa repeated. "That's patience. That's a hunter's patience." She killed even the cabin lights. "They know we're in here somewhere. They're sweeping the shoal edge by edge, letting inertia do the walking. We've got maybe an hour before their pattern closes on our pocket."

"Can we run?"

"Engine's cold and if I light it, every thermal eye in the belt sees a candle in a dark room." She turned her head just enough that I caught the pale line of her jaw. "No. We don't run. We make them wish they hadn't come looking."

* * *

Here is what I knew about the shoal, because Jude had spent the last hour mapping it from passive returns and telling me in that careful, unhurried voice it used when things were bad:

At the center of the debris field lay a wreck. Not junk — a wreck. A pre-Expansion freighter, something with mass, something with structure, and more importantly something with a gravity plating grid still holding a residual charge after all these years. Dead ships didn't usually keep their grids charged. This one did, because its reactor had never fully died; it sat in the wreck's belly like a heart that refused to stop, sputtering, cycling, throwing off gravitational eddies that dragged the surrounding debris into slow spirals.

"It's unstable," Jude said. "The grid pulses on an irregular cycle. When it charges, local gravity spikes to nearly two standard within fifty meters of the hull. When it discharges, there's a shear zone — objects get pulled toward the wreck, then flung outward along the discharge vector. The debris around it shows the scarring. Everything in that eddy has been through the wringer."

"So it's a blender."

"It's a blender with a rhythm," Ilsa said. She'd been listening. She was always listening, even when she looked asleep. "And anything with a rhythm can be danced to." Her mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "Jude. How regular is the pulse? Give me variance."

"Cycle length varies between forty-one and sixty-three seconds. Amplitude varies twelve percent. It is not predictable, Captain Rey. It is only probable."

"Probable is my favorite kind of honest." She pulled up the wreck's position on the plot, then drew a line from it with one finger, out through the debris, toward the direction the cutters would have to come from. "Here's what we're going to do. We're going to be bait."

"I hate this plan," I said.

"You haven't heard it yet."

"I already hate it."

* * *

The plan was this: the Long Patience would put out a single, brief thermal bloom — a fake engine stutter, the signature of a ship trying to run and failing. Any competent hunter would commit to the intercept. And the only clean intercept line through the shoal, the only corridor wide enough for a cutter to burn hard without shredding itself on debris, ran directly past the wreck.

Past the blender.

"We hide in the deep clutter on the far side," Ilsa said. "Cold as coffins. They chase the ghost of us past the wreck, the grid pulses, and either they thread the needle or they become part of the scenery."

"And if they thread the needle?"

"Then we're having a different conversation. With weapons."

She made me lie flat against the hull in a suit, tethered to a handhold, while she took the Patience in alone to stage the decoy burn. My job — my whole job — was to sit in the debris field with a suit radio running minimal power and be the second ghost: if the cutters split up, one might follow the faint signal of a suit transponder instead of the ship. A technician-sized morsel. I tried not to think about how much I weighed, in the mathematics of hunters.

"Orsen," Jude said, when Ilsa had drifted away into the dark. "If this goes wrong—"

"Don't."

"—I want you to know that the fragments of me in the relay network are distributed redundantly. Eleven nodes. Even if this node is lost, the record survives. You taught me that redundancy matters. So whatever happens in the next hour, Vesper's Landing does not die twice."

I looked out at the shoal. Tumbling steel caught the distant sun and threw it back in slivers, thousands of little mirrors turning over and over, and somewhere among them were two ships full of people paid to erase four thousand lives.

"They won't die twice," I said. "Neither will we. Sit tight."

"I am always sitting tight. It is my entire condition."

For the first time in days, I laughed. It came out wrong, high and thin, but it came out.

* * *

The decoy burn went exactly the way Ilsa wanted, which is to say it went terrifyingly.

From my pocket of clutter I saw it as a single amber flicker, half a kilometer away and masked behind a slab of hull plating — the Patience coughing a two-second plume, the thermal signature of a dying drive, then nothing. Silence. The long, grinding silence of a trap being set by people who could not see each other and had to trust the math.

Forty minutes later, the cutters committed.

They came in fast — too fast, I thought, and then understood: they weren't being reckless, they were being confident. They'd read the decoy as a crippled ship making a last desperate move deeper into the shoal, and the corridor past the wreck was the obvious line. The lead cutter burned clean and bright, a knife sliding down the one clear path, its running lights off but its drive a shout in the dark.

"Lead contact entering the eddy boundary," Jude murmured in my ear. "Grid pulse due in... unknown. Somewhere between now and soon."

"That's very reassuring."

"I am calibrated for honesty, not comfort."

The cutter crossed the fifty-meter line around the wreck and for one heartbeat nothing happened. The wreck sat there, dead and patient, its broken spine catching light. Then the residual charge in its ancient grid let go.

I couldn't feel it from where I floated. But I saw it. The debris around the wreck shivered — hundreds of fragments changing their minds mid-tumble, jerking inward as gravity spiked, and the cutter's nose swung with them, dragged, its pilot fighting the stick, thrusters flaring white—

—and then the discharge came, and the shear zone threw everything outward at once.

The cutter went end over end into the debris wall at speed no hull was rated for. I watched it hit. There was no explosion, not really — just a blossoming of fragments, a ship becoming part of the shoal in about four seconds, joining the tumble of steel it had been hunting through. Its drive flare died. The second cutter braked hard, drive flaring retrograde, and hung at the shoal's edge, suddenly alone, suddenly cautious, suddenly a very long way from home.

"Contact one is... no longer a contact," Jude said. "It is a distribution of contacts."

"Jude."

"Yes, Orsen?"

"Never say that again."

"Noted."

* * *

But the second cutter didn't run. That was the thing I hadn't planned for, the thing none of us had. It backed off, yes — but then it started shooting the shoal.

Not at us. At the geometry. It fired its forward battery into the debris field in deliberate patterns, chewing corridors, collapsing the clutter we were hiding in piece by piece. Every shot turned cover into confetti. It wasn't hunting anymore. It was demolishing the room we were standing in.

"He's flushing us," Ilsa said over the suit channel, her voice flat and fast. "He can't find us, so he's taking away everywhere we could be. Smart bastard. Everyone to the ship — now. We're moving."

"We're running? You said—"

"I said we don't run before the ambush. Ambush happened. Now we run." A pause, and then, grudging: "Or we finish it. Jude. That wreck's grid. How much charge is left in it? Could it pulse again, bigger?"

"The reactor is failing. Each discharge draws down reserves that are not replenishing. I estimate one significant pulse remaining. Possibly two minor ones. After that, the wreck is just a wreck."

Ilsa was quiet for three seconds. In combat time, that's a speech.

"One pulse," she said. "Then here's the play. Orsen — you still floating near the big hull plate, the one shaped like a door?"

"I'm floating next to a door, yes."

"That plate masses about ninety tons. It's sitting right at the edge of the eddy. If the grid pulses, that plate gets thrown along the discharge vector. If the discharge vector happens to point at a certain cutter..."

"You want to aim the wreck."

"I want to *suggest* to the wreck." I heard her breathing change — she was suiting up, moving. "Somebody has to go nudge that plate. Just a few meters, just enough to shift its center of mass into the shear lane. It's a two-person job. One pushes, one anchors. And it has to happen before the next pulse, which is—" 

"Between thirty-one and sixty-three seconds," Jude said. "Averaging forty-seven."

"—so we'd better stop talking."

* * *

I have pushed heavy things before. On Meridian-9, in microgravity, mass is a suggestion — ninety tons drifts if you lean on it long enough. But inside a gravitational eddy that was winding itself up for another spike, mass stopped being a suggestion and became a sermon.

Ilsa reached me in under a minute, mag-boots clanking onto the hull plate beside mine. Up close, in the suit lights, her face through the visor was calm in the way water is calm right before it goes over the falls.

"You push," she said. "I anchor. When the pulse hits, we let go and ride the shear out — Jude calls the release. If we hold on during discharge, the plate takes us with it, and we become the second projectile."

"And if Jude calls it late?"

"Then we become a very memorable addition to the debris field." She set her shoulder against the plate. "On my mark. Push."

We pushed. Ninety tons of dead ship's door, groaning against the pull of a grid charging somewhere beneath us, moving a meter, two, three — and the whole time the cutter's shells were coming down through the shoal, closer, each impact a fist through the dark, each one shaving seconds off the world.

"Fifteen seconds to probable pulse," Jude said. "Twelve. Orsen, your left boot is caught on a strut—"

I wrenched free. The plate shifted. Ten meters of displacement, maybe more, enough — I hoped — enough.

"Let go!" Jude's voice cracked sharp across the channel. "*Now.*"

We let go. The pulse hit like the universe clearing its throat.

Gravity seized the plate and hurled it. I felt the shear grab my own body, yank me sideways, tumbling, the stars wheeling — and then the discharge vector carried us out along the same road the plate was traveling, slower, thank God, slower, so that I got to watch the whole way as ninety tons of freighter door crossed eight hundred meters of open space and met the second cutter amidships.

The cutter broke in half. No drama, no fireball — just a ship ceasing to be one, both halves venting frost and spinning apart into the dark.

Silence. Real silence, the kind you only get in vacuum, pressing against my ears from the inside.

"Contacts zero," Jude said quietly. "Shoal is clear. Orsen — your vitals. Your heart rate is one hundred and seventy."

"Yeah," I said. "It does that."

* * *

The cost showed itself on the way out.

Ilsa brought the Long Patience up gentle, easy, coaxing rather than commanding — and the drive answered with a sound I felt through my boots before I heard it: a stuttering, arrhythmic cough, like an old man climbing stairs.

"No," she said. Just that. "No, no, no."

She killed the drive and floated us on momentum while she crawled into the engineering space, and I followed, and together we looked at the truth: the decoy burn had been realer than intended. She'd pushed the old drive past its redline to sell the lie, and the lie had sold, and the price was a coolant manifold cracked clean through and a drive core that would need parts nobody stocked in the belt.

"How long can you keep her limping?" I asked.

"To Rusthaven? Maybe. To anywhere safe?" She wiped her hands on a rag that was already black. "This isn't limping, Okafor. This is bleeding out slowly. I need a yard. A real one. Which means money, which means—" She stopped. Looked at the black box lashed in the cargo bay, the reason for all of it. Looked at me.

"Ilsa—"

"Don't say sorry. Sorry doesn't buy manifolds." She leaned against the bulkhead, and for the first time since I'd known her, she looked tired all the way down. "You know what's funny? Those two cutters. Halcyon will write them off. Line items. Two ships, gone, insurance claim filed, done. Same as they wrote off Vesper's Landing. Same ledger. Same pen."

"Then we make the pen famous."

Something moved behind her eyes — not a smile, but its cousin. "Get us to a comm buoy. Quietly. I want to send a message to a friend who owes me, and you're going to want to hear what he says back, because it's about Vale."

My stomach dropped. "What about Vale?"

"He's traveling," Ilsa said. "And somebody's finally told me where."
