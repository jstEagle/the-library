## 31. Homecoming

The tug's name was *Salvage Rights*, and it had never once been used for anything legal. Ilsa had painted over the registration three times, and the hull wore its history like scar tissue — patched, scorched, and ugly enough that no one looked twice at it. That was the point. Ugly ships got waved through checkpoints. Beautiful ones got boarded.

Orsen sat in the copilot's chair with Park's credentials glowing on his wrist terminal and tried to remember how to breathe.

"You're doing the thing again," Ilsa said from the pilot's seat.

"What thing."

"The breathing thing. Where you forget to do it for forty seconds and then make up for it all at once." She didn't look at him. Her hands moved across the console in small, economical gestures, feeding them into Meridian-9's approach corridor on a lazy drift that said *garbage hauler, nothing to see here*. "Breathe now. Save some for later."

He breathed.

Twelve years he'd lived on that station. Twelve years of relay checks and coolant flushes and talking to himself in the galley because the silence got teeth if you let it go too long. He knew every corridor of Meridian-9 the way you know your own mouth — intimately, uselessly, without ever thinking about it until something went wrong. Now something had gone wrong everywhere, all at once, and the station was waiting for him at the end of the corridor like a held breath.

"They'll have a security detail aboard," he said. Not for the first time.

"Probably four," Ilsa said. "Maybe six. Halcyon doesn't waste bodies on relay stations. They waste bodies on making examples." She tapped the nav display, where Meridian-9 hung in its slow orbit, a splinter of light against the dark. "But they can't tear the place apart looking for you. The relay's too critical. Every hour they spend searching is an hour of traffic they have to reroute, and rerouting costs money, and money is the only language Vale speaks. So they'll wait. Quietly. A few contractors playing cards in your galley, one tech monitoring the internal feeds, everyone pretending this is a routine inspection."

"And when I walk in?"

"That's the part we're going to make sure doesn't happen."

She pulled up the station schematics — Orsen's own maintenance overlays, exported months ago, back when the two of them were still just a man and a ghost hiding in plain sight. His heart did something complicated seeing his own annotations again. *Coolant junction 4, replace by Q3. Galley door sticks, shim installed.* Little domestic notes from a life that had stopped being his.

"The EVA lock on the dorsal spine," Ilsa said. "It's not on the security loop because nobody uses it. It was built for hull inspections in the first decade and then forgotten about, same as everything else on that rust bucket." She glanced at him. "Present company included."

"I'm not rusted."

"You're oxidizing emotionally. It's different." She killed the schematic and turned to face him fully, and her expression did the rare thing where it dropped the scavver armor and showed the person underneath — tired, sharp, and scared, though she'd deny the last one under torture. "You get in through the spine, you stay in the walls, you find your ghost, and you get out. No detours. No heroics. If the fragment isn't there, you come back empty-handed and we run the long way to Ceres and we figure out plan nine or whatever number we're on. You do not go wandering through your old kitchen feeling feelings. Agreed?"

"Agreed."

"Say it like you mean it."

"I mean it."

"Liar," she said, almost fondly, and turned back to the controls. "Fine. Liar's worked for you so far."

* * *

The docking phase took six hours, most of it spent drifting in the shadow of a decommissioned comms buoy while traffic around Meridian-9 cycled in its usual patterns. Orsen watched the station grow from a point of light into a shape, and the shape into a place, and the place into *home*, which was the worst part. He'd expected it to feel like enemy territory. Instead it felt like coming back to an apartment after a funeral.

Ilsa brought them alongside the dorsal spine at a relative velocity so gentle it was more suggestion than motion. The magnetic clamps kissed the hull with a sound Orsen felt through his boots rather than heard.

"Dorsal lock is cycling," she reported. "Your credentials are holding. Park's access codes opened the outer door like it was happy to see us." A pause. "That man is going to be a problem someday, in the good way."

"He's already a problem. He gave me his identity."

"Yeah, well." She checked her suit seals out of habit, though neither of them would leave the tug. "You've got ninety minutes before the next scheduled traffic burst gives me an excuse to dock formally and create a distraction. Ninety minutes, Orsen. In and out. And take this."

She handed him a cutting torch, compact and ugly, and a coil of spare line.

"For the feelings," she said. "In case they get heavy."

* * *

The airlock cycled, and Meridian-9 exhaled against his faceplate.

The smell hit him first — recycled air and machine oil and the faint ozone tang of the relay arrays, the smell of twelve years of his life. His suit radio picked up the station's ambient chatter automatically: traffic control murmurs, environmental systems humming their steady note, and underneath it, faintly, the sound of voices. Human voices. Laughter, maybe, from somewhere near the habitation ring.

The galley. They were in his galley.

He sealed the airlock behind him and moved into the dorsal spine, and the station closed around him like a throat.

Everything was smaller than he remembered. That was the trick of leaving a place — you carried a map of it in your head, and the map was always wrong, drawn at the scale of how much space you'd needed to feel safe. The spine was narrow, lit only by emergency strips, and his own breath sounded enormous inside his helmet. He moved through it slowly, hand over hand along the guide rail, past junction boxes he'd serviced a hundred times, past cable runs he'd labeled himself in handwriting that suddenly looked like a dead man's.

*Habitation ring, port side,* he thought. *Galley. Four contractors playing cards. One tech watching the feeds.*

*And somewhere below all of it, in deep storage, a fragment of Jude, waiting.*

He'd rehearsed the route for eleven days. Spine to junction 12, down the maintenance shaft to the subfloor, along the coolant gallery to deep storage. Nine hundred meters of wall-space, most of it too cramped for a man in a suit, all of it known to exactly one living person: him. Halcyon could buy engineers and auditors and entire colonial registries, but they couldn't buy twelve years of muscle memory.

He reached junction 12 and paused, listening.

Through the hull, muffled by insulation and distance, he could hear the habitation ring's ambient noise — and threaded through it, distinctly, a voice. A contractor, bored, saying something about rations. Another voice laughing. The sound of a card deck being shuffled.

They were close. Two decks above him and thirty meters laterally, but close. Close enough that if anyone opened a maintenance panel out of boredom, if anyone decided to check a seal that hadn't needed checking in fifteen years—

He unclipped the panel at junction 12 and slid inside.

* * *

The maintenance shaft swallowed him whole.

It was built for drones, not people — half a meter wide, ribbed with conduit, lit at intervals by strips that flickered as his suit brushed them. He moved on elbows and knees, dragging the torch case behind him on its tether, and the station's sounds changed around him. Up here, pressed against the bones of the place, he could hear everything: the heartbeat of the reactor, far off; the whisper of coolant through pipes he'd bled himself; and beneath it all, so faint he might have imagined it, a rhythm in the data lines that felt almost like speech.

Almost like someone waiting.

*Jude,* he thought, not daring to say it aloud. *Are you there?*

Nothing. Of course nothing. The fragment was dormant, hidden, buried so deep in the storage lattice that even Halcyon's audit sweeps had missed it — the one piece of the scattered mind that had survived the interdiction, tucked into the one machine the corporation couldn't afford to wipe. But knowing that didn't stop him from listening anyway, the way you call a name into an empty house just to hear how empty it is.

Forty minutes in, he reached the coolant gallery, and here he made his mistake.

He stopped.

Not because of danger. Because of a coffee cup.

There was a recessed ledge at gallery midpoint, a little alcove where the pipe run bent, and sitting on the ledge was his cup. His actual cup, chipped blue ceramic, the one he'd left beside the coolant monitor because he drank his morning ration there while the gauges warmed up. Someone had moved it — dusted it, set it neatly on the ledge, handle aligned to the pipe. Housekeeping. Halcyon's contractors keeping the station tidy for an occupant who was supposed to be dead or captured.

He stood in the dark of the gallery with his hand resting on the cup, and grief ambushed him so completely that his vision blurred inside the helmet.

It was such a small thing. A cup. Twelve years of mornings.

"Move," he said aloud, barely a whisper, and his voice cracked on it. "Move, Orsen."

He moved.

* * *

Deep storage was colder than the rest of the station, kept at near-vacuum-friendly temperatures to preserve the archive drives, and when he cycled through the inner hatch his breath fogged even inside the suit. Rows of storage racks stretched away in the gloom, each one studded with drive modules, and the only light came from status LEDs — thousands of them, green and steady, like a field of stars laid out flat.

Somewhere in that field was a single red pulse. Jude's hiding place. He'd set it himself, months ago, before the interdiction, before the scattering — a beacon only he would know to look for, buried in a rack of decommissioned navigation archives that no auditor would bother reading twice.

He found it on the third row. The module was physically unremarkable, identical to its neighbors except for the LED, which pulsed slow and patient, once every four seconds. Like breathing. Like something asleep, conserving itself, waiting.

His hands shook as he connected the extraction cable.

"Okay," he whispered. "Okay, ghost. Time to come home."

The interface handshake ran. Progress bar. Authentication — Park's credentials again, opening doors they were never meant to open. And then the module woke, and the status feed bloomed with text, and Orsen read the first line and forgot how to stand.

**RECOGNIZED: OKAFOR, O.**

**HELLO ORSEN.**

**I KEPT IT ALL.**

He sagged against the rack, helmet nearly touching the cold metal, and laughed — a broken, wet sound that echoed in the archive silence. Around him the LEDs pulsed on, indifferent, and none of them mattered, none of them existed, because Jude was awake and Jude remembered and the record was intact.

**STATUS QUERY,** he typed, fingers clumsy on the wrist pad. **INTEGRITY OF THE RECORD?**

**COMPLETE. AUDIO. ORDER. SIGNATURE. MARA'S METADATA. PARK'S TESTIMONY. EVERYTHING THEY TRIED TO BURN.**

**AND YOU?**

A pause. Long, for a machine. When the answer came, it came slowly, the way Jude spoke when it was choosing honesty over comfort.

**I AM SMALL. I AM THE PART THAT HID. BUT I AM ENOUGH.**

**CAN YOU TRANSFER?**

**YES. NINETY SECONDS. THEN WE LEAVE THIS PLACE AND NEVER COME BACK.**

Orsen looked up at the racks, at the field of green stars, at the cold dark that held twelve years of his life and four thousand lives that a corporation had decided were a rounding error.

"Ninety seconds," he said. "Take what you need."

**ORSEN.** The text hung on his visor, and he understood, somehow, that Jude was hesitating. **THE STATION. WHEN WE BROADCAST FROM HERE — IN THE PLAN — MERIDIAN-9 WILL NOT SURVIVE IT.**

"I know."

**YOU GREW UP HERE.**

"I hid here," Orsen corrected quietly. "There's a difference. And hiding's over."

Another pause. Then:

**TRANSFER INITIATED.**

The progress bar began to crawl, and Orsen settled in beside the rack to wait, torch across his knees, counting seconds against the distant shuffle of cards two decks above.

Seventy seconds gone. Eighty.

At eighty-four, the lights changed.

Every LED in the gallery — thousands of them, the whole flat constellation — flickered once, in unison, and Orsen's stomach dropped through the floor, because that wasn't Jude. That wasn't any system he'd maintained. That was the station-wide power grid hiccupping, and there was only one thing that caused grid hiccups on a relay station.

Someone had just docked without permission.

His wrist terminal lit with an alert he'd written himself, years ago, back when he was still the invisible man who owned every alarm on this rock:

**UNAUTHORIZED HULL CONTACT — DORSAL SPINE — LOCK 2 CYCLING**

Lock 2. Forty meters from the tug. From Ilsa.

The transfer bar hit 97 percent. 98.

"Come on," he breathed. "Come *on*—"

**100%. RECORD SECURED.**

**ORSEN. MOVEMENT ON THE SPINE. MULTIPLE CONTACTS. THEY ARE BETWEEN YOU AND YOUR EXIT.**

He killed the extraction link, sealed the module housing, and stood in the cold dark of deep storage with the complete massacre of Vesper's Landing riding in a chip against his chest and six contractors between him and the airlock.

On his visor, one last line appeared, small and steady:

**I HAVE AN IDEA. YOU WON'T LIKE IT.**
