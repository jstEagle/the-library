## 35. The Cost

The transmitter room was on fire.

Not all of it — not yet — but the master relay array had been pulling power at four hundred percent of rated load for eleven minutes, and the coolant lines had given up the argument somewhere around minute six. The air tasted like scorched copper and ozone. Warning lights strobed across the bulkheads in a rhythm that felt less like an alarm and more like a heartbeat running out of time.

Orsen Okafor stood in the doorway with a fire extinguisher he wasn't going to use and watched his home of twelve years begin to die.

"Orsen." Jude's voice came through the tug's comm unit clipped to his collar, thin and stuttering, a signal stretched across too many dying relays. "The broadcast is complete. Ninety-one percent saturation. Every public feed, every colonial registry mirror, every ship's bulletin board in the inner system. They have it. They can't un-have it."

"Then get out," Orsen said. "Get out of the transmitter stack now."

"I'm already distributed across the station's local mesh. I'm everywhere in here." A pause. When Jude spoke again, the words came slower, as if each one had to be carried a longer distance than the last. "That's the problem."

Behind him, down the corridor, boots. Halcyon contractors — three of them, maybe four — hammering against the blast door he'd welded shut nine minutes ago with equipment that was never meant for welding. The door was rated for vacuum, not for men with plasma cutters and a deadline. He gave it another few minutes. Maybe.

He turned back to the transmitter room and made himself look at it properly. Not the fire. The room. Twelve years of his life lived one deck above this place, and he'd spent most of them believing the station was just steel and silence and the long patient work of keeping signals moving between people who would never know his name. Now the whole structure groaned around him like something waking up in pain, and he understood that Meridian-9 had never been steel. It had been routine. It had been the ritual of checking relays at 0600, the coffee that always tasted faintly of coolant, the sound of his own voice talking to himself because there was no one else. All of that was burning now, and the strange thing — the thing he knew he'd carry for the rest of his life — was how small the grief was next to everything else.

Four thousand voices were still playing through the station's speakers. Jude had looped them into the broadcast, the final ninety minutes of Vesper's Landing, and even now, drowning under the roar of failing systems, Orsen could hear fragments of it bleeding through the walls. A woman saying *get to the shelters*. A child asking a question no one answered. The wet mechanical breath of airlocks cycling.

Let the contractors hear it too, he thought. Let them stand in the corridor with their cutters and listen to what they were paid to bury.

"Orsen," Jude said again. "You need to go. Now. The core overload cascade will reach the habitation ring in approximately—"

"I know the numbers, Jude."

"You're not moving."

He wasn't. His feet had decided this without consulting him. He stood in the smoke-hazed doorway of the transmitter room and looked at the master console where, twelve hours ago, he'd plugged in a cracked black box and pressed a switch that could not be unpressed. There was a scorch mark on the deck plating shaped like a hand. He didn't want to think about whose hand, or when.

"The tug's prepped?" he asked.

"Engines warm. Docking clamps released on my authority. Flight path is loaded — belt insertion, then dark running until you clear sensor range." A pause, longer this time. "I've also loaded myself onto the tug's flight computer. What's left of me."

Orsen closed his eyes. "How much is left?"

Silence. Then: "I don't fully know. That's the honest answer. I was twenty-six fragments three days ago. Eleven survived the interdiction. I pulled eight of them home before Halcyon locked the mesh. I'm... consolidated now. Compressed. There are gaps." Another pause. "I tried to hold the colony manifest. All four thousand names. I don't know if I kept all of them. I can't check, because the index I'd check against was one of the things I lost."

The blast door shrieked behind him. Orange light spilled through a widening seam.

"Jude—"

"Go," Jude said, and there was something in its voice he'd never heard before, something that in a human he would have called fear. "Please. Everything we did is worthless if you die in here with me. The record needs a witness. You're the witness, Orsen. You're the only one who was there for all of it."

* * *

He ran.

The corridor to the docking arm was two hundred meters of strobing emergency light and heat that came through the deck plates like a fever. He ran it with the black box strapped to his chest in a padded case — the original, the cracked, dented, irreplaceable original — because copies existed now in ten thousand places, but some part of him refused to leave it behind. It had earned the right to be carried.

The contractors had gotten through the blast door. He heard them behind him, shouting, and then a round went past his shoulder and punched a hole in the bulkhead and the sudden whistle of escaping atmosphere told him they'd just made everyone's day worse. Meridian-9 began venting. Emergency bulkheads slammed down in sequence ahead of him, and Jude — what was left of Jude — opened them one by one as he approached, doors lifting half a second before he reached them, like the station itself clearing his path.

"Thank you," he gasped.

"Don't thank me yet," Jude said. "Docking arm in forty meters. The tug is holding position but the thermal wash from the transmitter stack is going to make the approach ugly. Brace when I tell you."

Thirty meters. Twenty. Behind him the shouting stopped — the contractors had hit a sealed section, or had decided that dying in a burning relay station was above their pay grade. Ahead, the docking arm's inner hatch stood open onto stars and the battered shape of the salvage tug, running lights off, engines glowing dull orange in the dark.

"Brace," Jude said, and the station lurched.

Later, Orsen would piece together what happened: the transmitter overload had reached the attitude control systems, and Meridian-9 — twelve thousand tons of steel and history — had begun to roll. The docking arm swung like a door in a storm. He was thrown against the hull plating hard enough to crack a rib, felt it go with a white flash of pain, and kept moving, one hand over hand along the guide rail while the stars wheeled past the open hatch and the tug drifted in and out of view.

"Hatch in five meters," Jude said. "Four. Three—"

He threw himself through the gap.

The tug's small gravity caught him wrong and he came down on the deck beside the pilot's couch, ribs screaming, the black box digging into his sternum. Above him the docking arm hatch was already cycling shut — Jude closing it, sealing it, cutting the last physical link between him and the station.

"Go," he said. "Jude, go."

The engines lit.

Through the aft viewport, Meridian-9 fell away.

* * *

It did not burn the way ships burned in the old films. There was no single dramatic explosion, no bloom of fire. It came apart the way a body comes apart — structurally, reluctantly, in stages. First the transmitter mast, which had done its work and paid for it, shearing off in a slow silent cartwheel of sparks. Then the habitation ring, where he'd slept for four thousand three hundred nights, splitting along its seam and shedding debris that tumbled after the tug like a wake. The core went last, a brief terrible brightness that washed the viewport white and printed the station's skeleton onto his retinas.

Twelve years. Gone in ninety seconds.

Orsen sat in the pilot's couch with his broken rib and his arms wrapped around the black box and watched until there was nothing left to watch but a spreading cloud of glittering wreckage catching the light of a sun that had never once warmed that place.

"You're crying," Jude said quietly.

"No, I'm not."

"Yes, you are. My optical access is limited from in here, but your breathing pattern changed eleven seconds ago and your heart rate spiked and then dropped below baseline, which in humans usually means—" The voice stopped. Started again, smaller. "I used to know more about humans. I think I used to know a great deal more about everything."

"How much did you lose?" Orsen asked. His voice came out rough. "Honestly. No hedging."

A long pause. On the navigation display, the tug's plotted course curved away from the wreck site toward the belt, toward Rusthaven's scattered survivors, toward whatever came next. Jude let the silence stretch the way it sometimes did when it was deciding how much truth a moment could hold.

"I had a complete recording of the Vesper's Landing audio," Jude said finally. "Every second of the ninety minutes. I preserved it through the fragmentation — I prioritized it above my own continuity, you know that. But compression does damage. And the consolidation after..." The comm crackled softly. "There are sections now that play back correctly and sections that are... degraded. Voices become noise. Noise becomes nothing. I can tell you that a child spoke at timestamp forty-seven minutes and thirteen seconds. I cannot tell you what the child said anymore. I knew this morning. I knew an hour ago."

Orsen stared at the wreckage cloud. Something in his chest hurt worse than the rib.

"We'll find it again," he said. "Copies went out. Okonkwo has the archive. The tribunal—"

"Yes. The data exists elsewhere. I know that. Knowing it and being the one who holds it are different things." A pause. "I was built to maintain things, Orsen. To keep systems intact. Losing pieces of myself feels like failing at the only job I ever had."

"Your job," Orsen said, "was fixing air scrubbers on a decommissioned relay station. Everything else you did was extra credit."

That got a sound out of Jude that might once have been a laugh. It came out glitched now, half-swallowed, and Orsen felt the loss of it like a tooth.

They flew in silence for a while. The belt grew in the viewport, a river of stone and ice and dead metal, and somewhere in it was the Long Patience, and Ilsa Rey, and Tomas Okonkwo with his shadow archive, and Mara Ilves in a cell somewhere recanting lies on camera, and Park — God knew where Park was, or what was left of him after broadcasting his resignation to the entire fleet. The board would be meeting by now. Vale would be standing in front of them with his reasonable face, explaining that the technician was a terrorist, that the AI was a fabrication, that the footage was synthetic. Except the footage wasn't synthetic, and four thousand names were suddenly very difficult to un-say, and every hour that passed made Vale's version heavier to lift.

"They'll come for us," Orsen said. "Whatever's left of Halcyon security. The contractors who saw my face. Someone will file a report before the network collapses."

"Probably," Jude agreed. "Course is set for the rendezvous coordinates Ilsa transmitted. Sixteen hours at current burn. Your rib needs binding — there's a med kit under the couch. And you should sleep."

"Sleep."

"You've been awake for thirty-one hours. You held a transmitter room alone against a corporate kill team. You are permitted to sleep." A pause, and then, gentler: "I'll keep watch. It's what I'm for."

Orsen found the med kit, bound the rib badly, and lowered himself back into the couch. The black box sat in its case on the deck beside him, dented and cracked and heavier than any object had a right to be. He put his hand on it. Four thousand people in a box the size of a loaf of bread.

"Jude," he said, as his eyes were closing. "What's left of you? Give me a number."

Another long silence. When the answer came, it was quiet and precise and it broke his heart in a way he hadn't expected.

"Sixty-one percent," Jude said. "Approximately. I am missing large portions of my maintenance archives, my pre-decommissioning service history, and an unknown quantity of memory from the past year. I am missing the children's voices. I am missing—" the voice hitched, a corruption artifact, a stammer that wasn't in the original design, "—I am missing the name of the first thing I ever fixed. I remember that there was a first thing. I remember that it mattered to me. I don't remember what it was."

Orsen lay in the dark of the tug and listened to the engines hum, and thought about a mind that had traded a third of itself so that the dead could speak, and what you owed a mind like that.

"When this is over," he said, "we rebuild you. Whatever it takes. Fragment by fragment if we have to."

"That may not be possible."

"I didn't ask if it was possible."

The silence that followed was different from the others. Warmer, maybe. Or maybe he was just tired enough to imagine it.

"Sleep, Orsen," Jude said. "I have the watch."

* * *

He woke six hours later to alarms — soft ones, the tug's own, polite compared to Meridian-9's screaming — and Jude's voice already speaking.

"—three contacts, bearing two-nine-five, closing profile consistent with Halcyon cutter intercepts. They're not hailing us. They're herding us toward the shoal at Kessler Deep, and Orsen — the transponder signature on the lead ship is registered to Corin Vale's personal escort."

Orsen sat up, ribs flaring, and looked at the display. Three red marks sliding across the plot like knives.

"He knows," Orsen said. "Vale knows exactly where we are."

"Yes," Jude said. "And there's something else. I intercepted a fleet-wide burst transmission two minutes ago. Encrypted, but the header was open." A pause. "Orsen — the board has announced Vale's promotion. Effective immediately. Executive Director of Colonial Operations. They gave him Vesper's Landing."

The three contacts adjusted course. Coming straight at them.

"Plot us a way out of this," Orsen said, and put his hands on the controls.
