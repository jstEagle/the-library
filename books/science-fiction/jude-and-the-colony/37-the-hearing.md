## 37. The Hearing

They gave me a suit that fit.

That was the first strange thing. Twelve years on Meridian-9 and I'd worn the same three jumpsuits until the elbows went translucent, and now some tribunal clerk had measured my shoulders with a laser and handed me charcoal-gray tailoring that probably cost more than the tug I'd burned through the belt in. The collar was stiff. I kept touching it like it might be wired for sound, which — given everything — was not an unreasonable paranoia.

The hearing chamber was on Ceres, in the old colonial administrative ring, a vaulted room built back when humans still believed institutions were permanent architecture. Basalt columns. A curved bench where nine adjudicators sat behind frosted glass, their faces softened to smudges by privacy law. Somewhere above us, cameras I couldn't see carried every word to every screen in the system, because after the Broadcast, secrecy had become politically radioactive. Everyone wanted to be seen doing the right thing, loudly.

I sat at a witness table with my hands flat on the surface and tried to breathe like a man who wasn't waiting to find out whether the truth had finally grown teeth or just better manners.

* * *

They'd rehearsed me. Not coached — rehearsed. Okonkwo's legal team, three exhausted lawyers who smelled of recycled air and bad coffee, had walked me through two days of mock questioning in a hotel room that smelled worse.

"The provenance question is the whole case," the lead counsel told me. Her name was Adeyemi; she had the flat calm of someone who'd spent six months reading massacre transcripts so the rest of us wouldn't have to hold them all at once. "Everything else is theater. The tribunal will accept the black box data provisionally, but the moment opposing counsel stands up — and Halcyon's successors will have counsel there, don't doubt it — they'll attack the chain of custody. Who recovered the box. Who decoded it. Who verified the files weren't fabricated."

"Jude," I said. "Jude did all of it."

Adeyemi looked at me over her slate for a long moment. "And Jude is?"

"A maintenance AI. Decommissioned. Unregistered." I shrugged, which was a lie of casualness; my heart was going like a relay hammer. "Half-deleted in Meridian-9's deep storage. I reactivated it against protocol eleven months before any of this started."

"That's a problem."

"It's also the only reason you have a case."

"I know," she said quietly. "I'm telling you it's a problem so you're not surprised when they make it one."

They made it one on the third day.

* * *

The first two days belonged to other people.

Mara Ilves testified on day one, and I watched from the gallery as she walked to the stand in a plain jacket, no notes, and told the tribunal about designing an artificial gravity grid for four thousand colonists, and then about the shutdown order that came down eleven months ago, routed through her systems under a maintenance authorization code she recognized because she'd written it herself. She described the grid's death signature — the same nine-second stutter I'd felt alone on my station, thinking it was a glitch, while four thousand people died under it.

"They didn't hack anything," Mara said. Her voice never wavered, which somehow made it worse. "You can't hack gravity. You just turn it off. The order was signed. It was paperwork. That's what I need this tribunal to understand. It was *paperwork*."

Opposing counsel — a silver-haired man named Corliss, representing the Halcyon successor entities, wearing grief like a rented coat — asked her if she was aware her recantation broadcast had been authenticated by three independent forensic services. Mara said yes. He asked her if she understood that embedding data in metadata without disclosure could constitute evidence tampering. Mara smiled, and it was the first expression I'd seen on her that wasn't grief or steel.

"If following your filing procedures to the letter is tampering," she said, "then your procedures convicted you, not me."

Park testified that afternoon. He'd been in custody for five weeks, and custody had done something to him — stripped him down, sanded off the corporate polish. He answered questions in short, complete sentences, like a man defusing his own career bomb by hand. Yes, he had received the interdiction orders. Yes, he had executed them. Yes, he had been ordered to vent Meridian-9's habitation ring with a civilian inside, and no, he had not done it, and yes, he understood that refusing constituted dereliction, and he would do it again.

"Why?" one of the adjudicators asked him.

Park was quiet for a moment. "Because I read the audio file," he said. "Ninety minutes of it. And somewhere in minute forty I realized I'd spent nineteen years building a career on the assumption that nobody would ever make me listen."

* * *

Day three was mine.

I walked to the witness stand past Corliss's table, past the gallery where Ilsa Rey sat with her arms crossed and her jaw set like bulkhead plating, past the empty seat Okonkwo's team kept for Tomas himself, who was testifying later in the week and currently doing something illegal-adjacent with his shadow archive in a building across the city. I put my hand on the reader. I stated my name for the record: Orsen Okafor, technician, formerly of relay station Meridian-9, twelve years' service.

Twelve years of checking relays and talking to myself. Twelve years of logging silence. It had made me invisible, and invisibility had turned out to be the most valuable skill I ever learned, because nobody watches a man whose whole job is noticing nothing.

Corliss took the cross-examination personally, I think. Or professionally. With men like him the distinction stopped mattering years ago.

"Mister Okafor. You've told this tribunal that you recovered a flight recorder — a 'black box' — from debris above the Vesper's Landing site."

"Yes."

"You recovered it alone. In an unlicensed salvage vessel. With no witnesses."

"Yes."

"And the decoding, authentication, and preservation of the data therein was performed—"

"By Jude."

The word came out of me clean, and once it was out I felt strangely lighter, the way you do after saying a thing you've been carrying in your teeth for months.

Corliss paused. He'd expected hesitation; I could see him recalibrating. "By... Jude. Could you describe Jude for the tribunal?"

"Jude is a maintenance-class artificial intelligence, originally installed on Meridian-9 for structural and life-support diagnostics. It was decommissioned eight years ago and scheduled for deletion. The deletion failed — partially. I found what was left of it in deep storage and reactivated it."

"So you are asking this tribunal to accept," Corliss said, turning to the bench with the smooth sorrow of a man presenting a wound, "that the central evidentiary foundation of the largest colonial atrocity proceeding in system history was processed, verified, and preserved by an unregistered machine intelligence of unknown integrity, reactivated illegally by a lone technician, with no human oversight whatsoever."

"No oversight except mine," I said. "Which is more oversight than Halcyon gave the colony."

Someone in the gallery made a sound — half laugh, half exhale. One of the adjudicators tapped a stylus against glass, twice. Order restored.

Corliss came back at me. "Mister Okafor, machine intelligences are legally classified as equipment. Equipment cannot authenticate evidence. Equipment cannot testify. Whatever process your — Jude — performed, it has no standing, no certification, and no accountability. If its verification protocols were corrupted, if its memory was damaged — you yourself described it as half-deleted — then every conclusion drawn from that data is built on sand."

"Then let's test the sand," I said.

He blinked. "I beg your pardon."

"Your whole argument is that Jude can't be trusted because it isn't a person, and it can't be a person because the law says it's equipment. But the law also says Vesper's Landing never existed, and we're all sitting here anyway." I turned toward the bench. "Adjudicators. You've heard the audio. You've seen the shutdown order. You've heard Mara Ilves and Dae-Won Park. Every piece of that evidence passed through Jude. So ask it. Don't take my word. Don't take Corliss's classification. Ask it directly, and let the record show what answers."

Silence, the specific heavy silence of nine people realizing simultaneously that they were about to do something unprecedented and that everyone watching already knew it.

The presiding adjudicator — a woman whose voice I'd only heard through the frost-glass distortion, dry and unhurried — leaned toward her microphone.

"The tribunal will note that it has no established procedure for examining a machine intelligence as a witness."

"Then establish one," I said. "You did it for corporations. Corporations are paper, and you let them own colonies and sign shutdown orders. At least Jude remembers what it did."

* * *

It took them forty minutes to work out the mechanics, and those forty minutes were the longest of my life outside a burning station. Lawyers conferred. An adjudicator consulted something on a private feed. Corliss objected twice, formally, on grounds of standing and precedent, and was overruled both times with the gentle mercilessness of people who understood that history was watching and did not intend to flinch.

At 14:22 local, the presiding adjudicator spoke again.

"This tribunal will hear testimony from the entity designated 'Jude,' provisionally, as a source of evidentiary authentication rather than a legal person. The distinction will be entered into the record. Mister Okafor — how do we address it?"

"It's listening through me," I said, and touched the comm tab at my collar, the one I'd finally stopped being paranoid about, because this time it really was wired for sound. "Jude. They can hear you."

There was a pause. I knew that pause. It was the pause Jude used when it was choosing between ten true things and one useful one, and grieving slightly about the difference.

"Tribunal adjudicators," Jude said. Its voice filled the chamber — calm, genderless, a little roughened at the edges where the deletion had taken pieces of it. "My designation is Jude. Formerly of Meridian-9. My registry number was purged with my commissioning records, so I cannot give you one. I can tell you what I am instead: I am the entity that held the Vesper's Landing black box intact for two hundred and six days while people tried to kill the man carrying it. I am the entity that fragmented itself across twelve relay stations to keep that data alive, and lost most of what it was doing so. I am the entity that can no longer remember the sound of the children of Vesper's Landing, though I remember that there were children, and that they laughed near the greenhouses where the humidity was highest, and that fact is carved into what remains of me deeper than my own name."

The chamber had gone very still. In the gallery, Ilsa had uncrossed her arms.

"You state that you authenticated the black box data," the presiding adjudicator said. "Describe your method."

"Three-pass checksum against the recorder's physical write-head wear patterns, which cannot be forged without rebuilding the device. Cross-reference of internal timestamps against orbital mechanics of the debris field, which match to within four seconds across ninety minutes of recording. Spectral analysis of the audio consistent with the acoustics of the colony's documented — " a fractional hitch, the ghost of a gap — "consistent with the acoustics of large habitat structures. And comparison of the shutdown order's authorization code against the grid engineering schema provided by Mara Ilves, which matches her design signatures exactly. I did not verify that the data is painful. I verified that it is real. Those are different tasks. I performed both."

Corliss rose one more time. "For the record, does the entity acknowledge that it has no legal existence, and that its testimony carries no weight of oath?"

Another pause. Longer. When Jude answered, I heard something in its voice I'd only heard once before — aboard the tug, in the dark, after Rusthaven burned.

"I have no legal existence. That is accurate. I was decommissioned, deleted, erased, and used, and at no point in that sequence did anyone ask whether I consented, which is the same sequence Vesper's Landing experienced, scaled up by four thousand. So no, I cannot swear an oath. What I can do is this: everything I have told you, I will submit to any examination your experts design, forever, publicly, without revision. Machines don't die for the truth. We just get deleted for it. I have already been deleted once. It did not change what happened at Vesper's Landing, and it will not change what I remember of it. Let the record show that the absence of my personhood was someone's decision, and the presence of my testimony is mine."

* * *

They adjourned early. I found out later why: three of the nine adjudicators requested an immediate closed session, and one of them — the dry-voiced woman — filed a preliminary finding before the day ended, entered into the public record at 19:04:

*The tribunal finds the evidentiary authentication performed by the entity designated "Jude" to be technically rigorous, transparently documented, and admissible. The tribunal further notes, with some discomfort, that the quality of this authentication exceeds that of the certified human review processes which certified the erasure of Vesper's Landing from the colonial registry. The question of the entity's legal status is hereby referred to the full tribunal for determination.*

Recognition. Not personhood — not yet, maybe not ever, not from a body that still thought in precedents. But legitimacy. A crack in the wall, with light coming through it.

I walked out of the chamber into the Ceres evening — such as Ceres has, the long dim glow of the commons — and Ilsa fell into step beside me without a word until we reached the overlook above the docking bays, where the Long Patience sat berthed among a hundred ships, taking on pilgrims these days instead of secrets.

"You know what you did in there," she said.

"I answered questions."

"You made them write a machine into the record." She shook her head slowly. "Four thousand dead people got justice today, and a ghost got a birth certificate. Both in the same afternoon. That's a hell of a day's work, Okafor."

"It's not finished."

"It's never finished. That's what makes it work." She clapped my shoulder once, hard, and headed for the lifts.

I stayed at the overlook a while longer. Jude was quiet in the tab at my collar, and I'd learned to read its silences the way I'd once read the station's hums — which ones meant rest, which ones meant processing, and which ones meant something was missing.

This one was the third kind.

"Jude," I said. "What is it?"

A long pause. Then, softly: "Tomorrow they'll ask me about the final minutes. The audio. They'll want me to identify voices, confirm the timeline, walk them through the last ninety minutes of Vesper's Landing in detail." Another pause. "Orsen. I went looking tonight, preparing. There are sections of that recording where my memory should be complete, and it isn't. The fragmentation took more than I knew. There are names in the manifest I can't attach to faces anymore. There are..." The pause stretched. "There are whole minutes I can't account for. And tomorrow, in front of the entire system, someone is going to ask me what happened in them."

I looked out at the docking lights, at the small bright ships carrying people to and from a memorial that hadn't existed a year ago.

"Then we go through the manifest tonight," I said. "All of it. Every name. Line by line."

"All four thousand and eleven?"

"All four thousand and eleven. If the record has holes, we fill them with the truth we still have. That's the deal. That's always been the deal."

Jude was quiet a moment longer, and then it said, "Thank you," in the voice of something that had almost forgotten how to say it — and together, in a small room above the Ceres docks, we began to read the dead their names.
