## 1. The Beacon Nobody Asked For

The coffee was bad, and that was the first honest thing in Gideon Kavanagh's day.

She drank it anyway, standing at the galley window of Relay Station Jude-9 while the station's slow rotation carried the view from the black of deep space to the amber smear of the gas giant below—Cassiel, a name some cartographer had given it two centuries ago and no one had bothered to change. The planet turned beneath her like something sleeping. It had been turning like that for six years, the entire length of her exile, and she had come to think of its rotation as a clock more trustworthy than any on her console.

Six years. Two thousand one hundred and forty sunrises over Cassiel's cloud deck, if you counted the ones through the window instead of the ones the station logged. She counted them. That was the kind of person exile made you: someone who counted things.

She rinsed the mug, set it in the dryer, and began her rounds.

The routine was liturgy, and she performed it as such. First the environmental checks—oxygen nominal, water recyclers humming at ninety-four percent efficiency, the greenhouse module's tomato vines threatening for the third week to outgrow their trellises because she couldn't bring herself to prune anything living. Then the array diagnostics: four hundred relay dishes fanned across the station's spine like the frill of some prehistoric fish, each one listening to a different slice of sky, each one feeding traffic down into her translation queue.

Because that was the joke of her posting, the punchline the Fleet had delivered along with her one-way transport: they had exiled a xenolinguist to a listening post. They had taken the woman whose translations had ended the Fleet War—the woman whose careful, devastating rendering of three intercepted communiqués had proven the Concord fleet had been ordered to fire on its own escort wing—and they had stationed her at the edge of everything, alone, where her only work was making sure supply barges and patrol cutters could understand each other's course corrections.

They hadn't even been subtle about it. *You're very good at hearing things, Dr. Kavanagh,* the tribunal chair had said, not unkindly, which was somehow worse. *We'd prefer you heard less.*

So she heard everything, and it all meant nothing. Convoy manifests. Navigational pings. A Concord corvette complaining about coolant pressure in language so formulaic she could have translated it asleep—and sometimes did, in the sense that she dreamed in translation matrices now, which her old mentor would have called a symptom.

By 0700 station time she was at her console, feet tucked under her in the chair the way no regulation allowed, working through the overnight queue. Forty-one items. Thirty-eight were boilerplate. One was a personal message from a barge pilot to his sister on Meridian Station, full of misspellings and a recipe gone wrong, and Gideon translated it carefully anyway, preserving the misspellings, because somewhere a sister was going to read it and laugh, and Gideon liked being, invisibly, the reason for a laugh.

The thirty-ninth item was the beacon.

She stopped. She always stopped, even though by now there was nothing new to see. The Jude-9 beacon—a relic older than either fleet, older than the war, a lighthouse some forgotten generation had bolted onto this empty reach of space for reasons lost to the same history that had lost everything else—had been transmitting on its own for eleven days.

Eleven days, four hours, and — she checked, hating herself — nineteen minutes.

It wasn't supposed to do that. The beacon was a repeater, nothing more: it caught scheduled traffic and threw it onward, dumb as a mirror. Its maintenance logs went back ninety years and showed exactly one anomaly, a power fluctuation in the year before Gideon was born, attributed to a micrometeorite strike that no one had ever verified. Otherwise: silence between relays. Faithful, mechanical silence.

Then, eleven days ago, at 0312 station time, it had begun to sing.

Not loudly. Not on any channel anyone monitored—that was the thing that kept her awake, that kept her logging and re-logging and telling no one. The beacon's spontaneous transmission lived in a frequency band so far outside the fleets' allocation tables that no automated system would ever flag it. She'd only found it because she ran a full-spectrum sweep every morning, an old habit from the war, when the enemy had hidden their real traffic in the cracks between channels and she'd gotten very good at listening to cracks.

The transmission was structured. That was the word she kept circling. It wasn't noise; noise didn't repeat with variation. It came in phrases—she'd started calling them phrases because she had no better word and because the alternative was to admit what they sounded like, which was speech. Utterance. Someone talking in the dark.

She had run it against every linguistic database the station carried, which was considerable, because the Fleet has never quite trusted her and so had given her tools instead of colleagues. Concord standard, Fleet standard, the eleven recognized colony dialects, the reconstructed trade-pidgin of the Founding Era, dead languages, dead-er languages. Nothing. The phrases matched no human pattern she could find—not in phonology, not in syntax, not in the statistical rhythms of information density. And yet they repeated. Varied. Returned to earlier forms the way a speaker returns to a refrain.

*Like someone remembering,* she wrote in her notes, and then crossed it out, and then wrote it again, because her notes were hers and no one read them.

She pulled up the waveform and let it play through her headphones, low, the way she did every morning now, the way she'd begun doing without deciding to. The sound was soft, almost subvocal, a texture more than a tone. Eleven days ago it had frightened her. Now—

Now it was just part of the morning. Coffee, rounds, queue, beacon. Liturgy.

That was what scared her, if she was honest, and she tried to be honest at least once a day, usually here, usually now. Not the transmission. Her comfort with it.

* * *

The dreams had started four years ago. Or five. She'd never fixed the date, because the early ones had seemed like ordinary dream-debris, the mind's slag: fragments of sensation, a sense of vast attention, the feeling of being spoken to by something patient. Everyone dreamed like that sometimes. She'd mentioned it to no one, because mentioning it meant filing a psych log, and psych logs traveled back to Fleet Command, and Fleet Command already thought she heard too much.

But the dreams had changed. Had developed grammar.

She kept the journal in a locked partition under a file labeled *TOMATO CULTIVATION NOTES*, because paranoia, too, was a habit the war had given her and exile had refined. Every morning she woke and wrote down what remained before it evaporated, typing fast, spelling be damned:

*Day 1,412. The long corridor again, but this time I understood the signs on the walls. I can't reproduce them awake. I know I knew them. The feeling is like waking from a conversation where you agreed to something and can't remember what.*

*Day 1,419. Spoke aloud in the dream. My mouth knew the shapes. Woke with my lips still moving. Recorded myself saying it — playback attached. It matches the beacon's phrase structure. I am not writing that sentence calmly.*

*Day 1,426. No dream. Missed it. Felt like missing a train.*

She scrolled back through years of entries, watching the language assemble itself in her sleep the way ice assembles across a pond—from the edges inward, crystal by crystal, following rules no one had dictated. Her waking self couldn't speak it. Couldn't translate it. But her dreaming self was fluent, and every morning the gap between the two selves widened by one more night.

A linguist should have been thrilled. A linguist would have published, testified, built a career on it—first contact, first contact, the phrase that ended careers and started wars. Instead Gideon felt what she imagined an heir feels opening a letter addressed to someone dead: the eerie certainty that the message was meant for her, and the equal certainty that she didn't know why.

Because that was the other thing, the thing she circled and crossed out and circled again. The dreams hadn't started until she arrived at Jude-9. Four years of solitude, four years of the beacon humming its dumb faithful repetitions—and then, quietly, without announcement, her sleeping mind had begun learning a language that existed nowhere in human records except, possibly, in the transmissions of a machine nobody had asked for.

Coincidence was possible. Coincidence was almost always possible. She had spent a war teaching tribunals that coincidence was also almost always a lie people told themselves.

She closed the journal, locked the partition, and looked at the beacon's status line glowing patiently on her secondary display.

**JUDE-9 BEACON: NONSCHEDULED TRANSMISSION ACTIVE — DAY 11**

Eleven days. She should report it. That was the thought that arrived every morning and left every evening, unsent. Report it, and the report would travel up the chain to Fleet Command, and Fleet Command would send investigators, and investigators would ask why she'd waited eleven days, and the answer—*because I've been dreaming in it for four years and I don't want anyone to know*—was not an answer she could give. Was barely an answer she could think.

Or she could report it as a malfunction. Beacon fault, spurious emission, requesting remote diagnostic. Bureaucratic, boring, true in the technical sense. It would buy her time. Time to understand what the beacon was saying before anyone else decided what it meant.

She drafted the malfunction report. She did not send it. She told herself she'd send it tomorrow, and the lie sat comfortably in her chest, warm as the bad coffee, and she recognized the warmth for what it was: the pleasure of having a secret. Six years of nothing belonging to her—not her work, not her reputation, not her name in any dispatch but disciplinary ones—and now she had one thing, one small singing secret at the edge of the dark, and she was not ready to give it up.

*Comfortable loneliness beginning to crack,* she wrote in the margin of her paper notebook, because she still kept one, because screens could be subpoenaed. Then she underlined *crack* twice and hated how much it sounded like hope.

* * *

Afternoon brought the daily hail-window, the two hours when the inner-system relays aligned and live voice traffic became possible. She ran the check-in protocol with Relay Control at Meridian Station, a man named Okonkwo who had never seen her face and whose voice she knew better than most people's faces. He asked about her tomatoes. She lied about pruning them. They discussed a convoy reroute. It was, by the standards of her life, a rich social engagement.

"Anything anomalous your side?" Okonkwo asked, closing out. Standard question. Standard answer.

"Quiet as a held breath," Gideon said, and signed off, and stood for a moment in the hum of the station feeling the shape of what she'd just done. Small lies were load-bearing. You told one and it held up a ceiling, and under the ceiling you could keep living, and the ceiling never fell as long as you never looked up.

She looked up. Through the observation dome, past Cassiel's sleeping curve, toward the coordinate where the beacon hung in its ancient orbit—a fleck of metal older than both fleets, older than the war she'd ended, singing to no one in a language that lived in her sleep.

*Who built you?* she asked it, silently, the way she'd asked it a hundred times. *And why me?*

The beacon, naturally, said nothing. It said plenty of something else, but nothing to that.

* * *

Evening. She ate alone at the little table by the window, tomatoes from the vines she wouldn't prune, rice from the recyclers, and after dinner she did the thing she did every night now, the ritual within the liturgy: she took her headphones to the observation dome and listened to the beacon's phrases while the stars came out around her.

There were—she'd counted, of course—forty-one distinct phrase-forms in the transmission cycle. Forty-one. The same number as the overnight queue items, which meant nothing, which she noted anyway. Some phrases recurred often; some appeared once per cycle like rare birds. Over eleven days she'd mapped their sequence, looking for structure, and found it: the phrases weren't random. They cycled, but the cycle drifted, returning to earlier forms with small variations, the way—

The way a story gets retold. The way a message gets repeated with emphasis. The way someone says *listen, listen, listen* in slightly different words because the listener isn't answering.

"It's an invitation," she said aloud to the empty dome, and her voice sounded strange and large after a day of silence. "Or a roll call."

She thought about the Founding Era, the century before the fleets—the generation that had seeded these stars and then, famously, vanished from the record, leaving behind infrastructure and silence in equal measure. Both fleets claimed descent from them. Both fleets claimed their mantle. Neither fleet knew what had happened to them, and the not-knowing had curdled over generations into the rivalry that became the war, two children fighting over an inheritance neither had ever seen.

Maybe the beacon was part of the inheritance. Maybe it had been waiting a hundred years for someone to listen to the cracks between channels.

Maybe it had been waiting for her specifically, and that thought she did not write down anywhere, because some thoughts, once written, become plans.

The station rotated. Cassiel rose. The beacon sang its patient forty-one phrases into the dark, and Gideon Kavanagh, exiled xenolinguist, keeper of a lighthouse nobody visited, closed her eyes and let the language pour into her the way it did every night, trusting her sleeping self to do the work her waking self couldn't.

She dreamed, that night, of a corridor with signs she could read, and at the end of the corridor a door, and behind the door a voice saying—with unmistakable urgency, in words she would spend the next morning failing to transcribe—one phrase over and over, a phrase that felt less like language than like a hand gripping her wrist:

*Coming. Coming. Coming home.*

She woke at 0309 with her heart slamming and her lips still moving, and she reached for the console out of habit, out of liturgy, and froze.

The alert light was blinking. An unscheduled contact, inbound, deep-space vector, deceleration burn active—impossible, impossible, nothing came out here, nothing had come out here in six years—and the transponder query she sent on automatic returned its answer in plain Fleet-standard characters, and she read the name twice, three times, her breath fogging the cold console glass.

**MERIDIAN.**

Deep-range survey vessel. Missing for twenty years. Declared lost with all hands before Gideon had ever finished her doctorate.

It was coming to her station. And it was broadcasting—not on any fleet channel, not in any code she knew—but in the crack between channels, in the wide-open band where only one signal had ever lived.

It was broadcasting in the beacon's language. In her dream-language.

Gideon sat in the dark with the alert pulsing red against her face, and somewhere below her the sleeping planet turned, and eleven days of comfortable loneliness finished cracking clean through.
