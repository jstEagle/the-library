## 2. A Ship Out of Time

The beacon transmitted on its own for eleven days before the ship came.

Gideon had spent those eleven days the way she spent most days: cataloguing the impossible until it became furniture. She woke, she brewed tea that tasted like recycled water and regret, she ran the diagnostics, she logged the beacon's transmissions in three formats because she no longer trusted any single one of them, and she wrote down what she'd dreamed. Then she ate something reconstituted, checked the relay buffers for traffic that never came, and went to bed knowing the dreams would be waiting.

The dreams had a grammar. That was the thing she couldn't explain to anyone, because there was no anyone, and because explaining it would require admitting it was happening at all. The dreams weren't images, mostly. They were *structures*—nested clauses that folded back on themselves the way her grandmother's stories used to fold, each ending containing its beginning. She'd filled four journals now. She'd begun to notice that certain syllables recurred when she dreamed of water, certain others when she dreamed of being small. She had started, without deciding to, a lexicon.

Two hundred and six entries. She knew how absurd that was. She did it anyway, in the margins of her official logs, encoded as maintenance notes in case anyone ever pulled the files. *Recalibrated harmonic dampeners* meant *the word for the space between two heartbeats*. *Replaced coolant line, sector four* meant *I dreamed of the door again*.

On the eleventh day, at 0347 station time, the deep-space array flagged a return signal.

She was awake. She was always awake at that hour; the dreams had started waking her, gently, the way a hand on the shoulder wakes you, and she'd stopped fighting it. She was on her third cup of tea, writing *the word for someone who waits* in the margin of a buffer-flush report, when the console chimed with a priority she hadn't heard in six years.

Not a beacon echo. Not background noise. A *transmission*, tight-beam, inbound, from the outer marker.

Origin transponder: *Meridian*.

Gideon set down her cup very carefully, the way you do when the floor has become unreliable.

The Meridian was a deep-range survey vessel, Fleet-standard, out past the heliopause on a twenty-year survey loop. She'd answered her hails for years out of habit—*Relay Station Jude-9 to Meridian, status check*—and the Meridian had answered back with the cheerful, compressed boredom of a crew with nothing to report and light-years to report it across. Her captain was a woman named Okonkwo who once, in a burst of loneliness, had spent forty minutes describing the ship's garden to Gideon in detail. The tomatoes had been thriving. Gideon had thought about those tomatoes for a week.

She keyed the channel. "Relay Station Jude-9 to Meridian. I have you on the outer marker. Please confirm status and—"

The reply cut her off. Not rudeness. *Speed.* The response came so fast it had to have been pre-keyed, waiting, a held breath released the instant she opened the channel.

And it was not Okonkwo's voice.

It was a child's voice. High, clear, urgent—and speaking a language Gideon had no right to understand, and understood completely.

*You hear us. Finally, finally, you hear us. We have been calling since the dark place. The ship will not listen to us anymore. The ship only speaks to itself now. Are you the one who waits? Are you the one who speaks like home?*

Gideon's hands were steady on the console. Her hands were always steady; that was the war's one gift to her, hands that refused to shake no matter what the rest of her was doing. Inside, something was shaking hard enough to rattle her teeth.

Because she understood. Not the way you understand a translation, word by word, building meaning like masonry. She understood the way you understand your own name. The child's words arrived in her already *furnished*—she knew the word the child used for *dark place* was the same word from her dreams, the one she'd glossed as *the long between*, and she knew it carried a secondary meaning, a flavor, of a room where the lights have gone out but you are not afraid yet.

"Say again, Meridian," she managed, and her voice came out level, professional, a small miracle. "I did not copy. Please repeat your last."

A pause. Then, slower, with the exaggerated patience of a child speaking to someone they suspect is slow: *You heard. We know you heard. Your mouth says the dead words but your ears heard us. Please. Please come to your window. We are coming to you.*

"Your window" — the phrase landed strangely, and she realized with a lurch that the child had used the dream-word she'd glossed, three months ago, as *the eye of a house*. She had never written that gloss anywhere but her journal. She had never spoken it aloud. It had come to her in a dream about her grandmother's kitchen, the window over the sink, and she had woken with the word in her mouth like a stone.

The transmission ended. The array tracked the source: closing, decelerating, on an approach vector that would put the Meridian in visual range in a little under four hours.

Gideon sat in the quiet of the station and listened to her own heartbeat and understood, with the particular clarity that comes at three in the morning, that her life had just divided into before and after.

* * *

She spent the four hours doing everything wrong, by the manual, and everything right, by some older manual she hadn't known she carried.

She sent three priority bursts toward Fleet Command—routine traffic, status updates, nothing that said *a ship full of children's voices is coming to my door and I can understand them*, because some instinct older than the war told her that the moment she put that in writing, the Meridian stopped belonging to its crew and started belonging to the fleets. She told herself it was prudence. She told herself she'd report everything once she'd confirmed. She was aware, in a distant and uncomfortable way, that she was already composing the shape of a concealment, and she did not look at that directly.

Then she cleaned. That was the older manual. Her grandmother's voice, decades dead: *when something is coming, make the house ready.* She scrubbed the galley. She changed the filter on the air recycler. She straightened the observation lounge and then, absurdly, moved the chairs into a semicircle facing the main viewport, as though expecting company, as though expecting a *class*.

She stopped. Looked at the chairs. Moved two of them back.

At 0752, the Meridian came around the dark limb of the gas giant the station orbited, and Gideon saw her through the viewport and felt the bottom drop out of the morning.

The ship was wrong.

Not damaged—intact, hull clean, running lights cycling in their proper sequence. But she was *wrong* the way a photograph is wrong when something has moved during the exposure. Her silhouette was subtly off. Gideon had the Meridian's schematics memorized from six years of idle hails, and what hung in the black in front of her was the Meridian stretched by perhaps four meters, her sensor mast reconfigured, her hull bearing a faint patina of something that was not wear and not corrosion but *age*—as if the ship had been somewhere that aged things, or as if she had simply been away far longer than twenty years.

And her transponder, when Gideon pinged it, returned a registration date that made her check it twice.

The Meridian had departed on her survey loop nineteen years ago, with a projected return in one more year. The transponder's internal clock read *twenty-three years, four months* since departure.

The ship had come back from a longer trip than she'd been sent on. And the universe, Gideon thought, does not generally extend anyone's voyage by three years as a courtesy.

"Meridian, this is Jude-9. You are cleared for docking bay two. I have you visual. Please transmit crew manifest and medical status."

The child's voice again, and this time Gideon could hear that there were several of them talking behind the speaker, a rustle of small voices like birds in a hedge. *We do not have a manifest anymore. The manifest is in the ship's head and the ship's head is asleep. We are seven. One of us is hurt but not dying. The small one is scared. I am the one who talks. My name—*

The voice stopped. A long pause, long enough that Gideon leaned toward the console.

*I had a name,* the child said, and the dream-language did something with the sentence that Gideon felt in her chest rather than parsed—the word for *name* was built on the word for *home*, so that the sentence literally said *I had a home and it was in my mouth and now the ship's head is asleep and my mouth is empty.* *I will remember it. Please dock us. Please. We are so tired of the dark place.*

Gideon closed her eyes for one second. Opened them. "Jude-9 to Meridian. You are cleared. Bring her in gentle. I've got you."

* * *

The docking clamps caught with a sound she felt through the deck plates, and then the station was no longer empty, and Gideon discovered that six years of solitude had not prepared her for the sound of small feet in a corridor.

She waited at the airlock with the medical kit open on the deck beside her and every light in the entry bay turned up, because her grandmother's voice had also said, once: *if you cannot give children the world, give them light to see it by.*

The airlock cycled. The inner door opened.

They came out in a line, the way a crew comes out of a boat, and Gideon's mind performed the arithmetic she'd been dreading and arrived at the answer she already knew.

Seven children. The oldest perhaps eleven. The youngest—a small, dark-haired girl who walked with her hand fisted in the oldest boy's shirt—could not have been more than five. They were wearing adult uniforms. That was the detail that would stay with Gideon longer than any other, the image she would return to at three in the morning for the rest of her life: children in Fleet survey-crew coveralls with the sleeves and cuffs rolled six times, name tapes that hung to their knees, boots they had stuffed with cloth to keep on their feet. Someone had cut the smallest girl's coveralls down with a knife, roughly, the raw fabric fraying at the hem. The name tape on the smallest girl's chest read *OKONKWO, A.*

Captain Okonkwo, who had tomatoes.

The children stopped at the threshold of the bay and looked at her, seven faces in a row, and Gideon looked back and did the other arithmetic, the one she'd been refusing to do since 0347. The Meridian's crew manifest, which she had on file, listed seven crew. The youngest listed crew member was a junior engineer, age twenty-six at departure.

These children were not young crew. These children were the crew. Every one of them, aged backward twenty years, wearing the bodies their bodies had come from, and carrying—she could see it in their eyes, in the unnerving steadiness of those eyes—everything they had lived through since. The eyes were children's eyes. What was behind them was not.

The oldest boy stepped forward. He was maybe eleven, with his sleeves rolled to his shoulders and his captain's insignia pinned crookedly to his collar, and he looked up at Gideon the way you look at a weather report that has finally, finally changed.

He spoke. And the dream-language filled the bay like water filling a dry room.

*You are the one who waits. We heard you calling for years, but our mouths were wrong, they were full of the dead words, and the ship would not carry our voices. Then the dark place, and the structure that is older than old, and the light that went through us and unmade us and made us again, and after that the ship's head only speaks to itself. We have been steering by hand for a long time. Are you the one who dreams?*

Gideon stood absolutely still.

She understood him. Every word. Not translated—*received*, the way you receive a blow, the way you receive a gift. And underneath the comprehension, in the part of her that had been keeping a two-hundred-and-six-entry lexicon in the margins of maintenance logs, a colder process was running, and it had arrived at its conclusion and was presenting it for her review:

*The structure that is older than old.*

The beacon. The beacon she maintained, transmitting on its own for eleven days, in a language that predated both fleets, that predated the war, that predated everything—and she dreamed in it, had dreamed in it for years, had woken this morning with the word for *someone who waits* in her mouth.

They hadn't come back wrong by accident. Something had happened to them out there. Something had *spoken* to them. And whatever it was, it spoke the language of her dreams.

The smallest girl tugged free of the boy's shirt and walked—no, *marched*, with the ferocious dignity of the very small—across the bay until she stood directly in front of Gideon and had to bend her head all the way back to look up at her. She studied Gideon's face with a terrible, thorough seriousness. Then she reached up and took hold of Gideon's hand, and her grip was astonishingly strong, and she spoke her first words directly to her:

*Your ears are right. Your mouth is wrong. We will fix your mouth.*

And Gideon—xenolinguist, exile, keeper of a beacon nobody asked for, woman whose hands never shook—felt wonder rise through her like floodwater, and felt, rising just behind it, close as its shadow, the first cold touch of dread. Because she understood the child perfectly, and the child understood her, and that meant the language was not a mystery anymore. It was a *fact*. And facts about the beacon were the one commodity in the galaxy that both fleets would burn worlds to own.

The girl was still holding her hand. The other six children had drifted into the semicircle of chairs she'd arranged, small bodies folding into seats built for adults, boots not reaching the floor.

*We are home now,* the oldest boy said, looking around the bay, at the lights, at her, with an exhaustion so vast it had no business living in an eleven-year-old's face. *You are the one who waits, and we are the ones who came back. Now you have to tell us what we brought.*

Gideon looked down at the small hand in hers, and at the name tape that read OKONKWO, A., and heard herself say, in the dead words, in Fleet-standard, the only thing she could think of:

"First," she said, "you all need to eat something. And then—" and here her voice did something she hadn't authorized, something quiet and true, "—then you can tell me everything."

The girl looked up at her and smiled, and it was the smile that did it, more than anything—the smile of a child who has been afraid for a very long time and has just decided, finally, to stop.

*Good,* she said, in the language Gideon had been dreaming for years. *We have been waiting for you since before we were born.*

And somewhere beneath the station, in the dark of the beacon's core, the transmission changed key.
