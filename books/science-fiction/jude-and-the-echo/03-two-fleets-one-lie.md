## 3. Two Fleets, One Lie

The message from the Concordat fleet arrived at 0400 station time, which was a fiction Gideon maintained purely so she could hate mornings.

PRIORITY: RELAY STATION JUDE-9. VESSEL MERIDIAN CONFIRMED IN YOUR VICINITY. PREPARE FOR CUSTODY TRANSFER. ESCORT DETAIL EN ROUTE. ACKNOWLEDGE.

She read it three times, coffee going cold in her hand, and then the Republic's transmission arrived forty seconds later with almost identical wording and the word *custody* replaced by *recovery*. Both fleets had triangulated the Meridian's position off her own hails—her hails, logged and monitored by every listening array in the sector, because nothing she said or did out here had ever been private, not since the war ended and they'd decided that the woman who translated the surrender terms should be kept where she could be watched.

*Prepare for custody transfer.* As if the Meridian were cargo. As if it weren't twenty-six children wearing their parents' uniforms.

She looked down at her hands. They were steady. That surprised her; she'd expected them to shake. Eleven years of exile had taught her body a strange calm—the stillness of someone who has learned that panic is a luxury the deep dark doesn't stock.

She typed: *STATION JUDE-9 IS REGISTERED NEUTRAL GROUND UNDER THE ARMISTICE OF KESSLER'S REACH, ARTICLE NINE. ALL PARTIES WELCOME. NO PARTY MAY CLAIM. ACKNOWLEDGED.*

Then she sat very still for a long time, listening to the beacon hum its eleven-day-old song through the deck plates, and understood that she had just made a promise she didn't yet know how to keep.

* * *

They arrived within six hours of each other, which told her both fleets had been running dark toward the station for days before the Meridian ever answered her hails. They'd known. Of course they'd known. The Meridian had been missing for two decades; the moment it reappeared, both admiralties had probably lit up like festival nights.

The Concordat ship came in first—a heavy cruiser called *Vigilant*, all gun-metal angles and running lights arranged like a warning. It took up a parking orbit close enough to make a point. The Republic vessel, *Aegis of Thera*, matched it thirty minutes later on the opposite side of the station, equidistant, deliberate, the orbital geometry of two dogs deciding whether to fight over a bone.

Gideon stood in the docking ring control room and watched both approach vectors overlap on her display, forming a perfect X with Jude-9 at the center. She thought about Article Nine. She thought about how neutral ground only stays neutral as long as everyone finds neutrality more convenient than war. She thought about the truce—eleven years old now, fraying at every seam, held together by mutual exhaustion and the memory of how many ships had burned—and about the fact that the Meridian was, depending on your politics, either a miracle or an atrocity, and miracles and atrocities were exactly the kind of thing that started wars.

The docking clamps engaged. Airlock cycling. She straightened her uniform—an exile's uniform, gray, unbadged—and made herself breathe.

Commander Voss of the Concordat came through the airlock first, and Gideon's first thought was that he looked like a man carved by someone who'd been given strict instructions about jawlines. Tall, silver at the temples, immaculate dress grays with the Concordat's compass-rose insignia polished to a shine you could check your teeth in. His escort was four marines in combat armor, rifles slung but hands near, and behind them came a civilian woman with a data-slate who Gideon immediately catalogued as intelligence.

"Dr. Kavanagh." Voss extended a hand. His grip was dry and precise, calibrated to communicate respect without warmth. "I've read your work. The Fleet War translations. Remarkable."

"Most people who've read my work don't say remarkable," Gideon said. "They say *controversial*."

"I imagine they do." He smiled with exactly one-third of his mouth. "Commander Adeyemi is right behind me. I suggest we conduct this conversation together. It saves time, and time—" he glanced around the docking ring with the frank appraisal of a man pricing real estate, "—is the one resource neither fleet has much of."

Adeyemi arrived through the second airlock before he finished speaking, as if summoned by his name. Where Voss was carved, Adeyemi was weathered—dark skin creased around sharp eyes, Republic blues worn loose and practical, no dress insignia, escort of three who moved like people who'd rather be anywhere else. She looked past Gideon immediately, scanning the ring, counting exits, counting threats.

"Doctor." A nod, nothing more. "Where's the Meridian's crew?"

"In medical," Gideon said. "Docked in bay two. I brought them over yesterday after they responded to my hails."

"How many survivors?" Voss asked.

Gideon paused for exactly as long as a person would pause when delivering bad news. "Twenty-six."

Both commanders went still. Twenty-six was wrong. The Meridian had carried a crew of ninety-one when it vanished. Gideon watched them do the arithmetic—twenty-six survivors meant sixty-five dead, meant a disaster, meant blame, meant leverage—and she watched them arrive at the same conclusion at the same moment and look at each other across her small neutral corridor like two chess players realizing the board had changed.

"Sixty-five casualties," Adeyemi said quietly. "Cause?"

"That's what I'm trying to determine. I could use both your fleets' medical archives, frankly. What happened to them doesn't match anything in my databases."

"What *happened* to them," Voss repeated. "You said survivors. Are they injured?"

"They're alive," Gideon said. "That's the part I can confirm. Beyond that—" she spread her hands, let the gesture carry helplessness like ballast, "—you should see for yourselves. But I'll ask you both to prepare first. Because what you're going to see isn't anything either of us is ready for."

* * *

She made them wait while she briefed them—or performed briefing them—in the station's small conference room, feeding them a version of events she'd spent six hours constructing, sanding, rehearsing under her breath while the children slept.

"The crew is nonverbal," she said. "All of them. They respond to stimulus, they eat, they sleep, they show signs of recognition and attachment. But they don't speak. Not English, not Concordat Standard, not any language I can identify. And they are—" here was the hinge, the whole door swinging on it, "—they are children. Biologically. Every one of them appears to be between the ages of five and ten. Including Captain Osei, who would be fifty-three."

Silence. The civilian with the data-slate stopped typing.

"You're saying the Meridian's crew was de-aged," Adeyemi said. Her voice was flat, controlled, the voice of someone refusing to give the word *impossible* the satisfaction.

"I'm saying that's the only description that fits. Cellular scans show telomere length consistent with juvenile biology. Bone density, neural development, dental records matching pre-pubescent baselines. Whatever happened to them happened at the molecular level, and it happened thoroughly. These aren't adults in altered bodies. They're children. Their brains are children's brains."

"And they can't tell us what happened," Voss said slowly.

"No. Which is why I requested both fleets' records. If we can reconstruct the Meridian's final trajectory, its last transmissions, anything—"

"We'll provide everything," Voss said, too quickly, and Gideon saw Adeyemi register the eagerness and file it away. Both fleets were already composing their narratives. Sixty-five dead crew, a returned ghost ship, and a population of child witnesses who couldn't testify. Whichever fleet got custody of those children got custody of the story.

Which was why, Gideon reminded herself, there would be no custody. Article Nine. Neutral ground. She'd written the words herself into the acknowledgment, and now she had to make them true.

"Before you see them," she said, "there are conditions."

Voss raised an eyebrow. Adeyemi folded her arms.

"Medical access is joint or nothing. Any examination happens with observers from both fleets present. No interviews—I've told you, they can't speak. No removal from the station. And no weapons beyond the docking ring." She looked at Voss's marines, then at Adeyemi's escort. "This is a pediatric ward now, gentlemen. Not a prize vault."

"Doctor Kavanagh." Voss's voice was silk over something with edges. "You maintain a relay station. You are a civilian contractor of the Armistice Commission, technically employed by *neither* of us, which means you have authority here only insofar as both of us agree to pretend you do. I'd encourage you to remember that."

"I remember everything, Commander," Gideon said. "It's my job. Ask anyone from the war."

Something flickered across his face—she'd scored, and he knew she knew it—and Adeyemi made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth.

"Joint access," Adeyemi said. "Agreed. On one condition. My medical officer leads the examinations. The Republic has more experience with long-duration deep-space trauma."

"The Concordat's facilities are superior," Voss countered.

"Then your facilities can process the samples. My people take them." Adeyemi turned to Gideon. "Acceptable?"

"It's acceptable," Gideon said, and felt the first hairline crack of relief. Divide them. Make them negotiate with each other instead of against her. It was the oldest trick in diplomacy and it worked best on people who already hated cooperating.

They went to medical together, the two commanders and their escorts filling her narrow corridors like battleships in a canal, and Gideon walked ahead of them with her heart performing a slow, heavy percussion against her ribs.

* * *

The children were in the ward she'd converted from cargo bays—twenty-six small beds, twenty-six small figures, some sleeping, some sitting up, some watching the strangers arrive with the wide, unblinking attention of children assessing new adults. Gideon had dressed them in station coveralls rolled at the cuffs and ankles. Their original uniforms hung in a sealed locker, tagged and archived, because she'd decided in the first hour that seeing a seven-year-old in a captain's uniform would break something in whoever looked at it, and she needed these two unbroken for at least another day.

It broke them anyway.

Voss stopped three steps inside the ward and did not move again for a full minute. Gideon watched him scan the room—the small faces, the small hands, a girl asleep clutching a data-pad like a stuffed animal—and whatever calculation had been running behind his eyes stalled out. For a moment he was not a commander. He was a man looking at children and doing terrible arithmetic.

"These are..." He cleared his throat. "These are confirmed crew?"

"Biometric matches against Meridian service records. Ninety-eight percent confidence across the board." Gideon kept her voice clinical. Clinical was load-bearing. "That's Ensign Okonkwo. That's Lieutenant Marsh. That—" she pointed to a small boy with serious eyes sitting cross-legged on his bed, watching Voss with an intensity no five-year-old should have, "—is Captain Osei."

Adeyemi crouched down slowly, bringing herself to the children's eye level, and said, gently, "Hello. Can you tell me your name?"

The boy—Osei, the captain, though Gideon had privately taken to thinking of the youngest girl as Jude—looked at her for a long moment. Then he spoke.

He spoke, and the sounds that came out of him were the sounds from Gideon's dreams: liquid consonants, tonal rises, a rhythm like water finding its way downhill. Three syllables. Four. His small hands moved slightly as he talked, shaping the air.

Adeyemi straightened. "What did he say? Is that a language? Doctor, is that a *language*?"

"He vocalizes," Gideon said. "As I told you. It's non-referential as far as I've been able to determine. Patterns without semantics. Possibly neurological—their language centers may have regressed along with everything else. Some of the younger ones babble. The older ones produce more structured sequences, but I've found no correlation with any known linguistic family, living or dead. I've run it against everything in the Commission archives."

It was, she reflected later, the most elegant lie of her life: every word of it technically defensible, every claim one she could half-support with data, the truth hidden not by denial but by misdirection. *Non-referential.* She had spent two days mapping that language. It referenced *everything*.

Voss had recovered his composure, mostly. "And your assessment, Doctor? As our resident expert. What are the odds these children recover speech? Recover *memory*?"

Recover memory. Gideon felt the floor tilt beneath the question, because she had seen it already—fragments surfacing, the older children sometimes pausing mid-play to stare at a bulkhead as if remembering it being larger, a girl who had cried for an hour holding a wrench she'd found in engineering, calling it by a name in the dream-language that Gideon's gut insisted meant *my hands knew this*.

"Unknown," she said. "Neural regression of this completeness is unprecedented. If the memories are stored, they may be inaccessible. If they were overwritten—" she shrugged. "Then these children will grow up as children. New lives. New selves. The crew of the Meridian, functionally, died out there. What survived is... an echo."

An echo. She heard the word leave her mouth and felt it land somewhere in her chest and stay there, resonant, ominous, like a note struck against the hull.

Adeyemi was watching the children, not Gideon. Watching the way they watched *her*—because they were, Gideon realized with a cold slide of alarm, all of them oriented subtly toward the doorway, toward the newcomers, attentive in a way that nonverbal traumatized children shouldn't be. Attentive like listeners. Like people waiting to hear what would be decided about them.

"They seem calm," Adeyemi said. "For trauma victims."

"Children are resilient," Gideon said. "It's practically their defining feature."

It was the right answer. It was also, she suspected, the first lie Adeyemi didn't buy.

* * *

They left within the hour—Voss back to the *Vigilant* with orders to transmit full Meridian telemetry to the station, Adeyemi to the *Aegis* with a promise of medical archives—but the escorts stayed. Two marines from each fleet, billeted in guest quarters, nominally to assist, actually to watch. Gideon signed off on the arrangement with a smile that cost her more than the translation of the armistice had.

Alone again in the control room, she pulled up the internal log and recorded a private entry, voice low, encryption keyed to herself alone.

"Day twelve of beacon anomaly. Day three of the Meridian. Today I lied to two fleet commanders, to their faces, simultaneously, and they believed me. Or they believed me enough. Voss wants the children because children are evidence and evidence is ammunition. Adeyemi wants the truth, I think, but she wants it on her terms, in her hands, at her chosen hour. Neither of them can have it. Not yet. Maybe not ever."

She paused the recording. Through the viewport, the two warships hung in their mirror orbits, guns pointed politely elsewhere, and between them the beacon's signal sang its century-old song into the dark.

"Here's what I know that they don't," she continued. "Those children speak a language that predates both fleets. I dream in it. The beacon broadcasts in it. And today, when Captain Osei looked at Commander Voss and spoke, I understood every word. He said: *The tall one carries a key he does not know he carries.*"

She stopped the recording there. Some truths were too dangerous even for private logs.

Because there was one more thing—one detail she hadn't put in the report, hadn't spoken aloud, hadn't let herself think clearly. When the children had first seen her, three days ago, all twenty-six of them had gone silent at once. And then the littlest one, the girl she'd named Jude, had reached up and touched Gideon's face with one small hand and spoken a single sentence in the dream-language, a sentence Gideon had translated instantly, effortlessly, the way you understand your own heartbeat:

*You dreamed us here.*

Gideon stood alone in the control room of the loneliest place in human space, surrounded by two armed fleets and twenty-six impossible children, and began, carefully, to plan her first deliberate lie—not as a sin, but as architecture. A wall to protect them. A wall she would have to live inside.

Outside, the beacon pulsed. Patient as inheritance. Waiting to see what she'd build.
