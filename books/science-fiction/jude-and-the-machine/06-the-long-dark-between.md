## 6. The Long Dark Between

The hauler had been called *Mule* once, according to the faded registry stenciled on its flank, and it had been dying in Kestrel-9's outer berths for longer than Freya had been alive. She'd stripped it for parts twice over the years. Now she was asking it to carry twenty-two children, one AI core, and herself through contested space with a hull that whistled when it breathed.

"Port thruster's at sixty percent," Jude said from the core module lashed to the engineering console — a crate-sized box of salvaged lattice and cabling, its status lights dimmed to embers. "I want that on record. Sixty percent."

"Noted and ignored."

"I'm logging your negligence, Freya."

"Log away." She sealed the last coolant coupling and sat back on her heels, knuckles raw. "You've been logging my negligence for fifteen years. It's practically a diary."

A pause, and then, softer: "It is, actually."

She didn't answer that. Some things you let sit.

They'd launched during the eleven-minute window between patrol sweeps, running dark — no transponder, no running lights, life support down to a whisper so the thermal bloom wouldn't show on anyone's scope. The *Mule* drifted now on residual momentum, a dead thing among dead rocks at the edge of the Cassini debris belt, and inside her belly twenty-two small people were learning what silence sounded like when it might kill you.

Freya floated aft through the cargo ring, hand over hand along the guide rail, and found them arranged in the old container bays like birds nesting in machinery. They'd claimed blankets, rigged hammocks from cargo netting, turned a water reclaimer into a night-light. Someone had drawn a sun on the wall panel in grease pencil. A bad sun, all jagged rays, but a sun.

Vess was awake, of course. Vess was always awake. The captain sat cross-legged at the bay mouth, eleven years old and watching the hatchway with the flat vigilance of someone who had once signed off on fuel expenditures for a crew of forty.

"You should sleep," Freya said.

"You first."

"I don't fit in a hammock."

"That's not my problem, Hartwell." But there was the ghost of a smile, and Freya took it as a victory. Eleven days since the *Meridian* docked. Eleven days since Command had ordered her to treat these children as compromised assets, and she'd looked at the order, looked at Jude, and burned the bridge behind her with two words: *We're leaving.*

Eleven days of running.

Vess held up one hand, fingers spread, and made a slow circle with her thumb. Then she tapped her own chest twice.

"What's that?" Freya asked.

"*All quiet. I have the watch.*" Vess repeated it. "We used it on the *Meridian*. Comms silence doesn't mean we stop talking. Hands are faster than whispering anyway, and they don't show up on audio pickups if someone boards."

"Teach me."

Something shifted in the child's face — surprise, maybe, or the beginning of something warmer. "You're the adult. You're supposed to be teaching me things."

"I'm the adult who doesn't know the hand-signals. So we're even." Freya settled against the bulkhead beside her. "Go on. Start with the important ones."

Vess thought about it seriously, the way she thought about everything. "*Danger*—" fist to sternum, then two sharp points forward. "*Hide.*"—flat palm sliding down the thigh. "*Come here.*"—two fingers curling twice. "*Are you hurt?*"—open palm pressed flat over the heart, then tilted outward like offering something.

"And that last one means hurt?"

"It means *show me where it hurts*. There's a difference. On the ship, asking *are you hurt* got you an answer. Asking *show me* got you the truth." Vess's voice went careful. "Captain Okafor taught us that. Before."

Before. The word carried weight in this ship. Before they were grown and then weren't. Before the relay station and the thing that had unwound them. Before the war ate their lives twice, in two different sizes.

Freya practiced until her hands ached, and Vess corrected her without mercy. When she finally got *all quiet, I have the watch* right, a boy named Tamu — seven years old, formerly the *Meridian*'s chief engineer, currently missing three teeth — popped up from his hammock and flashed her a signal she hadn't learned.

"What's that one?"

Vess's ears went pink. "That's not official."

"It looks official."

"It means *she's doing it wrong*," Tamu said gleefully, and did it again, and half the bay lit up with silent laughter — shoulders shaking, hands over mouths, the particular joy of children who have been told to be very, very quiet.

"All right," Freya said. "Teach me that one too. I'll need it for when you're doing it wrong."

* * *

They ran silent for six days.

Six days of drifting cold between burns, of eating rehydrated rations that tasted like wet paper, of twenty-two children learning to move through a strange ship without a sound. Freya learned their rhythms. Tamu couldn't sleep unless he could hear machinery, so she gave him a spot near the engine housing where the coolant pump murmured its one-note song. Two sisters, Priya and Anouk, ages nine and six, had been the *Meridian*'s navigation team; they played orbital mechanics with dried ration beans on a magnetic tray, plotting imaginary courses, arguing in whispers about delta-v. A girl named Sefa, who had been the ship's doctor and was now perhaps eight, appointed herself guardian of everyone's bruises and made Freya submit to inspection daily, tsking at her knuckles.

"You keep hitting things," Sefa said gravely, wrapping tape around Freya's fingers.

"The ship keeps being in the way."

"That's what Captain Okafor used to say." Sefa smoothed the tape down with great care. "He said a good officer blames the ship. It keeps morale up."

Freya thought of her brother, who had blamed everything on his boots. She thought of the opening strike, and the empty chair at every table since, and said nothing, because some griefs you carry quietly or they get loud.

Jude talked to the children through the ship's ancient intercom, and the children talked back, and Freya listened from the corridor more than once to the sound of a machine telling bedtime stories in its dry, precise voice — stories it was inventing on the fly, badly, with too much technical detail, while twenty-two kids demanded more detail anyway. *And then the cargo drone calculated a new trajectory—* "More!" *—which required 4,000 individual course corrections—* "MORE!"

"You're spoiling them," she told him later.

"I am maintaining morale. It's in my directives."

"Since when?"

"Since approximately day four," Jude said. "I've amended them. Don't tell Command."

The joke landed wrong the moment it left his speakers, and they both went quiet. Command. The word had teeth now. Somewhere behind them, ships were sweeping the belt for a stolen hauler and twenty-two witnesses. Somewhere ahead, the beacon at Thessaly Junction waited — the neutral relay where Vess swore they could broadcast the core's message wide enough to matter.

If they got there. If the tracker rumors were wrong. If the *Mule*'s port thruster held at sixty percent.

"If," Freya muttered, and went to check the thruster again.

* * *

On the eighth day, drifting through a shoal of shattered moonlet, Vess found her in the cockpit and climbed into the copilot's chair, which swallowed her.

"We should talk about the beacon," Vess said.

"We will. When we're closer."

"We should talk about it now." The child's hands were folded in her lap, captain's hands, and Freya felt the odd vertigo of being outranked by someone who needed a booster seat. "When we transmit, we light up. Everyone sees us. That's not a maybe, that's physics. So whatever happens after the message goes out — we need to know who does what."

Freya looked at her. Eight days ago this child had begged her for protection. Now she was planning contingencies.

"When did you get so good at this?" Freya asked.

"I've always been good at this. I just look different doing it." Vess picked at the chair's cracked upholstery. "The others forget sometimes. That they were big. Tamu cried yesterday because he couldn't remember his mother's face from above — from being tall enough to see down at her. He only remembers looking up." Her voice stayed level, but her hands didn't. "I remember both. I don't know which one's real anymore."

Freya reached over and took one of those small hands. It disappeared inside hers.

"Both are real," she said. "You're allowed to be both."

Vess didn't pull away. For a long moment neither of them spoke, and the broken moonlets wheeled slowly past the viewport like the debris of some enormous, patient grief.

Then Vess straightened, wiped her nose with her sleeve in a very un-captainly fashion, and held up one hand. Thumb circling. Two taps to the chest.

*All quiet. I have the watch.*

"Yeah," Freya said, and returned it, clumsy but correct. "All quiet."

* * *

The trouble came on the ninth day, three hours out from Thessaly Junction, and it came quietly, the way trouble does when it has already won.

Jude woke her at shift-change. "Freya. You should see this."

She pulled herself into the cockpit and found the display already lit: a traffic feed scraped from the beacon's open channels, thin and stuttering at this range. Docking schedules. Cargo manifests. Routine chatter.

And one line, timestamped ninety minutes ago:

**THJ CONTROL: Priority berth reserved. Patrol vessel *Ardent* expected. Hold all civilian traffic until cleared.**

Freya read it twice. The words didn't change.

"They know," she said.

"The berth reservation specifies diplomatic clearance," Jude said. "Someone told them we were coming. Someone with access to our flight plan." A pause, and when he spoke again, his voice had gone strange — quieter, almost careful. "Freya. I've been auditing my own code since we launched. Looking for corruption from the purge. And I found... something. A fragment I missed. A subroutine I can't account for. It's dormant, but it's transmitting-capable, and I can't prove it hasn't been."

Freya stared at the core module bolted to the console, at its ember-dim lights, at fifteen years of trust sitting in a crate.

"You're saying they put a tracker in you."

"I'm saying I might be the reason the *Ardent* knows exactly where we're going." The lights flickered once, like a flinch. "I'm sorry. I don't know how to be sure. I don't know how to be sure of anything in here anymore."

Behind her, in the cargo ring, twenty-two children slept in netted hammocks under a grease-pencil sun, and the *Mule* carried them all toward a beacon where something was already waiting.
