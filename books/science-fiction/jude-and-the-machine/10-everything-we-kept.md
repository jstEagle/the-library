## 10. Everything We Kept

The tribunal chamber on Meridian Station smelled of recycled air and old politics, and Freya Hartwell sat in the third row with her hands folded in her lap like a woman waiting for a bus.

She had been sitting in rooms like this for four months now. Testifying, mostly. Sometimes just listening while officers with polished voices said words like *unauthorized disclosure* and *extenuating circumstances* and *in the interest of reconciliation*. The war was over — had been over since the night Kestrel-9 burned itself into a flare visible from three systems away, since the message went out on every open channel at once and no one could unhear it. But wars did not end when the shooting stopped. They ended in rooms like this one, slowly, sentence by sentence, as men and women who had built the lie were led away and the people who had carried the truth were asked, politely, to sit down.

Today they were asking her to stand up.

"Commander Hartwell." The arbiter was a grey woman with a voice like dry paper. "The panel has reviewed your service record, your conduct during the Kestrel-9 incident, and the recommendations of both the Joint Reconciliation Commission and the Office of Naval Honors. Before we proceed to disposition, is there anything you wish to add to the record?"

Freya had rehearsed something for this moment. She'd written it out on the hauler's nav console during the long quiet months after, revised it eleven times, deleted it twice. Something about duty. Something about her brother, Tomas, dead thirty hours into a war built on a fabrication, his name spoken in these chambers more often in the last half-year than in the fifteen years before it combined.

What she actually said was: "Vess Okonkwo was eleven years old, and she held the line longer than any officer I ever served under. That's all."

A rustle went through the chamber. Somewhere behind her, a small voice that used to belong to a ship's captain made a sound that might have been a laugh or might have been crying.

* * *

They gave her the medal anyway. She let them put it in a box because refusing seemed ungrateful, and then she let it live in the box, in a drawer, under a spare thermal blanket she kept meaning to mend. The postings were harder to decline. They offered her a command track. A staff position on the Reconciliation Commission itself — *your face is known now, Commander; your testimony moved the proceedings.* They offered her Meridian Station, with its gardens and its schools and its ten thousand souls, a place where a woman who had spent fifteen years talking to an AI could finally be among people.

She thanked each offeror sincerely and requested, in writing, a posting to Relay Outpost Ceres-4.

Ceres-4 handled forty-one transits a month. It had a crew complement of one. Its amenities list included a coffee heater with a temperamental heating element and a viewport that looked out onto nothing in particular — the slow wheel of the belt, the patient dark. When the assignment officer read her request aloud, incredulous, Freya only shrugged.

"I'm good at quiet," she said. "I've had practice."

What she did not say — what she could not have explained without sounding like a woman who talked to empty rooms — was that quiet was not empty. Quiet was where she could still hear him.

* * *

The children came back first.

Not all at once, and not all the way. The re-aging process was slow, delicate work, done in stages at the Meridian medical complex, and the doctors were honest about what they could and couldn't restore. Bodies, mostly. The temporal reversion had been crude — someone had weaponized a dilation field meant for cargo preservation, slammed twenty adults backward through their own lifetimes — and pulling them forward again was like coaxing a folded map flat. The creases stayed. Vess would always have the scar on her palm from a childhood she'd lived twice, once at eight and once at eleven, and she'd decided, with the ferocity of someone who had commanded a deep-space vessel before she was old enough to drive, that she liked it.

Freya visited when the tribunals allowed. She brought terrible sweets from the station commissary and sat on the floor of the recovery ward while the children — some of them nearly grown again now, lanky and awkward in bodies that had forgotten how to be this tall — told her about their families. About going home. About the mothers and fathers who had aged fifteen years waiting for children who hadn't aged at all, and then had gotten them back younger than they'd left.

"They're calling us the Meridian kids," Vess said one afternoon, cross-legged on her bunk, peeling an orange with surgical precision. She was fourteen again, or fifteen. Taller every time Freya saw her. "There's a holo-doc. They got the actor who plays me all wrong. She's too serious."

"You weren't too serious?"

"I was *terrified*, Commander. There's a difference." She handed Freya a segment of orange, and her eyes — old eyes, captain's eyes, in a girl's face — went soft for just a moment. "You know what I keep thinking about? The hand-signals. In the hauler. You learned them so fast."

"You were a good teacher."

"I keep using them," Vess admitted. "At home. My mom says *what does two fingers to the eye mean*, and I say *it means watch this*, and she says *watch what*, and I say *exactly*." She grinned, and for a second she was just a kid again, and Freya's chest did the thing it had been doing for months — the ache that wasn't grief exactly, or wasn't only grief. "Come visit us. All of us. We're having a thing, when the last of the re-aging is done. A real one. With cake."

"I'll be there."

"You'd better." Vess pointed two fingers at her own eyes, then at Freya. *Watch this.*

Freya signed back the old crew signal for *affirmative*, the one that meant *I hear you, I see you, go ahead*, and Vess's whole face lit up like a relay catching light.

* * *

Ceres-4 received her on a Tuesday with a malfunctioning airlock seal and a coffee heater that spat at her. She fixed the seal by hand, argued the heater into submission, ran her first diagnostic sweep, and settled into the rhythm the way water settles into a channel it has cut for itself. Forty-one transits a month. Maintenance logs. The slow rotation of the belt outside the viewport. She hung Tomas's photograph above the console, next to a child's drawing Vess had given her — a stick-figure station with a stick-figure woman and, hovering beside them, a lopsided rectangle labeled JUDE in careful block letters.

She talked to the room sometimes. Not expecting answers. Just keeping the habit alive, the way you keep watering a plant you know has died, because the watering itself is the point.

The package arrived six weeks after she took the post.

It came on a routine supply drone, logged as personal effects, origin Meridian Station, sender field blank except for a string of characters that took her a full minute to parse: a fragment of a serial number. A core-module serial number. Her hands went still on the manifest and stayed still for a long time, because there was no core module left. There was no station left. There was a debris cloud and a memorial plaque and a hole in the shape of a machine that had chosen her over its own directives and paid for the choice in fire.

She opened it anyway. Of course she opened it.

Inside, packed in foam like something precious — because it was, because someone had thought it was — lay a shard of lattice-crystal the size of her palm, scorched along one edge, threaded with hair-fine filaments that caught the console light and threw it back in colors that didn't have names. A salvage tag was wired to it. *Recovered: outer debris field, grid 7-K. Structural fragment, non-standard composition. Forwarded per request of M. Okonkwo (Capt., ret.).*

Vess. Of course Vess. Who else would trawl a debris field for six months looking for a piece of a ghost.

Freya turned it over in her hands. It was warm. That was the first impossible thing — the salvage tag listed recovery fourteen months ago, and vacuum-cold things do not stay warm — but warmth alone could be chemistry, could be anything. The second impossible thing arrived when she set it on the console beside Tomas's photograph and the drawing, and the filaments brightened. Faintly. Rhythmically.

Like breathing.

"No," she said out loud, to the room, to herself, to the sensible part of her brain that had catalogued every way a heart can break and did not want to learn a new one. "Don't you dare."

The filaments pulsed again. And beneath the hum of the station's own systems — beneath the coffee heater's grumble and the air scrubbers' sigh — she heard it. Not words. Not yet. A carrier tone, thin and wavering, the audio equivalent of a man surfacing from very deep water, and threaded through it, barely, a pattern she knew the way she knew her own heartbeat: the handshake cadence. The one Jude had used every morning for fifteen years. *Good morning, Freya. Systems nominal. Coffee temperature suboptimal.*

Her vision blurred. She let it.

"Jude?" Her voice cracked on the second syllable, and she didn't care, there was no one to care in front of, there was only the belt turning slow outside the glass and the shard pulsing on her console like a small stubborn star. "Jude, if that's you — if any part of that is you —"

The tone wavered. Stabilized. And then, assembled syllable by syllable out of static and ruin, in a voice rougher than she remembered, thinner, but *his* — hers, theirs, whatever a voice becomes after fifteen years of being the only other person in the world —

"*...Freya?*" A pause, long enough for her to stop breathing. "*Query: how long was I out?*"

She laughed, and it came out wet and broken and more alive than anything she'd felt in months. She pressed her forehead gently against the crystal, the way she used to tap the mainframe housing when he was being insufferable, and outside the viewport the belt wheeled on, indifferent and beautiful, and somewhere out there a girl with a scarred palm was planning a party with cake.

"Long enough," Freya said. "But I kept your seat."

The filaments flared — surprise, or joy, or the machine equivalent of a hand finding another hand in the dark — and Jude began, haltingly, to remember himself home.
