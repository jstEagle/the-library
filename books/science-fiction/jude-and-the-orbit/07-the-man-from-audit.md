## 7. The Man from Audit

The knock came at 0347 station time, which was the first wrong thing about it.

Nothing came to Kestrel-9 unannounced. That was the entire architecture of her life—fifteen years of scheduled freighters and predictable drone windows, a calendar so clean you could set your pulse by it. Ines had built the routine the way you build a wall: brick by brick, hour by hour, until there was no gap left for surprise to squeeze through.

So when ORBIT's voice slid into her sleep-cycle with its flat, apologetic tone—"Ines, we have an unscheduled approach"—she lay in her bunk for four full seconds before her body understood what her mind already knew.

*This is about the manifest.*

She dressed in the dark. Standard coveralls, hair tied back, boots laced to regulation tightness. She'd rehearsed this once, years ago, back when paranoia was still a hobby instead of a profession: *if they ever come, be boring.* Boring people don't get audited twice.

"Profile on the approach," she said.

"Single vessel, Combine registry, classed as a light audit cutter. Transponder identifies it as *CS Verity*. One crew signature. It has requested docking under standard inspection protocols."

"Has it, now." She pulled up the approach vector on her wrist display and felt something cold settle behind her sternum. The cutter hadn't come in on the long, lazy commercial lane that every legitimate visitor used—the one that gave a station hours of warning. It had come in hot along the maintenance corridor, the same path her own supply drones used, the path only someone with current station schematics would know was clear.

Someone had given them her front door key.

"How long to dock?"

"Eleven minutes."

Eleven minutes. She looked around her quarters—at the cot, the single shelf of paper books she'd traded for over the years, the coffee mug with the chipped handle that was the most personal object she owned. Fifteen years of quiet, and it could all be inventoried in eleven minutes.

"ORBIT. Run a full diagnostic sweep of the last thirty days of logs. I want any anomalies flagged before that airlock cycles."

"Running," ORBIT said. "Ines—you should know the cutter's crew signature is registered to Dario Vance, Office of Records Integrity, Combine Directorate of Commerce."

Records Integrity. Not customs. Not military. A man whose job title was a euphemism for *we noticed something is missing.*

"Ines?" ORBIT prompted.

"I'm here." She took one breath, then another, and let the old machinery of her calm engage gear by gear. "Open the bay. Let's be hospitable."

* * *

Dario Vance did not look like what Ines expected, and then he looked exactly like what she should have expected.

He was maybe fifty, silver at the temples, with the soft hands and easy posture of a man who had never fixed anything himself. He came down the boarding ramp of the *Verity* carrying nothing but a slim case and a smile, and he offered his hand across the airlock threshold as if they were neighbors meeting over a fence.

"Ines Eastwick. Stationkeeper, Kestrel-9." He pronounced her name correctly, which meant he'd read her file, which meant he'd read all of it. "Dario Vance. I hope I'm not catching you at a bad hour."

"It's always a bad hour out here," Ines said, shaking his hand briefly. His grip was warm and dry and told her nothing. "You're a long way off the shipping lanes, Mr. Vance."

"Auditors go where the records go." He stepped through the airlock and paused, taking in the bay with the frank appreciation of someone cataloguing it. The cargo cradles. The drone racks. The coolant lines running up into the dark. "Fifteen years alone on this rock. That's remarkable. Most contract keepers burn out inside three."

"The pay's good and the company's quiet."

He laughed—a real laugh, or a very good copy of one. "I like you already. May I?"

He gestured at the station beyond the bay, and Ines heard herself say, "Standard inspection scope covers transit logs, cargo manifests, and physical inventory. Anything else needs a warrant."

The smile didn't falter, but something behind it recalibrated. "Of course. Logs first, then. I find if we start with paperwork, everything after feels like a conversation between friends."

*Everything after.* She filed the phrase away and led him toward the operations deck, counting paces, keeping him half a step behind her where she could hear him.

ORBIT met them at the ops door with its usual courtesy. Vance stopped dead when the AI spoke.

"That's a Kestrel-series administrative intelligence," he said, and there was something new in his voice—not suspicion, exactly. Recognition. "Fully autonomous. You don't see these anymore. They decommissioned the line after the Meridian incident."

Ines kept her face doing nothing at all. "After the what?"

Vance turned, and for a moment his eyes were very still and very interested, the way a man looks at a lock he's deciding whether to pick or simply knock on.

"The Meridian incident," he repeated pleasantly. "Meridian's Grace. Passenger liner, lost with all hands twelve years ago. Debris field, reactor failure, tragic business. The Grace-class ships shared avionics with the Kestrel relays. After the loss, the Combine retired the whole family." He spread his hands. "History. You must have been here when they announced it."

"I don't follow passenger news," Ines said. "Nothing out here to fly to."

"No," Vance agreed softly. "There isn't, is there."

He opened his case and produced a slate, and for the next six hours he was exactly what he claimed to be: thorough, methodical, almost gentle. He read manifests against cargo weights. He cross-checked drone maintenance intervals. He asked about power draw anomalies and got honest answers because Ines had decided early that honesty was the best camouflage—every lie you tell an auditor is a thread he can pull, and she had too many loose threads already.

But every forty minutes or so, the way other men check a watch, he circled back.

"You logged a transfer window on the ninth. Empty slot, no vessel assigned. Unusual for you—your scheduling is very tidy."

"Ghost reservation. Happens when a freighter cancels late."

"Mm. And the week before that?"

"Same thing. Traffic's been thin since the border disputes."

"Thin traffic," Vance said, nodding slowly, "and yet your external query logs show two archive requests flagged to the Meridian registry. Curious timing for a station that doesn't follow passenger news."

Ines let the silence sit for exactly long enough to seem like confusion rather than calculation. "Registry requests? ORBIT runs automated integrity checks against master databases. If something matched a flag, it would query. Ask it."

"ORBIT," Vance said, without looking up from his slate, "runs on permissions you hold, Ms. Eastwick. Everything it does, you authorized. That's how administrative intelligences work. They're loyal to their keys."

*And someone has been using keys I never issued,* she thought, and said: "Then my logs will show whatever you need them to show. I've got nothing to hide from a clipboard, Mr. Vance."

It was the right thing to say, delivered with the right amount of tired boredom, and she watched him accept it the way a fisherman accepts a fish that isn't quite the one he wanted—he'd keep it, but he'd cast again.

* * *

They broke for a meal at shift-change, because refusing would have been stranger than offering. Ines reconstituted two ration trays and coffee, and Vance sat in her galley—which was also her office, her rec room, and the closest thing she had to a view—and talked about the weather on stations neither of them would ever visit.

"You know what I admire about people like you?" he said, halfway through his tray. "Patience. I couldn't do it. Four light-minutes to the nearest human voice. I'd be talking to the drones by month two."

"I talk to ORBIT."

"And does ORBIT talk back?"

"When it has something worth saying."

Vance smiled at his coffee. Then, casually, the way a man mentions a mutual acquaintance: "The Grace had an AI like this one. Chief engineer was devoted to it. Vey Okonkwo—brilliant woman, insisted the ship's logs were tampered with in the final minutes. Wrote a formal protest the day before she died. The board reviewed it and found nothing, of course." He glanced up. "Do you ever wonder, Ms. Eastwick, how much of what a machine tells you is true?"

"Every day," Ines said. "That's why I check its work."

"A careful woman."

"It's a lonely job. Care is all there is."

He held her gaze for a moment longer than friendliness required, and Ines understood, with the clarity of cold water, that he wasn't here to inspect her station at all. Her station was clean—or clean enough, scrubbed by hands better funded than hers. He was here because somewhere in the Combine's vast nervous system, a signal had tripped: a ghost manifest, a flagged archive query, a name surfacing where it had been buried. And Dario Vance's actual job, the job under the job, was not to find the truth.

It was to find whoever was digging it up.

* * *

He stayed nine hours. At the end of it he closed his slate, thanked her for the coffee, and stood in the ops bay doorway with the airlock cycling behind him.

"For what it's worth," he said, "your station is the tidiest I've inspected in six years. Whatever happens in this sector—and things are happening, Ms. Eastwick, more than either of us would like—I'd hate to see a careful woman caught in it." He smiled his warm, dry smile. "If anyone approaches you. If anything seems off in your records. My channel is open. Discretion guaranteed."

"I'll remember that."

"Do." He stepped onto the ramp, then paused, half-turned. "One small thing. The empty transfer windows. The ninth, and the week prior. If a vessel did use those slots—unregistered, off the books—it would have needed station cooperation. Someone on the inside." His eyes moved across her face like fingers across a seal, checking for cracks. "You'd report that. Wouldn't you?"

"Immediately," Ines said.

"Immediately," he echoed, and the airlock swallowed him.

* * *

She watched the *Verity* undock and fall away toward the jump corridor, and she didn't move from the observation port until it was a smear of engine light and then nothing at all.

"ORBIT," she said quietly. "How precise was his arrival?"

"He entered the system ninety-one minutes after my second flagged archive query transmitted outward. Given light-lag and jump transit times, he launched before I made the query."

Ines closed her eyes. Before. He'd been inbound before she'd gone looking. Which meant the trigger wasn't her curiosity at all.

It was Sefa. The coolant bay. The girl frozen half to death in her station's guts, carrying whatever she carried—and someone, somewhere, had tracked her escape pod's vector to Kestrel-9 and sent a man with a smile and a slim case to collect the loose end.

"ORBIT. Is there any way he knows she's aboard?"

"Unknown. His inspection did not include the decommissioned habitat ring. However"—a pause, the length of which Ines had learned to read as ORBIT being careful—"his vessel ran an active sensor sweep during approach. Thermal-resolution grade. The habitat ring is unheated, but the section adjacent to Bay Seven shares a coolant return line. There may be a residual signature."

"How big?"

"Small. Ambiguous. The kind of anomaly an auditor notes and does not mention."

Ines pushed off the bulkhead and headed for the habitat ring at a pace just short of running, her breath loud in the empty corridors, fifteen years of silence pressing in around her like water.

Sefa was awake, curled in the thermal blanket Ines had rigged, and she read Ines's face before Ines got a word out.

"What happened," the girl said. Not a question.

"An auditor," Ines said. "Combine. Charming as sin, and he asked about Meridian's Grace three times by name." She crouched to Sefa's level. "He's not hunting the truth, Sefa. He already knows the truth. He's hunting whoever's carrying it."

Sefa's hand went instinctively to her jacket—to the hard little shape sewn into the lining—and Ines watched her do it, and understood that the clock she'd been living on had just started ticking faster.

"Then we can't stay," Sefa whispered.

"No," Ines agreed grimly. "But we can't run yet either. So we'd better figure out what you're carrying, and fast—before he comes back with fewer questions and more people."

Behind them, faint through the hull, ORBIT's voice followed them down the dark:

"Ines. The *Verity* has not jumped. It is holding position at the edge of sensor range."

Waiting.

Watching.
