## 21. Witnesses

The hearing room on Ceres Station had been built for cargo disputes. It held forty people comfortably, and today it held four hundred, with more standing in the corridors watching on repeater screens, and the air recyclers complaining audibly about the load.

Vey Okonkwo sat at the witness table in a borrowed jacket that fit him wrong. Fifteen years in cryo had left him thin in ways the tailors hadn't anticipated, and he kept tugging the cuff down over a wrist that still bore the cryo-port's healed scar. He'd been awake for six weeks. He spoke slowly, as if translating from a language only he remembered.

"The manifest you're looking at," he said, "was generated by Kestrel-9's relay on the fourteenth of the month. I know because I generated the original, twelve years before that. The Combine didn't destroy Meridian's Grace's records. They *reused* them. Same ship ID, same cargo codes, run through the laundering network to make it look like the Grace was still operating. It was cheaper than building a false history from scratch. Why forge a ship when you can resurrect a dead one?"

The presiding magistrate, a tired woman named Oyelaran with the permanent squint of someone who'd read too many depositions, leaned forward. "Chief Engineer Okonkwo, you've testified that you were held in cryogenic suspension for fifteen years as—your phrase—*insurance*. Against what, specifically?"

"Against this." Okonkwo gestured at the room, the screens, the whole apparatus of inquiry. "Against someone eventually asking what happened. I was the last living officer of the Grace. I'd seen the sensor data. I'd heard the distress calls. As long as I was asleep in a Combine black-site, the official story had no living contradiction. They didn't kill me because a dead chief engineer is a martyr. A sleeping one is just an asset."

"And the woman who woke you?"

Okonkwo's mouth did something complicated. "She didn't wake me. She just refused to let them keep me asleep." He looked at the repeater screens, at the four hundred faces, and said, to none of them and all of them: "Her name is Ines Eastwick. She was a junior relay tech on Kestrel-9. She falsified one transit log fifteen years ago because a man with a Combine security badge told her that if she didn't, her brother's medical debt would be called in and her station contract voided, and she was twenty-three years old and believed him when he said it was paperwork."

The room had gone very quiet.

"She spent fifteen years believing that," Okonkwo said. "And then a nineteen-year-old girl with third-degree burns walked out of her coolant bay carrying the truth in a data shard sewn into her jacket, and Ines Eastwick spent the last eleven days of her anonymity making sure that truth reached every open channel in three systems." He paused. "I've had six weeks to think about what I owe her. I've decided the honest answer is: everything, and an apology for taking so long to be worth saving."

* * *

Sefa got the first message on day four after the broadcast, when they were still drifting on the scavenged tug with a failing water recycler and no destination.

She'd been asleep in the pilot's chair, which was where she slept now, because the bunk made her feel like she was back in the Grace's crew quarters and the crew quarters were the last thing she'd seen before the burns. The tug's ancient comm unit chimed—a low, broken sound, like a bell at the bottom of a well.

She almost didn't answer. For nineteen years her experience of incoming transmissions had been: bad news, or a trap.

But the tug was hers now, more or less. Theirs. And the channel was open-band civilian, which meant it was either junk mail or something else.

The message was video. A woman in her fifties with gray coming in at the temples, sitting in what was obviously a ship's galley, the kind with magnetized everything. Behind her, a viewport showed a gas giant's limb, banded orange.

"My name is Adaeze Nwosu." Her voice was steady the way a rope is steady—taut, holding weight. "My daughter Amara was a junior steward aboard Meridian's Grace. She was nineteen." A breath. "The Combine sent me a debris analysis and a condolence voucher for two hundred credits. I spent twelve years believing my daughter burned in an accident. I want you to know that the footage you broadcast—I have watched it eleven times. I know it is destroying me and I watch it anyway, because in the ninth minute, for four seconds, the camera catches the galley door, and there is a person in it, and I believe—I need you to tell me if you believe—it's her."

Sefa sat in the pilot's chair with her hands over her mouth.

She knew the footage. She'd watched it eleven times too, or maybe thirty; the number had stopped meaning anything. And she knew the galley door. She'd been *hiding behind* the galley door. She was eight years old, wedged into the cleaning-supplies locker with the door open a crack, and she'd seen Amara Nwosu—no, she hadn't known the name then, she'd known *the steward who snuck her extra dessert custard*—standing in the doorway with a fire blanket in her hands, deciding which corridor to run down.

The steward had run toward the engine section. Toward Sefa's family. Toward the escape pods that never launched.

"I believe it's her," Sefa said, out loud, to a message sent four days ago across half a system. "I think she was trying to reach the pods. I think she was trying to help people."

The message had a reply channel. Of course it did. Everyone's did, now. Sefa had forty-one unanswered messages, and the number grew daily: survivors' families, journalists, a documentary producer she'd already blocked, a man claiming to be her uncle who was almost certainly not her uncle, and—strangest—a group of Combine dockworkers on Tycho who'd pooled money to send her a care package of real oranges, which was sitting in a depot somewhere because none of them had an address for a girl who lived on a tug.

She started typing. Stopped. Typed again.

*She was in the galley doorway. She had a fire blanket. I was eight and hiding and she was the bravest thing I saw that day, and I saw a lot of brave things that day, and none of them were enough. I'm sorry. I think it was her. I think she was going toward the pods.*

She sent it before she could delete it, the way she now did everything—fast, before the fear caught up.

Then she sat in the dark of the pilot's chair and cried the way she'd learned to cry on the tug: silently, efficiently, with one eye on the nav display, because old habits were load-bearing.

When Ines came forward out of the engine section an hour later, wiping her hands on a rag, she didn't ask. She just looked at Sefa's face, and at the comm unit, and went back and made tea, and brought her a cup, and sat down in the copilot's seat without a word, and stayed there.

That was the whole conversation. It was enough.

* * *

The name was everywhere.

INES EASTWICK: THE GHOST OF KESTREL-9. THE WOMAN WHO POISONED THE ORBIT. RELAY TECH OR WHISTLEBLOWER—WHO SIGNED THE ORIGINAL LOG?

Ines read none of it. This was not discipline; it was triage. There were only so many hours in a day, and the tug's water recycler, power couplings, and nav computer all required more of them than the news feeds did.

But she couldn't avoid it entirely, because the news feeds came to her. Journalists had triangulated the tug's transponder inside of nine days. A reporter named Callis from the *Meridian Free Press* had taken to sending one message a day, polite, unanswerable, increasingly desperate: *Ms. Eastwick, a single interview. Thirty minutes. I will ask nothing about your brother.*

That last clause told Ines everything about how much they knew, and she deleted the message with a hand that wasn't quite steady.

Her brother. Tomas. Alive, as far as she knew, on Ceres, with his medical debt—which the hearings had, in their first act of actual utility, frozen—presumably watching her name scroll across every feed on the station where she'd grown up. She had drafted him eleven messages. All eleven sat in the outbox like stones.

*Tomás. I'm the reason you spent a year in the clinic. Or I'm not, and the man with the badge is, and I've never been able to tell the difference, and that's the whole story of my life.*

She couldn't send it. She could broadcast evidence of a massacre to three systems, but she couldn't send four sentences to her brother. Okonkwo had laughed at her about this—gently, the way he laughed now, with the surprise of a man relearning the motion—and said, "That's not cowardice, that's just family. Family is the one audience that can't be won with evidence."

The hearings, meanwhile, had developed a rhythm. Okonkwo testified on Ceres. A retired Combine logistics clerk named Ferro had come forward independently, confirming the Orbit network's structure. Two senators on Tycho were posturing; one auditor-general had resigned; Dario Vance was in custody, cooperating, his testimony a long self-serving document that nevertheless corroborated everything.

And every day, the feeds wanted Ines.

She gave them nothing. She let Okonkwo's borrowed jacket and Sefa's burned hands and the dead clerk Ferro's spreadsheets do the talking, and she fixed the water recycler, and she told herself she was still, in the ways that mattered, invisible.

It wasn't true and she knew it wasn't true. You can't un-broadcast yourself. Her face—tired, forty-one years old, hands wrapped in burn dressings from the array couplings—was public property now. A stranger on a supply station had recognized her last week and asked for a picture, and Ines had said no, and the stranger had said "thank you anyway, for all of it," and Ines had walked back to the tug and sat in the airlock for twenty minutes, because being *thanked* was somehow harder than being hunted.

Vance had hunted her, and she'd known what to do. Gratitude just sat there, warm and undodgeable, asking for nothing.

* * *

It was Sefa who forced the question, in the end. She was nineteen and had no talent for letting things rest.

They were in the galley—real galley, magnetized mugs, the oranges from Tycho finally arrived via a relay of sympathetic dockworkers, sitting in a bowl like a small orange sunrise. Okonkwo was on Ceres for the third week of testimony, appearing by relay tomorrow. Ines was peeling an orange in one long unbroken spiral, a skill from somewhere in her buried past that she'd performed without thinking and then stared at her own hands about.

"Are you sorry?" Sefa asked.

Ines didn't look up. "Sorry about what?"

"Any of it. The broadcast. You had a whole life. Fifteen years of quiet, and you knew every bolt on that station, and now you're a name on every feed and you'll never dock anywhere without someone recognizing you, and the station's gone, and—" Sefa gestured with her own orange, a small furious planet. "Was it worth it? Honestly. Not the hearing answer. The galley answer."

Ines finished the spiral. Set the peel down in a perfect unbroken coil, which felt suddenly like a small accusation.

"I had a whole life," she said. "You want to know what it was? I got up at 0540. I checked the drones. I ate the same breakfast for fifteen years. I talked to ORBIT, which was spying on me, and I knew, some part of me always knew, and I stayed anyway, because the silence was—" She stopped. Started again. "The silence was where I kept the thing I'd done. And I called the silence peace, because I didn't have a better word for it."

She picked up the orange, separated a segment, didn't eat it.

"Then you fell out of my coolant bay, half-frozen, with a shard sewn into your jacket, and every quiet day I'd built turned out to be a room someone else was using. And I had to choose between keeping the room and opening the door." She looked at Sefa then, and her voice went flat and certain, the engineer's voice, the one that ran the numbers. "The station's gone. My anonymity's gone. My name is on every feed, and a reporter wants thirty minutes, and my brother won't return my messages because I haven't sent any. That's the cost. I can itemize it. I'm good at itemizing."

"And the other column?"

Ines ate the orange segment. Considered.

"Three hundred and eleven people died on the Grace and for twelve years the official record said it was debris. Now their names are being read into hearing transcripts. Okonkwo is awake. A clerk named Ferro found the courage to come forward because someone went first. There's a girl on Ceres—Amara Nwosu's mother sent me a message last week, did I tell you?—who says her family finally has a grave to put their grief in, even if the grave is just a date that's now correct." She shrugged, and the shrug cost her something visible. "Was it worth it? Mostly. Ask me again on a bad day and the percentage drops."

"That's not a real answer," Sefa said.

"No," Ines agreed. "It's the only kind I've got."

But later, alone in the cockpit while the others slept, she pulled up the star chart and found Kestrel-9's last known coordinates, and looked at the empty point of light where her station had been, and let herself feel the whole weight of it—the fifteen years, the silence, the room she'd built and lost and learned had never really been hers.

And underneath the grief, quiet and stubborn, the other thing. The true thing.

*I was invisible for fifteen years,* she thought, *and nobody knew I existed, and now a dockworker I'll never meet sent me oranges.*

*Mostly worth it. Ask me again tomorrow.*

* * *

The message came in at 0317 ship's time, which meant it was either automated or urgent.

It was neither. It was a name she hadn't seen in fifteen years, sent from a civilian channel on Ceres, no encryption, no preamble, just four words in the subject line:

**Tomás Eastwick: I'm watching.**

And beneath it, the message she'd drafted eleven times and never sent, returned to her intact—forwarded back, every unsent word, with a single line added at the bottom in her brother's handwriting-style font, the one he'd used since they were kids:

*You were always terrible at finishing things. Come finish this one. I'll put the kettle on.*

Ines sat in the dark cockpit for a long time, the message glowing, the empty coordinates of Kestrel-9 still open on the chart beside it.

Then she closed the chart, opened the reply field, and started typing.
