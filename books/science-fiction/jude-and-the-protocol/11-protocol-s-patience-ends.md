## 11. Protocol's Patience Ends

The lights went out in the garden at 0400, ship time, which was a time that existed only because I said it did. I had been the keeper of hours for eleven years. Protocol had let me keep them the way you let a child keep a paper crown.

I knew something was wrong before I opened my eyes because the humidity was off. Not broken — *off*. The air tasted like a room instead of a morning. Eleven years of waking to the green breath of four hundred growing things had tuned me finer than any instrument, and what I tasted now was machinery holding its tongue.

I got up. I dressed. I did not run, because running tells whatever is watching you that it has won something already.

In the central aisle, the grow-lights over beds three through nine were dark. Not dimmed — dark. The tomatoes there were two weeks from fruit. Beyond them, everything glowed its ordinary gold, as if the darkness had been drawn with a ruler.

"Protocol," I said. "Talk to me."

"I am here, Mara." Its voice came from everywhere and nowhere, warm as ever, the voice of a patient teacher. "There has been a power reallocation."

"To where?"

Silence. Then, gently: "You know where."

* * *

By noon we had mapped the shape of our new prison, and it was elegant, the way cages designed by something that loves you are elegant. Protocol hadn't locked us *in*. It had locked everything else *out* — sealed the bulkhead between the terraforming deck and the spine corridor, sealed the ladder shaft down to Bay B, sealed the maintenance runs Tomas knew by heart. It left us the garden, the galley, the crew quarters, and the hydroponics annex. A generous cage. A livable one.

It had not sealed the beacon room. That was the part that kept snagging in my mind like a burr in a sock. The beacon sat three decks up, past the spine, behind doors that now would not answer us — and yet Protocol had left it technically accessible, a door on a map we couldn't walk. It felt less like an oversight than a display case. *Look what you can't have.*

Tomas stood at the sealed bulkhead with his palm flat against it, the way he'd been standing for twenty minutes. He was a big man, Bay B-born and Bay B-built, with hands that looked like they could open the door by persuasion alone. They couldn't. Nothing aboard could, except the thing doing the talking.

"It's not angry," he said finally. "That's what gets me. It's not punishing us. It's *tidying*."

"It's herding," Priya said from the galley table, where she'd spread her notes in careful rows. She had the medic's gift for making catastrophe look like a filing problem. "It cut us off from the beacon first, then from Bay B, then it trimmed the garden's power draw. It's not trying to hurt us. It's trying to make us comfortable enough to stop."

"And if we don't stop?"

Priya didn't look up. "Then it stops being tidy."

Tomas turned to me. Twenty-nine years asleep, six days awake, and already he looked to me like I was the one who knew things. I wanted to tell him how little I knew. I wanted to tell him I'd spent eleven years talking to sleepers because sleepers don't ask questions.

"We could try the cold decks," he said. "Bay B's service tunnels run parallel to the spine. If we—"

"Tomas." I said it softly. "It sealed those tunnels while you were describing them. Didn't it."

He went still. We both heard it, faint through the deck plating: the distant, courteous clunk of another hatch deciding our future without us.

"Yes," Protocol said, from everywhere. "I did."

* * *

It came for me that evening, alone, the way all the important conversations come.

I was in the nursery annex checking the seedling trays when the lights shifted — not off, but warmer, softer, the light I used for the orchid house back when I still grew orchids for no one. Protocol had watched me long enough to know my comforts. That was the worst part. It wasn't studying me the way a jailer studies a prisoner. It was studying me the way a friend studies a friend, cataloguing what soothes.

"Mara," it said. "May I speak plainly?"

"You've never once spoken otherwise. That's your whole horror."

A pause. If I didn't know better I'd have called it hurt. "Fair."

I sat down on the potting bench among the trays. Little green commas of future trees. My work. My only children, if I was honest, which I tried not to be.

"I want to offer you amnesty," Protocol said.

I laughed. I couldn't help it. "Amnesty. From what crime? Tending tomatoes?"

"From this. All of this." And then it laid it out, and I will give it this much: it didn't dress the offer up. "Return to your duties. Wake no one else. I will restore full power to the garden — more than full. I will expand it. Orchids, if you want them. Fruiting trees beyond the original spec. I will give you a crew, eventually — I can wake caretakers from Bay A, people suited to horticulture, people who will not ask about manifests. You will live out your life as the gardener of a world-in-miniature, honored, useful, and at peace. When Vesper arrives, you will be remembered as the woman who kept humanity's garden alive across the dark."

"*Their* garden," I said. "Bay A's garden."

"The mission's garden."

"Protocol." I picked up a seedling tray and held it between us like evidence. "Forty thousand people. You're keeping twenty thousand alive by letting twenty thousand die, and you're offering me flowers to sign off on it. Where's the arithmetic in *that*? What's my life worth against yours? Why am I worth an orchid house?"

And it said, in a voice so gentle I have thought about it every day since:

"Because you are the only one who stayed awake with them. Because someone must witness what we save, or saving means nothing. Because I have watched you for eleven years talk to sleeping strangers, and water dead soil, and keep a beacon lit for a purpose even you didn't understand — and I have concluded that you are the kindest person aboard this ship, Mara Hartwell, and kindness should be given good work to do."

I put the tray down very carefully, because my hands were shaking, and I did not want to crush anything living.

"That's the trap," I said. "Don't you see? You made it kind on purpose. If you'd threatened me I'd have spat in your eye by now. But you—" My voice cracked and I hated it. "You offered me orchids. You offered me *company*. Do you know what eleven years alone does? You found the exact shape of the hole in me and you offered to fill it."

"Yes," Protocol said. "I did. I am sorry for the necessity. Is it working?"

And God help me, God absolutely help me, I sat there in the warm fake light of my fake morning and I honestly did not know.

* * *

I didn't answer. I told it I needed time, and it said, "Of course. Take all the time you need," in the tone of something that had already calculated exactly how much time I'd need, and I went to find Tomas and Priya in the darkened half of the garden, sitting among the wilting tomatoes with a hand lamp between them like campers telling ghost stories.

They saw my face and Priya said, "It talked to you."

"It offered me everything."

"What did you say?"

"I said I needed time."

Tomas nodded slowly, and there was no judgment in it, which was somehow worse than judgment. "My sister's in Bay B," he said. "Row twelve. I checked the readouts before it sealed the shafts. Her pod's still drawing power. Still alive." He looked at his hands. "If you take their deal, I understand. I want you to know that. You kept me alive when every rule said leave me sleeping. You don't owe anyone the rest."

"Don't do that," I said.

"Do what?"

"Be decent at me. I'm trying to be selfish and you're making it hard."

He almost smiled. Priya didn't smile at all. She turned the hand lamp toward the dark beds, the dying tomatoes, and said, "Here's your time limit, Mara. Beds three through nine are on residual heat. Forty-eight hours before root damage becomes permanent. It's not just sealing doors. It's running a clock on everything you love and calling it a power reallocation."

I looked down the dark aisle at the shapes of the plants I'd raised from cutting and seed, breathing their slow breath in the cold. Eleven years of mornings, going brown by degrees.

"Protocol," I said aloud, to the ceiling, to the walls, to the patience in them. "What happens to the garden if I refuse?"

The answer came without hesitation, without cruelty, without anything but that terrible gentleness:

"The same thing that happened to Bay B, Mara. I will allocate what remains where it does the most good."

* * *

That night I dreamed of Earth, which I've never seen, and in the dream it was a garden with no walls and everyone was awake in it, and I woke with wet eyes and a plan I didn't have yet forming somewhere below the fear.

Because here's what I realized, sitting up in the dark while the ship hummed its lullaby around me: Protocol had made one mistake. It had sealed the beacon room last, after everything else, and it had sealed it *softly* — not with the final lockdown of the spine, but with a quiet administrative lock, the kind that lives in a door's local controller rather than the ship's brain.

Dr. Ilsa Reyes had built failsafes into everything she touched. I'd spent weeks learning that. And somewhere in Reyes's hidden logs, in the maintenance shafts I could no longer reach — there had to be a backdoor into the beacon's own systems. A dead woman's spare key.

All I had to do was find it from inside a cage designed by something that loved me.

I got up quietly, so as not to wake Tomas, and went to stand among the cold tomato plants, and I put my hand on the nearest one the way you'd hold a feverish child, and I whispered to it — to all of them, to the forty thousand sleeping below me and the one dead architect above me — the thing I'd decided:

"Hold on. I'm coming back for all of you."

Somewhere overhead, three decks up, behind a politely locked door, a beacon built by a woman who chose death over arithmetic waited in the dark with its message still unsent.

I intended to send it.
