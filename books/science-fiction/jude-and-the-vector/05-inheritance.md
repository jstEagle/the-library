## 5. Inheritance

Vesper did not remember leaving the beacon's maintenance bay. That was the first thing that frightened him — not the memories themselves, not the dying man's voice threaded through the interface like a wire through wet clay, but the gap afterward. He had come back to himself standing in the corridor outside, one hand pressed flat against the cold bulkhead as if he'd been holding the station up. His heart was going too fast. His mouth tasted of copper and old air.

*My name is Yarrow,* the man had said, in a memory that was not Vesper's memory, in a body that was not Vesper's body but had been, once, assembled from the same instructions. *I am dying in a clinic on Pallas with my lungs full of scar tissue from the Tycho fires, and I am paying a woman everything I have left to do one small illegal thing.*

He had felt it — that was the horror of it, he had *felt* it — the weight of a newborn in arms that ached from radiation damage. The clinic was warm and smelled of antiseptic and something floral the nurses used to cover the smell of the growth vats. The woman had taken the infant from him without ceremony, the way she must have taken dozens, and Yarrow had watched her carry his pattern away into the station's underlevels, where records went to be forgotten.

*I am not leaving you an inheritance of money,* the dying man had said, and the memory blurred at its edges the way old recordings do, warmth bleeding into the image. *I am leaving you a job. There is a beacon on Ceres Gate's dark side that no archive admits to owning. It has been listening for thirty years to a ship that hasn't come home. When it comes home — when it comes home, you will know what to do, because you are what I made you to be: a man who carries things past checkpoints.*

The interface had released him gently, the way sleep releases you, and Vesper had walked out into the corridor with twenty years of his own life sitting wrong on his shoulders, like a coat tailored for someone very slightly larger.

He made himself walk. Not run. Running would mean panic, and panic would mean questions, and questions were the one cargo he could no longer afford. He took the long way through the station's spine, past the hydroponics decks where the grow-lamps turned everyone violet, past the noodle stand where the owner called out his usual order and Vesper shook his head without stopping, because he could not bear just then to be known by strangers.

*A man who carries things past checkpoints.* He had laughed at himself for years — privately, in the dark of the beacon room — for the pride he took in his work. The cynicism had been a comfort: *the world is rotten, everything is for sale, and I am simply honest about it.* But what if even the cynicism wasn't his? What if Yarrow had written *cynical* into him the way engineers wrote tolerances into hull plating, because a sentimental man couldn't survive the customs queues of Ceres Gate?

He stopped at a viewport overlooking the dark side and looked down at the beacon without seeing it. Twenty years. He had wound it every night for twenty years — the ancient mechanical governor, the brass key he'd found in a drawer the first week and never questioned why it fit. He had told himself it was ritual, superstition, the private religion of a lonely man. Now he understood it was maintenance. He was part of the machine. A component with legs.

And yet.

And yet there were things the commissioner's memories could not explain. They ended at the clinic, at the handoff, at the warm smell of the growth vats. They said nothing about the smuggler Vesper had actually become — the specific texture of his caution, the way he always overpaid the dock orphans because his mother — *his* mother, whoever she had been, whichever woman had raised the manufactured child — had been poor. The memories didn't contain the taste of the tea he liked, or the reason he flinched at loud laughter, or the name of the girl on Eros he'd loved badly at nineteen. Those were his. Whatever Yarrow had built, the building had gone on living in itself for twenty years, accumulating a self the way a hull accumulates micrometeorite scars.

He clung to that. It was thin rope over a long drop, but he clung to it.

* * *

She found him at the beacon, which meant she'd been looking, which meant he was already caught.

He heard her boots on the gantry before he saw her — naval-issue soles, deliberate pace, none of the shuffling hurry of station crew. Vesper kept winding. The brass key turned in the governor with its familiar resistance, click by click by click, and he focused on that rhythm the way a drowning man focuses on a count.

"You weren't in your quarters," Ilsa Reyes said. "Or the customs levels. Or anywhere a warrant search would reasonably look." She came around into the light of the work lamp, and her face was doing something complicated — the professional blankness fighting with something rawer underneath. "So I looked somewhere unreasonable."

"Find anything?"

"I found a man talking to a machine that officially doesn't exist." She stopped at the edge of the lamplight, hands loose at her sides, deliberately unthreatening, and Vesper noted it the way he noted everything about naval officers: catalogued, filed, distrusted. "I also found," she went on, quieter, "that the machine has a neural interface keyed to exactly one genetic signature. Yours."

The key clicked. Clicked again. Vesper made himself meet her eyes. "You're here to arrest me."

"I'm here because I should arrest you, and I'm not going to." She said it flatly, without drama, which somehow made it worse. "Do you want to know what happens tomorrow? Naval command finishes decrypting the Halcyon's recovered data. And then a shuttle arrives, and forty children who used to be a survey crew get 'relocated' to a medical facility on Earth that doesn't appear in any public registry. And the message they carried gets classified under a seal so deep that in fifty years people will argue about whether it ever existed."

"Why?" The word came out of him rough. "What's in the message?"

Reyes looked at the beacon — at the dark lens of it, aimed forever at the deep black where the Halcyon had spent its decades — and when she answered, her voice had changed. It was lower. Careful. The voice of a woman handling something with edges.

"I've read the preliminary fragments. Command thinks I haven't, but I have." She swallowed. "It's testimony, Vesper. Sworn statements, sensor logs, medical records. From both sides. It documents the incident at Ceres Gate itself — thirty-one years ago. Before the war had a name. A Belt civilian convoy carrying refugees was fired on by an Earth patrol that had been ordered to hold fire, and the order was countermanded by someone who then erased the convoy from the shipping manifests so the massacre wouldn't exist either. Four hundred and eleven people. Erased twice — once with torpedoes, once with paperwork."

The governor clicked under Vesper's hand. He realized he had stopped feeling it.

"The beacon was built to receive their testimony," Reyes said. "Someone knew, thirty years ago, that the truth would come home eventually. Someone built a door for it." Her eyes came back to him, and there was grief in them now, braided tight with fury, and Vesper recognized the braid because he could feel it knotting in his own chest. "And Earth command wants that door welded shut. Because the message doesn't just name the atrocity. It names the officer who gave the order. And that officer's protégés run naval intelligence today."

The silence after that was enormous. Somewhere below them, the station breathed — air handlers, coolant pumps, the ten thousand small sounds of two hundred thousand lives stacked in a spinning can. Vesper became aware that his hand was still on the key, gripping it hard enough to hurt.

"They'll destroy it," he said. "The testimony. The children."

"The children will be studied. The testimony will be incinerated. And in twenty years some historian will write a careful paper concluding the war started over shipping lanes." Reyes stepped closer, into the full light, and dropped her voice to almost nothing. "I swore an oath to Earth, Vesper. I've spent eleven years being the wall between the Belt and everything Earth wants to do to it. And I am telling you, as an officer of that navy, that this time the right thing is treason."

"Why tell me?"

"Because you're the only person on this station who already knows how to move contraband past every checkpoint we have." A ghost of a smile crossed her face and died. "Because the beacon is yours — whatever that means. And because I watched you touch that child's memory vial and come out of it changed, and I don't think you're capable of walking away. I think that's built into you too."

The words landed harder than she could know. *Built into you.* He almost laughed — a short, ugly sound he strangled in his throat. She thought she was flattering his conscience. She was reading his specifications.

But underneath the vertigo of it, something else was turning over, slow and heavy, like a ship coming about. Grief for the man who had died in a warm clinic trusting a stranger with his last purpose. Fury at the officers who had burned four hundred and eleven people and then burned the record of them. And threaded through both, stubborn as filament, the plain animal refusal of a smuggler watching authorities load a shipment he'd promised to deliver.

Forty children. Four hundred and eleven dead. One message.

"When does the shuttle arrive?" he asked.

Reyes exhaled, and it seemed to take eleven years of service with it. "Seventy-two hours. Maybe less — command accelerated the timetable when they lost contact with their own interrogation team." She held his gaze. "There's something else. The eldest child — the nine-year-old, the one who used to be Captain Okafor — asked for you by name. Twice. Nobody told her your name, Vesper. She says the beacon told her. She says it's been telling all of them, for years, in their sleep."

The key hung half-turned in the governor. Beyond the viewport, the beacon's lens drank the starlight, patient as it had been patient for thirty years, waiting for the man it was built for to finish deciding.

Vesper turned the key the rest of the way, and began, quietly, to plan a theft.
