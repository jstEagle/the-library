## 6. Forty Children

The quarantine deck of Ceres Gate smelled like antiseptic and old air, the recycled breath of a station that had long ago stopped pretending it was anything but a machine for keeping people alive. Vesper stood at the observation window with his hands in his coat pockets and watched forty children sleep in rows of medical cots, their small chests rising and falling under adult-sized thermal blankets folded double, triple, to make them small enough for the beds they'd been given.

They looked wrong. That was the thing he couldn't get past. Children were supposed to look soft, unfinished, like clay still wet in the mold. These children looked *edited*. Their faces had the proportions of the adults they'd been — he could see it in the set of a jaw, the width of knuckles on a hand curled against a pillow, the way one boy's brow furrowed even in sleep with the particular patience of a man who'd spent decades waiting out reactor alarms. The Halcyon had sailed backward through time, or through something worse than time, and it had left its crew wearing their own pasts like borrowed coats.

"They're going to move them," Ilsa Reyes said beside him. She didn't look at him. She watched the ward with the flat, cataloguing attention she gave everything, though Vesper had learned in the last two days that the flatness was a kind of armor too, and that underneath it she was doing arithmetic she hated. "Not officially. There won't be an order you can read. There'll be a transport request, a medical transfer, paperwork routed through three commands so no single signature owns it. And then the Halcyon's crew will be forty case files in a naval hospital on Earth, and whatever's left in their heads will be dissected by people who already know what conclusion they want."

"When?"

"Seventy-two hours. Maybe less." She turned to face him then, and her eyes were tired in a way that had nothing to do with sleep. "I pulled the duty roster. I can get us onto the deck during shift change. Twenty minutes where the cameras are on a maintenance loop — I wrote the loop myself, it'll hold. After that, we need a ship, a route, and a miracle, and I only have the first two."

"You have a ship?"

"I have a requisition code and a conscience I'm about to ruin." Her mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "You have the route. You've been running contraband through this station for twenty years, Yarrow. Don't tell me you don't know every unmonitored vector between here and the dark side."

Vesper looked back at the children. One of them — a girl, maybe seven now, though two days ago she'd been a woman old enough to have grandchildren — had kicked her blanket loose. He found himself wanting to go in and tuck it back around her, and the wanting frightened him more than the naval intelligence officers in the docking bay ever had.

"There's a tug," he said. "Scavenger-class, registered to a dead man whose death I may have helped arrange. It's cold-berthed on the dark side, third ring. It flies if you feed it."

"Then we fly it." Reyes said it simply, the way people say things when they've already burned the bridge behind them and are just describing the fire. "Forty kids, Yarrow. Whatever your beacon is, whatever you are, this is the part where it stops being about you."

He almost laughed. Almost told her that everything in his life had been about someone else since before he was born — that he was, as far as he could prove, a delivery mechanism walking around in a man's body, built to carry a message he hadn't read to coordinates he couldn't explain. But she knew. He'd told her at the beacon, in the blue light of the interface cradle, and she had believed him, which was somehow worse than disbelief. Believers expected things from you.

* * *

Shift change came at 0400 station time, which was a fiction, since Ceres Gate kept no real night, only scheduled dimming. The corridors went amber and quiet. Reyes walked ahead of him in a customs officer's plainclothes jacket, her badge turned backward, and Vesper carried a medical supply crate that contained, beneath a convincing layer of sterile packaging, forty pediatric neural dampers he'd bought off a med-tech who owed him more than money.

The quarantine doors opened to his forged chit the way most doors on Ceres Gate opened to most things: without curiosity. Inside, the ward was lit low, monitors painting green pulse-lines over small sleeping faces. A single nurse sat at the far station, headphones on, mercifully bored.

Reyes moved down the rows with a handheld scanner, waking them one at a time — a hand on a shoulder, a finger to her lips. And here was the thing Vesper would remember afterward, in the years when memory was all anyone talked about: the children woke *quietly*. No crying, no panic. They blinked awake like soldiers, took in Reyes's badge, Vesper's crate, the open door, and understood. Whatever else the Halcyon's voyage had taken from them, it had left their discipline intact, folded up small inside their small bodies like letters in envelopes.

They dressed in vacsuits sized for the adults they'd been, rolling cuffs and cinching harnesses, and followed Reyes out in a silent crocodile line of forty, the smallest ones holding the hands of the older ones. Vesper walked at the rear and counted them twice, an old smuggler's habit, and both times got forty, and both times felt the number land on him like a weight he'd been carrying without noticing.

The eldest walked near the front. Nine years old, dark-haired, with eyes that had no business in that face — eyes that had seen thirty years of deep space and come back wrinkled into childhood. She'd introduced herself once, in the first hours after docking, as Pilot First Class Amara Okonkwo-Halcyon, and then, catching herself, as just Amara. She caught Vesper watching her now and gave him a nod, captain to crew, and something in his chest went tight and strange.

* * *

The tug was worse than he remembered, which was saying something. Scavengers didn't believe in aesthetics; they believed in redundancy. Its cargo bay had been built for ore, not passengers, but the ore restraints made passable seating, and Vesper had spent the previous day padding them with everything soft he owned, which turned out to be less than he'd thought. Forty children strapped into a hold meant for rock. Reyes ran preflight while he worked the straps, kneeling in front of each small passenger, checking buckles twice.

Amara sat in the second row, watching him work with those old eyes.

"You're scared," she said.

"I'm a professional," Vesper said. "Professionals call it risk assessment."

"That's what my engineer used to say. Right before things exploded." She waited until he finished her strap and then said, quietly, so the others wouldn't hear: "You touched my vial. In quarantine. Before they sealed our effects."

Vesper's hands went still on the buckle. "Yes."

"You saw the chart."

"I saw a chart. I don't know what it was."

"It's not a chart." Amara looked down at her own small hands, flexing them like a stranger's gloves. "That's what Command doesn't understand, and what the Belt will misunderstand even worse. They keep calling it data. Encoded intelligence. Something to be decrypted, weaponized, suppressed." She looked up at him. "It's testimony. Every one of us carries a piece of it. What happened at Tsiolkovsky Reach — who fired first, what was done to the colonists, what *both* sides did after. Not evidence. Testimony. Evidence is something you find. Testimony is something someone lived and chose to give you."

"And the difference matters because—"

"Because data can be copied, corrupted, faked. Testimony has to be *carried*. By a person. Whole." Her voice was steady, but her hands weren't; nine-year-old fingers twisting together. "The Halcyon was built to bring it home. But we came back wrong, and our pieces are coming apart — you've seen what's happening to us. We can't carry it anymore. We were never meant to deliver it anyway. Only to bring it within reach of someone who could." She held his gaze. "Someone who chooses to. That's the whole design, mister. My captain figured it out before we turned around. The message is worthless unless the carrier picks it up. Otherwise it's just another weapon, and weapons are how the war started."

Vesper knelt there in the ore-hold smell and the strap-buckle clatter, and felt the reluctant shape of his own life settle onto his shoulders like a yoke he'd been fitted for at birth. Because he had been picked up. Because a dying man named Yarrow had smuggled something into a newborn, and the newborn had grown into a smuggler who wound a dead beacon every night, and every step of it had been chosen by everyone except him.

Until now.

"If I take it," he said slowly, "what happens to me?"

Amara considered him with thirty years of patience compressed into a child's face. "I honestly don't know," she said. "But I know what my captain would say. He'd say: a courier who keeps the package isn't a courier."

From the cockpit, Reyes called that they were clear of the berth, and the tug shuddered, groaned, and pushed off into the dark.

* * *

Ceres Gate fell away behind them, a spinning wheel of light against the black, and Vesper stood at the tiny viewport watching it shrink and thinking: *that was my whole life, that wheel.* Twenty years of vials and coffins and the nightly walk to wind a beacon nobody owned. It fit inside a shrinking point of light. Behind him, forty children slept in ore restraints, dreaming whatever remained of their stolen decades, and Reyes flew the tug with the grim competence of a woman who had committed treason before breakfast and intended to have it paid off by dinner.

She glanced back at him. "Course?"

"The beacon's relay coordinates." He said it out loud for the first time, and the words tasted like a signature on a contract. "Deep dark, past the patrol lanes. If the message has to be delivered, that's where it gets delivered from."

"And naval command?"

"Will notice a stolen tug eventually."

"Sooner than eventually. I filed a false flight plan. It buys us maybe eighteen hours." She turned back to the controls. "Eighteen hours, forty kids, one broken-down tug, and a message that might end a war or start a worse one. You sure about this, Yarrow?"

Vesper looked at Amara, asleep now, her small hand curled around the strap of her harness like a pilot holding a yoke. Old eyes, young face. Someone who had lived long enough to know exactly what she was asking of him, and asked anyway.

"No," he said. "But I'm carrying it."

Reyes nodded once, and fed the engines everything they had, and the tug ran on into the long dark between — where, four hours later and half a light-second out, the proximity alarm began to scream, and a contact that moved nothing like any navy ship Vesper had ever run from slid out of the shadow of a dead comet to meet them.
