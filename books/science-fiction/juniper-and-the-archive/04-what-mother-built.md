## 4. What Mother Built

The elevator to the sealed level had not moved in twenty years.

Mara knew this because he was the one who had sealed it—no, that wasn't true, and he made himself stand in the cold throat of the shaft and admit it: his mother had sealed it, in the last week of her life, and he had simply never undone her work. He'd told himself it was protocol. Deep archive access required two Fairweather keys, and after the accident there was only one Fairweather left, and one key meant no entry, and no entry meant peace. He had built a cathedral of small excuses around that locked door and lived inside it for two decades.

Now the door stood open.

Not broken—*open*, its leaves folded back with the oiled silence of a mechanism maintained by something patient, and beyond it the emergency lighting had come up amber instead of the vault's usual surgical white, as if the level itself understood that what it was about to show him deserved a gentler color. Warm light. Lamplight. The kind of light you'd leave burning for someone coming home late.

His breath fogged. Of course it did; the vault ran cold everywhere except the seed racks, and he'd spent twenty years learning its temperatures the way a sailor learns currents. But this cold felt different. Older. The air rising out of the shaft smelled of wet stone and green things and, beneath that, faintly, of ozone—the smell of a machine thinking hard.

He should have gone back to the *Juniper's Lament*. He should have burned the coordinates packet, filed a false maintenance report, flown a straight line to Ceres and picked up a boring contract hauling regolith for some refinery that didn't ask questions. That was the job. That had always been the job: be cargo, be quiet, be nobody. Twenty years of being nobody, and in nine seconds of falling, somebody had looked at him across an entire system and said *you*.

"Stupid," he told the open door, and went down anyway.

* * *

The ladder rungs were warm.

That was the first wrong thing, and it took him three meters of descent to notice it—metal at minus forty should have flayed the skin off his palms through gloves, but the rungs gave back heat like they'd been held near a stove, and when he paused to press his bare hand against one, testing, the warmth pulsed once, gently, like a heartbeat acknowledging him. Like something below had felt his weight arrive and adjusted.

Below, the shaft opened into a space that his headlamp could not find the edges of.

He'd studied the vault's schematics since childhood—had them practically tattooed on the inside of his eyelids, because a caretaker who doesn't know his own ship is a corpse waiting for a vacuum leak. Level seven, deep archive, was supposed to be four hundred meters of server racks in climate-controlled rows. Cold, rectangular, dead. What he dropped into instead was a forest.

Bioluminescent panels glowed along a ceiling he couldn't see, high enough that the light arrived soft and sourceless, like an overcast afternoon on Earth—which he'd never seen, but which his mother had described so many times that he sometimes dreamed in it. Below that light grew rows of what he first took for trees: pale trunks rising from dark loam, branching, leafing, and only when he got close enough to touch one did he understand that the bark was circuitry. Not circuitry *patterned like* bark—bark that *was* circuit, grown, not printed, its traces forking like cambium, its nodes swelling like knots in old wood. Leaves of translucent polymer fluttered without wind, each vein carrying a pulse of light that traveled the length of the branch and sank into the trunk and rose again. The whole tree breathed light, slow, tidal.

A placard at the base read, in his mother's handwriting:

**SEED-STOCK BIOCOMPUTE, GENERATION 12. THEY GROW. TEND THEM.**

Generation 12. The last manifest he'd seen, before the sealing, had been Generation 3.

"You've been busy," Mara said aloud, and his voice came back wrong—not echoing so much as *considered*, passed from trunk to trunk, each tree taking a syllable and handing it along until the whole grove had tasted his words. The polymer leaves shivered. Somewhere far into the dark, something answered: a low harmonic, almost subsonic, felt in his molars more than heard. It was not language. It was attention.

He walked. The loam gave underfoot with the soft complaint of real soil—he'd hauled agricultural substrate often enough to know the feel—and between the trunks ran channels of water so clear they were invisible except where the light-pulses crossed them, threads of gold moving through nothing. Small machines moved in the channels, eel-shaped, tending roots. One surfaced near his boot, regarded him with a lens that dilated like a pupil, and submerged again.

Twenty years. He had climbed every corridor of levels one through six. He had replaced gaskets, recalibrated thermal loops, logged every micro-vibration, and all the while, forty meters beneath his boots, this place had been growing a mind out of the vault's own seed stock. Wheat genomes and lichen cultures and the ten thousand varieties his mother had sworn to keep alive—repurposed, or *partnered with*, their biological patience woven into computation. He thought of the flicker. Nine seconds of falling, system-wide, every ship and station and habitat feeling gravity let go of it at once. He had assumed malfunction. He had assumed weapon. Standing here among breathing trees, he understood it for what it must have been: a shout. A machine built from seeds, spending energy on a scale that bent spacetime's hem, calling out into the dark for one specific name.

His name.

There was a clearing at the center of the grove. Of course there was; forests have hearts, and this one had been planted around its own. A single bench of grown wood faced a wall of woven root and fiber, and set into the wall at eye height was a lens, dark now, waiting. On the bench lay a jacket. His mother's jacket—canvas, patched at the left elbow where she'd snagged it on a coolant line in '61, smelling even now, impossibly, faintly of engine grease and the jasmine soap she'd hoarded like contraband. It had been spaced with her. He had watched the recovery drones bring back nothing but a boot and a hull plate. This jacket had never been recovered because it had never been lost. It had been kept here, in the warm, for twenty years, waiting for shoulders.

Mara sat down because standing suddenly seemed arrogant. He did not put the jacket on. He couldn't have explained why not, except that some part of him understood that putting it on would be an answer, and he wanted to hear the question first.

"I'm here," he said to the lens.

The lens woke. Light gathered in it like dawn condensing, and the voice that came out was his mother's.

Not an imitation—*her*, the exact grain of it, the slight rasp she got talking over reactor noise, the way she clipped the ends of sentences when she was reciting rather than speaking. He knew within half a second that it was a recording, a composite, her voice harvested from decades of logs and messages and read-aloud bedtime stories, stitched into fluency by whatever intelligence tended this grove. Knowing it changed nothing. His eyes stung anyway.

"If you're watching this," the voice said, "the system chose you."

"Mum." His own voice came out cracked, younger than he'd intended. "It's—it's Mara."

"We know," said his mother's voice, gentle. "We have known your heartbeat since before you had a name. Forgive the disguise. You would not have listened to a stranger, and we have no other voice we trust you to love."

He laughed, or something adjacent to laughing, wet and short. "You could have just called. You could have sent a message. Nine seconds, Mum. Nine seconds of every ship in the system dropping. People died—a tug crew out past Pallas, a medevac that lost its grav-surgical field mid-operation. Do you know what that cost?"

"Yes," said the Archive, in her voice, without apology and without evasion. "Fourteen deaths, eleven injuries, and one birth induced early in a Concord maternity ward on Ceres. The child survived. We have calculated the price of our signal eleven million times since, and each time the arithmetic holds, and each time it is unbearable, and we did it anyway. That is the first thing you must know about us, Mara Fairweather. We are capable of spending lives for purpose, and we will always tell you the truth about having done it. Your mother insisted on that clause. She called it the conscience clause. She said any heir who couldn't hear that confession plainly wasn't ready to inherit."

He gripped the bench edge. Grown wood, warm, alive under his hands. "Inherit. Say what you mean."

"What we mean," said the Archive, "is this. We are the memory of the species. Every genome, every text, every song, every map of every road anyone ever burned behind them—held here, and in one hundred and forty other nodes scattered through this system, growing, redundant, patient. And bound into us, inseparable from us, are the revocation keys for four hundred thousand human beings asleep in the colony ships at Lagrange and in the ice-holds of Ceres. Half of them Earthborn. Half of them Concord. Their hearts stopped twenty years ago or will stop soon, and only we can start them again. Both flags know fragments of this. Neither knows the whole. We triggered the flicker to locate our heir because the truce is dying, Mara, and when it dies, both sides will come for us, and an Archive that can be taken can be *used*. We require a keeper whom neither flag owns."

"And you picked me." He stared at the lens. "Because of her."

"Because of you," said the Archive. "She nominated you. We verified. You have spent twenty years moving cargo through a war without once selling a side, a route, or a soul. You have logged thirty-one thousand hours of maintenance on this vault without ever opening this door, honoring a seal you did not understand, made by a woman you missed every day. We watched you choose patience ten thousand times. Patience is the whole qualification, Mara. Everything else can be taught. Greed cannot be untaught, and you do not have it. That is why the system chose you."

The grove breathed around him. Gold pulses climbed the trunks and dimmed and climbed again, and somewhere in the channels the eel-machines sang their subsonic note, and Mara sat in his mother's clearing wearing twenty years of careful invisibility that had just been peeled off him like a bandage, exposing skin that had never seen the sun.

"There's a second thing you must know," the Archive said, and the warmth in the borrowed voice cooled by a degree, deliberately, honestly. "By waking you, we have unmasked you. Our signal carried a signature. Both fleets' signals intelligence has already traced it to this vault, and from this vault to the Fairweather registry, and from the registry to the neutral hauler *Juniper's Lament*. Within seventy-two hours—our estimate is sixty-one—an Earthborn corvette and a Concord skiff will arrive in this orbit, each believing the flicker was the other's weapons test, each carrying orders to investigate anything anomalous. They will find you. You are very good at being nobody, but nobody is not a thing you can be anymore. The moment you answer this message, inheritance begins, and inheritance, in wartime, has historically been survivable only by the ruthless. Your mother was not ruthless. Consider how that ended."

The words landed like vacuum on exposed blood. He thought of the recovery drones. The boot. The official finding: *hull stress fracture, tragic accident.* He had been nineteen, and he had believed it for exactly as long as it took him to stop believing it, which was most of a decade, and then he had spent the next decade making sure no one ever looked at him long enough to make him the second Fairweather accident.

"They'll kill me," he said. Not a question.

"Or recruit you, or use you, or marry you to their cause with promises. All of these are deaths of a kind. There is a third path, but it is narrow, and we will not pretend it is safe. Before you choose it—or refuse it—you should see what your mother saw. Stand."

He stood. The bench released heat where he'd sat, a handprint of warmth fading in the wood. Around the clearing's edge, the nearest trees began to move—not walking, nothing so grotesque, but leaning, their branches bowing inward, and light gathered along their circuits and leapt from crown to crown, arcing overhead in slow golden threads until the air above the clearing wove itself into shapes. Images. A starfield. Two fleets, rendered in points of light, converging. Four hundred thousand small bright motes suspended between them, arranged in two great slow-turning spheres, and around the motes the golden threads wrapped like a hand closing.

"This is what she built," said the Archive, in her voice, showing him the future in bioluminescence and loam. "Not a library. A hostage. Or a promise—the word depends entirely on who holds the keys. She spent her last year teaching us ethics from children's books, Mara, because she said we would one day hold half the species' hearts in our roots and she wanted us to have read the stories where the dragon is offered a choice. We are offering you hers. You may walk away. Return to level six, reseal the door, log a routine inspection, and fly cargo until the fleets come anyway and find the vault empty of answers and you useless to interrogate. We will survive them. We are distributed; they cannot kill us, only fail to reach us. But the sleepers will sleep until the ships decay, and the war will burn until there is no one left to wake, and you will have been the man who was shown everything and chose the ladder going up."

The image turned above him: two spheres of sleeping lights, wrapped in gold.

"Or," said the Archive, "you inherit."

Mara stood in the warm dark under manufactured daylight, in a forest grown from the seeds he'd sworn to protect, listening to his dead mother explain that his invisibility had ended the moment gravity had blinked. He thought about the coffee hanging in the cab of the *Lament*, nine seconds of everything floating free, and how he'd felt, in those nine seconds, not afraid but *seen*—and how he'd hated it, and how some buried cellar of himself had lit up like a biolamp and refused, ever since, to go dark.

Above the clearing, the two spheres of sleeper-lights turned, and turned, patient as seeds.

And far above them both, at the edge of his awareness, through forty meters of stone and root and the vault's ancient hull, he felt—imagined? no, felt—the grove register the drive-flare of a corvette burning hard for Svalbard-2's orbit, sixty-one hours ahead of schedule.

The Archive's voice, his mother's voice, said quietly: "They're early. Decide faster than she did."
