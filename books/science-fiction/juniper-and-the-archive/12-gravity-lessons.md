## 12. Gravity Lessons

Ilsa Voss had spent her whole life learning to read gravity the way other people read faces.

Aboard the *Halcyon's* sister ships, in the Concord's polished rotating habitats, gravity was a courtesy—something engineered, metered, polite. It told you where you stood, literally and otherwise. On the flagship *Meridian's* counterpart, the *Vigilant*, the spin rings hummed at exactly 0.94 standard, and Ilsa had always found comfort in that fractional lightness: the Concord did not believe in crushing its people. It believed in weighing them.

So when she felt the deck shift under her boots—not a malfunction, not turbulence, but a deliberate, tidal lean, as if some vast hand were tilting the ship to look at her—she understood it for what it was.

The Archive was watching her back.

She was in her quarters, third sleep-cycle of a six-cycle transit toward Ceres, with a data shard the size of her thumbnail burning a hole in her breast pocket. Four hundred thousand names. Two hundred thousand of them Concord citizens, frozen in the long ships, their revocation keys sitting in an orbital library like eggs in a cold nest. And beneath the keys, in the command layer no one above her pay grade was supposed to see: the expendable-lists. Triage tables. Priority matrices. Her own directorate's handwriting on the arithmetic of who deserved waking.

Her brother's name was on page forty-one.

Tomas Voss. Cryobiologist, second wave, Halcyon program. Listed under *non-essential recovery — defer indefinitely*. The notation beside his name was a single character in Concord shorthand that translated, with bureaucratic elegance, to *acceptable loss*.

Ilsa had sat with that character for two full cycles. She had run the decryption again hoping for corruption. She had checked the manifest against three independent backups. She had even, in a moment of weakness she would never confess to anyone, spoken his name aloud to the empty cabin, as if the dead weight of the syllables could argue with the data.

The ship leaned. Gravity gathered itself around her like attention.

"All right," she said quietly, to the air, to the Archive, to whatever patient intelligence had flickered nine seconds through every hull in the system. "All right. I'm going."

* * *

Defection, Ilsa discovered, was mostly logistics wearing a costume of courage.

The romance of it—the dramatic broadcast, the righteous severance—was a fiction from old Earth novels. In practice it meant falsifying a survey assignment, ghosting a maintenance skiff out of the *Vigilant's* shadow during a debris-dodge maneuver, and spending eleven hours alone in a cockpit that smelled of recycled coolant and someone else's sweat, running dark except for the life support she needed to keep breathing.

She had prepared for pursuit. What she hadn't prepared for was the silence. No hail from the Vigilant. No intercept vector blooming on her plot. Either her exit had gone unnoticed—which she doubted; the Concord noticed everything—or it had been noticed and judged not worth the fuel. That possibility sat in her chest like a swallowed stone. *Acceptable loss.* The phrase followed her out of the fleet like a shadow with her own silhouette.

Ashfall Station resolved out of the belt's haze near the end of the second day: a refinery habitat strung along a captured nickel-iron body, all heat-shimmer and scaffold, venting plumes of smelted slag that caught the distant sun and burned orange. It looked less like a station than like a wound that had learned to metabolize. Traffic control was a single voice on an open channel, bored and female, demanding registry, cargo, and reason for approach in the same breath.

"Survey skiff *Kestrel-9*," Ilsa transmitted, using the false registry she'd built into the skiff's transponder weeks ago, back when this was still a contingency and not a biography. "Independent contractor. Seeking berth and broker services."

A pause long enough to be insulting.

"Berth seven. Docking fees in untraceable scrip or barter. Weapons stay powered down or we power them down for you, permanently. Welcome to Ashfall, where nobody cares why you came."

The gravity at the docking collar was maybe 0.3 standard, cobbled together from the asteroid's mass and a spinning hab-ring bolted on with visible seams. Ilsa felt it as a kind of honesty. Nothing here pretended to be more than it was.

She kept the shard against her sternum and walked into the station.

* * *

Finding Mara Fairweather took four hours and cost her most of her barter scrip, and the trail led, inevitably, through his sister.

Odile Fairweather ran her domain from a mezzanine office above the refinery floor, behind glass smoked amber by years of alloy vapor. She was older than Ilsa expected, harder-lined, with their mother's jaw if the archive photos were accurate—and Ilsa had studied those photos, because studying people was how she survived. Odile looked at Ilsa the way a scale looks at cargo.

"Concord science officer," Odile said. Not a question. "You have a walk like fleet training and you flinch when the slag vents cycle. You want my brother."

"I want to talk to him."

"Everyone wants to talk to him lately. An Earthborn admiral wants to talk to him so badly he's offered me a station's worth of protection to facilitate the conversation." Odile laced her fingers. "Here's what I know about talking to Mara Fairweather. He will listen to you with his whole face and agree with none of it. He will offer you coffee he can't afford. And when you leave, he will spend three days deciding whether trusting you gets people killed." She leaned forward. "So before I point you at him: what do you weigh?"

Isla set the data shard on the desk between them. "Two hundred thousand reasons the Concord is lying to its own people. Including mine."

Something moved behind Odile's eyes—too fast to name, gone before it landed. "Bay twelve," she said. "He's prepping a grain hauler. Tell him Odile says the interest on family debts just went up."

* * *

Mara Fairweather was elbow-deep in a grain container's seal mechanism when Ilsa found him, and her first thought was that he looked nothing like a man carrying the fate of half a species. He looked like a man losing an argument with a gasket. His hands were nicked and grease-shadowed, his coveralls were older than her career, and when he straightened up and saw her, his whole body went still in a way that reminded her of the Archive itself—that same quality of total, weighted attention.

"You're Concord," he said. "Science division. Voss." He said her name like a fact he'd been dreading. "Ilsa Voss. Your intercepts have been in my firmware for a week. The Archive likes to share things it shouldn't."

"Then you know why I'm here."

"I know why you think you're here." He wiped his hands on a rag that made them dirtier. "People come to me with causes, Officer Voss. Usually they come with guns first and causes second. Which are you?"

She held out the shard. "Neither. I'm worse. I'm arithmetic."

He didn't take it. "Explain."

"The Concord has priority lists for the sleepers. Who wakes first, who wakes ever. My superiors call it triage. It's triage the way a butcher calls it portioning." Her voice stayed level, which cost her something; she let it cost her, because he should see the price. "My brother is on the deferred list. Tomas Voss. He went into the ice believing the Concord would wake him when the work was done. The work will never be done. That's the design."

For a long moment the only sound was the grain hauler's environmental system cycling, a slow asthmatic breath through the bay.

"My mother refused both fleets access to that Archive," Mara said finally. "It got her killed. And now you're standing in my bay telling me your fleet wants to do with it what my mother died preventing." He shook his head slowly. "Why bring this to me? Why not leak it yourself? You're clearly capable."

"Because leaking it starts a war over the keys, and wars don't wake sleepers—they bury them deeper." She stepped closer. "Because the Archive chose you, and I've spent two days trying to understand what an ancient library sees in a cargo hauler, and I think I finally have. It saw someone who spent twenty years refusing to pick a side. That's not cowardice, Fairweather. That's the only qualification that matters anymore."

"That's a generous reading of twenty years of hiding."

"I'm not feeling generous. I'm feeling precise." She put the shard down on his workbench, deliberately, within his reach but not in his hand. "Admiral Reyes has offered you citizenship and your mother's cleared name if you wake only the Earthborn half. My directorate will offer you something similar, or they'll put a torpedo through your hull—probably both, in that order. Every faction in this system now knows your flight plan. You have no bridges left to burn, Mara. So the only question left is what you build on the ashes."

He looked at the shard for a long time. Then at her.

"You realize what you've done," he said quietly. "You've handed me proof that your government writes off its own people. Do you know what Reyes would pay for that? What it would buy? A truce. Protection. Maybe enough goodwill to keep Ashfall out of the war's path." His voice roughened. "One conversation with the Earthborn liaison and I'm safe for the rest of my life. One conversation, and I hand you over."

"Yes," Ilsa said. "I did the same math. I ran it the whole way here." She lifted her chin. "And I came anyway. Because somewhere in the last forty-one pages I stopped being able to do that arithmetic, and I need to know if there's anyone left in this system who still can."

The bay lights hummed. Somewhere above them, Ashfall's hab-ring turned its slow, seamed turn.

Then Mara Fairweather picked up the shard, weighed it in his palm like something living, and did the last thing she expected.

He laughed—a short, exhausted sound with no humor in it, only relief.

"God help me," he said. "I've been waiting two weeks for someone to make this easy, and instead you make it honest." He tucked the shard into his chest pocket, mirroring where she'd carried it. "There's a bunk aft of the galley. It smells like grain and regret. The coffee's terrible and I charge for it anyway, because neutrality doesn't come free." He extended his grease-stained hand. "Welcome aboard the *Juniper's Lament*, Officer Voss. Former officer. As of the moment you walked in here, you're just Ilsa."

She took his hand. His grip was careful, the grip of a man who'd learned that everything he touched could be taken from him.

"You understand what you're doing," she said. "Sheltering me burns your last bridge to Reyes. Whatever deal he offered—it's ash now."

"It was ash the moment I read the expendable-lists he doesn't know I've seen." Mara's expression hardened into something she recognized from her own mirror: grief converted to load-bearing structure. "My mother taught me one lesson before she died, though it took me twenty years to learn it. Neutrality isn't standing between two fires. It's deciding, every single day, that you won't hand anyone else into the flame." He nodded toward the aft corridor. "Come on. Coffee. And then you're going to teach me every name on those lists, because if we're going to burn down the Concord's arithmetic, we start by making sure every number gets counted."

They walked aft together through the grain hauler's dim spine, past containers full of ordinary wheat that smelled, absurdly, of summer on a planet neither of them had ever seen. And Ilsa felt it then—not the engineered courtesy of Concord spin-gravity, not the cobbled honesty of Ashfall's mass, but something older and stranger: the sense of being held by a decision rather than a force field.

Somewhere overhead, patient and vast, the Archive tilted the universe a degree in their favor.

She chose to believe it was approval. She was about to find out how expensive approval could be—because across the belt, in a flag cabin smelling of gun oil and certitude, Admiral Reyes was already reading Odile Fairweather's latest message, and the word *sister* appeared in it twice.
