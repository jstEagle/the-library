## 19. The Split Decision

Mara had expected the node to feel like a throne room. It felt instead like a greenhouse at night.

He stood in the memory-garden with Ilsa's hand hovering near his elbow—not touching, never quite touching—and watched the sleepers drift past in the Archive's slow projection. Rows of them. Four hundred thousand names rendered as soft points of light, each one a body in a cryo-cradle somewhere in the dark, each one a heartbeat paused mid-sentence. The Earthborn soldiers glowed amber. The Concord colonists glowed green. Between them, scattered like salt, the children of both lists burned a pale white that hurt to look at for long.

"You've been standing there for an hour," Ilsa said.

"I'm trying to find the order that doesn't kill anyone."

"There isn't one." She said it without cruelty. She'd been an auditor; she knew arithmetic when she saw it. "There's only the order that kills fewer people later."

"That's what Reyes would say."

"Reyes would say it and mean himself." She moved closer, and the light of the sleepers washed her face green and amber by turns. "You're not choosing who lives, Mara. You're choosing who gets the chance to be worth keeping alive. There's a difference. It's small. It's the only difference there is."

The Archive waited. He could feel it waiting—the way you feel weather building behind a mountain range. It had offered him absolute power with the courtesy of a butler presenting keys, and it had made no secret of what those keys could do: choose a half, and the choice would be enforced across the system with gravitational certainty. Nine seconds of falling had been its whisper. A full enforcement event would be its shout, and every hull in the belt would bend to hear it.

Mara thought about his mother's handwriting on a maintenance order dated three days before her death. *Let them share or let them lose everything.* He'd read it a hundred times since Ceres. He'd assumed it was a warning. Standing here, he understood it was instructions.

"Not half," he said slowly. "Not four hundred thousand against four hundred thousand. That's just the war with better odds."

Ilsa turned. "Then what?"

"One ship from each side." His voice came out steadier than he felt. "The Meridian and the Halcyon."

* * *

Ilsa Voss had spent eleven years aboard Concord vessels and had learned to read a ship's silhouette the way sailors once read clouds. So when Mara pulled the manifests and the two names resolved out of the data, she laughed—a short, disbelieving sound that echoed strangely in the garden's humid air.

"The Meridian is a hospital ship," she said. "Earthborn registry, decommissioned from frontline service after Tithonus. Two hundred crew, mostly medics and techs. Her spinal rail was stripped years ago—she couldn't fight a customs skiff."

"And the Halcyon?"

"Research vessel. Deep-survey chassis, no weapons bays at all—they were converted to sample labs before she ever launched. My brother—" Her voice caught on the word, and she let it catch. She was done pretending the word didn't exist. "My brother Tomas is frozen aboard her. Third-tier cryo deck. Listed as expendable priority in the Concord's own retrieval orders."

Mara was quiet a moment. "That's why you picked them too."

"It's why I agreed so fast when you said it first." She met his eyes. "Don't mistake me. I've run the numbers a dozen ways. If we wake the Meridian, the fleet gains no tactical asset—if anything they lose one, because a hospital ship needs protecting. Same for the Halcyon. We're not handing either side a weapon. We're handing each side a reason the other side can't be bombed."

"A hostage exchange," Mara said.

"A shared dependency." Ilsa smiled thinly. "The Concord has a word for it: symbiosis. The fleet probably calls it something dumber."

"They call it leverage."

"Leverage works." She looked up at the drifting lights, at the white-burning children scattered through both colors. "It's not mercy, Mara. I want to be honest about that, because you'll be tempted to call it mercy and someone will eventually use your own sentiment against you. Waking six hundred people out of four hundred thousand isn't salvation. It's a demonstration. It's proof of concept. The Archive enforces the truce around them, and everyone in the system watches two ships full of helpless people survive because neither faction can afford to break the peace."

"Resolution through constraint," Mara murmured. "Not virtue."

"Virtue ran out of budget around chapter nine," Ilsa said dryly, and he almost smiled.

She pulled up the wake protocols herself. Her hands shook—she noticed, and hated noticing, and did it anyway. The interface bloomed around her in cold Concord-standard glyphs, then reshaped itself into the Archive's older, stranger script, the one that looked like seedlings drawn by a careful child. Two authorization slots appeared. One biometric key already glowed in the first: FAIRWEATHER, E., the signature his mother had left embedded in the system twenty years ago, patient as a buried seed.

The second slot blinked empty. Waiting.

"It wants both of us," Ilsa said.

"It always wanted both of us. That was the whole test." Mara exhaled. "One hand can't hold all the keys. Not mine. Not anyone's."

"Then let's hold half each." She extended her hand over the slot. "On three?"

"No," Mara said. "No countdowns. Countdowns are for people who might change their minds."

They pressed their palms down together, and the garden filled with light.

* * *

The Meridian woke first.

Mara watched it from the node's observation blister while the Archive carried him along the data-streams like a man riding a river he'd helped dig. Two hundred cryo-cradles cycling down in sequence, frost sublimating off eyelashes, hearts remembering their old rhythm one chamber at a time. The ship's AI—old, gentle, built for triage rather than tactics—began running medical checks before a single sleeper had opened her eyes. Vital signs scrolled past in amber. All green. All waking.

Aboard the Halcyon, three hundred and twelve cradles answered in the same minute, because the Archive did not believe in favoritism even in timing. Green light. Green vitals. A research crew surfacing from a nineteen-year sleep into a system at war, groggy and confused and utterly unarmed.

And then the Archive spoke to everyone.

Mara felt it as pressure first—a deep, tidal shift in the system's gravitational fabric, subtle as a change in barometric weather. Every ship in the belt felt it. Every station. Every drifting hulk and hidden smuggler's cache. The Archive did not broadcast words; it broadcast weight. For exactly nine seconds, gravity everywhere leaned gently toward the two waking ships—not enough to damage, not enough to injure, but enough that every helmsman in the system understood, in their bones and their ballast and their coffee cups, that something vast had just drawn a line in space.

Two circles of calm, centered on the Meridian and the Halcyon. Inside them, physics itself would enforce the truce. Weapons would misfire. Engines would sputter. Boarding torpedoes would fall out of the sky like tired birds. The Archive had taken his mother's instruction—*let them share or lose everything*—and translated it into the only language both factions had ever truly respected.

Nine seconds of falling had started this. Nine seconds of leaning ended the argument.

Mara watched the fleet chatter erupt across the bands—panic, denial, fury, and underneath it all, unmistakably, relief. He heard an Earthborn gunnery officer on an open channel say, "Thank God, somebody finally made it stop," before his comms officer killed the line.

"They're calling it the Stillness," Ilsa reported from the console, monitoring intercepts with an auditor's flat efficiency. "Both fleets have issued stand-down orders within forty minutes of each other. First synchronized command decision in six years." She paused. "Neither side is admitting why."

"They don't have to admit it. They felt it." Mara watched the two ships drift toward each other on converging trajectories the Archive had chosen—slow, ceremonial, inevitable. "Constraint, not virtue. You called it."

"I also called it a hostage exchange, and I'm sticking with that. Hope is just leverage with better PR." But her voice softened as the feeds showed the Meridian's medics stumbling upright for the first time in two decades, gripping rails, weeping at the sight of stars through a viewport they'd last seen as younger people in a different war. "Look at them. They don't know yet what they are. They think they're survivors."

"Aren't they?"

"They're arguments," Ilsa said. "Six hundred living arguments for why the shooting stops. And arguments need feeding, housing, and defending—which means both fleets now have to keep them alive together or explain to their own people why they didn't." She closed the manifest. "Tomas is on the Halcyon. He'll wake up in a few hours. Nineteen years older than his little sister by the calendar, and I'm still the one who knows how the world works." Her jaw tightened. "I intend to be there when he opens his eyes."

"Then we should get moving," Mara said. "The Archive is already plotting the dock approach. Both ships, one harbor. Your vault."

"Our vault," Ilsa corrected, and did not look at him when she said it, which was how he knew she meant it.

* * *

The transit took eleven hours, and Mara spent most of them in the node's lower galleries, walking among the seed racks where the physical archive lived—ten thousand varieties of wheat and juniper and lichen and things with no common name, sealed in glass and patience. His mother had walked these rows. He could feel that now, the way you can feel a previous tenant's footsteps worn into stairs. Somewhere below his feet, the master node hummed its distributed song across the whole system, and every note of it was hers.

Ilsa found him there near the end of the transit, standing before a rack labeled in faded ink: JUNIPERUS COMMUNIS — ARCTIC STRAIN — VIABLE.

"Reyes is alive," she said. "The shuttle's transponder is pinging on schedule. Fleet picket recovered him eight hours ago. He's under medical quarantine, which means he's conscious, which means he's talking."

"I know."

"He'll spin it. He'll tell the fleet you spared him because you wanted a martyr, or because you're weak, or because the Archive made you. He'll build a movement out of being sealed in a box."

"I know that too." Mara touched the glass over the juniper rack. Cold seeped through his glove. "But he's alive, Ilsa. Whatever else happens, whatever story he tells—I looked at him in zero-g with a broken bulkhead strut in my hand, and I couldn't do it. Not because of virtue. Because if I'd done it, then every key I split would've been split by a killer, and the whole thing would've been worth nothing."

Ilsa studied him for a long moment. "You know what the Concord psych evals would call that?"

"Cowardice?"

"Restraint under provocation. Extremely rare. Usually fatal to the career." Almost a smile. "Keep it anyway."

The node chimed. Docking approach: Meridian and Halcyon, convergent vector, arrival in forty minutes. Around them the seed racks seemed to lean in, listening.

Mara took one last look at the arctic juniper—dormant, patient, twenty thousand years from its last bloom and ready for the next one—and turned for the docking gallery, where two ships full of strangers were about to arrive at a harbor neither of them had chosen, guarded by a truce neither of them could see, enforced by a gravity neither of them could argue with.

"Forty minutes," Ilsa said. "Time to decide who greets them."

"Both of us," Mara said. "Side by side. Let them see what they're part of before anyone tells them what they used to be."

The vault's outer doors began to open onto the black, and far off in the dark, two small lights—one amber, one green—came home.
