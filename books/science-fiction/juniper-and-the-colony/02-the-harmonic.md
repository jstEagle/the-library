## 2. The Harmonic

The trouble with nine seconds of falling, Mirabel decided, was not the falling itself. Falling was honest. Falling was physics doing you the courtesy of being straightforward. The trouble was what came after — the way his ship, the *Penny Dreadful*, kept creaking in the silence like an old man recounting a near-miss to anyone who'd listen.

He'd spent six hours since the flicker pretending everything was fine. Checking coolant lines that didn't need checking. Recalibrating a nav suite that had done nothing wrong. Making tea he did not drink, because his hands wanted occupation more than his throat wanted tannin. This was the smuggler's first commandment: when the universe does something strange, be very busy and very bored until it goes away.

But the harmonic hadn't gone away.

It lived now in the comm channels — every one of them, all forty-seven bands, civilian and military and the three illegal frequencies Mirabel kept for business purposes. Not a broadcast. Not a signal in any shape the manuals would recognize. More like a resonance, the way a struck tuning fork makes a wineglass hum across the room. Nine notes, repeating. He'd timed it against the gravity log. Nine seconds of failure, nine notes of song.

"That's not a coincidence," he told the ship. "That's a signature."

The *Penny Dreadful* creaked, noncommittal.

He pulled the recording into his analysis deck and did what he'd sworn off doing years ago, back when doing it had nearly gotten him shot: he thought like a historian. His mother had been one, before the war took her university and gave her a ration card instead. She used to say that every machine has an accent, and you can hear where it was born if you listen long enough.

So he listened.

The harmonic's carrier wave was analog — genuinely, stubbornly analog, the kind of signal you got from hardware built when the Colonies were still proud of themselves and the fleets were still one fleet. Nobody built like that anymore. Nobody *could*; the fabrication standards had died with the armistice, filed under *technologies of the conflict, restricted*. The signal wasn't just old. It was illegal in the way a fossil is illegal — evidence of a world everyone had agreed to stop mentioning.

And the pattern. He laid the nine notes out as a sequence and felt the hair on his arms stand up like a jury rising.

Distress cadence. Colony Standard Emergency Protocol, revision four — the last revision, the one from the final decade before the War, the one they'd taught in academy classes that no longer existed. Three rising tones: *we are here.* Three falling: *we are in need.* Three held long and low, the part every pilot had privately called the goodbye: *if no one comes, remember us.*

No living ship should know it. The fleets had scrubbed it from every registry, every training manual, every crewman's implant with the thorough, embarrassed efficiency of people erasing a crime scene. Mirabel only knew it because he carried other people's memories for a living, and one of the hundreds rattling around in the vault of his skull belonged to a dead Colony-era comms officer who had once spent an entire shift broadcasting that cadence into silence and waiting, shift after shift, for an answer that never came.

Mirabel knew what that waiting felt like from the inside. He'd bought the memory cheap, from a widow on Halcyon who said she couldn't sleep with it in the house.

He sat back and looked at the nine notes hanging in his display, patient as a held breath, and felt the two oldest instincts of his profession start to wrestle.

Instinct one: *run.* Something old and impossible was singing on every channel in the system, and things that were old and impossible had a documented history of being fatal. Whatever had caused nine seconds of gravity to hiccup across two whole worlds might still be out there. Might be *doing* something. Smart money, established smuggler money, the money that kept him fed and free, said: point the *Penny Dreadful* at Vesper Gate, deliver the cargo, collect the pay, and let the impossible be somebody else's problem.

Instinct two was greed, which is just curiosity wearing a nicer coat. And greed was whispering: *nobody else can read this. Nobody else even knows what it is. You are currently the only man in the system with the accent dictionary to translate a dead language, and dead languages, Mirabel, have always paid better than living ones.*

He made tea after all. Drank it. It had gone bitter.

"Damn it," he said, and set a course for Vesper Gate, where the fences were.

* * *

Idris Vane kept his office in the hollowed spine of a decommissioned ore hauler docked at the Gate's third ring, on the theory — which Mirabel had never once heard him admit aloud — that anyone who wanted him dead would have to respect the architecture. The man himself was a soft-spoken, silver-templed ex-quartermaster who wore cardigans in vacuum-rated colors and had a handshake like a promise you'd regret.

"Mirabel." Vane's face did the thing it always did, the small genuine pleasure of a man greeting his favorite unreliable asset. "You're early. Either business is good or business is terrible."

"The gravity flickered," Mirabel said, because with Vane you led with the strange thing; he'd smell evasion otherwise.

Vane's eyebrows performed a polite, noncommittal ascent. "So I heard. Nine seconds, wasn't it? Half the station fell over. A woman in the fish market is threatening to sue physics."

"Everyone's talking about the falling. Nobody's talking about the sound."

That got him Vane's full attention, which Mirabel recognized by the older man's hands going still. Vane's hands were never still. They counted things — credits, seconds, exits. When they stopped, someone was about to be worth money or worth trouble.

"Sound," Vane repeated.

Mirabel played the nine notes into the quiet of the office. He watched them land on Vane like weather. The old man's face stayed pleasant and blank, which on Idris Vane was the equivalent of another man going white and grabbing the bulkhead.

"Where did you get that?" Vane asked, very evenly.

"Every open channel. It's been repeating since the flicker. Nobody's claiming it. I ran the cadence through analysis and—" he watched Vane's eyes, "—it's Colony Standard Emergency Protocol, revision four."

The silence that followed had texture. Mirabel let it sit, the way you let a buyer sit with a price.

"Revision four," Vane said at last, softly, the way you'd name a dead friend. "You're certain."

"I own a comms officer's whole career, Idris. I'm more than certain. I'm *homesick*."

Vane turned to the window, where the traffic of Vesper Gate crawled in its lanes, all of it pretending the last thirty years hadn't happened. "There was a ship," he said, which was not an answer to anything, which was how Vane answered most things. "Before your time. Before mine, if I'm honest, though I've read the files that weren't burned. A colony vessel, long-range, one of the last launched before everything went to ash. Name of *Juniper*."

The name went through Mirabel like a cold draft. He didn't know why. He'd never heard it — he was almost sure he'd never heard it — and yet somewhere behind his sternum, in the crowded house where he kept three hundred stolen lives, something shifted in its sleep.

"Lost?" he managed.

"Officially, lost with all hands, cause unknown, year nine of the War. Unofficially—" Vane spread his hands, an entire philosophy of officialdom in the gesture. "Unofficially, nobody official ever looked. And now something is singing her distress call on every band in the system, and the fleets have gone very quiet in the specific way they go quiet when they're very busy."

Mirabel felt the room tilt a degree, the way it had during the nine seconds. "Hunting it, you mean."

"I mean," Vane said, turning back, and now his voice was the soft one, the one he used for prices and warnings and little else, "that a fleet survey vessel came through the Gate six hours ago asking quiet questions about anomalous signals. And that a man I know in traffic control sold them a copy of every ship that docked since the flicker. And that your *Penny Dreadful* is on that list, and that you are the only soul on it with any documented history of handling Colony-era signal material, because I sold them that history myself, three years ago, for reasons that seemed good at the time." He had the grace to look faintly apologetic. "I'm telling you because I like you. And because I have a job."

"Of course you have a job."

"Triple your standard rate." Vane named the figure, and it was obscene, and they both knew it was obscene, and Mirabel's greed — that well-dressed little animal — stood up on its hind legs and begged.

"Find where the signal is strongest," Vane said. "Find where it's *coming from*. You don't have to go all the way. Just triangulate, mark it, and sell me the coordinates. I'll find a buyer. There are buyers, Mirabel. There are buyers who would pay that figure again just to know, and buyers who would pay it a third time to make sure nobody else knows." He smiled his cardigan smile. "The past is the only commodity left with real scarcity value."

"And the fleet?"

"Is hunting it too. Quietly. Which means they want it before anyone else does, which means it's worth more, which means—" he gestured gently, *do the arithmetic* — "triple rate. Quadruple, if you're fast."

Mirabel looked at the old man for a long moment. "You know that's the most dangerous sentence you've ever said to me."

"I know it's the most *profitable*," Vane said. "Dangerous and profitable have always been sisters. Take a day. Think about it."

Mirabel took the day. He walked the third ring, bought noodles he didn't taste, and watched the fleet survey vessel sitting at its berth like a cat outside a mousehole — patient, official, pretending to be furniture. He thought about running. He was genuinely, sincerely committed to running. Running was the whole of his craft; he'd built a life on the principle that the wise man leaves the mystery for the corpse to explain.

But that night, alone in the *Penny Dreadful*'s cabin, he opened his memory vault to review cargo before the Vesper Gate customs scan, and one fragment surfaced unbidden — a piece he'd carried for years, provenance unknown, bought in a lot with a dozen others. A woman's hands on an analog console. Nine notes played by someone who was crying and playing anyway. A voice, young and tired, saying words he had somehow never quite listened to before:

*If no one comes, remember us.*

Mirabel sat in the dark with his heart going like a faulty engine, and understood, with the calm of a man watching his own good sense walk off a cliff, that he was not going to run.

He was going to find the *Juniper*.
