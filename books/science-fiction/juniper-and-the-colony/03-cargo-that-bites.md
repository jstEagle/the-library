## 3. Cargo That Bites

The *Kestrel's Regret* had a saying about cargo: if it doesn't hum, it doesn't bite. Mirabel Ashgrove had learned the hard way that this was a lie told by people who'd never carried memories.

He sat in the hold with his boots off and his skull open to the reader cradle, which was less gruesome than it sounded—a halo of copper filament that settled around his temples like a crown designed by someone who loved you but also distrusted you. The inventory display bloomed against the far wall in soft amber light.

Forty-one canisters. Forty-one lives he was carrying through the dark for people who could not carry them themselves.

"Right," he said to the empty hold, because talking to yourself was either a sign of madness or of long-haul competence, and he preferred the second interpretation. "Let's see who's trying to eat me today."

* * *

You are nine years old again, and you know it isn't real, but knowing has never once helped.

The kitchen smells of scorched sugar—your mother burning caramel for the third time this week because she refuses to admit the stove runs hot—and your sister is laughing at something you said, that particular laugh she saved only for you, the one that made her sound younger than she was. Outside the window, the orchards of Meridian stretch to the horizon in rows so perfect they look drawn on.

You know how this memory ends. You have carried it eleven years. You know that in four hours the fleet shells will come down on Meridian like the fist of a god who has read the reports wrong, and you know that your sister will not be in the cellar where she should be, because she will have run back for the cat, and you know—

You know all of it. That is the whole problem with memory. It is the only cargo that arrives already opened.

Mirabel surfaced from the canister gasping, one hand pressed flat against his sternum as if to check the ribs were still his own. The amber display blinked patiently: **CANISTER 07 — INTEGRITY 94% — RESONANCE NOMINAL**.

"Nominal," he muttered. "Sure. We'll call it nominal."

Canister seven was his sister's last day. He didn't smuggle it for anyone. It wasn't for sale at any price, though three separate buyers over the years had offered sums that would have retired him to a beach world with no history whatsoever. Some cargo you carry because it pays. Some cargo you carry because putting it down would mean admitting it happened, and some part of him—the coward part, probably, or maybe the honest part—believed that as long as he hauled it, Wren was still in transit somewhere, still moving, still not finished.

The rest of the manifest was less personal. Stolen childhoods, mostly. That was the bread and butter of the memory trade after the war: whole districts quarantined, whole archives seized as "strategic assets," families scattered across three systems with nothing left of their dead but what the fleets had confiscated. Mirabel stole childhoods back. A grandmother's first snowfall. A father teaching a son to solder, badly, both of them burned and delighted. A wedding conducted by a drunk officiant on a rooftop during an evacuation, which everyone involved agreed afterward was the best wedding any of them had ever attended.

And then there were the others. The ones that paid worst and weighed most.

* * *

You are standing in a corridor on Halcyon Station, and you are not yourself—you are a fleet lieutenant named Corvane, and your hands are steady in a way hands should never be when they are doing what yours are doing.

The order came down encrypted, triple-wrapped, deniable. The colony ship *Amaranth* docked without clearance, carrying refugees from a world the war had decided to unmake. Protocol says quarantine. The order says something else entirely. You watch the airlock cycle and you understand, with the terrible clarity of the obedient, that you are about to become a paragraph in someone else's classified file.

You do it anyway. That is the part the histories will never hold: not monsters, just men following orders with steady hands, and afterward a debriefing officer who tells you that what you feel is stress, treatable, temporary, forgettable.

Forgettable. As if forgetting were a mercy instead of a second crime.

Mirabel pulled out of Corvane's skull and sat very still for a while, breathing the recycled air of his own ship, which smelled of engine coolant and the tea he kept burning on the warmer. Canister nineteen. War crimes were the cargo that bit hardest—not because they were violent, but because they were *ordinary*. You lived inside them and found a person, not a monster, and the person was tired, and the person wanted to go home, and the person did it anyway.

He'd taken canister nineteen off a dying veteran on Vesper Gate who'd paid him half his pension to make sure it survived him. "Evidence," the old man had rasped. "Not confession. Evidence." Mirabel hadn't asked evidence of what. He'd known. Everyone who'd lived through the Colony War knew, in the way you knew about a bruise under a sleeve.

* * *

He worked through the manifest methodically, the way his first fence had taught him: light ones first, heavy ones spaced out, never two war crimes back to back unless you wanted to spend the night talking to the bulkheads. The gentle humor of the trade, if it had any, was that memory runners were the only couriers who got to sample everything they carried and were ruined by all of it.

By the fourth hour he'd cleared thirty-eight canisters. Three flagged for containment drift—minor, patchable, the memory equivalent of a slow tire leak. He patched them. He drank tea that had achieved a flavor best described as punitive. He thought about Idris Vane's offer—triple pay to trace the harmonic—and the way Idris's smile had stayed exactly where it was while his eyes went somewhere cold and calculating. Triple pay. For a signal that shouldn't exist, from a ship that shouldn't exist, hunted by a fleet that officially insisted nothing was wrong.

Greed said yes. Instinct, which had kept him alive through eleven years of smuggling through a silent, sullen, post-war system, said *no, absolutely not, take the long route to nowhere and let the dead keep their secrets.*

Instinct usually won. But instinct had never been broke.

He queued up the last three canisters for inspection. Thirty-nine: a fisherman's memory of his daughter's first catch, wholesome as bread. Forty: a fragment, provenance unknown, picked up in a lot sale on Halcyon before the quarantine closed—unlabeled, unread, the kind of orphan memory that turned up in every runner's hold eventually, waiting for someone to claim it.

He slotted forty into the cradle.

* * *

You are on a ship, and the ship is singing.

That is the only word for it. The hull carries a tone through the deck plates and into your teeth, a harmonic so pure it feels less heard than remembered, and around you people are calm in a way that frightens you more than panic would. An old woman takes your hand. Her palm is warm. She says words you don't fully parse—something about the vote, something about *choosing*, something about the children being asleep, thank God, the children are asleep—and her voice is gentle as someone explaining that rain is coming, nothing to be done, better to bring the washing in.

You look out a viewport and the stars are wrong. Not missing—wrong. Arranged in a pattern no chart matches, and beyond them a darkness with texture to it, a darkness that leans.

You are afraid, and beneath the fear is something worse: certainty. Whoever you are in this memory, whatever name you wore, you know exactly how this ends, and you have made your peace with it, and your peace is the loudest thing in the room.

Then the harmonic rises—one note, held, sustained, a note with the shape of a word in a language made entirely of grief—and

Mirabel tore free of the cradle so fast the copper halo sparked and clattered to the deck.

His heart slammed. His hands shook. The hold lights seemed too bright and too far away at once, and for three full seconds he stood in the middle of his own ship feeling like a stranger in it, tasting someone else's fear on his tongue like copper coins.

**CANISTER 40 — PROVENANCE UNKNOWN — RESONANCE ANOMALOUS**, the display reported, with the placid idiocy of machines. And beneath it, in a field that had never once lit up in eleven years of running:

**HARMONIC MATCH: 9-SECOND PULSE — CONFIDENCE 99.7%**

"No," Mirabel said aloud. "No, no, no."

He played it back. There it was again: the note. The same impossible harmonic that had flooded every channel in the system during those nine seconds of falling, the one Idris Vane was paying triple rates to trace, rising out of a canister he'd bought in a junk lot for the price of a decent dinner.

It wasn't a distress call he'd intercepted.

It was a distress call he'd been *carrying*. Sitting in his hold, riding behind his sternum, humming its one long note into his bones across half the system—while he'd complained about his tea.

He sank into the pilot's chair and pulled the manifest back up, staring at the orphan entry. Provenance unknown. Lot sale, Halcyon, pre-quarantine. Somebody had sold this fragment cheap, unlabeled, unclaimed—which meant somebody had wanted it lost, or somebody had died before they could label it, or both.

The old woman's hand. Warm palm. *The children are asleep.*

Colony-era architecture in the viewport. Stars that matched no chart. A harmonic that matched a pulse from dead space.

Mirabel had spent eleven years telling himself he knew what he carried. Stolen childhoods. Confiscated pasts. Grief with a price tag. He had a system for it, categories, weights, tolerances. The system had just informed him, politely, in amber letters, that it had missed something enormous—that somewhere in the dark between quarantined worlds, a colony ship full of sleeping children had voted for something, and a piece of that vote had ended up in a junk lot, and then in his skull, and now it was ringing like a struck bell and would not stop.

He looked at the nav display. Vesper Gate lay four days ahead, with its customs inspectors and its triple-paying fences. Dead space lay in the other direction, past the last beacon, where the charts went blank and the wrecks of the Colony War drifted like the bones of a whale nobody would name.

Instinct said Vesper Gate. Greed said Vesper Gate. Eleven years of professional self-preservation screamed Vesper Gate.

But the fragment was still resonating—he could feel it faintly behind his eyes even now, a tuning fork pressed to the bone—and it pointed the other way.

"Oh, Wren," he said quietly, to the canister that held his sister's last morning, to the sister herself wherever she was, "you always did say I couldn't leave a locked door alone."

He turned the *Kestrel's Regret* toward dead space.
