## 4. Customs at Vesper Gate

The coffee on Vesper Gate was, by Fleet regulation, coffee. Sefa Okonkwo had been drinking it for eleven years and had long ago made her peace with the fact that it was actually hot brown disappointment with ambitions.

She drank it anyway, standing at the observation window above Docking Bay Fourteen, watching the *Lark's Errand* ease into its berth like a man trying not to wake anyone on his way to bed.

"That's him," said Corporal Adeyemi beside her, who was twenty-four and treated every smuggler like a puzzle box he was dying to shake. "Mirabel Ashgrove. Three prior inspections, three clean passes. Too clean, if you ask me."

"Nobody asked you, Corporal."

"You were drinking coffee at the window at four in the morning, ma'am. That's basically asking."

Sefa allowed that this was true. Four in the morning was when the interesting ships came through, because interesting ships came through when the inspectors were tired. She had learned this from her mother, who had learned it from the war, which had learned it the hard way.

The *Lark's Errand* was a courier-class hull, forty years old at least, patched in the particular way that said *I have nothing to hide and also please don't look at my starboard cargo bay.* Her transponder squawked the usual litany: origin Halcyon, destination neutral, manifest of agricultural seed-stock and medical textiles. Boring. Boring was a costume, and Mirabel Ashgrove wore it well.

"Run his hull scan," she said. "Full spectrum. And pull the gravity anomaly data from his transit corridor."

Adeyemi's eyebrows went up. "The nine-second flicker? That's been flagged as sensor error. Half the system had sensor error that day."

"Half the system doesn't usually have sensor error in perfect synchronization, Corporal." She turned the coffee cup in her hand. "And half the system didn't get a harmonic on every open channel right after. Did you listen to it?"

"Listened to what?"

"Exactly." She set the cup down. "Nobody listened. Everyone filed it. Filing is how the last war started—not with a bang, with a folder nobody opened."

That wasn't strictly true. The last war had started with a lot of things: a disputed relay, a broken truce, a colony ship that went out past the beacons and never came home. But the folder version was tidier, and Sefa had learned that tidiness was a kind of armor. You put the terrible thing in a tidy shape or it cut you every time you picked it up.

Her family had been cut plenty. Two brothers, a mother, an entire orbital habitat called Amara's Rest that existed now only in memorial databases and in the specific way Sefa said *no* when she meant *absolutely not.* She had been nineteen when the ceasefire signed. She had joined the Fleet two years later because somebody had to stand at the door and make sure it stayed shut, and because grief, unattended, will find itself a job.

"Inspector?" Adeyemi again. "Hull scan's back. You're going to want to see the resonance profile."

* * *

The resonance profile was, in a word, wrong.

Every hull hums. That was basic physics and older Fleet poetry—*a ship is a bell that never stops ringing,* her first instructor used to say, right before telling the class to stop daydreaming. Cargo shifts the note. Contraband shifts it further, especially when the contraband is what Ashgrove almost certainly carried: memory stock, sealed in cranial containment or cold-cased canisters, dense little knots of other people's lives.

The *Lark's Errand* hummed like a struck tuning fork that hadn't quite decided to stop.

"Ma'am," Adeyemi said slowly, "this matches the harmonic."

"I know."

"The one from the anomaly. The one you said nobody listened to."

"I know, Corporal."

"Should I be writing this down?"

"You should always be writing this down. That's what the folder is *for.*"

She read the profile three times, and each time it got worse in a new and interesting direction. The harmonic wasn't random. It was structured. Old-structured. She pulled the archive, blew the dust off protocols that hadn't been used since before she was born, and found it: Colony-era distress pattern, designated Kettle-Six, retired from Fleet use forty years ago along with the entire Colony program, the entire Colony fleet, and—as far as the official record was concerned—the entire Colony story.

Kettle-Six was a distress call no living ship should know how to sing.

And yet a grubby courier out of quarantined Halcyon was humming it through his hull like he'd learned it as a lullaby.

"Get me his docking appointment," she said. "And Adeyemi? When he comes aboard, you're going to be polite, you're going to be thorough, and you are not going to say the word 'harmonic' where he can hear it. If he knows we know, he'll dump whatever's causing this into the nearest recycling stream, and then we'll have lost the only thread that's made sense in nine days."

"And if it's nothing? If it's just a bad coil?"

"Then I buy you a real coffee off-station and we both laugh about the paranoid inspector." She was already walking toward the airlock. "But it's not a bad coil."

"How do you know?"

Because, she did not say, her mother had served on the relay watch at the edge of dead space, in the years before Amara's Rest, and had come home once with a recording she wasn't supposed to have—a fragment of Kettle-Six, half-erased, that she played late at night like a prayer. Because Sefa had grown up falling asleep to the sound of a distress call nobody answered, and you did not forget the music your mother wept to.

"Inspector's intuition," she said instead.

* * *

Mirabel Ashgrove turned out to be a lean, careful man with the eyes of someone who counted exits in his sleep, and the manners of someone raised by people who believed courtesy was a weapon you kept sheathed.

"Inspector Okonkwo." He offered his hand. His palm was dry. His pulse, when she held the handshake a half-second past comfortable, was not. "I have a delivery window on Vesper surface. I'll be as quick as your paperwork allows."

"My paperwork," Sefa said, "is the only thing standing between this station and chaos, so let's give it the respect it deserves. Sit. Tea?"

"Do you have real tea?"

"I have Fleet tea."

His mouth twitched. "Then I'll suffer through a coffee."

She liked him immediately, which was inconvenient, and she noted the liking in her mental folder marked *things to be suspicious of.* The best smugglers were likable. It was practically the job description. The worst ones were likable too, but they were usually dead by the time you met them.

The interrogation—she preferred *conversation*, but the folder preferred the other word—went the way these things went. Manifest. Route. Duration of stay in Halcyon. Purpose of the resonance-dampening field generator listed on his equipment manifest as a "vibration isolator, agricultural."

"For seeds," Ashgrove said.

"For seeds."

"Seeds are very sensitive."

"Devastatingly." She let the silence sit. Good silence did more work than any question. Ashgrove sat in it like a man who'd been sitting in silences his whole life, which told her he was good, which told her he was guilty, which told her nothing she didn't already know.

Then, because she was tired and it was four in the morning and her coffee was cold, she did the thing she probably shouldn't have.

She played him the harmonic.

She kept her face neutral and watched his. It was a small thing, the reaction. A blink held a fraction too long. A breath taken through the nose, deliberate, the way you breathe when the floor has moved. If she hadn't grown up on that sound she'd have missed it entirely.

"Recognize it?" she asked.

"Should I?"

"It's an old distress pattern. Colony-era."

"Then no." He smiled, and it was a good smile, warm at the edges. "I haul seeds, Inspector. The Colony War was over before I was born."

"Most of it was," Sefa said. "That's the trouble with wars. They end on paper first. The rest takes longer."

She stamped his manifest. She wished him a pleasant stay on Vesper Gate. She watched him walk back down the corridor toward his ship, shoulders loose, stride easy, a man with nothing to hide.

"He bought it," Adeyemi whispered. "Right? He bought the routine?"

"He bought the routine." She opened her kit and took out a tracker the size of a lentil, Fleet-issue, technically requiring a warrant she would file for in approximately four hours and receive in approximately never. "Corporal, how are you with maintenance drones?"

"I'm rated on them, ma'am."

"Wonderful. There's a coolant line on the *Lark's Errand*'s ventral hull with a loose coupling. It needs inspecting. Take your time. Be very thorough."

Adeyemi looked at the tracker, then at her, then at the tracker again, doing the math on what she was asking and what it would cost her if it went wrong. To his credit—he was twenty-four and would go far—he didn't ask if it was legal.

"And if he finds it?" he asked.

"Then I'll have made a mistake, and I'll write it in the folder, and I'll stand in front of the tribunal and say I was tired and the coffee was bad." She closed her kit. "But he won't find it. Men like Ashgrove check for the trackers they'd plant. They don't check for the ones a grieving woman would."

* * *

She watched the *Lark's Errand* undock an hour later, sliding out of Vesper Gate's shadow and burning for the deep lanes. The tracker's first ping confirmed: heading not toward the neutral trade routes at all, but toward the old debris fields, the war graveyards, the lanes no one had charted in forty years because no one had the stomach to chart them.

Toward the edge of dead space.

Sefa stood at the window with her terrible coffee going colder in her hand and did the arithmetic she'd been avoiding for nine days. The truce had eleven days left. The fleet councils were already circling each other, all the old grievances polished up and ready, and the only thing standing between the worlds and the second war was the fact that nobody had a reason good enough to blame anybody for—yet.

A distress call from a ship both fleets had sworn was lost. A smuggler carrying a piece of it in his hull, or his head, or both. A signal that predated everything, that would prove the severance wasn't an accident, wasn't fate, wasn't God—it was a decision, made by men with folders, and her family had died holding the door shut on the truth of it.

If she let Ashgrove run, and he found what she suspected was out there, and he brought it back—there was no version of that which didn't shake the truce apart. The truth was a bomb. She'd spent her whole career defusing those.

If she stopped him now, the truth stayed buried, and the war came anyway, slower, the way rot comes—through the folders, through the silence, through the tidy lies everyone agreed to keep.

Duty said stop him. Conscience, the one she'd inherited from a mother who wept at an unanswered distress call, said *follow him, and God forgive us both for what we find.*

"Ma'am?" Adeyemi. "Request permission to file the anomaly report with Fleet Command? They're asking for updates."

Sefa looked at the fading drive-flare of the *Lark's Errand*, out past the last easy light, carrying a dead colony's song toward whatever had spent forty years waiting to sing it back.

"File it," she said. "And Corporal—ready the *Vigilant*. We're going to need a faster ship."

Adeyemi blinked. "Faster than what, ma'am?"

"Than the truth," Sefa said, and went to find out how to requisition one before the war got there first.
