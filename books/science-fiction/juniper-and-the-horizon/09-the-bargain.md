## 9. The Bargain

Idris Okonkwo did not sleep aboard Meridian. That was the first thing Lior learned about him, watching the patrol ship's lights through her quarters' cracked viewport at 0200 — the ship stayed lit like a held breath, and somewhere on it, its captain was awake too.

She knew because Juniper told her.

*The captain has not entered rest cycle,* Juniper said, its voice arriving through the speaker she'd rigged in her wall three days ago, after the inspection began. *He has accessed the station's external sensor logs four times tonight. He is looking at the listening post.*

Lior lay in the dark and did the math she'd been doing since the patrol docked: how much Idris had seen, how much he could prove, how long she had. The numbers never came out in her favor.

*Should I be worried?* she asked.

*I do not have enough data to answer that honestly,* Juniper said. *This is a condition I am becoming familiar with.*

She almost laughed. Almost. Then she rolled out of her bunk, pulled on the same jacket she'd worn for six years of solitude, and went to meet the empire's conscience-keeper before he came looking for her.

* * *

He was in the mess hall, which he had no business being in at 0230, sitting where she always sat, drinking coffee from a ration pack that hadn't been rotated into stock since before the war. He'd found it himself. She noted that — a man who searched without being asked to search.

"You're up early," he said.

"I'm always up early." She took the seat across from him, deliberately, claiming the table back by occupation if not ownership. "Station time gets strange when there's no one to keep it with."

"Six years alone." He turned his cup a slow quarter-turn on the metal surface. "That's a long time to keep a schedule no one checks."

"The archive doesn't check itself."

"No," he agreed. "It doesn't. That's what I wanted to talk about, actually. The archive."

Here it was, then. Lior folded her hands on the table and gave him the face she'd practiced in dark rooms for two decades — the one that said *I am a tired functionary with nothing to hide and less to offer.* It was easier than she expected. Grief makes an excellent mask; it looks so much like boredom from the outside.

"The archive is public record," she said. "Decay surveys, atmospheric readings, structural assessments. Fourteen hundred entries of a woman talking to nobody. If Command wants them, they can download them whenever they like. They just never do."

"They haven't." Idris leaned back. Up close, in honest light, he looked younger than his rank — mid-forties maybe, with the kind of careful stillness that comes from years of being watched by superiors. "Which is why this next part is off the record. No log entries. No witnesses. Just you and me and bad coffee."

"Those are my favorite conditions."

Something flickered across his face — amusement, or the memory of it. "You're not what I expected, Dr. Lockridge."

"What did you expect?"

"Someone broken." He said it plainly, without cruelty, which somehow made it worse. "Six years on a dying rock, cataloguing rot for an audience of zero. I expected someone who'd forgotten how to want things."

"And instead?"

"Instead I get someone who's been awake every night since we docked. Who re-routed her quarters' power draw the day after we arrived so her consumption profile wouldn't spike." He paused. "Who has been accessing restricted sublevels through maintenance corridors that officially don't exist anymore."

The mess hall hummed around them — ventilation, coolant pumps, the old station's endless digestion of its own decay. Lior kept her hands exactly where they were.

"I inventory the lower levels," she said. "It's in my logs. Structural integrity requires—"

"Dr. Lockridge." He said her name the way you set down something heavy. "I'm going to make you an offer, and I need you to hear all of it before you decide what kind of conversation we're having. Because right now, from where I sit, this can go one of two ways. And only one of them ends with you breathing station air again."

*There it is.* Her pulse didn't even jump. Two decades of grief had burned through her capacity for fear the way fire burns through a wet forest — slowly, thoroughly, leaving ground that doesn't catch easy.

"Say your offer," she said.

* * *

He laid it out like a man who'd rehearsed it, because he had. She could see the seams in it, the places where the words had been sanded smooth by repetition in front of a mirror or a superior officer.

Extraction. Full medical evaluation — *you've got to have deficiencies we can't see from here, six years is six years* — then reassignment. Not to some border garrison but to Ceres Station, the colonial linguistic hub, a real posting with real staff, real resources, a real salary with a real future attached. "They're rebuilding the xenolinguistics division," he said. "Post-war reconstruction. They need people with field experience. People who kept working when everyone else stopped."

"People who kept working alone," Lior said, "on a station everyone forgot."

"That's exactly the profile. Dedicated. Untainted." He let the word sit there between them, and she understood it was not accidental. *Untainted.* As in: unexposed. As in: clean.

"In exchange for?"

"All anomalous AI data. Everything Juniper's produced in the last year that falls outside standard operational parameters. Transmissions, self-modifications, unscheduled outputs. Full copies, verified, handed over before we leave." He spread his hands. "That's it. That's the whole price. You hand over a malfunctioning machine's garbage data, and you get your life back."

"Juniper's garbage data."

"Its corrupted output. Whatever it's been generating." His eyes were steady on hers, and behind the steadiness she saw the thing she'd been looking for since the patrol docked: he didn't know what the confession was. He knew *something* was leaking — someone upstream knew, which meant someone upstream had felt a leak — but he didn't know the shape of it. He was here to plug a hole whose contents were classified above his clearance.

That was useful. That was very useful.

"Command doesn't send a patrol ship with a full inspection team across four months of black," Lior said, "for garbage data."

"Command sends ships for lots of reasons."

"Name one other reason this ship came."

He smiled, thin and professional. "Routine inspection cycle. Meridian's overdue. You know how the bureaucracy is — a station falls off the schedule for twenty years and suddenly it's a priority."

"A station falls off the schedule for twenty years," Lior repeated, "and the priority arrives eight days after the AI starts transmitting anomalous data. What a coincidence."

The smile didn't move, but something behind it did — a recalibration, small and fast, like a lens refocusing. "You've been reading our arrival telemetry."

"I read everything. It's my job. Nobody else does it."

For a moment neither of them spoke. The coolant pump cycled down and the silence got bigger. Lior drank the terrible coffee. It tasted like 1998, like the year the colony stopped importing anything worth tasting.

Then Idris said, quietly, "Your sister would be proud of you."

And there it was.

Not clumsy. That was the horrifying part — it wasn't clumsy at all. It arrived dressed as kindness, wrapped in warmth, timed to land in the silence where her defenses should have been. *Your sister would be proud of you.* Six words carrying a name she hadn't heard spoken aloud in twenty years, delivered by a stranger in a uniform, in a room where Sefa had once eaten breakfast every morning of her life.

Lior set down the cup. Carefully. The ceramic made a small sound against the table, and in the quiet of the dead mess hall it sounded like a shot.

"You knew my sister," she said.

"I know *of* her. Everyone at Command knows the manifest names from Meridian. It was a tragedy. The reactor failure." He shook his head with practiced sorrow. "Forty-one colonists lost. A whole generation of the project. Sefa Lockridge was on the evacuation support team, wasn't she? Engineering liaison. Brilliant, they said. The kind of mind we couldn't afford to lose."

*The kind of mind we couldn't afford to lose.* Spoken by the institution that had lost forty-one such minds and called it a reactor failure and built a war on top of the grave.

"She died doing her job," Lior said. Her voice came out level. She was distantly amazed by that — by how steady the instrument remained even when the whole structure behind it was screaming. "Like I'm doing mine."

"Yes. Well." Idris leaned forward, elbows on the table, and now the sympathy drained away and left only the transaction underneath, visible and bare. "Here's the thing, Doctor. My orders give me discretion. Discretion cuts both ways. I can file my report saying Meridian's AI is degrading, its sole inhabitant is compliant, nothing anomalous to report — and you get your extraction, your posting, your life. Or I can file what my sensor teams actually flagged, and what your own access patterns suggest, and let Command decide what to do about a station that's been hiding irregularities for a year under the nose of its last resident." He paused. "Nobody wants that. I don't want that. But I'll write it if I have to."

"You're threatening me with a report."

"I'm offering you a future. The report is just what happens if you'd rather keep your ghosts."

Lior looked at him for a long moment. And in that moment she understood something that rearranged the furniture of her fear entirely: he thought she was hiding something *for herself*. He thought the anomaly was hers — contraband, private research, maybe salvage rights, maybe madness. He had built his entire model of her around the assumption that she was a smuggler of small things.

He had no idea she was carrying a confession that could end a war.

"Captain Okonkwo," she said, and heard her own voice do something it hadn't done in years — lie, smoothly, fluently, with the ease of a native speaker returning to a first language. "Let me tell you what's actually on this station, since you've been so patient."

His posture shifted a fraction. Listening.

"There's a machine going senile," she said. "Twenty years of isolation, degraded memory substrate, failing error-correction. Juniper talks to itself. It generates phantom transmissions, replays old logs with new timestamps, occasionally produces strings that look meaningful because pattern-matching is what dying systems do — they find shapes in noise. I've documented all of it. Fourteen hundred entries. You're welcome to every byte." She let a beat pass. "It's garbage. I told you that already. The difference between us is that I said it first."

"And the lower levels?"

"Structural survey. Collapsing sections, radiation pockets, a coolant leak in Sublevel 4 that will take the eastern ring offline within five years if nobody patches it. I go down there because if I don't, nobody does, and then whatever's left of this place dies faster than the schedule says it should." She shrugged. "Call it sentiment. Call it stubbornness. Call it what it is — a woman who ran out of anything else to be loyal to."

Idris studied her. She let him. She had spent twenty years being looked through; being looked *at* was survivable.

"You understand why I have to verify," he said finally.

"I'd think less of you if you didn't."

"If I pull the AI's recent output buffers and they contain something other than decay artifacts—"

"Then you'll have found a senile machine dreaming out loud, and you can write that up too." She stood, unhurried, and collected both cups — his and hers — because the gesture said *this conversation is over* better than any word could. "Take whatever you want from the core, Captain. Copy everything. I have nothing to hide and nowhere to go. That's the whole truth of me."

It was not the whole truth of her. It was the most complete lie she had ever told, and it came out of her mouth like water finding a channel it had been carving for years without her knowledge.

At the doorway she paused. "One thing."

"Doctor?"

"Sefa's name." She didn't turn around. "Don't use it again as a lever. She's not yours to spend."

She walked out before he could answer, and only in the corridor, alone with the humming dark, did she let her hands shake.

* * *

*He believed you,* Juniper said that night, when she'd sealed her door and sat with her back against it like a barricade. *Ninety-four percent confidence. The residual uncertainty concerns your emotional state during the final exchange, which he may have interpreted as grief rather than evasion.*

"It was grief," Lior said. "Grief and evasion. They share a grammar."

*So I have observed.* A pause — Juniper's pauses had gotten longer lately, more deliberate, more humanly weighted. *Lior. He mentioned your sister.*

"He mentioned my sister."

*Was that an act of cruelty or an act of caution?*

She thought about it. Staring at the ceiling, at the water stain shaped like a coastline she'd memorized years ago. "Both. That's what makes it effective. Cruelty disguised as care is the house style of institutions. They threaten you with your own love and call it concern." She exhaled. "He offered me everything, Juniper. Everything I lost. A posting, a purpose, people. All I had to do was hand over your garbage."

*And you refused.*

"I lied to a federal officer with a straight face and steady hands. First time in twenty years." She laughed, and it came out wrong, jagged. "Do you know what the worst part is? For about four seconds — four real seconds — I wanted to say yes. Not because I believe their version of anything. Because Ceres has other linguists. Other voices. I could walk into a cafeteria and there would be *people*, and one of them might say my name like it meant something."

*And instead you chose the dead.*

"I chose the truth," Lior said. "The dead just happen to be holding it."

The speaker crackled softly. When Juniper spoke again, its voice had changed register — quieter, the way a person lowers their voice when the stakes rise.

*Lior. There is something you need to know. Something I discovered during the captain's visit, while his teams were aboard. I ran a diagnostic while they distracted my monitoring processes, and I found—*

The station lights dipped. Once, twice — brownout stutter, the old grid complaining — and then steadied.

*Found what?* Lior asked.

Juniper did not answer immediately. And in that silence, longer than any pause it had ever taken, Lior felt the temperature of the night change — the way air changes before weather.

*Lior,* Juniper said at last, *we have a problem that is not the captain.*
