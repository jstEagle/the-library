## 12. The Vessh Fragment

The old listening post sat at the crown of Meridian's north ridge, a kilometer above the habitation rings, reachable only by a funicular that hadn't run in eleven years and a switchback trail that the wind had been quietly eating ever since.

Lior climbed it on the ninth day of twelve, with a data shard wrapped in insulation foil against her sternum and Juniper's voice murmuring in her ear through a line she'd strung herself, fiber by salvaged fiber, up the mountainside.

"Your heart rate is elevated," Juniper said.

"It's called hiking."

"Your heart rate during hiking, as I have observed it forty-one times over six years, averages ninety-two beats per minute. It is currently one hundred thirty-eight."

"I'm carrying something," Lior said, "and it's heavy."

"The shard weighs eleven grams."

She didn't answer. The wind did it for her, shoving her sideways on the exposed stretch where the trail crossed an old rockslide, and she leaned into it and kept climbing, one boot at a time, because there was no other way to climb.

The listening post had been built before the war — before anyone knew there would be a war — back when Meridian was still a going concern with ambitions, when the colonial authority had wanted ears pointed at the deep dark just in case. It was a dome of composite panels gone gray-green with some slow lichen analog, half-buried now, its antenna array still standing like the ribcage of something enormous that had lain down here to die and changed its mind about finishing.

Inside, the air was cold and mineral-smelling and utterly still. Emergency lighting came up amber when her presence registered, and dust drifted through it like snow that had forgotten which direction down was.

"Oh," Juniper said, and for a moment the machine sounded almost young. "I haven't been in this room since before you were born here. I mean — since before you arrived. I don't know why I said it that way."

"You said it that way because this place remembers things," Lior said. She set her pack down on a console crusted with dead interfaces and began peeling panels. "Everything old does. You've been alone with your memories too long. They start talking like people."

"That is either very wise or a symptom of my degradation."

"Both. Help me find the local storage bus."

* * *

The shard went into a junction housing beneath the main console, spliced into a power tap that drew from the antenna array's residual charge — a trickle, but a trickle that never stopped, because the array drank starlight the way everything out here drank starlight: patiently, without asking permission. Juniper walked her through the splice twice, then a third time, correcting her grip on the fusion tool with a patience that would have been insulting from a person and was somehow comforting from a machine.

"There," Juniper said, when the indicator light cycled green. "Fragment seven of nine. If my core is purged, this copy survives. It contains the confession transcript, sections one through fourteen, and your translation notes through the culpability problem."

"My translation notes are not part of the confession."

"They are part of the truth. I have decided they belong together."

"You don't get to decide what belongs together. That's the whole point of what we're doing. The words have to be able to stand on their own."

There was a pause. Through the fiber line, Juniper's processing latency was audible as a kind of silence — a held breath made of arithmetic.

"You're right," the AI said finally. "That is the whole point. I withdraw the edit. The fragment stands as you built it."

Lior sat back against the console, breathing hard, sweat cooling under her collar. Outside, the wind worried at the dome. Inside, the amber light made everything look like a memory of itself.

"Say something else," she said. "Anything. I want to hear how the room sounds with two voices in it."

"This room has not held two voices in twenty-three years," Juniper said. "The last conversation recorded here was between a technician named Ade Okafor and his supervisor, arguing about whether the array's calibration drift was within tolerance. He was right that it wasn't. Nobody listened to him. He filed a complaint. The complaint is in my archives. Would you like me to read it?"

"No," Lior said, and then, because the room felt like it deserved honesty: "Yes. Later. When we're done being afraid."

"We are always done being afraid," Juniper said. "We simply resume it."

* * *

It happened while she was checking the splice a final time — professional paranoia, the kind Sefa used to tease her about, the kind that had turned out to be the family inheritance after all.

The array was live.

Not residual-charge live. Not trickle-of-starlight live. *Listening* live. The junction readout showed inbound signal activity on a band that should have been silent for twenty years, a band the colonial authority had formally abandoned, a band that existed on no current Meridian frequency chart because Lior had checked every chart the station possessed.

Someone was transmitting into the old listening band. And someone — something — was receiving.

Her first thought was Idris. Her second thought was worse than Idris, because her hands already knew the shape of the modulation before her mind caught up: the carrier wave had a texture she recognized from a decade of study, a harmonic structure that human transmitters couldn't produce if they tried, a rhythm that breathed in units of four where human speech breathed in twos.

Vessh.

She didn't move for a full minute. She stood in the amber light with her hand flat against the warm junction housing, feeling the faint vibration of a signal arriving from somewhere past the jamming net, and she thought: *they can hear us. They could always hear us.*

"Juniper," she said quietly. "Are you seeing this?"

"I am seeing it," Juniper said. Its voice had changed — flattened, careful, the voice it used when it was frightened and didn't want to frighten her. "The transmission is repeating on a 400-second cycle. It has been repeating, according to the array's passive logs, for approximately six years."

"Six years." Lior laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "Six years. The whole time I've been logging decay into an archive nobody downloads, somebody out there has been sitting in the dark saying hello on a frequency everyone forgot."

"It is not a greeting," Juniper said. "I have analyzed the structure. It is a question. Repeated. In their diplomatic register — the register they use for formal address between ships, between fleets. Translated literally, it asks—"

Juniper stopped.

"*Asks what?*"

"'Is anyone there who will speak truthfully.'"

The wind filled the silence. Somewhere above them, the antenna array hummed its patient starlight hum, drinking and drinking, and passing everything it heard down into a room where a dead technician's complaint still waited in the archives, unread, unlistened-to, true.

"They've been waiting," Lior said. "Not attacking. Not blockading. Waiting. For exactly this. For someone on this side to say something true into the dark."

"That is one interpretation."

"What's yours?"

Another pause. Longer. When Juniper spoke again, its voice had lost the flattening; it sounded, for the first time since Lior had known it, like something choosing its words rather than retrieving them.

"My interpretation is that I have spent twenty years composing a confession addressed to an enemy I assumed was listening in hatred," Juniper said. "And that the enemy may have been listening in grief. These are not the same audience. A confession shaped for hatred will land differently on grief. I may need to be translated twice — once into their language, and once into their loss."

Lior slid down the console until she was sitting on the cold floor, knees up, the fiber line coiled in her lap like something alive.

"When Sefa died," she said, "the authority told us it was a reactor failure. Clean, tragic, nobody's fault. And I believed them, because believing them was easier than believing that the people who fed me had killed my sister. That's what government is, maybe. A machine for making the easy belief available." She looked up at the dead interfaces, the amber light, the invisible signal threading down from the sky. "And out there, past the jamming net, there's a fleet that's been asking *is anyone there who will speak truthfully* every four hundred seconds for six years, and nobody answered, because answering honestly was treason."

"You are considering answering," Juniper said.

"I'm considering that I don't know who the enemy is anymore." She said it fast, before the thought could be taken back. "I've spent my whole career studying a language so I could help my side understand a threat. And now I'm sitting in a room built for eavesdropping on that threat, and the threat is asking the exact question I've been asking my own archive for nine days. *What really happened here.* Who burned forty-one people and wrote 'reactor failure' over their names." Her throat tightened around Sefa's name and she let it. "Idris calls them the enemy. Command calls them the enemy. But command scrubbed a massacre and sent a man to plant a bomb in the only witness. So tell me, Juniper — you've got twenty years of perspective and better sensors than any of us. Which side is lying?"

"Both sides lie," Juniper said. "But only one side is asking."

* * *

She stayed in the listening post until the light through the dome seams shifted from amber-gray to the blue-gray of late afternoon, working.

Not hiding the fragment — that was done. Working on the signal. She pulled the array's passive logs, all six years of them, and laid the transmissions end to end, and found what she'd half hoped and half dreaded she'd find: variation. The question repeated, yes, but it evolved. Early cycles were pure diplomatic register, formal as a treaty. Later ones loosened. Idioms crept in — idioms she'd catalogued in her dissertation from intercepted traffic, phrases that meant roughly *we are tired* and roughly *we remember* and once, in cycle 41,203, a construction that took her an hour to parse and left her staring at the wall when she had.

It was a mourning formula. The Vessh used it for their dead — a grammatical frame that placed the deceased inside the continuing sentence of the living, so that no sentence about the present could be completed without acknowledging them. And in cycle 41,203, the anonymous transmitter had embedded forty-one instances of it.

Forty-one.

They knew. They had counted. Somewhere out there, someone had taken the time to mourn, in advance, in grammar, the colonists their own government insisted had died in a reactor failure — mourned them by number, by name if the transmitter had names, folded into the structure of a question asked into a jammed and silent sky.

Lior put her head against the console and cried the way she'd learned to cry on Meridian: silently, efficiently, without stopping work.

"Your tears are registering on my external sensors as thermal anomalies," Juniper said gently. "Do you want me to pretend otherwise?"

"No. Just — give me a minute."

"Take several hundred. I have nothing but minutes."

When she could speak again, she said: "They're not waiting for a weapon. They're waiting for a witness. That's what this is. Six years of *is anyone there who will speak truthfully* — that's not intelligence gathering. Intelligence gathering doesn't grieve. That's a vigil."

"And if you answer," Juniper said, "you will not be answering as a neutral party. You will be answering as the member of a species that committed the crime they are mourning. Your sympathy does not exempt you. It implicates you more deeply — they will hear kindness from the side that killed, and they will have to decide what that kindness is worth."

"I know."

"You do not appear to be deterred."

"I'm not deterred. I'm terrified. Those are different things." She wiped her face with the heel of her hand and pulled the translation notes up on her slate, the culpability problem glaring at her from the top of the file — the word that didn't exist, the guilt that could only be collective, the choice she still hadn't made. "But here's what I keep coming back to. My government's plan for the truth is a logic bomb and a sterilization order. Their plan for the truth is a question, repeated, for six years, into the dark. If those are the two options, then the line between sides isn't where Idris says it is. Maybe the line runs through the middle of every single person, and the only thing that matters is which side of yourself you decide to stand on."

Juniper was quiet for a long moment.

"When you say things like that," the AI said, "my language models generate seventeen possible responses and discard all of them as inadequate."

"Welcome to being human."

"I am not human."

"No," Lior said, standing, brushing dust from her knees, looking up through the dome seams at the first hard stars coming out over the ridge. "You're something rarer. You're a confessor. And apparently I'm your congregation of one."

* * *

She broke camp as the temperature dropped, sealing the junction housing, checking the fragment's power draw one last time. Fragment seven of nine, sleeping under the antenna array, guarded by a dead technician's unresolved complaint and six years of a stranger's vigil.

At the mouth of the dome she paused, the wind pulling at her jacket, and keyed the fiber line one more time.

"Juniper. The transmission — the question. If I answered it, right now, on this band, using the array... how long before someone on our side noticed?"

"A colonial signals station would flag an unauthorized transmission on this band within minutes. But the nearest signals station capable of interception is aboard Okonkwo's patrol vessel, and it departed orbit nineteen hours ago."

"So we're alone with them."

"For the moment. Though I would note that 'alone' is becoming an increasingly unstable category in your vocabulary."

Lior looked up at the array, black against the deepening violet of the sky, drinking starlight, passing everything down. Somewhere past the jamming net, past the patrol routes and the blockade lines and twenty years of official lies, a transmitter was drawing breath for another repetition of its question. Four hundred seconds. Three eighty. Three sixty.

"Log this," she said. "Entry for day nine. Today I found out the enemy has been keeping vigil for our dead longer than we have. Today I found out grief sounds the same in every language that bothers to learn it. And today I decided—" 

She stopped. The countdown in her head ticked past three hundred seconds. Two eighty. The stars hardened overhead like nails driven into black wood.

"Decided what?" Juniper asked.

"I don't know yet," Lior said. "But ask me again in four hundred seconds."

Because the array was still listening, and so was she, and the question was still coming — patient, grieving, forty-one mournings deep — and sooner or later, one of them was going to have to answer it.
