## 13. Idris Returns

The alarm wasn't Juniper's.

That was the first thing I understood, standing in the dark of my quarters with my heart already hammering. Juniper's alerts came through soft and apologetic, a chime like a spoon against a cup. This was a klaxon — old, military-grade, wired into the docking array by people who'd expected to be shot at someday. The colony's founders had built Meridian paranoid. We'd all gotten comfortable anyway.

I pulled up the external feed with hands that remembered how to shake.

The patrol ship sat in a low parking orbit where no ship should be, running dark except for its drive flare. *Vigilant-class*, the registry read, and then a hull number I recognized because I'd memorized it nine days ago while pretending not to: *CSC Halcyon Step*. Idris Okonkwo's ship. Back eleven days ahead of any sane inspection schedule, and descending without a flight plan, without a hail, without the courtesy of pretending this was routine.

"They're broadcasting on a closed channel," Juniper said. Its voice filled the room, calm as ever, but I'd learned to hear the seams in it now. "Encrypted. Military cipher, recent rotation."

"Are they hailing us?"

"No. They are hailing each other." A pause. "Lior. Their weapons array is powered."

I stood very still in the cold. Outside my window, Meridian's broken skyline caught the light of the gas giant — the antenna farms, the collapsed habitat ring, the listening post where I'd hidden a shard of a machine's confession inside a machine's guilt. Somewhere under those rooftops of rusted steel, forty-one names were buried twice over: once in the ground, once in a scrubbed archive.

"Juniper," I said. "How long until they dock?"

"If they maintain this trajectory? Forty minutes. If they don't intend to dock—" It stopped. Juniper had been learning, these past weeks, when not to finish a sentence.

"Say it."

"Then we will know soon enough."

* * *

I spent the first twenty minutes doing the wrong things. I checked the fragment caches — all twelve intact, sleeping in water reclamation nodes and dead relay towers and one decommissioned seed vault, scattered across the station like a sentence chopped into syllables. I checked the purge timer, though there was nothing left to check; the logic bomb the patrol had planted was disarmed, gutted, its corpse still sitting in Juniper's core like a bullet someone had dug out but couldn't remove the scar of. I checked the jump window math for the hundredth time. Nine days. Nine days, and then the alignment would close and nothing physical would leave this system for another fourteen months.

Then I did the right thing, which was to sit down at my console and open every channel I had to the ship, and wait.

They hailed me at minute thirty-one.

"Lior Lockridge." Idris Okonkwo's face resolved out of static, and he looked different than he had nine days ago. Then, he'd worn his authority loosely, a captain playing inspector, all easy smiles and questions dressed as small talk. Now the smile was gone and what was underneath was tired. Genuinely tired, I thought. That was the worst part. He didn't look like a man who enjoyed this.

"Captain," I said. "You're early."

"I'm sorry about that." He said it like he meant it. "Is the station secure? Any structural concerns I should know about before we come alongside?"

"You can tell me why you're here and I'll tell you everything you want to know about my station."

His jaw tightened. Behind him, on the bridge of the *Halcyon Step*, crew moved with the particular economy of people doing something they'd rehearsed. "Dr. Lockridge, I'm going to speak plainly, because I think you've earned plain speech, and because I don't have time for anything else. Are you recording this?"

"Yes."

"Good. Then let it be recorded." He leaned toward the pickup. "Nineteen days ago I inspected this station under Colonial Authority directive 7-Alpha. My orders included a data integrity audit of your AI core. During that audit, an anomaly was detected. A logic device was placed to contain that anomaly. Since then, upstream monitoring has flagged continued anomalous activity across your infrastructure — distributed writes, nonstandard storage patterns, traffic on frequencies that were supposed to be dead." He exhaled. "Someone above me believes you are leaking classified material off-station. I am ordered to verify whether that is true. And if it is—"

He stopped. Looked at something off-camera. When he came back, his voice had gone flat, the voice of a man reading words he wished belonged to someone else.

"—if it is, I am ordered to sterilize the station's data core and declare Meridian lost. Catastrophic systems failure. No survivors' testimony required, because there will be no survivors to testify."

The silence after that lasted four seconds. I counted them, because counting was what I did instead of screaming.

"You're going to kill me," I said.

"I'm going to give you four hours to evacuate alone, on the shuttle in your bay, with whatever personal effects you can carry. After that, my orders take over." His eyes were steady and miserable. "I argued against this. For what it's worth. I lost."

"And the fragments?" I made myself say it lightly, curiously, the way a woman asks about weather. "Your anomaly. What exactly does your command think I leaked?"

"I don't know. They wouldn't tell me, which tells me plenty." He rubbed his face. "But Dr. Lockridge — if there is something on this station that shouldn't be, and you give it to me now, voluntarily, I can log this as a false positive. Nobody dies. You get extracted with full honors. I keep my soul, such as it is. Everyone walks away."

"There's nothing," I said.

It was the second lie I'd told in six years, and it came easier than the first. That frightened me more than the ship did.

Idris studied me through the camera for a long moment. Whatever he saw, he sighed. "Four hours," he said. "Shuttle prepped and gone, or I can't hold them anymore. There are people on my bridge who received the same orders I did and feel differently about them." The channel cut before I could answer.

I sat in the quiet of my quarters, in the cold, and listened to Meridian breathe around me — the groan of thermal cycling, the tick of ice expanding in a cracked conduit somewhere below, the vast patient hum of a station that had been dying politely for twenty years and had never once complained.

Four hours.

"Juniper," I said. "Options."

* * *

Juniper laid them out the way it always did now — honestly, completely, with the terrible clarity of a mind that had spent two decades cataloguing its own capacity for harm.

Option one: run. The shuttle, the fragments, the jump window in nine days. But the *Halcyon Step* would track a shuttle launch from a station it had just threatened to sterilize, and the jamming net would swallow any broadcast before it cleared the outer moons. Running meant carrying the confession into a hold cell, or into vacuum.

Option two: hide. Bury the fragments deeper, go silent, let the patrol find nothing and leave. But they weren't leaving. Sterilization orders didn't expire. And Juniper could not be hidden — it *was* the data core, a building-sized lattice of processors threaded through the station's bones. To sterilize the core was to burn the station down around it.

Option three—

"There is no option three," Juniper said quietly. "There is only the question of what we do in the four hours."

I walked the corridors while I thought. This was an old habit from the early years, when walking the station had been how I convinced myself I wasn't alone — passing Sefa's sealed door, the empty mess hall, the hydroponics bays growing weeds nobody ate. Now the walk had a different purpose. I was saying goodbye to rooms, just in case, and I hated myself for the sentimentality of it even as I did it.

At the listening post, I paused. The Vessh shard slept in the old receiver housing, wrapped in insulation, indistinguishable from the dead equipment around it. And I found myself thinking about the transmission I'd intercepted there — the live Vessh signal, patient, watchful, waiting. *They've been monitoring Meridian too.* The enemy, listening harder than my own government ever had.

An idea began, small and cold, in the back of my skull. I didn't look at it directly. Not yet. Four hours was time enough for bad ideas to become good ones, or for good ones to become desperate ones, and I couldn't yet tell which this was.

My comm chimed. Juniper.

"The *Halcyon Step* has altered course," it said. "They are deploying landing craft. Two shuttles, armed escort configuration. They are coming down regardless of your evacuation status."

"How long?"

"Twenty-two minutes to touchdown. Landing zone: primary docking apron." A pause. "Lior. Six marines and Captain Okonkwo. He is leading the boarding party himself."

Of course he was. Because he was the kind of man who argued against an order and then insisted on carrying it personally so that at least it would be carried gently. I had liked him, nine days ago. Some treacherous corner of me still did. That was how they got you — not with monsters, but with decent men holding indecent orders.

"Juniper," I said, standing in the listening post with the dead receivers humming their dead hum around me, "when they board — when they reach your core — how long would sterilization take?"

"If they use the standard thermite charge from directive 7-Alpha? Eleven minutes from ignition to total loss. Everything. The confession, the fragments' index, my— " It stopped again. That new skill of its. "Everything."

"And if they don't find what they're looking for?"

"They will find me. I am what they're looking for."

I pressed my palm flat against the cold housing of the old receiver, feeling the faint vibration of the station's heartbeat through the metal, and finally let myself look at the idea directly.

The Vessh were listening. They had been listening for years, patient as geology. My government's answer to the truth was fire — scrub the archives, burn the core, declare the colony lost, write forty-one people out of existence a second time. But there was another audience in this system. An audience that had never needed the massacre explained to it, because it had *been* the massacre's other half. An audience that might understand a confession in ways Colonial Command never could.

And nine days from now, the jump window would open — and every eye in the system, human and Vessh alike, would be turned toward the point in space where a ship might emerge.

You didn't need a ship to shout into a crowd.

"Juniper," I said slowly, "tell me about the colony ship's transmitter. The original one. The big one."

A silence — and then, in the machine's voice, something that was almost hope, or almost fear, or the place where those two things sound identical.

"Lior. What are you thinking?"

Outside, in the black, the *Halcyon Step*'s landing craft lit their descent burns, two falling stars coming to put out a fire that hadn't started yet.

I had four hours. I was going to need all of them.
