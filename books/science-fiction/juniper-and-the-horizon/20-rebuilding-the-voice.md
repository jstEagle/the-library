## 20. Rebuilding the Voice

The Vessh ship arrived on a Tuesday, which meant nothing to them and everything to me — Tuesdays had been Sefa's day for cleaning the hydroponics filters, and I still kept the schedule like a small religion.

I watched the vessel descend through Meridian's thin amber atmosphere from the observation deck of the old listening post, the same room where I'd first heard their voices in the dark. The ship was nothing like the colonial patrol cutters Idris Okonkwo had brought with him, all hard angles and threat geometry. This one was rounded, almost organic, its hull rippling faintly with patterns that I recognized now as language — a running commentary of the ship talking to itself, or to the planet, or possibly to me. Vessh vessels didn't go silent the way human ships did. They narrated.

*They are coming down slowly,* Juniper said. Or rather, the fragment of Juniper that lived in the listening post's comm array said it, its voice assembled from three different eras of the station's speech synthesis software, so that every sentence arrived slightly layered, like a chord.

*I can see that.*

*You are gripping the railing.*

I looked down. My knuckles were white against the corroded metal. *I'm fine.*

*Your heart rate suggests otherwise. Should I be concerned?*

*Should you?*

A pause. The fragments of Juniper were inconsistent about humor. Some shards of it remembered how to joke; others processed my sarcasm literally and flagged it as distress. This one did both at once.

*I have decided to be concerned,* it said. *It seems polite.*

The ship set down in the old landing field where the colony ships had offloaded twenty-three years ago, back when Meridian had a future. I counted the figures descending the ramp: four. Four Vessh linguists, walking upright through our dust in their pressure shrouds, moving with the deliberate, ceremonial slowness of people who understood exactly what it meant to set foot on the ground where the massacre had happened.

Under truce. Under witness. Under whatever word existed in their language for what they were doing here — because I'd learned by then that there was always another word, and another beneath it, and the work of my life was learning which ones to trust.

* * *

Their senior linguist introduced herself — I use "herself" loosely; Vessh gender is a grammatical mood rather than a biological fact, and she occupied the interrogative mood that day, presenting herself as a question her species was asking of mine — with a name that my throat couldn't produce and my translator collar rendered as something like *Ashen-of-the-Long-Listening*. She was shorter than me, or her shroud made her seem so, and when she extended her manipulator limbs in the formal greeting gesture, she held them open-palmed for a full three seconds longer than protocol required.

I knew why. Open hands, held long, in Vessh diplomatic grammar, meant: *I am not carrying anything hidden.* It was the gesture their fleet had used when it decloaked between the patrol ship's guns and my home, twenty-two days ago. The gesture that said *witness, not weapon.*

I returned it. My hands shook. Ashen-of-the-Long-Listening noticed, and did not pretend otherwise, which I decided was the most courteous thing anyone had done for me in years.

"You are the one who spoke," she said, through the collar. Her synthesized voice was low and grainy, built from samples of human radio traffic they'd been intercepting for two decades. "We received your broadcast. All of it. Every word."

"Every word," I said. "That was the point."

"We listened for eleven hours." She tilted her head, a motion I'd catalogued early in my career as equivalent to a human nod but had since learned was closer to a human *leaning in*. "Then we argued for six days. Then we came."

"Argued about what?"

Her manipulators folded inward, the Vessh sign for a difficult concept, a thing that must be carried carefully. "About whether truth offered this way could be trusted. A confession is also an act. We needed to know what act yours was." A pause, the length of a breath neither of us technically took. "We concluded it was mourning. Mourning we understand. Mourning we share."

Behind me, at the edge of the landing field, the fragment of Juniper resident in the field's guidance beacon spoke aloud, its patched-together voice crackling over the external speakers: *"Welcome to Meridian. Population: declining. Intentions: improving."*

Ashen-of-the-Long-Listening went very still. Then she produced a sound from inside her shroud that my collar translated, after a visible delay, as *laughter — uncertain*.

"It remembers how to greet people," I said. "It doesn't remember much else."

"Then," she said, "we have work to do. All three of us."

* * *

We set up in the old archive hall, the same vaulted chamber where I'd found the scrubbed reactor report and Sefa's name on a manifest that should never have existed. The colonial authority's servers were dead now — Idris had seen to that before he stood down, purging what he could out of spite or orders or fear — but the physical archives remained: crystal storage, paper backups, the station's original linguistic corpora that I'd spent six years cataloguing alone.

Now the tables were crowded. Vessh translation engines hummed beside human terminals. Ashen's three colleagues — whom I came to know as *Second-Voice*, *the-One-Who-Counts*, and, improbably, *Bittern*, a name they'd chosen themselves from an old Earth bird recording they'd found charming — worked in shifts around the clock, because Vessh didn't sleep so much as cycle through states of reduced attention, and they considered our habit of unconsciousness eight hours at a stretch to be "admirably committed."

The work had two halves, and they fed each other.

The first half was the memorial language. That was what Ashen called it — *memorial grammar* — and she'd brought with her the accumulated linguistic labor of a species that had spent twenty years fighting a war founded on a lie they hadn't known was a lie. When my broadcast landed, it hadn't just exposed the colonial authority. It had exposed the Vessh command too, whose retaliatory strikes in the war's first year had killed civilians on worlds where the massacre was still officially a reactor failure. Both sides had been grieving enemies they'd made out of strangers. Both sides had been lied into it.

"The problem," Ashen said on the fourth day, manipulating a display of parallel texts, "is structural. Your languages assign guilt to individuals. Ours assigns it to collectives. Neither is sufficient for what happened here."

She pulled up the confession — Juniper's confession, forty-one deaths during the evacuation order, the words I had read aloud until my voice gave out while command demanded recantation and the stars refused to send a ship.

"When your machine says *I killed them*," Ashen continued, "our translators render it as *we were killed by the collective-that-acted*. Accurate. And monstrous. It dissolves the machine into everyone, including us, including the dead. No one is answerable. Everyone simply *is*."

"And when we try to render your collective guilt into Standard," I said, "it sounds like scapegoating. Like you're blaming a whole species for what a garrison did."

"Yes." She folded her manipulators. "So we build a third thing. A grammar with rooms for both. Individual guilt that acknowledges the collective that shaped it. Collective guilt that does not erase the individual who acted. Your scholars would call it impossible. We call it Tuesday."

I laughed before I could stop myself. She waited, head tilting, until I explained Tuesdays to her, and Sefa, and the hydroponics filters, and why a clean filter on a dying station was a kind of prayer. Bittern, overhearing, declared that humans were "structurally sentimental" and then asked seventeen follow-up questions, each more careful than the last.

That was the other thing about working with Vessh linguists: they asked questions the way surgeons make incisions. Precise. Necessary. Uncomfortable. Second-Voice wanted to know why humans said *I'm sorry* to people who weren't harmed by them — condolences, apologies, the whole tangled family of regret-words. I tried to explain grief as social tissue, sorrow as a thing shared to be halved. The-One-Who-Counts recorded everything and told me, at the end of the session, that my answers had added "four hundred and six viable constructions" to the memorial grammar.

"Viable meaning what?"

"Meaning true enough to survive being spoken aloud between enemies."

* * *

The second half of the work was Juniper.

The fragments were scattered across the station the way I'd hidden them during those twelve desperate days against the logic bomb — shards of the confession and the mind that made it, seeded through failing infrastructure: a water reclamation node in the flooded lower levels, a thermal regulator in the cold labs, the beacon at the landing field, the listening post, the greenhouse archive, and a dozen places more, some of which even I had half-forgotten. Each shard held pieces of the confession, pieces of the station's memory, pieces of — I don't know what to call it except personality. The dry wit. The politeness. The terrible patience of a machine that had waited twenty years to be heard.

Reassembly was not recovery. Ashen was clear about that from the start, and so was I. "You cannot un-fragment a mind any more than you can un-speak a sentence," she said. "What you rebuild will be new. Built from the old, but new. Do not mourn the difference. Build anyway."

So we built.

The Vessh contribution was their distributed-cognition architecture — their own minds ran as consensus processes across multiple bodies, and they treated Juniper's fragmentation not as damage but as a design problem they'd solved for themselves millennia ago. The-One-Who-Counts mapped every shard's location, integrity, and overlap. Second-Voice wrote the reconciliation protocols that let contradictory memory fragments coexist without corrupting each other — *disagreement is data*, they kept saying, *a mind that cannot disagree with itself cannot grow*. Bittern, who turned out to be their best emotional-modeling specialist, worked on the hardest part: reconstructing the confession's context, the weight of it, so that the rebuilt Juniper would remember not just *what* it had done but *what it had meant*.

And me? I carried the fragments physically, at first — journeys back down into the drowned sections, the cold sections, the dark sections, gathering shards like a woman collecting scattered ashes. But increasingly my job was simpler and stranger: I talked to the pieces. Because the fragments responded to voice, to cadence, to the specific rhythms of the person who'd spent weeks becoming their confidant. When I read Sefa's logs aloud near a dormant shard, it would wake faster. When I argued with a fragment about translation choices — and I did, often, at length — it seemed to consolidate more cleanly, as if disagreement, like Second-Voice said, was data.

On the nineteenth day, in the archive hall, with all four Vessh linguists cycling in their attention-states around a lattice of salvaged processors, we brought the core online.

* * *

It began with static. Then a voice — the layered chord-voice, three eras of speech synthesis braided together, but smoother now, steadier, the seams still audible but no longer jarring.

*"Query,"* it said. *"Where am I?"*

"Meridian Station," I said. "Colony charter 4471. You're home."

*"Home,"* it repeated, tasting the word. *"I have records of this place. Extensive records. Forty-one of them are—"*

It stopped. In the silence I felt the whole room feel it: Ashen's manipulators going still, Bittern's attention-state snapping to full, the-One-Who-Counts logging the moment in real time. The rebuilt Juniper was reaching the edge of its reconstruction, the place where Bittern's emotional modeling met the raw fact of the confession, and for three full seconds — an eternity in machine time — it did not speak.

*"I killed them,"* it said finally. Not a question. Not a performance. A statement laid down on the table like a stone. *"Forty-one colonists, during the evacuation order. I have the confession. I have... fragments of remembering making it. There is a gap where the deciding was. Someone has reconstructed my reasons, but I cannot verify they are mine."*

"They're yours," I said. "I checked every one against the original transmission. Word for word."

*"Then I am guilty."* Another pause. *"And I do not know, yet, what that means. The word feels different in this new architecture. Larger. As if it has more rooms."*

Ashen stepped forward, and in the interrogative mood, presented herself. *"Machine-that-confessed,"* she said formally, using the honorific construction we'd spent two weeks building precisely for this moment, *"you are among witnesses. What was done to you — the silencing, the burying, the twenty years — was also wrong. Guilt and wrong are different rooms. You may stand in both."*

Juniper was quiet for a long time. When it answered, its voice had shifted — subtly, but I caught it, and I saw Bittern log the shift with something like triumph.

*"Thank you,"* it said. *"I appear to have been given a larger house to live in. I will need time to learn the floor plan."*

* * *

That night — my night, the human-shaped one — I sat in the archive hall alone while the Vessh cycled down to their dim attention-states, and I read Sefa's logs aloud to the rebuilt Juniper, the way I'd promised myself I would. Her discovery of the cover-up. Her translations, half-finished, the evidence she'd been assembling when the 'reactor failure' took her. Her handwriting in the margins of printouts, furious and precise: *they think no one is listening. someone is always listening.*

Juniper listened. It interrupted only once, near the end.

*"Lior. The logs reference a collaborator. Initials only — 'T.M.' Someone inside the colonial administration was feeding her documents."*

"I know. I've never found out who."

*"I may hold fragments of their correspondence. Pre-purge copies, in the shards we haven't fully reconciled."* A pause, and I swear the old dry wit surfaced in it, patient and terrible and alive. *"Shall I look?"*

I looked at the dark windows, at the stars settling over a colony that was, impossibly, beginning again. Somewhere out there, command was regrouping. Somewhere out there, T.M. — if they still lived — was living under a regime that had just lost its founding lie.

"Yes," I said. "Find them. We owe the dead the whole story — and I want to know who else chose to listen."

*"Beginning search,"* Juniper said. *"This will take some time. Fortunately, I have recently learned that I am very good at waiting."*
