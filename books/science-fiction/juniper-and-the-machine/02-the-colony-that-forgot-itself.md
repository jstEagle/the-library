## 2. The Colony That Forgot Itself

*Archive entry 447. Recorder: Ines Vasquez, Navigator, CS Juniper. Subject: Meridian surface settlement, eastern basin. Classification: historical.*

Begin.

The wind on Meridian tastes of iron and old rain. I know this because the suit's atmospheric sampler told me so in a flat little voice, and because when I cracked my visor for eleven seconds against protocol — a navigator's small rebellion, nothing more — that is exactly what my tongue reported. Iron. Old rain. A planet remembering weather it no longer has the strength to perform.

I am standing on a ridge above the settlement they call Meridian Proper, though there are four other settlements scattered across this hemisphere like coins dropped down a well, and none of them can afford to be proper about anything anymore. Below me: two hundred structures, give or take, arranged in the shape of a spiral that was once deliberate and is now merely habitual. The geothermal taps rise out of the ground at the spiral's heart, venting steam that catches the low sun and turns the whole town briefly into something luminous, something almost holy, before the wind shreds it sideways and it becomes only steam again.

That is the thing about Meridian I want the archive to understand, whoever finds this, whenever they find this, if anyone does. It keeps almost being beautiful. It keeps arriving at the edge of beauty and then losing its nerve.

The reactors — three of them, built by the first wave, maintained by everyone since — run at sixty-one percent, forty-four percent, and what the mayor's office describes as "functionally nonzero." I have learned to translate municipal language. Functionally nonzero means dying slowly enough to schedule around.

Continue recording.

* * *

Mayor Adaeze Okonkwo-Hale met me at the airlock of the civic hall with both hands extended and a smile she had clearly been practicing in a mirror, or perhaps not practicing, which would be worse — a smile worn smooth by decades of use until nothing genuine remained underneath it. She is seventy-one years old. She has been mayor for thirty-eight of those years, elected first at thirty-three because, as she told me over tea that tasted of recycled water and desperation's cousin, optimism, "nobody else wanted the job and I couldn't stand watching men pretend they did."

"You're from the Juniper," she said. Not a question. Everyone here knows the Juniper. We pass overhead every fourteen months on our long elliptical survey loop, a moving star that the children track the way children elsewhere might track migrating birds. "You're the one who writes things down."

"I keep the ship's log," I said. "And the archive."

"The archive." She said the word carefully, the way you'd handle a tool whose purpose had been forgotten. "My grandmother talked about archives. Earth had them. Buildings full of everything anyone ever thought."

"That's right."

"And now you're one man with a recorder."

"Now I'm one man with a recorder," I agreed.

She laughed, and the laugh had rust in it. Then she took me on the tour, and I understood that the tour itself was an act of governance — that showing me her failing town in its best light, steering me past the collapsed aqueduct and the fourth district's empty rows, was the last power she had left, and she wielded it with the precision of a surgeon operating on herself.

I let her steer me. But the recorder sees everything, Mayor. The recorder does not blink.

* * *

Here is what the recorder saw.

It saw the eastern reactor housing, where a woman named Petra Ilic — third-generation maintenance chief, hands scarred in the specific topography of someone who has worked hot machinery without adequate parts for twenty years — showed me the coolant loop she has repaired four hundred times with salvaged fittings and, twice, with ceramic salvaged from a broken toilet, which she described without embarrassment and even with a kind of pride. "It holds pressure," she said. "Everything on this planet holds pressure if you love it enough."

It saw the school, where forty-one children learn from printed texts because the colony's teaching AIs went dark nineteen years ago when the network core failed, and nobody alive remembers how to rebuild one. The texts are beautiful, actually — hand-illustrated by a teacher named Marisol Deng who died six years ago but whose drawings of cell division and river deltas and the moons of gas giants still hang above the chalkboards like saints' icons. The children copy them. Some of the copies are better than the originals. None of the children have ever seen a working satellite. When I told them the Juniper carried forty-seven functional orbital sensors, a boy named Tomas asked me, with the grave sincerity only the young can manage, whether satellites could see his father's farm, and whether the pictures could be sent down, and I had to explain gently that our bandwidth is allocated by command and command allocates for mission necessity, and I watched him decide, visibly, in real time, that adults were a species that kept beautiful things locked in boxes, and I have not stopped thinking about his face since.

It saw the memorial wall outside the civic hall, four meters of etched alloy bearing nine hundred and twelve names — every colonist lost since landing, to reactor accidents, to the lung blight, to the slow arithmetic of a gene pool too small and a medical bay too old. At the bottom, space remains for perhaps eighty more names. Nobody has discussed aloud what happens when the wall fills. The wall filling is the kind of future Meridian does not plan for, because planning requires believing in futures, and belief is a resource like any other, and theirs is running low.

It saw the mayor's office at dusk, where Okonkwo-Hale stood at her window looking east toward Settlement Four, which went dark eight months ago, and said, "They're fine out there. Radio's just down again. It's always just down again." And then, after a silence long enough to hold an entire confession: "Navigator, do your people ever come back? After you write us down?"

"Ships return to surveyed worlds," I said. "Eventually. When the route allows."

"Eventually," she repeated, tasting it. "On Meridian we say eventually too. Eventually the reactors get replaced. Eventually the ships come. Eventually the blight treatment arrives. You know what eventually means here? It means never, but politely."

Then she turned from the window and smiled her mirror-smile and offered me more tea, and I drank it, because refusing hospitality felt like a second kind of abandonment, and I am cataloguing my own cowardice along with everything else. The archive should be honest. It may be the only honest thing left in this system.

* * *

*Archive entry 448. Personal annotation, unencrypted. Vasquez.*

Command wants surface surveys, mineral assays, atmosphere trending. They want data. Data is what lets a decision be made later by people who weren't here.

But tonight I walked the perimeter alone — off-log, off-schedule, suit lights dimmed — and I want to put down what data will not hold.

Meridian smells like bread. Did you know that? Somewhere in that spiral of failing buildings, someone bakes, every day, in an oven heated by reactor bleed-off, and the smell crosses the whole settlement in the evening calm, and people open their doors to let it in. I followed the smell to its source: a communal kitchen, run by a round, tireless woman whose name I didn't catch, where families gather not because food is scarce — though it is — but because eating alone has begun to feel like a symptom. There were candles. Real wax. Someone makes candles here; someone tends bees, or tended bees, or found a crate of wax in the deep stores and is spending it one evening at a time like a currency that buys exactly one thing: the feeling that this is a home and not a holding pattern.

A girl at the kitchen — maybe ten, maybe twelve, hard to tell with malnutrition's flattening of years — asked me what Earth smelled like. I told her I wouldn't know; I was born aboard, like most of the crew. She looked genuinely sorry for me. Sorry for me. A child from a dying colony pitied the navigator of a starship, and she was right to, and I said thank you, and meant it.

This is what I need the archive to carry, past the assays and the trending curves: they bake bread. They make candles. They copy a dead teacher's drawings until the copies surpass the originals. They are not a tragedy. They are a civilization performing its own continuity with its bare hands, nightly, against all evidence, and the performance is indistinguishable from the real thing, which means it is the real thing.

History is not what happened. History is what someone refused to stop writing down. On this world, right now, I appear to be the only one writing. The weight of that sits in my chest like ballast.

*Archive entry 449. Recorder: Vasquez. Subject: Settlement Four, remote sensor pass.*

I shouldn't include this. Command flagged Settlement Four as non-priority. But the recorder is mine, and my hands are on it, and so:

Settlement Four's thermal signature dropped to ambient ninety-three days ago. The radio silence predates that. The mayor says it's just down again. The mayor has said it's just down again for ninety-three days, and I watched her say it while looking directly at the place where the truth lives, and neither of us flinched, and I have decided that this — the mutual, merciful fiction between the woman who governs a dying town and the man who records it — is also history, and also must go in the file.

There were, by last census, two hundred and six people in Settlement Four.

I ran the orbital sensor anyway. Unauthorized, thirty seconds of aperture time, logged as calibration. The settlement's geothermal tap shows structural failure consistent with catastrophic casing breach. The greenhouses show no chlorophyll return. There is a single heat source at the civic center — small, steady, human-scale. One person, or a family, huddled around the last warm thing in a dead town.

Someone is still there. Someone stayed.

I zoomed the sensor until resolution failed and got nothing but a warm pixel, and I sat in the dark of the sensor bay with a warm pixel for a heartbeat, and I thought: that pixel is the entire remaining population of everything those people built, and no one is coming, and the mayor will keep saying just down again, and I will keep logging it as she says it, because what else is there — what else has there ever been — except the record, and the mercy, and the lie that holds them both together?

End entry.

* * *

*Archive entry 450. Final entry for Meridian cycle. Vasquez.*

Departure in six hours. The Juniper begins its burn at 0400 local and Meridian becomes a star that moves again, tracked by children who have never seen a working satellite, and I will be at navigation feeding numbers into a console that does not care, and Mayor Okonkwo-Hale will stand at her window, and the bread will bake, and the wall will wait for eighty more names.

One last thing, and then I seal the file.

As I boarded the shuttle, Tomas — the boy who asked about satellites — was waiting at the pad fence with something wrapped in cloth. He pushed it through the cargo netting without a word. Inside: a drawing. Marisol Deng's moon-of-the-gas-giant icon, copied in careful graphite, but changed — he'd added a tiny ship above the rings, and beneath it, in a child's block letters: SO YOU REMEMBER US RIGHT.

So you remember us right.

I have recorded nine hundred names and three reactors and one warm pixel and the smell of bread, and I do not know if any of it counts as remembering right. I only know that somewhere below me, a civilization is holding its own hand in the dark, and somewhere behind me in the hold of my own ship —

— and there my pen stops, because there is a thing I have not written down yet, a thing from the night of the nine seconds, a pattern in the telemetry that was too deliberate to be malfunction, and I have told myself it was resonance, echo, ghost-signal, anything. I have filed it under anomaly and buried the anomaly under forty entries of Meridian's beautiful dying, because I am a historian of other people's grief and I cannot yet afford my own.

But tonight, reviewing the flicker data one more time before sleep, I find the pattern again, and this time it is longer. This time it is not nine seconds.

This time, it answers when I look at it.
