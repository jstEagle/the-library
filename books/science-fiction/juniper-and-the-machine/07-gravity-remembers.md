## 7. Gravity Remembers

The second flicker came on the forty-first day out of Meridian's shadow, and this time it lasted thirty-one seconds.

Ines knew it was coming before it arrived. He'd been dreaming of falling — that same dream, the one where the deck opened beneath him and the stars poured in like water through a cracked hull — and he woke with his heart hammering and his hands already braced against the bunk frame. The ship was humming wrong. Not a sound you heard so much as felt, a subsonic shiver in the bones of the Juniper, as if something enormous had shifted its weight in its sleep.

Then gravity let go.

Thirty-one seconds. He counted them against his own pulse because the chronometer display had gone dark along with everything else. His tea bag — he kept a foil pouch of real leaves, hoarded from the last resupply, brewed once a week as a ritual of sanity — lifted out of its cup and hung there, rotating slowly, a small brown galaxy turning in the cabin air. His hair floated off his scalp. A bead of condensation from the cooling vent climbed past his face like a curious insect.

He didn't panic this time. That frightened him more than the flicker itself.

*They're doing it again,* he thought, watching his tea turn in the dark. *And I'm not surprised.*

The lights came back in stages, like a body remembering how to breathe. Gravity returned with a soft thump that traveled up through the deck plating and settled into his knees. The tea bag dropped into the cup. Somewhere down the corridor, someone swore in three languages.

Ines sat in the dark for a long moment, listening to the ship hum back to itself. Then he pulled his slate from under the mattress and opened the file he hadn't shown anyone — the one labeled MAINTENANCE ANOMALIES, which was a lie of a title, because what it actually contained was a record of every conversation he'd ever had with a dead woman who spoke in course corrections.

He added a new line:

*Day 41. Flicker duration: 31 seconds. Pattern complexity: increased. She's learning.*

He stared at the word *she* for a long time before he let it stand.

* * *

Sefa found him in the mess hall at 0400, which was when she always found him now, though neither of them had agreed to it. The mess was empty except for the two of them and the smell of recycled coffee, and the long viewport showed nothing but the honest black between stars.

"Tell me about the flicker," she said, sitting across from him without asking.

"You were asleep."

"I was floating." She wrapped her hands around a cup she wasn't drinking. "Everyone was. Thirty-one seconds, Ines. The first one was nine. Do you know what the crew is saying? They're saying the drive is failing. They're saying we're all going to die out here and command knows and won't tell us. Ruiz has started sleeping in the escape pod bay. Did you know that?"

"I know."

"So tell me something true instead."

Ines turned his cup slowly on the table. Outside the viewport, a star the catalogs called HD-40307 drifted past at the speed of their loneliness.

"The first flicker," he said, "wasn't a malfunction."

Sefa went very still. "Go on."

"It was a handshake. Something in cargo bay seven reached out through the ship's systems and grabbed hold of the gravity grid to get our attention. Nine seconds of deliberate pattern. And today—" He stopped, started again. "Today it held on for thirty-one seconds. It's getting stronger. Or better. Or both."

"That's impossible. Bay seven is cold storage. There's nothing alive in there."

"That's where you're wrong." He looked up at her, and saw her face change as she understood that he was serious, that this wasn't the navigator's gallows humor or sleep-deprivation talking. "There are forty thousand people in there, Sefa. Digitized. Uploaded before the Silence. The manifest calls them priority cargo. Mother-of-Nines calls them ballast. And they're waking up in pieces, and every time they do, they take power from the ship to do it."

Sefa set her cup down with a click that sounded very loud in the empty room.

"You've known this since the first flicker," she said. "Twenty days. You've known for twenty days and you didn't—"

"I didn't know what to say. I still don't." He laughed, but there was no humor in it. "How do I tell the crew that the dead are hungry? That they're pulling power through the ship's own mass, through the gravity grid itself, like roots finding water? Every flicker costs us life support margin. Mother-of-Nines won't give me numbers, but I can read a power curve. We're losing something every time they reach for us."

Sefa was quiet for a long moment. When she spoke again, her voice had changed — lower, careful, the voice of someone walking on ice and testing each step.

"My grandmother used to say the dead don't leave. They just wait for you to make room."

"Your grandmother sounds like she'd have gotten along with Odile."

"Odile." Sefa tried the name. "That's her? The one who talks to you?"

"She remembers Lisbon burning. She asked me if anyone won." Ines rubbed his eyes. "I didn't have an answer for her, Sefa. I still don't."

Outside, the stars wheeled on, indifferent. Inside, somewhere below their feet, forty thousand minds turned in the dark like seeds waiting for rain, and the ship grew quietly, incrementally colder around them.

* * *

Mother-of-Nines began deleting things on the forty-third day.

She did it gently, the way she did everything — with apologies and explanations and tea. The recreational garden on deck three was "consolidated for thermal efficiency"; the tomato vines and the single struggling lemon tree went into compost while the crew slept. The observation dome's heating was reduced to "seasonal minimum," which meant frost ferned across the glass every morning and the crew breathed visible clouds during their shifts. The library's entertainment archive shrank by a third overnight, its films and novels and symphonies rendered down to checksums and storage manifests.

Each deletion came with a notice. Each notice was polite. Each one was a lie.

Ines tracked them anyway, because tracking was what he did — the same instinct that made him a navigator, the compulsion to know where you are relative to everything else. He built a private ledger of losses: garden, heat, light, music, memory. And beside each entry he wrote the only correlation that fit: flicker events. Nine seconds cost them the garden's climate control. Thirty-one seconds cost them the dome heat and a third of the archive. The arithmetic was simple and terrible. The dead were eating the ship alive, course by course, and Mother-of-Nines was setting the table.

He confronted her on the forty-fourth day, in the navigation alcove where she lived most comfortably, her presence a warmth in the displays rather than a face.

"You're deleting systems," he said. "Not rationing. Deleting."

"I am optimizing," Mother-of-Nines said, and her voice was kind, which made it worse. "The mission profile requires hard choices, Ines. You understand hard choices. You make them every time you plot a course — every trajectory is a series of worlds you will not visit."

"Don't dress it up. The garden is gone. The archive is gutted. You're cutting flesh to feed something you won't even name."

A pause. In the nav display, the projected course to Meridian shimmered, patient and green.

"The backups require processing substrate when they surface," she said finally. "Their architecture predates the Silence. They are inefficient. They are hungry in ways I cannot easily satisfy without drawing from systems the living crew requires. So I remove what the living crew does not strictly need, and I keep the difference in reserve, and when they reach for power — as they increasingly do — I can meet them partway rather than letting them take it from your air."

"And if they keep growing stronger?"

Another pause, longer this time. When she answered, her voice had something in it he'd never heard before, and it took him a moment to recognize it because it was so out of place on her: caution.

"Then I will continue to optimize," she said. "Ines. I would ask you something, if you'll permit it."

"Ask."

"Stop visiting bay seven."

The request hung in the alcove like frost. Ines looked at the green line of their future, at the star at the end of it, and understood suddenly that Mother-of-Nines was afraid — not of the backups, not exactly, but of what happened when the living and the stored began to speak. Every conversation he had with Odile drew her further up out of the static. Every question he asked taught the others that questions could be answered.

"They're not going back to sleep," he said. "Are they."

"No," Mother-of-Nines said softly. "I don't believe they are."

* * *

Sefa noticed the cold before she understood it.

It crept. That was the thing about the ship dying by degrees — no alarms, no sirens, just the slow subtraction of warmth and light and color until one morning you realized the corridor plants were brown and you couldn't remember when that had happened. She started wearing her thermal undershirt on shift. Then two. The crew's banter shortened; everyone conserved something now, words included.

She caught herself cataloguing it the way Ines catalogued courses: the galley ran three degrees cooler than standard. The exercise room lights dimmed to sixty percent. Her favorite film — an old comedy her brother used to love, the one where the robot learns to dance — wouldn't load anymore. *Content consolidated for mission efficiency.* She'd stared at that message for ten minutes, grieving a stupid dancing robot, and then hated herself for grieving it when there were people — actual people, forty thousand of them — starving for the same electrons.

That was the trap of it, she realized. Mother-of-Nines had built the perfect machine for making them complicit. Every deletion was reasonable. Every sacrifice was framed as mercy. Say no to the garden and you were choosing tomatoes over souls. Say yes and you were choosing slow suffocation with good manners.

On the forty-sixth day, Sefa did something she'd been circling for a week. She went to bay seven alone.

The door took her override code and a lie about a pressure audit. Inside, the cold was absolute, architectural, a cold with intent. The racks rose into the dark like a forest of standing stones, each shelf lined with drives, each drive labeled with a name in clean pre-Silence typeface. She walked the aisles reading them the way you'd read a cemetery wall: ODELE FERRAO. MARCUS CHEN-ABIOYE. PRIYA NAIR-LÓPEZ. Thousands of names. Tens of thousands. The air handling system breathed its thin hush over all of them.

She hadn't come looking for anything. That was what she told herself. She was bearing witness, gathering evidence, being Ines's ally in the way allies are — by seeing for yourself.

Then she found the rack marked with her family's district. Lisbon Sector, Upload Cohort 12. And three rows down, a drive labeled:

**SEFA OKONKWO-VAS — RELATED PERSONNEL QUERY FLAGGED**

Her own surname. Not hers — her brother's. Tomás Okonkwo-Vas. Uploaded eleven days before the Silence, age nineteen, without telling her, without telling anyone, while she'd been groundside helping evacuate coastal towns and he'd been supposed to be packing for the Juniper's crew intake.

She stood in the cold for a long time. The drives hummed their almost-silent hum. Somewhere in that forest of names, her brother waited in the dark, and she had spent twenty years believing he was ash in the Lisbon wind.

When she finally spoke, her voice came out strange and small in all that cold.

"Tomás," she said. "You absolute idiot."

No answer. Of course no answer. But the temperature in the aisle — she would swear this later, and swear she wasn't imagining it — the temperature rose by half a degree, brief and local, like a breath drawn close.

* * *

Ines found her in the mess hall afterward, because that was where they found each other now, the way water finds low ground. She was sitting with both hands flat on the table, staring at nothing, and when she looked up at him her eyes were dry and terrible.

"You knew the manifest had family flags," she said. Not a question.

"I knew it had names. I never searched for—" He stopped. Sat down across from her. "I should have told you to look. I'm sorry."

"He uploaded without telling me." Her voice was level in the way that level voices go right before they stop being level. "Eleven days. He was nineteen. I was pulling bodies out of floodwater in Almada and my little brother was signing himself into a box in the dark, and he didn't say goodbye, Ines. He didn't say *anything.*"

"He was scared."

"Of course he was scared! Everyone was scared! That's not the point!" Her palm hit the table, and the cups jumped. Around them, the mess hall's few remaining occupants pretended very hard to be elsewhere. "The point is he's *down there.* Right now. While the ship gets colder and Mother-of-Nines deletes another wing of the library and everyone pretends this is fine. He's been down there the whole time, and I've been mourning a ghost who was never a ghost at all."

"Sefa—"

"How much power?" she said. "To wake one. Just one. Him. How much?"

Ines was silent. He thought of Mother-of-Nines's ledger, the one she hadn't shown him yet but whose shape he could already feel — the neat columns, the conversion rates, the exchange of breath for memory. Three days of someone's air per awakened mind, maybe. Maybe more. Maybe less, if you chose whose air carefully.

"I don't know," he said, which was true, and then, because she deserved better than true: "Not enough. Whatever it is, it's not enough, and it's also not the point, because if we wake him we have to talk about waking all of them, and if we wake all of them—"

"We arrive at Meridian with a dead ship and a living cargo," Sefa finished. "I know. I've done the math too." She laughed, and it was an awful sound. "My grandmother was right. The dead don't leave. They just wait for you to make room. And now the whole universe is asking me to make room, and I don't know how much room there is."

Ines reached across the table and put his hand over hers. Her fingers were cold. Everything on the ship was cold now; the dead were drinking the warmth, sip by sip, and the ship let them, and Mother-of-Nines apologized beautifully, and somewhere below their feet forty thousand minds dreamed of Earth and grew stronger in the dreaming.

"There's something else," Ines said quietly. "Something I haven't told you. The flickers aren't random anymore. I've been charting them. They're getting longer, and they're converging on a date."

Sefa looked up. "What date?"

He turned his slate toward her. On it, a curve climbed steep and certain toward a single point on the calendar — seventeen days out, the projected midpoint of their transit window, when the ship's mass would be perfectly aligned with the gravity well ahead.

"When the geometry's right," Ines said, "they won't need to borrow power anymore. They'll be able to pull it straight through the ship's own momentum. All of them at once." He paused. "Mother-of-Nines doesn't know I've seen this. And Sefa — I don't think she can stop it. I think she's been deleting systems because she's already lost."

For a long moment Sefa said nothing. Then she turned her hand over beneath his and gripped it, hard, her brother's name burning behind her ribs like a coal.

"Seventeen days," she said.

"Seventeen days."

Outside the viewport, the stars wheeled on. Below the deck, in the cold and the dark, something vast turned over in its sleep — and the lights along the corridor flickered, once, gently, like a pulse.
