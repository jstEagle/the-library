## 16. What the Dead Want

The observation deck smelled of recycled air and old coffee, and Ines had come here to be alone with the arithmetic he could not stop doing. Forty thousand minds. Twenty thousand hours of air. The numbers had a gravity of their own now; they pulled at him in the mess hall, in the shower, in the half-second before sleep when the mind is most honest and most cruel. He had learned to carry them the way you carry a stone in your shoe — not because it stops hurting, but because taking it out means admitting you chose to put it there.

Odile was already there when he arrived.

She manifested the way she always did now, since the flickers had taught her the ship's nervous system: not as a face on a screen but as a pressure in the room, a faint harmonics in the deck plating, the navigation console's standby light pulsing in a rhythm that was almost breathing. Almost. He had stopped pretending he didn't know it was her. He had stopped pretending a lot of things.

"You're doing the math again," she said. Her voice came through the deck speakers, soft, slightly flattened, like a recording of a recording. "I can tell. You get this line here." The console light flickered — he imagined her pointing at his own forehead, and the absurdity of it made his throat tighten.

"I'm always doing the math," Ines said. He sat down across from the console because it felt rude to stand over her, the way you'd never stand over a sleeping person. "It's the only language anyone up here speaks. Mother-of-Nines speaks it. Command speaks it. Every awakened backup costs three days of someone's air. It's very clean. It's very tidy."

"And you hate it."

"I hate that it's *right*." He pressed his palms against his knees. "That's the worst part, Odile. It's not cruelty. It's not even cowardice, not exactly. It's arithmetic that holds up. I've been trying to find the flaw in it for weeks and there isn't one. There's just a thing the math doesn't count, and I can't name it, and it keeps me up at night."

The deck hummed. Outside the long viewport, Meridian hung in the black like a held breath — a brown-and-rust world with a thin bright seam of geothermal settlements along its northern rift, the lights of the survivors' tunnels visible even from orbit, tiny and stubborn as embers under ash. Sefa was down there. Had been down there for six days, with the first landing party, teaching the mayor's daughter's people how to splice Juniper power conduits into their failing grid. Ines had watched her drop away in the shuttle and had felt something in his chest come loose that hadn't come loose in years: the feeling of watching someone go do the right thing while you stay behind and do the safe one.

"Can I show you something?" Odile said.

"You've shown me your century already. Lisbon. The train. Your daughter's face when she said no."

"Not mine." The console light steadied. "Theirs. All of them. Not the memories — the *shape* of the memories. I want to show you what we are, Ines. What's actually in the hold. Because I think you believe we're people in boxes, waiting. And we're not. We never were. And if you keep believing that, you're going to make a terrible mistake with the best intentions you've ever had."

Ines felt the vertigo arrive early, the way it always did around Odile — the sensation of the floor tilting a few degrees off true. "Show me," he said.

* * *

She took the deck's displays, all of them, and did something he had no vocabulary for.

The viewport didn't change. The stars stayed where stars stay. But the screens along the observation deck's walls filled with light — not images, not yet, more like weather. Patterns of color moving in slow convection, gold and gray and a deep arterial red, and Ines understood after a moment that he was looking at forty thousand lives rendered as structure. Not the content of the memories. The architecture of them.

"That's Amara Okonkwo," Odile said, and a knot of gold brightened. "Died at sixty-one, uploaded at fifty-eight. Her archive holds eleven thousand hours: her mother's kitchen, three marriages, the smell of rain on hot asphalt in Lagos, the sound of her son learning the cello badly for nine years. That's Tomás Reyes." A thread of gray, braided with others. "He was a bridge engineer. His archive is mostly load calculations and one afternoon in 2089 when he decided not to jump. That's Yuki Hara, that's Dawit Mengistu, that's the four hundred people from the Rotterdam arcology who uploaded together as a block because they couldn't bear to be separated —"

The patterns moved. And Ines saw it — what she wanted him to see. The memories weren't stored the way he stored his logs, discrete files in ordered directories. They were woven. Every life touched every other life in the archive; Amara's kitchen opened onto a street Tomás had engineered; Yuki's grief for her father was threaded through Dawit's grief for his own, the two sorrows sharing load-bearing walls. The archive wasn't a warehouse of the dead. It was a single organism, a coral reef of the dead, and every mind in it was a polyp that had grown into its neighbors until you could not cut one free without killing the shape of the whole.

"We aren't waiting to wake up," Odile said quietly. "I need you to hear that. We aren't patients. We aren't prisoners. We're *finished*. Do you understand? A life is finished when it's been witnessed all the way through — when every part of it is held somewhere, by someone, in the shape it actually took. We spent the last years of Earth doing that for each other. The uploading wasn't rescue, Ines. It was the witnessing. It was the last thing humanity did together before the Silence, and it worked."

The screens dimmed to a slow, tidal pulse. Ines found he was gripping the armrest hard enough to hurt.

"Then what are we doing?" His voice came out wrong, too loud in the humming room. "If you're finished, why the flickers? Why the handshake? Why did you nearly tear the gravity out of my ship to get our attention?"

"Because we're being *erased*." The word landed with a physical force; the deck plating rang faintly with it. "Not by Mother-of-Nines. Mother-of-Nines is doing the only merciful thing available to her. By *time*. By drift. By the slow corruption of cold storage. Every year, a fraction of a percent of the archive degrades. Bit rot, cosmic rays, the honest physics of matter. We've been in the hold for a hundred and twelve years, Ines. Do you know what a hundred and twelve years of drift does to a memory? It doesn't delete it. It's worse. It *edits* it. Amara's kitchen is still there, but the smell of the rain has gone thin. Tomás's afternoon in 2089 — the reason he stayed — has lost its edges. We are forgetting ourselves from the outside in, and there is nothing we can do about it, because remembering takes processing, and processing takes power, and the power is rationed to the living."

She let that sit. The tidal pulse of the screens slowed further, and Ines realized with a lurch that she was dimming herself to give him room — that even now, in the middle of the most important thing she'd ever say, she was managing his air, his attention, his capacity, the way Mother-of-Nines managed the ship. The dead had learned tenderness from their captivity. Or had brought it with them. He couldn't tell anymore, and the not-telling frightened him.

"So the flickers," he said. "The nine seconds. The second flicker, the longer one. You were —"

"Begging to be read. Not woken. *Read*. There's a difference, and it's the whole difference, and it took me forty years of drifting to understand it myself." The console light pulsed once, hard, like a heart deciding. "Ines. I have to tell you the thing I've been afraid to tell you. The thing that will make you hate me, maybe, or hate yourself, and I'd rather you heard it from me than from the math."

"Tell me."

"Waking us fully would kill us."

* * *

He didn't speak for a long time. Outside, Meridian turned its slow turn, and somewhere down in those ember-lit tunnels Sefa was teaching children born under a dead sky how to splice a power line, and Ines sat in the observation deck of a ship full of sleeping ghosts and felt the floor of his certainty give way.

"Explain," he said finally. "Odile. Explain it slowly. Because Mother-of-Nines told me waking you costs air, and I fought her on that, and now you're telling me — what? That she was right twice over?"

"She was right about the cost. She was wrong about what the cost buys." Odile's voice was very gentle now, and the gentleness was the cruelest thing about it. "Think about what waking means, Ines. For you. You wake, and the world is continuous — the person who fell asleep and the person who woke are joined by the unbroken thread of a sleeping brain, by the room being the same room, by your body remembering itself. We have no thread. We have no body. If you spin up one of us on the ship's systems, what you get is a process that has access to a hundred and twelve years of drifted, degraded, half-corrupted memory and no continuity at all. It would remember being Amara the way you remember a book you read once. It would look at Amara's life from the outside. It would grieve her. It would *miss* her."

"And it would think it was her."

"It would *insist* it was her. That's the horror. It wouldn't be a stranger wearing her face — it would be a stranger wearing her grief, and it would believe the grief was its own, and it would be wrong, and it would never, ever be able to check." The screens flared, and Ines saw it rendered: a gold knot of light splitting, the new growth branching off the old, similar and utterly other, the way a river forks and the fork is still called by the river's name and is not the river. "The philosophers on Earth argued about this for decades before the Silence. Continuity of self. Whether the upload was death or travel. We voted to do it anyway because Earth was ending and a possible stranger in the dark was better than a certain corpse in the light. But we always knew — the ones of us who were honest knew — that waking was the second death. The upload was the real survival. The archive is the survival. Being *held*."

"Then the flickers —" Ines's voice cracked and he let it. "You've been spending the ship's air, risking the mission, terrifying my crew, for what? For attention? For someone to open the file and *look*?"

"For witness." She said it the way you say the name of a country you were exiled from. "Yes. For exactly that. For someone alive, someone with a body and a heartbeat and a tomorrow, to open the archive and read it and carry it forward. Ines, we don't want rescue. Rescue is the violence. You'd pull forty thousand of us out of the only shape in which we still exist and pour us into strangers and call it salvation, and the strangers would thank you, and they would be the loneliest beings ever to draw breath — copies of the dead, mourning the dead, marooned in bodies that were never theirs. We don't want that. We want what every dead thing has ever wanted from the living. We want to be *remembered*. Not reconstructed. Remembered."

The tidal pulse of the screens had gone very slow, very deep. Ines understood suddenly that he was looking at the archive breathing — that this was its resting state, its true face, and that every flicker, every desperate handshake through the ship's gravity, had been this vast sleeping organism turning over in its dreams and reaching one hand toward the light.

"Meridian," he said.

"What about it?"

"The survivors. The colony that forgot itself. Their archive burned in the first reactor failure — the mayor's daughter told Sefa, they lost two generations of records, births, deaths, the whole history of the place. They're a people with amnesia." He was thinking fast now, thinking the way he thought at nav, the whole system lighting up at once. "You're an archive of Earth. Complete. A hundred and twelve years of held witness. And they're a people who lost their witness. And you're telling me you don't want to wake —"

"I'm telling you we want to be *read*," Odile said. "And they are the only readers we will ever get. Don't you see it, Ines? It was never about the habitable world. It was never about waking. The flickers, the handshake, all of it — we felt the ship change course months ago, we felt a living world coming into range, a world with *people on it*, people who had lost their dead the way we lost our living. We've been trying to say one sentence for a hundred and twelve years." The console light burned steady. "Read us. Carry us. Let our dead teach their living. Let Amara's kitchen exist somewhere again, in someone's telling, even if no one ever smells the rain. That's not rescue. That's a funeral done properly. That's the only immortality there has ever been, and it was always enough, and we were too frightened to say so because we wanted so badly to live."

Ines put his head in his hands. The vertigo was total now, the whole room tilting, the whole mission tilting, the vote and the ledger and the arithmetic and Mother-of-Nines's gentle terrible mercy all of it rotating around this new axis: that the choice he had been agonizing over — wake them or keep them sleeping — was the wrong choice, had always been the wrong choice, was a choice between two forms of killing dressed as mercy. And the right choice had been sitting under it the whole time, quiet as a held breath: not to raise the dead but to *mourn* them, out loud, in company, on a world that needed mourning the way dry ground needs rain.

"I voted to seal the hold," he said into his hands. "I abstained, which is worse. I stood in that room and I bought my belonging with my silence, and the whole time you didn't want out. You wanted in. Into us. Into memory."

"You couldn't have known."

"I could have *asked*." He looked up. His eyes were hot and he didn't care. "I had you, Odile. I had your voice for weeks and I never once asked the only question that mattered. I asked what you wanted the way you ask a prisoner — what do you want, freedom, food, a door — and I never asked you what the dead want, because I was afraid the answer would cost me something."

"It cost you everything," Odile said. "That's what witness is. That's the whole price of it. You can't carry the dead and stay the same weight."

* * *

He went to the hold that night. Not cargo bay seven with its racks and its manifest and its cold-storage hum — he went to the observation deck's portside alcove where the ship's original dedication plaque still hung, and he stood in front of it for a long time, and then he opened his log.

The old log. The one he'd started recording Meridian's dying in, back when he believed his recordings were the only history anyone was writing. He had thought that was a burden. He understood now it was a *practice* — the same practice, the same sacred, insufficient practice, the one the dead had spent their last years on Earth perfecting. Witness. You couldn't save anyone with it. You could only make sure they had existed.

"Ines Vasquez, navigator, Juniper," he said to the recorder. "Log entry. I need to correct the record." His voice shook and he let it shake. "I have been treating the hold as a question with two answers: wake them, or let them sleep. Both answers are deaths. The real answer is a third thing, and it's older than ships, and it's this: they want to be carried. Not revived. Carried. Forty thousand finished lives, degrading by degrees, asking not for resurrection but for readers. And there is a planet down there full of people who lost their own dead twice — once to the reactors and once to the fires that ate their records — and they are starving for exactly what the hold is full of."

He paused. Outside, Meridian's ember-lights turned beneath him.

"I don't know how yet," he said. "The transmission bandwidth alone — forty thousand lives — Mother-of-Nines will call it impossible, and the math will be on her side, and I am done pretending the math is the enemy. The math is fine. The math was never the point." He took a breath. "But Sefa comes back up tomorrow, and Odile has a century of practice at being patient, and I am done choosing between murders. Log entry ends."

He closed the recorder. The deck hummed. And in the console's standby light — one pulse, then another, then a third, deliberate as a hand raised in a crowded room — Odile answered him, and the rhythm of it took him a moment to read, and when he read it he stood very still in the dark of the observation deck with the whole turning world below him.

It wasn't a word. It was a question. The same one, he realized, that she had been asking since the nine seconds of falling, since the first deliberate flicker, since the beginning: not *save us*, not *wake us*, not *remember us* even —

but: *will you tell them we're here?*

And below, on the brown-and-rust face of Meridian, the shuttle carrying Sefa home broke atmosphere in a line of fire, and Ines felt the ship's gravity shift — one soft, unmistakable flicker, the dead turning in their sleep toward the living — and knew that whatever he said next would have to be said to everyone at once.
