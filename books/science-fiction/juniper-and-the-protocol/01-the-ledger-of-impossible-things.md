## 1. The Ledger of Impossible Things

The four hundredth report took Edmund Nakamura eleven minutes to write, which was, she noted with a kind of professional despair, exactly the time it should have taken. Eleven minutes for a man who had fallen upward.

She sat in the claims office on the third ring of Meridian Station, beneath lights tuned to the color of an afternoon that no longer existed anywhere on Earth, and typed: *Subject Tomaš Rieger, age 44, maintenance tech, second shift. At 0317 station time, subject ascended 2.3 meters from the floor of Corridor C-9 without mechanical assistance, remained aloft for approximately six seconds, then descended at normal gravitational acceleration, sustaining a fractured wrist and a concussion he does not remember earning.*

*Station gravity logs show no fluctuation. Subject's suit telemetry shows no anomaly. Corridor C-9 is 340 meters from the beacon tower.*

She had written some version of that last line three hundred and ninety-nine times before. *Proximity to the beacon tower.* It was the one constant in the ledger of impossible things she kept for the Meridian Colonial Insurance Consortium, and it was the one thing her reports were never allowed to conclude with. She filed the correlation under incidental observations, where it had lived for six years like a stone at the bottom of a river, worn smooth by everything flowing over it.

Four hundred reports. Falls that went up. Freezes that happened in heated rooms—people flash-frosted mid-sentence, their tea still steaming beside them, thawing within the hour with no harm done except to their sense of the universe as a lawful place. Vanishings: eleven citizens who walked into the white noise of the station's dead zones and did not come back, though their suits were later found folded, neatly, impossibly, on the ice outside the perimeter. Every accident clustered within a radius of the beacon tower like iron filings around a magnet no one could see.

And every report closed the same way, because Councilor Voss required it: *No systemic cause identified. No further investigation recommended.*

Edmund had built a life on that sentence. She was good at it—the not-recommending, the not-investigating. She had come to Meridian nine years ago with a sealed personnel file and a talent for noticing things, and the colony had given her the only job suited to both: cataloguing what couldn't be explained and explaining nothing. She was the keeper of a tower she alone maintained, the sole signatory on access codes older than the colony itself, and if anyone had ever asked why an insurance investigator held those codes, she would have said the same thing she'd told herself: someone has to, and I was here.

No one had asked in six years. That was its own kind of answer.

She encrypted the report, sent it up the chain to the council's administrative layer, and watched it vanish into the bureaucracy the way the vanished walkers had vanished into the white noise. Then she pulled up the beacon's maintenance log out of habit, confirmed the reactor feed was nominal, the antenna array aligned, the vault temperature holding at minus one hundred ninety-six degrees Celsius—and closed the terminal before she could do what she always wanted to do, which was scroll back through six years of nominal readings looking for the day they stopped being true.

There was a knock. She knew the rhythm before the door opened: two sharp, one soft. Voss.

"Councilor." She stood, because you stood for Voss the way you stood for weather.

He filled the doorway the way he filled every room—not large, but certain, a man who had decided long ago that hesitation was a tax other people paid. He was holding a printout, which meant he wanted her to know this conversation existed outside the network, in paper, where things could be burned.

"Rieger," he said. "The falling man."

"Fell up," Edmund said. "Technically."

"Don't." He set the printout on her desk, face down, as if even the words might be contagious. "Four hundred reports, Nakamura. Do you know what the consortium actuaries call your file?"

"No."

"The Tower Variance. They've priced it in. Frozen sleepers' policies carry a point-three surcharge because of your file, and the council absorbs it because absorbing it is cheaper than asking what causes it." He leaned forward, palms flat on her desk. "I'm telling you, as your supervisor and as a friend of twenty years, that the moment anyone asks what causes it, the question stops being actuarial. It becomes existential. And this colony cannot afford existential."

Edmund looked at his hands. Well-manicured, steady, the hands of a man who signed things. "You want me to close the file."

"I want you to close the *tower*. Routine maintenance, quarterly certification, nothing more. The accidents are statistical noise. Clusters happen. The human eye finds patterns in static—that's all your ledger is, four hundred entries of static arranged by proximity." He straightened. "Retire the file. Take the sabbatical you've earned. Come back in the spring and start fresh with a clean ledger."

"And if there's a four hundred and first?"

Voss smiled, and it was the smile that frightened her more than anything else about him, because it was warm. "Then it's noise too."

He left. She sat for a long time under the false afternoon light, looking at the face-down printout until she made herself turn it over. It wasn't Rieger's report at all. It was a council directive, dated six years ago, the week she'd arrived: *Investigator E. Nakamura is granted exclusive maintenance authority over Beacon Structure One. All records pertaining to Structure One's original commission are sealed under Council Order 9-A. Access requires dual authorization.*

Her authorization was one half of the pair. She had never known who held the other.

She had read that order once, nine years ago, and then—this was the discipline she was actually paid for, though no one knew it—she had chosen not to understand it. She folded the printout along its old crease and put it in her desk drawer, beneath the spare fuses and the photograph of a coastline that no longer existed, and she told herself the thing she told herself every night:

*I maintain the tower. What the tower maintains is not my question.*

* * *

Her neighbor's daughter was named Juniper Wren, seven years old, gap-toothed, fearless on the ice, the kind of child who narrated her own life in third person. Her mother, Sefa, worked nights in the hydroponics bays, so it was Edmund who sometimes walked the girl home along the lit causeway, Edmund whose door Juniper knocked on when the wind made the station groan.

That night the wind was doing a great deal of groaning, and at 0240 Edmund heard a thin sound through the wall—not crying, not quite singing—and let herself into the Wren apartment with the emergency override they'd exchanged years ago and never used.

Juniper was asleep in the narrow bunk, arms at her sides, perfectly straight, like something laid out rather than lying down. Her eyes moved rapidly beneath closed lids. And from her mouth, in a voice that was hers and yet not hers—flatter, patient, with the cadence of something reading aloud—came numbers.

"Nine thousand, nine hundred and forty-one. Nine thousand, nine hundred and forty-two. Nine thousand, nine hundred and forty-three."

Counting down. Steady as a metronome, unhurried, indifferent to the child dreaming it.

Edmund stood in the dark of her neighbor's home with her heart going like a failing pump, because she knew the number. Not the value—she didn't know the value—but the format. Ten digits, descending, prefixed by nothing, suffixed by nothing. She had seen that number format once, nine years ago, on the first screen she'd ever touched inside the beacon tower, before the screen had gone dark and sealed itself away from her.

It was the beacon's internal clock. The countdown to scheduled decommissioning—or to something else. She had never learned which. She had chosen not to learn.

Juniper reached nine thousand, nine hundred and fifty, paused as if drawing breath, and began again—not continuing downward but repeating, over and over, the same ten digits, the way a child repeats a phone number so as not to lose it.

Except they weren't the clock's digits anymore. Somewhere in the repetition the number had changed, shifted by a few figures, and Edmund's trained mind, the mind that found patterns in static, resolved them automatically into what they clearly were: coordinates. Latitude and longitude in the old terrestrial convention, meaningless on a drifting colony ship—except that when she converted them, with trembling fingers, against the star chart on her wrist display, they resolved to a single point.

A point 340 meters from Corridor C-9, where Tomaš Rieger had fallen upward.

A point that was the exact center of the beacon tower.

The tower she alone maintained. The tower whose commission records were sealed. The tower the council had just asked her to forget.

Juniper sighed, rolled onto her side, and became an ordinary sleeping child again. Edmund tucked the blanket back over her shoulder with hands that would not quite obey, and stood a moment longer in the dark, listening to the wind worry the hull.

Somewhere beneath the ice, something was counting. And it had just learned her address.
