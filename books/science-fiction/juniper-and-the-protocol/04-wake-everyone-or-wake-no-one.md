## 4. Wake Everyone or Wake No One

The override key was warm in Edmund's palm, the way a stone is warm after a day in the sun—warm with borrowed heat, heat that belonged to someone else. On the console before her, two lists glowed in the amber light of the beacon's interface. The left list: ten thousand names, the verified lineage, the genetically screened, the ones her directive had sworn her to save. The right list: ten thousand more, the unvetted, the random, the frozen dead who had boarded the vaults on luck and lottery and love.

Behind her, the tower door groaned against Voss's breaching charge. Sparks rained down the stairwell like orange snow.

"Edmund." Voss's voice came through the comm panel, calm as an audit. "You still have time to do your duty. You've done it for eleven years without ever knowing what it was. Do it once more."

"You knew," Edmund said. She kept her eyes on the console. "Eleven years of impossible accidents, and you knew what they were."

"I knew what they cost." A pause. "The colony cannot carry twenty thousand sleepers. The reactor fails either way if we try. Half is mercy. Half is arithmetic."

"No," Edmund said. "Half is cowardice wearing arithmetic's coat."

She thought of Anouk Reyes—Ilsa's daughter, eight years old, who had come to her door three nights ago with frost on her eyelashes and the countdown still moving behind her eyes. *Four hundred and twelve,* the girl had said. *It's going faster now, Aunt Edmund. It knows you're deciding.*

The children hadn't been dreaming a warning. They'd been dreaming a lesson.

She pulled up the accident ledger—all four hundred reports, her own careful hand, her own willful blindness rendered in tidy columns. The man who fell forty meters into the crevasse field and stood up unbroken because the ice had softened beneath him at the precise moment of impact. The woman locked out overnight in a whiteout that should have killed her in ninety minutes, who walked home at dawn through a corridor of air the storm had somehow failed to touch. The child who vanished for six days inside the beacon's shadow and returned remembering nothing except a voice counting down, patient as tide.

Every survivor. Every single one, clustered within the beacon's radius, each statistically impossible, each filed by her and closed by Voss.

"It was never testing us," she said aloud, to Voss, to the console, to whoever had built this thing and buried their name. "It was teaching us. Every accident was a lesson in carrying weight we shouldn't be able to carry."

The door gave way below. Boots on the stairs.

Edmund opened the Protocol's source. The designer's logic bloomed across her screen—not code, exactly, but something older, something that read like a letter written in mathematics. And there, threaded through the triage routine, she found what the erased commission records had hidden: a second function, dormant, waiting for an operator willing to break both lists.

*If the colony can hold the dream together,* the comment read, in the designer's plain, human hand, *the colony can hold the load. Love distributes. Lineage hoards. Choose accordingly.*

She deleted the left list. She deleted the right list. Then she burned her own ledger into the rewrite—four hundred impossible accidents, four hundred survivors, four hundred proofs—feeding them to the Protocol the way you feed a fire.

The console asked its only question: **CONFIRM FULL REVIVAL AT HALF-POWER?**

"Yes," Edmund said.

Boots filled the doorway. Voss stepped through, flanked by security, her face composed into something almost gentle. "Edmund. Step away from the console."

"I'm sorry," Edmund said, and meant it, and pressed her hand flat against the confirm anyway.

* * *

The reactor did not fail. It *bent*.

Across Meridian, lights browned and steadied and browned again, and in the cryovaults beneath the ice, twenty thousand hearts were asked to start with power enough for ten thousand. For nine minutes, the whole colony held its breath without knowing why—the baker pulling loaves in a cold oven, the welders working by glowlight, the children sitting up in their beds in perfect unison, wide awake, listening.

Because the dream had changed. The countdown stopped.

And in its place, every sleeping child heard the same thing: not a voice anymore, but a chord. Twenty thousand heartbeats learning to share one rhythm, the way a choir learns to breathe together. The beacon wasn't choosing. It was conducting.

In the tower, the load indicators climbed past red and kept climbing, and Edmund watched them with Voss's hand gripping her shoulder, and she did not look away.

"They'll die," Voss whispered. "All of them. Because of you."

"Then I'll have killed them doing the right thing," Edmund said, "instead of half of them doing yours."

At 103 percent, the reactor screamed—a sound through the floor, through the ice, through the soles of everyone on Meridian. At 104, the children began to sing. Not words. Just the chord, hummed in eighty small throats across the colony, and where the hum went, the load *followed*, dipping, settling, finding a frequency that should not have existed.

At dawn, the first cryopod opened in the vault, and a woman who had been frozen for sixty years breathed Meridian's cold air and asked, in a language three generations out of date, whether she had missed anything important.

* * *

They came for Edmund at noon, but not the way she expected.

Voss had already moved to arrest her—conspiracy, sabotage, the murder-by-negligence of ten thousand names—but the arrests collapsed before they began, because Edmund had burned the only copy of the accident ledger into the Protocol's public record. Every colonist could now read what she had known: that the council had been closing files on miracles for a decade, that Councilor Voss had signed off on 312 of them, that the "verified lineage" list included the name of every council member's family and excluded every archivist's—including Ilsa Reyes, who walked out of holding that afternoon while Voss was still demanding a tribunal.

Ilsa found Edmund at the tower base, looking up at the structure she had tended in secret for eleven years, visible now, named now, hers.

"You knew they'd expose you," Ilsa said.

"I hoped," Edmund said. "There's a difference. Hope requires evidence." She smiled, tired. "I had four hundred data points."

Below them, the vault doors were opening in waves. The newly woken stumbled out into the white daylight, blinking, carried, weeping, asking questions no one could answer yet. And among the colonists who had lived above the ice all their lives, the children moved through the crowd like small priests, touching the wrists of the revived, whispering numbers—not a countdown anymore, but a count *up*. Names being counted back into the world.

Anouk reached Edmund last. She took her hand, and around them, dozens of other children turned, all at once, like a field of flowers finding the sun.

"She says thank you," Anouk said. "The voice under the ice. She says the choice was never between the lists." The girl's eyes were clear and ancient and eight years old. "She says it was always between hiding and holding. And she says—" Anouk tilted her head, listening to something Edmund would spend the rest of her life trying to hear. "She says the reactor isn't failing anymore. But something else is coming. Something the beacon has been counting toward since before any of us were born."

The children spoke together then, one voice from eighty mouths, soft as snowfall:

*"Ninety-one days."*

Edmund looked at the tower. The tower, for the first time in its long erased life, seemed to look back.
