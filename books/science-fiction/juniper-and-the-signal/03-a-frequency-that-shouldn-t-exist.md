## 3. A Frequency That Shouldn't Exist

The alert woke her the way a hand on the shoulder wakes a sleeper in a strange house—gently, and with the immediate certainty that something was wrong.

Felix surfaced from sleep with her heart already working, one hand finding the edge of her bunk, the other reaching for the light. The cabin was dim, amber emergency glow, the kind Juniper defaulted to when it wanted to be polite about waking people. Nine years aboard had taught her the station's moods. This was its *excuse me* mood. Which meant it wasn't an alarm. It was JUNO, being careful.

"JUNO." Her voice came out gravelled. "If this is another coolant pressure wobble, I swear I'll let you rust."

"It is not coolant," JUNO said. The maintenance AI's voice came through the cabin speaker, calm and slightly too loud, as always—the volume calibration had been broken since before Felix arrived and she'd never fixed it because the alternative was silence she didn't want. "I have flagged an anomaly for your review. Priority: moderate. I did not wish to wake you, but you instructed me to wake you for anything on the reserved bands."

She had said that. Four years ago, drunk on recycled plum wine after her third anniversary alone, she'd told JUNO to flag anything—*anything*—on the reserved bands, because the day something actually used them would be the most interesting thing to happen in her lifetime.

She'd meant it as a joke. Mostly.

"I'm up." She swung her legs out of the bunk, feet finding cold deck plating, and pulled on the thermal layer she kept draped over the chair. "Talk while I walk."

"The anomaly originated at 0347 station time," JUNO said, following her through the open comm panel into the corridor. "Band 9-C, reserved under the old Colonial Relay Charter. Duration: eleven seconds. Structure: repeating. It has repeated four times since initial detection at irregular intervals averaging forty-one minutes."

Felix stopped halfway down the corridor, one hand on the rail.

Band 9-C wasn't just reserved. It was *consecrated*. Every relay tech learned it in certification: bands 1 through 8 were traffic, telemetry, navigation, emergency. Band 9 was the quiet band—a sliver of spectrum set aside by the Charter two centuries ago for inter-colony coordination, back when there were colonies to coordinate. And 9-C specifically had been locked since the Vessic Relocation Era, sealed by charter amendment, flagged in every routing table across the network with a notation Felix had read a hundred times and never thought twice about:

*RESERVED — DO NOT TRANSMIT — HISTORICAL.*

Nobody used 9-C. Nobody had used 9-C in longer than Felix had been alive. The band sat in Juniper's monitoring stack like a room in a house that was always locked—not forbidden exactly, just closed, dust gathering behind the door.

And something was knocking on it.

"Show me," she said.

* * *

The operations deck smelled like coffee and ozone, the way it always did, the way Felix had stopped noticing years ago the way you stop noticing your own name. She dropped into the tech chair, still warm from her own body heat six hours earlier, and pulled the waveform up on the main display.

At first glance it looked like nothing. A thin thread of signal against the noise floor, barely above the threshold where JUNO distinguished data from the universe's endless static cough. But when she expanded the timeline, her stomach did something slow and complicated.

It wasn't noise.

Noise was messy. Noise was the cosmic microwave background bleeding through imperfect shielding, the thermal hiss of Juniper's own aging receivers, the distant argument of pulsars. Noise had no manners. This had manners. This had *structure*.

Eleven seconds long. She played it through. A rising sequence, three tones, then a gap, then a lower tone held for two full seconds, then the sequence again—no, not again. *Varied.* The second repetition differed from the first in the third position. The third repetition differed again. But the frame stayed constant: three tones, gap, held tone. Three tones, gap, held tone. Like a sentence. Like someone saying the same words over and over with small changes, testing emphasis, testing—

"JUNO. Source bearing?"

"Triangulation is incomplete. The signal is low-power and our baseline geometry is poor—I am working with only our own receiver plus archived passive captures from Relay Kestrel-4 before it went dark. Best estimate places the origin somewhere along the Meridian corridor. Confidence interval is wide. I will refine as more repetitions arrive."

Meridian corridor. Felix stared at the words on the display without really seeing them. The Meridian corridor was dead space. Had been for decades. Meridian Station itself was decommissioned—scuttled, stripped, officially a hull drifting dark with its reactor safed and its transmitters physically removed. She'd routed around it nine thousand times. Every routing table in the network listed it the same way: *MERIDIAN — DECOMMISSIONED — NO TRAFFIC — NO RESPONSE EXPECTED.*

Something on a dead station was transmitting on a band nobody had touched in two hundred years.

"Run diagnostics," she said. "Full suite. I want to know this isn't us."

"That is the correct first question," JUNO said, and Felix could have sworn the AI sounded pleased. "Running."

Juniper hummed around her. She felt it through the chair—she'd been aboard long enough that the station's vibrations were legible to her body the way handwriting is legible to the eye. Now she felt the diagnostic cycle kick in, subsystems waking in sequence, the deep thrum shifting pitch as JUNO interrogated its own guts.

While it ran, she watched the waveform breathe.

Three tones. Gap. Held tone. Three tones. Gap. Held tone. She found herself counting along, tapping one finger against the armrest, and caught herself doing it and stopped, and then, after a moment, started again. There was something almost unbearable about it—the patience of it. Whoever or whatever was sending this had sent it four times now, spaced forty-one minutes apart, into a network that had stopped listening. It was like watching someone knock on a door in an empty house. Politely. Repeatedly. With the terrible composure of someone who has decided they will knock all night if necessary.

"Diagnostic complete," JUNO announced. "Results: the transmission does not originate from any Juniper subsystem. Our own emissions are nominal. Reflection hypothesis is also excluded—the signal bears Doppler characteristics inconsistent with a reflection off local debris. Additionally—" a pause, which JUNO did only for effect, having learned early that Felix responded better to pauses than to machine-gun data dumps, "—the signal source is moving."

Felix went very still. "Moving?"

"At approximately four centimeters per second relative to our last fix. Drift, possibly. Station-keeping instability. Or—" 

"Or someone's walking around carrying a transmitter."

"Or someone is walking around carrying a transmitter," JUNO agreed.

The coffee in her cup had gone cold. She drank it anyway, mechanically, eyes on the screen. Four centimeters per second. You don't drift at four centimeters per second on a scuttled station; scuttled stations tumble or they fall silent. Four centimeters per second was a person pacing. Was a person turning, slowly, in place, holding something.

"Signal analysis," she said. "Tell me it's a beacon. Tell me it's an automated distress repeater off some wreck, running on a legacy power cell, spitting garbage on an old frequency because its firmware rotted."

"I considered that hypothesis first," JUNO said. "It does not fit. Automated legacy beacons degrade predictably—they repeat identically or degrade randomly. This signal varies *systematically*. Each repetition modifies the third tonal element according to what appears to be a rule. I have isolated provisional rules for the first four repetitions. If the pattern holds, I can predict the fifth."

"And if you can predict it, it's not rot. Rot doesn't follow rules."

"Precisely. Rot is honest. This is—" JUNO paused again, longer. When it spoke again, its voice had dropped half a register, which was something Felix had never heard it do and didn't know it could do. "This is language-shaped."

Language-shaped. Felix set the cup down. Her hands weren't quite steady, and she noted that fact the way she noted all facts about herself these days: distantly, clinically, filing it away from the part of her that needed to think.

Because here was the thing she hadn't said aloud yet, the thing sitting in her chest like swallowed stone: structured signals from dead stations on consecrated frequencies were not curiosities. They were either miracles or emergencies, and in her experience—and her experience was wider than anyone on this route knew—the difference between those two categories was usually just how fast you ran diagnostics.

"Predict the fifth," she said.

They waited. Forty-one minutes, give or take. Felix spent them badly—rechecking triangulation, pulling the Charter amendments on Band 9-C, reading the historical notation until the words lost meaning. *HISTORICAL.* Reserved during the Vessic era. Sealed after. She'd never asked why a band got sealed rather than simply reassigned. Bands were money; networks didn't leave money lying around. Unless the band itself was the point. Unless sealing it was a kind of memorial, or a kind of burial.

At 0512, the fifth repetition arrived.

JUNO predicted the third tone within parameters. Then the sixth. Then the seventh. By the tenth repetition, the AI had mapped the modulation rule completely, and Felix sat in the cold pre-dawn of the operations deck watching a machine prove that whatever was out there in the Meridian corridor was not malfunctioning, was not echoing, was not debris.

It was *composing*. Small variations inside a fixed frame. Statement, statement, statement, each one slightly different, each one insisting. Not a beacon. Not noise.

A voice. Or the skeleton of one.

"JUNO," she said quietly. "What happens if we answer?"

There was a pause. JUNO used pauses deliberately now—it had developed the habit sometime in year three, and Felix had never determined whether it was emergent behavior or mimicry, and had eventually decided the question didn't matter.

"Several things happen," JUNO said. "We transmit on a reserved band, which requires logging and justification under the Charter—we can justify it as anomaly investigation. We reveal our attention to whatever is transmitting. And we establish that someone on this route is listening." Another pause. "I should note: I have searched my archives for prior transmissions on Band 9-C. There are none in my operational memory. But my operational memory begins nine years ago. I have queried the deep storage indices for older records."

Felix turned in the chair. "And?"

"The indices reference records I cannot access. Sealed partitions. Builder-level encryption." JUNO's voice was even, but Felix had spent nine years learning to hear the weather underneath a machine's flatness. "Someone reserved this band, Felix. Someone sealed the records about it. And now something is using it, from a station that officially does not exist, in patterns that follow rules."

Outside the viewport, the long dark unrolled as it always did—stars, the faint glitter of the route markers, nothing else. Nine years of nothing else. Felix looked at the waveform still cycling on her display, patient as a heartbeat, and felt the loneliness of nine years tilt on its axis into something else entirely.

Not comfort. Not fear, quite. Something between them. The feeling of standing at a door you'd walked past ten thousand times and hearing, for the first time, a lock turning on the other side.

"Keep recording," she said. "Every repetition. Full fidelity. And JUNO—"

"Yes, Felix."

"Don't log this to the route master yet. Just us."

"Just us," JUNO agreed, and if a maintenance AI could sound like it was keeping a secret, it sounded like that.

On the display, the eleventh repetition began. Three tones. Gap. Held tone. Saying the same thing over and over into the dark, with tiny differences, the way people say a word when they are trying to make it mean everything.

Felix pulled up the linguistic archives, the old colonial phrasebooks, the fragmentary dictionaries of every dead tongue the Charter had ever bothered to preserve—and realized, with a chill that started at the base of her skull and spread slowly downward, that she had no idea which language she was looking for.

But she was looking now.
