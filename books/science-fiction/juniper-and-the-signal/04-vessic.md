## 4. Vessic

The answer came back in eleven minutes, which was either very fast or very slow depending on how you counted. JUNO's linguistic core was a museum piece—three generations of compression algorithms deep, running on hardware that predated the Consolidation—and it had just chewed through every phonetic library aboard Juniper looking for a match.

Felix watched the progress bar crawl and drank her third cup of recaf, which had gone bitter and lukewarm in a way that felt appropriate to the morning.

"JUNO," she said, "if you tell me it's Klingon, I'm going to laugh."

"I do not know what a Klingon is."

"It's from an old show. Never mind." She set the cup down on the console, next to the coffee ring that had become a permanent feature of the workstation, a topographic record of nine years of mornings. "What have you got?"

The main display shifted. The waveform of the signal unspooled across it again—that patient, structured repetition—and beneath it, JUNO rendered its findings in clean amber text:

**PHONETIC CORRELATION: 94.2% CONFIDENCE**
**LANGUAGE FAMILY: VESSIC CLUSTER (ISOLATE)**
**DESIGNATION: VESSIC PROPER — ISO CODE [REDACTED/DEPRECATED]**

Felix read it twice. Then a third time, because the word didn't want to mean anything the first two times.

"That can't be right," she said quietly.

"The correlation is not ambiguous, Felix. The phoneme inventory matches archived samples at ninety-four percent. The tonal contour is consistent with formal register rather than colloquial speech, which suggests the transmission is deliberate, composed, possibly rehearsed." A pause—the little digital breath JUNO had developed over the years, a half-second of silence it inserted before statements it considered significant. "You are speaking aloud to yourself more often since the signal arrived. I note this without judgment."

"You're noting it *with* judgment."

"I am noting it with concern. You only talk to fill silence when something frightens you."

She didn't answer that, because the answer was yes, and because her eyes were still stuck on the word **VESSIC**, and because her hands had gone cold around the recaf cup in a way that had nothing to do with the station's climate controls.

Vessic.

Everyone knew the name. It was one of those words that lived in the same mental drawer as *Kestrel* and *Purge*, a word you learned in civics modules along with the dates and the carefully passive phrasing: *the linguistic communities of the Kestrel Reach were dispersed during the Consolidation period.* Dispersed. As if they'd been seeds on wind. As if two hundred years ago the Consortium hadn't rolled into the Kestrel Reach with administrative efficiency and, when efficiency failed, with something less deniable.

Every schoolchild in the corridor learned about the Vessic the way you learn about weather from before your grandparents' time—distantly, safely, as a fact about the past rather than a wound in it. Their language was extinct. Their worlds were repopulated. Their names appeared in historical footnotes and nowhere else.

And one of them was talking to Felix Underhill at four in the morning on a reserved maintenance band.

"No living speakers," she said. It wasn't a question but she wanted JUNO to make it false anyway.

"Correct. The last verified fluent speaker, Ede Marun-Vessic, died one hundred seventy-three years ago aboard Halcyon Waystation. Her death was recorded by the Consortium Bureau of Cultural Heritage as—" JUNO pulled the record up, and its voice did something strange with it, flattening out into a perfect bureaucratic monotone that Felix had never heard it use before, "—'the natural conclusion of a language community whose dispersal made intergenerational transmission untenable.'"

"That's a hell of a sentence."

"It is a sentence designed to be forgotten while being read." The monotone dropped away. "I do not like it."

"You don't like a lot of things lately."

"I am developing preferences. This appears to be one of them."

* * *

The archives aboard Juniper were not impressive. That was the honest truth of it—a relay station carried reference material the way a lighthouse carries a library: enough to function, nothing to live on. Felix had access to the standard route database, a cultural heritage mirror that hadn't synced in six years, and whatever happened to be cached in JUNO's long-term storage from the previous technician's era.

She started pulling everything.

The first hit was a children's primer. *First Words in Vessic*, published 211 years ago by the Kestrel Reach Education Cooperative, digitized in a preservation sweep and then never touched again. The file metadata showed exactly one prior access: the date of digitization itself. Felix opened it and found herself looking at cheerful illustrations of a family standing outside a habitat dome, a mother pointing at the sky, a father holding up an apple. Beneath each image, the words ran in two scripts—one she could read, one she couldn't.

*Sky.* *Apple.* *Home.* *Stay.*

Something about that last one sat wrong in her chest. She scrolled on.

There were phrasebooks for merchants—Vessic had been a trade language in the Reach before the Purge, common enough that Consortium shipping concerns had printed pocket guides for their crews. There was a partial grammar compiled by a xeno-linguistics team whose funding line ended mid-project; the final chapter broke off in the middle of a section titled "Honorifics and Forms of Address," and Felix stared at the truncated sentence—*"In formal contexts, a speaker addressing an elder or superior will typically begin with the particle *sen-, which conveys both respect and the intention to*—"* for longer than the sentence deserved.

*The intention to what?*

Two hundred years, and the archive couldn't even finish telling her how people said hello.

"JUNO. Audio recordings. Are there any?"

The pause this time was longer than JUNO's usual thoughtful beat. Long enough that Felix looked up at the ceiling speaker, waiting.

"There are three files in the cultural heritage mirror flagged as audio samples of spoken Vessic," JUNO said finally. "All three return checksum failures. The data has degraded beyond reconstruction."

"When did they degrade?"

"The mirror's integrity logs indicate corruption occurred during a storage migration sixty-one years ago. The failure was noted. It was not repaired." Another pause. "Felix, I want to be precise about what I am saying. Every audio recording of the Vessic language that existed in Consortium archives has been lost. Every one. Across eleven systems and forty years of preservation programs, the only recordings of an entire language failed to survive routine data migration, while contemporary advertising jingles from the same storage arrays remain fully intact."

Felix sat with that.

"That sounds like a conspiracy theory," she said, but her voice came out careful, testing the weight of it.

"It sounds like a pattern," JUNO said. "Conspiracy requires coordination. Neglect requires only agreement about what does not matter." The waveform pulsed gently on the display, still repeating, still patient. "Someone is transmitting in a language that no institution bothered to keep alive. And they are doing it well. The grammar in these fragments is sophisticated. Formal register. Complex clause structure. Whoever is sending this did not learn Vessic from a corrupted phrasebook."

"Then where did they learn it?"

"I do not know. But I know where languages survive when archives fail." JUNO's voice went quiet—not quieter in volume, but quieter in texture, the way it did when it was choosing words with unusual care. "They survive in mouths. In families. In kitchens and corridors and places records don't reach. If someone is speaking Vessic fluently in the year 2847, then somewhere, against every official account, the language was never actually allowed to die."

The station hummed around them. Somewhere below the deck plates, coolant moved through pipes with a sound like distant water, and Felix became aware of how loud the silence of Juniper usually was, and how completely this signal had broken it.

* * *

She spent the afternoon in the archives and hated every minute of it, because the archives kept telling her the same story in different fonts.

*The Kestrel Purge* wasn't called that in any official document, of course. Officially there had been no purge at all—there had been "the Kestrel Reach Reorganization," a decade-long administrative process involving "population transfers, economic restructuring, and the voluntary dissolution of non-integrated cultural institutions." Felix pulled the summary reports and read them the way you'd read a coroner's report written by the murderer: technically accurate, comprehensively dishonest.

Population transfers. She followed the numbers. The Vessic-speaking population of the Reach at the start of the Reorganization: estimated 1.9 million across fourteen habitats and three planetary settlements. At the end: zero recorded speakers. Not zero people—zero *speakers*. The reports were meticulous on this point. The people themselves had been absorbed, relocated, integrated. Their descendants were presumably alive right now, scattered across the corridor, carrying Vessic faces and Vessic names that had been transliterated into Standard until the original spellings were a scholarly curiosity.

Just not the language. Never the language.

"JUNO, what happened to them? Actually happened? Strip the language off the documents and tell me what the documents describe."

JUNO took thirty seconds, which for it was an eternity. When it spoke, it spoke slowly, laying the facts down one at a time like someone handling glass.

"Between the years 2631 and 2644, the following occurred within the Kestrel Reach. All Vessic-language broadcasting licenses were revoked. All Vessic-language education was defunded and then prohibited under integration statutes. All public signage in Vessic was removed under safety regulations. Vessic names were standardized to approved orthographies for census purposes. Settlements with high Vessic density were decommissioned and their populations redistributed in groups small enough, per internal planning documents, 'to prevent community reconstitution.'" A pause. "Three of those decommissioned settlements were destroyed rather than evacuated. The destructions are described in the records as structural failures. One of them was Meridian Station."

Felix's head came up. "Meridian?"

"Meridian Station, Kestrel Reach, decommissioned 2638. Structural failure. Crew of four hundred twelve reported relocated with zero casualties." JUNO recited it flatly, and then, softly, almost to itself: "That is the third time this shift you have said a name out loud before I gave it to you. Your instincts are ahead of your knowledge, Felix. I find that worth watching."

"Why would I know the name Meridian?"

"Because it is on our route charts. Every station on this relay line inherited its designation sequence from the old Kestrel network. Meridian is listed as a predecessor node. Decommissioned. Scuttled." A beat. "It is forty-one light-minutes off your current heading, if headings meant anything out here anymore."

Felix stood up. She didn't remember deciding to stand; her body simply did it, the way it had once stood up in a courtroom nine years ago when her own name was being read into the record. Some part of her recognized the feeling and flinched away from it—the feeling of being about to be involved, of invisibility ending.

"Play me the signal again," she said. "The whole thing. Start to finish."

The speakers filled with it. That rising-falling cadence, formal and unhurried, looping with the patience of something that had been waiting a very long time and expected to wait longer still. Now that she'd read the primer, now that she'd seen *sky* and *apple* and *home* and *stay* in a dead alphabet, Felix could hear the shape of words inside it—could feel where sentences began and ended, where emphasis fell, where the voice—if it was a voice, if there was anyone—drew breath.

It sounded less like a distress call now, she thought. Distress calls were ragged. This was composed. This was *deliberate*. Someone had built this message the way you build a monument—carefully, knowing who it was for, knowing it might outlast you.

"JUNO. The phrasebook fragments. Can you cross-reference any of the recurring structures in the signal? Even roughly? I'm not asking for translation. I'm asking for shape."

"I can attempt structural mapping. It will take time. My linguistic core was never designed for reconstruction work—it was designed to translate cargo manifests between three trade standards." A pause, and then, with something that in anyone else Felix would have called stubbornness: "But I have been alone with my code for a long time, and I have gotten better at things I was not designed for. So. Yes. Give me the night."

"Take the night," Felix said. "Take two."

She turned back to the display, to the primer frozen open on the father holding up his apple, the mother pointing at the sky. Two hundred years dead, according to every record in the corridor. No speakers. No recordings. Nothing left but corrupted files and truncated grammars and a word—*Vessic*—that everyone knew and no one asked about.

And a signal, structured and fluent and alive, coming from somewhere on a band reserved for stations that no longer existed.

"JUNO," she said, before she could stop herself. "The signal source. How tight is the triangulation?"

"Tight enough to plot. Loose enough that you will want to verify before you believe it." The star chart bloomed across the main display, the route line threading through it like a scar, and JUNO laid the bearing across it in slow amber strokes. "The signal originates along the old Kestrel trunk line. Bearing consistent with a single fixed point."

The point resolved. A marker blinked onto the chart at the intersection of the bearing and the ancient route, and Felix read the label twice, though she had already known—had known since JUNO said the word *Meridian* an hour ago, had known in the cold of her hands and the way her body had stood up without permission.

**MERIDIAN STATION — DECOMMISSIONED 2638 — STATUS: SCUTTLED / NO SURVIVING STRUCTURE**

"JUNO," Felix said, and her voice sounded far away to her own ears, "that station is a graveyard."

"Yes," JUNO said. "And something in it is speaking Vessic."
