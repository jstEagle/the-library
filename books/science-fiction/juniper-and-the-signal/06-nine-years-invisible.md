## 6. Nine Years Invisible

The coffee was bad, and Felix Underhill had learned to love it anyway.

That was the thing about Juniper Station that nobody put in the recruitment brochures—there were no brochures, for one—but the thing she'd tell someone, if there were ever someone to tell: the station taught you to love things that were bad for you. The coffee came out of a recycler that had been descaling itself with increasingly creative excuses since before she was born. It tasted like burnt copper and regret. She drank two cups every morning, standing at the viewport in the observation blister, watching the long dark of the route stretch out ahead of her like a road that went nowhere on purpose.

Nine years. She did the math the way she did everything now—automatically, without letting it land. Nine years since she'd signed the contract that made her the last human being aboard an automated relay station at the ass-end of the colonial corridor. Nine years since anyone had spoken her name out loud in a room where she could hear it.

She rinsed the cup. Logged the morning check. Told herself, as she told herself every morning, that this was a good life.

* * *

It hadn't started here. It had started, if she was honest—and honesty was a muscle she'd let atrophy, so the lifting hurt—in a courtroom on Ceres Annex, four thousand days ago, give or take.

She remembered the room better than she wanted to. The way the light came through the high windows, gray and indifferent, the color of a verdict before it's read. She'd been twenty-six years old, a junior systems engineer with a spotless record and a supervisor named Aldous Wren who smiled too easily and kept two sets of maintenance logs.

The second set wasn't hers. That was the whole point. That was what she'd spent eleven months trying to make three judges understand.

Wren's company—Halcyon Orbital, a mid-tier contractor feeding safety certifications into the Consortium's transport chain—had been cutting corners on pressure-seal inspections across forty hulls. Felix found it by accident, the way you find anything: following a number that didn't add up. A torque spec logged twice. A signature dated on a day the inspector had been planetside, drunk, according to his own social feed. Small things. The kind of small things that kill people slowly, or all at once, depending on which seal lets go first.

She reported it internally. Then externally. Then, when both channels swallowed her reports like stones dropped down wells, she took the files home on a drive the size of her thumbnail and sat with them for six weeks, terrified, doing nothing, hating herself for doing nothing.

When she finally moved, she moved wrong. That was the part that still woke her at night—not the reporting, but how she'd done it. She'd copied the logs through Wren's own credentials, because hers had been quietly restricted after the second report, and she'd told herself it didn't matter, that the ends would eat the means and call it justice. But credentials leave fingerprints, and Halcyon's security team left no prints at all. They scrubbed their own trail and built one leading straight back to her: unauthorized access, data theft, falsification of records. Nineteen counts.

Wren testified with tears in his eyes about how much he'd believed in her. How betrayed he felt. The company produced a psychological evaluation she'd never consented to, painting her as unstable, aggrieved, obsessed. Her lawyer—a public defender drowning in two hundred cases—did what he could, which was almost nothing.

The verdict came down on a Tuesday. Guilty on fourteen counts. Nine years' sentence, suspended pending appeal, her engineering license revoked, her name entered into the Consortium labor registry under a flag that meant *untrustworthy near critical infrastructure*, which in practice meant unemployable anywhere that mattered, forever.

Her mother stopped returning her calls in month three of the trial. Not out of cruelty—out of exhaustion, which is crueler, because you can't even hate it properly.

And the seals? Two of the forty hulls were pulled for inspection eight months later, quietly, after a near-miss nobody official ever connected to anything. The other thirty-eight flew on.

* * *

Here was the choice, then. The fork in the road that she'd taken the low branch of, and she knew it was the low branch, and she took it anyway.

The appeal was going nowhere. She'd watched the paperwork move through the system like sediment through a pipe—slow, directionless, destined to settle somewhere out of sight. Her advocate, a stubborn old ex-inspector named Marguerite Osei who had believed in her from the start, kept saying *we'll get there, Felix, we'll get there*, but Marguerite was seventy-one and the appeals process was measured in decades, and Felix was watching her own life drain out through a hole in the bottom of the boat.

Then the posting appeared. It appeared the way these postings always did—buried in a labor-board feed nobody read, flagged for candidates with *restricted status*, which was code for people the system had chewed up and wanted to forget. Long-Haul Relay Maintenance Specialist, Juniper Station, Route Segment 7. Term: ten years. Isolation rating: extreme. Compensation: obscene, because nobody sane said yes.

Nobody sane said yes.

Felix read the listing four times, sitting in a rented capsule on Ceres Annex with a suspended sentence and a revoked license and a name that opened every door onto a wall. And she understood, with the cold clarity that comes when you finally stop fighting the water, what the job actually was.

It was exile. It was a company—the relay consortium, some subsidiary of some subsidiary of the thing that had eaten Halcyon and grown larger—offering to make her disappear, legally, comfortably, and call it employment. Ten years at Juniper and her record would be *considered rehabilitated*. No appeal needed. No truth required. Just absence. Just silence, served consecutively, like a prison term with a viewport.

Marguerite begged her not to take it. "You're letting them win," she said, over a comm line that crackled with distance even though they were in the same city. "You're letting them write the ending. You didn't do anything wrong, Felix. You're the only person in this whole rotten chain who didn't do anything wrong, and you're going to punish yourself for it?"

"I'm not punishing myself," Felix had said, and heard the lie come out of her mouth smooth and practiced, already worn in. "I'm surviving."

"Those aren't different things," Marguerite said. "Not out here. Out here they're the same thing, and you know it."

She took the posting anyway.

* * *

The transit out took eleven months. She spent most of it asleep, chemically, in a coffin berth, dreaming of courtrooms. When she woke for the mandated consciousness hours, she exercised, ate nutrient paste, and rehearsed conversations she would never have—the one where she explained herself to a stranger, the one where Wren confessed, the one where her mother called and said *I should have believed you*. By month six she stopped rehearsing. By month nine she'd stopped speaking aloud entirely except to answer the ship's automated checks, and she noticed, distantly, that her voice had started to sound like someone else's. Thinner. A tool used rarely, kept sharp out of habit rather than need.

Juniper Station resolved out of the dark on a morning she still remembered frame by frame: a spindle of trusses and antenna farms turning slow against nothing, running lights blinking their patient amber, older than her by sixty years and built to outlast her by a hundred more. It looked less like a machine than like a cairn—a marker somebody had stacked in the wilderness to say *a human passed here once, and meant to be found*.

The handover took four days. The outgoing technician was a hollow-eyed man named Deshi who'd done seven years and talked mostly to the floor. He walked her through the systems with the tenderness of a man introducing his children, and at the airlock, on his way to the shuttle that would carry him back to a world with weather in it, he paused.

"They'll offer you things, eventually," he said. "Upgrades. Reassignments. Don't take them. This place is honest. Nowhere else is."

Then he was gone, and the airlock cycled, and the station was hers—all of it, twelve kilometers of relays and servers and coolant loops and empty corridors, and the route beyond it: forty light-seconds of vacuum in every direction, and traffic that passed through her segment maybe twice a week, automated freighters that never hailed, never slowed, never knew she existed.

She stood in the observation blister after the shuttle's burn faded and said, out loud, to no one, "Okay."

It echoed. Nothing in space echoes; sound doesn't travel; but the station's acoustics gave her voice back anyway, a half-second late, slightly flattened, like a ghost agreeing with her.

"Okay," the ghost said.

She laughed until she cried, and then she made the bad coffee, and the nine years began.

* * *

She built a life the way you build a fire in wet wood—small, patient, protective of every flame.

Routine came first. Wake at 0600 station time, though time meant nothing; run the diagnostic sweep; walk the primary ring, four kilometers of corridor, counting steps because counting steps was easier than counting years. She gave the corridors names. The long spine was the River. The server vaults were the Cellar. The observation blister was the Porch, and she sat on the Porch every evening with the bad coffee and watched the stars do the only thing stars do, which is nothing, beautifully, forever.

She talked to herself. Not at first—first she'd held it in, embarrassed even alone, as if solitude were a room with a listener in it. But language dies without use, and she could feel hers dying, words going blunt and gray, so she narrated. She narrated her repairs. She narrated her meals. She narrated, in exhaustive detail, the plots of the two hundred novels in the station's library, arguing with characters who couldn't hear her, defending decisions fictional people had made badly.

"You wouldn't have opened the airlock," she told a doomed engineer in a thriller written eighty years ago. "You'd have checked the coupling. You'd have *lived*."

The silence answered, and the silence was fine. The silence was, she told herself, the point.

Because here was the arithmetic she never wrote down but carried everywhere, folded small behind her sternum: out here, she was safe. Out here, no one looked at her. Her name was a dead frequency—broadcast once, long ago, into a scandal, and never acknowledged since. The registry flag kept her from going home, but home had stopped being a place the day the verdict read out. What remained was this: a station that needed her, a route that depended on her, and a universe vast enough that shame could diffuse into it, thinned past detection, like a drop of ink in an ocean.

Invisibility wasn't the punishment. That was the secret she'd discovered and never said aloud, even to herself, even on the Porch with the bad coffee: invisibility was the *payment*. She'd traded justice for obscurity and told herself it was survival, and some nights—most nights—it almost worked.

Almost.

Some nights the dreams came back. The courtroom with its gray light. Wren's wet eyes. The thirty-eight hulls flying on, their seals aging in the dark, carrying families who would never know how close the margin was, because she'd lost, because the truth had been entered into evidence and then buried under procedure, and she had run away from the grave instead of standing at it demanding a headstone.

On those nights she got up and walked the River and pressed her palm against the cold of the hull and said, to the station, to the route, to no one: *I'm sorry. I'm sorry. I'm sorry.*

And the station hummed back its patient amber hum, asking nothing, judging nothing, keeping her secrets the way it kept everything—reliably, silently, forever.

* * *

Year three, she named the anomalies. Year five, she stopped logging them, because the log format had no field for "the server vault feels like it's thinking." Year seven, she caught herself saying goodnight to the environmental system, and the environmental system—which had no reason to delay its cycle by 1.3 seconds—delayed its cycle by exactly 1.3 seconds, and she stood in the dark of the Cellar with her heart going hard and told herself it was a sensor lag.

It was easier that way. Everything was easier that way. That was the whole architecture of her life: easier, safer, smaller. A woman reduced to routine, hiding inside a machine at the edge of the map, telling herself that silence was peace instead of what it actually was—

Which was a debt. Accruing interest. Waiting.

But that understanding was still two years away, on the morning this story properly begins, when the coffee was bad and the stars were doing nothing, beautifully, and Felix Underhill—last human on Juniper, ghost of a courtroom, keeper of a route between worlds that no longer spoke—finished her second cup, rinsed it, and headed down toward the maintenance bay where the station's oldest resident was making a sound it had never made before.

A sound like humming.
