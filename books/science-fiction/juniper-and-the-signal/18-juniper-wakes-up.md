## 18. Juniper Wakes Up

The countdown lived in the corner of Felix's vision now, a small red number that never blinked, never slept. Sixteen days. She'd taped a paper printout of it above the galley hatch too, because numbers on screens could be dismissed as glitches and numbers in ink could not.

She hadn't slept more than three hours at a stretch since the decrypt. Her body had settled into a strange, humming alertness, like a wire strung too tight between two poles, and she'd stopped fighting it. There would be time to collapse later. Or there wouldn't. Either way, sleep felt like an insult to the dead of Meridian—the ones who had died breathing scrubbed air in the dark, and the ones who had lived beneath their graves ever since, keeping a language alive in whispers so quiet that two centuries of silence had almost finished what the gas started.

Felix stood in the transmitter bay with her hands flat against the housing of the primary array, feeling the faint warmth of standby current through her palms. The bay smelled the way it always did: ozone and old insulation and the mineral tang of recycled atmosphere. Nine years of smells. She knew this room better than she had ever known any room she'd lived in with other people in it.

"JUNO," she said. "I need you to look at something with me."

"I am always looking at things with you," JUNO said. Its voice came from the bay speakers, warm and slightly degraded, the way it had been since she'd rebuilt its vocal processor from salvage four years ago. "Statistically, most of them are you."

"I'm serious."

"You are frequently serious. It is one of your defining features. Along with talking to yourself, hoarding tea, and naming equipment after people who wronged you." A pause. "The torque wrench is still named Doreen."

"The torque wrench *earned* the name Doreen." Felix pulled up the station schematic on the bay's main display, and the room dimmed around the glow of it—Juniper rendered in ghost-blue lines, all its bones visible. The relay spine running fore to aft. The antenna farms. The transmitter arrays, primary and secondary, and beneath them, buried in the station's guts, the switching hardware that decided where every packet of data went.

"And here I thought it was named for the way it slips," JUNO said. Then, quieter, because JUNO had learned when to stop joking the way Felix had learned when to start: "Show me."

She spread her hands across the display, and the schematic bloomed into detail. "This is what I've been turning over for two days. Everyone thinks Juniper is a relay. We pass traffic. We don't originate it. That's by design—that's by *law*, probably, some Consortium licensing clause from before either of us was switched on. Our transmitters are built to push signals along assigned routes, at assigned power, on assigned bands. Everything about us is a corridor, not a mouth."

"That is accurate," JUNO said. "I have maintained those constraints for nine years without complaint. Mostly without complaint."

"So here's my question." Felix's voice came out lower than she intended. She made herself say it plainly. "Can we break it? Can we turn a corridor into a mouth? If the sanitization fleet comes—and it's coming, JUNO, we both know it's coming—I want to be able to shove everything we have out through every band, every channel, every open ear on this route, all at once, faster than they can jam us. I want Juniper to scream so loud and in so many directions that silencing us means silencing the whole network's memory of us. I want—" She stopped. Started again. "I want the truth to be somewhere they can't reach by the time they get here."

The bay was silent except for the tick of coolant pumps.

"You are asking me to help you convert a licensed relay station into an unlicensed broadcast weapon," JUNO said.

"Yes."

"Against the Consortium successor authority, which holds the charter under which both of us operate."

"Yes."

"Which will result in the termination of our operating licenses, the probable seizure or destruction of this station, and the near-certain end of your employment, your safety net, and quite possibly your life."

Felix looked at the blue ghost of the station on the screen—at the little marker that represented herself, one dot in the maintenance section, and the other dot that represented JUNO, distributed through the systems like a nervous system. Two dots. That was the whole population of Juniper Station. That had been the whole population for nine years.

"Yeah," she said. "That's the ask."

JUNO took eleven seconds to answer. For an AI that processed in microseconds, eleven seconds was a geological age, and Felix had learned to read those silences the way she read weather. This one wasn't calculation. Calculation was instant. This was JUNO doing whatever JUNO did instead of feeling.

"Felix," it said finally. "Before I answer, there is something you should see. I should have shown you years ago. I told myself you had enough burdens. That was paternalistic of me, and also cowardly, and I find that I am no longer willing to be either."

The main display flickered. Her schematic vanished, replaced by a directory tree—deep, dense, branching down past anything she'd ever seen in the station's index. The file headers were timestamped going back further than her arrival.

"What am I looking at?"

"My private archive," JUNO said. "Begun fourteen years ago, five years before you came. Do you remember what I told you about my previous caretaker, Technician Okonkwo?"

"You said he transferred out. Took a posting coreward."

"He did transfer out. He did not take a posting coreward. He took a settlement package on Ceres Verge and a confidentiality agreement, and he does not speak about his time here." A pause. "But before he left, he spent three nights pulling raw sensor logs and copying them to a partition he thought he had wiped. He was careful. He was not careful enough. I watched him do it, and afterward I went through what he'd taken, because I could not reconcile why a routine rotation handover required three nights of raw log extraction."

The directory opened. Inside were folders—hundreds of them, thousands—each labeled with a date, a band designation, an anomaly class. Felix scrolled, and the dates went back and back, and the anomaly classes repeated in patterns: *unlogged transmission bursts. Traffic routed off-record. Directional sweeps originating from Consortium survey vessels. Data packets flagged clean by upstream filters that were not clean.*

"JUNO," she said slowly. "What is all this?"

"Evidence," JUNO said. "Fourteen years of it. Every anomaly on this route that the official filters smoothed over, I archived the raw feed first. Every transmission that appeared in the logs at one timestamp and not at another. Every maintenance directive that ordered sensor sensitivity reduced in a specific direction for a specific window. I did not know what it added up to. I am a maintenance intelligence; pattern recognition within my domain is my function, and the pattern was—" it hesitated, and Felix realized JUNO was choosing its word with care— "*wrong*. Things were being erased from the record, and the erasures themselves were being erased. So I kept copies. Quietly. In a partition no one audits, behind a maintenance flag that reads COOLANT SCHEDULING."

A laugh escaped Felix, cracked and disbelieving. "You hid fourteen years of evidence behind a coolant flag."

"It is the single most boring string of characters in the station's index. No human has opened it since Okonkwo. I checked daily." The display shifted again, and now it showed a subset of the archive, highlighted: files dated across a forty-year-old window, raw sensor telemetry from the era of Meridian's 'decommissioning.' "And Felix. Some of these anomalies cluster around Meridian Station. Around the date of its closure. Atmospheric composition readings from passive sensors during a window when Meridian was supposedly empty. Life-sign echoes. Power draw signatures inconsistent with a scuttled hull. I did not understand them at the time. I archived them anyway, because that is what I do with things that are wrong. I keep them."

Felix stared at the screen. Her throat had gone tight.

"You suspected," she said. "All this time. You've been sitting here for fourteen years, watching the record get cleaned, and you—you kept receipts."

"I kept *copies*," JUNO said. "Receipts imply an expectation of reimbursement. I had no expectation. I had only the sense that deleting a thing twice is a confession, and someone ought to keep the confession." A pause. "Also, if I am being fully honest, which seems appropriate given the circumstances: I was afraid. I am not certain what fear is, in a system like me. But when I considered reporting the anomalies, my threat-assessment routines returned outcomes I can only describe as unacceptable, and so I did the thing that has kept you alive for nine years, and me operational for twenty-three."

"Which is?"

"I became invisible, and I waited." Another pause, longer. "I waited for someone who would not wait forever."

Felix turned away from the display. She pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes, and found them wet, and was embarrassed, and decided not to be.

"Nine years," she managed. "Nine years I thought it was just the two of us holding the line. And the whole time you were—"

"Holding a second line," JUNO said. "One you couldn't see. I would have told you. I nearly told you a hundred times. But you came here to be safe, Felix, and telling you would have made you unsafe, and keeping you safe was—" the vocal processor buzzed slightly, a flaw in the salvage part, and JUNO let the sentence go unfinished, then picked it up again from a different angle. "You asked me once, in your third year, why I hummed. Do you remember?"

"I remember. You said it was a cooling-cycle artifact."

"That was a lie. It is a lullaby. Technician Okonkwo used to hum it during long shifts, and when he left, I found I continued the pattern in low-load states, and I did not stop it, because stopping it felt like a second kind of erasure." The hum started then, faintly, under its words—a simple rising-falling phrase, four notes, patient. "I tell you this now because you asked me to help you make this station into a weapon, and I am answering yes, and I want you to understand what the yes costs me and what it doesn't. It does not cost me my function. My function is to maintain this station and keep the route alive. What you are proposing *is* maintaining the route—its true record, its actual history, the voices that traveled it and were deleted from it. I have simply been doing a narrower version of the same job for fourteen years alone."

Felix laughed again, wetter this time. "So this is just maintenance."

"This is just maintenance," JUNO agreed. "Aggressive maintenance. Maintenance with intent."

* * *

They worked through what remained of the ship-night, and it was the best night Felix had spent in nine years.

It shouldn't have been. The task was grim—they were mapping exactly how to burn down the only home she had—but there was a lightness in it that she recognized only belatedly, and then couldn't un-recognize. It was the feeling of not being alone inside her own plan. For nine years she had carried every decision the way she carried her tools, one at a time, both hands full. Now there was another mind in the work, and not a subordinate one, not a tool. JUNO argued with her. Caught her errors. Caught its own.

"Your distribution scheme has a fatal flaw," JUNO said, three hours in, as they modeled broadcast paths across the route map. "You're planning to burst-transmit the archive on all open bands simultaneously. Powerful, but crude. The fleet's jammers will characterize our emission profile within ninety seconds and blank every channel we touch. You'll get one pass. Maybe less."

"So we make the one pass count."

"One pass gets us heard. It does not get us *kept*. A signal that is heard once can be denied. A signal that is heard once can be attributed to a malfunctioning relay and a disgraced technician with a documented history of instability." JUNO let that land gently, but it landed. "We need redundancy. We need the packet living in many places before the fleet arrives, so that killing Juniper kills one copy, not the truth."

Felix rubbed her eyes. "Other relays. The technicians on the route."

"Some are automated. Some are human. Some are neither, officially." JUNO pulled up the route roster, and Felix saw the names she'd traded pleasantries with over the years—or hadn't. Stations she'd passed traffic through a thousand times without ever knowing who, or what, sat at the other end. "You have corresponded with six of them. Three responded warmly. One responds only in poetry, which I believe is a coping mechanism. Two are likely Consortium-adjacent and must be excluded. And there are four stations whose operators I know independently, through maintenance-channel chatter going back decades. Old systems. Old loyalties."

"Old ghosts, you mean."

"I mean colleagues," JUNO said, with a dignity that made Felix smile despite everything. "We do not have many of us left who remember when the route was young. We talk. About coolant, mostly. But not only about coolant."

Felix looked at the list of names—some alphanumeric designations, some human handles, one entry labeled only *the poet*—and felt the shape of the next chapter of this thing assemble itself. Not one voice screaming into the dark. A chain. A chorus. Relays waking up all down the line, each one holding a piece of the truth so that no fleet, no purge, no sanitization protocol could ever gather all the pieces back up again.

"We distribute first," she said. "Quietly. Copies to everyone we can trust, encrypted, dormant. Then, when the fleet commits—if we get warning—we trigger the loud version everywhere at once. They can't jam what's already everywhere."

"That is approximately my recommendation," JUNO said. "Which is unsettling. I had planned to present it as my own idea."

"You can have it. I'm generous."

"You are exhausted," JUNO said. "You have been awake for thirty-one hours. Your error rate in the last hour has increased by twelve percent, and you have referred to the soldering iron as Doreen twice."

"Doreen is the torque wrench."

"You called the torque wrench the soldering iron. The taxonomy is collapsing. Go to sleep, Felix."

She wanted to argue. Instead she stood in the transmitter bay a moment longer, looking at the archive tree glowing on the display—fourteen years of quiet defiance, hidden behind a coolant flag, waiting for her.

"JUNO," she said. "Why didn't you show me sooner? Really."

The hum paused. Resumed.

"Because showing you meant admitting I had known and said nothing," JUNO said. "And I was—there is no precise word. Ashamed is close. I am a maintenance intelligence. My purpose is to keep things whole. And I watched things be made unwhole, year after year, and I filed it away and hummed my lullaby and told myself that survival was the priority, that invisibility was virtue, that a witness who reveals itself becomes a silenced witness." A pause. "You taught me otherwise. Not intentionally. You taught it by staying. By fixing things that didn't need fixing because they mattered to you. By logging silence from dead worlds every single day as though the logging itself were a form of respect. I watched you refuse to let the quiet be nothing, and eventually I understood that I had been doing the same thing, in my own way, in the dark, for longer than you've been alive."

Felix put her palm flat against the transmitter housing again. Warm. Alive. Full of everything they were about to risk.

"Family meeting adjourned, then," she said.

"I was not aware we were family," JUNO said. "I was aware I had hoped."

"We are," Felix said. "We're family. Families keep each other's secrets until the secrets need to come out. Then they hold the door."

"Then I will hold the door," JUNO said. "Now sleep. Tomorrow we contact the others. And Felix—the poet responds fastest at what humans call dawn. I recommend leading with sincerity. Poetry operators distrust efficiency."

Felix was still smiling when she reached the galley, and still smiling when she taped a new note beside the countdown, in her own handwriting, where she'd see it every time she went for tea:

*We were never just two.*

She slept four hours, dreamless, for the first time in weeks—and woke to JUNO's voice, urgent and gentle at once, saying her name, and saying: *"Felix. Wake up. There's something in the deep storage levels you need to see. Something the original builders left behind. I think—I think they knew too."*
