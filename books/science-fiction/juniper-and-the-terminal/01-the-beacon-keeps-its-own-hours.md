## 1. The Beacon Keeps Its Own Hours

The station woke her the way it always did: not with sound but with light, a slow amber bleed along the ceiling seams that mimicked a dawn Coral-4 had never actually seen. Earth's dawns were secondhand to her—archived, compressed, stripped of their smells. She had been decanted in orbit, grown in a vat of warm salt and borrowed memory, and the first sky she had ever known was the one Juniper projected for her, six meters overhead, rendered by an aging display that flickered at the edges where the emitters were dying.

She lay still for the count of ten, as she always did. Ten seconds of being no one. Then the day began, and she became Coral Quintana.

Not Coral-4. Never Coral-4. The logs wouldn't accept a suffix; the consortium's registry had been built for exactly one woman with that name, and every record she touched—the maintenance reports, the beacon calibrations, the quarterly attestations of fitness—had to flow from that single signature like water from a single spring. So each morning she performed the small resurrection. She said her name aloud to the empty galley, voice rough with sleep: "Coral Quintana, keeper of Juniper." And the station, which knew better and said nothing, opened her day.

* * *

The routine had the architecture of liturgy. She had never thought of it that way until year three, when she'd caught herself moving through the corridors with her hands folded, her steps measured, and realized she had invented a religion whose only deity was a dead woman's schedule.

First: the spine walk. Juniper was a kilometer of trussed habitat and antenna farm strung along a central axis, and every morning Coral walked its length end to end, checking the hull with her palms as much as her eyes. The metal told you things before the sensors did—a tremor in the plating, a cold spot where insulation had slumped, the faint singing of a strut under thermal stress. Her predecessor's hands had walked this same path. She knew this because the handrails were worn smooth in exactly the places her own grip fell, and the thought arrived every morning without fail, punctual as the amber dawn: *you are wearing her grooves deeper.*

Second: calibrations. The beacon itself lived in the heart of the station, a chamber she thought of privately as the reliquary—a sphere of shielded lattice around a transmitter array older than she was, older than the murder, older than the war nobody called a war. Every day she ran the diagnostic cycle. Every day the numbers came back green within tolerances, and every day she adjusted them anyway, because the ritual demanded hands on the work, not just eyes on the readout. She tuned the phase arrays by fractions of degrees. She bled condensate from the coolant loops. She listened to the beacon's carrier tone through the old headset her predecessor had left hanging on its hook, a low note below hearing, more felt than heard, like the station's pulse taken at the throat.

Third: the log.

This was the part she both dreaded and needed most, and she had never admitted that to anyone, because there was no one to admit it to.

* * *

At 1400 station time—Juniper kept Earth hours out of some long-dead administrator's nostalgia—Coral sat in the operations crèche, a room barely larger than a lifeboat, and composed the daily entry. The console wanted formality. It got formality.

*Station Day 4,412. Relay integrity nominal. Beacon output nominal, drift 0.003 arcseconds corrected. Consumables at 71 percent and stable. Crew fitness: adequate.*

*Adequate.* She almost smiled at the word. Somewhere on Earth or Luna or one of the consortium's polished habitats, a clerk would skim that line and feel reassured. *Adequate* meant the asset was alive, functional, unremarkable. Unremarkable was the entire job description. The consortium hadn't stationed her here to be brilliant; they'd stationed her here to be continuous. A relay keeper who was also, conveniently, a copy of the woman whose name still opened doors in certain legal archives. A living notarization. When documents needed signing in Coral Quintana's hand—and they did, quarterly, in matters she was never shown the substance of—there she was, pen in her fingers, her dead original's muscle memory guiding the strokes.

She signed the log. The characters came out fluid and confident, a stranger's handwriting flowing from her wrist.

Below the official entry, in a partition she'd built herself over four years, encrypted three layers deep, she wrote the true log:

*Day 4,412. Talked to the tomato plants again. They don't mind. Ran the beacon tone through the headset and stayed under it for eleven minutes, which is a record. Dreamed about rain again, which is stupid, because I've never felt rain. Wondered, again, what she dreamed about, the night before they killed her.*

She stared at the last sentence longer than she meant to. Then she sealed the partition and made tea.

* * *

The tomatoes were her concession to biology. Hydroponics bay two, a quarter rack, six plants grown from seed stock so old the packet had been printed in a font style discontinued decades ago. She talked to them because the agronomy manuals claimed plants responded to vibration and attention, and because if she didn't talk to something, she could feel her own voice atrophying, going thin and strange in her ears until she sounded like a stranger even to herself. Some days she narrated her work to them. Some days she argued with them about the proper pH. Today she only stood among them while the nutrient pumps ticked, and let the green smell of growing things fill a space in her chest that recycled air never quite reached.

"Invisibility is a skill," she told the largest plant, the one she'd named Magellan for no reason she could defend. "It takes practice. You'd know all about that, growing in the dark."

Magellan offered no comment. His leaves had a slight curl at the edges today. She noted it, adjusted the misting cycle, and felt the small, clean satisfaction of a problem found and fixed—the closest thing to conversation her days reliably contained.

That was the shape of competent loneliness. Not the dramatic kind, not the castaway kind. Something quieter and more total: loneliness as a craft she had gotten good at. She could go weeks without speaking above a murmur. She could fill silence with useful motion. She could stand in the observation blister at the end of the spine, watching the slow wheel of stars past the antenna booms, and feel nothing she couldn't manage.

Mostly.

* * *

There was one hour each day she guarded jealously, and it was not the log hour or the calibration hour. It was the hour after lights-down, when she sat in the observation blister with the hull shutters open and watched the cryofleet transponders blink in the distance—two clusters of faint, patient lights, parked in stable orbits on opposite sides of the system like guests seated deliberately far apart at a dinner neither wished to attend.

The loyalists' fleet. The exiles' fleet. She knew them only as designations in the beacon's targeting tables: Fleet Aurelian, Fleet Meridian-South. Between them, equidistant, hung Juniper and its beacon, and the beacon held, encoded in its selection logic, the answer to a question Coral had been trained never to ask: *when the relay dies, who wakes?*

Her training was explicit. The beacon was a rescue system. When Earth's deep relay failed—and it was failing, everyone knew it was failing, the decay curves published in every briefing she'd ever received said decades, maybe less—the beacon would select the cryoships best positioned to preserve human continuity, according to criteria certified by the Continuity Consortium. That was all. That was the whole story. Keeper, maintain the beacon. Keeper, sign the documents. Keeper, do not inquire into selection logic, which is proprietary.

She had been very good at not inquiring. Four years good. She sometimes wondered if not-inquiring was the deepest thing they'd grown into her—not obedience exactly, but a kind of trained blindness, a reflex that looked away from certain doors before her curiosity even reached them.

Tonight, in the blister, she looked at the two fleets and felt the reflex twitch, and looked away, and hated herself a little for how easily it worked.

* * *

The anomaly came at the end of the day, the way anomalies prefer to.

She was running the weekly manifest audit—rote work, cross-checking the beacon's registered assets against the consortium's master ledger, a task designed for a clerk and performed by a keeper because the consortium trusted no one else out here. The audit took ninety minutes. For four years it had taken ninety minutes and produced nothing but confirmation.

At minute eighty-seven, the ledger returned a ship she had never seen before.

*MV MERIDIAN — STATUS: LAUNCHED — CREW MANIFEST ATTACHED.*

Coral frowned. There was no Meridian in her beacon tables. No Meridian in the traffic logs, which went back to the station's commissioning. She pulled the full record, expecting a ghost entry, a ledger corruption, the kind of data rot that crept into forty-year-old archives like damp into stone.

The manifest loaded. Seven names. She read them once, quickly, the way you skim anything bureaucratic.

Then she read them again, slowly, with her whole body gone still.

The fourth name on the list was CORAL QUINTANA. No suffix. No clone designation. No annotation of any kind—just the name, plain as a knife on a table.

And beneath it, the fifth name, unfamiliar and yet somehow ringing against something in her skull like a struck bell: ILYA VOSS.

The launch date field resolved. Coral did the arithmetic twice, then a third time, her breath loud in the quiet blister.

The Meridian had launched—or been recorded as launched—eleven months before she was decanted. Before her vat, before her training, before the first morning she'd stood in front of the mirror and practiced saying a dead woman's name until it stopped sounding borrowed.

She sat with the record glowing on her retinal display, the stars wheeling indifferent behind the glass, and felt the floor of her four-year routine tilt by a single degree—imperceptible to any instrument, but enough that she could feel everything on the station beginning, very slowly, to slide.

"Magellan," she said to the empty blister, to the fleets, to whoever had put a dead woman's name on a ship that never flew, "I think I just found a door."

The reflex twitched, telling her to look away.

For the first time in four years, she didn't.
