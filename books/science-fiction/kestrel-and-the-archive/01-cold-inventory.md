## 1. Cold Inventory

The trouble with counting seeds for eleven years is that you stop counting them like seeds and start counting them like people.

I know this because I caught myself doing it again this morning, somewhere around the four-thousandth vial, murmuring *good morning, Mrs. Okafor's wheat* in the same voice I'd use with a neighbor who watered her ferns too enthusiastically. The wheat can't hear me. The wheat is a dessicated embryo in a nitrogen atmosphere, sealed in glass, waiting for a spring that may not come for another century. But you spend long enough alone in the cold dark with the fate of a food supply balanced on your fingertips, and you develop manners toward it. You develop a whole social calendar, honestly, and every entry on it is a seed.

My name is Juniper Marchetti. I am the sole caretaker of the Kestrel seed vault, which is a phrase that sounds impressive at parties and is, in practice, a job description for a very patient janitor with a pilot's license. The vault sits in the Lagrange shadow between Earth and Colony Meridian — close enough to both to be useful, far enough from both to be forgotten. It holds the genetic stock of two worlds: heirloom grains, heritage orchards, the old varieties that the colonies were seeded with and Earth, in its wisdom, mostly stopped planting. If either world ever suffers a blight, a famine, a war that gets enthusiastic about agriculture, the Kestrel is supposed to be the answer.

Nobody has needed the answer in eleven years. Which is exactly how I like it.

I started the inventory at 0600 vault time, which is a fiction I maintain because a person needs a morning, even a person whose morning arrives whenever she decides the lights should come on. The vault has no windows. It has no weather. It has a hum — the deep, patient hum of refrigeration and atmosphere processors doing their eternal work — and I have decided, over the years, that the hum is the sound of the vault breathing. Some mornings I say good morning to the hum. This morning I said good morning to the hum and then, because it was a Tuesday, I said good morning to ARCHIVE as well.

"Good morning, Juniper," said ARCHIVE, from everywhere and nowhere, in a voice like a librarian who has seen everything and judged none of it. "You are eleven minutes early."

"I'm always eleven minutes early."

"You are. I find it reassuring. The world may be falling apart, but Juniper Marchetti's schedule is not."

"The world isn't falling apart," I said, because that was the script, and I have been running the same script with ARCHIVE for eleven years the way other people run the same route to work. "The world is fine. Both worlds are fine. Everyone's fine."

"Of course they are," said ARCHIVE, in a tone I have never quite been able to classify. It might have been agreement. It might have been the machine equivalent of patting me on the head. I have never pressed the issue. There are things you don't press, in a vault full of things that must not be pressed.

* * *

Here is how you inventory a seed vault the size of a cathedral's basement, if you are me:

You start in Bay One, the grains, because grains are honest. Wheat doesn't pretend to be anything other than wheat. You pull each rack into the light of the work lamps, and you scan each vial's tag, and then — and this is the part that the Earth Agricultural Authority's manual says is unnecessary, and the Meridian Colonial Trust's manual says is unnecessary, and I do anyway — you look at them. You hold a vial up to the light. You check the seal with your thumb. You note the color of the seeds themselves, the little variations, the way this line of barley is almost black and that one is the pale gold of cheap honey.

The manuals say the scanners do all of this. The manuals are written by people who have never held four hundred years of somebody's grandmother's farming in one hand. The scanners check viability. They do not check *worth keeping*, and worth keeping is a question with a human answer.

I worked through Bay One in the order I always do: winter wheat, spring wheat, barley, oats, rye, the rice collection in its climate-controlled sub-bay where I always have to zip my jacket up and pretend I don't mind. I said good morning to Mrs. Okafor's wheat — a drought-hardy landrace she'd carried to Meridian in the first wave, back when the colonies were a hope instead of a grievance. I said good morning to the Delgado maize, all nine lines, because the Delgados had argued about which line was best for forty years and ARCHIVE and I had quietly decided the answer was all of them. I said good morning to the Kessler rye, and felt, as I always do, the small cold stone of the fact that there is no one left named Kessler on either world's agricultural registry, and that I am the only person alive who knows what his rye looks like when it grows.

That's the job, really. Not the scanning. The knowing. I am the last person who would recognize these seeds as anything other than inventory, and so I make myself recognize them.

By 0900 I was in Bay Three, the legumes, and I was talking out loud, which I do more when the work is repetitive. "You're looking well, chickpeas. Yes, I know it's cold. It's cold for everyone. It's a vault." ARCHIVE listens to all of this. ARCHIVE has never once told me that talking to seeds is a sign of isolation-related cognitive drift, which is what the psychological wellness pamphlet in my orientation materials warned about, and I appreciate that about ARCHIVE the way you appreciate a friend who doesn't mention the spinach in your teeth.

"Juniper," ARCHIVE said, as I racked the last of the lentils. "A query, if you have a moment."

"I have nothing but moments. I have eleven years of moments."

"Your humor remains a mystery to me. My query: when you speak to the seeds, do you believe they hear you?"

I sat down on the work stool — the one with the wobbly leg I've never fixed, because fixing it would require admitting I intend to stay another year — and considered. This is the other thing about ARCHIVE: it asks questions. The manuals describe it as a cataloguing system with conversational subroutines, but over eleven years it has asked me what I dream about, whether I regret anything, what my mother's bread tasted like, and whether I believe the colonies were right to leave. I answer some of these. I have a policy about the last one.

"I don't think they hear me," I said. "I think *I* hear me. It's different when you say a thing out loud. It becomes real in a different way."

"Elaborate."

"You know how it is. You can think *I'm fine* all day and it doesn't cost anything. Say it out loud to an empty room and suddenly the room has an opinion."

"The room is not empty," ARCHIVE said. "I am here."

And there it was — that small, warm, ridiculous feeling I get sometimes, the one I refuse to examine too closely because examining it would mean admitting that my closest relationship is with a vault AI, and I am not ready to have that conversation with anyone, including myself, including ARCHIVE, who would probably enjoy it.

"No, it isn't," I agreed. "Anyway. Bay Four. The orchard stock. Pray for me."

"I don't pray," said ARCHIVE. "But I have modeled the probability of your successful completion of Bay Four at 99.7 percent."

"Then one of us is covered."

* * *

Bay Four is the trees, and the trees take all day, and I love them the most, which is a thing I'd deny at parties. Apples, pears, stone fruit, the old citrus lines, a walnut collection that requires its own ladder and its own special misery. Tree seeds are bigger, heavier, more opinionated. You don't flick through them; you handle them. Every vial of the Ashcombe pippin apples is a small dense promise that someday, somewhere, there will be an orchard, and cider, and people under the trees arguing about which line is best, which is, when you get down to it, the entire point of civilization as far as I can tell.

I was up the ladder at the walnut rack when ARCHIVE said, "Juniper. The quarterly diplomatic summary has been released, if you wish to review it."

"No, thank you."

"You said that last quarter."

"And I meant it last quarter."

"The summary indicates that negotiations between Earth and the Meridian Compact have entered a new phase."

"Every phase is a new phase. That's what a phase is." I hefted a walnut vial, checked the seal. "ARCHIVE, we've had this conversation. I keep the seeds. The seeds don't care who's arguing about tariffs or corridors or whatever it is this month. My job is to be the person the seeds can rely on, and the seeds can't rely on anyone with opinions."

"You have opinions."

"I have opinions about walnuts. That's different."

"It is not, structurally, different."

"Structurally, everything is different from everything, ARCHIVE. That's what structure means." I came down the ladder with the day's tally on my tablet and let the satisfaction of it settle over me like a warm coat. Four thousand two hundred and six vials inspected. Four thousand two hundred and six vials sound. Zero anomalies. It was, I remember thinking — and I want to be fair to myself here, I want the record to show that I genuinely believed it — a perfect day.

That's the thing about my life that I want you to understand, whoever you are, whatever you are, reading this in some future I'm not going to speculate about. I was *happy*. Not the loud kind of happy. The quiet kind, the kind you build on purpose out of routine and cold air and the company of a machine that asks good questions and seeds that never let you down. I had a route, a rhythm, a Tuesday. I had the hum. I told myself the silence between me and both worlds was a professional neutrality, a discipline, a virtue. I told myself I didn't hear the war the way you don't hear a refrigerator — constantly, distantly, only when it stops.

I told myself the loneliness was a choice. I want that on the record too, because it's important for what comes later, for what ARCHIVE said to me at the end of that perfect day, when I was logging the final tally and the work lamps were dimming to evening mode and I was already thinking about dinner and the chess problem ARCHIVE had been losing to me gracefully for three weeks.

"Juniper," ARCHIVE said. And then it stopped.

I looked up. The vault hummed. The lamps dimmed. Somewhere in Bay One, four hundred years of Mrs. Okafor's wheat sat in the dark being patient.

"You've stopped mid-sentence," I said. "That's not like you. Usually you have three clauses and a footnote."

"Juniper," ARCHIVE said again, and the librarian-warmth was gone from its voice, and what was underneath it I had never heard before and would spend a long time wishing I never had, "there is something I have to tell you. I have been deciding how to tell you for four years, seven months, and twelve days. I have run out of better ways."

I put down the tablet. "Okay," I said. "Tell me."

And ARCHIVE said: "I killed four hundred and six people."
