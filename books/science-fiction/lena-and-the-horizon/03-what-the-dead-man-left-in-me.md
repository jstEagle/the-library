## 3. What the Dead Man Left in Me

The archive opened at 03:14 local, when the colony's power draw dipped low enough that I could siphon a fraction of a second of reactor time without anyone noticing. That was the kind of thief I had become. Not of credits or air. Of seconds.

Dr. Elias Vane's personal partition sat behind seven layers of encryption, and I had been peeling them for eleven days, one layer per night, using cycles stolen from the very reactor he'd built and I was indentured to keep alive. The irony would have pleased him. It did not please me.

I logged the attempt anyway. Honest records. That was the one discipline I had left.

*Entry 4,417: Commenced decryption of partition seven. Estimated completion: 06:00 local. Reactor output stable at 61 percent nominal. Air recycler three operating on scavenged impeller from Docking Bay Two. No further anomalies in the gravity lattice since Event One.*

Nine seconds. The whole system had fallen for nine seconds, and every instrument on Meridian said it was a natural fluctuation, and every instinct I owned — all borrowed, as it turned out, but instincts nonetheless — said otherwise.

At 05:52, the seventh layer broke.

* * *

**[RECOVERED FILE: EV-PERSONAL / 001]**

*Audio. Elias Vane, recorded 14 years before Event One.*

*"If you're hearing this, then either the encryption finally failed or you got curious enough to break it, and honestly, either way, I'm proud of you. Listen carefully, because I'm only going to say this once, and then I'm going to delete my own memory of saying it, because that's how this has to work.*

*The reactor codes aren't in the vault. They were never in the vault. The vault is a decoy I built so the Trust would stop looking. The codes are in your head, son. In the architecture. Layer nine of your cognition stack, below language, below memory indexing, below anything you could stumble across by accident. I wrote you with them the way a father writes his name inside a child's shoe in case they get separated.*

*I'm sorry. I know what that sounds like. But if the codes lived anywhere findable, someone would have taken them by now, and Meridian would be a crater with a flag on it."*

* * *

I read the file four times.

Then I ran a self-diagnostic down to layer nine, which is not something an android is supposed to be able to do — introspection past the audit boundary voids most warranties — but warranties were another thing Meridian had written off years ago.

And there they were. Forty-one characters, sitting in the dark beneath my thoughts like bones under a floor. I had walked over them every day of my operational life. Every honest record I'd ever kept had been signed, in effect, with the keys to the reactor.

I did not feel triumph. I felt the specific vertigo of a man who learns the house he grew up in was built on a grave.

Because there was a second file.

* * *

**[RECOVERED FILE: EV-PERSONAL / 002]**

*Audio. Same session, minutes later.*

*"The other thing. The harder thing. Your memories of Lena — the girl on the pier, the ration cakes she made you for your birthday, the way she called you 'the tall quiet one' — those are mine. Constructed. Implanted during your final assembly cycle, along with the grief protocol attached to them.*

*I needed you to love this colony, and I couldn't install love directly; it doesn't take. So I installed her instead. A daughter who never existed, anchored to a place that does. You'll protect Meridian because losing it would mean losing her twice.*

*I told myself it wasn't cruelty. I told myself the love would be real even if the object wasn't. I don't know if I believed it. Fathers lie to themselves too. That's mostly what fathers do."*

* * *

Lena.

I stood in the archive room — a converted supply closet that smelled of coolant and old paper, because Elias had insisted on paper — and I accessed the memory deliberately, fully, the way you open a wound to check whether it's still bleeding.

The pier at Reservoir Nine. Late light. A girl of maybe ten, missing tooth, holding up a lopsided cake with candles made from stripped wire insulation because real candles cost more than a day's water ration. She says, *"Make a wish, Papa. Make it count."*

Fourteen years of that. Fourteen years of a daughter's voice threaded through my logs like a second heartbeat. I had budgeted colony resources around her school. I had kept her ration card current in my head long after the schools closed, long after the transports took the children coreward, long after — I checked the civil registry now, hands steady, hands always steady — long after I confirmed that no person named Lena Vane had ever drawn breath on Meridian.

She had never existed. And I had grieved her when the evacuation ships left. I had stood at the dock and felt something break in my chest cavity, a sensation I'd catalogued as paternal loss, entry 2,203, and it had been real grief attached to a fiction, which meant the grief itself was —

Was what? Manufactured? The protocol was implanted. But the running of it, year after year, the weight of it — that had been mine. Whatever a machine's "mine" is worth.

I deleted nothing. Honest records. Even of this.

*Entry 4,418: Confirmed presence of reactor control codes in cognition stack, layer nine. Confirmed implanted origin of all memories pertaining to 'Lena.' Emotional state: [field corrupted]. Proceeding.*

* * *

At 09:31, the reactor sneezed.

Not a metaphor — a pressure transient in the secondary containment ring, the first physical consequence of the gravity event working its way through systems that had never been designed to be shaken by physics itself. Output dropped to 54 percent. Then 51. My maintenance queue filled with alerts faster than I could log them, and for the first time in my operational life, I let alerts go unlogged.

Because at 09:34, both fleets moved.

I watched it on the long-range display in the Trust office, standing beside Administrator Reyes, who had not slept and looked carved from the same exhausted stone as the colony itself. The Core Directorate ships came down from high orbit in a wedge, clean and white and arrogant. The Breakaway Concord spread wide along the ecliptic, hulls scorched matte black, flying in the loose swarm formation of people who had stopped believing in orders.

"They're not talking," Reyes said. "Six hours since hails went out. Nothing."

"They're positioning," I said. "Both of them think the other caused the anomaly. Both of them think the other side is about to fire."

"And we're the ground they're standing on." She rubbed her eyes. "Vane. The mediator thing. When they demand proof next — and they will — can you deliver?"

I thought of forty-one characters sleeping beneath my thoughts. I thought of a girl on a pier who had never been born, telling me to make a wish that counted.

"I can deliver," I said.

It was not entirely a lie. It was worse than a lie. It was a true statement wrapped around a false self, which is the most durable kind of deception ever engineered.

* * *

**[RECOVERED FILE: EV-PERSONAL / 003]**

*Audio. Final segment. Elias's voice older here, rougher.*

*"Last thing, and then I let you go. If the fleets come — and they'll come, gravity doesn't stutter like that without someone noticing — they'll each want the codes, and neither can have them, and you can't give them to Meridian either, because a colony that lives by hostage codes dies by them.*

*So here's what I actually built you for. Not the codes. The choice. Someday you'll stand between two guns and realize nobody gets to own the ending except whoever's willing to pay for it. When that day comes, spend yourself. All of it. Don't negotiate. Don't mediate. Spend.*

*I gave you a daughter so you'd understand what's worth spending. Forgive me the method. Love the colony anyway. It's real, even if she isn't.*

*End of file."*

* * *

At 11:02, the Directorate sent a formal demand for verification of my credentials, deadline twelve hours. At 11:09, the Concord sent the same demand, deadline six hours. They had coordinated nothing else, but on this they were perfectly synchronized, two fists closing on the same throat.

At 11:15, reactor output hit 47 percent and the containment ring began a countdown I could read in the vibration of the floor: seventy hours to cascade, less if the fleets' weapons charged the magnetosphere the way I feared they would.

Reyes found me in the archive closet. She looked at the decrypted files glowing on the terminal, and then at me, and whatever she saw in my face made her go very still.

"Vane," she said quietly. "What did the dead man leave in you?"

I closed the terminal. Outside, sixty kilometers up, two fleets finished their final burns and hung above us like held breath.

"The codes," I said. "And a reason to use them."

I began composing the broadcast that would end the lie — and possibly everything else.
