## 1. The Archive Nobody Reads

The wind on Vesper's Reach had a sound like someone trying to remember a word.

Mirabel Fairweather stood at the window of his quarters in the archive annex and listened to it move through the dead grasses beyond the settlement's eastern fence. The grasses were not truly dead—not yet—but they had gone the color of old paper, and when the wind passed through them they made a dry whispering that his ear, trained across four decades and eleven languages, insisted on parsing as speech. *Sshh-ka. Sshh-ka-lu.* A Kessari phoneme cluster, or something close enough to it that his sleeping mind had begun to claim it as its own.

He turned back to the console. The room was small and warm in the way rooms get when the heating system has been cannibalized for parts elsewhere: a desk, a chair with one repaired armrest, a recording array older than most of the colonists' children. On the wall above the console hung the only decoration he permitted himself—a printed spectrogram of a Kessari mourning phrase, framed by the previous archivist, a man named Oduya who had left for Earth nineteen years ago and never sent so much as a message confirming arrival. The phrase meant, roughly, *the song continues without the singer*. Oduya had found it funny. Mirabel had never been sure whether he did or not.

He sat, adjusted the microphone out of habit though the array needed no adjustment, and pressed record.

"Field report four thousand," he said. His voice came out level, unhurried, the voice of a man reading coordinates. He had learned long ago that if he let any feeling into these recordings, the feeling would take over, and then what would remain would be testimony rather than documentation, and the archive—whatever the archive was, whoever the archive was for—deserved better than testimony. "Mirabel Fairweather, senior xenolinguist, Vesper's Reach colony, standard year 2411, local day 9,204."

A pause. Outside, the wind moved through the paper-colored grass.

"Continuing observations on the degradation of my dream-language." He said it plainly, because there was no other way to say it, and because saying it plainly was the whole discipline. "As previously reported in entries 3,971 through 3,998, my dreams have ceased to organize themselves around English. I want to be precise about this, because precision is all we have out here. It isn't that I dream in silence. It isn't even that I dream in images, which would be the ordinary human fallback. I dream in phonemes. Specific ones. This week the dominant clusters were—" he consulted his notes, though he knew them by heart, "—the velar fricative sequence the Kessari use for tidal movement, and an Ondu tonal rise-fall that their grammar attaches to statements of grief. Not grief itself. The grammatical marking of grief. In the dream I was being told, very carefully and correctly, that something sad had happened, and I could not for the life of me determine what."

He stopped the recording there, out of instinct, and sat with his hands flat on the desk. Then he started it again. Four thousand entries. He was allowed a moment of honesty at the four-thousandth.

"I should note for the record that I am aware of how this sounds," he said. "An isolated linguist whose dreams have gone native. There are papers about this. There were papers about this, once, when there were still journals to publish them in. Sensory deprivation plus sustained exposure to non-human phonological structures produces reorganization of the linguistic faculty. The mind reaches for whatever language is nearest, and mine have been reaching for languages that are forty light-years away and spoken by no one within eleven months' travel. I understand the mechanism. Understanding the mechanism does not make waking up easier. This morning I woke with a full sentence in my mouth—a complete, grammatical sentence, with stress patterns and everything—and it took me nearly a minute to accept that it was not English, that nothing in it was human, and that I would spend the day unable to translate it, because the two species whose tongues it belonged to have never once, in the entire history of this colony, sent us anything we could check it against."

He let the recorder run while he poured tea from the dented carafe. The tea was grown in the hydroponics bay, third generation of a strain that had been slowly giving up on flavor, and it tasted like warm water remembering being tea. He drank it anyway. Ritual was a kind of language too, and he was losing enough languages already.

"The archive question," he said, resuming. "Entry four thousand seems like the occasion to address it, since I have addressed it at every hundredth entry since roughly entry six hundred, and the answer has never changed, and there is a certain dignity in consistency."

He looked at the transmission panel mounted beside the console. Its status light glowed a patient amber: *queued*. Everything he had ever recorded was queued. The colony's uplink array—the great dish on the northern ridge, big enough to land a shuttle in—had been built to carry traffic between Vesper's Reach and the relay stations along the corridor to Earth. For the first decades of the colony's life it had done exactly that. Then the relays had gone quiet, one by one, in an order that suggested budget rather than catastrophe, and the last confirmed acknowledgment from anywhere had come fourteen years ago: a single automated receipt from a station called Halcyon Gate, timestamped, unsigned, and never repeated.

Since then, the dish had continued to transmit. It was what it was built for. And the archive software continued to queue his reports, compress them, encode them against corruption, and hand them to the dish, and the dish flung them at the sky at the speed of light, and the sky kept them.

"No confirmed recipients," Mirabel said. "No bounce-backs, no acknowledgments, no requests for clarification, which after four thousand entries I confess I would welcome even if the clarification requested were *please stop*. Either someone is receiving this and choosing silence—which is a form of reception—or no one is, in which case I am the sole reader of the most comprehensive linguistic record ever compiled on the frontier, and I read it aloud to a microphone in a warm little room while a colony dies politely around me."

He heard how it sounded. He let it stand. The dignity of work done for no one was not that you pretended there was someone; it was that you did the work properly anyway, because the work was the thing, and the thing did not stop being the thing just because the audience had failed to materialize. Oduya had understood that. Oduya had framed the spectrogram. Oduya had also left, but that was the other half of the truth, and the archive held both halves.

Outside the window, the light was doing what it always did in late afternoon: going thin. Vesper's Reach's sun was a red dwarf, patient and dim, and the colony had been designed for it, but the design assumed functioning atmospheric processors, and the processors had been failing for thirty years in the slow way things fail when no replacement parts will ever come. The sky had thickened. The growing season had shortened from two hundred days to ninety, then to sixty, then to a number the agronomists said quietly and did not put in official reports. The settlement's population had been nine hundred when Mirabel arrived. It was three hundred and eleven now, and everyone knew the number, and no one said it except at council meetings, where it was said in the tone people used for weather.

*The song continues without the singer.* He glanced at the frame. He had begun, lately, to suspect Oduya had chosen it as an instruction rather than a joke.

* * *

In the evening he walked the perimeter path, because walking was the other ritual, and because the reports were better when he had walked first. The path ran along the eastern fence, past the school—where lights still burned and, faintly, children's voices recited something in unison, a poem or a lesson, the sound of a community insisting on its own future—and past the fields, where the last planters had been pulled inside for the season and the soil lay open and gray-brown under the reddening sky.

Old Tomas was at the fence, as he was most evenings, looking at the fields the way other men looked at photographs of the dead.

"Evening, Mirabel."

"Tomas."

"Record your four thousand yet?"

"This afternoon."

Tomas nodded, satisfied, as if a milestone in an unread archive were a harvest brought safely in. That was the strange grace of this place, Mirabel thought—had thought many times, had tried once to articulate in entry 3,412 before deciding the thought wasn't ready: the colonists treated his work as real. They asked about it at the market. They brought him odd words they'd found in their grandparents' letters, loanwords from the founding era, thinking they might matter to him. They behaved as though the archive were a lighthouse with ships behind every horizon, invisible only because the horizon was far. Maybe they needed it to be true. Maybe a dying colony could afford one man whose work was aimed at the stars, the way a body can afford one organ that serves no purpose anyone can prove.

"How's the dreaming?" Tomas asked. Everyone asked; Mirabel's dreams had become, absurdly, a public concern.

"Louder," Mirabel said. "More grammar."

Tomas laughed, a short bark, and slapped the fence post. "Well. Somebody's got to talk to you, even if it's only yourself." He shuffled off toward the lamplit lane, and Mirabel stood a while longer, watching the first stars come out over the ridge where the great dish stood black against the deepening red.

*Somebody's got to talk to you.* He turned the sentence over on the walk back. It was kinder than it sounded. It was also, he suspected, more literally true than Tomas knew.

* * *

That night the dreams were dense.

He dreamed in the velar fricatives again, tide-language, wave upon wave of it, and beneath the waves something structured—morphology, actual morphology, affixes stacking in orders he almost recognized. He woke at 0300 with his mouth full of a sound like stones rolling underwater and lay in the dark doing what he always did: transcribing. He kept a slate by the bed for exactly this, and his handwriting in the dark was terrible, and it didn't matter, because IPA was IPA, and the shapes of the sounds were real whether or not anyone ever decoded them.

By lamplight he studied what he'd written. The clusters were Kessari, definitely Kessari, but the syntax wrapping them was wrong—was, in fact, not Kessari at all. It was Ondu. The two systems interleaved, Kessari phonology wearing Ondu grammar, which was impossible, which was like finding a sentence built of English words obeying Mandarin word order, unless—

Unless the dream was a translation. Unless somewhere, somehow, both languages were arriving at once, and his sleeping mind, with nowhere else to put them, had begun stitching them together.

He sat with that for a long time. The heating ticked. Somewhere down the corridor, a door closed. He wrote a note to himself in the margin of the slate—*check uplink logs; verify no inbound signal possible*—knowing even as he wrote it that the logs would show nothing, that the dish received nothing, that the sky over Vesper's Reach gave back only weather.

Then, because sleep was finished with him, he went to his office to work, and because the linguistic reports were done for the day, he opened the secondary task he permitted himself on sleepless nights: the audit.

It was honest work, of a sort. The colony's records were a mess—forty years of manifests, maintenance schedules, personnel files, decommission orders, kept by a dozen different administrators in a dozen incompatible formats—and someone ought to reconcile them before the storage drives began failing the way everything else was failing. No one had assigned him the task. He had assigned it to himself, entry by quiet entry, an archive of the archive. If the colony was going to end, it ought at least to end legible.

He worked through the shipping records, the old fleet of surface-to-orbit shuttles, the long list of vessels that had come and gone in the colony's vigorous years. He was skimming the decommissioned list—he knew most of the names the way you know the names of extinct birds—when one of them snagged his eye and refused to let go.

*Meridian.*

He knew the Meridian. Every archivist knew her: the long-range courier laid down in the colony's ambitious decade, intended to run the corridor to Halcyon Gate herself, crewed, provisioned, launched—except she had never launched. Budget collapse, relay failures, the slow withdrawal of everything from everywhere; the Meridian had sat in her cradle at the north yard until the order came down to strip her for parts, and the parts had gone to the atmosphere processors and the hydroponics and the school, and the hull had been scrapped, and the name had passed into the category of things that had almost happened. Decommissioned, year 2378. Crew manifest struck. Standard procedure.

Except the file in front of him was not the decommission file.

It was the maintenance log. And the maintenance log was current.

Mirabel set down his tea. He scrolled upward, slowly, the way you approach an animal you don't want to startle. Weekly entries. Power draw readings from a grid sector he had never had reason to think about. Environmental systems checks. Life support—life support—verified, signed off, dated. The entries were terse, professional, unsigned by name, tagged only with a maintenance code he had never seen before. And they ran unbroken, week after week, year after year, back past the decommission date, back past the scrapping order, back twenty years and further, to the last era when the Meridian had officially existed at all.

The ship that never flew was being maintained. Had been maintained, faithfully, weekly, for decades—for a crew.

He searched the struck manifest. Most names meant nothing to him, faces from a generation before his arrival. But near the top of the roster, listed under specialist designation, was a name that made the warm little room feel suddenly colder:

*Vasquez, Lena — Translation Systems.*

Mirabel Fairweather sat alone in the archive annex, in the oldest building in a dying colony, listening to the wind try to remember a word. On his screen, the impossible log waited, patient as the queued reports, patient as the sky.

There was no Lena Vasquez in the settlement registry. He checked twice. There was no Meridian in the north yard. He had walked past the empty cradle a hundred times.

And yet somewhere on Vester's Reach, every week, someone signed for the upkeep of a ship that had never flown—and a woman who did not exist was listed aboard her.
