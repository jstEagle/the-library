## 2. A Name on a Manifest

The rain on Vesper's Reach fell in long, patient sheets, as though the sky had all the time in the world and intended to use it. Mirabel Fairweather watched it from the archive window while his tea went cold beside him, and thought, not for the first time, that water was the colony's most honest citizen. It arrived without ceremony. It did its work. It left nothing behind but the smell of wet iron and the slow rusting of everything humanity had built here.

He turned back to his desk. The terminal's glow was the color of old honey, and on it waited Field Report 4,001 — no. He corrected himself with a small, tired smile. He had filed 4,000 yesterday, an anniversary he had marked alone with a glass of the colony's thin white wine and a toast to an audience of zero. Today's would be 4,001.

*Subject: phonemic drift in settler dialects, third generation,* he dictated, and the machine transcribed his voice into text with the gentle competence of something that would never judge him. *Continued erosion of the English dental fricative among children under twelve. The sound "th" is becoming "t" or "f" depending on social context. I note this without alarm. Languages are rivers; they carve new channels when the old ones silt up.*

He paused. Outside, the rain kept its rhythm against the pane.

*I note this without alarm,* he repeated, *and observe that I am the only person on this colony who finds it interesting.*

That was the truth of it, laid flat like a specimen on a slide. Forty-one years of reports. Grammar surveys, lexicon inventories, recordings of lullabies sung by women whose grandmothers had come here on the first ships. He had documented the birth of a dialect and, if the ecological assessments were to be believed, he would document its death as well — a language dying not by conquest or catastrophe but by simple abandonment, the way a garden dies when the gardener stops coming. His archive had no confirmed recipients. The transmission buoy at the system's edge still blinked its green confirmation light every time he uploaded, but green lights were cheap. A relay could blink forever into a dead channel and never know the difference.

He had made his peace with it years ago, or something adjacent to peace — the dignity of work done for no one, which is its own strange sacrament. A priest does not stop saying mass because the pews are empty. A lighthouse keeper does not stop trimming the wick because the shipping lanes have moved. And a xenolinguist, stranded on a forgotten colony at the ass-end of human expansion, does not stop recording the way people talk, because the record itself is the point. Someday, someone might read it. Or no one might, and it would still have been worth doing.

He finished the report, encrypted it, and sent it up through the atmosphere to the buoy, watching the progress bar crawl with the familiar mixture of hope and resignation. Then, because the rain showed no sign of stopping and the day stretched ahead of him like an empty corridor, he opened the secondary archives.

This was his vice, if a man could be said to have vices who had no witnesses. The decommissioned ship records. Vesper's Reach had once been a waypoint — that much of the official history was true — and in its heyday, before the trade routes bent away and the money dried up, it had maintained a fleet of seventeen vessels: ore haulers, survey craft, two passenger shuttles with names too optimistic for their fates. All of them were grounded now, hulks in the maintenance yards south of the settlement, slowly becoming geography. Their records lived in the deep storage tier of the archive, and Mirabel read them the way other men read novels. There were stories in them. A fuel log that stopped mid-entry because the pilot had died at his post. A cargo manifest listing six hundred crates of seed grain that had rotted in a hold while the colonists outside went hungry, because some administrator on Earth had frozen the release order and then forgotten the colony existed. Human folly, preserved in perfect digital amber.

He pulled up the registry index and let his eyes wander down the list of names. *Aldrin's Step. The Cartographer. Good Intent.* That last one always made him smile — someone in the founding generation had possessed a sense of humor dry enough to strike sparks.

He reached the M's. *Meridian.*

The name snagged something in him, a burr caught on cloth. He knew the Meridian — or knew of her. Deep-haul vessel, one of the last commissioned before the withdrawal, berthed in Yard Three where he'd walked past her hull a hundred times on his rambles. She had never flown. Not once. Her keel had been laid, her systems installed, her crew assigned, and then the withdrawal orders had come down from Earth like a curtain falling, and she had sat in her cradle ever since, forty years of rain sliding off her unbroken seal. The children used her as a landmark. *Meet me at the Meridian,* they said, the way children elsewhere said *meet me at the clock tower.* She was furniture. She was scenery. She had never flown.

So why, he wondered, did the file feel warm?

It was a foolish, unscientific thought, and he noted it as such even as he opened the manifest. But there it was: the crew roster, forty-two names, each with assignment codes and clearance levels, frozen on the day the fleet stood down. Standard. Expected. Except —

His finger stopped on the screen.

*Lena Vasquez. Chief Translator, First Class. Clearance: Obsidian.*

He read it again. Chief Translator. On a ship that had never launched. Clearance Obsidian — a level so far above anything on Vesper's Reach that Mirabel had only ever seen it referenced in historical documents about the early Fleet Charter. Nobody on this colony held Obsidian clearance. Nobody had held it in living memory. The colony's entire administrative structure topped out at Cobalt, and Cobalt holders could requisition tractors and approve school curricula. Obsidian was the kind of clearance that existed in legends about the Founding Wars.

And yet there she was, inked into the permanent record: Lena Vasquez, assigned to a ship that had never left its cradle, holding a rank that belonged to another age of the world.

Mirabel sat back. The rain filled the silence. He told himself it was a clerical ghost — records from the withdrawal era were notoriously messy, whole departments dissolved mid-audit, data migrated across three generations of storage media with errors accumulating like genetic drift. He had found ghosts before. Names misspelled, dates impossible, a supply officer listed on four ships simultaneously. The archive was full of phantoms, and most of them meant nothing.

But he didn't close the file.

Instead he did what he always did with anomalies, the habit of a lifetime's discipline: he followed the data downstream. If Vasquez was a ghost, the ghost should end where the ship's story ended — at the stand-down order, dated forty years past, signed by a colonial administrator whose name Mirabel recognized from the cemetery stones. Everything after that date should be dust. Decommissioned vessels got decommissioned paperwork: a final inspection, a preservation protocol, and then nothing but the slow administrative silence of a thing waiting to be scrapped or forgotten.

He scrolled past the stand-down date.

The maintenance logs continued.

His breath went shallow without his permission. Weekly entries. Not monthly, not quarterly — weekly, regular as tides, stretching from the stand-down order down through four decades to a timestamp from eleven days ago. Eleven days. While the settlement's own infrastructure logged failures faster than the repair crews could walk to them — while the water reclamation plant cycled through its third cascade malfunction of the season — someone, somewhere, had entered a maintenance log for the Meridian:

*Systems nominal. Translation array calibrated. Signal integrity confirmed. — L.V.*

Translation array. He searched the Meridian's original specifications, and found the phrase again in the engineering annexes: a translation suite, listed among the ship's systems, flagged with a classification code he'd never seen attached to any actual hardware. And every week, for forty years, someone signing as L.V. had confirmed it was working.

On a ship that had never flown.

Mirabel became aware, gradually, of how dark the room had grown. The rain had thickened toward evening without his noticing; the honey-colored glow of the terminal was now the only light, and it lit his hands where they rested on either side of the keyboard like artifacts on a display shelf. He realized he had been sitting motionless for over an hour. His tea had gone from cold to room temperature to something that had surrendered entirely.

He ran the checks anyway, because rigor was the only religion he had left. Cross-referenced the log timestamps against the archive's own integrity records: no tampering flags, no gaps, no evidence of automated generation — these were hand-entered, each one, forty years of them, more than two thousand entries typed by human fingers. Checked the signature against personnel files. Lena Vasquez appeared exactly twice in the colony's records: once on the Meridian's manifest, and once on the original settlement census, sixty years ago, listed as arriving with the second wave. Age at arrival: thirty-four. Which meant that if she still lived — if the initials L.V. belonged to living hands — she would be ninety-four years old.

Ninety-four, and calibrating translation arrays every week on a ship that had never flown.

He closed his eyes and saw the maintenance yard in his mind's eye: the long grey shapes of the dead fleet under their tarps, the puddled ground, the gulls — no gulls here, he corrected himself, the native skimmers, pale and silent, wheeling over the hulls. Yard Three. The Meridian, vast and sealed and never awake. He had walked past her a hundred times and felt nothing, because there was nothing to feel. A monument to a future that had been cancelled.

Except monuments did not file weekly reports.

There was a version of this that was innocent. He constructed it carefully, the way he constructed grammars from fragmentary corpora, testing each hypothesis against the evidence. Perhaps an old woman — a former crew member, marooned by the withdrawal like everyone else — had simply never stopped her routines. Grief does that. Ritual does that. A sailor abandoned ashore might well keep her logs out of devotion, or senility, or love, entering her weekly confirmations into a system that no longer listened, a private liturgy performed for a god that had stopped existing. It would be sad. It would be human. It would mean nothing beyond one woman's long fidelity to a dead ship.

It was a good hypothesis. It was also wrong, and he knew it was wrong, and what he knew it from was the word *calibrated.*

You did not calibrate an array on a ship that had no power. He checked. The Meridian drew nothing from the settlement grid — her berth was listed as cold, unpowered, sealed. Yet the logs spoke of signal integrity confirmed, of calibration runs, of a translation array being actively maintained at operational readiness for four decades. You could perform rituals on a dead ship. You could not calibrate equipment on one. Those verbs required electricity, required functioning hardware, required — the thought arrived with the weight of a dropped anchor — required a purpose.

Someone was keeping something alive in Yard Three. Something that needed a translator. Something important enough to warrant Obsidian clearance, important enough to hide inside a ship everyone believed was a corpse, important enough that its caretaker had spent forty years maintaining the fiction of the fiction — a dead ship within a dying colony, both of them pretending, while the rain came down.

And the settlement's official story — the story Mirabel had lived inside for twenty years, the story of a forgotten waypoint quietly failing, unwitnessed, unimportant, archived by one lonely man for no one — that story now had a crack running through it, hairline and clean, like the first fracture in a dam.

He looked at his reflection in the darkening window: an aging man, silver at the temples, lit faintly blue by a screen full of impossibilities. Behind the reflection, the lights of the settlement were coming on one by one, small and warm and ordinary — the school, the mess hall, the homes of people planting crops in soil that grew more toxic every year, raising children in a place the universe had stopped answering. They deserved the innocent version. He hoped, genuinely hoped, that the innocent version was true.

But curiosity, in Mirabel Fairweather, was not a feeling. It was a discipline. And the discipline had already begun assembling its questions: Where in Yard Three did the power come from? Who else knew? Why a translator — translators translated between parties, and between whom, out here at the edge of everything, did anyone need translating?

He saved his queries to a private partition, encrypted it with a key he had never had occasion to use, and sat for a long moment in the humming dark, listening to the rain.

Then he stood, took his coat from the hook, and decided that tomorrow he would take his afternoon walk somewhere new.

Tomorrow, he would walk to Yard Three.

* * *

That night, for the first time in months, Mirabel dreamed — and the dream was not in English.
