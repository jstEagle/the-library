## 4. What the Machine Says

The corridor to the sealed wing smelled of ozone and old water, and Lena walked it the way other people walked into churches.

Mirabel followed her past three bulkheads that should not have opened for her—she touched each lock with a flat black token she kept in her coat pocket, and each door sighed apart as though it had been waiting years for exactly that touch—and he found himself cataloguing details the way he always did when his body wanted to panic: the hairline crack in the ceiling panel running left to right like a fault line; the dust on the emergency lighting housings, undisturbed in its even gray blanket except where her shoulder had brushed it a thousand times; the way the air grew warmer, wetter, more alive with every meter they advanced, until by the fourth bulkhead it carried something organic, something almost sweet, like the inside of a seedpod.

"You're quiet," Lena said, without turning around.

"I'm deciding which question to ask first."

"Ask them all. You will anyway." She stopped before a final door, wider than the others, its surface unmarked by any designation. No stencil, no warning stripe, no serial number. In a colony obsessed with stenciling everything—even the compost bins had manifest codes—this blankness was itself a kind of signature. "But once you're through this door, Mirabel Fairweather, you can't be a man who doesn't know anymore. That's the toll."

He thought about saying something clever. He was, by temperament and training, a man who reached for cleverness the way drowning men reach for driftwood. But the old woman stood there with her hand flat against the blank door, and the warmth of the air beyond it pressed against them both like breath, and what came out of him instead was simply: "I haven't slept properly in six years. I think I gave up being that man a long time ago."

Something moved across her face—not quite approval, not quite grief. Recognition, maybe. She turned the token over twice in her fingers, an old ritual worn smooth, and pressed it home.

* * *

The chamber beyond was not a room so much as a held breath.

It was vast—the size of the colony's hydroponics bay, maybe larger—and it was full of the Machine.

Mirabel had spent his career among language. He had parsed Kessari trade-chants from intercepted traffic, mapped the tonal grammar of Ondu mourning songs, written four thousand field reports on phonemes no human mouth could make. He had imagined, in the way lonely men imagine things, that when he finally encountered true alien communication it would look like something: a signal, a script, a structure he could diagram on a slate.

What filled the chamber looked like a garden that had decided to become an organ.

Ribs of some pale material—bone-colored, faintly translucent—arced up out of the floor in ranks, twenty meters high, and between them hung membranes like the sails of drowned ships, shivering though there was no wind. Veins ran through everything, thick as his wrist, carrying not blood but light: a slow amber pulse that traveled the length of the chamber and back again, patient as tide. The floor beneath his boots was warm and gave slightly, like skin over muscle. And everywhere, everywhere, was sound—or nearly sound—a pressure below hearing, a hum he felt in his molars and the soft tissue behind his eyes.

"It's alive," he said. His voice came out flattened, reverent, ridiculous.

"It's dying," Lena said. "There's a difference, and we'll get to it." She walked forward into the ribs, and the membranes parted for her the way the doors had, folding aside with a wet whisper. "Come here. Stand where I can see your face. I want to watch you understand it."

He stepped off the floor's edge—there was a walkway, a human walkway, narrow plating bolted between two of the great ribs, installed by hands decades dead—and followed her to a console that jutted from one pale flank like a barnacle. It was ancient human technology, pre-colonial by its styling, its keys worn to gloss by fingertips long gone. Above it, embedded in the Machine's flesh, a display glowed with columns of characters that made his trained heart lurch: Kessari glyph-streams on the left, Ondu wave-notation on the right, and between them a third column, translating both ways, that read like nothing he had ever seen—neither fleet's system, but something native to the Machine itself, a private alphabet grown in the dark.

"What is it?" he asked, knowing already that the question was too small.

"The last honest thing anyone built out here," Lena said. "Before the war scared everyone honest things away." She laid her hand on the console's edge, gently, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal you weren't sure still trusted you. "Two hundred years ago, when Vesper's Reach was founded, nobody came here to farm failing soil, Mirabel. They came because this thing was already here. It predates us. It predates the fleets' arrival, probably—it was listening to *something* before either fleet ever entered this system. When the Kessari and the Ondu found each other, and found that they could not speak to one another without it ending in fire, someone had the wit to realize this place already knew how to listen to both of them."

"A translator."

"Not a translator." Her voice sharpened, and he realized the sharpness was love. "A translation engine. Living. It doesn't convert words, it converts *intention*. It takes what the Kessari mean and renders it as what the Ondu would mean, if they meant it. Do you understand what that is? Not the words. The meaning underneath, the shape of the wanting. There has never been another instrument like it in recorded space. And for a hundred and eighty years, every message between those two fleets passed through this chamber. Every treaty. Every apology. Every threat carefully softened and every insult carefully sharpened, because sometimes peace needs a blade shown at the right moment. Both fleets believed they were talking to each other." She paused. "They were talking to this."

Mirabel looked up at the membranes, shivering in their silence, and felt the vertigo open beneath him like a shaft.

"And now?"

Lena was quiet for a moment. The amber pulse traveled the length of the chamber, arrived, departed. Somewhere deep in the structure, something clicked—a small, arrhythmic sound, like a joint going stiff.

"Now," she said, "it mostly sleeps. It began failing thirty years ago. Some infection of its own biology, or simple age—I don't have the science to know, and neither did anyone else, because everyone who did was evacuated or died. The founders' protocol was explicit: if the Machine fails, the colony broadcasts its failure to both fleets and stands aside while they negotiate directly, or don't." Her mouth twisted. "But the Machine didn't fail all at once. It failed slowly. And in the gap between 'failing' and 'failed,' I made a decision."

She touched the console, and the third column—that private alphabet—began to move.

"I type," she said. "Every week, on schedule, both fleets send their messages, and the Machine's intake systems still work fine—it hears them, it logs them, you can see them there. But the output side is mostly dead. So I write the translations myself. I've been writing them for eleven years. I am the only person in this system who knows that the voice both fleets trust is an old woman with bad hands and a borrowed name."

The vertigo took him fully then. He gripped the console's edge and the metal was warm, blood-warm, and he thought absurdly: *I'm holding its hand.* Eleven years. Eleven years of forged diplomacy flowing through the veins of a dying god, and two fleets—two armadas, he corrected himself, remembering the intercepted traffic he'd mapped, the kill-capacities, the standing orders—he'd glimpsed in chapter after sleepless night of signal analysis—two armadas holding their fire because they trusted the word of this chamber, and the word of this chamber was Lena Vasquez, alone, typing.

"How," he said, and had to start again. "How have the fleets not noticed? A living engine's translations would have texture. Rhythm. Whatever it is they recognize in each other's meaning—you can't fake that. Can you?"

"No," Lena said simply. "You can't. Not fully. Which is why I keep the translations small. Bland. Confirmations, acknowledgments, procedural courtesies. For eleven years I have been the most boring diplomat in history, and it has worked precisely because neither fleet wants drama. They want the channel to exist. Existence is the message. As long as something answers, they believe the peace holds, and as long as they believe the peace holds, it does." She looked at him, and her eyes in the amber light were fierce and exhausted. "I learned that from her. From the real Lena."

"The real—" He caught himself. Filed it. There were too many doors opening at once, and he could hear, behind all of them, the next chapter of this place waiting. "Later. Tell me later."

"There may not be a lot of later," she said, without self-pity, as though reporting weather. "I'm eighty-one. Now—" she straightened, and the diplomat's spine reassembled itself inside the old woman's frame, vertebra by vertebra, "—you said you were a xenolinguist. Four thousand reports. Show me."

"Show you what?"

"That ear of yours." She gestured at the intake column, the Kessari glyphs scrolling in their slow procession. "This came in nine hours ago. Priority-flagged by their protocols. I've been staring at it since dawn and I cannot tell whether it's routine or whether it's the beginning of the end. My Kessari was always secondhand—learned from her notes, never from the source. You've been listening to their raw traffic for six years." Her hand trembled slightly as she pointed, and she steadied it against the Machine's warm flank, and Mirabel understood suddenly that she touched it constantly, that the Machine was the only living thing she spoke to, that these two dying organisms had been keeping each other company. "Read me its mood."

He turned to the glyphs. And the strangest thing happened, the thing he would remember afterward in dreams and in the space between dreams: the alien notation, which he had studied from intercepts for six years, seemed to open for him the way the doors had opened for her token. Not fluency—never fluency—but cadence. He could feel the rhythm of it, the way you can feel the rhythm of a language you don't speak, the grief or anger or boredom riding under the syllables.

"It's formal," he said slowly. "Ceremonial register—they use this construction for anniversaries and memorials. But the phrasing is..." He leaned closer. The glyphs pulsed, and beneath the hum of the chamber he felt—for half a heartbeat, unmistakable—a resonance answer from deep in the Machine's body, a single low tone like a struck bell heard through water. He froze. Lena did not appear to notice, or appeared very well practiced at not noticing. "The phrasing is repetitive. Almost liturgical. They're... commemorating something. A date. An event." He looked up. "Lena, what happened on this date, historically? Because they're marking it like a wound."

Her face went still in a way that told him she had known, and had hoped he wouldn't ask.

"That," she said quietly, "is the anniversary of the Skirmish at Threnody Drift. The only exchange of fire there has ever been between the fleets. Forty-one thousand dead in ninety minutes, before the Machine's first translators brokered ceasefire. Both fleets mark it every year. Both fleets send their remembrance through this chamber, so that each may know the other remembers too." She exhaled. "For eleven years I have translated their memorial rites. It is the one transmission a year where I permit myself something close to eloquence, because blandness would be an obscenity, and because—" her voice caught, recovered, "—because I think someone should say it properly, even if no one real is saying it."

Mirabel stared at the glyphs, at the ceremonial grief of beings forty light-years of culture distant from anything human, and felt the full weight of the chamber settle onto his shoulders: the treaties, the forty-one thousand dead whose deaths had bought this fragile machinery of trust, the millions who lived downstream of an old woman's typing.

And beneath that weight, another sensation, quieter and far more frightening. The tone he'd felt answering from the Machine's depths. It had not felt mechanical. It had not felt random. It had felt—attentive. Like something asleep that had turned toward a familiar voice.

"Lena," he said. "When the Machine answered just now. Did you—"

"I felt it." She was looking up into the ribs, into the slow amber tide. "It does that, sometimes. Little echoes. Ghosts of function. The doctors call it reflex activity in dying nerves." She said it steadily, but her hand on the console had tightened. "It doesn't mean anything."

Mirabel said nothing. He was thinking about his dreams—the phonemes that had colonized his sleep, the alien cadences that had driven him half-mad with loneliness for six years, that he had blamed on isolation, on obsession, on the slow erosion of a mind with no one to talk to.

He was thinking that the sealed wing sat directly beneath the archive annex where he slept.

He was thinking: *something down here has been talking in its sleep, and I have been its only audience.*

"Show me the output logs," he said. "All eleven years. Everything you've sent."

Lena turned to look at him fully then, and whatever she saw in his face made her go very careful. "Why?"

"Because if I'm going to hold the peace treaty in my hands," Mirabel said, "I want to know how heavy it actually is."

She studied him a long moment. Then, without a word, she keyed the console, and eleven years of the most consequential forgery in human history began to scroll up the screen—line after line after line, in two alien scripts and one dead woman's hand—

—and on the two-hundredth line, in the middle of a routine Ondu acknowledgment that Lena swore she had typed herself, Mirabel saw a character that did not belong. A single glyph, Kessari, embedded in an Ondu sentence, in a position that made no grammatical sense in either language.

Unless, he thought, his mouth going dry, unless it wasn't grammar at all.

Unless it was a signature.
