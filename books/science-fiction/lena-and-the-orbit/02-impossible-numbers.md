## 2. Impossible Numbers

The consortium's offices on Meridian Station occupied the top four floors of the Halcyon Spire, which meant they had the best view of anything on the station and the least interest in looking at it. The windows were polarized to a permanent soft gray, the way a mortuary keeps its light kind. Kestrel Hartwell had worked on the sixty-first floor for nine years and had never once seen Meridian's sun rise through them.

He liked it that way, mostly. Numbers didn't need daylight.

His office was a converted records vault — he'd requested it himself, back when he was still new enough to make requests that got granted — twelve meters by eight, no windows, walls lined with claim archives in climate-controlled drawers older than the war's end. The air tasted of ozone and cold paper. On his desk: a single lamp, a cup of tea gone lukewarm, and the accident files of the *Calloway Rose*.

He'd been staring at the same page for forty minutes.

Not because it was difficult. Because it was *clean*. And after nine years of adjusting claims in the shipping lanes between Meridian and Calloway, Kestrel had learned the first law of his trade: nothing real is clean.

The *Rose* was a grain hauler out of Calloway, lost with all hands eleven months ago near the navigation shadow the pilots called the Long Dark — the wide band of dead space between the two colonies where the beacon network thinned to nothing and ships ran on dead reckoning and prayer. Reactor cascade, per the salvage report. Total loss. Fourteen dead. Payout: 2.3 million standard, settled in nineteen days.

Nineteen days. Kestrel turned his pen over in his fingers. A total-loss settlement on a crewed vessel took a minimum of six weeks under consortium protocol — grief verification alone, next-of-kin confirmation, the mandatory waiting period for contested claims. Nineteen days meant someone upstairs had signed a fast-track waiver. Someone with authority.

He pulled the file apart the way he'd been trained to pull everything apart, gently, along its seams.

The waiver signature was a stamp code, not a name. Fine. That happened. He traced the disbursement. The payout hadn't gone to the fourteen families as a lump sum, the way crew-loss claims were structured. It had gone to an escrow agent — Meridian Consolidated Trust, account designation **VLT-0091** — which then disbursed to the families in installments. Also fine, technically. Some families preferred structured payouts. But the trust took a processing fee, and the fee schedule was wrong. Not fraudulent-looking wrong. Just *off* — four hundredths of a percent higher than the published rate, spread across every disbursement so thin that no grieving widow would ever notice, and no auditor would ever flag it, because four hundredths of a percent of anything looked like rounding error.

Kestrel noticed. Noticing was the whole job.

He sat back in his chair until the old springs creaked, and he let the itch start, because he'd learned not to fight it. The itch was the only honest colleague he had.

Four hundredths of a percent, skimmed off a 2.3 million payout, was pocket change. Nobody murdered fourteen people and faked a reactor cascade for pocket change. Which meant either the skim was incidental — a fee schedule quietly padded across hundreds of accounts, boring embezzlement, the kind that kept forensic accountants employed for decades — or the account itself was the point, and the money moving through it was just ballast.

There was one way to find out. You followed the account.

* * *

By the third hour, the tea was cold and the vault door was shut, which it wasn't supposed to be during working hours, and Kestrel had the consortium's entire claims ledger from the last six years open in a query window that would have made the compliance department weep.

He started with the account number. **VLT-0091**. Meridian Consolidated Trust was real — a licensed escrow house, forty years old, squeaky clean, the kind of institution whose name appeared on charitable endowments. Account 0091 had been opened nine years ago, one month after Kestrel joined the consortium, which meant nothing, which he told himself meant nothing, which did not stop the small cold feeling behind his sternum.

Nine years of activity. He ran the transaction history and watched the results scroll.

Mostly it was what you'd expect: structured settlements, estate disbursements, the quiet plumbing of other people's misfortune. Hundreds of entries. He filtered for outbound transfers to non-family recipients — payments that left the account for destinations that weren't the beneficiaries — and the list collapsed to thirty-one items over nine years. Consulting fees. Salvage arbitration costs. Vessel recovery expenses. All small. All documented. All routed through two secondary holding companies with names so generic they had to be deliberate: *Corridor Logistics Solutions*, and *Longwatch Holdings*.

Kestrel typed both names into the corporate registry and got back exactly what he expected to get back: incorporation certificates, registered agents, tax compliance stamps. Everything in order. Everything *bland*. Companies like this were called shelf companies in the trade, and the good ones were built to be found — because an investigator who finds a shell company feels clever, closes the thread, and moves on. The trick wasn't hiding the shell. The trick was making the shell interesting enough to satisfy you.

He almost fell for it. He wants that noted, later, when everything came out — that he almost fell for it, and what stopped him was not brilliance but boredom. He'd run this exact query pattern maybe four thousand times in his career, and something in the rhythm of the results felt rehearsed, like a witness whose story never changes wording. Real financial histories stutter. They have gaps, late filings, amended returns, a director who resigns in year three for personal reasons. These two companies had none. Nine years of flawless, frictionless existence.

Nobody was that clean. Except the dead, and the fictional.

So he went sideways instead of down. He stopped asking who owned the shells and asked a question nobody ever asked because it was too tedious: *what else touched them?* Every transaction has two ends. He pulled every inbound payment the shells had ever received, from any source, and cross-referenced the senders against the full consortium claims database.

Three hits.

Not consulting fees. Not arbitration costs. Three *claims*. Three separate accidents, three separate vessels, three separate years — and all three payouts, at some point in their disbursement chain, had passed through accounts connected to Corridor Logistics Solutions or Longwatch Holdings.

Kestrel printed the three files. There was still something in him that trusted paper more than screens, a habit from his mother, who had drawn her engineering schematics by hand long after everyone else had gone digital, and who used to say that a computer will show you what you asked for but paper shows you what's there. He laid the three claim folders side by side on his desk, weighted at the corners with his tea tin, a stapler, and a paperweight shaped like a lighthouse that he'd owned since he was six and had never thought about once in thirty years until this exact moment, when he looked at it and felt, absurdly, like it was watching him back.

The *Calloway Rose*. Grain hauler, reactor cascade, Long Dark, fourteen dead, eleven months ago.

The *Perpetua*. Passenger-freight hybrid, hull breach near the same coordinates, nine dead, three years ago. Cause listed as micrometeorite strike — statistically plausible; the Long Dark collected debris the way gutters collect leaves.

The *Anselm's Hope*. Survey vessel, total systems failure, seven dead, six years ago. Cause listed as navigational error in beacon-shadow conditions.

Three ships. Twenty-nine dead. Six years. And every single loss within a corridor of space barely wider than the ships themselves — the stretch of the Long Dark that charts marked, in the old survey language nobody used anymore, with a symbol that meant *unreliable instruments*.

Kestrel stood up and walked a slow circuit of his vault, because his body needed to move while his mind refused to. The pattern assembled itself in the air behind his eyes, unbidden, the way patterns do when you've fed them enough.

Three accidents. Different causes on paper — cascade, breach, failure. But the same region. The same settlement structure. The same escrow account. The same two shell companies skimming their invisible fraction off each payout. And here was the detail that turned the itch into a wound: the *settlement speed*. Nineteen days for the *Rose*. Twenty-two for the *Perpetua*. Seventeen for the *Anselm's Hope*. Fast-tracked, all three, all with stamp-code waivers, all signed under the same delegation authority — and when he pulled the delegation registry, the authority traced back to a regional claims directorate that had been dissolved four years ago, which meant the last two waivers had been signed by a ghost.

The numbers didn't lie. The numbers never lied. That was the whole reason he'd chosen this work over the work his mother had wanted for him, over engines and orbits and the beautiful hard physics she'd tried to teach him at their kitchen table with salt shakers for satellites. Numbers were the only witnesses that couldn't be intimidated, bought, or buried at sea. People lied. Institutions lied. Paper trails lied, if you let them lead you by the nose. But arithmetic held still and told the truth while everything around it rotted.

Twenty-nine people were dead, and the same quiet account had touched all three funerals, and somewhere in the consortium's upper floors somebody had signed those settlements faster than grief legally allowed.

That was as far as the numbers went. Past that line, there were only people. And people were the problem.

* * *

Vess Ondine kept an office on the floor above with actual windows, unpolarized, and a view of the docking ring that she never looked at either. She was fifty-something, silver at the temples, with the unhurried voice of a woman who had spent thirty years delivering bad news to people paid to receive it well. She had been Kestrel's supervisor for six of his nine years, and in that time she had defended his findings against three separate corporate challenges, backed him in a dispute that cost the consortium a seven-figure settlement, and once told him, over drinks neither of them finished, that he was the best adjuster she'd ever supervised and the worst employee, and that she valued both qualities in roughly equal measure.

She read the file he brought her in silence. All of it. She was one of the few people in the building who actually read things, and Kestrel had always counted that as a point in the world's favor.

When she finished, she closed the folder, squared its edges against the desk, and did not look up for a long moment.

"Walk me through it," she said. "Plainly."

"Three total losses over six years, all in the same corridor of the Long Dark. All settled through Meridian Consolidated Trust, account 0091. All fast-tracked under a delegation authority that was dissolved before the most recent two were signed. The disbursement chain runs through two shell companies that are too clean to be real. Somebody is skimming a fraction of a percent off every payout, and the skim is too small to matter, which means it isn't the point." He paused. "The point is the account exists, and twenty-nine deaths have passed through it."

Vess nodded slowly, the way she did when she was giving herself time. "And your conclusion?"

"That it needs a full forensic audit. Independent. Not internal — whoever signed those waivers had internal access. I want outside investigators, and I want the *Rose*, *Perpetua*, and *Anselm's Hope* files sealed pending—"

"Kestrel."

One word. His name, in her flat supervisor's voice, and he stopped, because in six years he had heard her use that voice perhaps four times, and it had always preceded something he didn't want to hear.

She finally looked up at him, and what was in her face wasn't the bureaucratic stonewalling he'd braced for. It was something worse. It was care.

"Close the file," she said gently.

He blinked. "You haven't even—"

"I read every word. I read it twice." She pushed the folder back across the desk toward him, and her hand stayed on it a moment longer than necessary, as if holding it down. "It's a good catch. It's a genuinely excellent catch — the fee schedule, the delegation ghost, all of it. Textbook. If you brought me a version of this file about a dockside cargo dispute, I'd have auditors crawling over it by lunch." She exhaled. "But these are total-loss crew claims from the Long Dark, and the Long Dark claims go through the Special Risk Directorate, and the Special Risk Directorate does not answer to me, or to you, or frankly — and I say this with love — to anyone I've ever been able to find."

"Then we escalate past them. Charter compliance—"

"Reports to the board. The board chartered the directorate." She said it without drama, without conspiracy, in the tired tone of a woman reciting an org chart she'd memorized against her will. "Kestrel. Sit down."

He sat.

"You've been here nine years," Vess said. "You're the best I have. So believe me when I tell you how this goes. You file your audit request tonight. By morning it's been reassigned. By afternoon you're invited to a meeting where a very polite person from Legal explains that your methodology has jurisdictional problems. Within the week, your caseload gets restructured — nothing punitive, nothing you can grieve, just a slow redistribution of everything interesting you were working on until your days are spent adjusting water-damage claims on Calloway fish freezers. I have watched it happen to two other adjusters in my tenure, and both of them were half the investigator you are, which is why it took them a year to figure out what was happening and yours will take a week." She folded her hands. "I'm telling you this because I am asking you, as a favor I will never repay and you will never know the size of: close the file. Mark it resolved. Contested-claim risk, insufficient evidence, whatever language you like. Put it in a drawer. And in five years, when you've got your own floor and your own window, you'll be glad you learned which fights aren't yours."

The vault-quiet of her office stretched out. Somewhere below them, a freighter docked, and the whole spire carried a faint subsonic shudder through the floors, like the building clearing its throat.

"And if I don't?" Kestrel said.

Vess held his gaze, and for a moment — half a second, no more — something moved behind her eyes that looked almost like fear, quickly filed away. "Then you'll be doing what you think is right," she said, "and I will be unable to protect you from the consequences of it, and I want you to understand that I mean *unable* in the most literal sense available to me. There are parts of this building I don't have clearance to know exist. Please don't make me learn whether they know about you."

He should have closed the file. He knew that even then, sitting in her office with the folder between them like a body on a table. Nine years of institutional instinct said close it, mark it, drawer it, survive. The consortium was not a villain in his mind; it was weather. You adjusted claims inside the storm because the storm was where the work was, and you didn't argue with weather.

But twenty-nine people. Three ships. One account. And a supervisor who was scared — not of him, not of the paperwork, but of something she wouldn't name, in a building she'd served for thirty years.

"The numbers don't lie, Vess," he said quietly.

"No," she agreed, and picked up her pen, and became again the composed woman with the window she never looked out of. "They don't. People do. That's rather the whole tragedy of the profession, isn't it?"

He took the folder back. In the corridor outside her office, under lights calibrated to soothe, he stopped and looked down at it — three names, twenty-nine dead, one account number — and understood that he had already decided, whatever he told himself on the walk back to his vault.

That night he didn't close the file. He copied it — twice, onto media that didn't touch the consortium's network — and then, because the account number had lodged in him like a splinter, he did one more thing. He ran a public-records sweep on the Long Dark corridor itself, pulling incident reports, salvage notices, distress logs, anything at all from the region around those three wrecks.

Mostly it was noise. Debris advisories. Beacon maintenance schedules. And then, buried in a Meridian civic feed from eight months ago, a human-interest piece he'd have scrolled past any other week: a primary school teacher named Amara Osei reporting that a cluster of her students had begun sharing what she delicately called *a recurring imaginative motif* — the same image, drawn independently by children who swore they'd never spoken of it. A tower of light, standing alone in the dark.

The article included one of the drawings, reproduced in grayscale.

Kestrel sat very still in his windowless vault, in the lamplight, looking at a child's crayon rendering of a slender tower radiating soft lines into blackness — and felt the hair rise on his arms, because he had seen that shape before. Not in a claims file. Not anywhere in his adult life.

In his mother's handwriting, on the corner of a schematic he'd found as a boy, in a box he'd never been able to bring himself to throw away.
