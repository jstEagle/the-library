## 3. Children of Two Worlds

The rain on Meridian fell in long silver threads that morning, and Amara Osei opened the classroom windows anyway, because the children had been restless for nine days and stale air made them worse.

She had taught at the Larkspur Commons School for eleven years, long enough to know that restlessness moved through a classroom like weather. It had sources she could name — the anniversary of the Blockade, the rationing notices pasted to the market boards, fathers home from the convoy runs with that particular silence in their shoulders. But this was different. This restlessness had no source she could point to. It had arrived all at once, the way a fever arrives, and it had settled over her students like something patient.

"Amara-teacher," said Juno Reyes, who was seven and had never once in her life used a full sentence when a fragment would do, "do towers dream?"

Amara paused with the attendance ledger half-open. "Towers don't dream, little one. Towers just stand."

"This one doesn't." Juno's dark eyes were perfectly serious, perfectly clear, the way they always were when she said the strange things. "It stands in the dark and it sings and the singing is a dream and we're all inside it."

"Inside the tower?"

"Inside the singing." Juno said it the way you'd say *inside the room*, as though it were the most obvious architecture in the world, and then she went back to her drawing, because Juno Reyes had been drawing the same thing every morning for nine days and did not intend to stop.

Amara walked the rows slowly. Thirty-one children, ages six through ten, in a room that smelled of wet wool and chalk dust and the lemon oil she rubbed into the old desks to keep them from splitting. And on thirty-one sheets of paper, in thirty-one hands — some careful, some furious, some still working out which way a line should bend — the same image.

A tower of light, standing in darkness.

It was never a lighthouse. She had checked that first, because lighthouses were in the primers and the storybooks and it would have been so much easier if it were a lighthouse. It was not a beacon tower either, not the relay masts that studded Meridian's southern coast and appeared in every civics textbook with the flag of the Provisional Assembly flying from its base. This tower had no flag, no base, no ground at all. It stood in a blackness that the children rendered differently — some with scribbled void, some with a deep unbroken blue, one, little Tomas Iwu, with actual black paint he had traded two dessert rations to obtain — but the tower itself was always the same. Tall and impossibly slender, a spine of light, and around it, in nearly every drawing, small radiating arcs that the children variously called *singing*, *breathing*, or, in Juno's case, *the dream being loud*.

Nine days ago, none of them had drawn it.

Amara had told herself it was a fad at first. Children caught images from each other the way they caught colds; one vivid imagination could colonize a classroom in a week. She had taught herself not to be alarmed by synchrony. But fads decayed. Fads mutated — the tower would grow a door, then a face, then become a rocket, then be forgotten. This had not decayed. This had *refined*. The drawings were converging, day over day, like witnesses' accounts of the same event being hammered into agreement. By the sixth day, the proportions matched. By the eighth, the arcs around it matched — seven of them, always seven, though none of the children could say why seven. This morning, Juno had begun labeling hers, and the label was a string of characters that Amara did not recognize and that Juno swore she had not made up.

*They come with the song,* Juno had said, when asked. *The song has writing in it. You can hear the writing.*

Amara set the ledger down and stood at the window a moment, watching the rain stitch itself into the harbor. Beyond the breakwater, a Meridian convoy was making ready — three freighters in Assembly gray, riding low with grain and refined volatiles, bound for the long route around the dead zone that had sat between the two colonies for forty years like a wound no one would let close. The children of Meridian grew up knowing the shape of that wound the way children in older wars had grown up knowing the shape of the enemy's coast. Calloway was not on any map in her classroom. Calloway was a word adults lowered their voices around.

"Amara-teacher." That was Pell, ten years old, the oldest in the room, a serious boy with his mother's long face. He had not drawn today. He was holding his pencil the way you hold a tool you've suddenly become unsure of. "Is it wrong that we all see the same thing?"

"Why would it be wrong?"

"Because we're not supposed to share things with them." He said *them* the way the newsreaders said it, flat and careful, a word with a fence built into it. "But the dream isn't only ours. My cousin works the listening post at Cape Verrell, and he says—" Pell stopped, aware suddenly that he had said too much, the way children do, and looked down at his blank paper.

"Say what, Pell?"

He was quiet for a long moment. Rain ticked against the glass. Thirty-one drawings of the tower looked up at her from the desks.

"He says the post caught a signal last week. Very faint. From the dead zone." His voice dropped. "And the same night, everybody's kids started drawing the tower. On both sides."

*On both sides.* Amara felt the words go through her like a needle finding a nerve. She kept her face still, because a teacher's face is a public building and must not show its cracks. "Your cousin shouldn't repeat what he hears at the post," she said gently. "But thank you for telling me."

Pell nodded and, after a moment, began to draw the tower. He drew it well. He drew it exactly.

* * *

That evening she took the tram out to Cape Verrell to see her brother.

Dele Osei had spent nineteen years at the listening post, and the post had spent those years slowly absorbing him, the way the sea absorbs a jetty. He lived in a room the size of a storage locker with a window he never opened and a wall of salvaged equipment that hummed at all hours. When Amara knocked, he looked at her for a long time through the crack of the door before letting her in, which was new, and which frightened her more than anything he said afterward.

"You heard," he said.

"I heard that you heard."

He made tea because there was nothing else in the locker to offer, and they sat with the mugs between them while the equipment hummed its patient chord. Dele had their mother's hands, broad and careful, and he turned his mug around and around on his knee.

"Nine days ago," he said, "we caught something out of the dead zone. Direction and distance put it well past the old quarantine line. Past everything. There's nothing out there — you know that, everyone knows that, there's been nothing out there since the war. A derelict zone. The charts just say *unreliable*."

"What was it?"

"A signal. Narrow-band, repeating, structured. Not noise. Noise has a texture you learn to recognize, and this wasn't noise." He turned the mug. "It was old, Amara. That's the part I keep — the modulation scheme is forty years out of date. Nobody has broadcast like that since before the Blockade. It's like hearing a voice through a wall and realizing the voice is speaking a language your grandmother used."

"Could it be a ghost signal? An old beacon still cycling?"

"We thought so. I thought so. I filed it as a derelict emitter, low priority." He looked up, and his eyes were the tired eyes of a man who had spent two decades watching a horizon for enemies and had finally seen something he hadn't been watching for. "Then I ran the numbers on the decay. A derelict emitter running forty years on whatever power it had should be a whisper by now, if it's anything at all. This isn't decaying. It's *steady*. Steady like something is maintaining it. And the content—" He stopped.

"The content, Dele."

"It repeats. It's a loop, or it's a lesson, I don't know which. But the structure — the intervals —" He rubbed his face with both hands. "I played it for my daughter's age group at the community center. Just a fragment, just to see. Ama, I've been at this post nineteen years and I have never once heard anything from that direction that wasn't static or a warhead's transponder. And now there is a *voice* out there, and the same week it starts, the children on this side of the line start drawing a tower none of them has ever seen, and—" He faltered.

"And the children on the other side," Amara said quietly.

Dele was silent.

"I have a student," she said. "Pell. His cousin works your post. The boy told the whole class the dream isn't only ours." She leaned forward. "Dele. Is it true? Is it on Calloway too?"

Her brother looked at the humming wall, at the window, at his tea. When he finally spoke, his voice had gone very careful, the voice of a man stepping out onto ice.

"Two nights ago, a Calloway fishing trawler — one of theirs, running dark near the quarantine line, which they do, which we all do — put out a distress call on the old common emergency band. Not the Assembly band. The *common* band. The one both sides used before the war." He swallowed. "The distress wasn't mechanical failure. The call said their boy — the captain's son, seven years old — had woken up screaming coordinates. Repeating them. Over and over. Numbers that matched, to the third decimal, the bearing and range of our signal. Our *signal*, Ama. A child on Calloway, who has never seen our charts, screaming the exact position of the thing in the dead zone."

The rain had stopped. In the silence, Amara could hear the equipment humming its patient chord, and underneath it, faintly, she could almost believe she heard something else — something structured, something waiting.

"What did the Assembly do with the report?"

"The report doesn't exist." Dele's mouth twisted. "The post got a directive the next morning. The signal is reclassified as atmospheric anomaly. All raw data is to be forwarded to Consortium oversight and deleted locally. Consortium, Amara. Not the Assembly. The *insurers* are classifying what comes out of the dead zone." He laughed, and there was no humor in it at all. "I forwarded the data. I did not delete it. I want you to know that, because I think — I don't know what I think. But I think someone is going to come asking who kept copies, and I want my sister to know her brother kept copies."

Amara sat with that. Through the window, the harbor lights of Cape Verrell burned in their long reflection, and beyond them the sea ran black to the horizon, and beyond the sea, somewhere out in that blackness past the quarantine line, a tower of light stood in the dark and sang — and children on two worlds that had not spoken in forty years were hearing it, together, in their sleep.

"Why children?" she said at last. It was the question she had been carrying all day, the one the teacher in her could not put down. "Why would a signal from a dead relay — if it is a relay, if anything is out there at all — why would it go to *children*?"

Dele was quiet for a long time. The equipment hummed.

"Maybe it's not going to them," he said finally. "Maybe it's been going to everyone, for a long time, very quietly. And children are just the only ones who haven't been taught not to listen."

* * *

She walked back to the tram stop along the seawall, and the night was clear for the first time in a week, and the stars over the dead zone were hard and white.

At the stop, a woman she half-knew from the fish market was waiting, and after a while, in the way of people who have been holding something too long, she said, without preamble: "My boy drew it too. The tower. All his friends draw it. My sister's boy, up in Calloway—" she stopped herself, glanced around, lowered her voice. "We still get letters, you understand. Slow ones. Through neutral freight. My sister writes that her boy wakes at night and stands at the window pointing at nothing, saying *it's singing, it's singing, it wants us to hear the end of the song.*" The woman's hands were shaking. "The end of what song, Amara? What song has an end that a seven-year-old on the other side of the war is waiting for?"

The tram came, humming, its lights washing the seawall white. Amara boarded and found a seat and did not look at the woman again, because looking felt like agreeing to something she could not yet name.

She thought about her classroom. Thirty-one drawings, converging. Seven arcs, always seven. Juno's characters that came with the song. A boy on Calloway screaming coordinates into the dark. A signal forty years old, steady, maintained, *patient* — patient the way a teacher is patient, the way you repeat a lesson to a room that isn't ready, over and over, for years, waiting for one child, then ten, then all of them, to finally look up and say *I understand*.

The tram carried her back through the wet dark streets of Meridian, past the convoy lights and the Assembly banners and the ration notices, past the whole careful architecture of silence that two colonies had built between themselves and called peace. And Amara Osei, who had spent eleven years teaching children to read, understood with sudden and total certainty that something out at the edge of dead space had been teaching too — longer than she had, farther than she had, across a distance no schoolhouse could span — and that the lesson was almost finished.

At her door, she found an envelope slid under it. No stamp. No return address. Inside, a single sheet of paper, and on the paper, in a child's careful hand she did not recognize, the tower of light and its seven arcs — and beneath the drawing, the string of characters Juno had sworn came with the song.

Amara had seen those characters only once before in her life, twenty years ago, in a university engineering archive, on the signature block of a set of sealed schematics the archivist had refused to let her copy.

They were her mother's initials.

She stood in the doorway a long time, the paper trembling in the night air, while somewhere out past the quarantine line the tower sang its patient lesson, and somewhere on the far side of the war a boy woke screaming, and somewhere in a consortium office on the other side of the city, a man she had never heard of was beginning to ask why the numbers didn't add up.
