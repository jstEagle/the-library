## 13. The Relay Appears

There was nothing there.

Kestrel had learned to trust that word — *nothing* — the way other men trusted a handshake. In dead space, nothing was not emptiness; it was an assertion. The nav beacons ended four days behind them, and since then the hauler's instruments had reported a universe of clean, featureless dark, the kind of dark that made a claim adjuster's job easy because there was nothing in it to insure. No traffic. No debris worth pricing. No reason for forty years of accidents to have happened here at all.

Except the numbers said otherwise. The numbers always said otherwise.

"Third time you've checked," Idris Fen said from the pilot's cradle, not turning around. "It's still nothing."

"I like confirming my disappointments."

"You'd have loved being an actuary." She rolled the hauler fifteen degrees, lazy as a whale turning, and the starfield slid across the forward glass like water over stone. "Two more hours to Juno's coordinates. Then we find out what a nine-year-old's nightmare looks like from the outside."

Kestrel didn't answer. He was watching the long-range array feed, the one he'd patched himself two days ago with wire salvaged from the galley, because the ship's own systems kept failing out here in ways that followed no failure curve he'd ever priced. Comms first. Then thermal regulation, cycling hot when it should cycle cold. Then the drive governor, twice, each time for exactly eleven seconds — the same duration, every time, which was not how machines broke. Machines broke messily. This was rhythm.

This was interference.

He'd written it up in his head already, the way he wrote everything up: *Subject vessel experiences cascading subsystem failures consistent with active electromagnetic suppression originating from an uncharted source. Probability of coincidence: negligible.* Somewhere behind him, Amara Osei sat with Juno asleep against her shoulder, and every time the lights dimmed on their eleven-second cycle, the girl stirred and murmured coordinates in her sleep. Numbers Kestrel had cross-checked against his mother's blueprints until three in the morning, hands shaking, tea gone cold.

The blueprints put a structure at those coordinates. Officially, it had never been built. Unofficially, it had killed enough people to generate six figures in claims, all laundered through one shell account, all signed off by men who now wanted him dead or worse, silent.

*Nothing*, the instruments insisted.

Kestrel had stopped believing instruments somewhere around the widow Okonkwo's kitchen table, when she'd pressed her husband's logbook into his hands and said, *He heard it singing, Mr. Hartwell. Tell me what sings out there.*

* * *

Juno woke ninety minutes out, and she woke the way she always did now — all at once, upright, eyes fixed on a point slightly above the horizon of the real.

"It's turned toward us," she said.

Amara smoothed the girl's hair back from her forehead. "What has, love?"

"The tower." Juno's voice was flat and certain, the voice children use for facts adults have forgotten how to hold. "It was lying down inside the dark, and now it's standing up. It knows we're coming. It's been waiting so long it forgot it was waiting, and now it remembers."

Idris glanced back over her shoulder. "Kid talks like a salvage manifest."

"She talks like someone who's been dreaming the same dream for two years," Amara said quietly. There was a rawness under her composure that Kestrel recognized; it was the sound of a woman who had smuggled herself off a colony world in a cargo pod to protect one child and had, somewhere along the way, become responsible for the shape of a whole generation's sleep. "Every child on both worlds draws it the same way. A tower of light in darkness. Do you know how statistically impossible that is? I taught statistics before I taught primary levels. I ran the numbers myself."

"And?"

"And they don't lie," Amara said. "People do."

Kestrel felt the sentence land in his chest like a coin dropped down a well. He'd been telling himself a version of it for weeks — since Vess closed the file, since the enforcers gutted his office, since he'd found his mother's signature on schematics for a machine that officially did not exist. *The numbers don't lie, but people do.* He'd built a career on that principle. He was about to find out whether it worked in reverse — whether a structure could tell the truth for forty years while everyone around it lied.

"One hour," Idris said. "Everybody strap in. If this thing is half what the kid says, I want nobody floating when we start maneuvering."

Juno climbed into the jump seat beside Kestrel without asking, the way children do when they've decided you're furniture that can be trusted. She studied him with the frank appraisal of the very young.

"You look like her," she said.

His breath caught. "Like who?"

"The lady in the tower." Juno traced a shape on the armrest with one finger — a spiral, an orbit, a signature he'd seen inked in the corner of every blueprint in his mother's estate. "She walks up and up and up. In the dream. She's always climbing. She never gets tired."

"What does she do at the top?"

Juno frowned, reaching for something just past the edge of recall. "She holds the door open," she said finally. "So the light can get out."

The hauler's running lights flickered — eleven seconds — and came back.

* * *

Forty minutes out, the power readings began.

At first Kestrel assumed instrument error, because the alternative was absurd. The spectrometer registered an energy output at the target coordinates that scaled, as they closed distance, from *negligible* to *implausible* to a figure that made Idris swear in three dialects and recheck her own calibration twice. Nothing out here generated power. Dead space was dead precisely because nothing out there had a reason to burn fuel, and no supply line to burn it from. The nearest star was a red ember eleven light-years off, too far to harvest, too dim to matter.

And yet the number climbed.

"That's a city's output," Idris said. "That's Meridian's capital district on a winter night. From a derelict. That's—" She stopped, jaw working. "That's impossible."

"Everything about this file is impossible," Kestrel said. "I stopped flagging it as an anomaly around day nine. Now I treat it as the baseline."

"Comforting."

"It's not meant to be. It's meant to be accurate." He leaned closer to the display. "Look at the spectrum. It's not fission, not fusion, not solar collection — there's no collector geometry big enough anyway. The emission profile is steady-state. Flat. Like it's not drawing from a source at all."

"Then where's it coming from?"

Kestrel thought of his mother's marginalia, the cramped pencil notes in the blueprint margins he'd spent four nights decoding: *Power is not generated here. Power is agreed upon here.* He'd assumed it was engineering shorthand, some in-joke of hers from the design bureaus. Standing in front of a live readout that defied conservation of energy, he wasn't sure anymore.

"My mother had a theory," he said. "She never finished it."

"Mothers never finish anything," Idris said. "They just leave really good notes."

Twenty minutes out, the hull began to sing.

It started below hearing — a pressure in the teeth, a tremor in the fluid of the eyes — and rose slowly through the registers until the whole ship resonated with it, a tone so pure it didn't sound mechanical at all. It sounded choral. Amara pulled Juno close, and the girl's eyes went wide and bright, and she said, "That's it. That's the song," and started humming harmony with a machine that officially had never been built.

Kestrel stood at the forward glass with his hands braced on the frame, and let the sound move through him, and understood, with the terrible clarity that only comes after months of denial, that he had heard it before. Not out here. As a child. Hummed by a woman in a kitchen on Meridian while she stirred soup, a tuneless little melody she'd claimed she couldn't remember where she'd gotten. He'd carried it his whole life without knowing it was a frequency. Without knowing it was a lullaby sung by a structure across the gulf between stars, to anyone patient enough to hear.

*The relay singing,* the dead man's logbook had said. Everyone who'd come out here had heard it. And everyone who'd come out here had died, and the consortium had paid the claims, and called it accident, and called it weather, and called it cost of doing business.

"Contact," Idris said softly. "Bearing zero-one-nine relative. Range... range is wrong. Recalculating. Recalculating again." Her knuckles were white on the yoke. "Kestrel. It doesn't have a range. It has a *threshold*. One moment it's not there, and—"

The nothing resolved.

* * *

Later, Kestrel would try to describe it, and fail, and try again, and fail better. Every account he gave would be true and none would be sufficient, because human language was built for objects that arrived, and this did not arrive. It *condensed*. One instant the forward glass held starfield and honest vacuum, and the next instant the dark between the stars had acquired architecture, the way a photograph develops — not appearing but emerging, layer by layer, as if it had been there all along and the universe had simply lacked the resolution to show them.

It was vast.

That was the first fact, and it flattened every other thought. The relay was a spire — a tower, exactly as the children drew it, exactly as his mother had drafted it — rising out of a root structure that spread wider than Meridian's largest arcology. Kestrel's eye tried to measure it and kept sliding off, the scale refusing to resolve; there were no reference points, no familiar hulls or beacons to hang a comparison on, until Idris threw the hauler's running lights into strobe and threw a shadow of the ship against the spire's flank, and the shadow was a fleck. A grain of dust against a cathedral.

"Scale reference," Idris whispered. "We are four hundred meters of ship. That thing is—" She shook her head. "I'm not going to say the number. The number sounds insane."

"How long?" Amara asked.

"Forty kilometers. Maybe more. The base keeps going past sensor floor."

Forty kilometers of structure, hanging in dead space, lit from within. That was the second fact, the one that made Kestrel's trained mind rebel: it was *lit*. After forty years of silence, of blackout orders, of claims paid and witnesses buried, every line of the spire glowed with a soft amber radiance that pulsed in slow waves, bottom to top, like breath, like circulation, like a heartbeat measured in geological time. The light moved up the tower and gathered at the crown, where a ring of emitters — his mother's emitters, he knew their part numbers, he'd fallen asleep reading them — burned with a steadiness no forty-year-old hardware had any right to possess.

No decay. No degradation curve. No maintenance logs, no fuel runs, no supply chain, no crew. Just light, patient and continuous, burning through the longest silence humanity had ever imposed on itself.

"Power source," Idris said numbly. "Somebody tell me the power source."

"Agreed upon," Kestrel murmured.

"What?"

"Nothing. Old family joke." His throat was tight. "My mother designed the antenna lattice. Third-stage drawings, her signature, dated eleven months before the blackout order. She drew that crown." He pointed at the ring of fire at the spire's summit. "She drew it knowing she'd never see it finished. There's a note in the margin. *Build it well. They will need it longer than they think.*"

The hauler drifted closer, engines cold, riding momentum alone because Idris refused to run active systems near a structure that had, by every record Kestrel could reconstruct, reached out and killed every ship that ever approached it with intent. But the failures weren't happening now. The eleven-second cycles had stopped. The interference had ceased the moment the relay resolved — as if the suppression had never been hostile at all, merely the effort of something enormous holding its breath, waiting to be seen clearly before it spoke.

Or waiting to see who had come.

Juno pressed both palms flat against the glass. The amber light washed over her face in slow waves, and she was smiling, and it was the most frightening thing Kestrel had seen in weeks, because it was not the smile of a child looking at a machine. It was the smile of a child being looked at.

"She's glad," Juno said. "She waited so long. She thought everyone forgot."

"Who's glad?" Amara asked gently.

"The lady. Lena." The name came out of the girl easily, unforced, a word she'd been carrying in her sleep for years. "She says the door is open now. She says—" Juno tilted her head, listening to something inside the song. "She says *tell the man with her eyes that the climb is shorter than it looks.*"

Kestrel's legs went unreliable. He sat down hard in the jump seat, and Amara's hand landed on his shoulder, warm and steady, and none of them spoke for a long moment while the tower breathed its light across the glass.

Then Idris's console chirped — an actual chirp, cheerful and domestic and utterly wrong for dead space — and every screen aboard filled at once with a single line of text, rendered in a protocol forty years out of date, in a cipher Kestrel had last seen stamped on the cover sheet of his mother's blueprints.

**RECOGNITION: HARTWELL, E. — CHIEF ARCHITECT.**
**DESCENDANT SIGNATURE CONFIRMED.**
**WELCOME HOME. WELCOME BACK. THE TRANSMISSION IS INTACT.**
**PLEASE ADVISE: DO YOU COME TO LISTEN — OR TO FINISH WHAT THEY SENT YOU TO END?**

Behind them, at the edge of sensor range, a second drive flare bloomed in the dark.

Idris read it, and her face went gray. "That's the Corvid," she said. "Sable Renn just entered dead space. And she's not alone — there's a bigger burn behind her. Consortium class."

The tower's light pulsed once, top to bottom, and held — bright, expectant, patient as it had been for forty years.

Waiting, at last, for an answer.
