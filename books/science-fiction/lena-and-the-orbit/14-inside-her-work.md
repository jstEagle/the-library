## 14. Inside Her Work

The airlock cycled with a sound like a held breath released.

Kestrel stood in the vestibule of the relay and let the silence settle over him — not the dead silence of vacuum, but a living one, the hum of systems that had been running for forty years without a single hand to tend them. The deck vibrated faintly beneath his boots. Somewhere above him, coolant whispered through conduits. The relay was breathing.

He'd expected a tomb. He'd found a body still warm.

"Kestrel." Idris Fen's voice crackled through his suit comm, thin and staticky at this range. "You've got six hours before I burn fuel I don't have holding position. Whatever you're looking for in there, find it fast."

"I'm looking for my mother," Kestrel said, and cut the channel before Fen could answer.

* * *

The first corridor was exactly as she'd drawn it.

He knew that before he understood he knew it. The walls curved inward at shoulder height — not for aesthetics but for structural load distribution, a detail from blueprint sheet fourteen, revision three, the revision dated eleven days before the official project was declared cancelled and every record of it sealed. He had spent three nights with those schematics spread across his bunk on Fen's hauler, tracing lines by lamplight until her handwriting was as familiar as his own reflection. *Load-bearing arch, offset 40cm. Do not center. The math is not sentimental.*

He'd laughed when he read that. His mother, who had never once told him she loved him in words, had left instructions to her future self in the margins of engineering documents, and the instructions were always the same: trust the work, not the feeling; measure twice; the math is not sentimental.

But walking her corridors now, he understood something the blueprints couldn't say. She had built this place to be walked through by human beings. The ceiling height was generous where a worker would carry equipment. Handrails appeared precisely where a tired technician would need them, on the correct side for a right-handed person descending. There was a niche at the first junction, exactly deep enough for a thermos and a data slate, positioned so someone waiting on a diagnostic could sit and watch the stars through a viewport she had angled toward Meridian.

Toward home. Toward the colony she'd never seen again after the war closed the lanes.

Kestrel pressed his glove against the wall and felt the vibration of the living machine. "You were here," he said aloud, and his voice sounded absurd and small in the helmet, and then it didn't sound absurd at all, because of course she had been here. Every weld was a sentence. Every corridor was a letter addressed forward in time, and he was only now old enough to read it.

* * *

The main spine rose ahead of him like the inside of a throat — a vertical shaft ringed with gantries, lit by strips of pale gold light that pulsed slowly, rhythmically, once every four seconds. He counted. Four seconds. The same interval as the signal in the children's dream. The same interval as the pulse that had been bleeding through dead space into sleeping minds on two worlds for years, patient as tide.

She hadn't just built a transmitter. She'd built a lighthouse, and lighthouses are built to be found.

His suit lights caught dust motes drifting in the shaft — actual dust, disturbed by his passage after decades undisturbed, each particle turning slowly in the golden pulse like snow falling upward. He clipped his tether to the guide rail and began to climb.

The rungs were spaced for her stride. He knew this because his own stride matched it, because he had inherited her legs along with her stubbornness, and somewhere around the twentieth rung he stopped climbing with his arms and started climbing with something older than muscle, some inherited memory of ascent, as if the body remembered a woman it had never met carrying cable up these same rungs while the war burned forty light-years behind her.

Halfway up, he found the first log station.

It was a simple console set into the gantry, its screen dark, its housing furred with a fine grey bloom of oxidation — except where a hand would rest, where the surface was worn bright. Someone had touched this console thousands of times. He fit his fingers into the wear pattern without deciding to, and the screen woke.

Text scrolled up in an engineer's terse hand:

**DAY 1,204. Relay operational. Uplink nominal. Awaiting traffic.**

**DAY 1,206. Still awaiting traffic.**

**DAY 1,209. Requested status update from Meridian command. No response. Requested again.**

Kestrel's throat tightened. Day twelve hundred. Three years after construction. Three years of a finished machine calling out to a world that had been ordered to forget it existed.

He climbed on.

* * *

The logs continued at intervals up the spine, each station a little higher, each entry a little sparser, and reading them was like watching a woman age in fast motion — not in flesh but in hope. Early entries were procedural, dense with numbers. Middle entries grew conversational, as if the console had become the only listener: notes about the food stores, about the aurora visible through the upper viewport during a solar storm, about a birthday she'd marked alone with reconstituted cake and two candles because one candle seemed like giving up.

**DAY 2,847. Played chess against the navigation computer. It cheats. Or I do. Hard to tell anymore.**

**DAY 3,102. If anyone ever reads this: the relay works. That's the whole message. Everything else is weather.**

And then, near the top of the spine, where the golden light gathered thickest, the entries changed character entirely. Shorter. Faster. The handwriting of urgency translated into text.

**DAY 3,411. Intercepted burst transmission. Source: both fleets. Simultaneous.**

**DAY 3,411 (later). They're talking to each other. After nine years of nothing, both commands opened a channel and they are TALKING. Ceasefire terms. Verification protocols. This is real. This is finally real.**

**DAY 3,411 (later still). My orders are to log all traffic and maintain blackout protocol per Directive Ash. But Directive Ash assumed the traffic would be hostile. This isn't hostile. This is the opposite of hostile.**

Kestrel's hands had stopped moving on the rungs. He hung in the golden pulse, tether creaking, reading the same line over and over. *Directive Ash.* He'd seen the name before — buried in the consortium's accident files, stamped across the suppression memos Vess had tried to keep out of his hands. Directive Ash: the blackout order. The instruction that had turned a peace transmission into classified wreckage and a working relay into a ghost.

His mother had been standing at the exact point where the war could have ended.

He climbed faster now, hardly feeling the rungs, following her entries upward like following footprints through snow.

* * *

The final log station sat in a small chamber at the crown of the spine, beneath the antenna array itself. The room was round, domed, lined with the original insulation — and it was warm. Not heated-warm. Occupied-warm. The kind of warmth a space keeps when a human being lived in it long enough to leave something of herself in the air, though the air had long since been scrubbed and recirculated ten thousand times. Kestrel felt it anyway. Grief is not scientific.

Her last console dominated the chamber, larger than the others, flanked by physical storage cores in protective cradles — dozens of them, each labeled in her handwriting on adhesive tags gone amber with age. He pulled off his helmet, needing to breathe unfiltered air even if it was forty years stale, and read the labels by the light of the golden pulse.

Most were telemetry. Weather records. System diagnostics. Her whole solitary decade, archived in careful order.

But the core in the central cradle — the one wired directly into the antenna above, the one with a secondary manual broadcast lever beside it, a lever designed to be thrown by hand — bore a different label. The handwriting was hurried, the ink doubled over where the pen had pressed hard:

**FOR WHOEVER COMES AFTER. PLAY THIS.**

Kestrel stood very still.

Then he sat down in the operator's chair — her chair, molded by years of her weight into a shape that accepted his immediately, as if the seat had been waiting for a particular skeleton and recognized the family resemblance — and keyed the playback.

Audio only. No video. Just voices, degraded by distance and encryption-stripping, restored by whatever miracle of engineering she'd performed in this dome.

First, a man's voice, raw with exhaustion: *"This is Chancellor Adeyemi of Meridian. To the Calloway Provisional Authority. We know you can hear this. Our cities are burning and yours will be next and there is no version of the next ten years where either of us is still standing. I am authorized to offer full ceasefire. Immediate. Unconditional. Verify anything you need to verify. Just stop."*

A pause, filled with static, the sound of nine years of war holding its breath.

Then a woman's voice, younger, cracked down the middle: *"Calloway acknowledges. This is Director Vashti Sorren. We accept. God forgive us, we accept. Send your verification teams. Send anyone. Just — tell your people it's over. Tell them tonight."*

Static. Then Adeyemi again, and Kestrel heard something in the man's voice he'd never heard in any official recording of the war's end: relief so total it was indistinguishable from weeping. *"Then it's done. Meridian stands down. Log it. Log everything. Let history see we tried."*

*"Let everyone see,"* Sorren said. *"Broadcast it wide. Both channels. Let the colonies hear their own leaders choosing—"*

The recording ended there. Not faded. Ended. Cut clean, mid-word, by something upstream of the microphone.

Kestrel sat in the dark and the golden pulse washed over him, four seconds at a time, and he understood.

They had accepted peace. Both of them. In the last hours of the war, the leaders of Meridian and Calloway had reached across the void and taken each other's hands, and they had ordered the moment broadcast to both worlds so no one could pretend it hadn't happened.

And someone had cut the transmission before it reached a single civilian ear. Had invoked Directive Ash. Had classified a ceasefire as a security breach, because a ceasefire signed in public could not be unwound, and certain institutions — insurers, armaments cartels, the shipping monopolies that fed on embargoed lanes — needed the war cold rather than over. Needed two colonies glaring at each other across dead space forever, paying premiums on the silence.

His mother had been here when it happened. She had captured the transmission on her relay, the only independent ear in the dark, and she had logged it, and she had spent the rest of her life guarding it — broadcasting it in the only way left to her, too weak to cross the jamming, too persistent to stop. Not shouting. Whispering. For years. Into the dreams of children, whose sleeping minds were the one bandwidth no censor thought to watch.

The tower of light. The patient teacher. She hadn't been sending a signal.

She'd been raising an audience.

"Oh, Mum," Kestrel said, and his voice broke on the second word, and he let it. He sat in her chair in her dome beneath her antenna and cried the way he hadn't cried at her funeral, because at the funeral he'd been mourning a stranger — a quiet woman who'd worked for a contractor and died of a failing heart in a rented room. Now he was mourning the actual woman: the one who had measured handrails for tired hands, who had played chess against a cheating computer, who had caught the end of a war in her instruments and refused, for forty years, to let it be deleted.

Grief went through him and came out the other side changed, the way ore goes through fire. What remained was not sadness. It was purpose, refined to a point.

He wiped his face with the back of his glove and looked at the storage core. **PLAY THIS.**

One problem. One enormous, structural problem. The relay's transmitter could whisper — that was all it had ever been able to do through the consortium's jamming net, a subliminal thread slipped through cracks in the censorship grid. A whisper had taken years to reach a few thousand sleeping children. The full recording, played openly, would be smothered in minutes.

Unless it wasn't whispered. Unless it was shouted — hand-thrown, manual, at maximum gain, straight through the array above his head, loud enough and clean enough that no filter could call it noise. The lever beside the cradle was built for exactly that. Built by a woman who had known, from the beginning, that someday someone would have to stand in this dome and choose to throw it.

The comm unit on his belt crackled. Idris, breathless, all the drawburned calm gone from his voice:

"Kestrel. Problem. Big one. Ship on approach vector — transponder says *Corvid*. And behind her, running dark but running fast—" a pause, static chewing the words, "—consortium enforcement. Vess's people. They'll be in orbit in under an hour."

Kestrel looked up through the dome at the antenna array, vast and patient against the stars, and put his hand flat on the storage core labeled in his mother's handwriting.

An hour. He had one hour to learn how to shout.

He stood up from her chair, and it was steadier than he was.
