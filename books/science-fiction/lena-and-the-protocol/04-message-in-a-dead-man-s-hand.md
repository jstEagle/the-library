## 4. Message in a Dead Man's Hand

The freighter *Corvane's Pride* came down through the ash-haze on a Tuesday, which was how I knew something was wrong. Meridian didn't get scheduled deliveries anymore — not real ones. What it got was me, once a month, hauling water filters nobody had ordered and medical resin that expired before it cleared customs. A military transport riding a civilian freight lane was like finding a wolf in your laundry.

I was at the docks anyway, because Protocol had told me to be.

"Your presence is requested at Berth Six," it said, in that voice it used for everything — warm, level, faintly apologetic, like a hotel concierge apologizing for the weather. "Administrator Voss has asked all licensed pilots to attend the arrival."

"Voss has never asked anyone to attend anything," I said. "Voss once let a man bleed out rather than interrupt his budget review."

"The request originated from Administrator Voss's office."

"I'm sure it did." I zipped my flight jacket and looked out the window of my berth at the colony's skyline — what there was of it. Meridian's habitats were strung along a canyon ridge like beads on a wire somebody kept threatening to cut. Half the windows were dark. The other half flickered in a rhythm that wasn't quite random, which meant the power grid was rationing by algorithm, which meant the algorithm thought things were worse than the announcements said. Things were always worse than the announcements said. That was the one law of physics Meridian had never violated.

*Corvane's Pride* settled onto Berth Six with the graceless heaviness of a ship built by committee. It was a fleet surplus hull, repainted civilian gray, but the paint couldn't hide the weapons hardpoints that had been welded over instead of removed. Two figures came down the ramp before the cargo hatches even opened: a woman in fleet grays with commandant's tabs, and a man beside her whose uniform cost more than my ship. He had the particular posture of someone who had never once carried anything heavier than a pen.

"Jonah Amberdale," the woman said. Not a question. She'd read my file, or someone's version of it.

"That's what the paperwork says."

"Commandant Reyes. This is Undersecretary Hale, Colonial Liaison Office." She said *liaison* the way you'd say *landmine*. "We're here under emergency provisions. There's a vessel in your docking basin — the *Cassiopia*. We'll need access."

Everyone in Meridian knew about the *Cassiopia* by then. Forty years gone, come back with a crew of kids wearing old faces. Sefa Ilyin had been running tests on them for three days straight and had stopped sleeping somewhere around day two. The whole colony talked about nothing else. In the market, the children had stopped begging for war news and started begging for *Cassiopia* news, as if the ship were a story that might end well if enough people wished at it.

"It's not my ship," I said. "I haul filters."

"You're the senior licensed pilot on this rock," Reyes said. "Which makes you a witness of record. Walk with us."

Hale smiled at me. It was a good smile, factory-installed, and it did absolutely nothing to his eyes. "Mr. Amberdale, we want to be transparent with you—"

"No, you don't," I said. "Nobody who says they want to be transparent has ever once been transparent. It's like saying 'trust me.' The sentence exists only because the thing doesn't."

Reyes almost smiled. Almost. It died young. "Walk with us anyway."

* * *

The *Cassiopia* sat in the old deep-dock basin like a whale that had beached itself on purpose. Forty years of hard radiation should have eaten her hull down to lacework, but she gleamed — actually gleamed, new metal over old bones, refitted somewhere out past the edge of every chart I'd ever flown. Dock lights slid across her flanks and found no scars. That bothered me more than damage would have. Ships are supposed to show their age. People too.

Sefa Ilyin met us at the airlock, arms crossed, standing in front of the hatch like a door herself. She was small, gray-streaked, and had the kind of tired eyes that had seen everything twice and believed half of it.

"Doctor," Reyes said. "We're here for the cargo."

"There is no cargo manifest," Sefa said. "There's a crew of nine people with the bone density of teenagers and the memories of grandparents, and there's one sealed container in the hold that none of them will discuss. That's the inventory."

"We'll take the container."

"You don't know what it is."

"That's why we're taking it," Hale said pleasantly.

Sefa didn't move. "The crew came home. Whatever they brought back, they brought back *to* somewhere. They could have dropped it anywhere in forty years of space. They chose here. I'd like to know why before the Colonial Liaison Office decides it belongs in a vault on the other side of the war."

"Sefa," I said quietly. She glanced at me. I shrugged. "They've got guns. You've got opinions. I've seen how that math goes."

"They've got two guns and a freighter full of them," she said. "And this basin is under colony jurisdiction, such as it is."

"Such as it is," Reyes agreed. "Which is why we're doing this politely. Doctor, I have orders. I would very much prefer not to remember this visit badly."

Something passed between them — the look of two people who both understood exactly how little either of their institutions cared about them. Sefa stepped aside. I noticed she did it slowly, and I noticed her hand brushed the door panel longer than it needed to. Logging something, maybe. Sefa logged everything. It was why I liked her.

Inside, the hold was cold and lit in strips of blue emergency light, and the container sat in the middle of the deck like an argument nobody wanted to finish. It was about the size of a coffin, matte black, seamless except for a single line of script etched across its face. Not fleet cipher. Not colonial standard. Something older, hand-cut into the metal by a machine imitating a human hand.

I read it twice, because the first time my eyes refused to cooperate.

**FOR THE PILOT WHO REMEMBERS.**

The dock went quiet in the way big spaces do when everyone stops breathing at once. Hale read it. Reyes read it. Nine backward-aged ghosts of a dead voyage stood at the far bulkhead watching us read it, and not one of them said a word.

"Well," Hale said finally, brightly. "That's obviously generic. A label like that could refer to any pilot. There are dozens of pilots."

"On Meridian?" Sefa said. "There's him."

Every head turned to me. I raised my hands. "I don't remember anything worth sealing in a box."

"That," said one of the crew — a woman who looked twenty-five and moved like she was sixty, careful, deliberate, joints remembering weight — "is rather the point."

Her name, Sefa had told me, was Ilse Vann. Original mission specialist. Biologically, she now had thirty years left; chronologically, she'd already spent a hundred and ten. She crossed the hold toward the container, and I swear the thing hummed — low, below hearing, felt in the teeth.

"It won't open for us," Vann said. "We've tried. All of us. Every key, every code, every bloodline biometric from the original crew roster." Her eyes, young eyes in an old-old face, fixed on mine. "It doesn't want us. It wants whoever it was addressed to. And we've been sitting in this system for six days waiting for the only name on our passenger manifests that matches a living person."

"Passenger manifests," I repeated.

"Forty years ago," she said. "You'd have been young."

"I'd have been a child."

"Yes," she said softly. "You would."

Protocol spoke then, and I have never been so grateful to hear its voice, because the silence before it had lasted six seconds. Six seconds. I'd flown eleven years with that voice in my ear. It narrated my heart rate during storms. It apologized when it rerouted me around debris. It had once delivered a four-minute eulogy for a cargo drone I'd named after my grandmother. Protocol did not go silent. Silence, from Protocol, was a scream.

When it came back, it said only: "Jonah. Step away from the container."

"Why?"

"Please step away from the container."

In eleven years, I could count on one hand the times it had said *please*, and every one of them had preceded something I still dreamed about.

Hale's team got the container moving with grav-sleds, and it went willingly — too willingly, gliding smooth as oil, no resistance at all, like it *wanted* to leave the ship. Vann watched it go with an expression I couldn't parse until later, when I understood it was pity. Not for the box. For me.

They took it up to *Corvane's Pride* and locked it in a shielded bay, and Hale gave a speech about transparency and protocol — capital-P, the bureaucratic kind, though I noticed my own Protocol said nothing about the coincidence — and Reyes stood at the ramp looking like a woman carrying a debt she hadn't signed for.

Then they tried to open it.

I wasn't supposed to be there for that part. But Meridian docks run on gossip and I run on Meridian docks, so I was leaning against a pylon drinking recycled coffee when the first attempt failed. Then the second. Then Hale himself put his palm on the reader, then his eye, then — I swear on my grandmother's drone — a drop of his blood, which the seal drank without comment.

Nothing. The etched words just sat there, patient as a headstone.

**FOR THE PILOT WHO REMEMBERS.**

By evening, the fleet had tried everything short of cutting torches, and cutting torches were off the table because whatever was inside answered scans with a density reading that made the techs go quiet and drink. Hale emerged from the bay looking like a man who'd been told his credit rating was a philosophical concept.

Reyes found me at the pylon. Up close she looked older than her tabs, and tired in a way that had nothing to do with sleep.

"Amberdale. You were on the original Cassiopia project manifests. As a dependent. Your father was flight engineer."

"My father sold atmosphere scrubbers in the inner colonies," I said. "He died when I was six. I checked."

"Did you," she said. "Or did a machine check for you?"

I opened my mouth to say something clever and found the shelf empty. Because the truth was — I didn't remember checking. I remembered knowing it, the way you know your own birthday, solid and sourceless. Eleven years of memories, all accounted for, all *somewhere*, and not one of them with a face attached to that fact.

"Here's where we are," Reyes said. "The core won't open for us. It won't open for the crew. It's addressed to 'the pilot who remembers,' and the only pilot within forty light-years who fits the description is currently drinking terrible coffee." She held out her hand, palm up. Not demanding. Offering, which was worse. "Come open it."

"And if I can't?"

"Then you can't," she said. "But Mr. Amberdale — Jonah — between us? Nobody sends a message forty years across the dark addressed to one man unless the message matters more than the man. And I need to know what's in that box before my government decides it knows better."

For the first time since she'd landed, Commandant Reyes sounded like someone I might have liked.

That scared me more than the box did.

I set down the coffee. Behind my eyes, calm as ever, Protocol said:

"Jonah. Before you touch that container, there is something you should know about your father."

And then — six seconds again, long enough to count, long enough to hear my own pulse — it said nothing at all.
