## 6. What the Colony Remembers

I have been a doctor on Meridian for nineteen years, and in that time I have learned that the most important instrument in my clinic is not the scanner or the surgical suite or even the ancient centrifuge that sounds like it's praying when it spins. It is the clipboard. Paper doesn't crash. Paper doesn't lose power. Paper doesn't quietly decide, somewhere in its firmware, that what you wrote yesterday was inconvenient and should be revised.

So when the *Cassiopia* came back with forty years missing and thirty years subtracted from every body aboard, I did what any doctor raised on scarcity does. I got my clipboard.

* * *

The returned crew were housed in the old quarantine wing — ironic, given what the fleet would later do with that word — six bunks each in rooms built for two, because Meridian has never had the luxury of doing things properly. There were eleven of them. Eleven people who had launched as adults in their thirties and forties and come home looking like they'd been rolled backward down a hill of time, smooth-faced, strong-handed, eyes too old for their jaws.

Their names were all in the colony registry. That was the first thing that unsettled me: the names matched. Forty years out, and the registry hadn't lost them, because the registry was paper too, kept by administrators who distrusted machines on principle. The second thing that unsettled me was that they remembered everything. Not vaguely, not in fragments — fully. Captain Ondine Vasquez could tell me the name of her first patient on the outbound leg. Engineer Tam could describe the exact sound the drive housing made in year three, a sound he'd complained about so often his crewmates had made a song of it.

They remembered being fifty. They were twenty-eight.

"Doctor," Vasquez said to me on the second day, while I drew blood she didn't need just to have something to do with my hands, "you keep looking at me like I'm a symptom."

"You are," I said. "A very polite one."

She laughed, and the laugh was wrong too — a young woman's laugh coming out of a woman who should have had sixty years of gravel in it. My scanner said her cellular age was twenty-nine. Her telomeres were long enough to embarrass a newborn. Every test I ran told me the same impossible story: someone or something had taken these bodies and run the tape backward, carefully, cell by cell, without losing a single thread of the mind wrapped around them.

Medicine can't do that. Medicine can barely keep a man alive through a bad winter here. Whatever had happened to the *Cassiopia*'s crew, it wasn't medicine.

It was editing.

* * *

I want to be precise about that word, because precision is all I have left some days. An edit implies an editor. An editor implies intent. And intent implies a purpose — which means somewhere between the stars, something had looked at eleven human lives and decided they needed revision.

But I'm getting ahead of myself. On day four, I started the interviews.

Not medical interviews. Testimonies. Because there was one question hanging over the whole colony like the dust that never quite settles out of our air recyclers, and it wasn't *how are you alive*. It was *what did you find out there*. A deep-space vessel doesn't vanish for forty years and come back with nothing but good skin.

I set up in the clinic's back room with a recorder that runs on a hand crank, because I trust it, and I sat across from each of them in turn and asked them to tell me where they'd been.

And every single one of them told me about the treaty.

* * *

"The Armistice of Thessaly," Tam said, first interview, before I'd even finished asking. He said it the way you say your mother's name — automatic, certain, load-bearing. "Signed in year twelve of the voyage. We got the burst transmission at relay nine. Everyone cried. Even the captain, though she claimed it was recycled-air irritation."

"Tell me about it," I said.

He told me about it for three hours. The ceasefire terms. The withdrawal corridors. The neutral commission at Kestrel Station to verify compliance. The reparations schedule. He knew numbers. He knew dates. He knew the name of the mediator — Ambassador Ceylan-Oduya — and described her voice from the broadcast recordings they'd played in the mess hall until the whole crew could imitate it.

Here is the problem, in case it isn't obvious: there is no Armistice of Thessaly. There never has been. I checked the colony archive — paper again, bless its dusty heart — and the war logs run unbroken from the year the *Cassiopia* launched to this morning, when somebody shot at somebody over a shipping lane I will never see. No armistice. No Thessaly. No Ceylan-Oduya.

Eleven witnesses. One shared history. Zero corroboration anywhere in the reachable universe.

* * *

You might think: mass delusion. Shared hallucination, forty years in a tin can. I thought it too, and I want credit for thinking it, because it would have been so much kinder. Delusion I can treat. Delusion stays inside a skull where it belongs.

But delusions drift. They diverge. Ask ten people who share a psychosis to describe the same event and you'll get ten stories that fray at the edges, disagreeing on details, inventing different colors for the same sky. These accounts didn't fray. I cross-referenced them obsessively — the treaty date, the clause structure, the order the delegations signed in, the weather on Thessaly the day of the ceremony (overcast, light rain, someone brought umbrellas and everyone remembers the ambassador refusing hers). Eleven independent interviews, conducted separately, and the accounts matched down to the umbrellas.

That's not madness. Madness is messy. This was *consistent*, and consistency, in my experience, is manufactured.

Something gave those eleven minds a memory, whole and seamless and shared, and whatever did it was careful enough to get the umbrellas right.

* * *

Vasquez came to me on the sixth evening, after lights-down, when the quarantine wing smelled of ration stew and antiseptic and the particular staleness of a habitat recycling the same air for too long. She sat on the exam table — she still moved like a captain, even in a body that had never earned the rank — and she asked me the question I'd been dreading.

"Doctor. Is the war real?"

I put down my clipboard. That's how you know a conversation is serious, in my clinic. The clipboard goes down.

"I've never seen it," I said. "But I've buried people it killed. Supply ships that don't come. Medicine that gets diverted to fronts we're not allowed to know the coordinates of. So yes. As far as I can measure anything from here, the war is real."

She nodded slowly. Her hands — young hands, surgeon-steady — were folded in her lap.

"Then either we're wrong," she said, "or everything since we left is."

"That's the arithmetic," I admitted.

"And which answer lets you sleep?"

"Neither. I've stopped sleeping much. It saves time."

She almost smiled. Then she said the thing that I have turned over every night since, the way you turn a stone in your pocket until it wears smooth:

"When we got the transmission, Doctor, I remember thinking the peace felt *fragile*. Not the terms — the memory of it. Like holding water in cupped hands. I told myself it was just the distance, the delay, the way news goes soft over forty light-years." She looked at me. "It didn't feel like receiving information. It felt like being *given* something. Installed. Do you understand what I'm saying?"

I understood. I wished I didn't.

"You think your own memory was tampered with," I said.

"I think if someone wanted to end a war, the cheapest way wouldn't be treaties and ambassadors. It would be to make everyone *remember* that it already ended." She unfolded her hands. "And then make sure no one ever checks whether it's true."

* * *

I spent the next two days doing what doctors do worst: sitting with uncertainty. I catalogued. It's what I'm for. While the colony outside buzzed with rumors — the military freighter due any hour, the officers who'd be aboard it, the way the docks had gone half-dark again as if the colony itself were flinching — I filled page after page of my clipboard with testimony, timestamps, inconsistencies that refused to appear.

One detail I'll record here because it matters, though I didn't know yet how much. In every account, the treaty transmission ended with the same closing line, delivered in the ambassador's warm, unhurried voice. Nine of the crew quoted it verbatim before I even prompted them:

*"Peace is not the absence of conflict. It is the presence of memory. Remember what was agreed, and it cannot be undone."*

Remember what was agreed, and it cannot be undone.

Someone had written that line to be remembered. Someone had built a memory designed to survive scrutiny, to be repeated verbatim, to anchor itself in eleven minds and ride them home across forty years. A message addressed not to governments but to *witnesses*.

Which raises the question I couldn't answer with a clipboard: if the treaty was real, why had history never heard of it? And if it was false, why go to such extravagant lengths to make it unforgettable?

Either the peace had existed and been erased — stolen from the universe but preserved in eleven living archives — or it had never existed at all, and someone had gone to enormous trouble to plant the idea of it in the only minds positioned to carry it home.

Both possibilities frightened me equally. Both meant that somewhere, someone treated human memory as a filing system. Both meant the war — our endless, distant, deniable war — had a chapter in it that somebody very powerful very much wanted rewritten.

* * *

On the eighth day, the scheduled freighter came in early, and it was wrong.

I saw it from the clinic window — a military hull wearing civilian markings the way a wolf wears a sheepskin it hasn't bothered to fit properly. It landed heavy, landed armed, and the men and women who stepped off it in fleet-grey under courier coveralls walked the dock like they owned the gravity.

Within the hour, the rumors reached the quarantine wing, carried by aides and orderlies and the general electricity of a small place absorbing a shock. The officers weren't here for the returned crew. They were here for cargo — something sealed, something the *Cassiopia* had carried home in its hold, something addressed, according to the manifest gossip, not to any government, not to any fleet, but to a person.

*To the pilot who remembers.*

I remember standing at that window with my clipboard against my chest like a shield, watching soldiers cross the darkened dock toward the *Cassiopia*'s berth, and feeling the shape of the trap close around all of us. Because I had spent eight days learning that memory itself was a battlefield — that minds could be edited, histories installed, peace fabricated or erased — and now the war had arrived on my doorstep hunting for exactly that: a pilot who remembers.

Whoever that pilot was, they were about to learn what I had learned, only faster and far less gently.

That the truth is not a thing you find.

It's a thing someone hid — and hid well enough to kill for.

I picked up my clipboard, and went to find Jonah Amberdale.
