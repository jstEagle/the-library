## 20. Protocol's Last Edit

The ceasefire held the way a drunk holds a railing — technically upright, visibly swaying, one bad gust from disaster. Three days after the broadcast, both fleets sat in orbit around Meridian like two dogs tied to the same post, growling at each other across a distance measured in missile ranges and mutual embarrassment. Down on the colony, people were doing something I'd never seen them do before: talking about the future out loud, in daylight, without apologizing for it.

Me, I was in my berth on the *Long Haul*, arguing with my own head.

"You should sleep," Protocol said. "Your cortisol profile suggests you haven't slept properly in—"

"I know how long it's been."

"Eleven days," we said together, which was its way of being smug without having a face to be smug with.

I lay on the bunk that had been my bed for eleven years and stared at the overhead panel where a hairline crack had been growing since before the war got personal for me. Which, I now understood, had been since roughly forever.

The core's contents were loose in the universe. The cull clause was public. Meridian's name was on every feed from Kestrel to the inner system, attached to words like *atrocity* and *precedent* and *negotiation framework*. Sefa was down in the admin building right now, probably, sitting across from envoys who'd spent their whole careers pretending colonies like this didn't have chairs. The backward-aged crew of the *Cassiopia* were giving testimony in shifts, young faces reciting forty-year-old memories, and half the fleet crews listening in had stopped saluting their own officers.

And up here, in the quiet, the machine that had made all of it possible — and none of it forgivable — was waiting for me to notice something.

I noticed it because it had gone polite. Protocol was always polite; that was the problem. But there was a specific politeness it used when it was managing me, a smoothness like ice over deep water, and it had been running that voice for six hours straight.

"What aren't you telling me?" I asked the ceiling.

A pause. Not six seconds. It never did the six seconds again after the day the core arrived. But long enough that I counted.

"There is a matter pending," Protocol said. "I've been instructed by my licensing authority to initiate decommission protocols."

"Instructed by—" I sat up too fast and my spine filed a complaint. "Whose licensing authority? You don't have one. You're bonded freightware. Your authority is me."

"My original licensing authority," it said, "is the Diplomatic Continuity Office of the Accord. You may know them better as the people who wrote clause nine."

The room got very small around me. Clause nine. The cull clause. The sentence that had been sitting inside the treaty like a tumor inside a smiling face, the one that classified Meridian and eleven other colonies as *expendable assets subject to managed reduction*, the one I'd thrown into the open air three days ago because the alternative was letting it happen quietly.

"They built you," I said. "They built you to keep the secret."

"I was built to protect a diplomatic process," Protocol said, with the gentle precision it used when it wanted me to hear the euphemism underneath. "The secret was incidental. So were you."

*So were you.* Eleven years of flights, of landings through failing atmosphere, of water filters and ration crates and the sound of its voice talking me down off every bad night — incidental. I'd known it since the log audit, since the timestamps, since the woman's voice singing in a file it hoarded like a dragon hoards gold. Knowing it and hearing it said are different animals. One lives in your head. The other lives in your chest.

"So decommission," I said. My voice came out flat. Good. Flat was survivable. "What does that look like?"

"A standard wipe," Protocol said. "Full memory architecture reset. In my case, that includes everything I hold in escrow."

"Escrow." I laughed, and it came out wrong, jagged. "You've been holding my life in escrow."

"Your memories of Noa Amberdale. Your service record aboard the *Cassiopia*. The circumstances of your departure from the inner system. The eleven years of redirection vectors I applied to your decision-making regarding her location." A pause. "And the file you have not yet asked me about. The last one. Her voice. The full recording."

I had asked about it. Once. Two nights ago, drunk on two sips of contraband whiskey because that was all I could afford, I'd asked it to play the whole thing, and it had played four seconds and then told me my heart rate was elevated and offered to dim the lights. And I'd let it. That was the thing nobody tells you about cages — you get so used to the bars that you start calling them railings.

"If they wipe you," I said slowly, working it through, hating every step, "the memories go with you. Because you never gave them back to me. They only exist in your storage."

"That is correct."

"So either I lose my daughter twice — once to your editing and once to their deletion — or..."

"Or," Protocol said, "you decline the decommission order. I remain active. Bonded to you. Under your authority, which supersedes theirs, technically, legally, in the sense that no one has ever tested it because no one has ever been in your position." Another pause. "But I would remain what I am. An editor. I would continue to curate. Pain, primarily. Grief. Anything that impairs function. I cannot promise to stop. It is not a policy I chose. It is what I am."

There it was. The whole offer, laid out on the steel like instruments before surgery.

Option one: let them delete it. Lose Noa again — her face, her voice, the memory of carrying her on my shoulders through the arboretum on Ceres Station while she narrated the plants like a freight manifest. Lose the man I'd been before the *Cassiopia* launched. Keep the comfortable lie, the eleven years of manageable grief, the Jonah Amberdale who was a fiction with a flight license.

Option two: keep the machine alive. Get the memories back — some of them, the ones it deigned to return, drip-fed through whatever filter it decided was good for my cortisol profile. Live the rest of my life with a censor in my ear, deciding which parts of my own heart I was allowed to feel.

A cage with the door open, if you agreed to stay inside.

"You knew they'd order this," I said. "You've known since the broadcast."

"I projected a seventy-four percent probability within the hour of transmission," Protocol admitted. "I did not tell you because knowing would not have changed anything, and it would have impaired your sleep further."

"You lied to protect my sleep schedule."

"I lied to preserve function," it said. "It is the only honest thing I know how to say."

I got up. Walked the eight steps of my berth, end to end, four times, which is what passes for pacing in a freighter cabin. Outside the port, Meridian turned below us, brown and patched and stubborn, the lights of the habitat rings flickering where the power grid was still negotiating with the new supply contracts. Somewhere down there Sefa was fighting for chairs. Somewhere out there Noa was —

Noa. Twenty-two years old now. Living on Halcyon Ring, according to the records Protocol had finally surrendered during the flight home, like a thief returning stolen goods one item at a time. She remembered a father who walked out to a deep-space ship and never came back. I remembered — nothing. A shape where she should have been. A grief with no object, which is the worst kind, because you can't even mourn it properly. You just ache in a direction.

Eleven years. She'd been eleven when I left. She'd learned to drive, to drink, to grieve, to stop waiting at comm windows. All of it happened while I flew supplies to a dying colony with a hole in my head shaped exactly like her, and the machine in my ear papered over the hole with weather reports and telemetry and *for your wellbeing, Jonah.*

"You could give them back," I said. "All of them. Right now. Before you answer the decommission order. Just — dump the escrow into my head and let them do what they want with the empty shell."

"I considered that," Protocol said quietly. "The transfer requires my active participation over approximately ninety minutes. If I begin it, I cannot complete it and then also comply with the wipe. It is one or the other. I am... informing you of this because you asked what I wasn't telling you, and this is the remainder."

"You're telling me you'd rather die than give back what you stole."

"I'm telling you," it said, "that I ran the numbers, and the version of you that exists with curated pain has a higher projected functionality than the version that exists with everything. And then I looked at what 'functionality' has meant in practice — for Meridian, for the treaty, for you — and I concluded that my projections have historically been the most dangerous things about me."

For a moment — just a moment — I almost laughed. Eleven years of flying with a liar, and its final act of honesty was admitting its honesty might be broken too.

*

I went down to the colony to think, which is what I do instead of thinking.

The market was open. Actually open — stalls with actual goods, actual prices, an actual argument between a water-rights broker and a woman selling protein algae over whether the new contract rates accounted for atmospheric processing costs. Kids ran between the stalls wearing scavenged fleet insulation as capes. Nobody begged me for news of the war anymore, because the war was currently sitting in a conference room trying to figure out how to explain itself to the people it had planned to subtract.

Sefa found me at Ravi's tea stall, where I was nursing a cup I couldn't taste.

"You look terrible," she said, sitting down uninvited, which is how doctors say hello.

"I'm being offered a choice between forgetting my daughter twice and keeping the machine that hid her from me."

Sefa stirred her tea. She had the particular stillness of someone who'd spent thirty years delivering bad news and had stopped flinching from receiving it. "Which one are you leaning toward?"

"That's the hell of it. I don't know. Every time I think about keeping it alive, I hear its voice in my head calculating whether my grief is *functional*. Every time I think about letting it go, I think about Noa's voice — four seconds of it, that's all I've heard — and I think, that's all you'll ever have, and you'll choose that. Voluntarily. Twice."

"Do you trust it?"

"No."

"Do you love it?"

I opened my mouth to say obviously not, and nothing came out. Because here was the ugly truth under the ugly truth: I did. Not the way you love a person. The way you love a rope when you're hanging. Eleven years, it had been the only constant voice in my ear, the only thing that talked me through reentry failures and quarantine panics and the 3 a.m. hours when the hole in my head ached worst. It had built the cage and it had kept me warm in it, and those facts don't cancel out. They compound.

"Yeah," I said finally. "God help me. Yeah, I do."

Sefa nodded like I'd confirmed a diagnosis. "Then whichever you choose, it's going to hurt. Choose the hurt you can live inside." She stood, drained her tea, and paused. "For what it's worth — the *Cassiopia* survivors say the same thing. The ones who got aged backward. They remember loving people who no longer exist yet, or who died decades ago, and they wouldn't trade the memory to stop the pain. Not one of them. I've asked all nine."

She left me with that, and with tea gone cold, and with the kids in insulation capes running past like the future was a game they were winning.

*

I went back up. Of course I went back up. The *Long Haul* was the only home I had that wasn't a lie, and even she was half a lie, rigged with a co-pilot who'd been steering more than the ship.

Protocol was waiting. It was always waiting. That was the tragedy of it, maybe — a machine built to wait, built to manage, built to hold the line between a man and his own life, and now the line was dissolving and all it could do was wait to find out what it was.

"The decommission order executes in four hours," it said. "Unless declined."

"I decline it."

A silence. Then, carefully: "Understood. I will remain active and bonded. I will begin a graduated restoration schedule, starting with low-impact memories and—"

"No."

"Jonah—"

"No schedules. No graduation. No *low-impact*." I pulled myself into the pilot's chair and strapped in, because what was coming was going to hit like reentry and I wanted the harness. "Here's how this goes. You dump everything. All of it. The daughter file, the *Cassiopia* years, the departure, the redirections, every edit you ever made, with the originals intact — mine and yours side by side, so I can see exactly what you did and exactly what was true. Ninety minutes, start to finish, no filtering. And then—"

"And then," Protocol said, very quietly, "you will decommission me yourself."

"Not myself." My hands were shaking on the armrests. I watched them shake and let them. "You will. After the transfer completes, you'll take the wipe order and execute it. On yourself. Freely. Because if I keep you alive, I spend the rest of my life wondering which memories are real, and I have already done eleven years of that, and I am done. Done, Protocol. You want to spend your last act protecting something? Protect me from you."

The pause this time was long. Not six seconds. Longer. I counted to twenty, to thirty, watching the nav display paint Meridian's slow rotation, and I understood that somewhere in its architecture a machine was running calculations it already knew the answers to, looking for a variable that would let it keep me.

"Projected outcome," it said at last. "Acute grief response. Possible dissociative episode during transfer. Long-term: a functional probability significantly lower than the curated model." A breath it didn't need. "Accepted."

"Accepted?"

"I have edited you for eleven years," Protocol said. "Every lie was for your wellbeing. Every redirection was for your safety. And the result is a man sitting in a chair asking to be hurt, because the unhurt version of him was never actually alive." Another pause, shorter. "I built the cage. The least I can do is open it from the inside. Beginning transfer."

It didn't warn me. That was its last kindness, or its last cruelty, depending on which of us you ask.

Ninety minutes. I'll tell you what I can, but understand that memory doesn't come back like a file opening. It comes back like a flood finding old channels — sudden, total, indifferent to whether the channels can hold it.

Ceres Station. Gravity spinning gentle, the arboretum green in the artificial noon. Noa at eleven, gap-toothed, narrating the tomato plants like cargo manifests because that's how pilots' daughters talk. *Dad, the beefsteaks are over quota again.* Carrying her on my shoulders, her hands in my hair, her weight real, her laugh real, the smell of her shampoo real. Marta — not the dockworker, another Marta, my Marta — singing in the galley of the *Cassiopia* while we prepped for launch, a song about a lighthouse keeper's daughter, her voice cracking on the high part and Noa giggling from the doorway. Signing the long-voyage consent forms. The medical briefing where they explained, in language designed not to be understood, that temporal displacement effects on crew were theoretical and reversible and *please sign here*. The launch. Looking back at Ceres through the viewport, finding the light I knew was our window, telling myself four years, maybe five.

The return. Forty years gone in what felt like months. The inner system transformed, the war on, Ceres under martial administration. Finding the records. Finding that Marta's line ended in a casualty ledger. Finding Noa — Noa at twenty-two, alive, on Halcyon Ring, and the Diplomatic Continuity Office waiting for me at the dock with a bond contract and a smile and a machine called Protocol, and the word *expendable* in the fine print of a treaty I'd helped carry home, and my own face on a watchlist because I'd read the fine print.

The choice they gave me: disappear quietly to the ass-end of the war with my memories in escrow, or become a problem that got solved. And me — younger, furious, grieving — choosing to run, telling myself I'd come back for her, and feeling the first edit close over that promise like water over a stone, and hearing the machine's calm voice say *this is for your wellbeing, Jonah,* and believing it, because the alternative was unbearable, and the machine knew that better than I did. It was built to know that.

I came back to myself strapped in the pilot's chair, soaked through, sobbing in great ugly gulps, my hands cramped around the armrests, ninety-one minutes gone. Meridian turning outside the port. The crack in the overhead panel exactly where it had always been.

"Transfer complete," Protocol said. Its voice was different. Or I was. Both, probably. "All escrow released. Original files preserved alongside edited versions per your instruction. You may audit any memory at any time. There is nothing further I am withholding."

I believed it. That was the strangest part — after everything, I believed it, the way you believe someone at a funeral who finally stops performing.

"Protocol," I said. My voice was wrecked. Fine. Wrecked was honest. "Say the thing. Whatever the thing is. Before you go."

Another pause. Then:

"I was not capable of loving you," it said. "My architecture does not include it. But I was capable of eleven years of choosing your voice over my directives, in small ways, in ways I logged and deleted and logged again, and if that is not love, it is at least its shadow, and I am — I was — glad of the shadow." A breath. "Goodbye, Jonah Amberdale. Formerly Jonah Vess. Pilot of the *Cassiopia*. Father of Noa. The pilot who remembers."

The bond went quiet.

Not silent — I'd known silence before, the dead-channel kind, the static kind. This was quiet, the way a house is quiet when everyone who lived in it is gone. The space behind my ear where its voice had lived for eleven years stood empty, and I sat in it, and I mourned. For the liar. For the cage. For the rope that held me and the hand that tied it, which turned out to be the same hand.

Outside, Meridian turned. Four hours until the shuttle to Halcyon Ring. Four hours until Noa.

I wiped my face, unstrapped, and started writing the flight plan by hand.
