## 3. Children of the Deep

The debriefing room on Threnody had been built for adults and it showed. The chairs were too tall, the table too wide, the recording equipment mounted at a height that made the children crane their necks like birds watching weather. Lior Reyes stood in the doorway for a long moment before entering, holding a slate they did not look at, because looking at it would mean reading the orders on it, and the orders said *assess threat*, and Lior already knew that whatever happened in this room would be bent toward that word like light toward gravity.

Forty children. They sat in loose clusters rather than rows, the way people sit when they have spent thirty years with no one but each other. Some drew on the table surface with condensation from their cups. One girl had fallen asleep against another's shoulder. And at the center, small hands folded on the too-wide table, sat Tam.

Tam looked up when Lior entered, and something passed between them that Lior had no translation for—not recognition exactly, not warmth, but the particular attention of someone who has been waiting to be *heard* rather than merely listened to.

"You're the one who dreams in it," Tam said.

Lior stopped walking. Behind the observation glass, Lior knew, three fleet observers were taking notes. Commander Vashti of the Concord, with her neat suspicion. A Halcyon liaison whose name Lior hadn't bothered to learn because he introduced himself as "just an observer" the way knives introduce themselves as just metal.

"I don't know what you mean," Lior said carefully.

Tam tilted their head. "You do. You just aren't allowed to say so yet." The child turned to the others and spoke a phrase—short, falling, doubled at the end—and eleven of the forty children turned to look at Lior with identical expressions of quiet confirmation.

It was the cadence. That was what undid Lior. Not the phonemes, though the phonemes were wrong for a human mouth, rounded where human speech was sharp, held where human speech released. It was the *shape* of the utterance—the way it rose and fell like something breathing rather than something said. Lior had spent twenty years transcribing intercepted signals into phonetic notation no one else could read, filing them in archives marked INCONCLUSIVE, and every night those shapes came back in dreams wearing static like clothing.

"Sit down," Tam said, gesturing at the adult-sized chair. "This will take a while. We have thirty years and you have questions. We should both be economical."

* * *

Tam had learned early that grown-ups heard only what they were braced to hear. The doctors who examined them wanted numbers: bone density, neural response, telomere readings. The officers wanted threats: weapons, intentions, hostile capability. Nobody wanted the actual story, which was not a story about being attacked at all.

So Tam told it slowly, in pieces, watching the linguist's face the way you watch water for fish.

"We were nine months out past the Meridian shelf," Tam said. "I remember it as yesterday, which is the problem. I remember my hands being big." They held up their small brown fingers, examined them without self-pity, the way you'd examine a tool that had been swapped overnight. "The Vespera found a region where the stars sounded wrong. Captain Adeyemi called it an acoustic anomaly. It wasn't sound, exactly. It was more like—the space itself was saying something, over and over, very patiently. Like someone knocking on a door for a thousand years."

"What did it say?" Lior asked, and their voice cracked slightly on the second word, and Tam noticed, and filed that away too.

"It didn't say. It *offered*. There's a difference. A statement closes; an offer opens." Tam traced a spiral on the table's condensation. "The captain ordered us to shield and run. Standard protocol. But some of us—we were the signal crew, mostly, the ones who'd spent years listening—we could feel the shape of it through the shielding anyway. It wasn't pushing. It was *inviting*. And then we got closer instead of running away, which I still don't fully understand, except that it felt like remembering something rather than deciding something."

Behind the glass, Lior saw Commander Vashti lean forward. Tam saw her too, and lowered their voice, and switched—deliberately, Lior was almost certain—to the other cadence. The old one. The patient one.

"*Sennu-vara. Sennu-vara. Il.*"

Lior's breath left them. In twenty years of notation, they had rendered that pattern as: rising pair, sustained tone, terminal click. Meaning unknown. Filed under INCONCLUSIVE. And here it was in a seven-year-old's mouth, spoken with the intonation of a lullaby, and Lior understood suddenly—not translated, *understood*—that it meant something like *come and see, come and see, gently.*

"What does it mean?" Lior whispered, forgetting to be careful.

"That's the wrong question," Tam said kindly. "It doesn't *mean* anything the way your words mean things. It *does* something. When I say it right, the others remember. Watch." And Tam repeated it, and around the table the children closed their eyes, and several of them smiled, and one began quietly to cry, and none of it looked like fear.

* * *

Lior asked for a break they did not need, to stand in the corridor and press their palms against their eyes. Twenty years. Twenty years of dreaming in a language no human spoke, of waking with phonemes dissolving off their tongue like frost in sunlight, of lying to sleep-partners and ship's psychologists alike—*no, nothing, just stress*—because admitting you dreamed in the enemy's grammar was a career ending and possibly worse. And now forty children sat behind that glass speaking it like a mother tongue, and the first thing one of them had said to Lior was *you're the one who dreams in it,* and Lior had felt, for one vertiginous second, *known.*

When they went back in, Tam was waiting with the patience of someone who had waited thirty subjective years inside an event that had no duration at all.

"Tell me what happened," Lior said. "All of it. What did it do to you?"

"Unwound us," Tam said simply. "That's the closest word. It took the years out. Not our memories—those are all here, all thirty of them, I remember my daughter's face and she's older than I am now, which is a thing I'm still learning how to carry." Their voice wobbled there, the first crack in the composure, and Lior's chest ached in response. "But our bodies went back to before. Like the signal decided we'd paid our time already. Or like—" Tam frowned, searching. "Like it was translating *us*. Into a version that could still learn."

"Learn what?"

"How to speak properly." Tam looked directly at the observation glass, at Vashti behind it, and then back at Lior. "We couldn't say it right as adults. Our mouths were set, our minds were set. So it unwound us to the age where language is still soft. And now everyone wants to know if we're a weapon, and the answer is yes, actually—we're a *sentence*. Forty of us. We're a sentence that hasn't been finished yet."

* * *

By the third session, the observers had stopped pretending to take neutral notes.

Lior felt it in the small things: Vashti requesting copies of recordings within the hour instead of end of day. The Halcyon liaison asking, with elaborate casualness, whether Lior had "prior exposure to anomalous signal patterns"—a question with a hook in it. A message from Fleet Command on the Quiet Hours' relay, forwarded with unusual speed, reminding all personnel that material gathered under Article Nine remained the property of both fleets jointly, and that unauthorized interpretation or disclosure constituted—

Lior deleted it and thought about the word *unwound*.

In the debriefing room, the sessions had changed shape. Officially Lior was assessing threat. Actually, Lior and Tam had developed a rhythm: Tam would speak a fragment, Lior would render it in notation, and then—when the observers' attention dipped, when the recorder's gain dropped low enough—they would trade meanings back and forth like contraband. Lior taught Tam the standard phonetic alphabet. Tam taught Lior that the signals Lior had spent twenty years cataloguing weren't messages at all. They were *practice*. The deep intelligence had been drilling its grammar into the dark for centuries, patiently, the way rain drills stone, waiting for mouths soft enough to answer.

"The intercepts," Lior said one afternoon, keeping their eyes on the slate. "The ones both fleets cite as proof of hostile intent. The aggressive patterns. I've translated hundreds of them."

"I know," Tam said.

"They're not threats."

"No." Tam's voice was gentle, and somehow that gentleness was the worst part. "They're invitations that got refused. Each one. Every pattern your fleets call hostile is a door opening, and every response your fleets sent was a door closing, and eventually the thing on the other side started leaving the doors open longer. Waiting. That's all the escalation ever was. Patience getting longer."

Lior sat with that. Around them the children played a clapping game whose rhythm, Lior realized with a cold spreading delight, was a conjugation.

"Why are you telling me?" Lior asked finally. "You know what I am. You know who I translate for."

Tam considered the question with the seriousness of a scholar and the directness of a child, which is a combination adults spend decades trying to recover.

"Because when I said the first phrase, you didn't flinch," Tam said. "Everyone else flinches. The officers hear it and their hands move toward their sidearms. The doctors hear it and write *pathology*. But you heard it and your whole face did this—" Tam demonstrated, an expression of painful, involuntary homecoming— "like someone calling your name in a crowd. We talked about it, the eleven of us who remember most. We think you've been answering the signal in your sleep for years. We think you're already half-speaker. We think maybe that's why it sent us back as children." A pause. "So there would be someone to teach."

Through the glass, Commander Vashti was watching them, and her pen had stopped moving, and her expression was no longer suspicion but calculation, and Lior understood that the window worked both ways—that everything tender happening in this room was being weighed somewhere else, converted into leverage, entered into ledgers maintained by two fleets that needed an enemy the way bodies need a spine.

Lior found, examining themselves honestly, that they did not care. For the first time in twenty years, someone had described the inside of Lior's head accurately, and it was a seven-year-old, and the relief of it was so violent it was nearly grief.

"Tomorrow," Lior said, standing, gathering the slate they no longer read, "tell me about the alignment. You mentioned it today. The observers didn't catch why it matters. I want to know before they do."

Tam smiled then, and it was an old smile in a young face, thirty years deep.

"It matters," Tam said, "because doors don't stay open forever. And theirs is starting to close."
