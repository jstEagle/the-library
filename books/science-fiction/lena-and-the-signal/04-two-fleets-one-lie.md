## 4. Two Fleets, One Lie

The message from Admiral Okafor arrived at 0400 station time, which meant it was meant to look routine. Lior had spent thirty years learning to read the grammar of officialdom—the way urgency dressed itself in blandness, the way a summons to "discuss translation methodology" was never about methodology. They read it three times in the dim of their quarters aboard *Quiet Hours*, the words glowing against the dark like bioluminescence in deep water, and felt something in their chest begin, quietly, to come apart.

*Report to my office. Threnody Station, Deck Nine. Come alone. — A.O.*

Come alone. As if there were anyone else to come with.

Lior dressed in the grey of Concord service, checked their appearance in the mirror out of habit rather than vanity, and caught themselves mouthing phonemes—*ssha-vel, ssha-vel*—the word that had been surfacing in their dreams all week, the word Tam had spoken on the second day of debriefs with exactly the same intonation contour, rising on the second syllable like a question asked gently by someone who already knew the answer.

They stopped. Stared at their own mouth in the glass as if it belonged to a stranger.

Then they went down to Deck Nine.

* * *

Admiral Adaeze Okafor's office aboard Threnody was a museum of a career: campaign plaques from the Border Incidents, a holo of the first Meridian patrol, a framed printout of the intercepted transmission that had started everything—thirty-one years ago now—rendered in the old notation system, before Lior's own work had replaced it. Okafor herself sat behind a desk too large for the room, silver-haired, spine straight as a pylon, her hands folded with the stillness of a woman who had learned long ago that hands betrayed people.

"Lior." She said their name the way she always had, with the particular warmth she reserved for the one student who had mattered. "Sit."

Lior sat. The chair was uncomfortable on purpose. Everything in a flag officer's office was uncomfortable on purpose.

"You've completed how many sessions with the Vespera children?"

"Eleven formal. More informal than I can count. They seek me out, sir. In the corridors, the mess. Tam especially."

"And what have they told you?"

"That's rather the point of the sessions, Admiral."

Something flickered across Okafor's face—not anger, not quite amusement. Something older and wearier than either. She rose, crossed to the window that looked out over the station's docking arm, where the *Vespera* hung in its cradle like a relic, like a wound.

"Do you remember your first posting?" she asked. "Meridian Relay Seven. You were twenty-four. You'd just published the phonotactic analysis that got you seconded to fleet intelligence, and you thought—" she smiled faintly, "—you thought you were going to save humanity with grammar."

"I thought I was going to understand something," Lior said. "Saving seemed ambitious."

"Yes. That was always the trouble with you." She turned back to face them. "Do you remember the first signal? The original? You worked from the archive copy for your doctorate. Did you ever request the raw capture?"

The question landed strangely, off-center, like a step onto a stair that wasn't there. "Of course. It was classified above my clearance at the time. By the time I had clearance, my own translations were the canonical record. There didn't seem to be a reason."

"No," Okafor said softly. "There didn't seem to be a reason. That was very carefully arranged, Lior. For thirty-one years, no one has had a reason."

The room's climate system hummed. Somewhere below them, a docking clamp engaged with a sound like a knuckle cracking.

"What are you telling me?" Lior asked, and heard their own voice go flat, careful, the voice they used for hostile transmissions.

Okafor returned to her desk but did not sit behind it. She leaned against its edge, close enough now that Lior could see the fine tremor in her folded hands, and for the first time in thirty years of knowing her, Lior understood that the tremor was not age.

"The Meridian Expanse was empty when we found it," she said. "That's the beginning. Not dangerous. Empty. The survey fleets mapped it for six years and found nothing but dust and radiation gradients and beautiful, useless nothing. And then both the Concord and the Compact realized that whoever held the Expanse would hold every jump route through this arm of the galaxy for the next century. Two fleets, one prize, and no legal framework left after the Fragmentation Wars to settle it."

"So they invented a reason neither could claim it."

"They invented an enemy." Okafor's voice did not waver; that was the horror of it, how steady it stayed. "A xenophobic threat in the deep Meridian. Something that had destroyed the survey ship *Anchises*. Something that justified standing fleets, armed stations, a truce line drawn through space that belonged to no one. The *Anchises* was lost to a drive cascade failure—a boring, human, stupid death. We buried the accident report and wrote a monster instead."

Lior's hands had gone cold. "The signals. There were signals. I've spent my life—"

"There were signals." She closed her eyes briefly. "Faint ones. Real ones. Patterned emissions from somewhere past the far survey markers—natural or otherwise, we never knew, we never tried to know. We took those fragments and we built a language of threat around them. Your predecessors assembled a hostile lexicon. Ambiguous phonemes became warnings. Repetitions became mobilization orders. And then, Lior, when the Concord needed a translator brilliant enough to make it all coherent—"

She opened her eyes and looked at them, and Lior felt the floor tilt beneath thirty years of their own life.

"—we found you. Young. Obsessive. Isolated enough to be grateful for the work. Brilliant enough that no one would ever check it. We gave you the doctored corpus and let your gift do the rest. Every translation you've ever produced for fleet command passed through filters first. Every one. The grammar was yours. The meaning was ours."

* * *

Afterward, Lior could not have said how long they sat in that office, or what else was said. Memory came back later in pieces, the way the children's memories came back—in fragments, in flashes, unmoored from sequence.

They remembered asking, in a voice that sounded borrowed: *The dreams. If the corpus is false, why do I dream in it? Why does it feel true?*

And Okafor, looking away toward the window, toward the *Vespera*: *Because the underlying signal is real, Lior. We falsified the interpretation, not the source. Whatever is out there speaks. We simply never let anyone listen.*

They remembered standing, legs unsteady, and asking the only question that mattered: *The children. What happened to them? What did they actually encounter?*

And Okafor, and here her composure finally cracked, a hairline fracture running through thirty years of granite: *I don't know. God help me, I don't know. But if what they're carrying is the real signal—if the thing we pretended to fear has been trying to speak all along—then everything I built, everything you built, everything these two fleets have pointed guns at each other over for three decades, is ash. Which is why, Lior—*

—and here she straightened again, and the admiral reassembled herself around the woman, and Lior saw what was coming the way one sees weather coming—

*—which is why you will write a report concluding that the children's speech patterns show evidence of conditioning by a hostile intelligence. That they are compromised. That whatever the Vespera encountered, it is the enemy we have always documented. You will do it convincingly, because you are the best, and because no one will ever check.*

* * *

Lior walked back through Threnody's corridors without seeing them. The station moved around them—Concord officers nodding, Compact traders haggling, dockhands shouting over cargo drones—all of it sustained, all of it shaped, by a lie they had spent their life unknowingly upholstering. Every translation they had ever signed. Every briefing they had delivered. The truce itself, the fragile peace they had believed they were holding together with honest work: it was mortar made of fiction, and they had mixed it.

In the mess hall, Tam sat alone at a corner table, small legs swinging above the floor, carefully dismantling a ration bar into geometric shapes. When Lior appeared, the child looked up, and their eyes—old eyes, impossibly old in a seven-year-old face—found something in Lior's expression.

Tam said nothing. Instead, the child arranged the ration-bar pieces on the table: a long strip, then three small squares, then a curve broken in the middle.

Lior stared at it. Their breath went shallow.

It was notation. Old notation—the notation from the framed printout in Okafor's office, the notation of the original captured signal, the one Lior had never been permitted to hear raw. And the arrangement—the broken curve followed by completion—it was a construction Lior knew intimately from their dreams, a construction that in the dream-language meant, as nearly as anything could be translated:

*What was taken can be given back.*

Tam watched them read it. Then the child pushed the pieces aside, took Lior's hand in both small palms, and spoke aloud, in a whisper pitched under the mess-hall noise, in cadences that no human throat should have found natural:

"They lied to you too, didn't they? We can hear it in how you talk now. The signal remembers everything, Lior. And so do we."

Outside the viewport behind them, beyond the station's hull, the stars of the Meridian Expanse burned in their ancient, patient silence—and somewhere out in that darkness, Lior understood now, something waited that had been speaking all along, unheard, mistranslated, made monstrous by men and women who needed a monster more than they needed the truth.

The alignment charts were public data. Lior had studied them for years, idly, professionally.

Nineteen weeks until the next one.
