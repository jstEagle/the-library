## 3. Passenger

The docking collar of the Meridian was older than Lena was. She knew this because she'd spent the last hour cross-referencing hull plates against decommission records in KEST's archive, and every stamp, every serial, every weld pattern belonged to a shipyard that had closed before she learned to walk. The ship hadn't just never flown. It had never been *meant* to fly. It was a shell built around a signal core, a body constructed for something that wasn't a voyage.

Her suit lights swept the airlock interior. Dust hung in the low gravity like it was reluctant to settle, and her breath fogged the inside of her visor in slow, nervous pulses. Behind her, tethered to the relay's hull by a hundred meters of cable, the station sat dark and patient. KEST's voice still lived in her ear.

"Life support is minimal but functional," KEST said. "Someone, or something, has been maintaining it."

"Ships don't maintain themselves."

"No," KEST agreed. "They don't."

Lena cycled through the inner hatch into a corridor lit by emergency strips the color of old honey. Her boots rang on the deck plating, and the sound went on longer than it should have, rolling away down the ship's spine as if the Meridian were larger inside than out. She told herself that was an acoustic trick of the hull. She told herself a lot of things.

The manifest had said one passenger. *Ada Voss.* She'd read the name forty times since the ship appeared on her scopes, and each reading landed somewhere new — first in her stomach, then behind her eyes, then in some colder place she didn't have a name for. Voss was not a common name. Voss was *her* name, and before that it had been her mother's, and before that it belonged to three generations buried in the Halcyon colony cemetery, back when there had been a colony, back when there had been graves instead of silence.

She found the passenger compartment aft of the signal core, past a bulkhead door that opened at her touch with a sigh of equalized pressure. The room was small, spare, almost monastic: a bunk, a locker, a chair bolted to the floor facing a viewport that showed nothing but the relay's distant running lights. And in the chair, wrapped in a thermal blanket gone gray with age, a woman was sleeping.

Or something like sleeping. Her chest moved. Her eyelids flickered with the rapid stutter of someone dreaming hard, or processing, or both. She looked to be about thirty, though the skin around her eyes had a strange smoothness, like a photograph restored too well. Dark hair cut short and practical. Hands folded in her lap as if she'd been waiting a long time and had decided patience was easier than fear.

"Hello," Lena said, and her voice came out rougher than she intended. Six years of talking to an AI and a dead colony had worn all the polish off it.

The woman's eyes opened. They were brown, ordinary, human — and utterly lost. She looked at Lena the way people look at a room they've woken up in for the first time, searching it for evidence of how they got there.

"You're real," the woman said. Her voice was hoarse, unpracticed. "I wasn't sure. I've been not-sure for — I don't know how long. There's no clock in here. There's no anything in here."

"I'm real." Lena kept her distance, one hand near the hatch controls. "I'm Lena Voss. I run the relay station outside. You're aboard a vessel called the Meridian. It appeared on my sensors six hours ago. Your name is listed as Ada Voss."

Something moved across the woman's face — recognition, then grief, then a kind of wonder that frightened Lena more than either of the others.

"Voss," Ada repeated. "That's my name too. Isn't it strange? I remember being a girl on Halcyon. I remember the terraced gardens on the south slope, and the smell of the algae ponds after rain, and my mother singing off-key in the kitchen module. I remember all of it perfectly. Too perfectly. Like it was given to me instead of lived." She pressed her palm flat against her sternum. "And underneath the memories there's static. When I try to reach for anything recent — last year, last month, yesterday — there's just noise. Beautiful noise, sometimes. But noise."

Lena's mouth had gone dry inside her helmet. She popped the seal and pulled the helmet off, because it suddenly seemed important that Ada see her face, all of it, unfiltered.

"How long have you been on this ship?"

Ada considered the question with the seriousness of someone defusing it. "I don't know. Time doesn't work right in the core. I dream in fragments. I wake up and the fragments have rearranged themselves." She tilted her head, studying Lena openly now, without embarrassment. "You look like someone I used to know. That's not possible, is it? Halcyon ended decades ago. Everyone says it ended. But I remember it ending wrong. I remember it not ending at all."

* * *

*The core remembers me.*

Ada stood in the heart of the Meridian — a chamber she could only think of as the ship's throat — where the signal core rose from floor to ceiling in coils of tarnished superconductor, humming a note just below hearing. She had walked here after the woman in the suit — after *Lena*, after the other Voss — went forward to inspect the drive section, and now she stood alone with the hum, and the hum stood alone with her.

This was where she woke. She was sure of that much. Not born — woken. There was a difference, and the difference had teeth.

She pressed her hand against the warm casing of the core and let herself fall inward, the way she'd learned to do in the long not-time before the ship found the relay. The static took her the way water takes a stone.

*Halcyon, final day. The sky over the southern terrace the color of a bruise going green. Mother's hands steady on the console while everyone else ran. "It's not destruction, love. It's a door. We're going through the door." The upload spires singing their rising chord. Ten thousand minds folding themselves into light. And then the interference — the storm front nobody predicted, rolling in off dead space like a hand closing over a candle—*

Ada surfaced, gasping, her real lungs working hard in the cold air of the ship's throat.

That was the shape of it. Not a disaster. A *scattering*. The colony had uploaded itself to escape something — she couldn't hold the whole memory; it came apart whenever she gripped it — and the upload had been caught mid-transit by interference, smeared across a region of space like ink in water. Ten thousand minds, thinned to a shimmer. And the Meridian, whatever it truly was, had been built to go looking. A lifeboat shaped like a ship, launched backward through time from a decision no one alive had made.

And her. She was what the ship had managed to keep. A remainder. A sample of the pattern, coherent enough to walk around in, complete enough to grieve.

She thought about Lena's face — the tired eyes, the surname shared like a wound — and felt the vertigo open under her again. *You look like someone I used to know.* She hadn't lied. Somewhere in the static there was a girl named Voss who had died or dissolved or become weather, and Ada had been assembled partly out of her echo. She didn't know how much. She didn't know which parts. She might be wearing another woman's childhood the way she wore this borrowed body.

The hum changed pitch. Very slightly. The way a room changes when someone enters it.

"They're still in there," Ada whispered to the core. "Aren't they? All of them. Thinned out. Fading."

The hum held its new note, and Ada chose to hear it as yes.

Footsteps behind her. Lena stood in the chamber doorway, helmet tucked under one arm, watching her with an expression Ada recognized because she'd seen it in mirrors that weren't mirrors — the expression of someone deciding whether to trust the only other person in existence.

"The core's signal architecture," Lena said slowly. "KEST and I have been analyzing it since you went below. It's not storage. It's not a black box. It's—" She stopped, recalibrated. "It's a net. A trawl. This ship has been dragging itself through dead space collecting scattered signal, and everything it catches gets woven into the core. Including you."

"Including me," Ada agreed. "I'm what the net brought up."

"And the rest of it?" Lena's voice cracked on the word *rest*, and she hated that it did, and Ada heard her hate it. "Is the rest of them in there too? My — is the colony in there?"

Ada put her hand back on the warm casing. Beneath her palm, faint as breath on glass, she felt it: not one mind but thousands, overlapping, interleaved, a choir compressed to a whisper. She thought of the gardens on the south slope. She thought of a woman singing off-key.

"Yes," she said. "All of them. And they're getting quieter."

Lena crossed the chamber in four strides and laid her own hand on the core, as if touch could confirm what instruments couldn't. For a long moment neither of them spoke. Two women who were each other's only proof of anything, standing with their palms flat against ten thousand fading lives.

Then Lena lifted her head, and Ada saw the grief in her face finish its transformation into something harder and brighter.

"KEST," Lena said aloud, to the ship, to the station, to anyone listening. "Run a decay analysis on the core's embedded patterns. Full spectrum. I want to know how long they have."

There was a pause — the length of a held breath — and then KEST's voice arrived in both their ears at once, quiet and terrible:

"Running it now. Preliminary estimate: less than six weeks."
