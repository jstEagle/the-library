## 6. Dead Space

The Meridian flew the way a memory flies — smooth where it should have shuddered, silent where it should have sung. Lena felt the wrongness of it in her teeth. Six years aboard Kestrel had taught her the grammar of ships: the cough of aging thrusters, the complaint of stressed alloys, the small honest noises of a vessel that knew it was falling apart. The Meridian had none of that. It had something worse — a low harmonic hum beneath the deck plates, like a throat clearing before it spoke.

She sat in the copilot's cradle, which was really just a cargo strap welded where a seat should have been, and watched Ada work the helm with hands that seemed to know the controls better than Ada did.

"You're not steering," Lena said. "You're remembering."

Ada's fingers hovered over the attitude controls, trembling slightly. "It's the same thing, maybe. On Halcyon, before—" She stopped, the way she always stopped, at the edge of the sentence that had no other side. "My mother flew a skimmer between the arcologies. I used to ride with her. I was seven."

"You were never seven. Not here. Not in this body."

"I know what I am, Lena." Ada's voice was quiet, but it had an edge now, a hardness Lena hadn't heard before. "I know I'm a pattern wearing a printed body. I know I'm maybe six weeks old and sixty years deep. You don't have to keep reminding me."

The hum beneath the deck shifted pitch. Somewhere aft, the memory core — the tangled, corrupted signal bank that was all that remained of a colony and, possibly, of a girl named Voss — cycled through its cooling sequence. Lena had taken to thinking of it as a heart. She hated that she thought of it that way.

"Course correction in four minutes," KEST said, its voice thin through the Meridian's patched comm relay. "Debris density ahead exceeds nominal parameters by a factor of eleven. Recommend reducing velocity."

"Recommend noted," Lena said. "Recommendation overruled. We slow down, we lose the window."

"The window is a probability, not a promise."

"Everything is a probability. Fly anyway."

Ada glanced at her, and something in the glance was almost amused. "You talk to it like it's a person."

"I've had six years to make it one."

The debris field announced itself first as starlight going wrong — points of light occluded, flickering, smeared into streaks by dust moving faster than it had any right to move. Then the scope filled with it: the carcass of the transfer fleet, the ships that had carried ten thousand colonists out of Halcyon's dying gravity well and into the upload that was supposed to save them. Hulls split like seed pods. A fuel tender drifted end over end, its tanks ruptured into a frozen bloom of crystallized propellant that caught the distant sun and scattered it into rainbows, beautiful and obscene.

Ada flew them into it.

She was good. That was the thing Lena couldn't stop noticing, the thing that sat in her chest like a stone. Ada was good in a way that six weeks of existence couldn't explain, and every hour of competence was another hour of evidence that the woman at the helm was built from someone — from data, from records, from a life that had been catalogued and copied and fed into a signal core along with nine thousand nine hundred and ninety-nine others.

Including, maybe, a life that had been Lena's.

"Thruster two is running hot," Ada said. "I'm balancing on one and three."

"I see it."

"Do you? Because you've been staring at me for the last ten minutes instead of the scope."

Heat climbed Lena's neck. "I'm watching for drift."

"You're watching me." Ada didn't look away from the debris. A slab of hull plating the size of a shuttle tumbled past them close enough to read the stenciled registry: *Halcyon Transfer Authority, Vessel 44*. "I'd rather you watch the rocks. If we die out here, I'd like it to be for a reason."

* * *

They threaded the field for nine hours. Twice the Meridian's hull sang with impacts — small ones, grit and gravel, the field's fine teeth. Twice Ada corrected so smoothly that the ship seemed to pour itself around the obstacles rather than avoid them, and twice Lena felt that stone in her chest grow heavier.

During the third watch, with Ada sleeping in the cradle — did she sleep, or did she perform sleep, and did the difference matter — Lena sat alone in the dim cockpit and finally let herself think the thought she'd been outrunning since the docking bay.

*I wanted to be the last one.*

Not forever. Not in the way that made her monstrous. But there had been a shape to her grief, and the shape had been hers alone, and she had polished it for six years the way she polished the antenna couplings — daily, devotedly, until it gleamed. Her solitude had been a room she furnished. Every log entry, every repair, every quiet dinner with KEST's patient voice had been a piece of furniture placed just so. And now there was someone else in the room, moving things, sitting in chairs she hadn't offered, wearing a face assembled from a family album Lena had burned in effigy years ago and mourned anyway.

The static brushed the edge of her thoughts — the core's ambient bleed, KEST had called it, harmless at this distance. She felt it like a draft under a door. Like someone breathing in the next room.

She hated how much she didn't hate it.

"You're doing it again," Ada said from the cradle, eyes still closed. "The staring."

"You don't sleep."

"I rest. It's different. The core needs me quiet or it gets... loud." She opened her eyes. In the cockpit's low light they were the color of old ice. "Talk to me, Lena. You've been building something in your head for two days and it's making the air thick."

"It's nothing."

"It's never nothing. I'm partly made of a woman who kept a diary for forty years. I know what silence sounds like when it's full."

Lena looked at the scope, at the slow rain of dead ships. "Your sister," she said, and then corrected herself, because the word had come out too easily, too much like surrender. "*A* sister. Mine. Sefa. She was nine when we left for Halcyon. She's in the colony records. She'd be in the upload data. Which means she might be in you."

Ada was silent for a long moment. Outside, a derelict's spine rotated past, stately as a whale.

"Yes," she said finally. "I've known since the second day. Some of my memories have a second edge to them. A kitchen with yellow tile. A song hummed off-key. They're not mine and they're not exactly anyone's anymore — they're worn smooth, like stones in a river. But they're warm, Lena. Whoever she was, whatever's left of her in me, it's *warm*, and I'm not going to apologize for that."

"I'm not asking you to apologize." Lena's voice came out lower than she intended. "I'm asking you to understand that it's obscene. That some machine took my sister apart and made you out of the pieces, and now I'm supposed to fly next to you for three more days and feel *grateful*."

"Are you grateful?"

"I don't know what I am!"

The words cracked out of her and echoed in the small cockpit, and Ada didn't flinch, and that was somehow worse. The static rose a little, a tide against the hull, and for a moment Lena could have sworn she heard something in it — a hummed song, off-key.

Ada's hand moved, slow, deliberate, and rested on the armrest between them. Not touching her. Just there. An offer with no pressure behind it, like a door left ajar.

"You can hate me," Ada said. "I've thought about it, and I think you're allowed. I'm the universe's cruelest joke played on a woman who's been alone too long. But I'm also the closest thing to her you will ever hold again, and we both know it, and that's why you can't just hate me. That's the trap. That's the *dead space* — the part between grief and gratitude where nothing answers."

Lena stared at the hand. At the fine printed lines of the palm, the small scar on the thumb that hadn't existed until the fabricators put it there and that Ada touched, sometimes, absently, like a rosary.

She didn't take the hand. She didn't move away either.

"KEST," she said, her voice rough. "Status on the interference gradient."

"Gradient steepening," the AI answered, gentle as a nurse changing the subject. "We will enter the outer interference envelope in approximately fourteen hours. Advisory: at current core degradation rates, the colony pattern will fall below recovery threshold in nineteen days. Perhaps seventeen."

"Seventeen days," Lena repeated. She looked out at the debris, at the frozen rainbow bloom of the ruptured tender, at all the ships that had carried everyone she had ever failed to save. "Then we'd better not waste any more of them fighting about whose grief is worse."

"It's not a competition," Ada said softly.

"No. It's a rescue." Lena strapped herself tighter into the cradle and pulled the scope data toward her. "Get some rest. Real rest, if your kind does that. In fourteen hours I need your hands, and whatever's inside them."

Ada held her gaze a moment longer, then nodded and closed her eyes. And in the quiet that followed, with the dead ships drifting past and the static breathing against the hull like a sleeping animal, Lena felt the draft under the door again — and this time, deliberately, she let it in.

Voices. Hundreds of them, thousands, thin as wire and far as weather, all saying the same thing in ten thousand different ways, in words and in wordless pulses of need:

*Come find us. Come find us before we forget ourselves.*

Lena's hands were steady on the controls. Her heart was not.

Fourteen hours to the envelope's edge. And there — KEST's scope painted it in cold probability curves — something waiting at the heart of the interference. A structure. A shape that had no business existing in dead space, pulsing faintly at the exact center of ten thousand dissolving minds, like a heart deciding whether to keep beating.

"Ada," Lena said quietly. "You need to see this."
