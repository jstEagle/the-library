## 10. Signal Found

The morning bell was KEST's idea.

Lena had argued against it — *I've been waking up on my own for six years, thank you* — but the AI had insisted that a colony needed rituals, and so at 0600 station time a soft chime rolled through the corridors of Relay Station Kestrel like water finding its level. She lay in her bunk and listened to it, and to the sounds underneath it: footsteps in the hydroponics bay two decks down, the murmur of voices in the mess hall, someone — she thought it might be Oren Vail, the agronomist who had woken whole three weeks ago — laughing at something before the laughter had even finished forming.

Ten thousand minds had gone into the static. Not all of them had come back out. But four hundred and twelve had, in one degree of wholeness or another, and every one of them made noise now, and the noise was the most beautiful thing Lena had ever heard.

She got up. She dressed. She walked the corridor from her quarters to the operations deck, and it took her twice as long as it used to because people kept saying good morning.

"Morning, Lena." Devi Okonkwo, who had been a child of nine when Halcyon went silent and was now a woman of thirty-four with her mother's laugh and no memory of her father's face. "KEST says the north array's coupling is drifting again."

"It's always drifting. It's a forty-year-old coupling." Lena fell into step beside her. "I'll look at it after breakfast."

"You could teach me," Devi said. "The repairs. In case you ever want a day off."

Lena stopped walking. It wasn't the offer itself — it was the shape of it, the casual assumption underneath it that there would be a future in which repairs needed doing, in which days off existed, in which someone else would be here to do them. For six years she had been the only pair of hands on the station, and hands alone don't get to imagine being replaced.

"I'd like that," she said, and her voice came out rougher than she meant it to.

Devi smiled and kept walking toward the mess hall, and Lena stood in the corridor for a moment longer than necessary, letting the ordinary weight of the moment settle into her bones.

* * *

Ada was already on the operations deck when Lena arrived, which was unusual, because Ada didn't sleep so much as she *cycled*, and her cycles had been getting stranger since the anchor process ended. She sat in the second chair — the chair that had been empty for six years — with her eyes half-closed and her fingers resting on the console, and the displays around her moved in patterns that weren't quite any interface Lena had built.

She looked better than she had in the static. Everyone said that about everyone, but with Ada it was true in a way that was hard to name. The upload had taken pieces of her — Lena knew that, had felt it happen through their joined neural link, had watched parts of Ada dissolve into the pattern to hold ten thousand minds together while they reassembled. What remained was not less, exactly. It was more distributed. When Ada looked at you now, it sometimes seemed like she was looking through you at everything else at once.

"Good morning," Ada said, without opening her eyes. "You're thinking loudly again."

"I'm not jacked in."

"You don't have to be. You're still carrying residue. We both are." She opened her eyes, and they were brown, and human, and only occasionally flickered with light that wasn't hers. "The north array is drifting."

"So I hear. I'm becoming a very predictable person."

"You were always predictable. That's why the pattern chose your template." Ada said it lightly, but Lena heard what lived under it — the same thing that lived under everything Ada said now, the knowledge that she was partly built from records of a woman named Mara Voss, Lena's sister, dead sixty years and uploaded at the end and never recovered whole. Ada was not Mara. They had established that in the first terrible week, in words shouted and then wept over and finally accepted. But Ada contained echoes of her, the way a bell contains the shape of its note, and some mornings Lena caught those echoes moving behind Ada's face and had to leave the room.

This was not one of those mornings. This morning it just felt like company.

"Status?" Lena asked, settling into her chair.

"Three hundred and eleven stable. Sixty-one fragmentary but improving. Forty are..." Ada paused, choosing the word with care, because the word mattered. "Sleeping. Deep pattern recovery. Some of them may surface. Some may not. I can't promise."

"I know."

"And the rest—" Ada's fingers moved on the console, and the display shifted: a field of dim points scattered through the interference zone like embers on a dark sea. "The rest are still out there. Thinner than we can reach yet. But not gone. The anchor is holding them. As long as Kestrel keeps broadcasting, they keep... cohering. Slowly."

"Then we keep broadcasting."

"We keep broadcasting," Ada agreed, and something passed between them that needed no link to travel — the quiet pact of two people who had built a lifeboat together and now intended to sail it forever if they had to.

* * *

The work filled the day the way work does when there is enough of it and it matters. Lena replaced the north array coupling with Devi watching over her shoulder, asking questions that were actually good questions, and by the end of it Devi had soldered two of the junctions herself, badly, and Lena had let them stay bad until tomorrow, because teaching meant tolerating bad solder.

In the afternoon she sat in on the memorial circle in the mess hall — a new ritual, Ada's design — where the colonists read the names of those still sleeping or lost, not as grief exactly, but as attendance-taking. *We know you're here. We're waiting.* Lena had resisted the first few circles; she had spent six years talking to people who couldn't answer and had sworn she'd never do it again. But this was different. This was talking *with* people about people, a chorus instead of a solo, and when they reached the Voss names — her parents, her sister, her grandmother, an uncle she'd barely known — Devi put a hand on her shoulder without being asked, and Lena found she could say the names out loud without her throat closing.

*Mara Voss,* she said, and Ada, sitting across the circle, said it too, at the same moment, in the same cadence, and neither of them flinched.

* * *

Evening. The observation blister, her old refuge, now with two chairs in it because Ada had welded the second one there herself, badly, and Lena had let it stay.

Below them Halcyon turned, green and cloud-wrapped and real, its cities dark but its fields recovering, tended now by colonists who had woken with farming in their hands even when they'd lost their own names. The relay's signal washed over the planet in a slow pulse — the anchor broadcast, the lullaby, the promise: *you are held, you are held, come back when you can.*

"I updated the log," Lena said.

Ada tilted her head.

"The maintenance log. The one I've been recording for six years." Lena turned her cup in her hands. "I recorded a final entry today. Entry 2,191. Except it isn't really a log anymore, is it? A log implies no one's listening."

"What did you call it?"

"A letter." She laughed softly. "First letter I've written in six years that anyone will read."

"Will you play it?"

Lena hesitated. Then she keyed the console, and her own voice filled the blister, warm and slightly rough, recorded that afternoon in the operations chair she'd occupied alone for six years:

*"Maintenance log, final entry. Station Kestrel, day 2,191. All systems nominal. Antenna couplings holding. Life support green across all decks. Current population: four hundred and twelve, plus one resident ghost of uncertain classification who insists she's not a ghost, and one station AI who has developed a sense of humor, which I'm told is my fault.*

*"To whoever finds this: the colony of Halcyon is not destroyed. It is here. It is incomplete, and it is grieving, and it is planting things. It sings off-key in the mess hall and argues about ration schedules and yesterday someone painted a mural on the wall of deck three that I am legally, morally unable to remove because it's genuinely very good.*

*"For six years I talked into the dark and believed no one answered. I was wrong. Something was always listening — the static, the dead, the almost-dead, the waiting. And when I finally stopped broadcasting and started building a place for the signal to land, they came home.*

*"So this is the last entry addressed to no one. From here on, these are letters. Addressed to Devi, who is learning to solder. To Oren, whose tomatoes are a crime against botany. To the forty who sleep, and the thousands still adrift: hold on. We're coming. We have time now.*

*"And to Ada — who is not my sister, and is not a stranger, and taught me that family is a thing you build rather than inherit — thank you for making me answer the door.*

*"End of log. Beginning of everything else. Kestrel out."*

The playback ended. The blister was quiet except for the hum of the arrays, the great slow heartbeat of the anchor, singing down at the world.

Ada was crying, silently, the way she did everything now — distributed, diffuse, tears arriving at her eyes from somewhere else entirely.

"You left out the part where you hated me for a week," she said.

"The historical record doesn't need everything."

"No," Ada said. "It needs the important things." She reached over and took Lena's hand, and her grip was warm and solid and faintly, faintly threaded with static, like a voice carried a long way on a clear night. "It needs this."

Outside, Halcyon turned beneath them, and somewhere in the interference sea beyond it, ten thousand embers pulsed in answer to the anchor's song — dim, patient, unlost.

Lena watched them, and squeezed back, and for the first time in six years, she was not keeping watch.

She was keeping company.
