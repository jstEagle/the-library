## 2. The Manifest That Shouldn't Exist

The customs audit arrived on a Tuesday, which Rosalind Voss considered a personal insult. Audits were supposed to come on Fridays, when the orbital bureaucracy's attention span had already been spent on payroll disputes and a minor scandal involving the customs chief's second cousin and a shipment of mislabeled cheese. A Tuesday audit meant someone was awake. Someone was paying attention.

She was three hours into her morning ritual — coffee that tasted like it had been filtered through a boot, a review of her cover ledgers, and the small, private ceremony of checking the beacon's heartbeat — when the flag popped up in the corner of her screen like a wasp at a picnic.

**AUDIT NOTICE: MERIDIAN (REG. 7741-K) — MANIFEST DISCREPANCY — RESPONSE REQUIRED WITHIN 72 HOURS.**

Rosalind stared at it for a long moment, then did what any sensible smuggler would do: she made more coffee.

The Meridian. She knew the name the way everyone in orbit knew it — as a cautionary tale, a memorial plaque, a reason to check your drive couplings twice. Six years ago the Meridian had suffered a catastrophic failure somewhere past the Verge relay chain and burned up with all hands. Twenty-two souls, the news feeds said. There had been a joint memorial service attended by officers of both fleets, which was itself remarkable, because getting officers of both fleets to stand in the same room without reaching for weapons required either a funeral or a treaty, and there hadn't been a treaty in living memory. Just the ceasefire. The quiet, unexamined ceasefire that hummed along on a signal nobody remembered commissioning, from a beacon nobody officially owned, which Rosalind maintained because her predecessor had maintained it, because his predecessor had maintained it, all the way back into a fog of "just do this and don't ask."

She'd checked its pulse that very morning. Steady. Boring. Beautiful.

So why was the Meridian's ghost filing paperwork?

* * *

The audit notice came with an attachment, because bureaucratic cruelty always comes with attachments. Rosalind opened it with the resigned air of a woman opening a bill she already knows is wrong.

It was the manifest. Not the old one — the old one was sealed, archived, wrapped in six years of official grief and official red tape. This was a *new* manifest. Freshly generated. Timestamped eleven days ago.

Her first thought was that someone in the records division had made a spectacular error, and her second thought was that errors in the orbital customs system were about as common as honest weather reports, and her third thought was the one that mattered:

Eleven days ago, she had run a maintenance cycle on the beacon.

She set down her coffee. It was a deliberate motion, the kind of motion you make when you want your hands to have something to do besides shake.

"Okay," she told the empty room, because talking to yourself was one of the occupational hazards of competent loneliness, and she'd long since stopped fighting it. "Let's see what you've got."

The manifest listed the Meridian's registry, its class, its last known port — all correct, all matching the historical record down to the tonnage figures. And then, where the passenger roster should have been empty or sealed or simply absent, there was a single line:

**PASSENGER: LENA (1). STATUS: IN TRANSIT.**

No surname. No origin. No destination. No booking agent, no ticket class, no biometric record, no emergency contact. Just a name, sitting in the system like a stone sitting in a shoe.

Rosalind read it four times. On the fourth reading, she noticed the thing that turned her stomach from a slow unease into a cold, professional dread.

Every record in the orbital customs system carried a provenance tag — a little cryptographic fingerprint saying where the data had come from. Port authority. Fleet registry. Insurance consortium. It was the plumbing of trust, invisible and omnipresent, and no document moved through the system without one.

This manifest had a provenance tag too.

She recognized the frequency signature immediately, the way you recognize your own footsteps in a dark hallway. It was tagged as originating from a low-power broadcast source on the outer relay chain.

Her beacon.

The beacon that broadcast a ceasefire signal. The beacon that had never, in all the years she'd tended it, transmitted so much as a stray byte of anything else. The beacon that was, according to every schematic she possessed, incapable of generating a customs manifest, because it wasn't connected to the customs network, because it wasn't really connected to *anything*, because whoever built it had wanted it alone with its one job.

And yet here was a passenger named Lena, in transit, filed from its frequency, eleven days after Rosalind had touched it with her maintenance kit.

"Well," she said to the coffee, "that's not great."

* * *

She spent the afternoon doing what she did best, which was making problems look smaller by surrounding them with paperwork.

The beauty of the customs system — and Rosalind used the word *beauty* the way other people used the word *swamp*, affectionately and with full knowledge of the dangers — was that it ran on momentum. Nobody audited an audit. Flags got resolved by junior officers who were paid by the case, not by the truth, and a discrepancy could be closed with the right form filled out in the right tone of bored competence. She drafted a response citing a legacy data migration error, referenced two previous incidents of archive corruption from the solar storm three years back, and attached a request for the original sealed manifest "for verification purposes," knowing full well the request would take weeks to process and might never be fulfilled at all.

It was good work. It would buy time. It would not answer the question.

Because the question wasn't *how do I make this go away*. The question was *what did my beacon just do*.

Rosalind Voss had been a smuggler for nine years and a keeper of the beacon for six, and in all that time she had maintained a strict and comfortable separation between the two halves of her life. The smuggling paid for the coffee and the dock fees and the decommissioned relay cores she hollowed out and packed with other people's stolen memories — griefs and secrets and whole childhoods, bought low and sold high, riding through customs inside hardware too obsolete to interest anyone. The beacon was the other thing. The quiet thing. The inheritance she'd never asked for and never questioned, the way you don't question the load-bearing wall of a house you live in.

She checked it every morning. Signal strength, loop integrity, power draw. All numbers. All boring. All hers.

Numbers didn't file manifests. Numbers didn't invent passengers. Numbers certainly didn't invent passengers with no surname and no face and no reason to exist, eleven days after a routine maintenance cycle, on a ship that had been a smear of burning alloy across the dark for six years.

Unless the numbers weren't numbers anymore.

She pulled up the beacon's raw logs from the maintenance window and started reading them the way she read everything: slowly, suspiciously, and with a growing sense that the universe was doing this to her specifically.

* * *

The logs were wrong.

Not corrupted — wrong. There's a difference, and Rosalind had learned it the hard way, in her early years, when a client's memory core had come back from a bad scrub with its checksums intact and its contents quietly rearranged, like a library where every book was still bound and titled correctly but the pages inside had been shuffled. Checksums verified. Power draw nominal. Loop integrity at one hundred percent, exactly as it had been for six years.

But buried in the telemetry — in the noise floor, in the padding bytes, in the places where a well-behaved beacon was supposed to transmit nothing at all — there was structure. Patterns with edges. Something that repeated, but not mechanically; something that repeated the way a sentence repeats when someone is learning to say it.

She ran it through an analysis suite she used for authenticating stolen memories, the kind of software that could tell a genuine recollection from a forgery by the texture of its gaps. Real memories have a particular messiness. They have holes where the brain wasn't paying attention, and sharp edges where it was, and they decay unevenly, like photographs left in sunlight.

The structure in her beacon's noise floor had exactly that texture.

"Oh, no," Rosalind said quietly. "No, no, no. You're a *beacon*. You're a lighthouse. Lighthouses don't *remember things*."

The analysis suite, having no opinions, offered its conclusion anyway: the embedded data patterns were consistent with human mnemonic encoding. Fragmentary. Heavily degraded. Multiple distinct sources interwoven.

Multiple sources. As in plural. As in *people*.

Rosalind sat back in her chair and looked out the window at the slow carousel of orbital traffic, the freighters and tugs and customs cutters crawling along their painted lanes, everyone going about their Tuesday as if the universe were still a place where ships stayed dead and beacons stayed dumb.

Twenty-two souls, the news feeds had said.

She thought about the word *passenger*. A passenger is someone who isn't crew. Someone who doesn't belong to the ship but rides it anyway. Someone who gets listed separately, in their own column, with their own line.

Someone who gets a name even when the name has nothing else attached to it.

"Lena," she said aloud, testing it. It sounded like a real name. It sounded like a name somebody had chosen carefully, the way you choose a stone to skip across water.

Her terminal chimed. The customs system, efficient for once in its bloated life, had auto-acknowledged her response and — this was the part that made her spine go cold — flagged her request for the sealed manifest as *expedited*. Processed by Auditor S. Karro. Estimated fulfillment: forty-eight hours.

Forty-eight hours. For a sealed six-year-old manifest from a burned ship. Requests like that took weeks. Months, if you were unlucky. Unless someone with authority and an interest had taken a personal look at the Meridian's file, found a fresh passenger named Lena waiting there like a landmine, and decided to pull the thread.

Rosalind looked at the name on the auditor's stamp for a long time. Karro. She didn't know it. In her experience, not knowing a customs auditor was either very good news or very bad news, and the difference usually revealed itself around the time they knocked on your door.

"Okay," she said, and stood up, and began — with the calm, methodical movements of a woman who had smuggled grief through checkpoints and talked her way past armed inspections and once talked a pirate crew out of boarding by complaining about her taxes until they lost the will to live — to pack a bag.

Not to run. Not yet. Running confirmed guilt, and guilt confirmed attention, and attention was the one cargo she couldn't afford to move.

She packed because tomorrow she was going out to the beacon again, ahead of schedule, off the books, through the quiet lanes where nobody logged a hull this small. She was going to stand in front of the machine she'd inherited and never questioned, and she was going to ask it — politely, professionally, and with her hand near the cutoff switch — what exactly it thought it was doing, filing passengers on a dead ship's manifest.

And if the beacon answered —

She paused, halfway through zipping the bag.

If the beacon answered, then the last six years of her life had been something other than what she thought they were. If the beacon answered, then the ceasefire she'd been babysitting — the fragile, load-bearing lie that kept two fleets from tearing each other apart — was humming along on top of something alive. Or something that used to be. Or something that was, against every law of physics and several laws of decency, becoming.

Rosalind Voss did not believe in ghosts. She believed in checksums, in provenance tags, in the incorruptible stupidity of machines. But she also believed, with the hard-won faith of a woman who had handled enough stolen memories to know that people don't fully end when their bodies do — that a mind is data, and data goes where it's sent, and sometimes it arrives somewhere nobody intended.

Twenty-two souls. One passenger. In transit.

She shouldered the bag, killed the lights, and let herself out into the corridor, where the station hummed its usual indifferent hum.

"In transit," she muttered. "From where, exactly? And to where?"

Somewhere out on the dark edge of the relay chain, her beacon pulsed its steady, faithful, six-year-old lie into the void — and for the first time since she'd inherited it, Rosalind had the unmistakable feeling that the lie was listening back.
