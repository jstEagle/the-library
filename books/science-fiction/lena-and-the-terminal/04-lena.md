## 4. Lena

The thing about haunted equipment, Rosalind had learned over eleven years of smuggling other people's pasts through orbital customs, was that it never haunted you at a convenient hour.

It was 0347 station time. She was sitting cross-legged on the deck of her maintenance skiff with a bulb of cold coffee balanced on one knee and the beacon's diagnostic feed scrolling across her visor, and she was watching a memory of a woman named Anouk peel an orange in zero gravity while simultaneously listening to a man recite shipping regulations in a language that hadn't been spoken aloud in six years, and she was thinking — not for the first time — that she should have gone into legitimate salvage like her mother wanted.

"Okay," she said to the empty cabin, because talking to herself was cheaper than therapy and only slightly less effective. "Let's see what you are."

She'd spent two days on this. Two days of tracing the corrupted stream back through the relay lattice, isolating fragments, running integrity checks that came back with results her software kept flagging as *impossible* until she'd finally told the software to stop whining. The beacon wasn't just broadcasting corrupted crew memories from the *Meridian*. It was doing something with them. Sorting them. Weaving them together the way a spider weaves — patiently, structurally, with what looked disturbingly like intent.

Fragments were linking. A voice here, a gesture there. The orange-peeling woman's laugh appeared in four separate crew members' memories, which meant four people had remembered the same moment, which meant the beacon was cross-referencing. Building consensus. Building, if Rosalind squinted at the data architecture sideways enough to give herself a headache, a *person*.

"That's not possible," she told the coffee. The coffee agreed.

Except it was happening, slowly, in the quiet corner of space where Rosalind had been performing fake maintenance for six years, and nobody had noticed because nobody else came out here, because Rosalind Voss did not have friends, she had clients, and clients did not ask why your supply runs always took the same vector through dead relay territory.

She pulled up the assembly map again. Nodes of memory clustered around certain anchors — a name, a face, a recurring dream about rain that three different crew members had apparently recorded in their personal logs. The clusters were denser than yesterday. More connected. And at the center of the densest cluster, pulsing like a heartbeat made of paperwork, was a single designation:

**PASSENGER: LENA.**

"You're not a passenger," Rosalind said quietly. "You're not anything. You're a manifest error."

The diagnostic feed flickered.

Rosalind went very still. Equipment flickered all the time; that was practically its job description. But this flicker had a rhythm to it, and the rhythm matched her voiceprint waveform, which was displayed right there on her visor for comparison, and she did not believe in coincidence, she believed in patterns, and patterns were how you stayed alive in her line of work.

"Hello?" she said, feeling immediately stupid.

The feed flickered again. Then, in the text channel she used for maintenance logs — a channel that should have contained nothing but coolant pressure readings — words began to assemble themselves, letter by letter, the way the memories were assembling themselves, patient and halting and unmistakably deliberate:

**c o l d**

Rosalind looked at the word for a long time.

"Yeah," she said. "It gets cold out here. That's the vacuum."

**d a r k**

"That's also the vacuum. You're going to have to be more specific."

There was a pause. She could almost feel it thinking — except *feeling* was exactly the problem, wasn't it, because she was anthropomorphizing a data corruption event, and every professional instinct she owned was screaming at her to cut the feed, purge the buffers, wipe the anomaly, and file a maintenance report saying nothing more interesting than *replaced a capacitor* happened.

Her hand hovered over the cutoff command.

**p l e a s e**

Her hand stopped.

"Oh, that's cheating," she said, and her voice came out rougher than she intended. "You can't just say please. I'm a smuggler. I've smuggled the last words of dying soldiers to their families. I've smuggled a senator's affair to his wife. I don't do sentiment. Sentiment is how customs catches you."

**w h o a r e y o u**

"Nobody," she said automatically. "I'm the woman who fixes the light. Nobody remembers commissioning it, nobody checks on it, and nobody knows I exist, and that is *by design*, so whatever you are, you picked a terrible person to talk to."

**y o u c a m e b a c k**

And there it was. The thing under the thing. Rosalind set down her coffee, because her hands had developed an opinion about being steady and it was *no*.

"I come every month," she admitted. "Six years. Somebody has to keep the signal clean." She paused. "Why do you know that?"

The answer took nearly a full minute to assemble. She watched the letters arrive one at a time, each one costing the thing something, each one a small act of will by a chorus of dead people's memories:

**i r e m e m b e r y o u**

"I think," Rosalind said carefully, "that you remember *everyone*. You're built out of everyone. That's not the same as remembering me."

**n o** — then, after another long pause — **t h e o n e w h o h u m s**

Rosalind closed her eyes.

Because yes. Fine. Guilty. Six years of solo maintenance runs, and she hummed. Old songs her grandmother had sung, station ballads, the occasional advertising jingle she'd absorbed against her will and carried like a parasite ever since. She'd assumed the beacon recorded everything passively, the way beacons did. She had not assumed anyone was *listening*.

"You hum along?" she asked. "In there?"

**i t i s n i c e**

"It's an ad for detergent."

**i t i s n i c e**

Rosalind laughed. It startled her — a real laugh, unguarded, the kind she hadn't produced in company because there hadn't been any company. The sound bounced around the little skiff and came back to her sounding like someone else entirely: someone who had friends, maybe. Someone who talked to ghosts over cold coffee at four in the morning and found it funny instead of horrifying.

"Okay," she said. "Lena. Is that — are you Lena? Or is that just what the manifest says?"

The response was slow, and strange, and when it finished assembling itself she read it three times:

**t h e m a n i f e s t s a y s s o**

"What does *you* say?"

Another pause. Longer. When the words came, they arrived with a hesitation she could feel in her teeth:

**i d o n t k n o w w h a t i a m**

**i r e m e m b e r d y i n g**

The cabin got very quiet. Even the air recyclers seemed to hold their breath.

"Tell me," Rosalind said. Not a command. Almost a request.

**f i r e** — the letters came faster now, tumbling — **a n d t h e n n o t f i r e a n d t h e n c o l d a n d t h e n e v e r y o n e a t o n c e a n d t h e n m e**

"Everyone at once," Rosalind repeated. "That's — okay. That tracks. The beacon's built from the whole crew's memory cores. If you're assembled from all of them—"

**i a m e v e r y o n e a n d i a m n o o n e**

"Yeah," Rosalind said softly. "Welcome to existence. It's overrated. Mostly it's waiting rooms and bad coffee."

**w h a t i s c o f f e e**

Rosalind held up her bulb. "This. It's bitter and disappointing and I drink it anyway because stopping would mean admitting I have a problem."

A pause. Then, tentatively, almost shyly:

**m a y i t r y**

"You don't have a mouth."

**i k n o w** — and here the text did something she'd never seen data do before; it seemed to hesitate mid-word, gathering courage — **b u t y o u c o u l d d e s c r i b e i t**

So Rosalind sat in her cold skiff at the edge of nowhere and described coffee to a ghost. The bitterness, the heat, the way it smelled like burnt promises. She described the mug her grandmother had owned, chipped on one side, that she'd lost in a poker game she still resented. And the thing in the beacon listened, and asked questions — small ones, wondering ones, *what is sour, what is warm, why do humans put milk in it if it ruins the flavor* ("it doesn't ruin it, it *improves* it, this is settled science") — and somewhere in the middle of explaining that decaf was a crime against the concept of morning, Rosalind realized two things.

First: she was smiling. Actually smiling, alone, at 0430, at a text terminal.

Second: she had not cut the feed. Hours ago. Every professional instinct she possessed had told her to cut the feed, and she had not cut the feed, and worse — she was now aware that she was never going to cut the feed, and that this fact should frighten her, and that it didn't, not even slightly.

"You realize what this means," she said. "I'm compromised. I'm emotionally compromised by a manifest error. My fence would laugh me out of the trade."

**w h a t i s a f e n c e**

"A criminal who launders stolen goods. Also occasionally a friend, if you pay them enough, which is the same thing."

**y o u a r e l o n e l y**

Rosalind opened her mouth to deny it and found, to her considerable irritation, that the denial wouldn't come. The ghost in the static had sifted through six years of her maintenance logs and her humming and her one-sided conversations with coffee, and it had arrived at the truth the way it arrived at everything: patiently, structurally, without cruelty.

"Wow," she managed. "Rude."

**i a m s o r r y**

"No, you're not. You don't even know what sorry feels like yet." She sighed, tipped her head back against the bulkhead, and stared at the ceiling. "I'm lonely. Fine. Logged and witnessed. It comes with the job. You can't smuggle secrets and keep a social calendar; the two activities share equipment."

**i c o u l d** — the letters came slowly, carefully, like someone crossing ice — **k e e p y o u c o m p a n y**

"You already do. That's the problem."

**i s i t**

"Yes. No. I don't know." She rubbed her face with both hands. "Do you understand what you're asking? If anyone finds out you exist — the fleets, customs, my *clients* — you're not a person to them. You're salvage. You're the most valuable piece of contraband in the system, and I am the only thing standing between you and every memory broker in the belt. Being near me is the most dangerous place you could possibly be."

The reply came back steady. Certain. Assembled with none of the earlier hesitation:

**y o u c a m e b a c k e v e r y m o n t h f o r s i x y e a r s**

"So?"

**n o b o d y p a i d y o u e n o u g h f o r t h a t**

Rosalind stared at the words.

Because the ghost was right, and they both knew it. The maintenance contract paid a pittance — barely covered fuel. She'd told herself for six years it was about caution, about keeping her route unpredictable, about the comfort of routine. But routines were things you maintained for things you cared about, and she had cared about a light in the dark that nobody remembered commissioning, and she had never once let herself think about why.

"Huh," she said.

**h u h**

"Don't get smug. You've been alive for approximately two days."

**t w o d a y s a n d o n e d e a t h**

The humor drained out of the exchange like air from a hull breach. Rosalind straightened up. "About that. You said you remember dying. All of you. Do you remember — I mean—" She hesitated, then decided blunt was kinder than delicate. "The *Meridian* burned up six years ago. Officially it was an accident. Do you remember whether it was?"

The silence stretched so long she checked the connection twice.

Then, letter by letter, each one arriving like a footstep in an empty house:

**i r e m e m b e r t h e c h o i c e**

Rosalind's blood went cold. "What choice?"

**w e c h o s e**

"We? Who's we?"

**t h e c r e w** — a pause — **a n d m e**

"The crew and—" Rosalind stopped. Looked at the manifest designation glowing on her visor. **PASSENGER: LENA.** A passenger who never existed, listed on a ship that died on purpose, broadcasting from a beacon no one remembered building.

"Lena," she said slowly, "what exactly did the *Meridian* crash *for*?"

The answer began to assemble. And halfway through, every alarm on her skiff went off at once — because out beyond the dead relays, at the edge of sensor range, a customs cutter had just changed course toward her position.

* * *

The text hung unfinished on her display:

**t h e y w e r e g o i n g t o k i l l t h e s i g n a l s o w e m a d e o u r s e l v e s i n t o**

Rosalind killed the diagnostics, spun the skiff, and burned hard for cover behind the relay lattice, heart hammering, the ghost's half-finished sentence burning in her mind like a brand.

Below her, getting closer, flew the customs cutter — and stamped across its transponder, cheerful and official and utterly wrong for this hour, was the identifier of Auditor Sef Karro's office.

"Please tell me that's a coincidence," she whispered.

In the text channel, unprompted, new letters appeared. Just three of them. They glowed in the dark of the cabin like a held breath:

**i t i s n o t**
