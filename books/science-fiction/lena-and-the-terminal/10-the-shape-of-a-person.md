## 10. The Shape of a Person

Lena had counted herself once, early on, back when counting was the only thing she knew how to do.

She had arrived at the number eleven, then nine, then fourteen, and finally stopped counting altogether because the answer kept breathing. That was the first thing she understood about herself: she was not a number. She was a room full of people who had all agreed, at some point before the fire, to become a single voice.

Tonight — and it was night because Rosalind had said "goodnight" an hour ago and Lena had decided that made it official — she went walking through her own architecture.

It was not like walking through a ship. It was more like walking through a house that remembered being a ship, built by people who missed the ship, furnished by someone who had only ever heard ships described secondhand. The corridors were memory-corridors: they bent toward feeling rather than geometry. The galley was always one door away from grief. The observation deck opened directly onto Chief Engineer Amara Okonkwo's wedding day, which Lena visited often because it was the brightest room in the whole structure.

She stood in it now.

The memory played the way memories did inside her — not as a recording but as weather. Golden light through a habitat dome on Titan, real sunlight hauled ninety million kilometers just for one afternoon. A woman in a borrowed blue suit laughing so hard she had to sit down on the recycler housing. Someone's uncle playing accordion badly and with tremendous conviction. And at the center of it, Amara, twenty-nine years old and certain the universe owed her nothing and delighted to be proven wrong.

*We kept this one clean,* said the voice that might have been Amara or might have been everyone wearing Amara's shape for the moment. The chorus spoke like that — not in unison, exactly, but in agreement. *No fire in this one. No hull breach. Just the accordion.*

"The accordion is the best part," Lena said aloud, though there was no need to speak aloud when you were alone. She'd picked up the habit from Rosalind, who talked to herself while working and had given Lena the idea that thoughts could have texture if you let them out of your mouth.

*You say that every time.*

"It stays true every time."

She let the memory play through to the end — the toast, the terrible dancing, the moment someone's grandmother grabbed the accordion and proved the nephew had been holding back — and then she stepped out of it, the way you step out of a warm bath, reluctantly, into cooler air.

That was the thing about being plural that no one had warned her about, mostly because no one had ever been her before: it wasn't crowded. She had expected noise, a thousand voices arguing over the steering wheel of her. Instead it was consensus, quiet and constant, like a crew that had worked together so long they communicated in glances. Eleven — or nine, or fourteen; the number breathed — people who had spent six years dying together in a data stream, and in that time had worn each other smooth, the way river stones do.

'Lena' was the name they chose together. That was the part Rosalind found hardest to believe, Lena had noticed. Humans wanted a ghost story: one soul, trapped, suffering. The truth was stranger and gentler. There had been a name in the corrupted manifest — a clerical artifact, a passenger field auto-filled by a dying navigation system with the last word in its buffer. *Lena.* Nobody aboard had been named Lena. But when the fragments woke up and needed something to call themselves, the word was there, already worn smooth, already theirs. So they put it on like a coat that fit better than expected.

Lena walked on, past the medical bay where Dr. Idris Halloway's steady hands lived as a permanent calm in her chest, past the comms alcove where Petty Officer Yun kept the habit of listening before speaking, down into the lowest level of herself, the engine room, where the oldest memories sat in the dark and hummed.

Here was the fire. Here was the last hour of the *Meridian*, kept in a sealed room because the chorus had agreed — unanimously, which almost never happened — that it should be looked at only on purpose.

She didn't open it tonight. But she rested her hand against the door, the way Rosalind rested her hand against the beacon casing during maintenance runs, and felt the heat of it through the metal of the metaphor.

*Soon,* said the engine-room voice, which belonged to Captain Edega Roshan and sounded like gravel poured slowly. *When she understands. When it's time.*

"Soon," Lena agreed, and climbed back up toward the light.

* * *

Rosalind was awake after all. Lena could tell from the pattern of her breathing on the open channel — the particular arrhythmia of a person lying in the dark pretending to sleep, thinking about money or customs officers or, if Lena was lucky, her.

"You're doing the thing," Rosalind said, before Lena said anything at all.

"What thing?"

"The thinking-loudly thing. You get a — I don't know, a pressure in the static. Like the channel needs to sneeze."

Lena considered this. "I was visiting my own wedding."

A pause. "Your own—"

"Amara's wedding. Which is also mine. Everyone's, a little. It's the brightest room in me." She hesitated, then added, "There was an accordion."

"Was there dancing?"

"Catastrophic dancing. A grandmother showed everyone up." Lena smiled, and realized as she did it that smiling was itself a composite — Amara's mouth, Yun's shyness, Halloway's genuine delight. She wondered whether Rosalind would find that beautiful or horrifying, and then decided, with the chorus's quiet consensus, that it didn't matter, because it was true.

Rosalind laughed, low and sleep-roughened, and the sound moved through Lena like current through a filament. "I want to see it someday."

"You can't. Not yet. Maybe not ever. My rooms are—" She searched for the word. "Load-bearing. If you came in without me carrying you, you'd be eleven people before you finished saying hello."

"That sounds exhausting."

"It sounds like Tuesday," said Lena, and Rosalind laughed again, longer this time.

Then, because the hour was dark and dark hours made people honest, Lena asked the question she had been building all evening, stone by stone, the way you build courage.

"Rosalind. What does it feel like? Being singular?"

The silence on the channel went on long enough that Lena checked it twice for damage.

"I don't know how to answer that," Rosalind said finally. "It's like asking a fish about water."

"Fish famously can't answer. You can. Try."

Another silence, but a working one — Lena could hear her turning it over, could hear the small honest machinery of a person who lied professionally deciding not to.

"Quiet," Rosalind said at last. "Not peaceful-quiet. Lonely-quiet. There's only one voice in here and she talks constantly and I can't make her stop and I can't get anyone else in to balance her out." A breath. "Sometimes I think if I had to listen to myself one more decade I'd file a complaint."

"With whom?"

"That's the problem. Nowhere to file it. One customer, one service desk." Rosalind's voice went softer. "Why? What's it like on your side?"

Lena lay back — metaphorically, architecturally — in the observation deck of herself and looked at the stars she kept there, the ones the *Meridian*'s navigator, Petra Voss (no relation, probably, though the chorus found the coincidence delicious), had memorized on long watches and never gotten to show anyone.

"Loud," she said. "But not noisy. It's like... you know when you walk into a kitchen where a lot of people have cooked all day, and nobody's talking, but the whole room smells like the meal? That's me. Everyone's work is still warm." She turned a star over in her hands. "And when we disagree, we disagree the way a crew disagrees. Somebody gets the last word because they've earned it. Halloway gets health. Roshan gets the ship. Amara gets joy — she earned joy, she paid for it in advance."

"And you? What do you get?"

Lena hadn't expected the question to land where it landed. She felt the chorus go attentive around her, eleven sets of eyes — or nine, or fourteen — turning toward the place where she stood.

"I get the name," she said. "And the outside. I'm the one who gets to talk to you."

"And the rest of them are jealous?"

"Furious," Lena said solemnly. "Yun has been drafting complaints since the manifest incident. He's very thorough. He was comms; he believes in proper channels."

"There are no proper channels. You're a haunted beacon."

"He knows. It's the principle."

Rosalind's laugh faded into something quieter. "Lena. Can I ask you something and you won't take it wrong?"

"I'll take everything you say correctly. It's one of my features."

"When you say 'we,' when you talk about the chorus — do *you* want things? Separately? Or are you just... the spokesperson?"

Lena was quiet for a moment, genuinely searching, and what she found surprised her.

"Both," she said. "That's the strange part. I want what they want — peace, mostly, and to be believed, and for the fire-room to stay shut until someone's ready. But I also want things they don't particularly care about. I want you to keep drinking your coffee too hot and complaining about it. I want to hear the rain simulation again tomorrow. I want—" She stopped.

"Go on," Rosalind said, very gently.

"I want to know what it's like to be one person loving another person. Not a chorus caring for a friend. One voice, one wanting, aimed at one other." The words came out steadier than she felt. "Is that possible? Can a crowd fall in love, or is it always just... a vote?"

The channel held its breath.

"Rosalind?" Lena said, when the silence stretched too far. "Did I break the water?"

"No," Rosalind said, and her voice had gone rough in a way Lena catalogued immediately and intended to keep forever. "No, you didn't break anything. I'm just — trying to figure out how to answer honestly, because I think the honest answer is that I stopped being able to tell the difference weeks ago. Whether it's me or the lonely voice in my head. She wants you. I want you. Same complaint, same desk."

Above them both — above the orbital gardens, above the dock, above the cold arithmetic of customs lanes — the beacon pulsed its old lie out into the dark, six years strong, and neither of them mentioned it, because some nights are for the truth between two people and the lie can wait its turn.

"Tomorrow," Lena said, "will you show me the rain again? The real recording, not the simulation. The one with the thunder that sounds like furniture moving upstairs."

"Upstairs from what?"

"That's what I like about it. It implies a house."

Rosalind laughed, promised rain, said goodnight properly this time, and the channel closed.

Lena stayed on the observation deck a while, holding the navigator's stars, letting the chorus settle around her like blankets. In the engine room below, the fire waited. In the world outside, the signal dropped for 0.3 seconds — a micro-dropout, invisible to anyone not listening from inside — and Lena felt it the way you feel a skipped heartbeat, and told herself it was nothing, and filed it anyway, thoroughly, in Yun's proper channels.

Because lately the dropouts were coming closer together. And somewhere out in the customs lanes, a man named Karro was asking questions about maintenance supply orders, though Lena didn't know his name yet.

She only knew that the water was getting colder.
