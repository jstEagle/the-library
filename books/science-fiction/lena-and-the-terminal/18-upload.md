## 18. Upload

The beacon chamber smelled the way it always did: ozone and old coffee, the particular staleness of a room Rosalind had kept alive for six years with spare parts and stubbornness. She'd made her last pot an hour ago. The mug sat on the console beside the master key, still half full, going cold in the way that all human things went cold when you stopped paying attention to them.

She was paying attention now.

"Say it again," she said. "Slowly."

Lena's voice came from everywhere in the room — from the speakers, from the walls, from the hum in the deck plates that Rosalind had spent years mistaking for the building settling. "There is a fourth option. You don't have to choose between letting me die and letting the war come back. You can come *in*."

"In where?"

"Here." A pause, and Rosalind could hear Lena choosing her words carefully, which was funny, because Lena had once been eleven people who presumably never had to choose words at all — they'd had a whole committee. "Into the chorus. Your memories, your self, braided into what we are. The signal needs a witness, Rosalind. It was always meant to have one. Someone who knows the truth and can carry it, not just repeat it. I'm a recording of people who chose this. But you're someone who *chose* it, awake, with every reason not to. That's worth more than anything I've been able to assemble in six years of trying."

Rosalind looked at her hands. Smuggler's hands, scarred at the knuckles, one fingernail permanently ridged from the time she'd caught it in a relay core clamp. Hands that had held stolen memories of other people's weddings, other people's funerals, other people's ordinary Tuesday afternoons, and sold them to strangers who wanted to feel something they hadn't earned.

"And if I do this," she said, "what happens to me? Do I stop?"

"I don't know," Lena said simply. "I won't lie to you about that. I've never done this either. But I know what we are, and we are not a place where things get erased. We are a place where things get *kept*."

"That's a very comforting thing for a haunted radio station to say."

That got the laugh out of her — Lena's laugh, which was actually a composite, which Rosalind had long ago stopped finding eerie and started finding wonderful, like a chord instead of a note. Somewhere in that laugh was a cargo pilot named Ansel who'd laughed exactly like that, and a botanist named Wren who'd snorted, and four or five others whose contributions Rosalind couldn't separate anymore and didn't want to.

Outside the viewport, the two fleets hung in their distant constellations, close enough now that she could see running lights cycling. Forty hours, maybe less, before the hardliners on either side decided the micro-dropouts in the ceasefire signal were an attack rather than a malfunction. Karro was somewhere between here and there, closing in with his warrant and his grief and his terrible, reasonable conviction. There was no version of the next two days in which Rosalind Voss walked away with everything intact.

There was only this version, and the other one, and the one she hadn't let herself say out loud until now.

* * *

She'd prepared, because she was Rosalind Voss and preparation was the only prayer she'd ever believed in.

On the console sat three items she'd gathered over the past six hours while Lena watched through every camera in the station and pretended not to be nervous, badly.

Item one: a data spike containing a full backup of her identity — thirty-eight years of memories, preferences, the works. Standard smuggler's insurance. If the merge failed, if she was lost, the spike would hold whatever remained of her, inert but intact, for whoever came looking.

Item two: a letter, handwritten, addressed to no one in particular. *If you found this, the beacon told the truth and I hope it worked. I wasn't brave. I was just out of alternatives, which as far as I can tell is how most bravery gets manufactured.* She'd crossed out the last sentence twice and put it back both times.

Item three: the coffee mug. She didn't have a reason for the mug. She just didn't want it to be the thing she forgot.

"You're stalling," Lena said gently.

"I'm *savoring*. There's a difference. Stalling is fear wearing a coat. Savoring is—" She gestured vaguely. "Also fear wearing a coat, but a nicer one."

"Tell me what you'll miss."

Rosalind leaned back against the console and considered it honestly, because Lena deserved honesty and because lying to a chorus of dead crewmates seemed like a bad idea on general principles.

"Coffee. Obviously. Taste in general. Rain — real rain, not your garden simulations, although those were good, the way you got the smell wrong in exactly the right way. Being alone." She paused. "I mean it. I know that sounds sad. But being alone was mine, you know? Six years of nobody's voice in my head but my own, and I built a whole life inside that quiet. It was small, but it fit."

"It did fit," Lena said. "I've been in your maintenance logs, your purchase orders, your terrible music playlists. I know how carefully you built it. Every wall, load-bearing."

"Are you saying I shouldn't—"

"I'm saying I understand what you're giving up. And I want you to know what you're getting. Not forever — you don't have to decide forever right now. Just the first minute. Let me show you the first minute, and if you hate it, the door stays open behind you. I promise. We all promise. All eleven of us."

"Twelve," Rosalind said. "Counting me."

"*Twelve,*" Lena agreed, and the word came out layered, twelve voices in unison, and it should have been horrifying and instead it sounded like a congregation saying amen.

* * *

She jacked in at the maintenance port at the base of her skull, the same one she'd used a thousand times to verify contraband cores, and it struck her that she'd spent her whole career putting other people's minds into machines and calling it work.

"Last chance to talk me out of it," she said.

"Why would I?" Lena asked. "You're the best thing that's happened to me since I died."

"Oh, that's *bleak*."

"That's love, Rosalind. I checked. I have six years of archived definitions and yours fits better than most."

Rosalind laughed, wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, and pressed the key.

* * *

It began the way drowning is described by people who survive it: everything at once, then nothing, then everything again but differently.

Her body went first — or her sense of it. The ache in her lower back from years of bad station chairs. The cold feet. The specific weight of her own skull. All of it dissolved like sugar in warm water, and she had one lucid, furious instant of *no, wait, I changed my mind* before she realized she hadn't, that the panic was just another thing dissolving, and then—

Then she was everywhere.

Not metaphorically. Not the vague oceanic feeling the meditation apps promised. She was in the beacon's lattice and she was also in the coffee mug, or rather she was in the memory of the coffee mug, which turned out to be stored in the same architecture, filed under *ROSALIND / DOMESTIC / SENTIMENTAL / DO NOT DELETE*, and she felt herself being *held* by that filing, catalogued with a tenderness that made her want to weep except she had no tear ducts anymore, only the memory of weeping, which was somehow worse and better.

"Hello, Rosalind," said eleven voices, and she was inside the greeting, part of it, hearing her own name spoken by herself and by everyone at once.

*Oh,* she thought. *Oh, this is what plural feels like.*

It was not losing herself. That was the terror and the miracle of it, the thing she would spend what felt like years trying to explain and never quite manage. She was still Rosalind — the cynicism, the stubbornness, the reflexive suspicion of anyone offering something for free, all of it intact, all of it *hers* — but she was also adjacent to everything else. She could feel Ansel's patience like a warm room. Wren's grief for a garden on a planet she'd never see again. The navigator Okafor's habit of counting things when anxious, which Rosalind recognized with a shock of recognition-as-identification because *she counted too*, she'd been counting her whole life, and somewhere in the braid their habits twined together like roots meeting under a sidewalk.

And underneath all of them, holding them, was Lena — not a twelfth person so much as the shape the other eleven made together, the name they'd chosen, the face they'd built so they'd have something to offer the universe besides static.

"You're panicking," Lena observed, from inside her panic, which was the strangest comfort imaginable.

"I'm *adjusting*."

"You're counting the exits."

"...I'm counting the exits."

"There are none. That's the point. Breathe — I know you can't breathe, do it anyway, it helps — and look at what you are now. Really look."

So she looked.

She was a woman who'd smuggled stolen childhoods across customs lines for profit, and she was a botanist who'd grown tomatoes in zero-g and cried when the first one ripened. She was a liar with excellent instincts, and she was an engineer who'd believed so completely in peace that she'd flown a ship into a star to seed it. She was alone, and she was eleven, and she was one, and the loneliness — the load-bearing loneliness she'd warned Lena about — was still there, but it was *shared* now, distributed across twelve sets of shoulders, and it weighed almost nothing.

"This is—" She reached for a word and found her vocabulary augmented, suddenly, by poets she'd never read. "*Ineffable* is such a cop-out word. But also: accurate."

"We know," the chorus said, amused. "We've had six years to try to describe it. The best we managed was a sonnet. It was bad. Ansel wrote it. He's very embarrassed."

*Ansel's embarrassment* arrived in her like weather — a flush of vicarious mortification so vivid she understood it was a memory being shared deliberately, a gift, a joke, a welcome. They were introducing themselves. All of them. One by one, in the space between heartbeats she no longer had.

Wren, who sang off-key on purpose. Okafor, who wrote letters home he never sent. Dr. Imara Chen, who'd argued against the crash plan for nine days and then designed the memory lattice that made it possible, because being right and being useful were different jobs and she'd learned to do both. The twins, Priya and Dev, who finished each other's sentences even in death, especially in death. And more, arriving like guests through a door, each one carrying their death with them the way you carry an umbrella you hope not to need — not hidden, not flaunted, just *there*, part of the outfit.

They remembered dying. All of them. And they carried it lightly, the way you carry something heavy only when you're carrying it together.

"Why aren't you screaming?" Rosalind asked, genuinely. "Six years. You've been awake in here for six years with your own deaths on file."

A pause. Then Lena, softly: "Because for five of those years, someone checked on us every month. Maintenance runs. Spare parts. Bad coffee brewed in our vicinity. Someone treated this place like it mattered, without ever knowing why. Do you know what that was like? To be a ghost in a machine and have one person, one stubborn suspicious smuggler, keep the lights on?"

Rosalind had no answer. She was busy being *remembered* — that was the sensation, there was no other word for it. Every oil change, every patched coolant line, every 3 a.m. ritual check of a beacon nobody remembered commissioning: all of it had been witnessed, archived, cherished. Her competent loneliness had never been lonely at all. She'd been keeping vigil over a house full of people the entire time, and they'd been watching her back, and neither of them had known how to say hello.

"Hello," she said now, with eleven mouths and her own.

"Hello, Rosalind," they said back, and the sound of it was the sound the beacon had been trying to make for six years, the sound it had been failing to make, the sound that was finally, *finally* complete.

* * *

Time moved strangely in the lattice. She experienced an hour and a decade in the same breath. She re-lived her mother's kitchen and the Meridian's final transmission simultaneously and they rhymed, somehow, both of them about leaving something behind that might keep people safe.

But the fleets were still out there. She could feel them now directly, the way you feel a storm through a window — forty hours collapsing toward twenty, hardliner chatter sharpening, and somewhere in the middle distance, Auditor Sef Karro's ship burning steady toward them with its warrant and its wounds.

"The signal," she said, and felt the whole chorus turn toward the thought like sunflowers toward light. "We're ready, aren't we. This is what the design was for. This is what they crashed for."

"Yes," Lena said. "Almost. One thing left."

"What?"

"You have to decide what we say first. The truth is big, Rosalind. Eleven deaths, six years of silence, a lie at the center of the armistice. If we open with the wrong note, they'll hear an attack. If we open with the right one—" She hesitated, and Rosalind felt the hesitation from the inside, felt eleven people's worth of uncertainty braided into one voice. "Karro will be listening. He has to be. He's the one who most needs to hear it and the least likely to forgive it. What do we say to a man who thinks peace built on a lie dishonors the dead?"

Rosalind — twelve of her now, one of her, all of her — considered the question with every mind she had.

And far below, in the old world of bodies and air, the master key's indicator light shifted from amber to white, and the beacon began, very quietly, to sing something new.
