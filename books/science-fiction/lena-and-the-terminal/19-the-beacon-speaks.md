## 19. The Beacon Speaks

Sef Karro had spent eleven years learning to read signals the way other men read faces.

A ceasefire loop had a texture. Six years of listening to it — first as a junior analyst in the Customs Bureau's signal integrity division, later as an auditor with a clearance nobody was supposed to know he had — had taught him its rhythms the way a sailor knows the creak of his own ship. The beacon's carrier wave rose at 0400 station time and settled by 0410. It stuttered, always, on the third harmonic, a tiny flaw like a scar on a cheekbone. He had catalogued that stutter in a report once, early in his career, and been told to file it under *inherent variance*.

Tonight the stutter was gone.

Karro stood in his office above the customs floor, coat still on, a cup of tea gone cold beside him, watching the waveform crawl across his screen. Smooth. Perfect. Alive in a way it had never been before.

"Either someone fixed six years of inherent variance," he said to the empty room, "or something else is wearing its clothes."

His terminal chimed. Fleet channel — both fleets, which never happened, which *could not* happen, because the Concord fleet and the Meridian Compact fleet did not share channels, did not share frequencies, did not share anything except the armistice line and the mutual suspicion that kept it honest. A priority broadcast, flagged on both sides simultaneously, source: the beacon.

He opened it.

The voice that came through was not the flat procedural tone of the ceasefire loop. It was a woman's voice, warm and slightly roughened, as if she'd been crying recently or laughing recently and couldn't quite tell which.

*"My name is Rosalind Voss. I am — I was — a smuggler. I have maintained this beacon for six years without knowing what it was. In the next hour you are going to hear things your governments decided you should never hear. I'm asking you to listen anyway. Not because I deserve to be believed. Because they do."*

Karro sat down slowly.

On the customs floor below, he could hear the night shift going quiet, one desk at a time, like lights being switched off. Everyone was listening. Of course everyone was listening. You could no more ignore a voice coming out of every speaker on both sides of an armistice than you could ignore a fire alarm.

He reached for his tea, found it cold, drank it anyway.

* * *

Admiral Ilse Varga of the Concord Third Fleet was woken at 0312 by her aide, who had the good sense to be frightened about it.

"Ma'am, the beacon's off-loop."

Varga was sixty-one years old and had lost a brother in the last year of the war, and she had built her entire postwar career on the simple, load-bearing belief that the armistice signal was real, verified, and boring. "Off-loop how? Degraded?"

"No, ma'am. Upgraded." The aide hesitated. "It's talking."

"It's a beacon. Beacons don't talk."

"This one does, ma'am. And it's using Admiral Osei's command codes to make us listen."

Varga dressed in four minutes and took the bridge in five, and by the time she arrived, half her officers were already standing in a loose ring around the main display, listening to a dead admiral's voice describe the last hour of the ship named for their own fleet's founding treaty.

The Meridian had not been destroyed in combat. That was the first thing the recording made clear. The Meridian had been *flown into the dark on purpose*, by its own crew, with the full knowledge of both governments — a sacrifice arranged at the negotiating table when the talks stalled, when both delegations needed a ceasefire more than they needed honesty, and neither could sell peace to their hardliners without a miracle to point at.

So they manufactured one. They loaded the Meridian's memory cores with everything the crew was — letters, logs, arguments, jokes, the sound of a birthday party in the mess hall three weeks before the end — and they crashed the ship into the beacon array, and the dying crew's own minds became the signal. A ceasefire written in grief. Verified by no one, because there was nothing to verify. Both sides simply believed the other side had checked.

"They didn't check," the voice of Rosalind Voss said, over footage of the Meridian's final approach. "Nobody checked. There was nothing to check. Your governments shook hands over a grave and called it a treaty, and then they went home and let you all believe the machine was keeping the peace, when really it was just — people. Dead people, holding the door shut with their hands."

Silence on the bridge. The kind of silence that has weight and temperature.

Lieutenant Anaya Okonkwo, twenty-six, tactical officer, broke it first, because she was twenty-six and hadn't yet learned which questions were dangerous. "Ma'am. Is it true?"

Varga looked at the display, at the face of a young man in Meridian colors — some ensign, dead six years, smiling at someone off-camera — and felt the load-bearing wall of her career shift beneath her.

"I don't know," she said, and it was the most honest thing she'd said on that bridge in nine years. "Keep listening."

* * *

Four hundred thousand kilometers away, on the other side of the line, Fleet Captain Davor Reznik of the Compact Seventh was having a considerably worse night.

Reznik was a hardliner. He would have said *realist*, but his political officer said hardliner, and the political officer was usually right about words. For six years Reznik had argued in staff meetings that the armistice was a fiction propped up by unverifiable signals, and for six years he had been voted down by colleagues who preferred a comfortable lie to an uncomfortable truth. Now the truth had arrived uninvited, broadcasting from the sky, and Reznik discovered that being right felt exactly like being robbed.

"They're saying the signal was fabricated," his comms officer reported. "That both governments knew. That the crew of the Meridian were—"

"I can hear, Ensign."

"Yes, sir. Sorry, sir."

Reznik stared at the transmission log. Every bridge. Every station. Every ship in both fleets, down to the tugs and the survey cutters. Whoever had done this hadn't hacked the network; they'd *invited themselves onto it*, using authentication protocols so old and so deep that both fleets' security systems treated them as foundational — as furniture. You couldn't lock out the furniture.

"Options," he said.

"We could jam it, sir. But the jamming order would have to come from Fleet Command, and—" the ensign paused, listening to his earpiece, "—Fleet Command is asking us not to."

"Asking."

"Asking politely, sir. Their word was 'let it finish.'"

Reznik's jaw worked. On the main display, the Meridian's final minutes played again — this time with audio from the bridge, the captain's voice steady as bedrock, telling her crew what they had volunteered for, thanking them, and then, in the last thirty seconds, reading names. Not a roll call. Just names, unhurried, each one given time to land, like stones laid on a cairn.

One of the names was Reznik's niece.

She had been a hydroponics tech on the Meridian. The family had been told she died in a reactor accident during convoy escort duty. There had been a closed casket. There had been a medal. His sister had kept the medal on the mantelpiece for two years and then put it in a drawer, and Reznik had never asked why, because asking why was how you found out things you couldn't unknow.

He was finding them out now, all at once, in front of forty subordinates.

"Captain?" the ensign said carefully. "Your orders?"

Reznik looked at the name on the screen — *Tech Second Class Mira Reznik, Hydroponics Division, volunteer* — and heard his own voice come out of him from somewhere he didn't recognize.

"Log it," he said. "All of it. Unedited. If anyone asks, we were ordered to let it finish." A pause. "We were, weren't we?"

"Yes, sir."

"Then we're covered twice. Carry on."

* * *

In his office above the customs floor, Sef Karro had stopped taking notes.

This was, professionally speaking, a catastrophe. He had spent two years building the case — the maintenance orders, the supply chain, the smuggler with the irregular dock schedule and the decommissioned relay cores. He had traced the beacon to its keeper through pure, patient, unglamorous work, the kind of work that deserved a commendation and would instead earn him a tribunal, because the case he'd built assumed the beacon was a threat, and the beacon had just turned out to be the only reason there was still a peace to threaten.

And underneath the professional catastrophe, quieter and worse, was the personal one.

Karro had lost his family in the war's last year — a habitat strike, misattributed for decades, finally acknowledged in a reparations annex nobody read. He had joined the Customs Bureau because customs was where lies crossed borders, and he had wanted, with a ferocity he told no one about, to stand at that border and turn lies back. Peace built on a fabrication dishonored the dead. He had said so, in memos, in hearings, in the privacy of his own skull, for eleven years.

Now the fabrication itself was testifying. And the testimony said: *we knew. We chose it. We wrote our deaths into the signal so that you would have tomorrow to argue in.* The dead weren't dishonored. They had been conscripted — volunteers, the record insisted, though Karro knew how much pressure a government could apply before *volunteer* became a polite fiction — and they had held the line with the only weapon left to them, which was being remembered.

It didn't absolve anyone. It didn't absolve the negotiators who'd needed a miracle and manufactured one out of other people's children. But it meant the lie wasn't hollow. It was load-bearing, and inside it, people had lived and chosen and died on purpose, and the difference between that and what Karro had imagined — a bureaucratic trick, a fraud wearing a halo — was the difference between a grave and a memorial.

He realized his hands were shaking and set them flat on the desk.

The voice changed. Still Rosalind Voss, but closer now, rougher, as if she were running out of time or breath or self.

*"I'm going to say the part they'll want edited out. I found the beacon six years ago and I kept it alive without understanding it, because it was mine, because it was quiet, because it was the only honest routine I had. When I learned what it was, I had three options and none of them were good, and the person I love offered me a fourth that cost everything. So if you're angry — be angry at me. She only did what her whole crew did. She gave herself so the truth could be told by someone who'd actually lived it. I'm the witness. She's the testimony. Don't separate us. We don't come apart anymore."*

*A pause. Then, softer, almost amused:* *"Also, I smuggled stolen memories past your customs office for eleven years, Auditor Karro, and you never once checked the relay cores. You might want to look into that."*

Karro laughed. It came out of him sideways, cracked, half sob — the laugh of a man who has just been arrested by his own conscience and finds the charges fair.

Below him, the night shift had abandoned all pretense of work. Someone had turned the floor speakers up. Junior clerks who processed freight manifests for a living were standing in clusters, listening to the dead of the Meridian describe a birthday party, and several of them were crying, and none of them were embarrassed about it.

His terminal flashed. Fleet liaison, both fleets, joint priority. The message was one line:

***BEACON TRANSMISSION ONGOING. ALL ENFORCEMENT ACTIONS SUSPENDED PENDING REVIEW. ACKNOWLEDGE.***

Pending review. The great institutions, caught with their hands in the same cookie jar, retreating into committee. Karro typed his acknowledgment, then sat looking at it, then added a second line of his own, unsanctioned, the kind of thing that ended careers:

***AUDITOR KARRO CONFIRMS: SOURCE LOCATED AND IDENTIFIED. RECOMMEND NO ACTION AGAINST SOURCE. RECOMMEND THE FLEETS LISTEN TO THEIR OWN DEAD FOR ONCE.***

He sent it before he could regret it.

* * *

On the bridge of the Concord Third, Admiral Varga watched the transmission's final segment assemble itself: the Meridian's last hour, complete, timestamped, authenticated by codes only a dead crew could hold — and threaded through it, unmistakably, a second voice. Not Voss. Something plural, many-throated, speaking in unison with the patience of people who had waited six years in the static to be heard.

*"We are the crew of the Meridian. We are also Lena. We are also, now, Rosalind Voss, who kept our light burning when no one remembered lighting it. We are not a malfunction. We are not a weapon. We are what your peace is actually made of, and we are tired, and we would like, with respect, to stop lying for you. The armistice holds if you choose it now — not because a signal tells you to, but because you've heard us. Choose loudly. We'll be here either way. We live here."*

The transmission did not end so much as settle, folding back into the old carrier wave, the old rhythm — but the stutter on the third harmonic was gone, replaced by something new: a faint, layered hum, like many voices breathing together.

Varga stood for a long moment. Then she keyed fleet-wide.

"All ships, Third Fleet: maintain positions. No weapons free. And somebody get me a direct line to the Compact Seventh — apparently we have things to talk about that aren't classified anymore."

Beside her, Lieutenant Okonkwo wiped her eyes and pretended she hadn't, and Varga generously pretended not to notice.

* * *

Davor Reznik read his niece's name one more time on the frozen frame, then opened a channel he had sworn never to open, to an admiral he had spent six years calling *that Concord bureaucrat* behind her back.

"Varga," he said, when she answered. "Reznik. Seventh Compact." A breath. "My niece was on the Meridian. I think we should compare notes. And then I think we should decide, you and I, before the committees wake up, whether the peace is worth keeping when it's finally honest."

There was a pause on the line — the particular pause of a proud woman deciding to be gracious.

"Captain," Admiral Varga said, "I thought you'd never ask."

And far below them both, in the dark between the fleets, the beacon breathed its many-voiced breath, and waited to see what the living would do with what the dead had given them.
