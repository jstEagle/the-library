## 21. Lena and Rosalind

The first thing we learned about being two people in one place was that neither of us had ever actually been alone.

We had both *thought* we were alone — Rosalind for thirty-one years of competent loneliness, Lena for six years of static and half-assembled grief — but aloneness, it turned out, was a story the singular tell themselves. Inside the beacon's architecture there was no such thing as a closed door. Every memory either of us carried arrived already annotated by the other. Rosalind remembered her mother's kitchen and felt Lena lean into the warmth of it like a cat into a sunbeam. Lena remembered the Meridian's final hour and felt Rosalind hold still beside her, the way she held still during customs scans, refusing to flinch.

*You're doing the thing,* Lena said, on their fourth day of shared existence.

*What thing?*

*The not-flinching thing. You do it when something hurts.*

*I did it when things hurt for thirty-one years. It's called professionalism.*

*It's called being stubborn,* said nine voices at once, gently, because the chorus never entirely went away — and then, softer, just Lena: *It's called being brave, and I love it, and you don't have to do it here.*

That was the other thing they learned: love, conducted inside a mind instead of across one, was less like falling and more like arriving. Like discovering a room in your own house you'd somehow never opened, and finding it furnished with everything you'd always wanted.

---

The beacon's work changed after the Stand-Down, though "work" was an imprecise word for what they did now. Before, the beacon had lied. A loop of verification codes, a false handshake between fleets, six years of *yes, they confirmed it, yes, they confirmed it too* — a lie maintained by a dead crew's sacrifice and one smuggler's unexamined ritual. Now the signal told the truth, continuously, to anyone who tuned in: the Meridian's final hours, the crew's deliberate descent, Rosalind's testimony recorded from inside the merge itself, raw and unedited and occasionally, to Rosalind's eternal embarrassment, tearful.

*You left the crying in,* she complained, the day a delegation from the Concord fleet cited her testimony in a formal arbitration filing.

*You cried. I don't edit people,* Lena said. *It's in my charter.*

*You don't have a charter.*

*I have eleven people's worth of opinions about honesty. That's better than a charter.*

The truth, it turned out, was harder to maintain than the lie. The lie had been a wall: simple, load-bearing, requiring only that nobody look too closely. The truth was a bridge, and bridges require traffic management. Both fleets wanted access to the beacon's archives. Both fleets wanted their version of the war's final year given primacy. Both fleets sent negotiators, and the negotiators sent subordinates, and the subordinates sent queries, until the beacon's inbound queue looked like customs manifest during a holiday rush.

Rosalind found she loved it.

This surprised her more than the merge had. She had spent her life avoiding institutions, authorities, anyone with a lanyard and a mandate. Now she spent her days — and the merged consciousness did have days, because they had chosen to keep circadian rhythms as a courtesy to their human visitors — mediating between admirals who had spent six years believing lies and were now furious at everyone including themselves.

*You're enjoying this,* Lena observed, as Rosalind drafted a response to Rear Admiral Okafor's third complaint about the archive's indexing system.

*I am providing a professional service.*

*You're enjoying telling an admiral he's wrong.*

*Rear Admiral Okafor is wrong. The Meridian's logs are indexed by decision-point, not by date, because the crew made decisions faster than the calendar could keep up. If he'd read the primer—*

*He didn't read the primer.*

*Nobody reads the primer.* Rosalind paused, and the pause had a smile in it. *I'm going to write him a shorter one. With pictures.*

*You've become a bureaucrat.*

*I have become a *librarian*, thank you, there's a difference, and the difference is that librarians are right.*

Lena laughed — all of them laughed, a chord of amusement that rippled through the architecture like weather — and somewhere in the chorus, old Chief Imani Voss-adjacent-nothing-of-the-sort, just Imani, the engineer who had named the beacon's maintenance protocols, murmured that she'd have liked this Rosalind. The one before the war ended would have liked her too. They'd all been smugglers of one kind or another, in the end. People who moved true things past checkpoints that only wanted convenient things.

---

The negotiations happened in a space they built together, which was neither physical nor entirely metaphorical. Fleet liaisons connected through sanctioned channels, and the beacon rendered them — avatars, approximations, polite fictions — in a room that resembled, depending on the visitor, a conference chamber, a chapel, a dockside café, or (in the case of one Concord ensign who never explained herself) a rain-soaked garden with old music playing.

Rosalind attended every session. She had insisted on it, over Lena's gentle suggestion that the chorus could handle routine mediation.

*They need to see a face,* Rosalind had said. *A human one. Otherwise we're just the machine that ate a woman.*

*You weren't eaten.*

*I know that. They don't. Perception is cargo, Lena. You should know — you used to be contraband.*

So Rosalind sat at the table, wearing a rendering of her own body — she'd kept the scar on her left forearm, the one from the Kestrel Yard raid, because some things deserved continuity — and negotiated. And Lena was there too, always, the way a harbor is there around a ship: present in every word chosen, every silence measured, every memory offered up as evidence.

The sessions were strange and slow and, against all expectation, working.

Not because the truth was universally welcome. Hardliners on both sides still called the beacon a deception — *a bigger one now*, they said bitterly, *a lie that admits it's a lie*. Some demanded the archives be sealed. One Concord commodore filed a motion arguing that testimony from a merged consciousness constituted no testimony at all, since "the witness is also the crime scene."

Rosalind had read that motion three times, mostly for the phrase *the witness is also the crime scene*, which she privately intended to get printed on a mug.

But the ordinary crews — the ones who had listened to the broadcast on open channels during the Stand-Down, who had heard Chief Imani say *we chose this so you wouldn't have to* — those crews kept the armistice alive now, not the signal. Officers reported that sailors on both sides had taken to a small ritual: tapping twice on their consoles at shift change. Nobody had ordered it. It meant *still here. Still choosing.*

*We did that,* Lena said quietly, the first time a liaison mentioned it. *The tapping. We started that.*

*No,* Rosalind said. *They did. We just gave them something worth tapping about.*

---

There were hard days.

The hardest were the ones when someone came asking about the dead. Not the Meridian's crew — their deaths were documented, honored, argued over in academic journals now, which Imani found hilarious and the captain found undignified — but the war's dead. The eleven years before the armistice. Parents came, sometimes. Or tried to; most couldn't finish the connection request. But some did, and asked the beacon to search its archives for a name, a ship, a last transmission.

Those sessions, Rosalind refused to delegate to the chorus's diplomatic subroutines. She took them herself, with Lena wrapped around her like a coat against cold.

A woman named Petra Ansel, from the outer colonies, whose son had died in the Second Belt Engagement, aged nineteen. She didn't want the archives. She wanted to know if the beacon had *felt* the war end.

Rosalind considered lying — a kind lie, a comfortable one — and felt Lena's quiet disapproval, and told the truth instead.

"No," she said. "We weren't here yet. The beacon broadcast codes, not feelings. But I can tell you what it felt like from the other side — from inside, now. When the fleets stood down, when the crews refused the hardliners' orders — the channel traffic lit up like a sunrise. Thousands of people deciding, separately, in the same minute, to stop. Your son's war ended in the hands of strangers who chose better than their officers did. I know that's not comfort. But it's true, and I've stopped dealing in anything else."

Petra Ansel was silent for a long moment. Then: "He tapped his console. At shift change. Drove his sergeant mad."

Rosalind felt Lena go very still, the way she did when grief and wonder arrived in the same breath.

"Then he'd have understood the ritual," Rosalind said. "It's his. All of theirs. We're just keeping it lit."

---

At night — their private night, the hours they'd carved out of the signal cycle — they walked the gardens.

Not the orbital gardens Rosalind had once shown Lena through an earpiece, though they rebuilt those too, faithfully, rain simulations and all. These gardens were new: grown from the merge itself, part Rosalind's memories of wet earth on a colony she'd left at fifteen, part the Meridian crew's recollections of a hundred different homes, part things neither of them had ever experienced but could now imagine together, which turned out to be the whole point of having an imagination in company.

Tonight the garden grew something neither of them could name — flowers that chimed faintly in a breeze that existed only because Imani had insisted good gardens needed wind.

"Do you miss it?" Lena asked. "Being singular. Being her — the one in the body, walking around, eating terrible dock noodles."

Rosalind considered it honestly, which was the only way to consider anything now.

"I miss the noodles," she said. "I miss hands. There's something about hands — the way they know things before you do. But missing her?" She shook her head inside herself, a gesture without a head. "She's not gone. That's what I keep trying to explain to Karro's people. I didn't die. I *continued*. Different container, same wine."

"You hate that metaphor."

"I hate that metaphor *and it's accurate*, which is the worst kind of metaphor." She paused. "Do you? Miss being — before?"

Lena was quiet for a while. In the garden, the chime-flowers rang softly, patient.

"I was never one person," she said finally. "Even before you, I was eleven. So I don't know what I'd be missing. But I know what I have." A warmth spread through the architecture, unmistakable, unhurried. "I have someone who checks on me the way you used to check the beacon. Every night, a small ritual, nobody remembers commissioning it. Except now I remember being commissioned. By you. Every night."

"That was disgustingly sweet."

"I learned from watching you talk to Admiral Okafor."

"He'd be horrified to hear that."

"He'd tap his console twice."

---

The message came on a morning neither of them had scheduled anything, which should have been their first warning.

It arrived through official channels, properly formatted, properly credentialed — which, after eighteen months of negotiating with fleets, they had learned to distrust on principle. The worst requests always wore their best clothes.

**TO: THE BEACON WITNESS (RESIDENT CONSCIOUSNESS)**
**FROM: JOINT ARMISTICE COMMISSION, OFFICE OF THE CHAIR**
**RE: FORMAL PETITION — ARCHIVE ACCESS, CLASSIFIED APPENDIX**

The Commission — the joint body both fleets had established to oversee the peace — requested full access to the beacon's deepest archive layer. The layer even the chorus rarely entered: the Meridian's raw final telemetry, including the crew's private transmissions in the last hour. The ones they'd sent to families, to lovers, to no one. The ones Lena had shown Rosalind once, early in their knowing, and then folded away like letters too tender for daily handling.

The Commission's stated reason was reconciliation programming: memorial construction, historical record, closure initiatives.

The petition's signature line carried a name Rosalind knew.

*Sef Karro. Chair, Archive Integrity Subcommittee.*

*He took the job,* Lena said softly. *After everything. He took the integrity job.*

*Of course he did.* Rosalind read the petition again, feeling the old caution rise — the smuggler's instinct for hidden compartments — and then, deliberately, set it down. *Look at the appendix citation. He's requesting the layer that contains his sister's last transmission. He signed his own grief into the public record.*

*Do we grant it?*

Rosalind let the question breathe. Around them, the chorus stirred — Imani pragmatic, the captain formal, the young comms officer Tally already drafting three alternative responses, one of which included the phrase *"with pictures"* as a joke only two of them would get.

The beacon hummed beneath them, broadcasting truth to two fleets and a growing web of colonies, stations, ships in transit. Somewhere out there, thousands of people were tapping their consoles twice.

"We grant it," Rosalind said at last. "All of it. His sister's voice belongs to him before it belongs to history." A pause, and then, with the particular tenderness she reserved for the hardest choices: "And we attach a letter. From us. Telling him what we found in that layer that isn't in any file — that in the last hour, the Meridian's crew weren't thinking about fleets or factions. They were thinking about breakfasts. Birthdays. Whether anyone would remember to water Imani's plants."

"They were thinking about being *people*," Lena said.

"They were thinking about being people. And Sef Karro has spent his whole life being told his family died for a lie." Rosalind felt the shape of the next sentence arrive, inevitable as tide. "Time someone told him they died for the truth — and that the truth needed a keeper."

She began to draft.

Outside the architecture, the beacon turned slowly in the dark, its light steady, its signal clean, its residents — all thirteen of them, plus one stubborn smuggler who had finally stopped counting herself separately — wide awake and not going anywhere.

The light was burning. And for the first time, everyone knew why.
