## 1. The Garden for No One

The wheat woke before Coral did.

He knew this the way he knew most things aboard the *Meridian's Wake* — not by instrument or schedule but by the faint change in the air. At 0500 ship-time the grow-lamps over Bay Four cycled from their night spectrum of deep red to something closer to honest gold, and eleven thousand wheat plants exhaled in unison, a soft rush of oxygen and green scent that drifted down the corridor and through the greenhouse's pressure door like a tide coming in. Coral had learned to sleep with his bunk's vent cracked open so he could hear it. Not hear it, exactly. Feel it. The garden breathing was the closest thing he had to being greeted in the morning, and after seven years he no longer found that sad.

Most mornings.

He dressed in the dark — coveralls faded to the color of old rope, sleeves already rolled — and took his tea standing at the counter because sitting down felt like an invitation to think, and thinking before dawn was a habit he'd trained out of himself the way you train a weed out of a seedbed: patiently, daily, without ever quite believing it was gone.

His hands ached. They always ached now, in the cold hours. He held them up to the counter's dim work-lamp and looked at them the way other men looked at old photographs. The left one carried a burn scar across the palm from a coolant line that had ruptured during the Evacuation Wars, back when his hands were someone else's problem, issued to him along with a rank and a clearance code. The right one carried the newer history: a puncture from a peach thorn, a white ridge where a pruning shear had slipped, the fine crosshatching of a thousand small abrasions that soil leaves on skin like a signature. Military hands had been smooth once. These hands had earned their texture.

He flexed them, winced, and went to work.

* * *

Bay Four was wheat, all of it destined for sleepers who might never taste it.

That was the arithmetic Coral performed every morning without letting himself perform it consciously, the way you can know a room has four walls without counting them. Half of humanity rode behind him in the cryo-bays — forty thousand souls packed into frost-rimed drawers stacked ten high, dreaming whatever cryo-dreams there were, if there were any at all. The other half lived here, on the habitat rings and down on the moon itself, terraforming a rock that two governments claimed and neither could hold. The wheat in Bay Four was for the sleepers. The wheat in Bay Five was for the living. Same seed, same water, same light. Different futures.

Coral tended both identically. That was the point. That was the whole quiet architecture of his days.

He moved down the rows with a moisture probe and a notebook — actual paper, a luxury the quartermaster grumbled about but permitted, because Coral had asked for it in the flat, unremarkable voice that made people stop asking him questions. He checked root zones. He pinched a leaf here and there, reading its color like text. The middle third of the bay ran dry; he adjusted the drip lines, kneeling in the aisle with his sleeves soaked to the elbow, and for a while there was nothing in the universe but the smell of wet loam and the hum of the nutrient pumps and the small, enormous task of keeping eleven thousand plants alive for people who could not thank him.

This was the trick of it, he'd learned. Work small enough to fill the hands leaves no room for anything else.

"Morning, Thornbury."

He didn't startle. Seven years had taught his body not to startle, though it had taken nearly three of them to unlearn the reflex of reaching for a sidearm that lived now in a locker on a ship he'd burned out of his memory. He turned and found Priya Nair leaning in the bay doorway, holding her own tea and wearing the expression of a woman who had come looking for conversation and intended to have it.

"Priya."

"You're in early."

"I'm always in early."

"You're always in early," she agreed, "and I keep saying good morning as if one day you'll say something back besides my name." She came down the aisle anyway, because Priya was the kind of person who treated silence as a door left open rather than a door shut. She was hydroponics, second shift, mid-forties, laugh lines carved deep around eyes that missed very little. "Council meeting yesterday. You weren't there."

"I don't go to council meetings."

"Nobody goes to council meetings. That's why they're terrible." She watched him check a drip emitter with more attention than any drip emitter deserved. "They're arguing about the reactor again. Dov Kessler stood up in front of everyone and said the core's degradation curve is steeper than the last report, and half the council looked at him like he'd announced a plague, and the other half looked at him like he'd made it up." A pause, delicate as a graft knife. "You'd know something about reactors, I'd wager. Hauler like you. All those years running cargo through fleet space."

"My job is plants, Priya."

"Your job is plants," she said easily. "I only meant—" She stopped herself, which was unlike her, and studied him for a moment with those miss-nothing eyes. Whatever she saw — the set of his shoulders, maybe, or the way his scarred hand had gone still on the emitter valve — she filed it away rather than pressing. "Wren's saplings are leafing. The peaches. Thought you'd want to see before shift change."

Something in his chest loosened, the way it always did at the mention of Wren Okafor's name, and he hated how legible that must have been, and let it be legible anyway. "I'll be along."

Priya went. At the door she paused. "You know, Thornbury, most men who move to the end of the world do it to get away from something." Her voice was kind, which somehow made it worse. "I've just never figured out what you're getting away from. The view's the same everywhere on a ship."

She left before he could decide whether to answer, which was probably mercy, and probably deliberate.

* * *

The peach saplings lived in Bay Nine, under lights tuned to a spring spectrum, and Coral allowed himself to love them in a way he allowed himself to love nothing else aboard the ship.

There were sixty-two of them, grown from seed stock Wren had coaxed awake from a vault packet older than the voyage itself, and they were absurd things — thin as whips, stubborn as weeds, each one tagged with a number and a date in Wren's looping hand. They would not fruit for another three years even under ideal conditions. Ideal conditions assumed the sleepers woke. Ideal conditions assumed the moon stopped being contested. Ideal conditions assumed a great many things that Coral had stopped assuming sometime around year two, and yet every morning he walked to Bay Nine and checked them anyway, because a man who stops tending trees for people who may never eat is a man who has started dying in place, and Coral knew exactly what dying in place looked like. He'd seen entire crews do it.

Wren was there when he arrived, of course. She was almost always there at this hour, a long silhouette against the grow-lamps with her sleeves pushed up and her hair tied back with whatever was nearest — today a strip of pH test paper, which should have looked ridiculous and instead looked like her. She knelt among the saplings with a hand lens pressed to a leaf, murmuring to the tree in the low, continuous way she talked to everything growing, and Coral stood in the doorway a moment longer than a man merely passing through would stand.

"The third node's breaking bud," she said without turning around. She always heard him; he'd given up wondering how. "Number thirty-one. Come look."

He came and knelt beside her, close enough that their shoulders nearly touched, not quite. Between them lay a distance measured in millimeters and years.

"Bud break on thirty-one." He took the lens when she handed it, and bent over the sapling, and there it was: a swelling at the node, green splitting brown, the first argument spring had ever made aboard a ship that had never known a season. Something in him went quiet and glad. "Well," he said. "Look at that."

"Fourteen months from flower, if we're careful."

"If we're careful," he agreed.

Wren Okafor was the colony's senior botanist, which meant she was technically his superior, which meant nothing at all, because Coral had arranged his life aboard the *Wake* with the precision of a man laying mines: useful enough to be left alone, invisible enough to be forgotten, competent enough that no one ever wanted to look too closely at the competence. It had worked for seven years. It had worked on everyone except, apparently, Wren, who had begun about a year ago to seek him out at odd hours with questions about nitrogen cycling that she did not need answered, and who now sat back on her heels and looked at him with the particular patience of a woman waiting for a man to notice he was already caught.

"You were talking to Priya," she said.

"Priya talks. I stand there."

"Mm." She reached past him to turn a sapling a quarter-turn toward the light, and her arm brushed his, and neither of them moved away from the contact, which between the two of them constituted a conversation. "Dov's scared, Coral. I've known him nine years and I've never seen him scared. Whatever the reactor numbers say, it's worse than he's telling the council."

"He'll tell them what they need to hear, when they need to hear it."

"And what if what they need to hear is that we can't wake everyone?" She said it lightly, testing the weight of it, the way you drop a stone down a well to learn its depth. Coral kept his face still. He had gotten very good at stillness; it was the one skill from the war that transferred cleanly to gardening. "Forty thousand sleepers. One failing core. I do the math in my head sometimes at night and then I make myself stop."

"Don't do the math," he said quietly. "Tend the trees."

"That's not an answer, Coral."

"It's the only answer I've got."

She looked at him then, fully, and he endured it the way he endured all the things he couldn't fix — without flinching, barely. There was dirt on her cheekbone and the strip of test paper slipping loose in her hair and she was, he thought with a pang that felt like an old wound reopening, the best thing in a life he had deliberately built small.

"Imani drew a picture yesterday," Wren said, after a moment, and her voice had changed — lighter, but with something underneath it, a current under calm water. "My daughter. She draws constantly, you know how she is. This one was a garden." A pause. "It was this garden, Coral. Bay Four, the wheat rows, the pump housings, the crack in the east viewport. She's never been down here. Children aren't cleared for the ag decks until they're eight."

"She's six."

"She's six," Wren agreed. "And she drew your garden perfectly. Every row. And at the far end of it, where the pressure door is—" Wren's hand rose, absently, and touched her own sternum, as if the memory sat somewhere physical. "She drew two doors, Coral. One gold. One blue. And she said—" Wren laughed, a small unsteady sound. "She said, *the gardener knows which one opens first.*"

The greenhouse hummed. Somewhere in the stacks, water moved through pipes in the dark, patient as a pulse.

Coral knelt among the peach saplings with dirt on his scarred hands and seven years of carefully constructed silence around him like a hull, and he felt, for the first time since he'd fled to the one direction no one ever looks — forward, always forward — that somewhere beneath him, something vast had opened one eye.

"Kids dream strange things," he said.

"They do," Wren said, watching him. "But forty-one children don't draw the same strange thing."

* * *

That night he did not sleep.

He lay in his narrow bunk with the vent cracked open, listening to the garden breathe, and told himself the story he had told himself for seven years: that the war was behind him, that the barge was behind him, that names and ranks and neural keys belonged to a man who had died in a shuttle burn over Tycho Reach and been reborn as a hauler with dirty fingernails and no history. He told himself that children drew doors because children drew doors. He told himself that Dov Kessler's reactor was an engineering problem, and engineering problems had engineering solutions, and none of it required a man named Coral Thornbury to become anyone else.

At 0300 he gave up, dressed, and walked the long way to Bay Four through the empty corridors, past the viewing gallery where the cryo-bays glowed their cold blue behind glass — forty thousand sleepers stacked in frost and silence, humanity's other half, the half that had boarded believing someone would wake them. He stood at the glass a while, as he did some nights, and did not let himself count the drawers.

Then he went down to the wheat, and knelt in the aisle, and worked until his hands ached too much to think.

He was still there at 0600, when the day-shift traffic began and the comm panel in the corner chimed with the morning manifest: a supply run scheduled for Thursday, down to the lunar transfer station, standard orbit, standard route. Routine. He read it twice, signed for it with the flat anonymity of a man signing for someone else's mail, and was turning back to the wheat when the panel chimed again — a priority flag, amber, rare enough that his stomach tightened before his mind caught up.

*NOTICE TO ALL VESSELS: Earth Loyalist patrol assets have increased interdiction activity along the Meridian approach corridor. Cargo manifests subject to inspection. Reactor telemetry records may be requested under Emergency Provision 9.*

Reactor telemetry.

Coral stood in the gold light of the grow-lamps with the message glowing on the panel, and around him eleven thousand wheat plants breathed their slow breath for sleepers who might never wake, and far below in the cryo-cathedral, in the blue dark behind the glass, something old and patient waited — though he did not know that yet. He knew only that the word *telemetry* sat in his chest like a splinter working toward the heart, and that Thursday's routine run had just stopped being routine, and that for the first time in seven years, Coral Thornbury was afraid.

Out in the corridor, a child's laughter echoed — early, impossible, some crew kid running the decks before shift — and it sounded, for half a heartbeat, like it came from inside the walls.
