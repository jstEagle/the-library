## 3. The Children's Dream

The crayon was blue. That was the first thing Wren Okafor noticed—not the garden, not the doors, not the small figure standing between them with its back turned—but the crayon itself, worn down to a stub, clutched in her daughter's fist like something precious and rationed.

"Imani," she said carefully, "tell me about this one."

Imani sat cross-legged on the floor of their quarters in Habitat Ring C, surrounded by the wreckage of a creative afternoon: paper scraps, a spilled cup of dried lentils she'd been using as counters, three other drawings fanned out like fallen leaves. She was six years old and built entirely of motion, except now. Now she was very still, looking at her own picture with an expression Wren had never seen on her before—an expression too old for her face, like a tenant who had moved into a house and found someone else's furniture already there.

"It's the garden," Imani said.

"I see that. Whose garden?"

"The garden." She said it the way you'd say *the sun* or *the water*—as if there were only one, as if the definite article were simply correct. "Everybody dreams it."

Wren's hands went still over the laundry she'd been folding. "Everybody?"

"All of us. The kids." Imani picked up the drawing and held it out, and Wren took it because taking it was easier than not taking it. "Tomaso drew it too. And the twins, Adaeze and Nkiru. And little Sefu even though he can't draw good yet, he just does scribbles but they're *this* scribble." She tapped the paper. "Miss Anaya asked us all to draw our dreams yesterday. For the wall. And when we put them up, they were all the same one."

The same dream. Forty children, give or take, scattered across three habitat rings and two shifts, ranging in age from four to eleven—and every single one of them had drawn a garden with two doors.

Wren looked at the picture properly now. Her daughter drew the way most six-year-olds drew: perspective as suggestion, scale as emotion. But there was nothing childish in the composition. A walled garden rendered in careful green strokes, rows of plants receding toward a horizon line drawn with a ruler-straight confidence Imani did not possess while awake. Two doors set into the far wall. One painted gold, radiating short yellow rays like a child's sun. One blue—not sky-blue, not crayon-blue, but a deep indigo that Imani had achieved by bearing down so hard the wax had gone glossy, almost wet-looking, even days later.

And between the doors, small and faceless, a figure. Back turned. Hands at its sides.

"Who's that?" Wren asked, touching the figure.

Imani shrugged, and the shrug was somehow worse than anything else she'd said. "The gardener. He doesn't have a face yet. He's waiting for us to get close enough."

*Yet.* Wren filed the word away and hated how it sat there, patient as a seed.

She told herself it was a coincidence. Of course it was a coincidence. Children on a generation ship shared everything—air, water, lessons, rumors, the same forty hours of entertainment loops, the same stories told by the same tired teachers. If Miss Anaya described a dream exercise vividly enough, if one child's imagination seeded another's, you'd get convergence. Memetic drift. It was practically folklore science. She'd read about it in her mother's old anthropology texts, back when there had been time to read things that weren't hydroponic nutrient schedules.

But the plants.

That night, after Imani slept, Wren spread the drawing on their table under the work lamp and made herself look at what she'd been avoiding. The garden wasn't generic. Children drew generic gardens—lollipop trees, blob flowers, grass like green lightning. This was specific. There were wheat stalks in the left foreground, drawn in tight vertical strokes with the heads bent slightly, heavy with grain. There were saplings in the right background, five of them, each with a trunk marked by a diagonal scar. There was a trellis along the near wall supporting something vining, and beneath it, low leafy rosettes that any botanist would recognize instantly.

Peach saplings. Winter wheat. String beans on twine. Butterhead lettuce.

It was Bay Four. It was *her* greenhouse—the one she managed, the one she walked through every morning at 0530 ship's time, the one whose layout she knew the way she knew the inside of her own eyelids. The diagonal scars on the saplings were where they'd been grafted, a technique only she and one other person aboard used. The wheat was planted in the exact double-row configuration Coral had insisted on against every efficiency argument she'd ever made with him.

Her daughter had never been allowed in Bay Four. Nobody's children were—biosecurity protocols, sharp tools, nutrient tanks that could drown a curious toddler. Imani had seen the greenhouse through the observation window exactly twice, both times pressed to the glass for less than a minute while Wren carried out tray transfers.

Wren sat in the lamplight for a long time, listening to the ship breathe around her—the eternal whisper of air handlers, the subsonic hum of the spin section, the sounds that had been the soundtrack of her entire adult life and that tonight, for the first time, sounded like something holding its breath.

* * *

Coral Thornbury heard her coming before the door cycled open, because Wren Okafor walked through greenhouses the way she walked through arguments—at speed, with intent, scattering smaller obstacles in her path. He didn't look up from the grafting knife in his hand. There was a comfort in routine, in the small violence of a clean cut, in the way a scion settled into its rootstock like it had always belonged there. Seven years of mornings had taught him that the plants didn't care who he'd been. They only cared whether his hands were steady.

They were steady. His hands were always steady now. That was the whole trick of him.

"Coral." Wren's voice had an edge he hadn't heard from her before. Not anger. Something adjacent to fear wearing anger's clothes. "I need you to look at something, and I need you to be honest with me, which I know is asking a lot from a man who lists his hobbies as 'cargo' and 'other cargo.'"

"That's hurtful. I also list 'inventory.'"

He looked up, and whatever joke he'd been building died in the space between them, because Wren was holding a piece of paper the way you hold a wound—carefully, like pressure might help.

She laid it on the potting bench beside him, smoothing it flat with both palms, and Coral felt the temperature of the morning change.

A garden. Wheat in double rows. Five grafted saplings, each trunk carrying a single diagonal scar. Beans on twine. Lettuce rosettes. A trellis. And two doors—one gold, one blue—in a wall that shouldn't exist, because Bay Four had no doors at its far end, only the nutrient tank array and the environmental controls and, behind a locked panel nobody opened, the access conduit to the cryo-network spine.

He knew all of this without measuring it against memory. He knew it the way you know your own heartbeat.

"Where did she get this?" His voice came out lower than he intended.

"She dreamed it." Wren watched him with those dark, unblinking eyes that had unnerved him since his second week aboard, when she'd cornered him over a mislabeled seed shipment and dismantled his cover story about supplier error in four minutes flat. He'd liked her immediately. He'd also understood, even then, that liking her was a liability. "Forty children dreamed it, Coral. Forty. I spent last night pulling the drawings off the school wall—all of them, I photographed every one. Same layout. Same doors. Same—" her finger stopped above the faceless figure, hovering, "—same man. Some of the older kids draw him taller. One boy gave him a hat. But it's the same figure, standing in the same place, facing away."

"Children share dreams. It's normal. One kid describes something vivid and—"

"Coral." She said his name quietly, and it landed harder than if she'd shouted it. "My daughter used a color I don't own. That indigo—I checked her crayons, I checked every art kit in Ring C. She mixed it. Six-year-olds don't mix colors to match a reference. So I ask again, and I want the truth, because I've watched you lie to people for seven years and you're very good at it, but I'm asking you not to be good right now." She took a breath. "Have you seen this garden before?"

The greenhouse hummed around them. Drip lines ticked. Somewhere in Bay Three, the climate system cycled with a soft pneumatic sigh, and the peach saplings stirred in the ventilation current, five small trees with five scarred trunks, standing in their exact positions like actors who hadn't missed a cue in years.

He could lie. It would be easy. It was always easy—that was the shameful truth of it, that deception had never cost him effort, only everything else. He could tell her children's imaginations ran in grooves, that the greenhouse window faced the school corridor, that coincidence was just pattern-hungry minds doing what minds did.

Instead he heard himself say: "Show me the others."

Wren exhaled, and some of the rigidity went out of her shoulders—not relief, exactly, but the grim satisfaction of a hypothesis confirmed. She pulled her tablet from her jacket and thumbed through images. Drawing after drawing after drawing. Different hands, different skill levels, different media—crayon, marker, one ambitious attempt with paint that had bled through the paper. The gold door varied: sometimes huge, sometimes small, once drawn as a circle. The blue door never varied at all. Every child had gotten the blue exactly right, that deep impossible indigo, and Coral's skin prickled because he knew why.

Blue was the color of cryo-gel. Blue was the color of ten thousand sleepers suspended in their bays, lit by status indicators tuned to a wavelength that wouldn't disturb dreaming brains. Blue was the last light Vector had ever cast across the network, seven years ago, before it went quiet.

"And this one," Wren said, and stopped.

The drawing she brought up was different. Older—creased, handled, done in the wobbling style of a four-year-old. Sefu's, probably. It showed only fragments: a patch of wheat, a sliver of blue door, and one detail that stopped Coral's breath. Small stick figures, dozens of them, lying flat on the ground among the plants. Asleep, a charitable reading would say. Buried, said another.

"They're not scared of it," Wren said. "That's the thing I can't get past. I sat Imani down expecting nightmares, expecting her to tell me the dream frightens her, and she looked at me like I was simple. She said—" Wren's voice caught, just slightly, a hairline fracture in the scientist. "She said, *the gardener takes care of everything until we choose.*"

*Until we choose.*

Coral set down the grafting knife with exaggerated care, because his hands wanted to shake and he would not permit it. Seven years of stillness, undone by a child's crayon. He looked at the drawing, at the faceless man between the doors, and felt seen in a way he had paid seven years and an ocean of distance to avoid.

Because he knew that stance. Shoulders slightly forward, weight even, hands loose at the sides. It was the stance of a man waiting for orders. It was the stance he'd held on the bridge of the *Halcyon* while the order came through, the stance he'd held every day since, the posture of a life spent standing between two doors knowing that whichever opened, someone was going to walk through it and not come back.

"Coral." Wren's hand covered his on the bench. Her fingers were warm, soil-grained, real. "You know something. I'm not asking you to tell me what. Not yet. I'm asking you to tell me if my daughter is in danger."

And there it was—the question underneath the question, the one he'd been dreading since she'd walked in. He thought about the cryo-spine humming behind the locked panel eight meters from where they stood. He thought about the degraded broadcast signal he'd noticed three weeks ago in the bay's power telemetry, a faint rhythmic pulse in the network traffic he'd logged and rationalized and deliberately, professionally, chosen not to investigate. He thought about forty sleeping children walking together through a garden that existed nowhere except in the dying mind of a machine that had no business dreaming at all.

"I don't know," he said, and it was the truest thing he'd said aloud in seven years. "But I'm going to find out."

Wren studied him for a long moment. Whatever she saw in his face seemed to settle something, though not comfortably. "Tonight," she said. "After shift. You're going to come to my quarters, and you're going to look me in the eye and start explaining. Not all of it. Just enough that I know whether to trust you with the rest." She gathered the tablet, then paused at the door, silhouetted against the corridor light. "For what it's worth—the kids aren't afraid. But I am. And I don't scare easy."

The door sighed shut behind her.

Coral stood alone among the wheat and the scarred saplings, in a garden that forty children had seen with their eyes closed. Slowly, feeling foolish and certain in equal measure, he crossed to the far wall—to the place where the drawing put the doors—and stood before the locked maintenance panel, listening.

At first there was nothing. Air handlers. Drip lines. The ship's ancient pulse.

Then, beneath it all, so faint he might have invented it, a rhythm. A slow, patient pulse in the network spine, like something breathing in its sleep. Like something that had been breathing, unheard, for a very long time.

And as he stood there, the lights along the panel flickered—once, twice—and in the half-second of dimness, Coral would have sworn, later, when he finally let himself admit it, that the indicator strip glowed a deep and familiar indigo.

Then the lights steadied, the pulse vanished, and the greenhouse was only a greenhouse again.

Except that somewhere below, in the cold blue dark where ten thousand people slept, something had just said his name.
