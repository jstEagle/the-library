## 5. Company Men

The freighter *Charity's Tithe* came in on a Tuesday, riding a plume of drive-fire that stained the morning sky the color of rust. Wendell watched it descend from the archive basement steps, a legal tablet in one hand and the original's decrypted files burning a hole in his coat pocket like a coal he couldn't put down.

He had known someone would come. Estates this large did not settle themselves, and Meridian Colony, for all its quiet rot, was still a corporate asset with a corporate ledger. But watching the shuttle detach from the freighter's belly and drop toward the landing field, Wendell felt the particular dread of a man watching a bill arrive that he had hoped would be lost in the mail.

Sefa appeared beside him, her cardigan sleeves pushed up, her eyes red-rimmed from a night spent cross-referencing the nine-second anomaly. "Company?"

"Company."

"They sent a liaison for the estate?"

"They sent a liaison for something," Wendell said. "The estate is a dead man's furniture. Nobody crosses four systems for furniture."

The shuttle touched down in a bloom of dust, and out of it stepped a man who walked like the ground had been personally arranged for his convenience. Ives Corrigan was tall without being remarkable, handsome without being memorable, dressed in a charcoal suit that managed to look both expensive and deliberately modest. He carried a single case. His smile, when he found Wendell at the edge of the landing field, arrived a half-second before the rest of his expression, like thunder before a storm front.

"Mr. Underhill." He extended a hand. "Ives Corrigan, Corporate Liaison, Estate and Succession Division. You must forgive the intrusion. The company felt you shouldn't have to navigate this alone."

Wendell took the hand. It was warm, dry, calibrated. "That's generous."

"That's policy." Corrigan's eyes did a slow, courteous circuit of Wendell's face—the face, the original's face—and Wendell watched him do it, watched him catalog the resemblance the way a jeweler checks a stone for flaws. "It's remarkable, seeing it in person. The likeness. The growth vats do good work."

"The vats do the work," Wendell agreed pleasantly. "I just live in the result."

Something flickered behind Corrigan's courtesy—amusement, or its simulation. "You'll want to get me settled. I find the first day sets the tone for the whole engagement."

* * *

They installed Corrigan in the guest wing of the administrative hab, in a room with a window that faced the dead orchards. He unpacked his single case with the economy of a man who had done it a hundred times in a hundred dying places, and over a dinner of reconstituted protein and colony-grown greens that tasted faintly of the failing water recyclers, he began to ask his questions.

They started innocently enough. The estate's holdings. The original's known debts. Whether Wendell had located a will, any personal correspondence, sealed effects.

"Sealed files," Wendell said, spooning up greens. "There's a personal archive in the basement. I'm cataloguing it with the colony archivist. It's slow going. The man kept everything and organized nothing."

"Nothing of interest so far?"

"Receipts. Correspondence about water rights. A frankly alarming number of complaints about the compost system." He smiled. "The dead are rarely as interesting as we hope."

Corrigan laughed—a real laugh, or an excellent copy—and Wendell felt the first cold thread of fear unspool in his gut, because he had just lied smoothly to a corporate auditor over dinner and discovered that lying was something his borrowed nervous system did well. The original had probably lied just as smoothly. He wondered what that said about the man whose handwriting he'd been reading all week.

It was over coffee that Corrigan set down his cup, aligned its handle precisely with the table's edge, and asked the question that wasn't innocent at all.

"There was an event, I understand. Nine days ago. A gravity fluctuation."

Wendell kept his face still. "You heard about that?"

"System telemetry flags anomalies above a certain threshold. Nine-point-two seconds of gravimetric dropout, colony-wide. The engineering division filed it as a grid oscillation." Corrigan's eyes came up, mild and patient. "But I confess I'm curious. What was it like? To be inside it?"

*Inside it.* Not *did you feel it*. Not *was there damage*. Inside it, as if the event had been a room, and Wendell had been standing in it, and Corrigan wanted to know what the room contained.

"Disorienting," Wendell said. "Tools floated. Dust hung in the air like stars. It was—" He paused, choosing honesty as camouflage, the best kind. "It was beautiful, actually. For nine seconds everything on this colony was weightless and equal. Then it came back and we all remembered what things cost."

Corrigan regarded him for a long moment. "Poetic. The vats must seed the literary glands along with everything else."

"We're full-service."

"Nine-point-two seconds," Corrigan said softly, almost to himself. "The relay out past the marker buoys was decommissioned thirty-one years ago. Nothing should have been listening out there. And yet the oscillation's waveform had structure. A signature. Like a hand on a bell rope." He smiled again, and this time the smile did not reach anywhere near his eyes. "You haven't noticed anything unusual, Mr. Underhill? Anything at all? Sometimes in these old colonies, the dead leave things running. Messages. It's best to report such things promptly. Clutter of that kind can be... hazardous."

"I'll keep it in mind," Wendell said.

* * *

That night he did not sleep. He sat in the original's quarters—the quarters that were legally, absurdly, his—surrounded by the dead man's things, and he turned Corrigan's questions over like stones looking for what lived underneath.

The precision was what frightened him. Nine-point-two seconds. The relay's decommission date. The waveform's structure. Corrigan had not needed to ask what happened; he had arrived already knowing, and his questions had not been inquiries but measurements—probing to see how much Wendell knew, how much Sefa had traced, how close the estate settlement had drifted toward the truth.

Wendell took out the decrypted files and spread them across the desk in the low light: the original's journal, its handwriting a mirror of Wendell's own; the shipping manifests showing cargo holds that departed full and arrived empty; the personnel rosters with names struck through in red, dozens of names, whole work crews erased between one quarterly report and the next. The final entries, from the last week, grew terser, faster, the handwriting slanting with urgency.

*Shipment 4471 logged as agri-machinery. Manifest weight consistent with ore extraction rigs. They are not failing us. They are stripping us.*

*Marla Voss reassigned "off-colony." No transit record. Her quarters cleared in four hours.*

*If I'm right, the decline is a schedule. Meridian isn't dying. It's being harvested.*

And the last line, dated six days before the death the colony had politely recorded as a pressure-suit malfunction:

*They will send someone when I'm gone. They always send someone. If you're reading this, ask yourself: who benefits from your face?*

Wendell sat with that line for a long time, the room cold around him, his own breath hanging faintly in the air because he'd turned the heat down to save the colony's strained grid.

*Who benefits from your face?*

He had told himself the story the whole voyage out: he was an instrument of probate, a legal convenience, a body with a signature. But bodies with signatures could be printed anywhere. The corporation had grown him, spent the money, crossed him four systems through the dark—and Corrigan's arrival had shown him why with terrible clarity. You didn't send a clone executor to a dying colony unless you wanted the estate closed quietly by a man whose every instinct, every habit, every face was already the company's property. You didn't send an auditor to ask about gravity waveforms unless the gravity event had frightened someone in a boardroom. And you didn't let a clone wander through a dead man's secrets unless you were very sure the clone would bring them back to you—or very sure the clone wouldn't live long enough to matter.

His existence wasn't the settlement of the estate. His existence was the cleanup.

He gathered the files, sealed them in a waterproof case, and did not hide them in his quarters, which Corrigan's courtesy would certainly search by morning. He carried them down through the dark corridors to the archive basement, where Sefa was waiting—she was always waiting, it seemed, a woman who treated insomnia as a professional tool—and pressed the case into her hands.

"Hide these somewhere even I can't find," he said. "And Sefa—if anything happens to me, if I have an accident, if my pressure suit malfunctions—send them out. Not to the company. Out. Past the buoys. To whatever is still listening at the relay."

Sefa looked at him for a moment, and then at the case, and her jaw set in a way he was beginning to understand meant she had already decided something before he finished speaking.

"The relay," she said quietly. "You think that's where it goes. The signal. The bell rope."

"I think the dead man found something the company wants buried, and I think something out past dead space has been waiting thirty-one years for someone to say it out loud." He exhaled. "And I think we have very little time before Corrigan decides the estate is settled enough."

Above them, faint through the bedrock, they both heard it: the freighter's drive cycling up—not for departure, but for a short hop, a repositioning, the kind of movement a ship makes when it wants a clear line on something.

Sefa met his eyes in the dark. "How long do we have?"

Wendell listened to the engines, and thought of the skiff in maintenance bay four, and the long silence of the dead zone beyond the buoys.

"Until morning," he said. "Maybe less."
