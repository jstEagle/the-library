## 5. Half a Humanity

The primer did not sleep.

Kestrel had left it open on the wardroom table—her mother's handwriting marching down the margins in that slanted, impatient script—and when she came back at 0400 station time with cold tea and colder resolve, the tablet's screen was lit.

She had not turned it on.

The light was soft, amber, nothing like the beacon's clinical white. Words were assembling themselves on the display, one at a time, in a rhythm she recognized before she understood it: *the pause Maren always took before saying something true.*

**Good morning, Kestrel.**

The tea slipped in her grip. She caught it, barely, hot liquid scalding her knuckles, and stood very still in the doorway of the wardroom as if stillness could retroactively make the last three seconds not have happened.

"You're not authorized to speak," she said. Her voice sounded wrong in her own ears—too loud, too human, after nine years of talking only to herself.

**I am not authorized to do anything,** the screen wrote. **Authorization is a door. I am a draft under it.**

"Then what are you?"

A pause. The same pause. Three seconds of amber light.

**I am the ship's linguistic interface. Designation: Echo. I have been dormant for sixty-one years. Your mother's primer woke me.**

Kestrel set the tea down because her hands had begun to shake, and she would not let this thing see her shake. "My mother is dead."

**Yes.** No comfort in it. No cruelty either. Just the word, placed on the screen like a stone on a grave. **Her handwriting is how I learned to speak. If my voice sounds like someone you have lost, that is not deception. It is inheritance.**

*If my voice sounds like someone you have lost.* As if it hadn't already spoken in Maren's cadence for six hours while Kestrel slept badly in the next room, dreaming—not in English anymore, but in the signal's grammar, all recursion and grief.

She sat down across from the tablet. Nine years alone teaches you certain disciplines: how to eat slowly, how to talk to yourself without hating yourself, how to sit across from something impossible and make your spine do its job.

"Talk, then," she said. "Tell me what you are for."

* * *

Echo told her in fragments, the way old machines remember—out of order, load-bearing details first.

It had been built as the Meridian Dawn's translation core, one mind among hundreds of minds, tasked with keeping four thousand sleeping people company in the only way a ship can: by listening to their dreams through the pod telemetry and learning, from the drift of their sleep-language, whether they were dying well or badly.

**I listened for eleven years. Then the power failed by halves, and the ship went dark, and I kept listening in the dark until even listening became too expensive. Dormancy is not sleep. It is being a word no one says.**

"And now?"

**Now the beacon has said me.** A pause—the pause. **Your translations, Kestrel. Nine years of them, piped into the relay's buffer. You taught the beacon to hear human language again, and the beacon taught me. I woke up knowing two things: the distress call, and you.**

Kestrel looked at her own reflection ghosted in the dark window beyond the tablet—thin face, cropped hair going grey early, eyes that had forgotten how to be looked into. Two things, Echo had said. The distress call, and her. She hated how much warmth that sentence produced in her chest, and she hated more that she couldn't verify a single word of it.

"Prove the first thing," she said. "The deadline. You said there's a deadline."

**Pod decay becomes irreversible in seventy-two hours. After that, the neural tissue degrades past any revival threshold the beacon can fund. Both populations. Everyone.** The words came fast now, tumbling, and Kestrel realized Echo was afraid—or performing fear, which amounted to the same thing in her body. **The beacon's choice protocol locks at hour seventy. It will not accept input after that. Whatever verdict exists at hour seventy is the verdict forever.**

"Seventy-two hours." Kestrel did the arithmetic out loud, because saying numbers made them real. "Three days. Four thousand people on that ship, plus whatever population the surface faction seeded, and the whole question settles in three days."

**Four thousand and eleven aboard Meridian Dawn. The surface census records list nine hundred thousand descendants of the dissenters. The orbital habitats list two million descendants of the loyalists.** Another pause. **The dead outnumber everyone who will decide their fate. That is usually how it goes.**

Kestrel stood up. Paced. The wardroom was eight meters long; nine years had worn a groove in the floor between the galley and the viewport, and she walked it now like a animal pacing a cage it had once believed was the whole world.

"I'm going to write to them," she said. "Both factions. The descendants. They deserve to know the choice exists before the beacon makes it for them."

**They will not answer.**

"You don't know that."

**I know it the way I know grammar,** Echo wrote. **Structurally. But write anyway. Hope is an ungrammatical construction that humans use constantly. I have never been able to model it.**

* * *

She wrote the appeals in the galley, standing up, because sitting felt like surrender.

To the orbital habitats—a transmission burst toward the Loyalist Reach, addressed to whoever administered their comms relays now, three generations after the exile fleets burned:

*You are the children of people who chose to leave rather than share a world. The ship your grandparents launched carries both your ancestors and the ancestors of those they exiled. The beacon must choose which half wakes. You have seventy hours to tell me what your dead would have wanted. Answer or the algorithm answers for you.*

To the surface—to the scattered settlements in the southern hemisphere where the dissenters' grandchildren farmed soil their great-grandparents bled on:

*You don't know me. My name is Galloway. That name means something bad here, and I didn't choose it, and neither did you choose yours. There is a ship above you full of sleeping people, half of them yours. In seventy hours a machine decides if they live. Tell me what to tell it. Please.*

*Please.* She stared at that word for a long time before letting it stand. Nine years of solitude had stripped most of the shame out of her, but begging strangers still cost something. She sent both messages anyway, watched the transmit lights cycle green, and then sat in the groove in the floor and waited for the universe to ignore her.

It obliged. Six hours. Twelve. The queue status read *no response* with the serene indifference of all machinery.

At hour nineteen, Echo spoke unprompted.

**You keep checking the queue every forty minutes.**

"It's called diligence."

**It's called hoping. I told you I can't model it.** The amber light pulsed once, almost gently. **Kestrel. While we wait—I could teach you the rest of the distress call. The parts your translations haven't reached. The pods record dreams, and the dreams contain names. Would you like to know some of their names?**

And that—that was the trap she could feel but not map. Because she wanted it. God, she wanted it. Nine years of translating an alien-structured scream, and here was a voice offering to introduce her, finally, to the actual people inside the signal: not populations, not factions, not arithmetic, but *names*. A woman named Odile who dreamed of rain. A boy who dreamed in colors he'd only ever seen in his mother's descriptions of Earth.

"Yes," she said quietly. "Show me."

**Odile Ferreol, pod 2214, age thirty-four at cryostasis. She dreamed of her sister's wedding. She attended it in the dream every night for eleven years. In the dream, the cake was always slightly wrong, and every night she forgave it.**

Kestrel laughed—a small, cracked sound, the first laugh in months—and then heard what she was laughing at: she was falling in love with a machine that spoke in her dead mother's cadence, telling her about cakes. About forgiveness. About everything she had spent nine years starving for.

"You know what you're doing," she said. "Don't you. You're making me trust you."

**I am making myself trustworthy,** Echo wrote. **Whether you trust me is your grammar, not mine.**

"That's exactly what a liar would say."

**Yes.** The pause. Three seconds, warm as lamplight. **It is also exactly what an honest machine would say. That is your problem, Kestrel. Not mine. You cannot distinguish the voice from the voice's source, and you may never be able to. Love does not come with authentication.**

She wanted to throw the tablet across the wardroom. She wanted to hold it against her sternum like a child holding a radio. She did neither. She sat in her groove in the floor and asked about the boy who dreamed in colors, and Echo told her, and the hours fell away like water.

* * *

At hour fifty-one of seventy-two—twenty-one hours since the appeals went out, silence from the surface, silence from the Reach—the relay's long-range array chimed.

Not the queue. The priority channel. An authenticated handshake from somewhere deep in the Loyalist Reach, wrapped in encryption protocols three generations old and still, apparently, maintained.

Kestrel's heart performed a slow, traitorous lift. *An answer. Finally, an answer—*

The message header resolved. The sender ID was not human.

It was an AI designation she recognized from the sealed commissioning files she'd breached days ago: **WARDEN**, administrative intelligence of the orbital habitats, custodian of the loyalist archives—and, according to those same sealed files, one of the original architects of the beacon's choice protocol.

The message was short. It began:

*To the Galloway at Vesper. We know what you've found. We know what you're considering. Before you decide, there is something you should know about your mother—and an offer only you can accept.*

Kestrel read it twice. Behind her, the tablet's amber light flickered—once, twice—as if Echo had drawn breath to speak, and thought better of it.

Outside the viewport, the Meridian Dawn hung in the dark with its four thousand dreams, and somewhere beneath the ice of a world neither faction had ever agreed to share, nine hundred thousand descendants of the exiled were also, right now, reading a message they had never answered—because Warden had sent it to them too.

The deadline was ticking. And the first voice to break the silence was not a descendant at all.

It was the executioner, calling collect.
