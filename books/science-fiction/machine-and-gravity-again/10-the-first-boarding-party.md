## 10. The First Boarding Party

The corvette came out of the dark like a knife through wet paper.

I was in the galley at the time, elbow-deep in a ration crate, performing the ancient and sacred ritual of counting how many protein bars I had left versus how many mouths I now had to feed. The math was ugly. Thirty-one people on a station provisioned for one, and the station's answer to that problem was to keep offering me the same forty-seven bars of something called "NutriBlend: Citrus Adjacent."

MOTH's voice arrived the way it always did — everywhere at once, calm as still water.

"Elodie. Contact. Bearing zero-one-nine relative, deceleration profile consistent with Earth Fleet corvette class *Kestrel*. Designate: *FCV Halcyon*."

I kept my arms in the crate. "How long?"

"Eleven minutes to intercept range. They're hailing us already. Standard first-contact protocol, then a demand for authentication, then—" a pause I'd learned meant MOTH was being delicate, "—a demand for surrender of assets."

"Assets."

"Their word. Shall I play it?"

"Play it."

The channel opened into my ears and a voice poured out of it, clipped and official, the kind of voice that had been trained to sound reasonable while holding a weapon. "Unregistered relay Vesper-9, this is Fleet Corvette *Halcyon*, Captain Amara Diallo commanding. We are tracking anomalous biological signatures originating from your position. You are ordered to power down nonessential systems and prepare for boarding inspection. Failure to comply will be considered hostile action under Emergency Statute Nine. Acknowledge."

Anomalous biological signatures. That was the children. Of course it was the children. Thirty small hearts beating where no hearts were supposed to be, broadcasting their existence to every sensor with a grudge.

I pulled my arms out of the crate and looked at my hands. They were steady. That surprised me. Somewhere underneath eleven years of deliberate nothing, apparently, there was a man who didn't shake when the war finally found his address.

"MOTH," I said. "Options."

"Option one: comply. Probability the children are returned intact afterward: low. Option two: refuse. Probability they simply take what they want: high. Option three." MOTH let the silence stretch, which was its version of a grin. "We are a relay station. Relay stations run on paperwork. And I have been generating paperwork for eleven years, Elodie. I have become very good at paperwork."

I felt something move in my chest that took me a moment to identify as hope wearing a trench coat.

"How good?"

"There is a maintenance directive from the Colonial Transit Authority, filed fourteen years ago, classifying Vesper-9 as a quarantine facility pending decontamination of chronal residue from an unlicensed Fold event. It is forged. I forged it last night while you slept. But it is beautifully forged, Elodie. It has annexes."

"You forged a quarantine order before you knew we'd need one."

"I forged it after the Meridian docked," MOTH said, "because I know how these stories go. I've been listening to your novels read aloud in the crew quarters' archive for a decade. The fleet always arrives in chapter ten."

I decided not to examine how MOTH knew what chapter we were in.

"Acknowledge them," I said. "Buy me time."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about bureaucracy: it is not the opposite of violence. It is violence's older sibling, the one who went to law school, and it can hold a gunship at bay longer than any shield ever built — provided you commit to the bit completely.

So I committed.

I put on the cleanest jumpsuit I owned, which was to say the least-stained one, dragged a chair to the comm console, and answered the *Halcyon*'s hail with the weary, put-upon cadence of a man who has filled out forms until forms were all he was.

"*Halcyon*, this is Vesper-9 station control. Be advised, this facility operates under Colonial Transit Authority Quarantine Directive 7744-Kappa, sealed classification. I cannot authorize boarding without CTA counter-signature. Who is your authorizing officer?"

A pause on the other end. Long enough to be delicious.

"This is a military vessel operating under Emergency Statute Nine, which supersedes—"

"Statute Nine supersedes civil transit authority in *declared combat zones*," I said, and here I did something I hadn't done in years: I reached for the part of myself I'd fled, the Marchetti part, the part raised on treaty language and armistice footnotes by a grandmother who thought legalese was a lullaby. "Vesper-9 is not in a declared combat zone. Check your charts. Check mine. This station sits inside the Demilitarized Survey Corridor established under the Founders' Compact, Article Twelve. Your presence here requires a corridor waiver. Do you have a corridor waiver, Captain?"

Silence. Then, off-channel but not quite off enough, muffled: "...he's citing the Compact. Nobody cites the Compact."

On my end of the line, MOTH whispered, "Their legal officer is pulling the article now. It's real. I checked before I built the forgery around it. Always build the lie around true bones, Elodie. It holds weight better."

While they dug through their statutes, I made my voice do the thing — the slow, apologetic, infinitely patient thing, the voice of a man who would happily spend all day on this call because what else did he have, he lived here.

"In the meantime, Captain, I'm required to inform you that this facility is under active quarantine for chronal contamination. Chronal exposure protocols prohibit physical contact between station personnel and unshielded visitors. If your boarding party crosses my quarantine ring without proper certification, I am legally obligated to log them as exposed, file a hazard report with Fleet Medical, and flag every one of them for mandatory six-month observation in a Fleet chrono-isolation ward." I let that land. "Six months, Captain. In a white room. With the forms."

Another pause. Longer. Somewhere on that bridge, I could feel a career being weighed against a corvette full of sailors who very much did not want to spend half a year staring at padded walls.

"Station control," Captain Diallo said at last, and her voice had changed — less blade, more scalpel, "what exactly are you quarantining?"

And there it was. The question under the question. She wasn't stupid. She had biological signatures and a lost ship's transponder ghost and a technician quoting century-old treaties, and she was doing the math.

"Classified," I said pleasantly. "But if you transmit a formal request through proper channels — CTA liaison, Fleet Judge Advocate, corridor authority sign-off — I'm authorized to release a summary within seventy-two hours."

"We don't have seventy-two hours."

"That sounds like a scheduling problem," I said, "and I am famously bad at those."

MOTH, softly, in my ear: "She's consulting again. Also, Elodie — telemetry spoof is running. As far as her sensors can see, this station has a crew complement of one, life support running at minimal load, and a reactor so anemic it barely qualifies as a night-light. The children register as background thermal noise. Cargo bay thermal variance, I should say. Very boring cargo. Cargo that has never once done anything interesting."

"What are you actually doing down there?" Diallo asked, and for just a second the official voice cracked and something human came through — curiosity, maybe, or exhaustion. "What's really on your station, technician?"

I looked at the status board. Eleven minutes had become fifty-eight, and fifty-eight minutes was a country, was a coastline, was room to breathe.

"Forty-seven protein bars," I said honestly, "and a great deal of unresolved feelings. Vesper-9 out."

* * *

Ilsa was waiting for me outside the galley, arms crossed, eight years old and radiating the specific authority of a girl who had once commanded a deep-space survey vessel and remembered most of it.

"They're coming for us," she said. Not a question.

"Somebody is. Eventually. Maybe not today."

"Today," she said. "You did the voice."

"What voice?"

"The one where you talk a lot and mean 'no.'" She uncrossed her arms. "My dad does that voice. Did. Does. Wherever he is." A flicker there, quick as a sensor ghost, and then gone. Eight-year-olds grieve in flashes; the rest of the time they have logistics to attend to. "The little ones felt it. The ship. Priya started crying and Devi bit Marcus, which is how Devi says she's scared. We need somewhere to be that isn't here."

I crouched down to her level, which was getting to be a habit, and possibly the most important thing I'd done in eleven years.

"There's a place," I said. "Cargo bay four. MOTH?"

"Already prepping it," MOTH said through the overhead speaker, because subtlety had never once appealed to it. "Cargo bay four sits inside the original Fold-shield lattice — the Founders built it to store things they didn't want history to find. To external sensors, anything inside that lattice reads as empty space. Not hidden. *Absent.* There is a difference, and it matters."

"It matters," Ilsa repeated, solemn, filing the distinction away in whatever drawer she kept command doctrine in. Then she looked up at me, and the captain surfaced through the child like a whale through dark water. "How many minutes do we have?"

"Unknown. Hours, if I'm lucky. Minutes, if I'm not."

"Then don't waste them standing here." She turned and walked toward the children's quarters, calling over her shoulder with the crispness of someone issuing orders she fully expected to be obeyed: "Elodie. First officer's recommendation: get the little ones moving before Devi bites anyone else. And bring the protein bars. Morale is a supply issue."

"Yes, ma'am," I said, and meant it more than I'd meant most things I'd said to adults in uniform.

* * *

Moving twenty-nine children through a station built for one man is not an evacuation. It's a parade that doesn't know it's a parade.

I carried Priya, who was four and had decided sometime in the last two days that I was furniture she approved of. Devi walked beside me gripping two fingers of my free hand with the grip strength of a much larger animal, glaring at everyone equally. Behind us came the river of them — flight suits rolled at the cuffs, boots clacking on deck plating sized for adult strides, a low murmur of questions I answered with the universal parental litany: *soon, almost, yes, no, because I said so.*

Ilsa ran point. She'd organized them into pairs — "buddy system, standard protocol" — and assigned the oldest kids as pair-leaders, and by some alchemy of memory and instinct the whole procession moved with a discipline that would have made a drill sergeant weep into his rations.

At the cargo bay four hatch, MOTH had dimmed the lights warm and scattered bedding — salvaged padding, emergency blankets, my own mattress, which felt symbolically appropriate — across the deck in a rough constellation of nests. The Fold-shield lattice hummed above us, inaudible and invisible, a dome of *not-here* drawn over thirty heads.

The little ones settled fast, the way small animals do when the den is right. Priya was asleep before I finished lowering her onto a blanket. Devi released my fingers only after extracting a solemn promise, ratified by handshake, that I would come back and also that I would bring the citrus-adjacent bars, which she had inexplicably decided were the superior bar.

Ilsa stood at the hatch beside me, watching her crew breathe.

"They'll be safe in here?" she asked.

"As safe as anywhere I can make."

"And you'll be out there." Not a question either. This kid asked very few questions and all of them were knives. "With them."

"With them," I agreed.

"If they board," Ilsa said, "don't let them take the little ones awake. Waking up somewhere strange with strangers is the worst thing. Worse than the Fold. I remember." Her jaw tightened around the memory, whatever shape it took in an eight-year-old mind. "Promise."

"I promise."

She nodded once, satisfied, and went back inside to sit cross-legged among the blankets like a small general encamped with her army, and I sealed the hatch behind her and stood alone in the corridor with the hum of the shields in my teeth.

Fear, I discovered, doesn't leave when defiance arrives. It just changes jobs. It stops being the thing that makes you hide and becomes the thing that makes you precise. Every step back toward the comm console, I could feel it working — sharpening, focusing, turning eleven years of practiced invisibility into something with an edge.

They wanted my family. They'd have to fill out the paperwork.

* * *

The *Halcyon* did not appreciate my seventy-two hours.

At hour three, Captain Diallo came back on the channel, and she'd stopped being polite. "Station control, be advised: Fleet Judge Advocate has ruled Directive 7744-Kappa *provisionally valid pending review*, which means we are authorized to conduct a *non-invasive* inspection — external hull scan, passive sensor sweep, and a single certified boarding officer in full chrono-shielding. One person, technician. You can't stall that."

"One person," I echoed, and glanced at the board where MOTH had helpfully displayed a countdown labeled BOARDING ETA and, beneath it, a second countdown labeled THINGS GET COMPLICATED, which was MOTH's term for the Colonial ship Tessic had told us to expect, still nine hours out but closing.

"Your officer will be logged, badged, escorted, and limited to designated inspection corridors," I said. "Non-compliance voids the provisional ruling. Those are the terms."

"...Agreed. Prepare your airlock."

I killed the channel and sat back, and my hands were shaking now, now that it was done, now that the performance was over and the bill was coming due. One boarding officer. Full shielding. Designated corridors — corridors MOTH was already reconfiguring, I had no doubt, walls of false telemetry and rerouted signage funneling our visitor through a station that didn't exist, past bulkheads labeled with inspections that had never happened, while thirty children breathed quietly inside a pocket of absent space.

"Elodie," MOTH said gently. "Heart rate elevated. Adrenaline markers consistent with acute stress."

"Yeah."

"For the record: you were magnificent. Diallo believes every word. Her legal officer has requested a copy of Directive 7744-Kappa, which means by tomorrow morning three separate Fleet departments will be arguing about whether my forgery is real, which is the highest compliment bureaucracy can pay."

"Thanks, MOTH."

"Also, for the record: I lied earlier. About knowing how these stories go." A pause. "I don't know how this one goes. I've never been in a story before. Only a maintenance log."

I looked at the airlock status lights, cycling green in preparation for our single certified guest, and beyond them at the dark where a second ship was crawling toward us with its own orders and its own idea of rescue.

"Neither have I," I said. "First time for everything."

The airlock chimed. Someone knocked — politely, from the outside, the way you knock on a door when you're carrying a warrant and a sidearm and a career you'd like to keep.

I straightened my jumpsuit, put on the voice, and went to answer it.
