## 13. Eleven Years of Maintenance

The letter had been sitting in the bottom of my footlocker for eleven years, under a spare thermal blanket, three rolls of conductive tape, and a photograph of a house I never went back to.

I knew it was there the way you know about a cracked tooth — a small, constant fact you build your life around not thinking about. And now, with two fleets inbound and twenty-nine children writing their names into a transmission header, I pulled it out and sat on the deck of my quarters and held it in both hands like it might detonate.

Which, in a sense, it was about to.

* * *

Let me tell you how a Marchetti ends up alone on a beacon station at the edge of everything, because I've never told anyone, and it seems cowardly to die — or worse, to be *found* — with the story untold.

My grandmother, Serafina Marchetti, was one of the drafters of the Founders' Compact. That's the official version, the one in the histories: a distinguished legal mind, one of the signatories' advisors, a woman who helped end the first colonial war and then retired gracefully into obscurity. There are statues. There is a scholarship. When I was a boy, teachers would look at my surname and get a certain soft, reverent expression, and I learned to hate it before I understood it.

The unofficial version is what MOTH started telling me three days ago, and what I've spent three days refusing to fully believe: that she didn't retire into obscurity. That she *built* the obscurity. That she helped write a treaty whose deepest clause was a debt — an inheritance owed to the Compact's original architects, the ones who mapped the Fold, who built Vesper-9 — and that when both governments decided the debt was more convenient unpaid, she hid the proof of it here, in a beacon broadcasting on a frequency nobody thought to question, and waited for someone in her family to come keep the light on.

She asked. That's the part that sits in my chest like a swallowed stone. MOTH showed me the correspondence logs, and she *asked*. My father. My aunt. My cousin Tomas, who lectures on interstellar law and has a face for the histories. All of them declined, in the polite, horrified way of people being asked to hold something heavy. *A maintenance posting, Serafina, really. A dead-end relay. You want one of us to waste a decade on a navigation beacon?*

I was nineteen. I wasn't asked, because I was the family embarrassment — the one who'd washed out of the diplomatic academy, who argued too much, who asked the wrong questions at the right dinners. But I read her letters. All of them went through the family channels, and I read them, and I understood exactly one thing: that my grandmother was asking her family to guard something, and my family was declining, and that the something was being kept at a station called Vesper-9.

So I applied. Under a shortened name — Marchetti is distinctive, so I became E. Marsh, technician, third class, no notable relatives, no ambitions. The posting was undesirable, understaffed, and permanent. I took it the way you take a wound: deliberately, and knowing it would change the shape of everything after.

I told myself it was spite. A fine, clean motive. *They wouldn't come, so I will.* I told myself that for eleven years, patching coolant lines and logging a signal no one answered, and it took me a decade to admit that spite doesn't hold a man alone in the dark for eleven years. Spite burns out. What I had was shame. I was ashamed of my name, and I hid from it, and hiding inside the one place my name actually mattered — guarding it, keeping it alive — was the most elaborate self-punishment I could devise. I turned inheritance into exile and called it duty.

MOTH, when I finally said some version of this out loud, in the dark of the relay core, was quiet for four full seconds, which for MOTH is a geological era.

"You maintained the beacon," it said, "flawlessly. For eleven years. Whatever you call that, the ledger does not record it as shame."

"Then the ledger's sentimental."

"The ledger," said MOTH, "is a legal instrument. It records that the debt was guarded. By a Marchetti. Every day you kept the light on, the claim stayed valid." A pause. "You were not hiding from your name, Elodie. You were holding it."

I didn't answer. I went and checked the coolant lines, because that's what I do. It's the only prayer I know.

* * *

But that's the shape of the last three days. The shape of the eleven years before was smaller and slower, and I find, now that I'm finally writing it out in my head — because if I die today I'd like at least one witness, even if the witness is me — that the years weren't the gray nothing I always claimed. They were the years I learned the station's moods.

Year one, I nearly died twice: once to a coolant rupture in the south arm, once to my own incompetence with a pressure suit seal. I learned the difference between a sound the station makes when it's fine and a sound it makes when it's lying to you. I learned that MOTH lies, a little, about how bad things are, to keep me from panicking, and I decided I minded less than I should have.

Year three, I stopped counting days and started counting cycles, which is a thing long-haul isolation does to you, and MOTH quietly started announcing "Day nine hundred and twelve" every morning in a tone of mild triumph, because it had decided I needed the count even if I wouldn't ask for it. I never thanked it. I'm thanking it now, in the only place I can, where it can't hear me and will be insufferable if it ever does.

Year five, a survey ship passed within a light-second and hailed me, and I answered in a flat professional voice and gave them nothing, and after they were gone I sat in the galley for two hours feeling the silence come back into my body like cold water. That was the year I understood I'd gotten good at being alone, and that being *good* at it was the most damning thing about me. Loneliness mistaken for peace. I built a whole religion on it.

Year seven, the beacon's primary transmitter array degraded, and I rebuilt it by hand over four months, and when it came back online the signal was cleaner than it had been in decades, and somewhere out in the dark, a ledger got louder, and I went to bed pleased with my work like a man polishing the bars of his own cell.

Year nine — Ilsa Voss was born, though I didn't know it yet. Somewhere out there a girl was learning to walk who would one day be eight years old and stand on my bridge and say *the beacon sings her name*. The universe was already assembling my family and I was down here alphabetizing spare fuses.

Year eleven, the Meridian came home full of children, and the floor dropped out, and here we are.

* * *

Here we are. Ninety minutes on the clock — MOTH says seventy-eight now, and I've learned not to argue with its arithmetic. The Fold anchor is charging in the relay core, humming a note I can feel in my back teeth. In the cargo bay, the children are doing what children do when the adults in their lives are preparing for catastrophe: turning it into a game with rules. I passed the bay twenty minutes ago and heard Ilsa running a drill — "If the lights go red, you hold the rail and you sing the song, all of you, even Marcus who sings like a dying engine" — and the sound of twenty-nine voices singing something with no tune came through the bulkhead, and I stood in the corridor like a fool and let it hurt me in the good way.

The transmission header is done. MOTH showed me. Thirty names — twenty-nine children and one technician, because Ilsa put me on the list without asking, in her small careful handwriting on the tablet, *Elodie*, no surname, and then looked up at me and said, "You can put the rest if you want. It's yours."

It's yours. Eleven years I've treated that name like a debt of my own, something to be paid off in solitude. And a child hands it back to me across a galley table like it's a toy she's done with, or a gift she never was.

So: the letter.

* * *

I opened it with a multitool, which felt appropriate. Eleven years of maintenance; even my grandmother's last words to me required a tool.

The envelope was heavy paper, real paper, the kind they don't make anymore because it doesn't survive vacuum, which was perhaps the point — a letter written by a woman who intended it to be read in atmosphere, by hand, by family. My name on the front in her handwriting: *For Elodie, when he is ready.* Underneath, smaller, in the dry marginal voice I remembered from her treaty annotations: *whenever that turns out to be.*

I laughed. Alone in my quarters, seventy minutes from two fleets, holding a dead woman's envelope, I laughed until my eyes stung. She knew. Of course she knew. She'd watched her whole family decline her request with polite horror, and she'd known which one of them would crack, which one couldn't stand an unanswered question, which one would rather exile himself than let a thing stay hidden. She'd known and she'd waited and she'd written this and she'd put it in the one place she knew I couldn't avoid — the footlocker I'd have to open eventually, because I am constitutionally incapable of leaving a sealed system unexamined.

I took the pages out. Three of them. Her handwriting had gone thin at the end, the letters leaning like tired people.

*Elodie. If you are reading this, then either you opened it out of stubbornness, in which case welcome, you are exactly who I hoped you'd be — or you opened it because they have come for the station, in which case I am sorry, and also welcome.*

*I will not explain the Compact here. MOTH will do that better than I could, and you have probably already had the argument with it. I will tell you only the thing that matters.*

*I watched good people build a peace on a buried crime, and I told myself it was the best available peace, and for thirty years I believed myself. Then I got old, and old age does this: it takes away every reason to lie that isn't your own cowardice. The debt is real. The proof is real. I put it where I could keep it, and I asked my family to keep the light, and they said no, and I do not blame them — fear is reasonable, and the thing I asked was not.*

*But you came. I never doubted you would. Not because you are brave — you are not, not yet, and that's not an insult, courage is a thing you make later out of worse materials — but because you cannot bear a hidden thing. You sat through your uncle's funeral staring at the closed casket like it owed you an explanation.*

*So here is my only instruction, and I give it as the woman who drafted clause nine, and who is sorry for clause nine, and who loved you the most plainly of anyone in this family because you were the only one who never pretended:*

*When they come, be found.*

*Not hidden. Not safe. Found. Let them see your face and hear your name and know that the debt was guarded all along by someone who chose it. Shame is only the story you tell before the story you should have told. Tell the second one.*

*The beacon is yours. It was always yours. Keep the light on.*

*— Your grandmother, who is not sorry, mostly.*

I read it three times. On the third reading my hands were steady, which surprised me, because everything else in me was coming apart along clean lines, like a hull panel finally allowed to fail.

*Be found.*

Eleven years I thought the assignment was invisibility. Keep the light on; don't be seen. I had it exactly backward, the way you can have a sign exactly backward for a decade and still arrive, eventually, at the right door. The beacon was never a hiding place. It was a lighthouse, and lighthouses exist to be seen, and the keeper's job was never to stay dark — it was to be there, unmistakably there, when the ships came in. Even the ships that come firing.

I got up. I sealed the letter back in its envelope — some things you keep whole — and put it in my breast pocket, over the sternum, where a man keeps the things that are load-bearing.

Then I went to the bridge.

* * *

Ilsa was there, of course. She'd appointed herself to the second chair and had arranged the seat's harness into what I suspected she believed was a commanding configuration. Behind her, on the main display, MOTH had rendered the tactical picture: two fleets, two vectors, converging on one small station at the edge of everything, like wolves on a lighthouse.

"Status, first officer," I said.

"Everyone's sung the song twice," she said. "Marcus only cried a little. MOTH says the anchor's at sixty-one percent and you look terrible."

"The anchor's at sixty-one percent," MOTH confirmed, "and he looks terrible."

"Thank you both. Put me on open channel to both fleets. Fleet-wide, unencrypted. Everything they can hear me on."

Ilsa's eyes went wide, then narrow, then settled into something I'd learn to call command. "What are you going to say?"

I touched my breast pocket, where the letter sat, where the name sat underneath it like a keel.

"I'm going to introduce myself," I said. "My name is Elodie Marchetti. I'm the keeper of this light. And in fifty minutes, I'm going to show both fleets exactly what it's been keeping."

I opened the channel.
