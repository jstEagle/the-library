## 17. Broadcast

The countdown on MOTH's main display read 00:14:22 when Ilsa climbed onto the chair beside me and put her hand flat on the console, the way she'd seen me do it a hundred times.

"Is it supposed to feel like this?" she asked. "Like the station is holding its breath?"

"The station doesn't breathe," I said. "It cycles coolant and vents atmosphere on a schedule."

"It's holding its breath," she repeated, with the absolute authority of an eight-year-old who has decided how reality works, and I didn't argue, because my hands were shaking too badly to type and arguing would have required me to admit I'd noticed it too.

Fourteen minutes. Ninety minutes had seemed like a fortune when Okonkwo agreed to them — a whole hour and a half of borrowed time, enough to charge the Fold anchor, enough to write a transmission header, enough to say goodbye if it came to that. But time is like ration packs: it shrinks the closer you look at it. We'd spent forty minutes on the anchor charge alone, MOTH pulling power from every system that could spare it and several that couldn't. The lights in the aft ring were at emergency amber. The gravity in section four had gone soft, and the children kept floating their spoons around during breakfast as though we'd done it on purpose, which, honestly, was the only good decision anyone had made all day.

"Header's locked," MOTH said, its voice coming through the bridge speakers with that careful neutrality it used when it was worried about me. "Twenty-nine names plus yours, formatted per Founders' Compact protocol. It will be the first thing both fleets see."

"Good." My voice came out steadier than I felt. "Let them know exactly whose fault this is."

"Elodie." MOTH paused. "I have run eleven thousand simulations of the next ninety seconds. In nine thousand two hundred of them, someone fires before the transmission completes."

"And in the other eight hundred?"

"In those, everyone waits to fire until after."

I laughed. It came out wrong, more air than sound. "That's the optimistic scenario?"

"For you? Yes."

* * *

00:09:47.

Okonkwo's hail came through on the private channel we'd negotiated — her face sharp and tired in the corner of my display, the gun sight geometry of her fleet visible behind her: three destroyers in a wedge, the corvette *Halcyon* hanging back like a dog that had been kicked once too often.

"Technician Marchetti." She used the name deliberately. We'd been through this already — the armistice talks, the woman named Marchetti who'd argued for the ledger clause nobody wanted to hear about, the grandmother I'd spent thirty years pretending not to be related to. "Status."

"Anchor's charged. Transmission ready. Both channels open, your fleet and Tessic's, simultaneous burst, no editing, no embargo. That was the deal."

"I know what the deal was." Something moved behind her eyes — not doubt exactly. More like a person standing at the edge of a high dive remembering every reason they'd never learned to swim. "My orders say that the moment you transmit, I'm to consider the station hostile and reduce it."

"And yet here you are, chatting."

"Here I am, chatting." A ghost of a smile. Gone fast. "Elodie. Whatever's in that ledger — I've spent twenty years fighting a war built on things I was told were true. If you're about to tell me they weren't—"

"They weren't," I said. "That's the whole point. That's all it is."

She closed her eyes for one second. When she opened them, she was a fleet captain again. "Ninety minutes starts now. Okonkwo out."

* * *

00:08:03.

Tessic's channel was different. No video — just audio, his voice warm and gravelly, the voice of a man who genuinely believed he was the hero of this story.

"Marchetti. Last chance. Hand over the crystal, come aboard the *Vigilant*, bring the children. Sanctuary. Citizenship. Protection. I meant everything I said."

"I know you did," I said. "That's what makes this hard."

"You're going to broadcast anyway."

"Yes."

A long silence. Somewhere on his ship, I could hear a chair creak. "You understand what happens then. Not to you — to us. To my people. Everything we've bled for, everything the Colonies built our identity on, and you're going to tell twenty billion people it was a lie from day one."

"No," I said. "I'm going to tell them it was a debt from day one. Those are different things. One of them can be paid."

Another silence, longer. Then, quieter, almost gentle: "You sound like her, you know. Your grandmother. She said almost exactly that, at the armistice. 'One of them can be paid.' And they laughed her out of the room, and I've wondered for forty years whether she was right."

"She was right, Colonel. That's why the beacon's been singing for sixty years."

He exhaled, a sound like a man setting down something heavy. "Then God help us all, Marchetti. Tessic out."

* * *

00:04:30.

The children were in the cargo bay, all twenty-nine of them, because Ilsa had ordered it and because even the four-year-olds understood that today was not a spoon-floating day. But Ilsa herself was on the bridge, strapped into the second seat, watching the countdown with the stillness of a much older person wearing a very small body.

"You should be below," I said.

"I'm your first officer."

"You're eight."

"I'm the one who remembers dying," she said, matter-of-fact, terrible. "Everything else is easy."

I didn't have an answer for that. I reached over and checked her harness strap, which was fine, and she let me, which was its own kind of permission.

"MOTH," I said. "Final systems check."

"Fold anchor: charged to ninety-four percent and stable. Beacon array: aligned, both channels open. Transmission package: verified against crystal data, checksum clean. Header: thirty names." A pause. "Elodie. Once this fires, there is no version of history where Vesper-9 stays invisible. You understand that."

Eleven years. Eleven years of coolant lines and unanswered logs and talking to an AI because talking to an AI didn't require me to be anyone. Eleven years of being no one, safely, on purpose.

"Yeah," I said. "I finally do."

* * *

00:01:00.

MOTH counted down aloud, because I asked it to, because I wanted a voice other than my own heartbeat in the room.

At 00:00:10, Ilsa took my hand. Her palm was small and slightly sticky and absolutely steady.

At 00:00:05, every screen on the bridge went white with standby glow.

At 00:00:00, MOTH said, "Transmitting," and the beacon sang.

I don't have words for what it sounded like from inside. For eleven years the beacon had been a whisper — a navigation pulse, a ledger fragment, a lullaby nobody answered. Now it was a shout. The whole Compact ledger went out in a single burst: the engineered famine on Ceres Reach, crop blights patented by Earth agri-conglomerates and released on schedule; the forged attack at Meridian Gate, the Colonial patrol boat destroyed by its own government's drone to give Earth its casus belli; the debt — the original debt, the Founders' Compact, the terms signed by the great-grandparents of the men and women currently running both governments, the payment schedule that had come due six decades ago and been buried under a war instead. And at the bottom of it, the signatures. Real ones. Verified ones. Names that were on statues on both sides of the border.

It went out at lightspeed-plus along the beacon's folded channels, hitting both fleets simultaneously, hitting relay buoys, hitting civilian receivers, hitting everything, everywhere, all at once.

And then —

Silence.

Not radio silence. Worse. The kind of silence where twenty warships and two fleets and half a solar system all stop transmitting at once because nobody knows what to say. The channel indicators flickered. The fleets hung in my tactical display like insects in amber.

Ilsa squeezed my hand. "Did it work?"

"We'll know in—"

Tessic's ship lit up.

Not toward us. The *Vigilant*'s weapons capacitors spun up and fired — every lance, every rail, full spread — but the targeting solution wasn't Vesper-9. It wasn't the children. It was the *Meridian*'s old heading, the vector straight through the middle of Okonkwo's formation, at the flagship *Constitution*, where Fleet-Captain Rhea Okonkwo sat with the same ledger open on her screens that we'd just made public.

"He's firing on his own ally's target," Ilsa breathed. "No. He's firing on—"

"Tessic," I said. "Ordered to silence him. Or ordered by whoever gave him his orders — the ones whose names are at the bottom of that document."

The first lance crossed the gap between the fleets in four seconds. Okonkwo's point defense lit up like a struck match.

The war hadn't ended.

It had just changed sides.
