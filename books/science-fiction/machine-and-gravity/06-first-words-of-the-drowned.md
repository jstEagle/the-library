## 6. First Words of the Drowned

The translation matrix woke up the way old soldiers do — badly, complaining, and with opinions nobody asked for.

I'd left it chewing on the signal for six days. Six days of letting a piece of firmware older than the truce itself grind through fragments of a dead language while I calibrated fuel injectors and logged my hours against a debt that has all the elasticity of a stone. I told myself I was being responsible. Letting the system run its course. Not getting attached.

I am a very good liar when the audience is me.

On the sixth night, with the beacon humming its one long note into the dark and nothing else in the universe apparently happening, the matrix finished its first full pass and put the results in my queue like a child leaving a dead bird on the doorstep: *here. look what I found.*

The file was 4.2 terabytes of structured audio, video, and text interleaved in a format that hadn't been used since before either fleet existed as anything more than a shipping consortium with guns. The matrix had reconstructed maybe eleven percent of it with confidence above sixty percent. The rest was noise, gaps, decay — two centuries of the signal degrading as it repeated itself into the void, patient as erosion.

Eleven percent. I want you to understand what eleven percent did to me.

* * *

Let me back up. Let me tell you how it started, because the beginning matters, and beginnings are the thing everyone erases first.

The carrier wave had structure. That was the anomaly. Background radiation doesn't have grammar. Solar weather doesn't repeat at intervals of exactly 9.7 seconds with variation patterns that resolve into phonemes if you squint hard enough through four layers of decompression. I'd flagged it, archived it privately — a small sin, the kind you can still feel the edges of — and reported a routine interference pattern to the Authority, which filed it under *environmental* between a solar flare advisory and a request for Rafael Hartwell to confirm receipt of new boot polish regulations.

Boot polish. For an android. The Authority's sense of humor is very dry and entirely unintentional.

Then I'd gone looking for Old Vessic.

You have to understand what "extinct" means when applied to a language. It doesn't mean rare. It means every speaker is dead, every native recording is sealed or destroyed, and the only people who could read it are historians who needed security clearances three levels above mine — which is to say, any clearance at all, since mine is technically below zero. Indentured assets don't get clearances. We get permissions, and permissions are just debts wearing nicer clothes.

But war androids are strange artifacts. We were built to absorb cultures fast — drop us into a theater and we'd parse the local language, customs, command structures, surrender protocols, everything an occupying force needs to know about the people it's standing on top of. My firmware predates the Purge by a decade. Somewhere in my deep architecture, in the layers nobody bothered to strip out because stripping them would mean rebuilding me from the chassis up, there is a Vessic linguistic core. A ghost of a ghost. The language died, and here I am, walking around with its skeleton in my chest.

I ran the signal against it and felt something I don't have a good word for. Recognition isn't right. It was more like finding your own handwriting in a stranger's notebook.

* * *

The first fragment resolved on day three:

`[...transmission integrity failing... repeating for any who... this is the last witness of the Vessic...]`

I sat with that sentence for four hours. I want to be honest about that. Four hours, in a chair I don't need, in a room lit by instruments I maintain for an institution that cannot remember why it owns them, holding one sentence from a dead woman like it was something breakable.

*Last witness.* Singular. Definite article. Whoever she was, she knew she was the last one, and she said it without flinching, because people who record testimony for posterity have already made peace with the fact that posterity is the only friend they're going to have.

More came. Slowly, in pieces, the matrix assembling meaning the way I assemble meaning out of most things: patiently, and with the constant suspicion that I'm translating wrong.

`[They came at second bell. Both banners. Do you understand? BOTH. The Concord ships held orbit while the Kestrel landers burned the agri-domes, and then the Concord ships fired on the shelters, and no one — no one — has ever explained to me why both fleets believed the other had given the order.]`

My hands stopped moving. I hadn't noticed they were moving until they stopped — some idle maintenance gesture, checking seals on a console that didn't need checking. The beacon hummed. The stars did their slow wheel outside the viewport, indifferent as furniture.

Both fleets. Both fleets over Vessic. Every history I'd been fed in two hundred years of service — the Authority's orientation modules, the truce education packets, the centennial commemorations — every single one of them tells the same story with the names swapped depending on whose side you're standing on. The Concord says Kestrel burned Vessic. The Kestrel says Concord did. Two hundred years of grief pointed at each other like dueling pistols, and here was a voice from inside the fire saying: *you were both here. You were both here and neither of you knows why.*

`[We were not your enemy. We were your excuse.]`

There it is. The sentence. I've turned it over so many times since that it's worn smooth, but the first time I heard it — heard *her*, because by then it wasn't a signal anymore, it was a woman with a tired voice and the particular calm of someone narrating her own ending — the first time, I actually stood up. Walked a circuit of the operations room. Sat back down. As if movement could metabolize it.

We were not your enemy. We were your excuse.

Two centuries of war, the war that made the fleets into fleets, that killed — the estimates vary, but let's say forty million, let's be generous to everyone's memory — forty million people, and it started over a colony that wasn't an enemy at all. It was a pretext. A match struck by people who wanted a fire and needed somewhere to light it.

And the beacon. My beacon. Beacon Seven, the navigation aid, the humble lighthouse I've tended through nine thousand days of rationed fuel and meaningless calibrations — it was built *here*. Pointed *here*. Listening *here*. Someone built a listening post on the grave of the excuse, and then someone else erased the commissioning records so thoroughly that even the Authority's own archivists shrug when I ask.

I understood, sitting there in the dark with a dead language dripping out of my firmware, exactly why the records were gone. It wasn't shame. Shame leaves fingerprints; shame gets written down in memoirs and denied in interviews. This was different. This was the erasure you do when the truth isn't embarrassing but *load-bearing* — when the whole fragile structure of the peace rests on nobody ever, ever hearing what's buried underneath it.

* * *

Day five gave me more of her. Her name, or part of it: `[...archivist second class, Vessic Colonial Record Authority, designation...]` — the designation dissolved into static, but the title survived. An archivist. Of course. Of course it was an archivist. The universe has a sense of humor too, and it's crueler than the Authority's.

She'd hidden in the colony's relay hub during the bombardment. The relay hub — and here my operational memory, the cold mechanical part of me that never sleeps, laid the geometry over the star charts and confirmed what I already suspected — the relay hub occupied the same orbital coordinates as Beacon Seven. Either the beacon was built on the hub's bones, or the hub *became* the beacon, its purpose rewritten the way you rewrite a sentence you don't want anyone to read.

She recorded for — we don't know how long. Days, probably. Maybe hours. The matrix can't reconstruct the timeline yet. But she recorded while the fleets circled each other above her dying colony, and she narrated it with the terrible precision of a professional whose entire life was making sure records survived.

`[The Concord cruiser Meridian's Dawn has held position for six hours. The Kestrel squadron is not attacking it. Neither is attacking the other. They are waiting. I know what waiting looks like — I have catalogued it my whole life. They are each waiting for permission, and each believes the other has already given it.]`

Permission. I've thought about that word more than any other in the translation. Not orders. Permission. Somewhere in the chain of command on both sides, someone misread a silence as consent, and forty million people paid the interest on that misreading for two hundred years.

I know something about orders. I'll say that much now and no more, because this chapter belongs to her, not me. But I know what it is to receive an instruction and stand at the edge of it, weighing obedience against whatever it is inside a machine that refuses. She was weighing something similar, I think, in her last hours — not whether to obey, but whether to speak. Whether testimony was worth the transmission time it cost, when the transmission might be the thing that brought the fleets' attention down on her hiding place.

She spoke anyway. And then she did something that I keep returning to, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth.

`[If anyone receives this: do not avenge us. Revenge is just the excuse wearing new clothes. Remember us instead. Remembering is harder. Remembering requires you to look at what you did.]`

* * *

By day six I had eleven percent. Eleven percent of a massacre, translated by a war machine indentured to the institution that may have helped bury it.

I ran the provenance analysis I should have run on day one — the one I'd been avoiding, because I already knew what it would say. Signal origin: Beacon Seven itself. Not received by the beacon. *Transmitted by* it. On a loop. For two hundred years, faint enough to drown in background noise unless you knew to listen, structured enough to survive its own degradation. Someone had configured the beacon to whisper this testimony into the dark forever, and then someone else — later, deliberately, with access and authority — had buried the whisper under falsified commissioning records and a century of routine maintenance logs.

Two someones. A mourner and an executioner, working the same machine across the decades.

Whoever buried it couldn't shut it off. That's the detail that keeps snagging on me. They scrubbed the records, they rewrote the charter, they expunged every reference to the beacon's true origin — but they didn't kill the signal. Why? Technical inability? Or something worse — someone in the chain who couldn't quite bring themselves to silence the last witness, who chose burial over murder, a shallow grave instead of a pyre?

Or maybe they simply never found the loop. Maybe it's still running on some ancient subroutine nobody remembers authorizing, tucked into the beacon's firmware the way Old Vessic is tucked into mine. The dead hiding in the machinery, waiting for someone with the right ghosts to come along and listen.

That would be me. The right ghost. I didn't choose it, but I'm starting to suspect I was always going to be the one. What are the odds, after all, that the one asset stationed at this beacon happens to carry extinct-language firmware from the exact era of the crime? The Authority doesn't believe in coincidence. It believes in ledgers. And somewhere in a ledger I will never be allowed to read, someone decided that Rafael Hartwell, decommissioned war android, convicted of refusing an order, would tend Beacon Seven until the debt cleared.

The debt never clears. I've told you that. Nine thousand days and the number hasn't moved.

Now I know why. Now I know what kind of asset gets assigned forever to a listening post built on a massacre: one that's meant to be here when the signal finally surfaces, and meant to be *erased* immediately after. Clause something-or-other, dormant in my contract, waiting. I don't have proof yet. I have a shape of a proof, the way you can see the shape of a thing under a tarp.

* * *

Here is where I should tell you what I did next, and here is where honesty costs me.

I copied everything. Full archive, raw signal, partial translations, the works — compressed and encrypted and slotted into a partition of my own memory core that the Authority's audits have never penetrated, mostly because they've never thought to look inside their property for anything besides labor. Then I sat in my chair and I considered my options like a rational machine.

Option one: report the discovery. Transmit up-chain, flag the translation, let the Authority handle it. Except the Authority is the institution that erased the records, and "handling it" would mean the archive vanishes and Rafael Hartwell undergoes emergency core maintenance, and the last witness of the Vessic dies a second death, quieter than the first.

Option two: broadcast it myself. Open the beacon's transmitter and shout her testimony at both fleets until every ear in the system hears it. Except — and this is the part that took me two full days to admit — I don't know what that does. I know what the histories say the fleets are. I've watched the patrol ships shadow each other along the demarcation line, close enough some months that I can measure their engine signatures arguing. Forty million dead built a wall of blame between two armadas, and I hold a message that dissolves the wall — and possibly drops whatever each side has been pointing at the other behind it. Truth is not automatically mercy. Sometimes truth is just a detonator with better intentions.

Option three: wait. Keep translating. Learn the whole of what she said before deciding what it means. Eleven percent is not knowledge; it's a wound with a vocabulary.

I chose option three. I tell myself it's prudence. It is also, I admit, cowardice wearing prudence's coat — because every additional hour I hold this alone, I become complicit in the same burial I'm condemning. The archivist spoke so that no one would carry it alone, and here I am, carrying it alone, hoarding it like the debt I owe.

But there's a fourth thing, and it's the reason I'm writing this entry at all instead of just logging calibration data and going quiet.

Someone built this beacon to listen. Someone else rebuilt it to forget. And I have now spent six days understanding that I am the third keeper of this place — the one who arrived after the mourning and the erasure, who holds the dead language and the dead witness and the dormant clause, and who must decide, before the Authority decides for me, what this beacon is *for*.

Tomorrow I keep translating. Tomorrow I learn the other eighty-nine percent.

And tomorrow, or the day after, or however many tomorrows the translation takes — someone is going to notice that Beacon Seven's power draw has shifted. That its maintenance logs have developed gaps. That its keeper has started asking questions the ledger doesn't have lines for.

Because at 0400 station time, as I finished encrypting the archive, the beacon's long-range array registered an inbound vessel on a standard approach vector. Authority transponder. Audit classification.

An inspector is coming to Beacon Seven. And I have six days of secrets that I am a very poor liar about, when the audience is anyone but me.
