## 14. Two Fleets, One Grave

I keep my evidence in a ammunition crate because that's what was available when I moved onto Relay Nine, and because there's a certain poetry to it that I've never had the luxury of appreciating until now. Concord-issue munitions box, stenciled serial number half worn away, holding not shells but data slates wrapped in vacuum cloth. My whole life's work. It weighs about as much as a sleeping cat.

Rafael looks at the crate the way you'd look at a stranger holding your grandmother's photo album.

"You said you had proof," Oyelaran says. She's standing with her arms crossed, which I've learned is her resting state, and she's positioned herself between me and Rafael, which I've also learned is her resting state. The woman audits everything, including conversations.

"I said I had *everything*," I tell her. "Proof is just the part that fits in a report."

The relay's operations bay is cramped — it was built for two technicians and a coffee machine, and now it holds three people and a dead language — but I've made it home over four years, and I know exactly where every cable runs and which floor panels hide the good salvage. I kick one open and start laying out slates on the deck plating like a card sharp showing a hand.

"Vessic Colony," I say. "Population eleven thousand, four hundred and six at last census. Both fleets agree on that number, by the way. It's the only thing they agree on."

"Juno." That's Rafael, quiet, careful. They've been quiet and careful since we got back from the relay's outer ring where I'd shown them the interception array. "Maybe start slower."

"No." I hear my own voice come out flat and hard, and I let it. "You've had your slow starts. You've had weeks of translating in your little vault while the clock runs down. I've had *four years*, Rafael. Four years of listening to this signal and not knowing what it was, and then months of knowing, and every single day both fleets sail around out there telling their children stories about who burned Vessic. So no. I'm not starting slower. I'm starting now."

Oyelaran doesn't move. Rafael doesn't either. Fine.

I flip the first slate active and hold it up so the light catches both their faces.

"This is Concord fleet doctrine, edition nine, historical annex. 'The Kestrel Armada opened fire on the civilian colony of Vessic without provocation, an act of barbarism that necessitated the war.'" I tap the slate. "Standard issue. Every cadet memorizes it. Now."

Second slate.

"Kestrel Armada founding charter, ratified after the armistice. 'Concord's unprovoked destruction of Vessic remains the original sin from which all subsequent hostilities flow.'" I let that hang. "Same sentence. Different villain. Identical grammar."

"They can't both be right," Oyelaran says slowly.

"They can't both be right," I agree, "but here's the beautiful part — they don't need to be. Each one only needs its own audience. Concord cadets never read Kestrel charters. Kestrel children never see Concord doctrine. Two fleets, two histories, zero overlap. It's not even a good lie. It's just a well-*fenced* one."

I'm aware, distantly, that my hands are shaking. I keep going anyway.

*

Here is the thing I don't say, the thing sitting under my ribs like shrapnel: I know what reprisal raids look like. I was six years old when the Kestrel hit Meridian Station, the supply hub my family's habitat cluster orbited. My mother worked air recycling, third shift. When the hull breached on her deck, the vacuum took forty-one people in under a minute, and Concord's response was to burn a Kestrel shipyard, and Kestrel's response to *that* was to hit another civilian station, and so it went, year after year, an argument conducted entirely in funerals.

My father stopped speaking for almost a year after my mother died. When he started again, he only talked about the war. Who owed whom. What the ledger said. He kept a ledger, actually — an actual paper book, absurd and precious, where he wrote down every attack and every retaliation, trying to find the bottom line, trying to find the entry that would balance everything out and let him stop.

He died before he found it. There is no balancing entry. I checked his math myself, later, when I was old enough and angry enough. The ledger doesn't close. It was never designed to close. That's the whole point of it.

So when I tell these two — the auditor and the android, the institutionalist and the machine — that I want to broadcast the testimony *now*, tonight, immediately, understand that I am not being reckless. I am being *precise*. I have done the arithmetic of grief, and I know exactly how much silence costs per day, because my family paid the rate for twenty years.

*

"The archivist's testimony confirms it," I say, pulling up the fragments Rafael and I have cross-translated. "Listen to the sequence. Both fleets received falsified sensor returns near Vessic. Both fleets were told the other had already fired. Both fleets' forward elements engaged the colony's defense grid believing it was hostile — believing they were *responding*."

"That's still engagement," Oyelaran says. Her voice has gone very careful, the way it does when she's filing something internally. "Firing on a civilian colony under false pretenses is still firing on a civilian colony."

"Yes!" I throw my hands up. "Thank you! That's the point nobody wants to reach! Everyone wants this to be a misunderstanding story — oops, bad sensors, tragic accident, let's all hold hands. No. Somebody *built* those false returns. Somebody forged the orders. And whoever it was, they did it knowing both fleets would pull the trigger, and then both fleets spent two centuries pointing at each other across the smoke so nobody would ever look for the arsonist."

Rafael has been silent through all of this. Standing there with that terrible patience they have, the patience of something that has counted seconds for decades. When they finally speak, their voice is low.

"You're right about the lie. All of it. I've verified the translation independently — the testimony matches the signal structure, the dating, everything Juno's decoded."

"*But,*" I say, because I've been around long enough to hear the word coming.

"But," Rafael acknowledges, "a broadcast right now doesn't indict the arsonist. It indicts the audience. Both fleets are postured at the demarcation line with weapons hot and centennial ceremonies scheduled. If the testimony goes out raw, each side will hear the parts that implicate the other first. Concord intelligence will strip-mine it for anti-Kestrel material inside the hour. Kestrel will do the same in reverse. The truth becomes ammunition before anyone finishes reading it."

"So we wait? We wait for the *right moment*?" I laugh, and it comes out ugly. "There is no right moment. There's never a right moment. That's what everyone told my father — wait, be patient, the peace process needs time. Twenty years of waiting. You know what waiting bought us? A bigger ledger."

"I'm not saying wait forever—"

"Then how long? Give me a number, Rafael. How many more reprisal raids is the truth worth delaying for?"

Rafael doesn't answer. That's the thing about them — they won't lie to spare you, even when lying would be kinder. They stand there in the dim light of the relay bay, this decommissioned weapon wearing a maintenance tech's coveralls, and they take my fury full in the face without flinching, and they don't answer, because there isn't an answer that survives contact with the numbers.

Oyelaran clears her throat. "The Commission has contingency protocols for disclosure scenarios. Staged release, authenticated provenance, simultaneous delivery to both fleets' diplomatic channels—"

"Diplomatic channels," I repeat. "You mean the same diplomatic channels that have been sitting on the Vessic question for a hundred years? The ones jointly redacting the beacon's charter?" I turn on her. "Inspector, your institution helped build the fence around this lie. You want me to hand the truth back to the people who buried it and trust them to unwrap it gently?"

Her jaw tightens. For a second I think I've finally cracked the composure, and part of me — the ugly part, the part that keeps my father's ledger in my head — wants to see it break.

It doesn't break. But something shifts behind her eyes, some recalculation, and when she speaks again her voice is quieter.

"No," she admits. "I don't fully trust them. I trust process, and process is failing, and I'm aware of the distinction." She looks down at the slates spread across the deck. "I've spent twenty years believing the record could be corrected from inside. This week I've learned the record was designed never to be corrected. So I understand urgency, Ms. Reyes. I promise you I do. I'm asking you to help me aim it."

"Aim it," I say. "At what? At whom? Because if we wait for the perfect shot, we'll still be aiming when the truce expires and both fleets start shooting on schedule."

Nobody answers that either. The relay hums around us, carrying traffic from a hundred ships that are all, right now, sailing peacefully toward a cliff edge with flags flying.

*

Later — hours later, after Oyelaran has left for the beacon and Rafael has stayed behind, ostensibly to help me re-secure the interception array — I find Rafael standing at the viewport, looking out at nothing.

"My family died in the reprisal raids," I say. I haven't planned to say it. It just comes out, the way confessions do, sideways and unsolicited. "Meridian Station. I was six. My mother was on shift when the hull went. My father spent the rest of his life keeping a ledger of who owed whom, trying to make it balance. It never balanced. I finished the math after he died. It doesn't close, Rafael. It was never supposed to close. That's why I can't sit on this. Every day of silence is another line item."

Rafael is quiet for a long moment. Out in the dark, a navigation light pulses on some distant hulk, patient as a heartbeat.

"I was present at actions during the war," they say finally. "Before my court-martial. Some of them involved civilian proximity. I have fragments. Incomplete records." Their voice does something I've never heard it do — it frays, slightly, at the edges. "I cannot fully refute your fury, Juno. I can only tell you that I have seen what happens when grief commands the trigger. I was built for it. I am, in a sense, the ledger your father kept, given legs and a service history."

"And?"

"And I think the archivist knew that too." Rafael turns to look at me, and their optical sensors catch the starlight in a way that makes them seem almost wet. "Her final instruction wasn't 'avenge us.' It wasn't 'punish them.' It was 'let neither side carry this alone.' She watched both fleets pass her dying colony without stopping. She understood that blame distributed evenly becomes blame abandoned entirely. If we broadcast this as a weapon, each fleet will use it to put the whole weight on the other, and the weight will crush someone else's mother on someone else's station, and the ledger grows another page."

"So we do nothing."

"We do the hardest thing," Rafael says. "We make them carry it together. At the same time. In the same room. Or not at all."

I want to scream at them. I want to tell them that's a luxury of someone whose family isn't ash, that principle is easy when you're not the one doing the paying. I open my mouth to say all of it—

—and that's when the array chimes.

Not the routine traffic tone. Not the beacon handshake. This is the priority intercept alert I configured myself, the one keyed to encrypted military-band transmissions originating from within the system.

I cross the bay in three strides and pull the decode. My blood goes cold reading the header, because I recognize the command structure prefix. Concord Fleet Command, routing flag: internal security directorate.

And the destination field isn't a ship. It isn't a station.

It's Beacon Seven.

"They're not waiting for the truce to expire," I whisper. "Someone in Concord knows about the testimony. And they're sending someone to collect it — or to bury it."

Behind me, Rafael has gone very still, the way machines do when they're running calculations that humans would call fear.
