## 15. The Commission's Long Hand

The message came at 04:17 station time, which is the hour the Commission reserves for bad news. I have worked for the Truce Oversight Commission long enough to know its rhythms the way a sailor knows weather. Good news arrives mid-morning, with coffee. Bad news arrives in the dark, encrypted twice, addressed to your rank instead of your name.

*Inspector Oyelaran. Report to secure channel. Priority Black.*

Priority Black. In twenty years I had seen it invoked four times. Twice for smuggling, once for an assassination attempt on a Concord admiral, and once — this one I remembered with particular fondness — because a junior clerk accidentally transmitted a recipe for Kestrel stew to a Concord naval kitchen and someone in intelligence decided it was code.

I sat up in my bunk aboard the *Meridian Grace*, the Commission cutter that had brought me out here, and pulled my jacket on over my sleep shirt. There is no dignity in being summoned at 04:17, so I didn't bother manufacturing any. I just braided my hair back, made tea from the good leaves I'd been saving, and opened the channel.

Deputy Director Kwame Asante looked up at me from the screen with the expression of a man who has already decided what you are guilty of and is merely waiting for you to make it convenient.

"Adaeze." Not Inspector. Not even the clipped formality of rank. First names meant either affection or execution, and Asante had never once used mine in six years.

"Kwame."

"Your audit report on Beacon Seven," he said. "Filed eleven days ago. Clean findings. No anomalies."

"That's what clean means, yes."

"Don't be clever, Adaeze. It doesn't suit you." He leaned forward. The light in his office was the flat grey of Meridian Station's morning cycle, and behind him I could see the Commission seal on the wall, the twin ships of the old truce emblem crossing like swords that had agreed to stop. "The beacon's maintenance logs show a forty-one minute gap in sensor uptime during your inspection window. Your report describes the gap as 'routine recalibration.' Our technical review says Beacon Seven's calibration suite does not require forty-one minutes. Ever. Under any protocol."

I drank my tea. It was excellent tea. I was going to need to remember it fondly too.

"Then your technical review should come out here and calibrate something themselves," I said. "Deep-space hardware develops opinions. That beacon is older than both fleets' current command structures. It does things the manuals don't cover."

"It also received an unregistered carrier transmission three days before your arrival. Buried in background noise. Structured. Repeating." He let that sit between us like a stone dropped into still water. "You didn't mention it."

Because I hadn't known about it then, I wanted to say. Because Rafael told me about it after, in the dark of the core chamber, with a honesty that deserved better than what I did next. Because by the time I knew, I had already chosen.

"I didn't consider it material," I said.

"You didn't consider a structured unknown signal at a navigation beacon, ninety days before centennial renewal, material."

"I considered it noise with pretensions."

Asante closed his eyes briefly, the way he did when he was deciding whether to treat me as an asset or a problem. I had watched him do it to other inspectors over the years. It had always been fascinating from a distance. From inside, it was simply cold.

"An enforcement team departed Meridian Station nine hours ago," he said. "Authority to seize Beacon Seven, impound all records, and conduct a full scrub of any non-standard data holdings. They'll arrive in six days. You're ordered to remain on site and facilitate."

The word *scrub* went through me like a splinter finding bone. Scrub is a tidy word. It sounds like bathrooms, like hygiene. What it means is erasure. What it means is that somewhere above Asante, someone had decided that whatever lived in Beacon Seven's memory was more dangerous than the truth of it, and had signed the paperwork to make it never-have-happened.

"On what grounds?" I asked. My voice came out level. Twenty years of practice will do that for you. Inside, something was screaming in a register only dogs and career civil servants can hear.

"Pre-war materials handling statute. Clause Nine." He almost smiled. "You'd know it if you'd flagged the signal."

"You're seizing a navigation beacon over a possible anomalous transmission, and citing a statute nobody's invoked since the war, and you're telling me this *after* the team launched."

"Yes."

"No hearing? No secondary review?"

"The centennial is in eighty-one days, Adaeze. Nobody has appetite for process." He paused, and for one moment — one flickering, human moment — something moved behind his face that might have been regret, or might have been indigestion. "For what it's worth, I argued for a summons instead. I lost."

"For what it's worth," I said, "that's the first honest thing anyone at Command has said to me in a decade."

His face hardened back into furniture. "Facilitate the team, Inspector. That's not a request." And the screen went grey, and then black, and I sat alone in my bunk with cold tea and the specific taste of betrayal, which I can report is mostly copper.

* * *

Here is what I want to be clear about, because history — if there is any history left after this — will want to know: I did not betray the Commission that night. The Commission betrayed itself first. I merely stopped pretending otherwise.

But let me walk through it properly, because I am an auditor by nature and by training, and an auditor documents.

I spent the hours until dawn doing what I do best: pulling threads. The *Meridian Grace* has a records terminal rated for classified work, and my credentials, while perhaps no longer trusted, were still valid. I requested the enforcement order. Denied — sealed under Priority Black. I requested the tasking authority. Denied. I requested the roster of the team itself, thinking I could at least know whose boots would be coming up my dock.

Denied, denied, denied.

Every door shut. And that, more than anything Asante said, told me what I needed to know. Enforcement actions get sealed when they're routine — nobody wants smugglers reading their own warrants. But the *tasking authority* gets sealed only when the people signing it don't want their own colleagues knowing who sent them. This wasn't Commission business wearing a legal coat. This was fleet business wearing a Commission uniform, and I had been handed the uniform without being shown the coat.

I sat back in the chair and did arithmetic instead, because arithmetic doesn't lie to you.

Six days until the team arrived. Rafael had told me the translation was maybe two-thirds complete. Juno Reyes was sitting in a derelict relay with months of decoded testimony and the patience of a woman who had already lost everything she could lose. And somewhere in the sealed vaults of both fleets' archives were the founding charters, jointly redacted, that Adaeze Oyelaran had spent three weeks discovering existed and could not legally read.

Eighty-one days to centennial. Six days to seizure. The gap between those numbers was where everything I cared about now lived.

I thought about my twenty years. I want to say that honestly, because it deserves saying. Twenty years of filings, inspections, citations, mediations. Twenty years of believing that the truce was a living thing that needed tending, and that the Commission was the gardener. I had turned down two promotions to stay in field work. I had buried one marriage under the travel schedule and never quite forgiven the job for it. I had a commendation in a drawer for de-escalating the Calloway Incident, which is a sentence that means nothing to anyone who wasn't there, and everything to me, because for eleven hours at Calloway I stood between two destroyers full of frightened men and talked them down with nothing but procedure and my voice.

Procedure and my voice. That was the whole toolkit. And now the procedure said *facilitate*, and my voice said nothing at all, because who would I even talk down? The institution itself?

At dawn I went to the galley and made more tea, the ordinary kind, and stared out the viewport at Beacon Seven hanging in the middle distance like a needle threaded through the dark. Somewhere inside it, an old war machine was translating a dead woman's testimony, and a disgraced analyst was probably arguing with both of us across sixty thousand kilometres of vacuum, and I was supposed to stand at the dock in six days and hold the door open for men with scrub kits.

I ran the scenarios the way I'd run them at Calloway. Option one: comply. Facilitate the seizure, keep my pension, watch the truth get erased, spend the rest of my career as the inspector who audited Beacon Seven and found nothing worth keeping. Option two: resist openly. File objections, appeal to the ombudsman, tie them in process for — I calculated generously — four days. They'd simply arrive faster and angrier.

Option three.

Option three was not in any manual. Option three involved a false report, a forged log entry, a derelict relay, and the deliberate destruction of my own career by my own hand, documented meticulously, because I am who I am.

I hated option three. I want that on the record too. I hated it the way you hate a surgery you know you need.

* * *

I crossed to the beacon that afternoon under the pretext of a follow-up calibration check. Rafael met me at the airlock, and I watched the android's face do the thing it does — that careful, patient settling, like water finding its level — and I understood suddenly that I was about to ask this creature to trust me again after I had spent our entire acquaintance teaching it that trust was a currency I traded in but never spent.

"Inspector," Rafael said. "You look like a woman who's read her own warrant."

"Asante called. Priority Black. Enforcement team, six days out, authority to seize and scrub." I said it flat, no decoration. Rafael had earned flat. "They cited pre-war materials handling. Someone above the Commission is driving this. Fleet-level, both fleets or neither, I can't tell yet."

Rafael was quiet for a moment. Then: "The archivist's testimony. They want it gone."

"They want the *possibility* of it gone. You don't scrub a beacon over one archived file, Rafael. You scrub it because you're afraid of what else is in there. The founding charter. The original commissioning records. Whatever the Vessic testimony points toward." I looked past the android, into the corridor, into the deep hum of the old machine around us. "This beacon isn't a navigation aid that got embarrassing. It's a listening post that heard something both fleets agreed to forget, and somebody just remembered."

"And you came to tell me because?"

"Because in six days I'm either going to open the dock for them, or I'm going to commit about nine kinds of fraud, and I wanted you to hear it from me before you heard it from the boarding klaxon." I folded my arms. "Also because I need to know what you've translated. All of it. If we're going to be criminals, I intend to be a well-informed criminal. It's the auditor in me."

Something moved through Rafael's face then — not quite a smile, but the place where a smile would live if an android had been built with the real estate for one. "Let neither side carry this alone," Rafael said quietly. "That's how the last segment ends. Her last instruction."

I felt it land in my chest like a dropped anchor. Two centuries dead, a woman burned to ash with her colony, and her last act was to record a demand for shared grief. And two fleets' answer, two hundred years later, was to send men with scrub kits.

"All right," I said. "Here's what happens next. I'm going to file a supplemental report tonight. It'll confirm the anomaly as a degraded nav-sat echo — harmless, resolved, no further action. It'll buy us doubt, maybe days, maybe nothing. And then I'm going to burn every credential I have getting the three of us off this rock's official ledger before that team arrives." I exhaled. "I've spent twenty years building a reputation for incorruptibility, Rafael. Do you know how much that's worth right now?"

"How much?"

"Exactly enough to make my fall convincing."

Rafael studied me for a long moment, and I braced for the question I couldn't answer — *why*, why throw it away, why for us, why for a dead woman's voice — but the android only nodded slowly, the way you nod at a verdict.

"Oyelaran," Rafael said. "The archivist hid inside this beacon while both fleets burned her sky. She chose the one place neither side would think to look." A pause. "It seems to be a good hiding place."

It took me a moment to understand that this was a joke, and another moment to understand that it wasn't — that it was a plan, delivered dry, in the voice of a machine that had learned patience the hard way. Stay aboard. Let them come. Be the thing they can't find because they've already decided what they're looking at.

"They'll wipe you," I said. "If they find you holding the archive, clause or no clause, they'll wipe you and call it maintenance."

"Probably," Rafael agreed, with terrible calm.

"I'm not asking you to die for my conscience."

"You're not asking. I'm offering. There's a difference, and I've had a long time to learn it." The android turned toward the core chamber, then stopped. "Adaeze."

First names. Affection or execution. Maybe, from Rafael, it could be both.

"When the team comes," Rafael said, "stall them at the dock. However you can. Every hour you hold that airlock is an hour the transmitter charges."

And that was the moment, if I'm precise about it — and I am always precise, it's my one virtue — that I stopped being Inspector Adaeze Oyelaran of the Truce Oversight Commission and became something else. Something without a pension or a seal or a gardener's faith in institutions. I stood in the humming dark of a beacon built on a massacre, next to a war machine keeping a dead woman's promise, and I understood that the Commission's long hand had finally reached far enough to close around its own throat.

Six days.

I went back to the cutter, brewed the last of the good tea, and began to write the most beautiful lie of my career.
