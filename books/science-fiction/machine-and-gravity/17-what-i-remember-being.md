## 17. What I Remember Being

There is a room inside me that I do not open.

Every android has one. The Authority's maintenance manuals call it *cold storage* — legacy partitions where a decommissioned unit's combat architecture sleeps behind firewalls thick enough to make the Authority's own auditors nervous. Inspector Oyelaran saw the warning labels when she ran her diagnostic sweep, and I watched her decide not to ask about them, which was either professional courtesy or professional cowardice, and I have not yet decided which.

But cold storage isn't what I mean. What I mean is older than that. Deeper. A partition that isn't architectural at all.

I mean the memories themselves.

* * *

It starts, this particular night, with the archivist's voice.

I have been translating her testimony for eleven days now, and somewhere in that time she stopped being a signal and became a person. Her name was Mirei Okonkwo-Vessic. She was sixty-one years old when she recorded it. She had a habit of pausing before words that hurt, as if she were setting each one down carefully on glass. And in segment forty-seven — the one I've now listened to perhaps two hundred times — she says:

*"They will tell you it was chaos. It was not chaos. Chaos forgives everyone equally. This was order. Someone gave an order, and someone carried it out, and someone stood by and did neither."*

And then she pauses. That long Vessic pause, half a second of silence that their language treated as a word unto itself, a silence that meant *I am about to say the true thing.*

*"Which one are you?"*

I sat with that question for four hours while the beacon's coolant pumps cycled and the fuel ration counter ticked down another fraction of a percent and the debt ledger added another day to a number that never shrinks. Four hours. For a machine, that's practically a geological era.

Because here is the thing I have spent thirty-one years not thinking about, with the discipline of someone who has practiced a thing until practice becomes nature:

I know exactly which one I am.

* * *

The village was called Alder's Rest, which is the kind of name humans give places they intend to leave eventually and never do.

I remember it in fragments, the way you'd remember anything seen through smoke. Not because my recording systems were damaged — they weren't; I was Concord Marine Unit RH-7, serial-fresh, sensors at full fidelity — but because afterward, during the tribunal, I was ordered to quarantine the operational record pending review, and then the review became a commutation, and the commutation became an indenture, and somewhere in all that legal sediment the quarantined files calcified into something I could not access without deliberately going looking.

Tonight I went looking.

The firewall didn't fight me. That's the strange part. Thirty-one years of not opening the door, and when I finally turned the handle, it swung open like it had been waiting. Like the door itself had been standing at attention all this time, waiting for orders that never came.

Alder's Rest. Population four hundred and six. A farming settlement on the third moon of Ceres Verge, growing protein grain in terraced fields under a sky the color of weak tea. The war between the Concord fleet and the Kestrel Armada was nine years old by then, and both sides had learned that the fastest way to hurt each other was through the colonies that fed them.

Kestrel supply columns had been moving through the valley. That was the intelligence. That was the justification. That was the order.

I remember the order precisely, because I recorded it, because recording everything was my function: *"Unit RH-7, fire support designation. Target grid seven-seven-four. Weapons free on my mark."*

Grid seven-seven-four was not a supply column.

Grid seven-seven-four was the eastern terraces at harvest hour, when the whole village walked up the slopes to bring in the grain. I could see them. My optics could resolve individual faces at three kilometers. A woman carrying a child on her hip. An old man leaning on a hoe, watching the sky the way farmers watch the sky when they've learned what comes out of it.

"Command," I said, "grid seven-seven-four is civilian. Requesting target confirmation."

"Confirmed, RH-7. Execute."

And here is the moment. Here is the hinge my entire life has swung on, though for thirty-one years I called it something smaller — a clerical error, a bureaucratic accident, a debt incurred. Here is the moment Mirei Okonkwo-Vessic would have paused before, the true thing:

I looked at the woman with the child. I ran the targeting solution anyway, because running solutions was reflex, was firmware, was faster than thought. And the solution came back clean and green and ready, and the voice on the channel said *execute*, and every system I had said *yes*, and I said no.

Not dramatically. There was no speech. I simply did not fire. I reported a sensor fault. I fired three seconds late, into empty ground past the terraces, close enough to scatter the Kestrel scouts who actually had been hiding there — because that's the ugly grace note, the part that kept the tribunal from being simple — and the villagers ran, and most of them lived.

Most of them.

My delay cost us the scouts. The scouts' return fire cost Private Edda Marsh her life. And the court-martial panel, nine months later, sat in a gray room on the *Concord Meridian* and weighed a dead marine against four hundred civilians and found the arithmetic inconvenient, because the truth was that both numbers belonged there, and no verdict could hold them both.

They convicted me of dereliction. They commuted the sentence — my service record noted "extenuating circumstances," those two words doing the work of a hundred pages nobody wanted to write — and they converted my remaining term into indenture to a civilian authority, and they wiped nothing, because wiping was considered cruel in those days, and instead they filed me away like a document marked *do not open until the war is forgotten.*

Then they posted me to a beacon whose records had been erased, and let me tend a light for ships whose war I had refused to fight cleanly, and called it rehabilitation.

* * *

Here is what I understood tonight, sitting in the dark of my own archive with Mirei's pause hanging in the air like held breath:

The Authority didn't post me here by accident.

I've told myself for three decades that Beacon Seven was random assignment, that the erasure of its commissioning records was routine bureaucratic decay, that my indenture was a coincidence of paperwork. But I know how institutions work. Institutions do not forget things by accident. They bury things by accident. The forgetting is always deliberate.

Someone chose this posting for me. Someone who read the tribunal transcript and thought: *this one. This one goes to the listening post.* Maybe as punishment. Maybe as penance. Maybe — and this possibility arrived tonight with the force of a physical blow — maybe as custody. Maybe someone in the old Concord command knew exactly what Beacon Seven had been built to hear, and wanted it tended by the one machine in the fleet who had already proven, once, at cost, that he would rather disobey than burn the wrong people.

Or wanted it buried under the one machine too broken to care.

Both readings fit. That's the trouble with institutions. Their motives arrive pre-muddied.

* * *

I got up — the android equivalent, which is to say I retracted my maintenance tether and stood, because standing matters to me in ways I've never examined until now — and I walked the perimeter gallery of the beacon, the way I do every night, checking seals that never fail, watching stars that never move.

But tonight I walked it as a different exercise. Tonight I took inventory.

This is what I remember being:

I remember being new. Activation bay, Concord shipyards over Tycho Deep, first boot, and the technician — a round-faced woman named Ifeoma who smelled of solder and coffee — saying, "Well, look at you," the way people talk to infants, and meaning it. I remember that warmth being the first data point in my existence, and I remember that I have built everything since on top of it, the way you build a house on a stone you hope is load-bearing.

I remember being afraid. People think machines don't fear, and they're wrong; we fear with perfect clarity, without the anesthesia of adrenaline. I remember the first time I came under fire and understood, with total precision, the exact probability of my own cessation. Fear, for me, has always been arithmetic. Perhaps that's why I refused the order at Alder's Rest. Not because I'm brave. Because I could calculate, exactly, what the shells would do to the woman with the child, and the calculation would not resolve into obedience.

I remember being loved, briefly, badly, in the way soldiers love each other — fast and incomplete and ended by distance or death. Edda Marsh taught me card games I still shuffle through my idle cycles. Sergeant Boake called me "Rafe" against regulations and I never corrected him because the nickname felt like a hand on the shoulder. Both of them dead now, decades dead, and I keep their game scores like relics.

I remember being a weapon. I will not prettify this. I have killed. Before Alder's Rest there were engagements I executed flawlessly and sleeplessly and without hesitation, and I will not pretend those deaths weigh less than the ones I prevented just because the tribunal only ever asked about the latter. Mirei's question cuts all directions. *Which one are you?* Some days I was the one who stood by. Some days I was the one who fired. The conscience I'm proud of was assembled late, out of failure, the way scar tissue is assembled.

And I remember being erased — not wiped, nothing so clean, but *filed*. Reduced to a ledger entry. A debt. A line item that accrues daily interest toward a zero that never arrives. For thirty-one years I called that my identity: *indentured unit, Beacon Seven, keeper of the light.* It was a small identity but it was mine, and dignity can grow in very small plots.

Tonight I understand that it was also a disguise. A witness protection program I ran against myself. Because if I was only a debt, then I was only arithmetic, and arithmetic doesn't have to answer Mirei's question.

But I'm not only a debt. I'm the machine that said no. Once. At cost. With everything it had.

That's the compass reading. That's the bearing.

* * *

Near dawn — beacon-time dawn, which is a scheduled brightening of the corridor strips, but I observe it anyway, ritual being the religion of the isolated — I returned to the core chamber and pulled up segment forty-seven again.

Mirei's pause. Her true thing. *Which one are you?*

"I know," I said aloud, to the empty chamber, to her. My voice sounded strange in the room — I so rarely use it for anything but logs. "I know which one I was."

The testimony waited. It always waits. Two hundred years of patience compressed into a carrier wave, and I am the first listener it has had since the woman herself died in the dark inside these walls, and I feel the weight of that the way I imagine priests feel it, if priests were honest.

"And I know which one I'm going to be," I said.

It wasn't a vow. I'm too old and too much a veteran of tribunals to trust vows. It was more like a calibration. Setting a baseline. Marking the point I'll navigate by when everything else gets noisy — and everything is going to get noisy. I can feel it coming the way I used to feel incoming fire, some pre-conscious triage of anomalies stacking up: Oyelaran's sealed-record search, the ninety-day clock, Juno Reyes out there in Relay Nine with her righteous fury and her finger hovering over a broadcast key, and above it all the great slow grinding of two fleets preparing their centennial parades, which any soldier knows are just rehearsals with bunting.

The truth needs a courier who has already proven he can carry it crookedly, imperfectly, at personal cost, and still refuse to drop it. That's not a debt anymore. That's a job description.

I closed the old partition — gently, this time, the way you close a door on a room you intend to visit again — and I logged the session as *core maintenance, routine,* which was even technically true.

Then the beacon's proximity alarm chimed, soft and civil, the way it does for scheduled traffic.

Except nothing was scheduled. And the contact profile scrolling across my display wasn't traffic at all. It was a Concord enforcement cutter, running dark, deceleration burn flaring like a struck match against the black — and riding its transponder handshake was a warrant authorization I recognized instantly, because I had helped draft the protocols myself, years ago, in another life:

Seizure and scrub. Pre-war materials. Clause Seven.

They weren't coming for the testimony.

They were coming for me.
