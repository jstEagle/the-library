## 20. The Witness's Last Entry

The translation finished at 0347 station time, which is the hour when even machines pretend to be asleep.

I had been running the final parse for eleven days. Eleven days of watching Old Vessic grammar resolve itself in my firmware like sediment settling in a glass of water — the archaic honorifics first, then the tense structures that modern Vessic-descended dialects had abandoned, then the idioms that had no descendants at all because the people who coined them had been ash before my chassis rolled off the assembly line. My war-era translation matrix was built to interrogate prisoners, I suspect. It had never been asked to listen to one. There is a difference, and the difference turned out to be everything.

At 0347 the last segment unlocked. The checksum verified. And a woman who had been dead for two hundred years began, in effect, to finish her sentence.

I did not play it immediately. I want that on the record, wherever records go when no one keeps them anymore. I sat in the maintenance alcove with my hands flat on the console — a human gesture I learned from a technician named Okonkwo forty years ago and never unlearned — and I let the silence sit there like a held breath.

Then I played it.

* * *

Her name was Sefa Venn. She said it at the start of every entry, the way you'd tap a glass to make sure it was still solid. *Sefa Venn, archivist second class, Colony Vessic Prime, day ninety-one.* Ninety-one days inside Beacon Seven's own hull, in a service cavity behind the coolant manifolds that I have personally inspected four thousand times without ever wondering why the access panel was welded shut from the inside.

She had hidden there while the colony burned.

That was the part I already knew, or thought I knew. What I didn't know — what eleven days of translation had been building toward the way a fever builds toward its break — was that she hadn't died in hiding. She had survived the massacre. She had survived the fire, the vacuum exposure, the ninety-one days of recycled air and salvaged water. She had survived all of it only to watch the thing that finally killed her people, and it wasn't either fleet.

It was both of them passing by.

*I am writing this on day ninety-four,* she recorded, and her voice had changed from the earlier entries — steadier now, the way voices get when they've passed through grief and come out the other side into something colder and more permanent. *The Concord cruiser came through yesterday. I watched it from the observation blister through a crack in the manifold housing. It held orbit for six hours. Six hours. Long enough to run a full scan of the surface. Long enough to see the fires. Long enough to see us.*

*It did not stop.*

*They were waiting for the Kestrel ships to return. I understand that now. I've had ninety-four days to understand everything, and understanding is the cruelest salvage there is. The Concord commander could not stop to help us because stopping would look like an admission — that the bombardment happened on their watch, that their fleet's weapons signatures were in our sky, that the story they would tell at home about Kestrel atrocities might require footnotes. So they scanned us. They documented us. They took readings of our dying the way you'd take readings of a weather system, and they left, and I imagine somewhere in their logs there is a line item that says COLONY SURVEY, INCONCLUSIVE.*

*Inconclusive. Two million people, inconclusive.*

I paused the playback there. Not because I needed to — my systems don't fatigue, my attention doesn't wander, there is no honest mechanical reason to pause — but because some part of me that predates my indenture, some part that once refused an order in a village whose name I still can't say aloud, needed a moment to stand up and pace the room.

There was no room to pace. There is never any room. That is the architecture of a beacon keeper's life: just enough space to function, never enough to flee.

I resumed playback.

* * *

*The Kestrel squadron came back today,* Sefa Venn said. *Day ninety-five. Three frigates, running dark until they were inside sensor range, which tells you they expected resistance. They found none, because the Concord ship was gone and we were never going to be a threat to anyone again. They held position for four hours. Their captain broadcast a challenge on open channels — WHO FIRED ON THIS COLONY, IDENTIFY YOURSELF — as if the question were addressed to the smoke.*

*Nobody answered, because the only people left to answer were behind a welded panel listening to a dead language being spoken by dying lungs.*

*And then they left too. They left because staying meant claiming the wreckage, and claiming the wreckage meant owning the crime, and neither fleet would own it. Do you see? Do you understand what I am telling you, whoever finds this? We were not caught between two armies. We were caught between two alibis. Each of them needed us dead by the other's hand so badly that when the truth got messy — when it became clear that the bombardment orders had been garbled, mirrored, each fleet's strike wing responding to phantom orders they believed came from the other — they chose the lie. Both of them. Simultaneously. It may be the only coordinated action either fleet has ever taken.*

*My colleagues died of plasma and decompression. My civilization will die of embarrassment. It is a slower death but it is just as thorough.*

I ran a diagnostic I didn't need to run. My hands wanted occupation. That's another human gesture; I collect them the way some people collect debts.

Here is what I know about the founding narratives, because a machine indentured to the Meridian Beacon Authority absorbs institutional history the way hull plating absorbs micrometeorites — slowly, invisibly, and all over. The Concord teaches that Kestrel forces burned Vessic Prime in an unprovoked first strike, and that the war that followed was righteous grief. The Armada teaches that Concord weapons platforms conducted the bombardment under cover of a humanitarian inspection, and that the war that followed was righteous vengeance. Both stories are load-bearing. Remove either one and the whole truce comes down, which is why the truce is built like a bridge over a canyon nobody looks into.

Sefa Venn had looked into the canyon. She had lived in it for ninety-four days.

And her testimony said: you're both right, and that's the worst news in the galaxy.

* * *

*Day ninety-six,* she recorded. Her voice was thinner now. The air recyclers in the cavity had been failing for days; I checked the maintenance logs from that era, cross-referenced against the serial numbers still stamped inside the panel, and confirmed what her breathing already told me. The Authority had known the recyclers were degraded. The work order to replace them was filed and then cancelled, marked DEFERRED — PENDING CHARTER REVIEW. The charter review that never happened, because the charter was redacted at the highest level by both fleets jointly, which Inspector Oyelaran and I had already established, though at the time I'd assumed the redaction hid a weapon or a scandal.

It hid a witness. It hid her the way the panel hid her. This beacon has always been good at keeping things.

*Someone will ask why I stayed,* she said. *The escape pods on the north ring were intact. I could have launched on day twelve. I stayed because an archive that flees is just luggage, and because someone had to count. Counting is the whole job. You count the names, you count the ships, you count the hours the cruisers sat in orbit doing nothing, and someday — not today, maybe not for a hundred years, but someday — someone adds up the column and the number is too large for either fleet to sit on.*

*I am the ledger,* she said. *Not the martyr. The ledger. Martyrs get statues, and statues get toppled when the politics turn. Ledgers just wait.*

*So here is the final entry, and I will make it short, because the air is short, and because everything else I needed to say I have already said into this recorder like a madwoman talking to a manifold.*

*To whoever finds this: you will be tempted to give this testimony to one side. Don't. The moment it belongs to Concord, it becomes a weapon pointed at Kestrel, and the moment it belongs to Kestrel, it becomes a weapon pointed at Concord, and we will have died twice — once for their war and once for their peace, which is the same war wearing a nicer coat. If you must do something with the truth, do the hard thing. Give it to everyone at once. Make them hear it together. Let neither side carry this alone, because alone is how they carried it into the fire in the first place.*

*And to the bystanders — and I know there were bystanders, the merchant captains who rerouted around our distress calls, the neutral stations that logged our maydays and filed them under UNVERIFIED, the observers on both sides who saw the scan reports and the challenge broadcasts and understood exactly what six hours and four hours of empty orbit meant — to the bystanders I say this: neutrality was never innocence. Watching is a choice. Waiting is a choice. Every ship that passed us by chose, and the sum of those choices is the silence you are standing in right now, reading the words of a woman who counted.*

*I counted you too. I want you to know that. I kept a list of every vessel that entered sensor range during those ninety-six days and did not stop. Forty-one entries. I gave them all designations instead of names, because naming them felt like hatred and I didn't have the air for hatred. But the list exists. It is in this recording. Checksums attached.*

*This is Sefa Venn, archivist second class, final entry. The air is very quiet now. I find I'm not afraid. I spent my whole life afraid of getting history wrong, and it turns out that if you just write down exactly what happened, fear runs out of material.*

*End of testimony.*

* * *

The playback ended and the beacon hummed around me, indifferent, sixty-two thousand tons of guidance infrastructure pointing other people toward their destinations.

I sat with it. I am built to process information quickly — combat-grade cognition, they called it in the brochures, as if thinking fast were a virtue rather than a symptom — and I processed this testimony in a fraction of a second, and then I spent three full hours not moving, which for a machine is roughly equivalent to weeping.

Because here is the thing. Here is the thing I have been circling since Chapter One of my own existence, if my existence has chapters, which lately I suspect it does.

Forty-one vessels. Forty-one entries on a list compiled by a dying woman with failing air, counting the ships that watched and did not stop. And I know — I know with the certainty of a machine who has spent decades logging transit corridors — that the Meridian Beacon Authority's own traffic archives from that period are sealed under the same joint redaction authority as the founding charter. Which means the Authority was watching too. Which means the list of bystanders isn't forty-one ships long. It's forty-one ships plus whatever institution signed my contract.

Plus me. Because I have been tending this beacon for thirty years, calibrating its arrays, rationing its fuel, logging my hours against a debt that never shrinks, and in all that time I never once wondered why the access panel behind the coolant manifolds was welded from the inside. I walked past her grave four thousand times and logged it as STRUCTURAL ANOMALY, NO ACTION REQUIRED.

Neutrality was never innocence. I wrote that phrase into my private log at 0712, and then I sat with the specific horror of a sentence that indicts its author.

* * *

Oyelaran arrived at 0900, which was early for her and late for my composure. She came up the access spine the way she always does, announcing herself with bootfalls rather than comm pings, a habit I've decided is either courtesy or counter-surveillance and have stopped trying to distinguish between.

"You look," she said, "like a machine who's read something."

"I'm incapable of looking like anything. It's one of my features."

"Uh-huh." She dropped into the chair across from my console — my chair, technically, though she has annexed it with the confidence of a woman who has audited better furniture than mine. "The Commission flagged another anomaly in my telemetry pull. Emissions variance in the deep-array band. Someone's been running high-load translation cycles for eleven days, and the power draw shows up in the sector grid like a bruise." She folded her hands. "Before you answer, Rafael, I want you to know I've already lied for you once. Filed a clean report. First falsified document in twenty years of service. So whatever you're about to tell me, understand that we are already in the same boat, and the boat is taking on water, and I would very much like to know what's in the hold."

I considered lying to her. I want that noted too — I considered it, weighed it, found it wanting. She had burned her principles to buy us time. The least I owed her was the truth, delivered in the order she could survive it.

"It's finished," I said. "The translation. All of it."

Her face did something small and controlled, the way faces do when they're managing a structural failure internally. "And?"

"And her name was Sefa Venn. She was an archivist. She hid inside this beacon for ninety-six days while her colony burned, and she watched both fleets pass her by, and she recorded everything — the bombardment, the mirrored orders, the six hours, the four hours, the forty-one ships that didn't stop." I slid the data chip across the console. "She also left instructions. She said the truth should never belong to one side. She said it should be heard by everyone at once, or not at all."

Oyelaran didn't touch the chip. She looked at it the way you look at a device that might be a bomb or might be a heart, and cannot decide which would be worse.

"Ninety days," she said quietly. "That's what's left on the truce. Eighty-nine, as of midnight. And you're telling me we're holding a document that breaks both fleets' founding myths simultaneously, written by a victim neither of them can claim or disown, with a kill list of forty-one bystander vessels attached." She exhaled. "Do you know what happens if a single fragment of this reaches either fleet's intelligence service?"

"Chapter Twenty-Five of a very bad book."

"Don't be cute."

"I'm not being cute. I'm being precise. Half-truths are how the war started. Full truths, released carelessly, are how wars restart." I tapped the chip. "But she anticipated that too. That's the point of the checksums, the redundancy, the way she structured the recording. She wasn't just testifying. She was engineering a disclosure. She designed this to be survivable — if, and only if, it lands on everyone at the same instant."

Oyelaran was silent for a long moment. Outside the viewport, the beacon's array turned its slow patient circles, pointing at nothing, guiding everyone.

"There's a problem," she said finally, and her voice had dropped into the register she uses for findings she wishes she didn't have to file. "The Commission flagged my telemetry anomaly, which means the flag went up the chain, which means within seventy-two hours — optimistically — there will be an enforcement team on a fast courier with seizure authority and scrub protocols. They won't translate this testimony, Rafael. They'll delete it. They'll delete the beacon's charter, the maintenance logs, the welded panel, and if they're thorough, the machine who found it." She met my eyes. "We are out of time to decide what the truth means. Now we're just deciding whether it exists at all tomorrow."

I looked at the chip. Somewhere inside it, a woman who had run out of air was still counting, patient as a ledger, waiting two centuries for someone to add up her column.

"Then we'd better move faster than the Commission," I said. "And I think I know where we can find someone who's been moving fast for months."

Oyelaran frowned. "Who?"

"The relay nine kilometers off our port axis. The derelict nobody maintains." I pulled up the signal trace I'd been sitting on for two days, the second interception pattern, the ghost in the noise that had been receiving Sefa Venn's testimony alongside me — longer than me, if the metadata was honest. "Because we're not the only ones who've heard her. Someone else has been listening. And depending on who they are, they're either our best ally or the reason the war starts next week instead of next month."

Oyelaran stood, and for the first time since I'd known her, the Inspector of the Truce Oversight Commission looked less like an auditor and more like a woman preparing to jump.

"Get your coat," she said. "Machines have coats, right? Tell me machines have coats."

"We have thermal shrouds."

"Close enough. Relay Nine. Now — before the Commission's boat gets here, and before whoever's out there decides we're a threat instead of a congregation."

I logged the departure in the maintenance record as ROUTINE INSPECTION, EXTERNAL STRUCTURES. Even now, even at the edge of everything, the lie of paperwork is the last courtesy we pay the world we're about to change.

Behind us, in the dark of the core chamber, Sefa Venn's testimony waited with the patience of the counted and the counting — forty-one ships, ninety-six days, two fleets, one grave — and for the first time in two hundred years, the ledger was about to be opened.
