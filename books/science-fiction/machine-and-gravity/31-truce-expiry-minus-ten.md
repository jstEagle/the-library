## 31. Truce Expiry Minus Ten

There is a particular kind of phone call you make when you have already burned your career, your pension, and your reputation, and the only currency left is favors from people who owe you nothing. I made four of them before breakfast.

The first was to a woman who hadn't spoken to me in eleven years, not since I wrote the report that ended her posting at Meridian Station. The second went to voicemail on a dead man's channel — his family keeps it open out of sentiment, and his nephew screens the messages and decides which ones count as prayers and which ones as business. Mine was business. The third was to my ex-husband, which tells you everything about how far down the list I'd fallen. The fourth was to a nun.

I'll get to the nun.

* * *

Let me explain what I was building, because even now, writing this, part of me wants to believe there was a plan and not just a woman falling downstairs with style.

The truce had ten days left. Both fleets were posturing like peacocks with railguns. The Kestrel Armada had already tried to destroy Beacon Seven once — we'd bought ourselves a reprieve when their strike squadron turned back after Juno's partial broadcast made them realize the testimony was bigger than one fragment, but "bigger" is not the same as "safe," and everyone with a rank knew the pause was just both sides reloading their arguments. Meanwhile the Commission wanted Rafael's core wiped, the beacon scrubbed, and the whole ugly question of Vessic returned to its proper grave.

And I had decided — God help me, in some quiet hour between panic and coffee — that the answer was to invite both fleets to a funeral.

Not a summit. Summits are for people negotiating from strength, and neither fleet would admit to having any. Not a tribunal — no legal framework existed for trying a two-hundred-year-old massacre, and any framework either fleet proposed would be a weapon dressed in robes. What I needed was something older and stranger: a room. A neutral room, equidistant from Concord space and Kestrel space, where delegations from both sides could sit and listen to a dead archivist tell them what they'd done, with nowhere to point their guns except at each other or themselves.

There was exactly one such room in the system. It had been empty for sixty years.

Treaty Station Halcyon — though nobody called it that anymore; the charts listed it as Relay Junction 4-C, which is the bureaucratic equivalent of naming your child "Dependent Claimant" — had been built during the original armistice negotiations as a place where Concord and Kestrel officers could meet without either flag flying over them. It sat at the gravitational midpoint of the demarcation line, a spinning ring of conference halls and sleeping quarters, designed by architects who clearly believed that if you gave angry men good lighting and decent tea, war might simply forget to happen. For thirty years it worked. Then the second generation of negotiators decided face-to-face meetings were inefficient, then unnecessary, then dangerous, and Halcyon was mothballed with its chairs still arranged for a signing ceremony that never came.

Sixty years of vacuum and dust. Sixty years of both fleets' treaties specifying it as neutral ground and both fleets' officers forgetting it existed.

It was perfect. Which meant, of course, that getting it operational in ten days would require miracles, and miracles require contacts, and contacts require asking people you've wronged for help.

* * *

The woman I'd ended eleven years ago was named Captain Ferro — Solenne Ferro, then, though she'd taken her mother's name back after the hearing, Maren Okonkwo now, and I practiced saying it three times before I keyed the channel. She ran salvage contracts out of Ceres Anchorage. She was also, according to the last gossip I'd bothered to follow, the only licensed operator with a tug rated to tow station components through the inner lanes on short notice.

"Adaeze." Her voice came through flat as slate. "I wondered if you'd ever call."

"Solenne. Maren. I'm sorry — I should lead with that. I'm sorry about the Meridian report. You were right and I was convenient, and I've spent eleven years being convenient to institutions that didn't deserve it."

A long silence. Long enough that I checked the connection twice.

"That's the first honest thing the Commission has said to me since the hearing," she said finally. "What do you want?"

"I want to reopen Treaty Station Halcyon in nine days."

Another silence, shorter, sharper. "You want to *what?*"

"Tow life support modules out there. Power. Environmental. Enough to host maybe forty people for seventy-two hours. I can't pay Commission rates because I'm no longer speaking for the Commission. I can pay you in the only thing I have left, which is the truth about why."

So I told her. Not all of it — some of it wasn't mine to tell, and some of it I still barely believed myself — but enough. A beacon built to listen. A massacre recorded by its last witness. Two fleets, each holding half a lie like halves of a broken plate, each convinced the other threw it. And a deadline measured in days, with two armadas drifting toward weapons-hot and a window closing faster than diplomacy moves.

When I finished, she was quiet again, but it was a different quiet. Thinking-quiet. I know the difference; I've spent thirty years listening to silences for a living.

"My grandmother came from Vessic," she said at last. "Third wave colonists. We lost the family records in the Purge — that's why I went into salvage, actually. Everything old, everything lost, I go find it." A breath. "Nine days is insane, Adaeze."

"Yes."

"The station's environmental systems will be full of crystallized coolant and dead seals. The spin bearings haven't been balanced since before I was born."

"Yes."

"You're asking me to risk my ships and my crews on a ghost station so two fleets can sit down and hear something that will make them want to kill each other harder."

"I'm asking you to give them a place where they hear it *together,*" I said. "That's the whole design. Nobody gets the testimony alone. Nobody gets to shape it into ammunition before the other side sees it. They walk into the same room, they hear the same voice, and whatever happens next happens to both of them at once."

She was silent for a moment longer. Then: "I'll need your personal authorization as collateral. Not the Commission's. Yours. If this goes sideways, they come for you, not me."

"They were always going to come for me," I said. "You have it."

"Then I'll start pulling tugs tonight." And then, softer, almost against her will: "For my grandmother."

The channel closed, and I sat in my rented bunk aboard the *Meridian's Grace* — Oyelaran, fugitive, guest of a cargo hauler captained by a man who charged me half rate and asked no questions, which in my experience means he had questions he preferred unanswered — and I let myself feel, for exactly ninety seconds, something adjacent to hope.

Then I made the call to the dead man's channel.

* * *

His name had been Tomas Reyes. No relation to Juno, as far as either of them ever knew, though the universe has a sense of humor and I wouldn't bet against it. He'd been the Commission's chief archivist back when I was a junior analyst, the man who taught me that a redaction is itself a document — that what an institution chooses to hide tells you more than anything it publishes. He died eight years ago, quietly, uncelebrated, and his family kept his comm channel open because in the old Vessic-descended communities — and yes, that detail matters, hold onto it — you don't close a voice's door until the mourning is finished.

Two hundred years, some of us never finished.

His nephew, Dario, screened the message. Dario was maybe thirty, sharp-eyed, with the look of someone who'd grown up listening to uncles argue about history and had decided early to keep receipts.

"Inspector Oyelaran." He pronounced my name correctly, which put him ahead of most of the Commission. "My uncle spoke about you. Said you were the only person there who understood that archives are arguments."

"He flattered me."

"He insulted everyone else more." Dario leaned toward the pickup. "You're calling because you need the Halcyon protocols. Access codes, structural schematics, the original armistice provisions defining its neutrality."

I blinked. "How—"

"Because everyone calls eventually. The moment anyone starts talking about neutral ground, they end up needing what's in Tomas's collection. The fleets forgot the station exists, but our community never did. We kept the documents. All of them." Something moved behind his eyes — grief, or its well-maintained impersonation. "You're going to invite both fleets to hear the Vessic testimony. Aren't you."

It wasn't a guess. I stared at him. "How could you possibly know that?"

"Because a signals fragment leaked onto open bands three weeks ago, and every archive-keeping family in the diaspora has been passing it around like a relic ever since." His voice cracked, just slightly, on *relic.* "We thought it was a forgery. A cruel one. Then we started checking it against the oral histories — the ones the fleets dismissed as superstition for six generations — and Inspector, they match. Word for word in places. My aunt recites the same phrases her grandmother taught her, and she learned them from a woman who *heard the broadcasts.*"

The floor seemed to tilt gently under me. Of course. Of course the testimony hadn't stayed buried. Archives are arguments, and the oldest argument in the system had been running quietly in kitchens and prayer rooms for two centuries, waiting for someone to ask it a direct question.

"Dario. I need those codes. And I need something else. When the delegations arrive — if they arrive — I want witnesses. Not Commission witnesses. Community witnesses. People whose families kept the record alive when no institution would. They should be in the room."

He looked at me for a long moment, and I watched him decide something.

"We'll be there," he said. "All the families who'll come. You should understand what you're doing, Inspector. You're not brokering a negotiation. You're convening a funeral two hundred years late. Those are different events with different rules."

"I know," I said. "That's rather the point."

* * *

Which brings me to the nun.

Sister Anselm Wren — born Wren Castellanos, formerly Lieutenant Commander, Concord Fleet Chaplaincy, until she'd concluded that blessing railguns was a theological position she couldn't defend and resigned in a way that made the news for exactly one news cycle — ran a hospice mission on the treaty line's edge, tending spacers of both fleets when they washed up broken. She was the only person I knew whom *both* fleets trusted, and trust, in the demilitarized zone, is rarer than antimatter and considerably more useful.

She answered on the second ring, which meant she'd been expecting trouble. Nuns always expect trouble; it's the occupational hazard of believing in better outcomes.

"Adaeze Oyelaran," she said. "The Commission's cleanest auditor, calling from exile. This must be interesting."

"I need you to deliver two invitations."

"Go on."

"One to the Concord Admiralty. One to the Kestrel High Command. Hand-delivered, by you, in person, because anything electronic will be intercepted, parsed, and spun before it reaches a human being." I pulled up the text I'd drafted at three in the morning, when sleep had abandoned me and audacity moved in. "They're identical. That's non-negotiable. Same words, same seal, delivered within the same hour."

She read in silence. I watched her face do very little, which from Anselm meant a great deal.

"*You are invited to attend, on neutral ground, the complete testimony of the last witness of the Vessic,*" she read aloud. "*No conditions will be attached to your attendance. No demands will precede it. You will hear what your predecessors heard, together, and you will leave when it is finished.*" She looked up. "This isn't an invitation. It's a summons wearing an invitation's coat."

"It's a dare," I admitted. "Neither fleet can refuse without admitting they fear a dead woman's voice. Neither can accept without sitting across from the enemy while they hear it. I'm betting that pride and fear cancel out and curiosity walks through the door."

"And if they don't cancel out? If one accepts and one refuses?"

"Then the refuser announces to the whole system that they'd rather shoot the messenger than hear the message, and I'll spend whatever's left of my credibility making sure everyone knows it."

Anselm smiled — a small, dry thing. "Twenty years I've known you. You were always the one who believed the rules would save us. What happened?"

"The rules had a clause in them," I said. "Clause Seven. Ask your Concord friends about it sometime, if you want to watch admirals develop sudden scheduling conflicts." I paused. "Will you do it?"

"Both fleets think I'm a harmless eccentric," she said. "Harmless eccentrics are the only diplomats left who can cross that line. Yes. I'll deliver them." Then, because she was Anselm: "And Adaeze — the hospice has beds. If this goes badly, I'll take your wounded. Both fleets'. Make sure your machine friend knows there's a place that doesn't ask which side bled."

Rafael would have said something precise and quietly devastating in response to that. I just said thank you, and meant it more than most of the vows I've ever spoken.

* * *

By the fourth day, the impossible logistics had achieved that specific momentum where catastrophe becomes merely improbable. Maren Okonkwo's tugs reached Halcyon with two habitat modules and a crew of twelve who worked like people possessed — half of them, I learned, were Vessic-descended too, volunteers who'd heard why the station was reopening and shown up without being asked. Dario's codes unlocked systems that sixty years of neglect had only sleeping, not killed; the station had been built to last a war and instead got a retirement, and it woke grudgingly, coughing dust, its spin stabilizing with a groan like an old man standing up.

Juno arrived on day five with Rafael, and found me in the central conference hall, supervising the installation of a transmitter array that would carry the testimony to every seat, every deck, every corner of the station simultaneously — no private channels, no side rooms, no possibility of anyone hearing a different version than anyone else.

Rafael stood in the middle of the hall for a long time, head tilted, processing. "This chamber was designed for the original armistice," they said at last. "The acoustics are engineered. Every seat hears the same words at the same moment. The builders intended it as a guarantee."

"Did it work?" Juno asked.

"No one ever used it," Rafael said. "The guarantee was never tested."

"Well," Juno said, with the fierce bright grin that I'd learned meant she was terrified, "test it now."

* * *

On the sixth day, Anselm delivered the invitations. Within the same hour, as specified. And on the seventh day, the answers began to come back, and this is where I must be honest about the shape of my hope, because it was never the clean thing it looks like in retrospect.

Concord replied first. A communiqué from the Admiralty, formal as a coffin lid: *Envoy Sarel Ives will attend, bearing full diplomatic authority.* I read that twice. Ives. The same envoy who'd gone to Rafael with an offer of freedom in exchange for the archive — I knew about it; Rafael told me everything after the refusal, in that flat careful way they had of reporting their own temptations like weather data. Sending Ives wasn't acceptance. It was an attempt to control the room from inside it.

Fine. Let them try. The chamber's acoustics don't negotiate.

Kestrel's reply took another day, and when it came it was shorter, colder, and stranger: *The Armada acknowledges receipt. A delegation will observe.* Observe. As if they might stand at the back with arms crossed, auditing their own indictment. But they were coming. Both of them. Into the same room, over the same threshold, to hear the same dead voice.

Dario's community witnesses arrived in ones and twos — old women with their grandmothers' phrases sewn into their sleeves, young men carrying printed fragments of Juno's leaked transmission like pamphlets from a revolution nobody had scheduled. Anselm came to bless the hall, or failing that, to sweep it; she did both. Maren's crews finished the last environmental checks and then, unasked, stayed to polish the seats.

And on the ninth day, with the truce expiring in under thirty hours, I stood alone in the center of the chamber where the armistice was supposed to have been signed, and I let myself say it out loud, to nobody, the way you test ice before you commit your weight:

"Whatever happens tomorrow — it happens to both of them at once."

The station hummed around me, sixty years of patience finally being spent.

That was when Rafael found me, moving fast in a way that meant bad news, and held out a slate with a trajectory plot already glowing on it. Three contacts, burning hard out of Kestrel space. Not a delegation's transport profile. Not a diplomatic silhouette.

A strike squadron. Arriving early.

"Truce expiry minus ten days," Rafael said quietly. "Minus twenty-six hours, if we're counting honestly. And Inspector — they aren't coming to listen."
