## 2. The Dreaming Children

Tamsin Lockridge woke before the alarm, as she always did, to the sound of the Kestrel's Wake breathing around her: the tick of cooling conduits, the hum of the scrubbers, Okafor's snore rolling through the bulkhead like distant surf. For three seconds she lay in the dark of her bunk and let herself believe it was an ordinary morning.

Then she remembered the customs officer's face, and the three seconds were gone.

She dressed in the grey half-light and climbed to the galley, where the smell of synth-café and burnt toast meant Devi was already up. Devi cooked the way she did everything—loudly, generously, with more heat than precision. She slid a plate across the counter without looking up from her tablet.

"You were quiet on the run home," she said. "Quieter than usual, which for you is a medical condition."

"Officer Vey held my case open for eleven minutes." Tamsin wrapped her hands around the café cup. The warmth felt borrowed. "Eleven minutes, Devi. Standard hold is ninety seconds."

"And she found nothing."

"She found nothing." Tamsin turned the cup a quarter-turn, then another. "But she *looked*. Nobody looks anymore. Customs is a formality—a stamp, a yawn. This one read the manifest twice. She asked me about the beacon maintenance contract like she actually cared about the answer."

Devi finally looked up. She had the kind of face that made skepticism look like affection. "So don't take the next run. Let the beacon sit a cycle or two. Riko can fly the milk-run."

"The beacon doesn't sit." It came out sharper than Tamsin intended, and she saw Devi register that—the small flinch, quickly smoothed over. Tamsin softened her voice. "It needs hands. Mine. You know that."

"I know you've never told us why."

The galley hummed. Somewhere below, Riko was arguing with the water recycler, which was winning. Tamsin drank her café and said nothing, because there was nothing true she could say that wasn't everything, and everything would break something she couldn't fix.

Ordinary life, she had learned, was a load-bearing wall. You didn't tap it to see if it was hollow.

* * *

Idris Okonkwo-Vey—no relation to anyone important, her mother always said, laughing a beat too fast—counted crates by feel before the sun cleared the ridge line. Ten years old and already faster at tally than half the dock crew, because her fingers knew the difference between a crate of protein stock and a crate of something the manifest didn't name, and her mouth knew better than to say so.

Her father worked the heavy lifters. Her mother kept the ledgers for Berth Nine. Idris herself had the morning shift on the tally board and the whole afternoon free, which was how she'd come to know every roof, culvert, and climbable antenna mast in Halcyon Reach.

That morning, though, she almost fell off the tally platform. Not from climbing—from yawning. A huge, bone-deep yawn that made her eyes water, and behind it, the tail end of the dream again, dissolving even as she grabbed for it:

*A red number, counting down. Above it, a sun with no light in it, black as a hole punched in paper. And a feeling underneath, vast and patient, like something enormous waiting to be allowed to move.*

"Grim night?" asked Old Sefa, who ran the tally office and believed children ran on sugar and gossip.

"Just tired," Idris said, and wrote a seven that should have been a nine, and caught it, and hated that her hand wasn't steady.

At midday break she sat on the seawall with Tam and Junie from Berth Eleven, and Junie said, around a mouthful of flatbread, "Did either of you dream about the number?"

Idris went very still.

"The red number," Junie pressed. "Counting down. And the black sun over it."

Tam laughed, but it came out wrong, too high. "I dreamed about flying a kite."

"I didn't ask what you dreamed," Junie said, with the terrible precision of eight-year-olds. "I asked if you dreamed about *the number*."

Idris looked out at the harbor, at the fishing skiffs and the freight cranes and the ordinary, glittering water, and heard herself say: "It was at four hundred thousand and something. Yesterday. Today it was lower."

Junie nodded slowly, unsurprised, and that was the worst part—not that they'd shared the dream, but that neither of them seemed to find it strange anymore. As if some part of them had already agreed, silently, weeks ago, not to make a fuss.

"How do we all have it?" Tam whispered.

Nobody answered. Down the seawall, other children were sitting in the same hunched clusters, talking low, glancing at the sky between sentences. Idris counted six of them before she stopped counting, because counting made it real.

Above the harbor, past the thin blue ceiling of atmosphere, something was ticking down. She couldn't have said how she knew that. She just did, the way you know your own heartbeat.

* * *

Doctor Amara Sesay's clinic smelled of antiseptic and old paper, and Tamsin sat in it feeling like contraband, which after eleven years of practice she usually managed not to feel.

Amara closed the door. That was the first sign. The second was that she didn't offer tea.

"Tamsin. How many memory chips passed through your hands last quarter?"

"That's not a question a doctor asks."

"That's not an answer a friend gives." Amara sat down across from her, elbows on knees, and Tamsin saw that under the clinical calm the woman was frightened. Amara never got frightened. Amara had once set Tamsin's broken wrist during a hull breach with steady hands and a running commentary on local politics. "I've seen nineteen children this week. Nineteen. Same presentation: disturbed sleep, daytime fatigue, and when I get them talking—an identical dream. Red countdown. Black sun. I've written it up as mass psychogenic illness, but I don't believe my own report, and I'm asking you because you're the only person on this rock who moves memories for a living."

"Leaking," Tamsin said slowly. "You think a chip cracked in transit and it's bleeding into... what? Into kids' heads?"

"I think you smuggle pre-Collapse archives through a customs net that hasn't been properly audited since the war. I think somebody's been playing old recordings on old hardware, and old hardware does strange things." Amara's eyes didn't waver. "I think you would know if anyone on this colony was broadcasting something they shouldn't be."

And there it was—the question under the question—and Tamsin felt the load-bearing wall shiver under her palm.

Because she *did* know. She was the only one who knew. Every month she climbed the beacon's spine with her toolkit and her silence, replaced what needed replacing, logged what needed logging, and never once—not in eleven years—had she decoded the transmission itself. Her mentor, Emeric Vance, dead now these six years, had left her two instructions carved into the maintenance protocol like commandments: *Keep it running. Don't ask what it says.*

She'd thought it was shame. Some old smuggler's embarrassment, a pirate frequency, a private grief. She'd built a whole tenderness around not asking, the way you build a life around a locked room.

But children were dreaming a countdown, and Amara was looking at her with fear, and the locked room had started humming.

"If something were leaking," Tamsin said carefully, "from a transmitter nobody questions, on a schedule nobody audits—it wouldn't show up in adults first. Adults filter. We've got too much noise in our heads already." She was thinking aloud now, and hating it. "Kids are quieter. Cleaner receivers."

Amara went pale in the way of a woman doing arithmetic she doesn't want finished. "You're telling me this might be real. Not contamination. *Signal.*"

"I'm telling you I need to check something before I tell you anything." Tamsin stood. Her legs felt far away. "Give me two days. If I'm wrong, you'll never know I wasted them."

"And if you're right?"

Tamsin reached the door and paused, hand on the frame, and for a moment she was ten years old again herself, standing outside a locked room, listening to it hum.

"Then the war everyone says is over," she said quietly, "may not be as over as we think."

Behind her, Amara said nothing at all, which was worse than any argument.

* * *

Idris climbed the antenna mast behind the desalination plant at dusk, higher than any sensible child, because from up there the sky stopped being a ceiling and became a place. She could see the orbital traffic lights crawling their slow lanes. She could see, if she squirmed and squinted, the faint fixed star that the grown-ups called the Beacon and never looked at twice.

She looked at it twice. She looked at it every night now.

*Three hundred ninety-one thousand,* hummed the dream-knowledge in her chest, patient as tide. *Three hundred ninety thousand.*

Below her, the colony lit its windows one by one. Her mother calling her in for dinner, three notes, familiar as her own name. The smell of frying onions. Ordinary life, spread out warm and small beneath her, all of it balanced on a number none of the grown-ups could see.

Idris pressed her palm flat against the cold metal of the mast, as if she could feel the signal through it, and made a promise to the black sun: *I'll count with you. And when the grown-ups finally ask us what we know, we'll tell them everything.*

Far above, the beacon pulsed once against the dark—steady, faithful, and lying.
