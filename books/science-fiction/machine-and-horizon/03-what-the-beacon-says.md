## 3. What the Beacon Says

The beacon's signal had a texture Tamsin knew better than her own heartbeat.

She'd been climbing the switchback path to the ridge station since she was nineteen, when Hollis Vane had first pressed the maintenance key into her palm and said, *You'll know what to do when it matters.* Eleven years of dawn ascents through the frost-fern meadows above Halcyon Reach, eleven years of checking coolant lines and antenna tension and the slow, patient pulse of a transmitter that never once, in all that time, had asked anything of her but attention.

Tonight it asked more.

She'd pulled the raw transmission dump at 0300, after the third night of children's dreams rippling through the colony like wind through wheat. The doctor's question still sat in her chest—*could contraband memories be leaking?*—and Tamsin had come up the mountain half to disprove it, half because she couldn't bear not to know. The Kestrel's Wake was docked and dark below; Ryn had offered to come, Okafor too, but this was the one thing she did alone. Had always done alone. The loneliness of it had a shape she'd grown fond of, the way you grow fond of an old scar.

Now she sat in the beacon's cramped analysis room with the signal spread across three screens, and the shape of it was wrong.

Not broken. Wrong the way a forged signature is wrong—the strokes confident, the hand unfamiliar.

"Okay," she murmured to the empty room, to the hum of the reactor two floors down, to Hollis wherever he was now, dead four years and still somehow the most present man on this moon. "Show me."

* * *

Decoding was smuggling work turned inside out. On the Kestrel's Wake, Tamsin built false memories: taking a real recollection—a wedding, a funeral, a child's first steps—and wrapping it in synthetic context so thoroughly that orbital customs' truth-sieves found nothing but sincerity. The best forgeries were ninety percent true. That was the whole art. You didn't hide the lie; you buried it under so much honesty that digging for it felt like vandalism.

So she recognized the technique in the beacon's carrier wave before she understood its content.

The signal was layered. Surface layer: standard colonial time-sync telemetry, boring as gravel, the kind of traffic no fleet analyst would ever flag. Beneath that, encoded in the timing jitter between pulses—elegant, old-school, pre-war tradecraft—sat a second stream. And beneath *that*, compressed into the checksums themselves where only someone who knew to look would ever look, a third.

Tamsin's hands were steady. She noticed that about herself with distant surprise. Her mouth had gone dry and her heart was doing something arrhythmic and frightened, but her hands moved through the decryption sequence like they were braiding hair.

The second layer resolved first.

**PRIORITY BROADCAST — CONCORD HIGH COMMAND / MERIDIAN FLEET COUNCIL — JOINT COMMUNIQUÉ**

**ARMISTICE OF THE THRESHOLD — RATIFIED [DATE] — ALL HOSTILITIES CEASED — ALL WEAPONS STATUS: COLD — VERIFICATION CHANNEL OPEN PERMANENTLY**

She read it three times. The date was thirty-one years old. The war had ended thirty-one years ago—or rather, the war had *ended*, officially, in every history file on Halcyon Reach, in the school curricula Idris Okonkwo and ten thousand other children memorized, in the memorial plaques along the harbor promenade listing the dead of a conflict concluded by treaty.

Tamsin had grown up on those histories. Everyone had. The Armistice of the Threshold was as settled as gravity.

Except.

Except that both fleets were still out there. She'd seen them—everyone on the night side saw them on clear evenings, two faint constellations of engine-glow parked in high orbit like dogs circling a bone, close enough to threaten, far enough to pretend otherwise. For thirty-one years. Fleets don't hold station for three decades over a finished war. Fleets go home.

Unless they've never been told it's finished.

"No," she said aloud, and her voice cracked on it. "No, that's not—" She stopped, because the third layer was decrypting, and the third layer was signed.

*H.V.*

Hollis Vane. Her mentor. The man who'd taught her to splice memory-thread without scarring it, who'd fed her burnt coffee and terrible poetry, who'd carried her out of a customs raid on Meridian Station when she was twenty-two and too green to run. Who had built this beacon—she'd always known that much, had assumed he'd built it as a navigation aid, a lighthouse, one honest machine keeping one small colony safe.

The third layer wasn't a broadcast. It was logs. His logs. Personal, unencrypted to anyone holding his cipher—which meant, she realized with a cold slide of understanding, unencrypted to whoever inherited his key. To her. He'd left them for her the way you leave a letter you can't bring yourself to send.

She read until the sky outside the observation port went from black to bruise.

* * *

*Day 12 of the ceasefire talks,* the first entry began. *Both delegations are ninety percent agreed. Ninety percent is the most dangerous number there is.*

*I've spent my life around smugglers and liars, and I know what a nearly-finished thing looks like. The Concord delegation wants reparations language the Meridians will never sign. The Meridians want a war-crimes tribunal the Concord will burn their own ships before accepting. Both sides are one incident away from walking out, and everyone in this room knows it, and nobody says it.*

*Today a Concord patrol fired on a Meridian courier. "Misidentification," they say. The courier was carrying peace delegates.*

*It's going to happen again. It's going to keep happening until someone fires something bigger than a courier, and then it won't matter what anyone signs, because the fleets are primed and frightened and thirty seconds from mutual annihilation, and fear doesn't read treaties.*

*So I'm going to do the worst thing I have ever done, and maybe the best.*

Tamsin set the reader down. Her hands had stopped being steady.

Through the port, the two fleets hung in the upper dark, faint as breath on glass. She thought about the children. She thought about red numbers counting down beneath a black sun, dreamed nightly by dockworkers' daughters and harbor brats and the doctor's own nephew, identical across a hundred sleeping minds, and she understood—with the particular horror of a forger recognizing masterwork—forgeries, that the dreams were the beacon's raw voice leaking through. The true signal underneath the lie. Children slept light enough, dreamt open enough, to hear what adults had been trained not to hear.

She picked the reader back up.

*They'll call it treason if they ever find out. Let them. Here is what I know that the admirals don't: peace isn't a document. Peace is a held breath. Someone has to keep holding it until both sides remember how to breathe on their own.*

*The armistice I'm broadcasting is false. Every word of it. There was no treaty. There was no ratification. There was a room full of exhausted people who almost managed to save themselves from each other, and then a courier died, and the almost died with it.*

*But if both fleets believe the other has stood down—if the verification channel shows cold weapons on both sides, which I can fake, which I am faking right now—then neither side dares fire first. Not out of goodness. Out of arithmetic. And arithmetic buys time, and time is the only material peace has ever been made of.*

*Thirty years. Give them thirty years and maybe the generation that remembers why they hate each other dies off, and the next one negotiates like human beings instead of survivors.*

*I'm telling no one. A lie this size needs to be carried alone or not at all. If you're reading this, then either I'm dead or the reactor's dying, and either way the breath is about to leave my hands.*

*I'm sorry. I'm so sorry. I'd do it again.*

*—Hollis*

* * *

Tamsin sat in the grey light of morning and let the betrayal move through her the way weather moves through a valley—arriving, breaking, passing.

He had lied. Not to her—to everyone, to two civilizations, to the ten thousand children of Halcyon Reach who learned in school about a treaty that existed nowhere but in a transmitter's patient pulse. He had stolen the ending of a war the way she stole memories, wrapped a forgery in ninety percent truth, and buried it so deep that thirty-one years of digging-for-it had felt like vandalism.

And he had kept the colony alive. Kept *both* colonies alive—Halcyon Reach and the Meridian mining settlements on the far side, all of them living under the same fabricated calm, farming and birthing and burying their dead and teaching their children history that never happened, because the alternative was a fireball and everyone, everywhere, knowing exactly what had really happened for the last four minutes of their lives.

Her eyes stung. She let them.

"You absolute bastard," she whispered, and it came out sounding like *thank you*, because it was.

That was the thing about Hollis she should have expected, had perhaps always known: even his treachery was a kind of shelter. Even his lie had been built like a house, with rooms in it for other people. He hadn't hidden the truth from her—he'd *left* it for her, sealed, dated, waiting for the day the seal broke. Trusting her to find it and understand. Trusting her to decide.

The maintenance key sat heavy in her pocket. *You'll know what to do when it matters.*

She looked at the screens again, and this time she read them the way a smuggler reads, looking for what's missing. The broadcast was intact. The forgery was holding. But the reactor diagnostics in the corner of the telemetry—numbers she checked weekly and had never once worried over—showed output variance creeping upward over the past six months. Small. Fractional. The kind of drift you'd only notice if you were comparing against archived baselines.

Which she was now doing, hands moving again, steady in the way of hands that have decided to be afraid later.

The curve bent the wrong way.

The beacon was dying. Slowly, quietly, on a timescale of months—but dying. And when it stopped, the held breath would stop with it. Two fleets would wake from a thirty-one-year dream into silence, and silence, between armed and frightened powers, had only ever meant one thing.

Outside, far below on the harbor road, a tram rattled past carrying the early shift down to the docks. Somewhere on it, probably, a child who had dreamed the countdown again last night, who would dream it tonight, who was dreaming it *right now* in some sense—because the numbers weren't a nightmare.

They were a readout.

Tamsin gathered her notes, killed the screens, and started down the mountain at a pace just short of running, already composing the lie she'd tell her crew, and hating herself for how easily it came.
