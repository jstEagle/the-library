## 7. The Price of Loyalty

I knew Rhee had sold me before he told me. That's the thing about spending four years on a ship with eleven other people — you learn the acoustics of their guilt the way you learn a language. Rhee's guilt had a specific grammar: he stopped meeting my eyes, started calling me "Voss" instead of "Odile," and reorganized the cargo manifest three times in one watch, which for Rhee was the equivalent of pacing a hole in the deck.

He came to the galley at 0300 station time, when the Long Silence was still clamped to Ceres and the station's spin made the coffee swirl in lazy spirals against the cup walls. He set two cups down. He never brought me coffee. Coffee was a transaction, and transactions meant bad news.

"Consortium agents found the Halcyon's data core," he said. "They're going to find you next. I gave them the coordinates."

I looked at the coffee. It kept swirling. Somewhere in the station's guts, a pump cycled, patient as a heartbeat.

"Okay," I said.

"Okay?" His voice cracked on the second syllable. "That's it? Okay?"

"What do you want me to say, Rhee? That I'm hurt? I've been waiting four years for you to do the math. Eleven lives against one stowaway with a broken head. It's not even a hard equation."

"It was a hard equation." He sat down across from me, and his hands were shaking, which they never did, not even when we'd taken micrometeorite damage through the number three hold and he'd held the patch on with his bare palm for twenty minutes. "I sat in my cabin for six hours doing it. And then I did it again. And every time, the answer came out the same, and every time, the answer made me sick."

"So why—"

"Because they had Juno's sister's address." He said it flat, like reading off a manifest. "They had all our families' addresses. They had the debt liens on this ship, and they were going to call them in, and we'd have been scavenging in the Belt by the end of the month with a Consortium flag on our transponder. I did the math because the math was the only thing standing between my crew and a reeducation facility."

I nodded slowly. It was, I want to be clear, a completely reasonable betrayal. That was what made it unbearable. The Consortium had learned long ago that you don't need to torture information out of people — you just need to show them a ledger.

"When?" I asked.

"Six hours. They're suiting up at the Meridian docking arm. I told them you'd be in the cargo bay, asleep in the ice containers, because I figured—" He stopped. Rubbed his face with both hands. "I figured six hours was enough time for you to be gone. That's the deal I made with myself. I sell you, and you run, and nobody has to watch anybody die."

"Rhee."

"Don't."

"Rhee, look at me." He looked. His eyes were red-rimmed and furious and terrified, and I thought: this man has carried me like contraband for four years, and this is what it cost him. "I can't run. You know that. If I run, they follow the ship, and if they follow the ship, they find the Halcyon's trajectory data I copied, and then everything I've done for seven years was for nothing. The countdown doesn't stop because I'm hiding in a water tank."

"The countdown." He laughed, and there was nothing warm in it. "You and your countdown. Odile, I've spent four years pretending I believe your ghost stories because you're the best ice-hauler I've ever had and because you laugh at my jokes even when they're terrible. I don't know what's in your head. But I know what's in theirs, and it's worse."

"Then don't sell me," I said. "Unsell me."

"That's not a thing."

"Make it a thing. You're Rhee Alonzo, and there is not a docking authority in the Belt that doesn't owe you a favor or fear your cargo manifest. Unsell me."

He stared at me for a long time. Outside the viewport, Ceres turned, its terminator crawling with the lights of stations that had been mining ice before either of us was born. Somewhere on that lit face, agents were checking seals on combat suits.

"You know what the worst part is?" he said finally. "I already decided. Before I came in here. I sat in my cabin doing equations for six hours and the whole time I already knew I was going to throw them out the airlock. The selling was — it was me trying to find a version of cowardice my crew could survive. And there isn't one. There's no version where I hand you over and sleep at night. So." He stood up, and something in his spine reorganized itself, the way it did when he walked into a customs office with a hold full of things that were definitely not water ice. "We unsell you. Get your kit. Wake Juno and Okafor. And Odile — when the shooting starts, you stay behind me. That's not a request. That's the whole point of me."

* * *

The Consortium agents were good. That's what I remember most clearly, afterward — not the fear, not the noise, but the competence. Six of them, moving through the docking arm in pairs, seals hissing, weapons slung low and ready. They'd been told they were picking up one unarmed xenolinguist from a tramp freighter. Nobody had told them about Rhee.

The plan — and I use the word generously — was Okafor's. Okafor had been a station security officer before the Consortium decided his politics were a liability, and he still thought in chokepoints. "They have to come through the cargo lock," he said, pulling up the Long Silence's schematics on the galley table. "It's a two-meter aperture. We control the aperture, we control the fight."

"Or," said Juno, who was nineteen and had grown up running contraband in the Belt and had never once in her life chosen the boring option, "we vent the docking arm."

"We are not venting the docking arm."

"We vent it *partially*—"

"Juno."

I tuned them out. I was in my bunk, pulling the data core — the real one, the copy I'd made of the Halcyon's trajectory files — out of the lining of my mattress, and my hands were steady, and that frightened me more than the agents did. Seven years of running had trained my body to treat catastrophe as weather. Somewhere under the calm, the countdown was ticking, and I could feel it the way you feel a storm coming through your teeth.

The alarm went off at 0547. Not ours — theirs. The agents had gotten tired of waiting.

What happened next happened fast, and I've replayed it so many times that the memories have worn smooth, like stones in a river. Okafor sealing the inner cargo lock. Juno killing the lights. Rhee on the comms, opening a channel to the agents with the same cheerful, gravel-voiced authority he used on customs inspectors: *"Meridian Control to boarding party, be advised, your docking authorization has been revoked, please detach and depart."*

The reply was a breaching charge.

They came through the cargo lock in a storm of flashbangs and hard vacuum protocols, and Okafor met them the way he'd promised he would, and it was ugly, and it was brief. I was supposed to be in the aft compartment with the data core, staying behind Rhee. I made it as far as the galley.

I will not pretend I was brave. I crouched behind a cargo net with my ears ringing and my heart trying to climb out through my throat, and I listened to people I loved fight for my life, and the only thing I could think, absurdly, was that Rhee had brought me coffee and I had never once brought him any, and now I might never get the chance to fix that.

The agents were better armed. We were better positioned. Those two facts ground against each other for four minutes, and then one of the agents — the team lead, a woman with a Consortium crest stenciled on her shoulder — got a clear line on the aft compartment, on me, and raised her weapon.

Rhee stepped into the line of fire.

I screamed something — I don't know what, maybe nothing, maybe a word in the language that lives in my head, the one no human mouth should make. But he didn't need to dodge, because Juno, nineteen years old and never once boring, dumped the number three hold's atmosphere on a manual override, and the decompression alarm screamed, and everyone stumbled, and in that stumbling half-second Rhee put two rounds through the lead agent's shoulder and the fight broke like a wave.

"Seal and burn," Rhee shouted, and the Long Silence tore herself out of Ceres's docking clamps with a shriek I felt through the deck plates, and the station fell away behind us, and for about ninety seconds I let myself believe we'd won.

Then the Consortium cutter came around the terminator with its guns hot.

* * *

We ran. There's no glory in it to describe — a freighter with a hold full of water ice running from a military cutter is not a battle, it's a rabbit with a head start. Rhee flew like a man possessed, threading the Meridian Belt's gravel with the kind of flying that ages you in dog-years, and Okafor and Juno and I braced ourselves against the bulkheads and listened to the hull ring like a bell.

They hit the aft compartment on the third pass. Breached the outer door. The inner seal held, but it was cycling — failing — and someone had to hold the manual lock from inside the compartment, which meant someone had to be inside the compartment, which meant—

"I'll go," I said.

"You absolutely will not," said Okafor.

"You'll go," Rhee said to Okafor, and his voice was terrible and calm, the voice of a man doing the math one last time and hating the answer. "You're the only one who knows the manual override sequence."

Okafor looked at Rhee for a long moment. Then he looked at me, and grinned — Okafor's grin, the one that said *the Consortium can take my pension but not my sense of humor* — and said, "Keep her alive, Captain. She's the only one of us who knows what's coming."

He sealed himself in the aft compartment and held the lock for eleven minutes. Eleven minutes is nothing. Eleven minutes is the time it takes to drink a cup of coffee, to argue about a flight plan, to tell someone a joke. Okafor held a two-meter aperture against a military cutter for eleven minutes with a manual lever and a fire extinguisher, and when the compartment finally blew, he was still holding it.

The last transmission on the ship's log is his voice, and I have it memorized, and I will have it memorized until the day I die: *"Seal's good. Tell my sister I paid off the boat. And Voss — read the damn signal. Read it twice."*

* * *

We burned for the outer system for six hours before Rhee would let anyone sleep. Juno sat in the galley with a blanket around her shoulders, not crying, just staring at the bulkhead, and I sat with her, and neither of us said anything, because there is nothing to say that isn't smaller than the silence.

Rhee came in at the end of the watch. He looked ten years older than he had at 0300. He set two cups of coffee down — his and mine — and drank his standing up, and then he said, "Where are we going, Odile?"

I pulled the data core from my jacket and set it on the table between us. The Halcyon's trajectory. The path back to the thing at the signal's source. The structure, the choice, the door.

"Out," I said. "Past the Belt. To where the Halcyon went."

"And what's out there?"

I thought about the countdown ticking behind my eyes. About Amara Chen, eight years old with thirty years of memories. About a door that only opens for a mind that speaks the language.

"A way to save everyone," I said. "Or a way to lose everything. I won't know until I'm there."

Rhee looked at the data core for a long time. Then he looked at me, and something in his face settled — not peace, exactly, but the thing people find on the far side of peace, when they've already paid the price and decided it was worth it.

"Course plotted," he said. "Hold on to something."

Behind us, Ceres burned in the long light of a sun that was counting down. Ahead, the dark. And in the dark, faint as a heartbeat, the signal was waiting — and for the first time in seven years, it wasn't calling to me.

It was answering.
