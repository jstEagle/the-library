## 1. Contraband in the Blood

The memory weighed nothing and everything.

Lena Galloway had learned, over eleven years of running the Meridian–Vesper lane, that contraband was a matter of mass. A kilo of unlicensed pharmaceuticals had mass. A data chit of council propaganda had mass. Even the little brass tokens Vesper's elders minted for their dead—stamped with names the orbital registries refused to recognize—had mass enough to sing against her hull lining if a customs drone swept close with the right resonance. But a memory, spliced into the wet architecture of her own neural lace, weighed nothing at all. It was just electricity arranged in someone else's shape. And yet it was the only cargo she'd ever carried that made her hands sweat on the yoke.

She didn't look at it. That was the rule. Her rule, self-imposed, load-bearing, the one piece of structure in a life otherwise built from drift. The widow on Meridian's Ring Four had paid half up front—a fortune by Lena's standards, though she'd haggled it down out of some stubborn instinct toward fairness—and pressed her palm to Lena's temple in the back room of a tea house that smelled of burnt chicory and old grief. *He was seven,* the woman had said, as the splice took hold. *His name was Tomas. He fell from the maintenance gantry when the gravity cycled wrong. I want his mother to have him. The grandmother. She's down there. She's all he has left besides me, and I can't go.*

Lena hadn't asked why the widow couldn't go. Nobody went down. That was the whole shape of the world: Meridian above, spinning fat and bright in its long orbit, and Vesper below, sealed under sixty years of silence, two halves of a fractured humanity who had stopped speaking before Lena was born. No traffic. No transmissions. No trade except what moved through people like her—the smugglers, the couriers, the quiet hands who kept a thin thread of human warmth stitched between the colonies when every official channel had been cut.

The thread was dying. She could feel it in the way this run felt routine, in the way the customs sweep patterns had grown lazy, in the way Marisol's payments had started arriving short. Routes were like bodies. They aged. And nobody, as far as Lena could tell, was trying to keep this one alive.

She checked the lace readout for the hundredth time: one package, sealed, integrity green. *Tomas, age seven.* She looked away from the descriptor before it could bloom into anything more. Not her grief. Never hers.

* * *

The descent window opened at 0400 station time, which meant nothing on a ship that lived between worlds, but Lena kept station time anyway because it gave her days edges. The *Kestrel's Lament*—a name she'd chosen precisely because it sounded like something a lonely person would name their ship—slipped free of Meridian's docking ring with the grace of a thing that had done this four hundred times. Below her, the blue-brown curve of the planet turned slow and indifferent, and somewhere on its night side, buried under rock and protocol, Vesper waited without knowing it waited.

The first customs gate was automated. It always was. The drones came out of Meridian's outer shell like a school of patient fish, and Lena killed her transponder, dimmed her thermal signature to match the debris field she'd been threading since her second year on the route, and let the silence do the work. The scanners washed over her hull in long, indifferent strokes. Somewhere inside her skull, the memory of a boy named Tomas sat quiet as a held breath.

Neural lace scans were the trick. Meridian's customs authority cared about weapons, about sedition, about unlicensed biotech—but memories? Memories were legal tender on both sides of the divide, technically, so long as they stayed above. What was illegal was the carrying. What was illegal was the *down*. So the lace scanners looked for active transmission, for broadcast signatures, for the telltale shimmer of a memory being copied or sent. They never looked for a memory simply being *kept*, because keeping wasn't a crime anywhere, and because no one had ever imagined a courier foolish enough to carry contraband in the one place they couldn't search without cracking her open.

Lena had been foolish once, eleven years ago, and had built an entire life on top of the foolishness.

The gate cleared her. The drones peeled away. She exhaled, slow, and let the *Kestrel* fall toward the well.

* * *

The long descent was the part she hated most, because it was the part where there was nothing left to do but be alone with herself. Eight hours of controlled burn through atmosphere that grew thicker and warmer by degrees, while the stars wheeled overhead and the comm bands stayed empty as they always did, always, always. She'd tried music once, early on. She'd tried audiobooks, voice dramas, even the cheap synthetic companionship programs sold in Meridian's gray markets. None of it stuck. There was something about the silence between the colonies that got into a person, colonized them, made other voices feel like intrusions.

So instead she talked to the ship. To the memory she wasn't looking at. To the ghost of a boy named Tomas she would never meet.

"Your mother loves you," she said to the empty cabin, to the sealed package in her head. "Did you know that? She paid more than she should have. I let her." A pause. The autopilot ticked through a course correction. "I shouldn't have let her. But I did."

This was the ritual. This was how she kept the loneliness from curdling into something worse. She narrated the runs to her cargo, gave each delivery a small dignity, treated every memory like a passenger rather than freight. She'd carried wedding vows from a Meridian groom to a Vesper bride who'd been evacuated as a child and never allowed home. She'd carried a deathbed apology across the divide, a father's last words to a son who'd become an elder. She'd carried recipes, lullabies, the coordinates of a grave that officially did not exist. Forty-one deliveries now, if you counted this one. Forty-one threads in a tapestry no one but her could see whole.

And in eleven years, she had never once watched what she carried.

It wasn't squeamishness. It was engineering. A courier who knew her cargo became a courier with opinions about her cargo, and opinions made you slow, and slow made you caught, and caught made everyone who'd ever trusted you into a suspect. But underneath the engineering there was a simpler truth, one she kept in the same sealed room where she kept the faces of everyone she'd ever delivered for: if she watched, she would start wanting things. She would start wanting the families whole. She would start wanting the divide gone. And wanting things, in her experience, was how you ended up either dead or disappointed, and she'd already spent her youth on both.

The atmosphere thickened. The *Kestrel* shuddered through the upper veil of cloud, and below, at last, the night side of the continent resolved into a scatter of lights too faint and too regular to be natural. Vesper. Sealed city. Silent enclave. Sixty years of doors closed.

Lena keyed the approach channel—the old one, the unofficial one, the frequency that existed in no registry and was maintained by word of mouth passed hand to hand among maybe thirty living people. "Vesper Approach, this is Kestrel's Lament, inbound on the milk run. Requesting the usual door."

Static. Then, faintly, a voice she knew: old Dele, the groundside coordinator, sounding older than he had three months ago. *"Door's open, Galloway. Same cradle. You're late."*

"I'm always late."

*"You're always late,"* he agreed, and cut the channel, because even here, even among friends, brevity was survival.

* * *

The cradle was a decommissioned mining shaft two kilometers outside Vesper's sealed perimeter, its elevator long dead, its walls furred with mineral bloom that glittered in the light of Lena's work lamp like frost. She set the *Kestrel* down on the old landing pad with the tenderness of a woman setting down something breakable, and then she sat for a moment in the cockpit after shutdown, listening to the hull tick as it cooled, letting the silence of the ground settle over her like snow.

Dele met her at the airlock, as always. He was seventy-something now, bent like a question mark, wrapped in the layered wool the Vesperers favored against the chill of their underground warrens. His eyes found hers in the lamplight with the particular sharpness of a man who had spent six decades reading faces in a place where faces were the only news.

"You're thinner," he said.

"You're older."

"Both true. Both rude." He held out a weathered hand, and she clasped it, and for a moment they stood like that, two points of contact in a universe of distance. "You have her?"

"I have him."

Dele's eyebrows rose a fraction. He understood. He always understood; that was the terrible gift of him. "The grandmother's waiting. Third level, the old infirmary quarter. She's ninety-four, Lena. Ninety-four, and she's been sitting by her door every evening for a week because she dreamed—" He stopped himself. Shook his head. "Because she hoped."

Lena nodded and said nothing, because saying something would have required opening the sealed room, and the sealed room was not for opening on landing pads in the cold.

The transfer took twenty minutes. She jacked into the relay cradle Dele had rigged from salvage—half of it older than she was—and walked the memory across the gap between her lace and the local archive with the care of a woman carrying water across ice. No corruption. No bleed. The package arrived whole: *Tomas, age seven, laughing on a maintenance gantry, gravity cycling wrong, falling, falling, and then the memory ends where the widow had chosen to end it, at the laughter, before the fall.*

No. She hadn't watched. She didn't know that's what it contained. She only knew the shape of it from the widow's words in the tea house, and the shape was enough.

When it was done, she sat back, flexing her fingers, feeling the strange lightness and hollowness that followed every delivery—the sense of having given away a room in her own head. Dele unplugged the cables with gentle hands.

"She'll come tomorrow," he said. "Or tonight. She doesn't sleep much anymore."

"Tell her—" Lena started, and stopped. Tell her what? That the memory was clean? That her grandson's laugh had crossed forty thousand kilometers of hostile vacuum hidden in a stranger's skull? There was no sentence for that. "Tell her the sender says hello."

"I'll tell her the sender loves her," Dele said. "That's what she'll want to know."

* * *

Payment came at dawn, Vesper time, which was a fiction maintained by lamps and discipline. Dele counted it out onto the crate between them: hard currency, mostly, the old stamped alloy that still circulated underground, plus a credit chit keyed to a Meridian account that would take weeks to launder. Lena counted it twice, because counting was professional, and then counted it a third time because the numbers weren't adding up.

"That's short," she said. "That's forty percent short."

Dele wouldn't meet her eyes. He busied himself folding the relay cables, his knuckles swollen with age. "Prices are down. All along the route. Fewer requests coming up from the city. The elders have been—" He chose the word carefully. "*Discouraging* new contact with above. Quietly. Nothing formal. But families notice when the elders stop helping, and families stop asking."

"The elders are cutting the line."

"The elders are old," Dele said. "Old men mistake silence for peace. It happens." He finally looked up, and what was in his face wasn't apology—it was warning, wrapped in affection. "You should know, Lena. There's talk below. Strange talk. The children are saying things."

"Children say things."

"Not like this." He lowered his voice, though they were alone in a shaft two kilometers from anyone. "Three of my neighbor's students drew the same picture last week. Same picture, Lena. A corridor. Frost on the walls. Pods, rows of them, like coffins standing up. And a door at the end with numbers on it, counting down. Three children, same drawing, never met each other's paper. And this week it's not three. My granddaughter came home Tuesday and asked me what the Halcyon is."

The name landed in Lena's chest like a dropped tool. The *Halcyon*. The derelict generation ship in the long orbit, the hulk every child on both sides learned about in history modules and then forgot—the ship that had carried the founders, the ship both colonies had supposedly been built from, the ship that had been dark and dead and drifting for as long as anyone alive could remember. Nobody thought about the *Halcyon*. It was furniture. It was a monument. It was a corpse.

"What did you tell her?" Lena asked.

"I told her it was a story we tell ourselves about where we came from." Dele's mouth twisted. "She said, 'Grandfather, the story is dreaming me.' And then she showed me the drawing, and I swear to you, on my wife's grave, the frost on those pods was drawn exactly right. Exactly. Like she'd stood in that corridor."

Lena looked at him for a long moment. The lamp hummed. Somewhere far above, invisible behind rock and sky and vacuum, the *Halcyon* drifted in its long orbit, dark and patient.

"It's a coincidence," she said, and heard how thin it sounded.

"Yes," Dele agreed. "Probably. Coincidences happen." He pushed the short payment across the crate toward her, and his eyes were kind and frightened in equal measure. "Take your money, Galloway. Take it and run your lane and don't think about drawings. That's what I'm going to try to do."

But neither of them moved for a moment, and the silence between them was the same silence that lay between the colonies, and for the first time in eleven years, Lena Galloway wondered what else might be living in it.

She took the money. She flew home through the long dark with an empty room in her head where a boy's laugh used to be, and she did not sleep, and above her—though she did not know it yet—the *Halcyon* turned its face toward the world like something beginning, very slowly, to wake.
