## 1. The Long Route

The courier ship *Marrow* had a name that Tamsin Brightwater had never quite forgiven, because it suggested bone and death and the hard business of keeping things alive, which was, he supposed, exactly right. He'd named her himself at nineteen, in the first raw year after the war's opening strike, when grief made him literal. Eleven years later she was still the ugliest working vessel on the loop: hull patched with mismatched plating the color of old teeth, cargo bay smelling permanently of nutrient gel and wet soil, one maneuvering thruster that coughed like an elderly man climbing stairs.

He talked to her anyway. That was the thing about the long route — you either talked to your ship or you went quiet in a way that never quite reversed.

"All right," he said, easing her out of Verdance Station's shadow. "Easy does it. Don't show off."

*Marrow* drifted clear of the station's spinning ring, where the gardens turned in their slow artificial day, ten thousand seedlings drinking light that had never touched a sun. Tamsin watched them through the viewport until the curve of the ring swallowed them — rows of green under grow-lamps, tended by people who would mostly die before the trees they planted grew tall enough to sit under. That was the whole covenant of the Verdance, written into the founding charter in language so beautiful it almost hid what it asked: *You will plant for hands you will never hold.*

He'd signed it at seventeen without understanding. He understood now. Understanding was most of his job.

The run to relay buoy Halcyon took six days if you didn't push her, seven if you were honest about the thruster. Tamsin had made the trip two hundred and eleven times. He knew every meter of it the way you know a hallway in a house where you grew up — the stretch where the comm chatter from the station frayed into static, the long dark middle where the only sound was the recycler breathing, the final approach where Halcyon's beacon blinked its patient amber blink, unchanged since before the war, since before him.

He spent the first day in the cargo bay, because he always did. The bay held this month's consignment: forty crates of seedling stock bound for the outer habitats, each crate lined with gel-beds and labeled in the careful hand of the propagation crew. And strapped beside them, wrapped in thermal cloth like something sleeping, the special cargo — three hundred cuttings from the Mother Grove, destined for a colony ship still being welded together by children not yet born.

Tamsin unwrapped one cutting and held it in both palms. It was a rowan, barely taller than his hand, leaves like small green coins. Somewhere ahead of it in time was a person who would eat its fruit. He found this thought did not comfort him so much as steady him, the way a railing steadies you on stairs you've climbed a thousand times.

"Hello, little one," he said. "Long ride ahead. Try to be brave about it."

The cutting said nothing, being a cutting, but its leaves trembled slightly in the bay's ventilation, and Tamsin chose to take that as agreement.

* * *

On the second day he called home, because the window between the station's rotation and the relay's interference gave him twenty clean minutes, and he never wasted them.

Deshi answered on the second chime, his face resolving out of static — Deshi Okara, hydroponics chief, sixty years old with forearms like dock cable and a laugh that could be heard through bulkheads. Behind him, the green haze of Bay Four.

"Tamsin Brightwater." Deshi said the whole name every time, as if shortening it would be a discourtesy to the universe. "Tell me you checked the pH on the rowan stock before you sealed those crates."

"I checked the pH twice."

"You checked it once and felt guilty about it, which is nearly the same thing." Deshi leaned closer to the pickup, squinting. "Is that thruster misfiring? I can hear it in your voice. You always sound like the thruster."

"The thruster is fine. I am fine. Everything is fine."

"Mm." Deshi's skepticism was a family heirloom; he'd inherited it from no one and passed it to everyone. "Petra says if you're back late again she's eating your share of the plum wine. She means it this time. She's said so four times."

"Then tell her I'll be early, and she should prepare for disappointment."

Behind Deshi, a small shape darted through the frame — quick, dark-haired, gone again. Tamsin caught himself smiling before he could decide whether to.

"Was that Iyo?"

"It was Iyo pretending it wasn't Iyo." Deshi's voice gentled in the particular way it did when he spoke of the girl. "Gardener's apprentice now, officially. Nine years old and already better with the grafting knife than half my adults. She asks about you, you know. Every week. *When is Tamsin coming back, when is Tamsin coming back.* Like you're a weather pattern."

"Tell her I'm a very reliable weather pattern."

"I'll tell her you're a drought with a courier license." But Deshi lingered on the call a moment longer than the joke required, and Tamsin knew why, and was grateful that neither of them said it. Six days out, alone with the dead and the seedlings — Deshi always lingered. *You're not alone out there,* the lingering said. *You're ours.*

"Safe flight, boy," Deshi said finally.

"Safe growing, old man."

The call ended. The static came back like water closing over a stone.

* * *

Nights on the long route, Tamsin kept a ritual that he had never told anyone about, because telling it would have made it smaller.

In the aft cabin, behind a locked drawer, he kept a data slate containing everything that remained of Dr. Sefa Brightwater: her voice on fourteen recordings, her handwriting in the margins of a botany text, a photograph of her laughing in the Mother Grove with dirt on her face and her sleeves rolled up. She had been the Verdance's chief xenobotanist and, more importantly, his mother, and she had been aboard the transport *Larkspur* eleven years ago when the war's first strike came out of nowhere and unmade the ship and everyone on it.

No one had ever claimed the strike. That was the cruelest architecture of the whole war — Earth blamed Verdance, Verdance blamed Earth, and somewhere in the middle a transport full of gardeners became a slogan both sides used.

Tamsin would pour himself tea he didn't drink, open the drawer, and play one recording. Not all fourteen. One. Rationed, like medicine.

Tonight's recording was old — Sefa dictating field notes while he, age eight, audibly crashed around in the background of her lab.

"—note that the rowan stock is thriving past all projections, which I attribute to neglect, honestly, we coddle these plants—" A crash. A giggle. His own small laugh, high and birdlike, preserved in amber. "Tamsin! If you break the centrifuge I'm selling you to the composters—"

And then her laugh, warm and unguarded, the laugh of a woman who did not yet know she had nine years left, and Tamsin sat with his untouched tea and let the sound move through him the way rain moves through dry ground — painfully, thoroughly, leaving things softer than it found them.

Eleven years, and he still expected her voice on the comm when he docked. Eleven years, and love had become maintenance: checking seals, calibrating lights, talking to cuttings, keeping the route. The Verdance taught that planting was hope. Nobody warned you that tending could be mourning with its sleeves rolled up.

He closed the drawer. He slept badly, which was normal, and woke early, which was also normal, and put the kettle on for tea he would drink this time.

* * *

Day five. The long dark middle. Halcyon's amber beacon had grown from a pinprick to a pulse, and Tamsin was running diagnostics in the cockpit with his feet up on a console that strictly forbade feet, when the comm crackled with a signal that shouldn't exist.

Not station traffic. Not the buoy's standard telemetry ping. Something else — buried under the noise floor, faint as breath on glass. A carrier wave, old-format, cycling on a frequency the war had retired a decade ago.

Tamsin sat up slowly. Feet off the console.

He ran it through the analyzer twice. The signal wasn't broadcast, exactly. It was *looping* — repeating on a fixed interval, patient as tide, emanating from the direction of Halcyon itself. Old military-relay architecture. Pre-war encryption scaffolding, stripped down to bare repetition, like a message that had forgotten it was ever meant for anyone.

And threaded through the carrier wave, almost lost in the static, was something that stopped Tamsin's heart and restarted it wrong.

A voice. Singing.

Just a fragment — three notes, maybe four, rising and falling in a pattern that snagged on something deep in his memory like a hook in knit fabric. He played it back. Played it again. Turned the gain up until the cockpit filled with soft, corrupted sound, a woman's voice singing through decades of decay.

He knew that melody. He couldn't have said from where — a lullaby, half-remembered, from a life that ended when he was nineteen. But he knew it the way you know your own name called from another room.

His hands were shaking. He noticed them distantly, as though they belonged to someone else, someone whose mother was still alive to sing to them.

Halcyon's beacon blinked its patient amber blink, unchanged for thirty years, and Tamsin Brightwater sat alone in the dark with a ghost on his comm, and after a long moment he keyed the throttle and flew toward it.
