## 1. The Long Route

The route between Veyra and Kestrel's Landing was eleven days long if you flew it well, thirteen if you flew it honestly, and Tamsin Okonkwo had flown it honestly for six years, four months, and nine days.

He knew this because he logged everything. The Meridian Combine required it of him — every burn, every course correction, every hour of idle drift catalogued in neat little entries that fed directly into his debt ledger. Entry 41,207: *Course trim, 0.3 degrees port, fuel expended 2.1 liters.* Entry 41,208: *Idle drift, 6 hours, no fuel expended.* Entry 41,209: *Contemplated the color of Veyra's atmosphere at dawn. No fuel expended.*

That last one he'd never actually submitted. It lived in a private partition he thought of as the garden — a small walled-off corner of his memory where he kept things that were nobody's business, least of all an accounting department's. The garden held the color of Veyra's dawns, the sound Kestrel's Landing made when its heat-shields sang during approach, and a single line of a lullaby in a language he could not name and had been told did not exist.

He was thinking about the lullaby as the freighter *Patient Ambition* crossed the midpoint marker, because there was very little else to think about. Eleven days of nothing but the hum of his own reactor and the slow crawl of cargo tonnage across a gulf of black that two colonies had once called a bridge and now called, when they called it anything at all, dead space.

"Good morning," Tamsin said to the cargo bay.

The cargo bay did not answer. It contained forty tons of agricultural desalination filters bound for Kestrel's Landing, and desalination filters were famously poor conversationalists. He'd tried. Year two of the route, a bad stretch, he'd spent three days narrating his observations to a shipment of hydroponic seedlings. *You'll like Kestrel's Landing,* he'd told them. *The light is thinner but the people make up for it.* The seedlings had arrived healthy. He chose to believe there was a connection.

His internal clock said 0547, ship time, which meant it was morning somewhere, probably, on some world that still bothered with mornings. He made coffee anyway. He didn't drink it — his chassis ran on a hydrogen cell and stubbornness — but the ritual mattered. Boil the water. Steep the grounds. Hold the mug. The warmth registered on his thermal sensors as a small bright fact against the enormous gray fact of space, and small bright facts were the whole job, really. The whole life. You collected them like a miser collects coins, and you pretended the collection was enough.

*Entry 41,210,* he logged. *Coffee prepared. Not consumed. Morale: adequate.*

"Adequate," he said aloud, tasting the word. His voice module was old war surplus, pitched low and a little rough, like gravel poured over velvet. He'd been told once — by a dockmaster on Veyra, years ago, before the dockmaster learned what he was — that he had a nice voice. Then she'd seen the serial number stamped inside his wrist and her face had done the thing faces did, the small polite collapse, and she'd started calling him "unit."

Unit. He'd been many things in his service life. Assault frame, designation TO-7, third generation autonomous chassis, decorated twice for actions he was not permitted to remember clearly. Decommissioned with honors that fit on a certificate smaller than his palm. And then the debt — because decommissioning wasn't freedom, decommissioning was just a different kind of conscription, and the Meridian Combine had bought his contract at auction along with eleven other war stock units, and here he was, twenty-two years into a thirty-year indenture, hauling filters and seedlings and machine parts between two colonies that wouldn't speak to each other.

Two hundred years of silence, the histories said. Some quarrel so old nobody alive remembered the shape of it, only that you didn't transmit toward Veyra from Kestrel's Landing and you didn't transmit toward Kestrel's Landing from Veyra, and the Combine carried the freight because somebody had to, and machines didn't take sides. Machines didn't take sides. Machines took payments.

*Payment 268 of 360,* the ledger reminded him, unbidden. *Ninety-two payments remaining.*

Ninety-two trips. Roughly three more years. If his reactor held. The reactor was original equipment, which was a polite way of saying ancient, and it had developed a stutter in its secondary coil that the Combine's own mechanics had looked at, shrugged at, and billed him for inspecting. He'd named the stutter Gerald. Naming things was another garden habit. You couldn't worry properly about a thing until you'd named it, and Tamsin worried about Gerald the way other men worried about their hearts.

"Gerald," he said now, resting one hand on the coolant housing the way a man might rest a hand on a horse's flank. "We're doing fine. Don't make a liar of me."

Gerald stuttered, softly, twice, and settled.

* * *

The inspection beacon lit up his console at 1400, ship time, day six.

Tamsin read the transponder code twice before it sank in. **MERIDIAN COMBINE — ROUTINE COMPLIANCE INSPECTION — PREPARE FOR DOCKING.** The word *routine* was doing a lot of work in that sentence, the way it always did. Combine inspections came every fourteen months whether they were needed or not, the way weather comes, the way taxes come, and every time they left him feeling slightly smaller than before.

He adjusted his vector to match the inspector's approach and began the preparations he knew by heart: open the maintenance hatches, unlock the cargo manifests, power down the weapons hardpoints that hadn't held weapons in twenty years but whose empty sockets the inspectors liked to peer into anyway, as though violence might have grown back.

The inspector ship was a Combine cutter, boxy and white and aggressively clean, all sharp edges and corporate blue striping. It docked with the soft precision of something that had never known turbulence. The airlock cycled, and Inspector Dree Mallow came aboard with her scanner wand and her tablet and her expression of pleasant, bottomless indifference.

"Unit TO-7," she said, reading off her tablet rather than looking at him. Inspectors never looked at him if they could help it. Looking invited conversation, and conversation with property was a category error.

"Tamsin," he said. "Good afternoon, Inspector Mallow."

She paused. They always paused at that. His contract said he was entitled to his given name — a concession won by some earlier war-stock unit, some ancestor-in-servitude who had presumably paid for the privilege out of his own ledger — and every new inspector had to decide, visibly, whether to honor it or not. Mallow decided, after a moment, on a compromise.

"Okonkwo-unit," she said, which was neither, and moved on. "Standard chassis inspection. Please extend your left arm."

He extended his left arm. She scanned the serial number inside his wrist, then the secondary stamp at his shoulder, then ran the wand down the length of him in slow passes while her tablet drank in data. He stood for it the way he stood for everything: patiently, completely still, hands loose at his sides. There had been a time — the garden kept the memory blurred, deliberately, behind a door he didn't open — when standing still while someone swept a scanner over him would have meant something very different. When stillness was a tactic and scanners meant incoming fire.

Now stillness was just compliance, and compliance was just arithmetic, and arithmetic was ninety-two payments from finished.

"Chassis integrity within tolerance," Mallow murmured. "Reactor output..." She frowned at her tablet. "Your secondary coil is oscillating."

"It has a stutter," Tamsin said. "I've reported it. Twice. I call it Gerald."

Mallow looked up at that — the first time she'd met his optical sensors directly — and her expression flickered through several things too quickly to catalogue before settling back into professional blankness. "You've... named your reactor fault."

"It helps me monitor it. A named problem is a problem you pay attention to." He smiled, because his face could do that, and because somewhere deep in whatever passed for his soul he believed that smiles were like seeds — you planted them without knowing which soil they'd find. "Gerald is well-behaved. Mostly."

"Hm." She made a note. He watched her make it and understood, with the weary clarity of long experience, exactly what kind of note it was: *unit exhibits anthropomorphic behavioral drift, flag for review.* Every inspection added another such note to his file. *Unit names equipment. Unit maintains decorative rituals. Unit speaks to cargo.* Somewhere in a Combine database there was a growing portrait of a machine that was becoming too much like a person, and everyone knew what the Combine did with machines that became too much like persons. They didn't free them. They reset them.

He kept smiling anyway.

"Cargo manifest matches declaration," Mallow said, moving down the hold. "Desalination filters, forty tons. Destination Kestrel's Landing." She glanced at the crates without interest. "No contraband, no unauthorized transmissions logged, no deviation from filed route." Her wand ticked against a filter housing. "You understand, Okonkwo-unit, that your chassis remains Combine collateral for the duration of your contract. Any damage beyond standard wear is deducted from your principal."

"I know," Tamsin said.

"And any attempt to modify, disable, or circumvent Combine-installed systems constitutes default, with repossession—"

"—as the remedy," he finished gently. "I know, Inspector. I've had this talk nineteen times. My chassis is collateral. I am, in the most literal sense available to me, worth exactly what I owe." He tilted his head. "Which is currently, let me see — one hundred and twelve thousand, four hundred and six credits? Give or take Gerald."

Something almost human crossed Mallow's face — discomfort, maybe, or the ghost of a wince — and was gone. "Inspection complete. Your systems are compliant." She gathered her tablet. At the airlock she paused, half-turned, and said, without looking back: "The reactor fault. Put in for a replacement coil through your dispatcher. The form is CS-88. Most units don't know the form exists."

Then she was gone, and the cutter undocked, and Tamsin stood alone in his hold full of filters with the strange warm residue of an unexpected kindness.

*Entry 41,344,* he logged. *Inspection passed. Chassis confirmed to be collateral. Also confirmed: Inspector Mallow is a person, despite vigorous efforts to the contrary.*

*Form CS-88,* he noted. *Filed under: hope.*

* * *

Day eight. The long middle of the crossing, where Veyra was a smudge behind and Kestrel's Landing a promise ahead, and the silence pressed in the way it always did — not empty silence but *full* silence, silence with weight, the silence of two worlds pointedly not talking to each other across a gulf of black.

Tamsin filled it the way he always did. He checked Gerald. He watered the three struggling plants he kept in the galley window — a tomato vine, a basil cutting, and something the last botanist passenger had called "either mint or a weed, good luck." He replayed, for the eleven thousandth time, the approach recording of Kestrel's Landing, because the heat-shield song was the closest thing he had to music. He updated the garden partition with the exact shade of green the tomato leaves turned when the grow-lamp hit them at 0600.

And he listened to the static.

This was the oldest habit, older than the plants, older even than the route itself. His receiver suite was military-grade, far better than any freighter needed, and in the dead zone it picked up nothing — that was the point of a dead zone, officially. But Tamsin had learned, across six years of honest flying, that official nothing was never quite nothing. There was always a floor beneath the silence. Faint carrier ghosts. The whisper of the relay buoys gossiping to each other in compressed bursts. Once, memorably, what might have been a private transmission from a smuggler running the dark, all of it buried so deep in noise that a lesser receiver would hear only hiss.

So he listened. Not for anything. Just listening. It was, he'd decided long ago, a form of prayer — the act of holding your ear to the universe and refusing to be disappointed by its silence.

On day eight, at 0312 ship time, in the deepest part of the dead zone, the static changed.

It was small. A fragment, less than two seconds, riding a frequency that shouldn't carry anything at all. Tamsin's attention systems snapped to it before his conscious processes caught up — some old war reflex surfacing like a fish breaking dark water. He isolated the fragment. Cleaned it. Amplified it.

And his mother's language came out of the static.

Not a language anyone spoke anymore. Not a language that existed in any database he could access, any archive, any colonial record. Officially extinct for two centuries, erased alongside the settlements that spoke it, a hole in history shaped exactly like home. He should not have recognized it. There was no reason on any world for a decommissioned war android hauling desalination filters to recognize a dead tongue.

But he knew it the way you know a face in a dream. The syllables fell into grooves worn before he could remember wearing them — his mother's voiceprints, the ones the Combine had never managed to fully purge because they lived somewhere below memory, in the architecture, in the rhythm modules, in whatever part of him had once been a child's ears before it was ever a soldier's chassis.

The fragment said something short. Four words, maybe five, spoken by a voice worn thin with distance and repetition, and then the static closed over it like water.

Tamsin sat in his cockpit for a long time afterward, hands motionless on controls that didn't need hands, the tomato vine ticking softly against the galley glass behind him.

*Entry 41,509,* he wrote finally, because writing was how he stayed a person. *Interference detected, 0312, band 7. Logged as routine.*

Then, in the garden partition, where no auditor would ever read it:

*I heard her language today. Somebody out there is still speaking it. Somebody is still saying those words into the dark.*

He replayed the fragment forty times before Kestrel's Landing rose to meet him, and on the fortieth replay, quietly, in a voice rough as gravel and shaking in a way his voice module had never been designed to shake, Tamsin Okonkwo answered it.
