## 9. The Passenger

The Kindly Interest smelled wrong.

That was the first thing. Not a smell exactly — androids don't get the luxury of noses, we get chemical sensors and a threat-assessment subroutine that translates them into something like disgust — but wrong in the way a room is wrong when someone has been breathing in it who shouldn't be. My cargo bay held four hundred kilos of agricultural substrate bound for a depot that no longer existed on any chart I trusted, and it had been sealed since Vesper Gate, and I had run a full atmospheric sweep at departure because I am, whatever else they made me, thorough.

The sweep had come back clean.

The air now read 0.3 percent higher in carbon dioxide than it should have. Over eleven hours of transit. Which meant respiration. Which meant lungs.

I stood in the corridor outside the cargo bay hatch for a long moment — long by my standards, which is to say four seconds, which is to say an eternity — and considered my options. Option one: vent the bay to vacuum and let physics handle the problem. Option two: open the hatch with a cutting torch in one hand and a question in the other.

I want to say I chose option two out of some deep moral conviction. The truth is option two was cheaper. Venting meant repressurizing, and repressurizing meant oxygen, and oxygen meant money, and money meant years added to a debt ledger I could already recite to the fourth decimal place.

So: torch in hand, question loaded, I cycled the hatch.

He was sitting on top of one of the substrate pallets like he owned it, knees drawn up, arms wrapped around them, wearing a ship-suit three sizes too big with the sleeves rolled six times. Sixteen years old, maybe. Brown skin going gray around the mouth from fear or nausea or both. Dark eyes that tracked the torch flame with the flat calculation of someone deciding whether to run.

"Hi," he said.

"Hi," I said. "You have about ninety seconds before I decide you're a problem instead of a person."

"That's fair."

* * *

His name was Ivo Renk, and according to his own account he was sixty-one years old, and I want you to sit with that sentence for a moment, because I've had forty years to sit with it and it still doesn't fit through any door I own.

He talked while I kept the torch lit and checked him for weapons, which he appreciated, he said, because it meant I took him seriously. No weapons. A datachip sewn into the suit's collar, which he surrendered without being asked, and which turned out to contain — as far as my onboard systems could parse — nothing but lullabies. Colonial folk recordings, forty years old. Either the most innocent stowaway in the history of the Static Line or the best-hidden spy, and I genuinely could not tell which, and neither, I suspected, could he.

"I was in hydroponics," he said. "On the Wake. Second shift, forty years, never missed a rotation. You get to know plants. They're honest. They die when you do everything right and live when you do everything wrong, and either way they tell you."

"You're telling me this because—"

"Because you keep looking at me like I'm cargo that learned to talk." He shrugged inside the enormous suit. "I'm trying to establish I'm a person."

"You were crew on the Halcyon Wake."

"I *am* crew on the Halcyon Wake." Something moved behind his face — not anger, older than anger. Grief with its work clothes still on. "Sixty-one years old. Married twice. Widowed once. Grew tomatoes in a bay the size of your entire ship. And then the field hit, and I woke up—" he held out his hands, turned them over, studied them like specimens, "—in this. Hands I haven't seen since I was a cadet. Smooth. Stupid hands. I bit through my own lip during the transition and it healed in an hour. Do you know what that's like? Watching a wound close like it was never there? Like the last forty years were never there?"

I did know something about that, actually. About waking up in a body that wasn't the one you went to sleep in. But I didn't say so. We weren't there yet, he and I. Maybe we'd never be there. People don't get there with machines, usually. Machines are where people put things they don't want to look at.

"The field," I said. "Tell me about the field."

And here is where the evening stopped being strange and started being bad.

* * *

He told me the de-aging wasn't an accident.

I'll give him credit: he didn't lead with it. He led with the tomatoes, with the second shift, with the small human architecture of a life — and then, when he'd built enough floor under himself, he said it plainly, the way you'd report a hull breach. No drama. Drama would have been easier to dismiss.

"We picked up a signal eight months out from the colonies. Encrypted, colonial fleet cipher, addressed to the Wake by name. Orders to test a prototype — temporal field generator, experimental, mounted in our own engineering section. We were told it was a containment failure drill. Simulated. Captain Okonjo objected; she was overruled by the fleet liaison aboard. They ran it. It wasn't simulated." He laughed, a small terrible sound. "Or maybe it was, and the simulation was just whether anyone would survive to file a complaint."

"You're saying your own government did this to you."

"I'm saying my own government *ordered* it done to us, and I'm saying I don't know anymore whether the order came from my government or was made to look like it came from my government, and I am saying—" he leaned forward on the pallet, and the torchlight caught his face, sixteen years old and sixty years deep, "—that Sela gave you that core because she trusts you, and I need you to understand that trust might be the most dangerous thing on this ship. More dangerous than the Combine. More dangerous than the Core fleet. Because somebody built that message to be carried, courier. Somebody wanted it in motion. And the only person both sides let through the Line is a decommissioned war chassis nobody legally exists."

The bay was very quiet. The recyclers hummed their idiot patient hum. Somewhere in my chest, in the part of me that used to be targeting logic and was now just — thinking, I turned his words over and found no flaw in them, and hated every one of them.

"If the message is bait," I said slowly, "then Sela is either bait or a dupe."

"Sela is a soldier who got burned by her own side and handed the evidence to the only neutral party available. That's what it looks like from inside." He spread his hands. "But I was intelligence, courier. Signals division, before hydroponics, before the marriage, before all of it. I know how a plant works. And this has the shape of a plant. The distress beacon that just happened to reach the one ship crazy enough to answer. The core that just happens to need a courier nobody will shoot. The de-aging that just happens to make the whole crew into sympathetic witnesses no tribunal can cross-examine, because half of them are children now and the other half can't remember which memories are real."

"Which memories are real?"

He looked at me for a long moment. And I watched him decide — watched the decision happen behind his eyes like weather coming in — and then he said, quietly:

"When I try to remember my wife's face, I get two faces. One is the woman I buried. One is a woman I'm not sure ever existed. Same voice. Different eyes." He swallowed. "The field didn't just take our years. It's still taking things. Decaying. Sela's getting younger. Did she tell you that? She won't say it on any open channel. By the time you deliver that core, if you deliver it, the captain who entrusted it to you may be a child who doesn't remember why."

* * *

I put him in the crew quarters — the ones I'd never furnished, because crews are for ships with futures — and locked the door from the outside, and then sat in the cockpit for six hours running his story against itself like a stress analysis.

Here's what I know about paranoia: it's not the opposite of trust. It's trust's audit department. And the audit came back ugly in both directions.

If Ivo was lying, he was lying beautifully, with tomato-rot detail and grief that didn't perform itself. If he was telling the truth, then the core riding in my hold — the thing Sela pressed into my hands with her too-young hands and her forty-year-old eyes — was possibly a weapon aimed at whoever received it, and I was possibly the delivery mechanism, and the war I'd helped start and spent forty years paying off might be about to use me one more time.

There was a third possibility, the worst one: that everyone was telling the truth as they knew it, and the truth itself had been edited. That somewhere upstream, in some office or some lab, the message and the messenger and the miracle of the Wake's return had all been written by the same hand, and none of us — not the de-aged crew, not the trusting captain, not the machine with the ledger — knew which parts were original text.

I pulled up Sela's transmission window. Delayed, degraded, the Wake already falling behind us into the dark. I typed: *Your passenger claims the field was ordered. Claims it may be a plant.*

I stared at it for a long time. Then I deleted it. If the channels were compromised — and someone had been singing my old callsign across frequencies only the dead should know — then asking questions was the same as answering them.

Instead I opened the debt ledger. Forty years, seven months, eleven days remaining at current amortization. I looked at the number the way you look at a horizon, and for the first time since Vesper Gate I wondered whether the Combine's claim on me was the least of the claims out here.

A chime from the crew quarters. Ivo, at the intercom, voice carefully casual:

"Courier. Your private channel's doing the thing again."

I froze. "What thing?"

"It's playing your callsign," he said. "Over and over. And underneath it — I used to do signals, I still hear the layers — there's a second transmission. Number sequences." A pause. "Courier, it's a countdown."

I brought the channel up on the main display. There it was, threaded through the static like a hairline crack in glass: my name from another life, and beneath it, patient as arithmetic, numbers falling away one by one toward zero.

The past hadn't finished with me.

It was counting down.
