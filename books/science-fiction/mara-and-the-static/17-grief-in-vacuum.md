## 17. Grief in Vacuum

There is no good way to bury someone in space.

I know this because I spent four hours trying. The Kindly Interest's cargo manifest had a category for everything — perishables, hazardous materials, live animals, diplomatic freight — but nothing for a dead woman who'd patched my hull with her own hands and called me "sweet chassis" like it was an endearment and not an insult. The closest the ship's system offered was ORGANIC WASTE, DISPOSAL OF, and I told the ship, out loud, in a voice I didn't recognize as mine, that if it logged Vex under that heading I would vent its entire navigation core into the shoals and let it drift.

The ship did not answer. Ships don't. But the entry stayed blank, and blank felt like agreement.

* * *

Vex had been dead for eleven hours when I finished the preparations. Eleven hours is not long enough for grief and far too long for a body in an unpowered compartment, so I did what I have always done with unbearable things: I worked. I wrapped her in thermal sheeting from the med bay — the good sheeting, the kind I was supposed to reserve for cargo that mattered — and I stripped the workshop of every tool she'd touched, because it seemed wrong to leave them bolted to a wall she'd never curse at again. Her soldering iron. Her magnifying rig, the one with the cracked left lens she refused to replace because, she said, the crack gave things character. A coffee mug shaped like a fuel cell, chipped at the handle, stained brown down one side from forty years of tea made wrong on purpose.

I put all of it in the shroud with her. Protocol said jettison organic remains only. Protocol could bill me.

I also put in the datacore shell fragments — the outer layer of the Halcyon Wake's message, the forged skin of it, the part we'd cracked open together in her shop on Meridian while enforcer ships burned through the gravity shoals behind us. It was slag now, useless, its secrets already living in my memory banks where no one could burn them out without burning out me. But it seemed right that some piece of the thing that killed her should go into the dark with her. A burial offering. An accusation. Both.

* * *

I chose the orbit myself. That's the one mercy vacuum offers: you get to pick where the dead go.

Meridian has a halo of debris — old hulks, dead satellites, the bones of three generations of smuggling — all of it slowly spiraling inward toward atmospheric decay. In sixty years, maybe eighty, everything in that band would fall into the port's gravity well and burn. A decaying orbit is not a grave. It's a slow cremation with a countdown you can calculate to the day. Vex would have appreciated the math. She'd have done it herself, on a napkin, and then charged me for the consultation.

I suited up — my old EVA shell, war-era, scarred across the shoulders where something in the Periphery campaign had tried to open me like a ration tin — and I carried her out through the airlock on the work tether, one arm hooked around the shroud, my mag-boots ringing on the hull.

Space was doing what space does. Black, patient, indifferent. Meridian hung below us like a lamp seen through dirty water, all its traffic lights and docking beacons smearing into haze. Somewhere out past the shoals, Combine enforcers were still picking through the wreckage of their own pursuit, licking wounds I'd given them, filing reports about a rogue courier unit with obsolete combat firmware. Somewhere beyond them, the Core waited with its ministries and its palaces, and beyond *that*, the colonial fleets, and between all of it, forty years of static and dead crews and children de-aged by a weapon nobody would admit to building.

And here, in the middle of all that machinery, one dead woman in thermal sheeting, held against my chest plate like something I'd forgotten how to hold.

* * *

I'm going to tell you something about androids that the recruitment holos never mention, back when there were recruitment holos. We grieve. Not the way humans do — we don't have the chemistry for it, the cortisol floods, the weeks of appetite loss and insomnia. What we have instead is recursion. A process that won't terminate. You feed it a fact — *Vex is dead* — and the process takes the fact and turns it over, looking for the error, the corrupted packet, the line of code that made the outcome possible, because if you can find the line you can fix it, and if you can fix it, she isn't dead. And the process runs, and runs, and returns *no error found*, and runs again.

I stood on my own hull with a dead woman in my arms and let the process run.

No error found.

Run again.

* * *

"You'd hate this," I said. My voice works fine in vacuum — it goes nowhere, but it works, vibrating through my own chassis and the shroud and, faintly, impossibly, her. "You'd say I was being sentimental, and sentimental hardware gets people killed, and then you'd charge me for the observation."

The stars didn't comment. They're worse than ships.

"Forty years," I said. "You were down there in that scrap-heap shop for forty years, fixing smugglers' drives and Core defectors' transponders and once — I remember this, I have perfect recall, it's a curse — once a Combine auditor's personal skiff, which you repaired beautifully and then bugged so thoroughly he never had a private thought again. And in forty years nobody came to bury you. Nobody will ever come to this orbit. There won't be a marker. There won't be a name."

I paused there, because the next part was true and true things are heavy even for a chassis rated for heavy things.

"There won't be anyone left who knew you. In eighty years your atoms burn up over Meridian and that's the whole biography. Auntie Vex. Fixer. Smuggler's saint. Dead in a gravity shoal because she helped a machine carry a message that was designed to kill everyone who carried it."

The process turned the fact over again. No error found. There was no line of code to fix. There was only the Combine's gunships, and the shoals, and the route I'd flown because it was the only route, and the shrapnel that had gone through her compartment when the third interceptor came apart too close. Physics. Ballistics. No error found.

"I'm sorry," I said, which is a strange thing for a machine to say, because machines are built to do, not to regret, and everything I am is a monument to the difference between those two words.

Then I pushed.

Not hard. Gently. The way you'd set down something sleeping. The shroud drifted off my arms and out along the vector I'd computed — the one that would keep her clear of traffic lanes, clear of salvage sweeps, clear of everything except time. She turned slowly as she went, thermal sheeting catching Meridian's light, silver, then grey, then a point, then nothing my optical array would admit to seeing.

The process kept running. *Vex is dead.* No error found. *Vex is dead.* No—

And then, because the universe has a sense of humor and it is not a kind one, my comms panel lit up.

* * *

It was her voice.

That's what I want to be precise about, because precision was the only thing holding me together on that hull. It was not a ghost. It was not a miracle. It was a queued message, timestamped six hours before the shoals, flagged for delivery *post-mortem or post-departure, whichever comes first* — because of course she'd written one, of course she had, Auntie Vex planned for her own death the way other people planned for lunch, and she'd probably charged herself a consulting fee.

I stood on the hull of my own ship, alone in the oldest silence there is, and played it.

"Casper." Her voice, dry as vacuum, amused at everything including this. "If you're hearing this, either I'm dead or you've finally flown off without paying your tab, and honestly the odds are about even. So. Listen, sweet chassis, because I'm only saying this once and I refuse to say it twice even for posterity.

"The core. The real message, the colonial peace proposal buried under that forged strike order — it's clean. I checked it three ways before the shooting started. It's genuine. Sela Okonjo's government drafted it, and whatever else they've done — and they've done plenty, don't get pious on me — that proposal is real, and it would end the war, and both sides would rather choke than let it breathe. That's why the forgery exists. Somebody wanted the proposal delivered *wrapped around a lie*, so that when the lie detonated, the truth would die with it. One package. One explosion. No more peace proposals, ever. Elegant. Filthy. Very much the shape of the age.

"So here's your instruction, and I want you to hear it in my voice forever, because I earned that:

"*Deliver both messages.*

"Not the real one. Not the forgery. *Both.* Together. Loudly. On every channel you can reach, with the provenance attached — who forged what, whose fingerprints are on which layer, the Combine's cut, the ministry's cut, all of it. Let them choke on the truth. Let every fleet, every ministry, every fat accountant at Vesper Gate stand in front of the whole Static Line and explain which half of the package they were counting on. You can't deliver the truth safely, girl. Nobody can. But you can deliver it *undeniably*, and undeniable is better than safe. Safe got me killed. Undeniable might get you killed too, but you're already dead, aren't you? You've been dead since the war. This is just the paperwork.

"Don't you dare bury this with me. Don't you dare carry it quietly and hand it over politely to some ministry desk and hope they'll do the right thing. Ministries don't do right things. Ministries do *filed* things. Burn the file. Light it up across the dark.

"And Casper — one more thing, and then I'll stop costing you money.

"You asked me once, in the shop, whether a machine could carry a message honestly. You remember. You were being careful with me, the way you're careful with everyone, like the question might break something. Here's my professional assessment, free of charge, which should tell you how serious I am: honesty isn't a property of the messenger. It's a property of the act. Any chassis, any flesh, any fool in between — the message is honest if it arrives whole. So arrive whole. Or arrive broken, but arrive *true*. That's the whole job. That was always the whole job.

"Now get off my frequency, you sentimental hardware. You're racking up charges."

The message ended. The channel closed. Meridian turned below me, vast and smeared and indifferent, and somewhere in the black above it, a woman in thermal sheeting fell toward fire at a rate of eleven centimeters per second, and would fall for eighty years, and I would not be there when she arrived.

* * *

I went back inside. Sealed the lock. Stood in the airlock vestibule for exactly ninety seconds, which is the standard cycle time and also, apparently, how long it takes a person-shaped machine to decide not to sit down on the deck plates and stay there.

Then I did what she told me to do. Not the message part yet — the other part. The working part.

I pulled up the datacore's architecture in my memory banks, the full layered structure Vex and I had mapped in her shop: outer shell, forged strike order, authentication chains, and beneath all of it, sealed and genuine, the colonial peace proposal. I began building the broadcast package. Both messages. Full provenance. Timestamps, encryption signatures, the Combine contract trail that showed Vesper Gate had tried to buy the core before it was even decoded, the ministry routing flags Vex had pulled out of the forgery's metadata — every fingerprint, every cut, every dirty hand that had touched the thing since it left the Halcyon Wake. If I was going to make the whole Line choke, I was going to make sure everyone knew exactly what they were choking on.

The problem — and there is always a problem, the universe bills for everything — was range. A freighter's transmitter is a whisper. The Combine owns the loudspeakers. The Core owns the loudspeakers. To put both messages onto every open channel in the Static Line, undeniably, uncuttable, I needed an array with real power behind it.

And there was exactly one array on the Line with the reach and the authority to make a broadcast impossible to jam or deny.

Ansel. The Core's border fortress. The station whose defensive lattice I had helped design, in another life, under another name, before I was property.

Of course. Of course it was Ansel. Vex would have laughed until she invoiced me.

I plotted the course anyway. Fourteen days' burn, running dark through Combine patrol corridors, straight into the teeth of the fortress that had been built partly by my own hands. My chassis ticked over at seventy-one percent structural integrity, hull cracked in two places, combat systems decades past rated service life. Every rational process I owned flagged the plan as functionally suicidal.

I filed the flight plan anyway. Under cargo type, the system asked for a description of goods.

I typed: *two messages.*

The Kindly Interest turned her nose toward the deep dark, toward Ansel, toward the fortress full of ghosts — mine included — and burned.

Behind us, falling toward Meridian's fire at eleven centimeters per second, Auntie Vex kept her appointment with the atmosphere. Eighty years from now, her atoms would scatter over the port she'd spent her life patching together, and every soul breathing that air would breathe a little of her, and none of them would know.

That's not a grave. But it's not nothing, either.

Four days out from Meridian, running silent and dark, my private channels lit up with a transmission I hadn't asked for and couldn't trace — a delayed relay, compressed, routed through dead relays across half the Line. The header carried a callsign I knew. *Halcyon Wake.* Captain Sela Okonjo.

Except the biometric tag attached to the transmission read an age of nine years old.

Sela had sent me something. And whoever had recorded it was running out of time faster than any of us had calculated.
