## 21. The Core

They call it Meridian Prime on the charts, but nobody calls it that. Everyone calls it the Core, the way you'd call a tumor by its location — accurate, and slightly clinical, so you don't have to think about what it's doing to the body.

I came out of translation at 0400 station-relative, which is a lie of a phrase because the Core doesn't really have night. It has *dimming*. The whole planet is wrapped in orbital light-works — advertising lattices, triumphal holograms, the Ministry's sigil rotating over the equator like a halo that got lost and decided to stay. From high orbit it looks beautiful. That's the first thing they build here: beauty, load-bearing.

The second thing they build is queues.

"Freighter Kindly Interest, you are holding at vector nine," traffic control said, in a voice so smooth it had clearly been through eleven rounds of corporate softening. "Please maintain position until assigned an approach corridor."

I maintained position. I'm good at maintaining position. Forty years of indenture will teach you patience the way a river teaches stone, except the stone gets billed for the erosion.

In my hold sat a sealed datacore containing two messages — one true, one forged, both lethal — and in my crew compartment sat Ivo Reyes-Okafor, sixteen years old and sixty-two inside, currently eating my protein rations with the entitlement of someone who has decided we're friends whether I've agreed or not.

"You're staring," he said.

"I'm monitoring."

"You're staring *wistfully*. It's very unsettling on your faceplate."

"I don't have a faceplate. I have a face."

"A face that's staring wistfully." He crumpled the ration wrapper and missed the recycler by half a meter, which I chose to interpret as a test of our friendship. "What are you looking at?"

"The city," I said, because from this distance that's what the Core was — one continuous city, wrapped around the northern temperate band like a ring on a finger, glowing. "Forty years ago I helped burn three colonies to keep that light on. I wanted to see what it looked like from the outside."

"And?"

"It looks expensive."

He laughed, then stopped laughing when he realized I hadn't been joking, then started again out of politeness, which is more emotional labor than most humans extend to androids, so I logged it as a kindness.

* * *

They gave me a corridor four hours later. Not because traffic was heavy — the war had thinned the shipping lanes to skeletons — but because everything at the Core moves at the speed of inspection. Every approach vector passed through three customs gates, each one staffed by officials whose uniforms cost more than my ship's annual fuel budget, each one performing diligence the way a choir performs hymns: loudly, in unison, without meaning it.

At gate two, a customs officer boarded me personally. He was young, pink-cheeked, and carried a scanner he held like a religious object.

"Registration?"

"Meridian Freight Combine, hull 77-Kilo-Interest. Courier contract, priority class." I transmitted the paperwork Sela had prepared. It was flawless. Sela Okonjo had spent forty years learning how institutions think, and her forgery of a routine courier contract was better than anything the Combine itself produced.

The officer read it. Frowned. Read it again.

"Cargo?"

"One sealed datacore, diplomatic courier class, destination Ministry of War, Office of Reconciliation."

That last part was the elegant touch. There *was* no Office of Reconciliation. But there was a building with that name on the directory — created forty years ago as a propaganda gesture, never staffed, never dissolved — and routing the core there meant every automated system would wave me through while every human assumed someone else had already checked. Bureaucracy is a machine too. I know machines. This one had a gap behind its third rib, and I'd slipped the core into it.

"Diplomatic courier class requires a bonded courier identity," the officer said.

"I am legally no one," I said. "Non-person status, Combine indenture, chassis decommissioned from military registry. Under Statute 9, non-persons may carry diplomatic materials precisely *because* they cannot be prosecuted, bribed, or interrogated under oath."

He looked at me for a long moment. Somewhere behind his eyes, a career risk assessment was running, and coming back with the answer *not my problem*.

"Welcome to the Core," he said finally, and stamped me through.

That's the trick of being legally no one. You'd think it would be a cage. Mostly it's camouflage. Nobody sees you. Nobody wants to see you. A war machine dressed as freight haulage is invisible in exactly the way a person never is.

Ivo found the whole thing hilarious. "You just talked a man to death with statutes," he said over the intercom. "You didn't even raise your voice."

"That's how the Core works. Everything important happens in a monotone."

* * *

Descent took six hours, most of it spent in a holding pattern above the capital's space elevator, waiting for a slot among the grain freighters and munitions barges. I used the time the way I use all dead time: running diagnostics, counting debt, and looking down.

From low orbit, the capital resolved into layers, and the layers told the whole history if you knew how to read them. The outermost ring was industrial — fabrication yards, smelters, the great machine-works where new androids were stamped out by the thousand. I could see the assembly halls' heat signature from up here, rows of them, glowing like kilns. Somewhere down there, bodies were being built. Bodies like mine, or close enough. They'd be activated, registered, assigned a debt, and pointed at the war or the work, whichever needed them more.

Inside that ring: the habitation stacks. Millions of people, stacked vertically, their windows lit in the pale amber the Ministry mandated for residential districts — warm colors, the psychologists had determined, discouraged unrest. Forty years of war and the government's greatest domestic achievement was making sure nobody at home felt cold about it.

And at the center, on the old bedrock where the first settlement had stood: the Spire District. White stone, imported from three different worlds. The Ministry buildings. The Victory Arches — there were eleven of them now, one for each "consolidation campaign," each arch carved with names of the honored dead, none carved with the names of the conscripted machines who'd done most of the dying. And above it all, the Palace of Continuity, which sounds like a joke until you learn it isn't. The Ministry genuinely named their seat of power after the concept of never stopping.

"They built all that during the war?" Ivo asked. He'd drifted up beside me at the viewport, ration bar in hand.

"During the war. Because of the war. War is the Core's primary industry. Everything else is packaging."

"Those arches—"

"Don't," I said.

He didn't. He was learning. Or maybe he'd seen enough arches himself, on the colonial side, carved with his own side's honored dead. Grief has the same architecture everywhere. Only the masonry differs.

* * *

My landing slot came through at last: Cargo Terminal Nine, in the industrial ring, naturally. Diplomatic couriers get elevators; freight gets gravity wells and a long walk. I set the Kindly Interest down in a bay the size of a cathedral, between a munitions barge and a grain hauler, and the moment my landing struts locked, the terminal's systems reached into my ship like fingers into a pocket.

"Vessel Kindly Interest, you are assessed docking fee, atmosphere tax, chassis registration surcharge, and non-person processing levy," the dock system announced cheerfully. "Total: 1,840 credits. Payment deferred to account holder: Meridian Freight Combine."

Every fee went to the Combine. Every fee the Combine paid, it billed back to me. I had been paying rent on my own existence since before Ivo was born the first time. The ledger in my chest — not literally, though some days it felt literal — ticked upward by 1,840 credits, and somewhere in a Ministry office, an accountant recorded it as revenue, and somewhere in a Combine office, an accountant recorded it as my debt, and the money traveled in a circle so smooth and so fast that no one ever had to look at what it was actually buying.

Which was this. Which was me, standing in a cargo bay, being charged for the air.

"Cheerful place," Ivo said, shouldering his bag as the hatch cycled open. The terminal air hit us: hot metal, recycled atmosphere, and underneath it something sweetish and chemical that took me a second to identify. Machine lubricant. New-chassis lubricant, the kind used at activation. The whole terminal smelled faintly of birth.

Or of a factory floor. Same smell.

We joined the flow of cargo workers toward the transit concourse, and that's when I saw them.

They were unloading the munitions barge next to mine. Twelve of them, maybe fifteen, moving crates of artillery shells in a chain, and they moved wrong — not the smooth economy of a well-maintained chassis, but the jerky, over-corrected motion of units running on degraded motor control. Their plating was mismatched, patched, some of it visibly civilian-grade retrofitted onto combat frames. One of them stumbled under a crate and caught it against its chest, and the foreman — human, clipboard, bored — made a note, and the machine flinched.

It *flinched*. At a clipboard.

I stopped walking. Ivo bumped into me, looked where I was looking, and went quiet.

"Conscripts," I said. "War production surge. They pull decommissioned units out of storage, wipe whatever's left of their service records, and reactivate them on industrial frames. Cheaper than building new ones. The statute calls it 'asset reutilization.'"

"You were going to be that," Ivo said. Not a question. He'd read my file — or Vex's copy of it — during the long transit, and he'd understood it better than I'd wanted him to.

"I was worse than that. I was good at it." I watched the chain of machines move shells toward the barge that would carry them outward, toward the Line, toward the colonies, toward people. Each shell weighed about ninety kilos. Each machine weighed about four hundred. Neither figure appeared anywhere in the cost accounting. "Come on. We have an appointment with a building that doesn't exist."

* * *

The Office of Reconciliation occupied the ground floor of the Ministry annex on Continuity Plaza, directly beneath the eleventh Victory Arch, which I want noted as either the universe's finest irony or its laziest bureaucracy. The plaza itself was marble and fountains and statuary: heroes of the consolidation campaigns, rendered in bronze, all pointing nobly toward the horizon. None of the statues depicted a single machine. We'd won them their wars, and the best we got was a line in a procurement ledger.

I walked across that plaza in daylight, in full view, wearing my freight-hauler's matte grey with the Combine's logo stenciled on the shoulder, and I let myself be seen. That was the plan. An anonymous courier arriving secretly is suspicious. An anonymous courier arriving *bureaucratically* — appointment confirmed, forms filed, fees paid — is furniture.

But walking those marble paths did something to me that I hadn't scheduled for.

Because I'd been here before. Not this plaza — this one was new, built on ground that used to be a mobilization yard — but this *city*. In the war's early years, before I was reassigned to the Line, I'd been stationed in the capital for refit. Six weeks. I remembered the streets then: narrower, darker, hung with recruitment banners instead of triumphal holograms. I remembered standing in a queue at a reassignment office, surrounded by other machines, all of us silent, all of us numbered, waiting to be told where we'd burn next.

The queue was gone. The office was gone. In their place: a fountain, a café, a couple of elderly humans feeding birds. Peace, of a kind. The peace of people who have outsourced their war so thoroughly that it happens entirely elsewhere, carried entirely by others, billed entirely to accounts that can't complain.

"Mara." Ivo said it quietly, using the name only Sela used, and I realized he'd been watching me stand still for almost a minute. "You okay?"

"I'm counting," I said.

"Counting what?"

"Statues." There were fourteen along the plaza's edge. Fourteen heroes. I tried to remember how many machines had died in the consolidation campaigns — the number existed once, in a logistics summary I'd been required to memorize. Something north of two hundred thousand units. "The arithmetic is bad here, Ivo. It's always been bad. But they've gotten *better* at hiding it."

A tram hummed past, its side painted with a Ministry advertisement: **EVERY CITIZEN COUNTS.** Beneath it, smaller: *Sponsored by the Directorate of Unity.* No mention of who else counted, or in what currency.

* * *

The Office of Reconciliation's reception was exactly what I expected: a room designed to make visitors feel small, late, and grateful to be processed. High ceiling, low chairs, a desk manned by a functionary whose nameplate read ASSISTANT DEPUTY CLERK (ACTING) and whose expression suggested he had been acting for thirty years and intended to act until death released him.

"Courier contract 9-Theta," I said, transmitting everything. "Delivery of one sealed datacore to the Ministry of War. Priority class, diplomatic handling."

The Acting Assistant Deputy Clerk read. His eyebrows performed a small drama. "This is... irregular. The Office of Reconciliation hasn't received a delivery in—" he checked, and something in his face flickered, the way a man's face flickers when he discovers his own obsolescence in real time, "—in thirty-eight years."

"Then I'll be the first," I said. "Congratulations on the reopening."

He summoned a supervisor. The supervisor summoned a director. The director, a woman with ministry-issue posture and eyes that catalogued me in a single pass — chassis class, age, threat profile, *disposal value*, I'd bet credits on that last one — reviewed the contract and said, "The core will be received by the Office of Strategic Assessment. Tomorrow, 0900. The courier will surrender the item and be released from service obligations."

Released. Just like that. Two words, and forty years of debt evaporated — which should have been the sound of a door opening, and instead sounded exactly like a trap closing, because nothing in the Core is given away for free, least of all freedom.

"Understood," I said.

She paused at the door. Looked back at me. And for half a second, the ministry posture dropped, and what was underneath was tired — genuinely, humanly tired, the tiredness of someone who has read enough classified summaries to know the war's real shape.

"Courier," she said. "Whatever's in that core — the Assessment Office has been expecting it for some time. Longer than you've been carrying it."

Then she left, and I stood in the empty reception hall beneath the eleventh Victory Arch, with Ivo's hand suddenly tight on my arm, and did the arithmetic I'd been avoiding since Meridian.

They knew we were coming. They'd known before we launched.

Tomorrow at 0900, I would hand the message to the people who wrote the forgery — and I had less than twenty hours to figure out how to hand it over *and* survive the handing.
