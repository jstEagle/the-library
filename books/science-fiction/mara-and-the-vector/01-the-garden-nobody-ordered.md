## 1. The Garden Nobody Ordered

There is a particular sound a greenhouse makes at 0500 station time, and it is not birdsong. It's the nutrient pumps cycling over — a wet, patient *shhhk-shhhk* like someone very large breathing through a straw — and beneath that the whisper of the misters, and beneath *that*, if you've lived here long enough to stop filtering it out, the faint electrical hum of Beacon 7-Null doing whatever it is beacons do when nobody remembers commissioning them.

I wake to all three. I have for eleven years.

"Good morning," I tell the tomatoes, because somebody has to say it and the tomatoes are the least likely to make it weird.

They don't answer. That's fine. Eleven years of one-sided conversation has taught me that silence from a tomato is not rejection; it's photosynthesis. There's a difference, and knowing that difference is most of my job description.

My name is Lena Nakamura, though the paperwork says otherwise. The paperwork says *Caretaker, Verdance Station, personnel file sealed by order of Fleet Administration, access restricted.* Which is a very bureaucratic way of saying: nobody is coming to check on you, ever, so please water the beans.

I swing out of my bunk in the hab module — a converted seed-storage closet with a cot, a desk, and a window that looks out on the dark side of a dead world turning slowly below us like a marble nobody picked up off the floor. Kestrel-4, the charts call it. The colonists who were supposed to come call it nothing, because they haven't been born yet, or they're still on Earth filling out emigration forms, or they died in the war. The war being another thing the paperwork doesn't discuss with me.

First order of business, always: coffee. Second order of business: pretending the coffee is good. It isn't. It's grown from a hydroponic cultivar labeled *Robusta-Adjunct (Tolerant)*, which tells you everything about how much respect this bean commands even among its own breeders. But it's hot, it's mine, and it pairs beautifully with the view of an entire uninhabited planet, which is the kind of pairing no restaurant on Earth could offer.

Then the garden.

* * *

Bay One is the oldest bay, the original bay, the one the architects drew first when Verdance was still a gleam in some committee's eye. Sixty meters of tiered growing racks under full-spectrum lights, air scrubbed to a humidity the engineers calibrated by guesswork and prayer. The tomatoes live here, along with the peppers, the leafy greens, and the dwarf wheat that took me four years to coax into behaving. Bay Two is orchard stock — apples, citrus, a pair of figs that produce exactly nine fruits a year out of what I can only assume is spite. Bay Three is the seed vault proper: cryo-drawers, backup generators, and eleven thousand accessioned lines of genetic patience waiting for a world that can hold them.

And Bay Four — Bay Four is mine in every way that matters, because Bay Four is where the seedlings go after germination, the nursery racks, the little ones. The ones with names.

Today's work list, self-assigned, as all my work lists are:

1. Prune Bay One tomatoes.
2. Nutrient cycle flush, Bays Two and Three.
3. Log beacon pulse.
4. Repot the alder seedlings.
5. Talk to somebody before the loneliness sets in like weather.

Item five is not officially on any list. Item five is just how the day works.

The pruning goes the way pruning always goes: meditative, slightly bloody, deeply satisfying. Tomato plants are drama queens. Left alone they'll grow into a green riot of stems and leaves and forget entirely about fruit, spending all their energy on ambition instead of delivery, which is a personality type I recognize from every staff meeting I attended in a previous life. So you cut them back. You find the suckers — the shoots that grow in the crotch between stem and branch, siphoning strength toward more plant and less tomato — and you pinch them off, and the plant sulks for a day and then gets down to business.

"There you go," I murmur, snapping a sucker free. "Less empire, more dinner."

The trick to talking to plants, if you're going to do it — and I'm going to defend this practice to my grave, alone, probably — is to say true things. Plants don't care about your feelings, but they respond to carbon dioxide, and breath is breath, and besides, saying true things out loud in an empty room is cheaper than therapy and has roughly the same success rate.

So I tell them things. I tell the wheat that the soil chemistry in rack twelve is finally balanced, after two years of me playing nitrogen whack-a-mole. I tell the citrus that its rootstock graft has taken and it's going to be magnificent someday, assuming someday cooperates. And in Bay Four, moving down the nursery racks with my tray of pots and fresh medium, I tell the seedlings about their people.

This is the ritual. This is the part I'd never admit to anyone, if there were anyone to admit it to.

"This one's an oak line," I say, lifting a seedling barely taller than my thumb, two leaves wide open like small green hands. "Accession 4471. You're for the northern river valley, whenever there's a northern river valley. Your person is going to be — I don't know. A kid, maybe. Somebody's kid who's never seen a tree that wasn't in a picture. And you're going to be the biggest thing they've ever stood under."

I set it back in its cell. Next rack.

"These are the rice lines." A whole tray of them, thin as thread. "You're for the delta terraces. Your people are farmers, probably. Real ones, with dirt under their nails and opinions about rain. They're going to argue about you. Which variety holds the paddy, which one tastes better, which one their grandmother would have planted. You're going to start arguments that last generations. Congratulations."

The seedlings do not thank me. Seedlings never do. Gratitude is a harvest-season emotion.

But here's the thing I figured out somewhere around year three, when the silence got loud enough to ring: talking to them about their people keeps *me* honest. Because it's easy, tending a garden for nobody, to let it slide. To skip a flush cycle. To let the pH drift half a point and call it character. But you don't skip cycles for a kid who's never seen a tree. You don't call drift *character* for a farmer with opinions about rain. The future makes a demanding supervisor precisely because it never shows up to inspect your work — you have to be both slacker and auditor, forever.

Eleven years of that. And honestly? Most days, it works.

Most days I can look at this place — the green stacks rising into the light, the mist hanging in the aisles like held breath, the whole warm humming organism of it — and feel something adjacent to contentment. Not contentment itself. Contentment requires witnesses, or at least that's my theory. What I have is something quieter: competence without audience, purpose without deadline, love without recipient. A letter written daily and never mailed, except the paper is alive and the ink keeps growing.

It's fine. It's genuinely fine. I want that on the record, wherever the record is, since the official record appears to have been sealed.

* * *

Midday, the nutrient flush. This is the least romantic work on the station and therefore the work I do most carefully, because unromantic work is where gardens actually live. Reservoir drains, lines purge, fresh solution mixes from concentrates I measure by hand because the auto-doser developed a stutter in year six and I decided I'd rather trust my own eyes than recalibrate a machine that might be lying to me.

Bay Two first. The orchard trees drink deep — you can almost hear it, a faint creaking in the young trunks, wood stretching toward the wood it intends to become. Then Bay Three, the vault antechamber, where the working propagation stock lives behind glass.

I'm halfway through the flush when I catch myself narrating again.

"—and tomorrow we'll check the mycorrhizal inoculation on the figs, because apparently you two need a whole underground social network just to make nine pieces of fruit—"

I stop. Listen. The pumps breathe. The misters sigh. Somewhere overhead, faint as a thought, the hum.

"You know," I inform the empty aisle, "a person could talk to herself so long she starts expecting answers."

No answer. Naturally. Though I'll confess — quietly, here, where no one can hear — that on certain days, in certain moods, the hum sounds almost like one. Not words. Just attention. Like the station itself is listening the way a dog listens: not understanding, but present.

Beacon 7-Null sits at the top of the spindle, above the bays, above the hab ring, in a compartment I visit once a day the way you'd visit a sleeping animal whose temperament you don't fully trust. Officially — insofar as anything about it is official — it's a navigation aid. A pulse transmitter. Every six hours it emits a coded burst into the dark, a heartbeat, and every six hours I log that heartbeat in a ledger that no one will ever read, and the ledger entry takes four minutes, and those four minutes are the closest thing I have to a religious observance.

I climb the spindle ladder after lunch. The compartment is cold, always cold, kept that way by equipment that predates my arrival and answers to no maintenance schedule I've ever found. The beacon itself is a black cylinder two meters tall, featureless except for a single status strip that glows a soft, patient amber. Amber, in beacon language, means nominal. Means *alive, quiet, none of your business.*

"Pulse check," I announce, because even rituals deserve narration.

The console wakes at my touch — the only touch it accepts, keyed to my biometrics, which is another item on the long list of things about this station that don't match its paperwork. Why does a decommissioned navigation beacon care whose hand is on it? I asked Fleet Administration that question once, in year one, in a formal communique. The reply came back eleven weeks later, three sentences long, and said essentially: *Thank you for your inquiry. Please continue logging.*

Please continue logging. The motto of my entire adult life.

The pulse comes at 1400 sharp. On the oscilloscope it renders as a pattern — a cascade of spikes and intervals, precise as handwriting. I copy it into the ledger, date, time, signature. My signature. *L. Nakamura, caretaker.* The same entry, eleven years running, four thousand times give or take, each one identical, each one faithfully recorded for an audience of zero.

Except.

Here's the thing I don't think about, the thing I fold up small and put in the back drawer whenever it surfaces, which is exactly where it's surfacing from right now, today, while I stand in the cold with my pen hovering:

Every other system on this station has maintenance records going back to construction. Pump serials, filter changes, firmware patches — eleven years of my entries stacked on top of decades of prior caretakers' entries, a continuous paper spine running down through the station's whole life.

The beacon's records stop.

Not at construction. Not at decommissioning. They stop eleven years ago — mid-entry, actually, a log line begun and never finished, dated two weeks before I arrived — and everything before that point is gone. Not archived. Gone. Scrubbed clean as a surgical field. Whoever maintained Beacon 7-Null before me left no name, no history, no trace, except for one interrupted sentence in a database that someone went to great trouble to hollow out.

And the interrupted entry is signed *Nakamura.*

My pen hovers over today's blank line. The amber strip glows its patient glow. Below my feet, sixty meters down, the garden breathes its wet green breath, and beyond the hull a dead world turns, and somewhere out past all of it there is a war I am officially too peripheral to notice.

"Nakamura," I say aloud, to the beacon, to the cold, to whoever scrubbed those files and thought that would be the end of it. "That's my name. Who were you?"

The beacon hums. The beacon always hums. It has been humming, patient and alive and none of my business, for eleven years — and it occurs to me, standing there with my pen finally touching paper, that I have logged its heartbeat every day without once asking why a navigation aid needs a biometric lock, or why its history was amputated rather than archived, or why the last person to sign its ledger shares my surname in a universe where surnames are not common currency.

Four minutes. Date, time, pulse pattern, signature. *L. Nakamura, caretaker.*

I close the ledger. Tomorrow I'll prune. Tomorrow I'll flush. Tomorrow I'll tell the alders about the kids who'll climb them.

But tonight, after the lights dim and the pumps settle into their nighttime register, I'm going to pull the maintenance archive and read that interrupted entry one more time — all eleven words of it, the sentence someone started and couldn't finish —

*If you are reading this, the beacon must never—*

—and this time, I'm going to find out how it ends.
