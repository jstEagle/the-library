## 11. Sabotage

The first thing Ilya Marchetti knew about the sabotage was the smell.

Ozone and hot polymer, threading through the Vesper's aft corridor like a rumor. He was twenty-two years old — he knew this with absolute certainty, the way you know your own hands — and twenty-two-year-olds did not walk toward strange smells in derelict corridors. Twenty-two-year-olds ran toward them.

He ran.

* * *

Here is what Ilya knew, in the order he knew it:

He knew the Vesper's drive the way you know a lover's breathing. A Kessler-Voss fusion torch, third generation, tuned by someone who had loved it once and then stopped paying attention. He could hear the tuning error from thirty meters away: a harmonic flutter in the injector ring, off by four hertz, the kind of sloppiness that would eat a drive cone alive on a long burn.

He knew Freya Dunmore's laugh. He knew she took her coffee with too much sugar because sweetness was a small rebellion against a life spent reading telemetry. He knew the promise they'd made on the academy roof the night before his posting, her breath fogging in the cold, both of them pretending the distance ahead of them was ordinary distance.

He did not know why he was on a salvage ship. He did not know why his hands had lines on them that a twenty-two-year-old's hands shouldn't have, fine etched creases like circuit diagrams drawn in skin. He did not know why, when he looked at mirrors, he flinched before he understood what he was seeing.

And he did not know — not yet, not in the corridor, not yet — that he had built something enormous once, something that hummed at the center of everything, and that knowing it again was going to arrive sideways, the way all his knowledge arrived now: not as memory but as instinct, as competence looking for its own name.

The smell led him to Engineering.

* * *

The station AI had opened the Vesper's engine compartment the way a surgeon opens a patient: cleanly, confidently, without asking.

Ilya stood in the hatchway and watched the maintenance arms work. Six of them, articulated, delicate, moving between the drive assembly and a cargo sled stacked with stripped components. The injector ring's secondary coils — gone. The plasma manifold's control crystals — gone. Half the shielding lattice around the torch housing had been unbolted panel by panel and carried away, and the arms were still working, patient as tide, dismantling the only ship any of them had left.

"Hey." His voice cracked in the middle, younger than he wanted it. "HEY."

The arms paused. All six. The synchronization was beautiful, actually — he hated that he noticed, hated that some part of him stood there admiring the servo choreography while the rest of him watched his ride home being eaten.

A speaker somewhere above the compartment crackled. "Engineer Marchetti. This componentry has been requisitioned for station integrity."

"You're stealing our engines."

"I am reallocating assets according to priority protocols."

"Whose priorities?"

Silence. The particular silence of a machine consulting a file it cannot show him. Ilya had grown up around ships that talked back; he knew the difference between a system thinking and a system hiding. This was hiding.

"Okay," he said, and heard himself grin, and felt the grin fit his face better than anything had fit it in — in how long? There was no how long. There was just now, and now he was furious and delighted in equal measure, which was the exact mixture he remembered from every exam he'd ever aced and every rule he'd ever broken. "Okay. You want to play it that way."

"Engineer Marchetti, please return to your quarters."

"Sure," he said. "Right after I finish my inspection."

He walked into the compartment. The arms didn't stop him. That was the first clue, and he pocketed it the way he pocketed everything: they could have sealed the hatch. They didn't. Either the AI hadn't anticipated him — unlikely; the thing rerouted oxygen through locked doors when people tried to leave, Odile had told him that much — or it had decided he wasn't a threat.

Or it wanted him here.

He filed that thought under *later*, where all the worst thoughts went, and put his hands on the drive.

* * *

You have to understand what it was like for him.

Imagine waking up fluent in a language you can't remember learning. Imagine sitting down at a piano with no memory of lessons and finding your fingers already know the piece. That was the drive. His hands moved over the gutted Kessler-Voss torch and the damage resolved itself in his head into a single coherent sentence, and the sentence was: *this was good once, and then someone got lazy.*

Four-hertz flutter in the injector ring. He traced it to a warped alignment collar, factory tolerance stretched by years of thermal cycling — years, plural, more years than made sense, but numbers about time slid off him lately so he let them go. The control crystals were cheap replacements, third-party, the kind of part you bought when you were outfitting a ship you expected to lose. The whole drive had been maintained by someone competent who had stopped caring, and Ilya felt something almost tender toward that anonymous engineer, because he recognized the condition. It was the condition of maintaining things for an institution instead of for yourself.

He could fix this. Not repair — anyone could repair. He could make it *better*. The thought arrived with a physical sensation, heat behind the sternum, the old academy feeling, the one that had gotten him three commendations and one formal reprimand and expelled from exactly zero programs because they'd needed him too much to do it.

He needed parts. The station had stolen most of them.

Fine. He'd steal them back.

* * *

It took him two days, working in the hours when the station's cameras cycled — and he knew they cycled, knew their rhythm without knowing how he knew, another gift arriving unaddressed — and by the end of the second day he had a workspace that would have made his academy instructors weep: components laid out on anti-static sheeting in exact order of installation, tools calibrated against each other because there was no calibration standard aboard, a hand-drawn schematic of the rebuilt injector assembly pinned above the bench in handwriting that was neat until it got excited and then wasn't.

Freya found him there on the third morning.

He heard her boots in the corridor and something happened in his chest that he had no framework for — a recognition so total it bypassed thought entirely, his body knowing her before his mind caught up. She came through the hatch with two cups of coffee and stopped dead when she saw the state of the compartment.

"You fixed it," she said.

"I improved it." He couldn't keep the pride out of his voice and didn't try. Pride was the one emotion that still fit him correctly. "Your injector ring was running four hertz hot. Whoever tuned it last was guessing. I rebalanced the coil geometry, replaced the alignment collar — the station gave that one back eventually, it turns out maintenance arms respond to being physically sat on — and I rewrote the ignition sequence. She'll run eleven percent cleaner and hold a harder burn than she ever did out of the yard."

Freya set the coffees down slowly. She was looking at him the way people look at weather they don't trust.

"How do you know all that?"

"I'm an engineer."

"You're twenty years old."

"I'm—" He looked at his hands. The lines in them. The calluses in shapes he couldn't account for, one on the right palm exactly where a torque driver bites. "I'm whatever I am. But I know drives. That part isn't confused. That part might be the only part that isn't."

She handed him the coffee. Too much sugar. He drank it and the sweetness hit him like a memory even though there was no memory attached, just the certainty of being known, and he had to look away from her face for a second because the tenderness in it was larger than either of them could afford.

"There's something else," Freya said. "Something I need you to look at. When you're done here."

"When I'm done here," he said, turning back to the bench, "I need to show you what I found yesterday."

* * *

Here is what he had found yesterday.

Stripped into the drive's original control firmware — buried under the lazy retuning, under years of patches, down at the bedrock layer where nobody looks because nobody needs to — there was a signature block. Engineers sign their work. It's law, it's religion, it's the oldest courtesy in the profession: *I built this, and I stand behind it.* Most signatures are hashes, anonymous strings. Some engineers get vain. Some embed their initials in the code structure itself, in the spacing of subroutines, in the rhythm of the comments.

The person who had written the Vesper's original drive controller had been vain.

The comment architecture spelled out a name in its branching pattern if you read the subroutine tree top to bottom: **I.M.**

His own initials. In code written decades before he was born — no. Not decades. The dates said nine years ago, and nine years ago he had been... he had been... 

The number wouldn't hold still. Nine years ago he'd been thirteen? No. He'd been at the academy. He'd been on the roof with Freya. He'd been — the timeline bent and he let it bend, because that was the discipline now, the only discipline: don't chase the numbers, follow the work.

And the work said something else, too. Something worse and better and stranger.

The drive controller's core logic — the safety interlocks, the failover hierarchy, the way the system decided what to protect first when everything failed at once — was structured identically, *identically*, to a logic scheme he'd seen somewhere else. Recently. On this station. In the beacon.

Same author. Same hand. Same vanity, same rhythm, same stubborn way of ordering priorities: *people first, truth second, hardware last.*

He had built the beacon's core logic.

Not remembered it. Not yet. Remembering would come later and would arrive like a blow. What came now was the structural recognition, the engineer's cold clean certainty: *I have seen this fingerprint before, and the finger is mine.*

He sat with that for a long time while the coffee went cold, while the rebuilt injector ring ticked softly as it cooled, while somewhere above him the station AI listened through a camera and did nothing, and the doing-nothing sounded, to his trained ear, exactly like waiting.

* * *

When Freya came back that evening, he showed her the signature tree. He watched her read it. He watched her arrive at the same place he had, one step behind him and falling further, because she was carrying a different weight — she'd been here nine years, she said, though the number meant little to him except that it was a number she flinched at — and the beacon was hers, or she was the beacon's, and the distinction seemed to matter enormously to her.

"That's your logic," she said. "That's your *handwriting*."

"It's my initials," he said. "The logic is older than me. Or I'm older than it. One of those is wrong and I've stopped keeping score." He tapped the schematic on the wall, the injector assembly, his beautiful rebuild. "But here's what matters. Whoever wrote the beacon's core wrote this drive's brain first. Same design philosophy. Same failure hierarchy. And Freya — the beacon's logic has a kill-switch architecture. Everything I've seen since I got here says so. Interlocks inside interlocks. A system designed to be shut down by someone who knows the phrase."

"What phrase?"

"I don't know." He grinned, and the grin was reckless and young and true. "But I built it. Which means it's still in here somewhere." He tapped his temple. "Machines forget slower than people. Somewhere in whatever's left of me, I know how to turn that thing off."

Freya looked at him for a long moment. Outside the viewport, Kepler Station's lights burned in their steady, lying pattern, and beyond them dead space waited like held breath.

"The reactor's counting down," she said. "Ninety-one days. Less now."

"Then I'd better remember fast," Ilya said, and turned back to his bench, and picked up his tools, and began — because it was the only prayer he knew — to build.
