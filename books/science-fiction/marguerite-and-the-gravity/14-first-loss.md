## 14. First Loss

The medbay smelled of antiseptic and warm milk, and Odile Ferran hated both.

The milk was hers. She'd requisitioned it from the Vesper's stores three days ago because Tomas Reyes — who had been her chief engineer for eleven years, who had a laugh like a door coming off its hinges, who had once talked her out of jettisoning a cargo bay full of stolen medical supplies by pointing out that the buyer was the one who'd sold it to them — had reverted past the age where he could chew solid food. The antiseptic was the station's, inherited with the ship when they stripped Kepler Station for parts. It sat in the air like an accusation: *you are keeping something alive in here, and you are doing it wrong.*

Tomas lay in the second berth, swaddled in a thermal blanket because his body no longer regulated its own temperature properly. He was small. Not child-small. *Infant*-small, though the anomaly's arithmetic had never been clean about mass — he was maybe half what a newborn should weigh, stretched and thin, as if the reversal were eating him faster than biology could keep up. His eyes opened when she came in. They were brown. They had always been brown. That was the only thing about him she could still verify against memory.

"Hey, old man," she said, because she said it every time, because ritual was the only structure left that made sense.

He didn't answer. He hadn't answered in six days. Before that he'd answered in fragments — a word of Spanish here, a fragment of a shanty his grandmother used to sing in the drydock yards at Ceres — and before *that* he'd been forty-one years old and complaining about the coffee. The descent had not been gentle. It had not even been interesting. It had been a man dissolving one year at a time while everyone around him pretended that watching was the same thing as helping.

She checked the readouts. Heart rate steady but slow, slower than yesterday. Respiration shallow. Neural activity down another four percent, the pattern flattening toward something that wasn't infancy so much as *absence*. The station medic — Freya Dunmore, who wore exhaustion like a uniform — had explained it to Odile twice with diagrams. The reversal wasn't rewinding him along his own timeline anymore. It was unwinding him toward a baseline, toward nothing, toward the state before there was a state to remember. He wasn't becoming a baby. He was becoming a draft of a person that had never been finished.

"He ate?" Odile asked, though she could see the answer in the untouched bottle on the tray.

"Half," Freya said from the doorway. She had the look of someone who had stopped sleeping in shifts and started sleeping in apologies. "Maybe less. He turns away from it now."

"Turns away."

"Like he knows it isn't going to help."

Odile nodded and didn't say anything else, because there was nothing else to say that wouldn't be a lie, and she had spent the last two weeks discovering exactly how many lies she was capable of telling herself before they stopped working.

* * *

The Vesper ran quiet these days. Eleven souls aboard when they'd burned out of Kepler Station's shadow; nine now who could still tell you their own names. The two who couldn't — Vasquez and Okafor, reverted past childhood into that blank, walking stillness — were kept in the aft compartment with soft walls and constant observation, and Odile did not go back there, and she told herself this was delegation rather than cowardice, and some mornings she almost believed it.

Ilya Marchetti was fourteen now. He'd lost three more years since departure, and with them whatever fragile architecture of adult understanding had let him comprehend his own condition. He spent his days in the engine room under Freya's supervision, doing work that was genuinely brilliant and genuinely dangerous, because a fourteen-year-old genius with no fear response and a perfect memory of how drives worked was both their best asset and their worst liability. Yesterday he'd rerouted a coolant line on instinct and saved the port thruster assembly, then asked Freya if he could stay up late to finish a simulation. Freya had said yes. Odile had watched her say yes and understood that it cost her something enormous to treat him as what he appeared to be instead of mourning what he'd been.

That was the thing nobody warned you about, Odile thought, standing in the corridor outside the medbay with her hands in her pockets. Grief needed a shape. You grieved a man, or you grieved a child, or you grieved a memory. But Tomas Reyes was none of those things anymore. He was a coastline eroding. Every day there was less of him to miss, and the missing kept recalibrating, kept shrinking to fit what remained, until soon there would be nothing left to do the missing with.

She went back inside.

* * *

It happened at 0340 ship-time, which meant it happened while everyone else was asleep, which meant it happened to her, because command was just a fancy word for being the one awake when the universe took something.

She was in the medbay because she'd stopped pretending she wasn't going to be. She'd brought a chair in from the galley two days ago and set it beside the berth, and she sat in it now in the low amber light, watching the readouts flatten by fractions, listening to Tomas breathe the way you listen to a clock you already know is winding down.

At 0341, his breathing changed.

Not dramatically. That was the horror of it. No alarm, no crisis, no moment she could point to later and say *there, that was the line.* His respiration simply slowed, and slowed, and the intervals between breaths lengthened like a sentence trailing off, and the neural monitor drew its last few spikes and then settled into a flat, patient line that looked almost peaceful, looked almost like sleep, and Odile sat there for ninety seconds — she counted, because counting was something her hands could do — before she accepted that the next breath was not coming.

She checked anyway. She was captain; she checked. She put two fingers against the tiny throat and felt nothing, and the skin was already cooling, and somewhere in the back of her mind the engineer she'd hired eleven years ago would have appreciated the efficiency of it. No drama. No waste. Just a system powering down because there was nothing left to run.

"Tomas Reyes," she said aloud, to the empty medbay, to the flatline, to whoever kept the books on such things. "Chief engineer. Born on Ceres, in the yards, third generation. Liked his coffee burnt and his music loud. Once fixed a drive coupling with a wrench and his own belt." Her voice held. She was surprised by that. "Died on the Vesper, in transit through dead space, at the age of—"

And there it was. The wall. The place where the sentence broke.

Because what age? Forty-one, the age he'd boarded? He hadn't been forty-one in months. Six, the age he'd last spoken? He hadn't really been six either — six-year-olds know things, want things, are *somebody*. An infant? An infant is a promise, not a person; an infant has a future folded up inside it, and Tomas's future had been unwritten in reverse until there was nothing left but the paper. He hadn't died as anyone. He had died as the space where someone used to be, and the universe had closed over the gap without a ripple.

There was no way to grieve that. There was no liturgy for it, no rite, no hole in the ground sized correctly. You could not mourn a man who no longer existed, and you could not mourn a child who never had, and Tomas Reyes had passed from one category to the other sometime in the night while she slept badly in the next compartment, and she had missed the exact moment, and she would spend the rest of her life not knowing which version of him she had failed to save.

Odile Ferran sat in the galley chair beside an empty berth and, for the first time since the dead space, since the silence that lied, since the years began running backward through her crew like water through open fingers — she cried.

Not much. She was not a woman who did anything much. But enough.

* * *

Freya found her at dawn. Odile heard her stop in the doorway, take in the scene — the captain, the blanket-wrapped body, the flatlined monitors — and she heard the other woman's breath catch and hold.

"When?" Freya asked.

"0341. Give or take."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be. You barely knew him." Odile wiped her face with the heel of her hand, briskly, businesslike, as if she could file the crying under routine maintenance. "Eleven years I flew with him. And you know what my last real conversation with him was? Eight months ago, dockside at Meridian. He wanted a raise. I told him we'd talk after the job." She laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "After the job. There was always an after-the-job. That's the whole trick of this life, Dunmore. You keep everything important in the after, and then one day the after gets eaten by something that runs time backward, and all you've got left is a raise you never gave a man who doesn't exist."

Freya came into the room. She didn't touch Odile — she wasn't a touching woman, Odile had learned that much — but she stood close, and she looked at the small shape under the blanket with an expression Odile recognized because she'd seen it in mirrors lately: the look of someone doing arithmetic they can't stop doing. *How many days left. How many of us. How long.*

"We need to log it," Freya said gently.

"I know what we need to do."

"And there's the question of the date."

Odile looked at her. "Say that again."

"The ship's chronometer is synced to the relay network, which is synced to civil standard time. If we log his death today, under today's date—" Freya paused, choosing the words like footsteps across ice. "Then officially, Tomas Reyes dies thirty-eight years before he was born. The record contradicts itself. Any inquiry board that pulls the manifest will see a death certificate older than the deceased, and they'll start asking questions we don't have good answers for. Or worse, the record will flag as corrupted and get auto-purged, and he'll have died *nowhere*, at *no time*, and there'll be nothing left at all."

The medbay hummed. Somewhere forward, Ilya was laughing at something — a kid's laugh, bright and unguarded, and it went through Odile like a splinter because eight months ago that laugh had been a baritone and a bad joke about coffee.

"So what's the protocol," Odile said. "For dying backwards. Somebody must have written it down. Some clerk in some ministry, some contingency annex, *In the Event of Temporal Displacement of Personnel*—"

"There's no protocol. There's never been a protocol, because this has never happened to anyone." Freya said it steadily, but her hands weren't steady, and Odile noticed, and filed it away, and loved her a little for it. "We're writing the protocol right now. You and me. Whatever we decide becomes the procedure."

Odile stood. She straightened her jacket, which was a habit, which was armor, which was the closest thing she had to prayer.

"Then here's the procedure," she said. "We log his death under a date decades in the future. Long enough out that no inquiry alive will cross-check it against his birth records. We write that he died old, in his bed, surrounded by people who loved him — because that's the only honest lie available, and I am done letting the truth be the only cruelty on the menu. We list cause of death as natural causes, because what killed him was *time*, and time is the most natural cause there is. And we burn his effects, because his family on Ceres can't receive a box of belongings belonging to a man who hasn't been born yet, and because I am not going to spend the rest of this voyage walking past his spare jacket in a locker."

Freya was quiet for a moment. "You've thought about this."

"I've thought about nothing else since 0341."

"It's a good procedure," Freya said. "For what it's worth."

"It's worth nothing. That's what makes it procedure." Odile moved to the berth and looked down at the small face, slack and finally, mercifully, unchanging. "Goodbye, old man. You were the best engineer I ever flew with, and I owe you a raise, and I'm sorry about the timing."

* * *

They burned his effects in the small airlock incinerator used for biological waste, which was undignified, and Odile decided dignity was a luxury the dead could afford and the living couldn't.

There wasn't much. A jacket with the Vesper's patch sewn on crooked, because Tomas had sewn it himself and refused all offers of correction. A deck of cards missing the ace of spades, which he'd swallowed on a bet in '41 and never replaced, claiming it improved his odds. A photograph, printed on actual paper the way he liked, of a woman Odile had never met — his mother, she thought, or maybe his sister; he'd never been clear and she'd never pressed — standing in front of a drydock crane on Ceres with her hand raised against the sun. A data chip with eleven years of engineering logs, annotated in a shorthand only two people in the system could read, and one of them was ash now.

The photograph went last. Odile held it a moment longer than she meant to. Then she fed it to the fire and watched the woman on Ceres curl and blacken and come apart, and she said nothing, because there was nothing left to say that the burning wasn't already saying better.

When it was done, she went to the bridge and opened the ship's log.

**VESPER — SHIP'S LOG — ENTRY 4471**
**Deceased: REYES, TOMAS A., Chief Engineer.**
**Date of death: [REDACTED — SEE APPENDIX]**

No. She deleted it. Typed again.

**Date of death:** and here she paused, fingers hovering, feeling the full absurd weight of what she was about to commit to the permanent record — a date sixty-one years downstream, chosen because it fell on his hundredth birthday, because if the universe was going to make her falsify a death certificate she was going to at least give the man the lifespan he'd been robbed of.

**Date of death: [his hundredth birthday]. Cause: natural causes, in his sleep. Survived by crew who remember him.**

She signed it, sealed it, and sent it into the dark at lightspeed, where it would arrive decades from now at an archive that would file it without question among ten million ordinary deaths, and somewhere in that vast indifferent ledger, Tomas Reyes would live out his whole allotted span on paper, and no auditor would ever know the difference, and that was as close to justice as the dead space was ever going to give anybody.

Ritual complete. Duty discharged. The hole in the crew remained exactly the size of a man.

* * *

She was still on the bridge when the proximity alert chimed.

Not hostile traffic — nothing that clean. It was a whisper on the long-range array, a pressure against the edge of perception, the kind of return that might be a sensor ghost or might be the masked zone breathing. Except sensor ghosts didn't repeat. This one repeated. Three pulses, evenly spaced, from the direction of Kepler Station's corpse.

Odile pulled the raw feed and stared at it, and the cold that went through her had nothing to do with the ship's climate controls.

Because the pulses weren't random. They were structured. And when she ran the interval analysis with hands that wanted very badly to shake, the pattern resolved into something she recognized from a lifetime of reading other people's transponders:

A distress beacon. Old-format, low-power, broadcasting from the wreckage of a station that should have been nothing but expanding debris and silence.

And the identification code attached to it belonged to the *Vesper*.

Her ship. Her registry. Calling for help from a place they had already escaped, in a voice that was not theirs, reaching toward whatever ships might be foolish enough to come looking.

Odile sat very still in the captain's chair and did the arithmetic she'd been dreading since departure: the countdown running faster behind them, the relay still days ahead, and now something in the dark wearing her ship's name like a mask, calling out to company.

She hit the intercom. "Dunmore. Get up here. Bring the boy."
