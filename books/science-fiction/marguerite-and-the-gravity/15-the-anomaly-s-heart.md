## 15. The Anomaly's Heart

The first sign that we'd crossed into the masked zone was the coffee.

I'd made it at 0640 station-relative, carried it to the cockpit of the Vesper, set it in the holder beside Odile's elbow, and gone back to check on the medbay. When I returned eleven minutes later, the cup was full again, steaming, sitting exactly where it had been — and the first cup was still warm in my hand.

Two cups. One coffee. Same moment, twice poured.

"Odile," I said carefully. "Did you drink your coffee?"

She didn't look up from the nav display. "I haven't had coffee yet."

"Then what's that?"

She looked. Her jaw tightened in a way I was learning to read as her version of screaming. "That's the third one this week." She picked it up, sniffed it, drank it anyway. Pragmatism down to the bone. "Drink yours before it happens again. Wasted coffee is the least of our problems, but I refuse to let it be wasted *twice*."

Outside the viewport, dead space did what dead space always does: nothing. No stars dimmed, no colors shifted, no dramatic curtain of distortion rolled across the glass. The anomaly didn't announce itself. It just quietly stopped agreeing with the rest of the universe about how time worked, and everything inside its reach had to decide, moment by moment, which version of reality it preferred.

We were nine days out from Kepler Station. The countdown stood at seventy-eight days. And somewhere ahead, past the heart of the thing that had unmade the Vesper's crew once already, a derelict relay waited with an antenna fused shut and a transmitter that hadn't spoken in decades.

I drank my coffee. It tasted like the first cup. That was somehow worse.

* * *

By day ten, we'd developed rituals for the stuttering.

Meals were the worst of it. You'd eat lunch, log it, and two hours later find yourself hungry again with a clean plate in front of you and no memory of washing it. Rations vanished from stores in ways that didn't match anyone's consumption logs. Odile instituted a system: every meal eaten got written on a slate bolted to the galley wall, in physical marker, because digital logs kept rewriting themselves. If you found yourself standing in the galley unsure whether you'd eaten, you checked the slate. If your name appeared under today's date, you went back to work hungry.

"It's not dangerous," Odile said, during one of our evening conferences in the cramped wardroom. "Not by itself. The ship's systems are hardened — Marchetti saw to that. The stutters are local. Small. A few seconds here, a few minutes there. The danger is psychological. People go mad in repeating time faster than they starve in it."

"How would you know?" asked Vashti Okonkwo, the comms officer. She was one of the four who still had their minds, and she guarded them like rations.

"Because I've read the reports from the first transit," Odile said flatly. "My own reports. Written in handwriting I don't remember using, describing events I don't remember surviving." She tapped the table. "We've done this crossing before. We just don't remember doing it. That's what the zone takes first. Not your body. Your *continuity*."

Nobody said anything for a while. In the corner, Tam Okafor — the youngest of the reverted crew, now mentally about six years old and physically drifting toward five — sat coloring with crayons someone had found in a supply locker. He colored the same picture over and over: a ship, a sun, three stick figures holding hands. Every time he finished, he turned the page and started again, perfectly content, as if repetition were the most natural thing in the world.

Maybe, in here, it was.

Ilya sat across from me. He was fifteen now — I'd stopped tracking the exact number, because the number only hurt — and he looked up from the schematic he was drawing and caught me watching him.

"What?" he said. Fifteen-year-old defensiveness, but underneath it, those same eyes. Whatever the anomaly took from him, it never touched his eyes.

"Nothing," I said. "Just checking."

"You're always checking." He grinned. "It's kind of annoying. But also nice. I can't explain it." He went back to his schematic. Then, without looking up: "You chew your pen when you're worried. You've done it since the academy. Did you know?"

"I know," I said softly. "You told me. Twice."

"Good." He bent over his drawing. "It's important somebody remembers."

* * *

The bad night came on day twelve.

I was woken at 0317 by the sound of Ilya screaming.

Not the nightmare-screaming he'd been doing since the transit began — those had become almost routine, the dreams of a boy losing years he couldn't see going. This was different. This was the scream of a grown man waking up inside the wrong life.

I found him in the corridor outside his bunk, braced against the wall, breathing like he'd been running. And when the emergency lighting caught his face, my stomach dropped through the deck plates.

He was older.

Not fifteen. Not seventeen. Late twenties — the age he'd been when the Vesper left port, the age of the man in the service records, the age of the face I'd spent nine years mourning. Lines at the corners of his eyes. The engineer's calluses back on his hands. He looked at me and there was no confusion in his expression at all, no boyish wariness, just a terrible adult clarity.

"Freya." My name in his adult voice. I hadn't heard it in twelve days and it undid something in my chest I'd only just finished re-bracing. "How long. How long has it been — tell me exactly—"

"Twelve days since departure. Seventy-six on the countdown. Ilya, you're—"

"I know what I am." He pushed off the wall, steadied himself, and I watched him take inventory of his own body the way he'd inventory an engine room: hands, reflexes, memory. "It's the zone. I can feel it — it's pulling forward instead of back. Like a tide changing direction." His voice cracked on the last word, and for a second both ages showed through at once. "Freya, I need to tell you things while I have them. While I'm *me*. I don't know how long—"

"How much do you remember?" I hated the question even as I asked it. It was the medic's question. The clinical question. But we didn't have time for tenderness.

"All of it." He said it fast, urgent, a man dictating a will. "The academy. The posting. The beacon — Freya, I designed the beacon, I know, you confronted me with it before we left, I remember that conversation, I remember all of them. The sabotage. The engines. Everything up to—" He stopped. Something moved behind his eyes that I recognized as grief arriving ahead of schedule. "Everything up to the part where I start disappearing again."

"Then tell me the important parts. Fast."

"The kill-switch." He grabbed my wrist. His grip was an engineer's grip, calibrated, certain. "There's a phrase. It's buried in the beacon's core logic — I built it in, a failsafe, in case the mask ever needed to be dropped from inside. It's not written down anywhere. They made sure of that. It exists in exactly two places: the code structure itself, and my head." He laughed, short and awful. "*Had* existed in my head. Now it exists in whatever's left of my head, dissolving backward through twenty years of memory, and I can feel it going, Freya, I can feel it getting *smaller*—"

"Then say it now."

"I can't." His face twisted. "It doesn't work like that. It's not words I memorized — it's woven into how I think about the code. I can't recite it deliberately any more than I can recite my own heartbeat. It comes out sideways. In dreams. In fragments. It'll come, but I can't force it, and every day I lose more of the context that holds it together." He exhaled, long and shaking. "So write down everything I say. Even the stupid things. Especially the stupid things. The phrase is hiding inside them somewhere."

I promised. We sat down right there in the corridor, cross-legged on the deck plating like cadets, and I filled six pages while he talked — about reactor harmonics, about his mother's kitchen, about a girl named Marguerite from his second year who'd pressed a flower into his textbook, about the exact torque specification for a coolant coupling, about a lullaby his grandmother sang in a dialect neither of us could name. None of it made sense. All of it mattered. Somewhere in the noise was a key, and we were panning for it.

At 0441, mid-sentence — he'd been describing the smell of solder, of all things — he stopped talking.

Stopped mid-word. Stopped breathing for one full second. And then his shoulders dropped, the lines smoothed out of his face, and when he looked up at me his eyes were wide and young and frightened.

"Why are you crying?" he asked. Fifteen again. Maybe fourteen. "Why are we sitting on the floor? Why do you have my notebook?"

I looked down at the pages in my lap. Six pages of an adult man's dying testimony, written in my own hand, in ink that was still wet.

"You were telling me about solder," I said, and my voice held, barely. "You said it smells like being useful."

He frowned, reached for the notebook, read his own future backward. "This isn't my handwriting," he said slowly. "But it's about me." He looked up. "Was I older?"

"Yes."

"Did I say anything important?"

I closed the notebook and held it against my chest. "All of it," I said. "You said all of it was important."

* * *

It happened on day fourteen, and afterward I could never fully convince myself it hadn't happened at all.

We were in the heart of the zone. Odile had warned us what that meant — instruments lying, clocks disagreeing, the navigation display showing three different positions in three different colors and none of them provably wrong. The ship groaned in ways that weren't structural. Sound arrived before its causes. Once, I watched Tam drop a crayon and caught it before it fell.

I was alone in the medbay, cataloguing the sedatives, when the stutter took me.

It wasn't like the meal repetitions. Those were small folds, seconds doubled over themselves. This was a door opening. One moment I was counting ampules; the next I was—

—standing on the relay's command deck, and it was *lit*, consoles glowing, antenna freed, and Ilya was there — Ilya at seventeen, Ilya alive and whole and *finished*, whatever finished meant for him now — and the transmitter was cycling up through its warmup whine, and Odile was calling out power margins in a voice hoarse from shouting, and the main board read TRANSMITTING in letters I could have kissed, and then—

—and then the board changed. RESPONSE INBOUND. A signal coming back down the beam from somewhere impossibly far away, and the whole deck held its breath around me, and Vashti decoded the header with shaking hands and read it aloud:

*"Kepler relay, this is Core Authority Deep Space Traffic Control. We hear you. Say again, all of it. We're listening."*

*Listening.* Someone was listening. The truth had gone out through ninety light-years of silence and a human voice had come back and said *we hear you* —

—I was counting ampules.

My hands were on the medbay shelf. The count stood at thirty-one. My heart was slamming and my eyes were wet and I had no idea why, except that I did, I remembered, I remembered all of it, the lit consoles and the response inbound and the words *we're listening* — except that even as I reached for the memory it slid through my fingers like water, details going first, then the voices, then the words themselves, until all that remained was the *shape* of it. The certainty. Three hours, maybe, lived somewhere ahead of me, and I couldn't hold onto a single second of the content.

But I knew how it ended. Not the details. The *fact*.

Someone answers.

I sat down on the medbay floor and cried for a while — good, productive crying, the kind that clears a blockage — and then I went and found Odile and told her.

She listened without interrupting, arms crossed, wearing the expression of a woman doing arithmetic she didn't enjoy. When I finished, she was quiet for a long moment.

"A precognitive episode," she said finally. "The zone is full of them. Time doesn't flow straight in here; sometimes it pools, and things fall in from upstream." She uncrossed her arms. "I've had two myself. In the first one, we all died. In the second one, I was standing on a beach I've never visited, watching a sunset with no sun in it. So forgive me if I don't build a shrine to yours."

"It might not be real," I agreed. "But it's evidence. It's the only evidence we have that the plan works at all. You want to throw that away because your visions were worse?"

"My visions were *honest*," she said. "Yours is a sales pitch." But she said it tiredly, without conviction, and I watched her decide — visibly, the way she decided everything, like pulling a lever — to believe me at least provisionally. "Three hours of future," she mused. "In which the relay transmits and someone answers." She looked at the nav display, at the three contradictory positions crawling toward the relay's coordinates. "Sixty-two days on the countdown. Eleven days of repairs once we arrive, if the damage is what the charts suggest. That leaves us fifty-one days of margin, minus whatever else this zone steals from us on the way through."

"So it's possible."

"Everything's possible. That's the problem with possibility — it doesn't discriminate." She turned back to the display. "Get some sleep, Dunmore. If the future wants to keep sending you memos, I want you rested enough to take dictation."

* * *

I didn't sleep. I went to the observation blister instead, the little bubble of reinforced glass on the Vesper's dorsal hull where you could stand and look at nothing forever, because that was mostly what dead space offered.

Behind me, the station was forty trillion kilometers back and burning down its countdown in the dark, broadcasting a distress call in my voice to ships that had no idea they were flying into a trap. Ahead of me, the relay waited with its fused antenna and its decades of silence. And somewhere in between — everywhere in between — the anomaly breathed, folding time into shapes it wasn't supposed to hold, giving me coffee twice and a future I couldn't keep.

Hope as evidence. That was what I had. Not proof — the zone didn't deal in proof — but evidence. A glimpse. Three hours of a tomorrow in which we made it, in which the boards lit up, in which a voice came back across the dark and said *we hear you.*

Unstable. Unverifiable. Possibly a hallucination handed to me by a phenomenon that ate memories for breakfast.

And still the truest thing I possessed.

The hatch cycled behind me. Small feet. I didn't turn around.

"You're supposed to be asleep too," I said.

"I know." Ilya came and stood beside me, his shoulder level with my ribs now, and looked out at the nothing with the serious expression he wore when he was working a problem. "I had a dream," he said. "A weird one. There was a room full of lights, and everyone was shouting numbers, and you were crying, but happy-crying. And there was a word on a big screen." He frowned, digging for it. "It started with an R. Response? No..." He shook his head, frustrated, the way he got frustrated with equations that wouldn't balance. "It'll come back. It always comes back."

I put my hand on his head, on the hair that used to be gray at the temples and was now the brown of a nineteen-year-old, and I looked out at the dark between us and the relay, and I thought: *hold on, whatever part of you knows the way out. Hold on sixty-one more days.*

"Hang onto that dream," I told him. "However small it gets. Hang onto it like a rope."

"Okay," he said, because he trusted me, because at fifteen trust was still simple. "Freya? Can I ask something weird?"

"Always."

"When I was older — before, in the corridor — did I seem scared?"

I considered lying. Decided he'd earned better. "Terrified," I said. "And brave anyway. Both at once. That's the whole job, I think."

He nodded slowly, filing it away, and we stood together in the blister while dead space did its nothing around us, and behind his eyes something old and vital counted itself down toward childhood, carrying a secret it couldn't pronounce.

Somewhere below decks, the ship's clock ticked 0300, then 0259, then 0300 again.

The zone was listening too.
