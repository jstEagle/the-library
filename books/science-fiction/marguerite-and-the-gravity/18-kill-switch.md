## 18. Kill-Switch

The relay's main transmitter array looked, up close, like a cathedral that had lost an argument with the sea.

Freya stood in the maintenance cradle beneath it, magnetic boots anchored to the truss, and studied the fused antenna nodes with the flat, unblinking attention she'd spent nine years learning. Micrometeorite scarring had welded whole sections of the feed horn into slag. Eleven days of proper repair work, Odile had said. They had four. Maybe five, if the countdown was feeling generous, and the countdown had never once felt generous.

*Four days to fix what needed eleven.* The arithmetic sat in her chest like a swallowed stone.

Behind her, inside the relay's gutted command module, she could hear the sounds of desperate industry: Odile arguing with the power distribution bus, the two crew members who still had their minds — Sefa and the navigator, Brandt — running cable through conduits that hadn't carried current since before Freya had been born. And somewhere below, in the Vesper's cramped medbay, Ilya was sleeping. Seventeen years old now. Sleeping the way boys sleep, boneless and total, and reciting in his dreams the phrase that had come out of him like a splinter working its way to the surface.

*Kepler-actual, Kepler-actual, douse the light, douse the light, the garden sleeps.*

She'd recorded it on the third night, sitting on the floor of his cabin with the recorder held close to his mouth, hating herself a little for the intimacy of it, hating the necessity more. He'd said it four times that night, each time in a slightly different cadence, and the fourth time he'd added a string of alphanumerics that matched, character for character, the root authentication string buried in the beacon's core logic — the code Ilya himself had written, nine years and a reversed lifetime ago.

It was a kill-switch. There was no other honest name for it.

She keyed the cradle's comm. "Odile. Status."

Odile's voice came back frayed at the edges. "Main array's at maybe forty percent conductivity. I can get you a transmission window of — call it ninety seconds before the capacitors cook themselves. Longer than that and we're not broadcasting, we're just making expensive light." A pause. "You're sure about this?"

No. She was not sure. She was sure of almost nothing anymore except the weight of the recorder in her pocket and the sound of a boy's voice saying words he'd written as a man.

"I'm sure it's the only thing we haven't tried," she said.

* * *

They gathered in the command module because there was nowhere else, and because Odile had insisted — *if we're doing this, we do it where everyone can see it fail* — and Freya stood at the ancient console with its dead displays and its one live terminal she and Brandt had coaxed back to life with parts cannibalized from two different centuries.

The phrase glowed on the screen in plain text. It looked so small. Nine words and a hash string. The sum total of nine years of erasure, one man's unmaking, two blank-eyed crew members drifting in the medbay, a reactor counting down beneath a mountain of telemetry noise.

"Recap," Odile said, arms crossed, because she was the kind of captain who needed the shape of a thing spoken aloud before she could commit to it. "We transmit the kill phrase through a half-dead relay. If the system that built the beacon honors its own authentication — and that's a hell of an *if* — the countdown stops. If it doesn't, we've spent our only working amplifier announcing our position to whatever's out there listening."

"And if we do nothing," Freya said, "the countdown finishes in seventy-two hours and this whole region of space gets unwritten. Us included. The ships in the traffic lanes included. Everything."

"Recap accepted," Odile said dryly. "Transmitting on your mark, Dunmore."

Freya looked at the phrase one more time. She thought about Ilya designing this code at twenty-eight, brilliant and compromised, building the lock before he understood what it locked. She thought about him trying to resign, and being erased for it. She thought about the version of him sleeping downstairs who had loved her first at nineteen and was now, impossibly, approaching that age from the other direction, and how somewhere in the middle of him there had been a man who tried to stop all this with a single sentence and failed.

She would finish the sentence for him.

"Mark," she said.

Brandt keyed the transmitter.

The relay had been silent for decades, and it came back to life the way old things do — badly, loudly, with complaint. The capacitors shrieked. The fused antenna nodes glowed a dull, angry orange where the current forced its way through slag. On the terminal, the power curve climbed steep and ugly, and Freya watched the ninety-second window begin to burn down.

The phrase went out at lightspeed, encrypted in the old authentication format, signed with Ilya Marchetti's root credentials. *Douse the light. The garden sleeps.*

Ten seconds. Twenty. The capacitors screamed. Sefa had both hands flat on the bus housing as if she could hold the power in by touch.

Thirty-one seconds in, the terminal chimed.

Freya's breath stopped. An acknowledgment handshake — coming back down the transmission path from somewhere deep in the masked zone, from the direction of Kepler Station, from the direction of the thing beneath it. The terminal parsed it and displayed it in plain, bureaucratic text, and somehow that was the worst part, that the end of the world would use a standard protocol:

**COUNTDOWN PAUSED. AUTHENTICATION ACCEPTED. AWAITING SECONDARY CONFIRMATION.**

"It worked," Brandt breathed. "It — oh, it worked, it actually—"

The terminal chimed again.

**SECONDARY CONFIRMATION REQUIRED: PHYSICAL PRESENCE AT PRIMARY NODE. AUTHENTICATION TOKEN EXPIRED.**

**COUNTDOWN RESUMED.**

**RATE ADJUSTED: +340%.**

The number on the screen changed, and Freya felt the change in her body before her mind caught up — seventy-two hours collapsing into something under sixteen, the stone in her chest suddenly a fist.

"It's asking us to come *back*," Odile said flatly. "It paused, looked at us, and said *no, in person, and hurry up about it.*"

"It's not asking," Freya said. Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "It's insulting us. It's saying the phrase wasn't enough. It wants the person who wrote it, standing in the place it was written, and it knows he's seventeen and half a universe away, and it doesn't care." She laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "Ilya built a kill-switch and the thing he built it for grew a deadbolt."

* * *

They were still standing in the wreckage of that moment — the paused-then-punished countdown, the sixteen hours, the insult of it — when Sefa's console lit up with traffic.

Not their traffic. Not anything they'd sent.

"Signal intercept," Sefa said, and her voice had gone carefully, professionally blank, the way voices go when they're carrying something too heavy to show. "Origin: Kepler Station. It's a distress call."

Freya crossed the module in four steps and looked at the screen.

The call was clean, professional, heartbreaking. A station emergency broadcast, standard format, priority routing. *This is Kepler Station, beacon tender Freya Dunmore speaking. Reactor breach in progress. Nine souls aboard. We are not going to make it. Anyone receiving, please, anyone—*

It was in her voice.

Not an imitation. Not a synthesis with the seams showing. It was *her* voice, every hesitation, every crack, the exact timbre of the messages she'd sent to supply coordination over nine lonely years. Whoever — whatever — had built the beacon had recorded her, or learned her, or simply reached into the archive of her life and taken the sound of her and made it beg.

"They're coming," Brandt said. He was watching the traffic plot, where three contacts had just altered course in the outer lanes, transponders flickering as their crews woke to a distress call. "Two haulers and a patrol cutter. They'll be inside the masked zone in — at their burn rates — eleven hours."

"Which puts them at the station," Odile said, "right about when the countdown finishes. Right when the region gets unwritten." She turned, and her face in the terminal light was gray and hard and older than her reversed years. "It's not even trying to hide anymore. The trap's sprung. It's ringing the dinner bell."

Freya stared at the screen, at her own voice pleading in text form, at the ships sailing obediently toward an erasure dressed as a rescue.

Nine years she had kept the light. Nine years she had believed the silence was duty — that somewhere above her pay grade, someone had decided this sector should be dark, and her job was to hold the darkness in place, and holding it in place was *good*. She had built a whole self out of that belief. A woman made of maintenance logs and quiet and the faithful tending of a lie.

And now the lie was using her mouth to kill people, and the darkness she'd kept was a fuse, and the faithful tending had been the cruelest job anyone had ever handed her.

*Staying invisible failed.* The thought arrived with a strange, cold calm, the calm that had lived under her panic since the Vesper first docked. *It was always going to fail. It was designed to fail. The whole architecture — the beacon, the mask, the countdown — it was built expecting no witnesses, and we are the witnesses, and the system doesn't have a plan for us except to make us complicit or make us gone.*

So. Not invisibility, then.

"Telling the truth," she said aloud, and found that saying it steadied her spine like a hand between her shoulder blades. "That's what's left. Not the phrase — the phrase was a key and the lock laughed at it. The *truth*. All of it. The erased contracts, the countdown, the reversed crew, the distress call in my voice. Everything, broadcast on every band, loud enough that every ship in the traffic lanes hears it before they cross the line."

"With what?" Odile said. "That array's cooked. We've got maybe one more ninety-second window in it, and the phrase already burned our credibility with the system. It knows we're here. It'll jam us."

"Not with the relay's reactor." Freya turned to face her. "With yours."

The module went quiet. Even the capacitors seemed to hold their breath.

Odile looked at her for a long moment, and Freya watched her do the arithmetic — the same brutal cornered arithmetic they'd all been doing for days, the kind that always ended with something on the table that couldn't be taken back. The Vesper's reactor. Their engines. Their ride home. Their *everything*, wired into a dead relay and spent in a single scream.

"And the ship?" Odile asked. "After?"

"After, we're standing on a derelict relay at the edge of dead space with a broadcast beacon and no ride." Freya held her gaze. "But alive. Unwritten is forever, Captain. Stranded is just a problem with a long timeline."

Odile's jaw worked. She looked at Sefa, at Brandt, at the traffic plot where three innocent ships burned toward a lie in Freya's voice. Freya watched her weigh her crew against strangers, her ship against a region of space, and Freya did not envy her the scale, and did not look away either.

"Get me the coupling specs," Odile said at last, hoarse. "And somebody wake the kid. If we're wiring his reactor into a corpse, I want the man who built the beacon's core logic telling us where the bones go."

* * *

Freya went down to the medbay alone.

Ilya was awake. He sat up on the cot when she came in — seventeen, sharp-eyed, all the recklessness of his academy years burning in him like a pilot light — and he read her face the way he'd always read her face, before he'd ever had a reason to hide anything from her.

"It didn't work," he said. Not a question.

"It worked and it punished me for it. The countdown's faster. And the station's broadcasting a distress call in my voice, and there are ships coming." She sat down on the edge of the cot, close enough that their knees touched, because she was done pretending the distance helped either of them. "I need your reactor, Ilya. Odile's agreeing. We're going to burn the Vesper to shout the truth loud enough to be heard. And I need you to tell me how to do it without killing everyone in the room."

He was quiet for a moment. Seventeen years old, and quiet the way he'd been quiet at nineteen, when the two of them used to sit in the academy reactor lab after hours and he'd work a problem with his whole body, jaw set, fingers tapping the table like the answer was hiding underneath it.

"The coupling's the hard part," he said finally. "The relay was never built to take a live feed like that. The junction will want to blow itself apart the second you push full power through. You'll need someone holding it closed. Physically. Through the whole transmission." He looked up at her, and there was something in his face that was seventeen and something that was twenty-eight and something that was neither, something that was simply *him*, the constant underneath all the versions. "Freya. I remember building the beacon. I remember the day I decided to try to stop it. I remember writing that phrase and thinking, *this is the one thing they can't erase, because it's inside me.*"

"I know."

"I was wrong. They erased it anyway. They erased *me*." His voice cracked, boy-bright and man-old at once. "So this time — this time we don't send a phrase. We send everything. We make it so loud they can't unhear it. That's the only kill-switch left. Us. All of it, out loud, forever."

Freya took his hand. Seventeen years old, callused in the wrong places now, or maybe the right ones — the hands of a boy who built engines in his head before he was old enough to be trusted with one.

"Then we'd better be loud," she said.

Above them, faint through the relay's old hull, the traffic plot ticked. Three ships, eleven hours, burning toward a lie with her voice on it. Sixteen hours on the countdown, and running.

Behind them, in the dark of the masked zone, Kepler Station waited with its stolen voice and its loaded reactor, certain — the way only machines and liars are certain — that no one would ever tell.
