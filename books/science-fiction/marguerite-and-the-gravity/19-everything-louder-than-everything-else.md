## 19. Everything Louder Than Everything Else

**Odile**

The Vesper's reactor was never meant to come out of the Vesper.

Odile Ferran stood in the relay's gutted engineering bay and said so out loud, because someone had to, and because saying it made it a decision instead of an accident. The core housing alone massed four hundred kilos. It was bolted into a cradle she'd helped design, fed by coolant lines that ran through bulkheads she'd helped weld, and every manual she'd ever read agreed that removing it was how you turned a ship into a coffin.

"Then we don't remove it," Freya said. "We bring the ship."

She meant it literally. The relay's docking arm was rated for tugs and mail packets, not a salvage hull, but the Vesper had been rated for nothing good in her whole ugly life. Odile looked at the arm, at the ship, at the eleven-day repair schedule dissolving in the countdown's accelerating tick, and did the arithmetic one more time.

Strap the hull to the arm. Cut the reactor's output leads. Run them straight into the relay's transmission bus. Let the beacon-killer signal, the truth, the whole obscene record of what had been done to this sector, ride a reactor pulse instead of a polite little antenna whisper.

It would burn the reactor out in under a minute. Probably burn the relay too. Possibly burn the Vesper, the arm, and everyone standing on either.

"Everyone off the ship when we light it," Odile said. "Except whoever's on the coupling."

Silence, the specific silence of six people each deciding not to look at the coupling.

"I'll be on the coupling," Ilya said.

He was seventeen now. Three days ago he'd been nineteen and had argued with her about coolant flow rates like a man twice his age. Now he stood with his sleeves rolled up, forearms still smudged with the grease of a drive he'd rebuilt from memory he shouldn't have had, and looked at the coupling the way Odile imagined he'd once looked at blueprints: as a problem that wanted solving, not a place to die.

"No," Freya said.

"It has to be someone small enough to fit in the access frame and someone who knows the bus architecture," Ilya said. "That's me twice. You know it's me."

"I know you're seventeen."

"I know I was twenty-six last month and the universe ate it." His voice didn't crack, and that was somehow worse. "I'm not asking for forever, Freya. I'm asking for the sixty seconds it takes to broadcast."

Odile turned away and started pulling cable. Some arguments you don't referee. You just make sure the ship is ready either way.

* * *

**Freya**

They worked through what Freya's chronometer insisted was night, though out here night was just a number that agreed to get darker. The Vesper crept in on thrusters, kissed the docking arm, and Odile's crew lashed her down with cargo webbing and prayer in roughly equal measure. Then the cutting began.

Freya ran the data side. It was the work she understood and the work that frightened her most, because the data was the point. She had spent nine days assembling it in the relay's dying archives and her own salvaged records: her employment contract dated after the station went live, signed in handwriting that was almost hers. The commissioning records erased down to the directory inodes. The countdown, ninety-one days collapsing to nine, encoded beneath telemetry noise by someone who'd wanted a witness and never expected one. The Vesper's medical scans, six crew members aging backward like film run wrong. Ilya's engineering signature in the beacon's core logic, and his resignation request, and the erasure order that had turned a man into a memorial.

Everything. She had promised herself everything, and she'd built the packet to prove she meant it.

"You're hesitating," Odile said, appearing at her shoulder with two bulbs of recycled coffee and handing one over like ammunition.

"I'm checking it a fourth time."

"You checked it three times."

"The fourth time is for everyone who can't check it themselves." Freya looked at the file tree glowing on her display. Somewhere in it was the answer to a question she'd stopped asking years ago: who. Who had wanted this sector dark. The packet didn't say. It just proved the dark had been built, on purpose, by hands, and hands could be found.

"Good," Odile said. "Then stop checking and start being right."

* * *

**Ilya**

The access frame was a meter wide and shaped like an apology. Someone at the relay's construction yard had routed the main transmission bus through a junction that could only be reached by crawling in and holding the primary coupling closed manually, because the auto-latch had fused sometime in the last forty years and nobody had cared, because nobody had ever needed to push this much power through this little door.

Ilya cared now.

He lay on his back in the frame with the coupling seated against his chest, both hands on the lock ring, and listened to the ship around him breathe. The Vesper's reactor was spooling two decks down, its output leads rerouted through cabling that Odile had run like she was braiding something, and every meter of it was a decision he'd have made the same way at twenty-six, at nineteen, at seventeen. That was the one thing the reversal couldn't take: the knowing. It ate his years and left the craft. He'd built the beacon's core logic at twenty-four. He could feel it in his hands now, the shape of that work, the way you routed a signal so it would survive anything except being told the truth about itself.

"Ilya." Freya's voice in his ear, close, like she'd leaned into the pickup. "Status."

"Coupling seated. Lock ring at seventy percent. I can hold it." He flexed his fingers against cold metal. "Freya."

"Say it."

"If the surge comes up the bus, you cut power. You don't wait for me. You cut it."

"I'm not—"

"You cut it, or the last thing I ever built is the thing that kills you." He made his voice as steady as he could. "I've been erased once already. I know how it's done. It's done by people who love you deciding the mission matters more."

A long silence. He could hear her breathing, and under it, faintly, the countdown — they'd piped it into the ship's address system, all of them agreeing without discussion that they'd rather hear it than not.

"Sixty seconds of broadcast," Freya said finally. "Then I cut it. Not before, not after. You hold for sixty."

"Sixty," he agreed. "It's a good number. Prime."

"It's not prime, Ilya."

"It feels prime from in here."

* * *

**Odile**

Odile gave the order from the Vesper's bridge, because if you were going to use your ship as a disposable amplifier you should at least be standing on it when it happened.

"Reactor to full transmission output on Freya's mark. Crew status."

Around her, the survivors answered. Sefu on the bus junction, monitoring line temperatures. Anouk in the medbay with the two who had gone quietest, the ones who had reverted past the point where they could be scared, because someone should be holding their hands and Anouk had volunteered before Odile finished the sentence. Marta on the docking arm, webbing checks. And the youngest, Ilya, in the frame, breathing slow, hands on the lock ring.

The two who were gone had voted too. Odile had put it to them anyway — run or stay — and they had looked at her with the flat patience of very small children and said nothing, and she had counted their silence as a vote to run, because she refused to log it any other way.

"Odile." Freya's voice. "Packet's loaded. Kill-switch phrase is in the header. If this works, the countdown stops. If it half-works, the countdown gets angrier. If it doesn't work—"

"Then we'll have told the truth to an empty sector, and at least the empty sector will know." Odile settled into the command chair. "On your mark."

"Mark."

* * *

**Freya**

The reactor's voice came up through the hull as a rising note, and then the truth went out.

Not a whisper. Not the relay's polite, geriatric carrier wave. A shout — the full record at full power, the erased contracts and the forged signature and the countdown's arithmetic and the medical scans of six people being unwritten, all of it hammered into the dark on a beam wide enough to reach the shipping lanes, the patrol stations, the inquiry boards, anyone with a receiver and the decency to be horrified.

Freya watched the transmission meter and thought: *nine years of silence, and this is what I sound like.*

For eleven seconds it was beautiful.

Then the anomaly noticed.

She felt it before any instrument did — a pressure change, a wrongness in the way the light from the work lamps fell, the specific sensation of a room being watched by something that wasn't in the room. The relay's displays bloomed with feedback. The anomaly was coming up the transmission path, riding the beam backward toward its source, the way water finds the hole in a dam. Every watt they pushed out, it pushed back in.

"Line temperature's climbing," Sefu reported. "Bus is at redline. Ilya, talk to me."

"Holding," Ilya said. His voice had gone thin. "Lock ring's slipping. It's — the coupling's trying to chatter, I've got it, I've got it—"

"Freya." Odile, flat and fast. "How long?"

"Forty-one seconds left on the broadcast."

"Then we hold for forty-one."

* * *

**Ilya**

The surge, when it came, felt like being remembered.

That was the only way Ilya could describe it, lying in the access frame with the coupling bucking against his chest and the anomaly pouring up the bus toward him: it felt like every version of himself arriving at once. He was twenty-six and signing a resignation he'd never send. He was twenty-four and writing the beacon's core logic, line by careful line, telling himself it was a lighthouse. He was nineteen and pressing a marguerite flower into a book he'd give away and regret giving away. He was twelve and taking apart his first radio in a kitchen that smelled like rain. All of it at once, all of it loud, and underneath it all a single clear thread that was Freya's voice saying *sixty seconds, hold for sixty.*

The lock ring slipped. He caught it with his forearm jammed against the frame and held it there, and the metal burned, and he held it.

He understood, in the strange cold way the frame forced on him, that the anomaly wasn't attacking the coupling. It was attacking the signal — trying to close the channel the way it had closed this whole sector, to fold the truth back into silence before it landed. The coupling was the door. He was the door. Everything he'd ever built had been, one way or another, a door, and this was the last one, and it was going to stay open if he had to be the hinge.

"Thirty seconds," Freya said.

"Still here." The words cost him. Something was pulling at him from the other side of the coupling, not his body, his *years* — he could feel them going loose, the way a tooth goes loose, and he thought with something like fury: *no. Not those. I just got those back.*

He held the ring. He held the door.

"Twenty."

The burning in his arms had stopped, which meant the nerves had, which meant he should be afraid, and he filed the fear under *later*, a folder he was getting very good at using.

"Ten."

Through the frame's gap he could see a sliver of the engineering bay, and in it, Freya, who had come down from the data station against every protocol they'd agreed on, standing where he could see her, one hand flat against the frame's edge. Not touching him. Just *there*, the way she'd been there at the academy when he'd failed his first orbital mechanics exam, the way she'd been there at the dock when his deep-space posting lifted, the way she had been there for nine years at a station built on a lie, keeping a light on.

"I've got you," she said.

"I know," Ilya said, and held.

"Three. Two. One."

The broadcast ended. Freya's hand slammed the cutoff, and the reactor died mid-note, and the silence that came down over the relay was so total that Ilya heard his own heartbeat, and then, faint and impossible and coming from everywhere, an answer.

* * *

**Freya**

The answer was not a voice. It was a change in the pressure of the dark — the specific sensation, Freya would think later, of a held breath finally being let out.

Her display, still lit on cutoff power, showed the acknowledgment first as noise, then as structure: a handshake protocol, distant, official, *real*. Traffic control from the Meridian lanes. A patrol relay. Then a third signature, older, slower, the kind of authority that didn't bother announcing itself until it had already decided.

They had heard. They were coming. And the countdown—

She pulled the feed with hands that shook. The number that had eaten ninety-one days down to nine was frozen. Mid-second. Not paused — *unwritten*, the way a word is unwritten when the page burns, except the page had burned and the word had survived anyway, because enough people had heard it said.

"It stopped," she said, and her voice came out as a stranger's. "Odile. It stopped. They answered. It stopped."

She turned back to the access frame, and the relief lasted exactly as long as it took to see what the surge had left behind.

Ilya was still in the frame. Still holding the coupling, though it no longer needed holding — the lock ring had seated, fused, done. His arms were burned from wrist to elbow where the ring had slipped. His eyes were open and tracking her, and they were clear, and he was alive, and he was seventeen, and he was going to be seventeen.

The reversal had stopped where it stopped. Not at twenty-six. Not at nineteen. Here. This was the self the universe had decided to keep, the way you keep a pressed flower: not the living thing, but the thing that survived the pressing.

"Freya." His voice was steady. His hands, when she took them, were shaking. "Did it work?"

"It worked." She got her arms around him, there in the frame, metal and blood and burnt skin and the impossible smallness of him, and felt him hold on back with the full strength of everything he'd lost. "You held the door."

"Sixty seconds," he said into her shoulder. "I counted."

Behind them, far off in the dark where Kepler Station turned its broken beacon through a silence that no longer had a reason to exist, something began, very quietly, to come undone.

* * *

**Odile**

Odile watched the long-range feed and did not like what it showed, and made herself look anyway.

The anomaly was collapsing — inward, the way a debt collapses when it's finally called in. Kepler Station sat at its center like a coin at the bottom of a draining pool, and around it the masked zone was unmasking, star by star, as if a hand were lifting a cloth off the sky. Instruments that had lied for nine years were remembering how to tell the truth.

The station had maybe hours. Maybe less.

"Freya," she said into the ship-wide. "You'll want to see this."

On the feed, small and bright and already going, the light that Freya Dunmore had kept for nine years began, at last, to go out.
