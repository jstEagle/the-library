## 9. Thirty-Six Days

The Kestrel's galley smelled of recycled coffee and burnt insulation, and Odile Ferro had learned to do her worst thinking in it, because the flight deck was too honest a place for arithmetic you didn't want to face.

She spread the fuel manifest across the table like a hand of cards she already knew she'd lose. The paper was old habit — Tam kept everything digital, but Odile liked the weight of it, the way numbers looked smaller on paper than they did when they came out of an engine and became distance.

Thirty-six days of burn left. Not thirty-six days of life support, not thirty-six days of food — those were fine, those were generous. Thirty-six days of thrust. The only currency that mattered out here, where dead space didn't forgive and didn't negotiate.

She ran the calculation again because she had run it wrong twice before, and both times the error had been hope, sneaking into the decimals like water into a hull seam.

*Reach the relay: twenty-nine days at cruising burn.* She wrote it down. *Reserve to turn back from the halfway point: requires forty-one.*

The numbers sat there looking at her. They were very patient numbers. They would wait all day.

*You can go forward or you can come home,* they said. *You cannot do both.*

Odile poured coffee she didn't want and drank it anyway, because the bitterness gave her mouth something honest to do while the rest of her lied to itself. Through the galley's narrow viewport, dead space went on being dead — no charts, no beacons, no traffic, just the long dark that cartographers had given up on two centuries ago and salvage crews had been quietly picking apart ever since.

Behind her, the deck plating hummed with Tam's engine work. Somewhere forward, Junie was reorganizing the medbay for the fourth time this week, which was what Junie did instead of sleeping. And aft, in the converted cargo bay they'd grudgingly cleared for him, the linguist sat with his headphones on, translating ghosts.

Ferro had taken passengers before. Smugglers, surveyors, one memorable tax auditor fleeing three marriages. She knew the type: people running toward something or away from something, and the fare was the same either way. Zhou was both at once, which made him either the most complicated passenger she'd ever carried or the simplest — a man running toward and away from the same point in space.

She hadn't asked. That was policy. But policy was starting to chafe, because she'd watched him through the open cargo bay door last night, hunched over his console at 0300, whispering to himself in a language that sounded like rain deciding whether to fall, and she'd thought: *I know that look. I've worn that look. That's a man rehearsing an apology he'll never get to deliver.*

Her wrist comm chimed. Tam.

"Bridge," Ferro said.

"Captain, you need to see the flow numbers on the number two injector. And then you need to sit down."

"I'm already sitting down, Tam."

"Then hold onto something."

* * *

Tam had spread her own set of numbers across the flight deck, holographic this time, glowing blue and merciless above the nav console. She stood beside them with her arms crossed and her jaw set in the particular way that meant she'd already argued with herself and lost, and now needed Ferro to lose too so she wouldn't be alone in it.

"Injector two is running eleven percent rich," Tam said. "I can tune it, maybe claw back four, five points. But here's the shape of it." She flicked her fingers and the display rearranged itself into two arcs — one reaching toward a small amber marker labeled RELAY 7-DELTA, one curving back toward the thin line of charted space behind them. "Forward burn gets us there with six days of reserve. Six days, captain, sitting at a relay that's already dying, next to a reactor on a countdown we don't control. If anything goes wrong — if the survivors need a tow, if Voss's people are already stripping the place, if the injector eats itself — we don't have the fuel to come home. We become part of the salvage."

"And turning back?"

"Turning back means telling Zhou his signal dies unheard. Means telling whoever's on that seed-ship—" Tam stopped. Started again, flatter. "Means we go home, refuel, file a report, and some government committee decides whether those people are worth a rescue mission. You've read the history on the Ashkari, captain. How do you think that committee votes?"

Ferro looked at the two arcs. Forward and home. They crossed nowhere. That was the whole cruelty of it — no middle path, no clever third option hiding in the margins. Just a fork, and her hand on the switch.

"Junie's opinion?"

"Junie says we're not a rescue ship and we're going to get everyone killed." Tam's mouth twisted. "Junie also restocked the trauma kits yesterday. All of them. Doubled the coagulant stores. Junie's opinion and Junie's behavior are having a disagreement, and my money's on the behavior."

"And yours?"

"My opinion is that I'm the engineer, and engineers don't get opinions, they get tolerances." Tam tapped the display, and the forward arc pulsed. "But since you asked. My tolerance says we can make it there. My tolerance does not say we can make it back. Those are different failures, captain, and only one of them is mine to prevent."

Ferro nodded slowly. "You want me to say it out loud."

"I want you to say it out loud so that when it goes wrong, I remember you chose it with your eyes open. Not because I doubt you." Tam's voice dropped, and something older moved underneath it. "Because I've served under captains who chose things with their eyes shut. I'd rather be angry at you than grieve you."

There it was. The thing Tam never said directly, wearing its everyday clothes.

"Get some sleep," Ferro said. "I'll have an answer by second watch."

"You'll have an answer by the time you finish that coffee." Tam gathered her tools, paused at the hatch. "For what it's worth — and it isn't worth much — Zhou hasn't slept either. He's been running the signal analysis against the decay curve every hour. He knows the math better than we do. He knows what thirty-six days means." She shrugged. "He hasn't asked us to turn around once."

After the hatch cycled shut, Ferro stood alone on the flight deck with the two arcs burning their quiet blue accusation, and let herself remember the last time she'd stood at a fork like this.

* * *

The Meridian was a ore-hauler out of Ceres Verge, and Odile Ferro had been twenty-nine years old and second-in-command on her, and the Meridian had broken her in ways she still found pieces of in odd corners of herself, like shrapnel working its way toward skin.

It hadn't been dramatic. That was the thing nobody understood about cowardice — how quiet it could be. No alarms, no fire. Just a drive failure in deep space, nine souls aboard, and a launch shuttle rated for six. And a captain — her captain, a good man named Adeyemi who'd taught her everything except how to leave — standing on the bridge saying, calmly, *we draw lots*, and Odile looking at the lot in her own hand and knowing, with a certainty that felt like falling, that she was going to lie.

She'd traded slips with Reyes, the youngest of them, told him the drawing was done, told him his name wasn't called. He'd thanked her. That was the part that lived in her chest like a stone — he'd *thanked* her, gripped her arm with his cold kid hands and said *thank you, Ferro, thank you* — and she'd walked up the shuttle ramp carrying a lie shaped like mercy, and the Meridian's lights had shrunk behind them, and four days later the rescue tug had found the hulk and found Adeyemi and the others in the cold sections, and found Reyes among them, and found the log entries Adeyemi had written in the last hours, level and forgiving even then, describing how the crew had voted to give the shuttle seats to the injured rather than draw lots at all.

They'd chosen sacrifice. She'd chosen survival, and dressed it as fate, and a boy had died thanking her for it.

The inquiry had cleared her. That was the deepest rot of it — the universe hadn't even punished her. It had simply filed the truth away inside her body, where it accrued interest for twenty years.

She'd told herself a hundred stories since. That she'd believed the lots were drawn. That she'd panicked. That it didn't matter now, that everyone aboard was dust and memory, that guilt was a luxury for people with time on their hands. She'd built a whole career out of small ships and small jobs and staying exactly far enough from other people's emergencies that she'd never again have to weigh a life against a liter of fuel.

And then a man with shaking hands had walked onto her ship asking passage to a distress call in a dead language, and she'd said yes, and told herself it was just freight rates.

Ferro turned off the display. The two arcs vanished, but she could still see them printed on the dark of her vision, forward and home, crossing nowhere.

*You can save yourself or you can spend yourself,* the dark said. *Pick.*

* * *

She found Zhou in the cargo bay at end of second watch, exactly where she'd known he'd be. He'd fallen asleep at his console again, cheek on his forearm, headphones half-slipped, the waveform of the Ashkari signal scrolling above him in slow green pulses. Alive. Still alive. Fainter than last week — she could see it in the amplitude, the way you could see hunger in a child's wrists — but alive.

She stood in the hatchway longer than she meant to, watching him sleep the way people sleep when sleep is a defeat they've postponed as long as possible.

On his console, pinned under a data slate, was a scrap of actual paper — real cellulose, expensive as sin out here — covered in handwriting. She shouldn't have read it. She read it.

It was a translation, half-finished, the Ashkari glyphs rendered in careful romanization beneath. She couldn't read the source language, but the English draft was there, crossed out and rewritten:

*We count our days by the children's breaths. There are enough days if someone is coming. Please be someone coming.*

Below it, in smaller writing, almost hidden, as if he'd written it without deciding to:

*I am sorry. I am sorry. I am—*

The third *sorry* trailed off into nothing, unfinished, the pen lifted mid-word.

Ferro set the slate back exactly as she'd found it and stood there in the humming dark of the cargo bay with her heart doing something complicated and painful against her ribs.

*Please be someone coming.*

Well. Someone was coming. The only question — the question she'd been circling for six hours like a moth around a lamp — was whether *someone* meant a ship with enough fuel to matter, or a ship with enough fuel to arrive and then die alongside them, converting one tragedy into two.

Adeyemi would have drawn lots. Adeyemi would have put it to the crew, fair and square, and borne whatever came.

But Adeyemi was twenty years dead, and Odile Ferro had finally, at fifty-three, learned the difference between fairness and leadership. Fairness distributed the weight. Leadership decided where the weight belonged — and it belonged here, on her shoulders, where she'd spent two decades making sure nothing heavy ever landed again.

Self-preservation was a corruption, her first captain used to say. The slowest kind. It doesn't take you all at once; it takes you a gram at a time, until one day you look down and you're mostly hollow and the wind blows through you clean.

She'd been hollow a long time.

"Zhou." She said it quietly, then again, louder, and he surfaced from sleep with the disoriented violence of a man accustomed to waking into bad news.

"Captain. Is it the signal? Did it—"

"The signal's fine. Your signal." She pulled the spare chair over and sat, knees nearly touching his, close enough that retreat would be obvious. "I need to tell you what the fuel looks like, and then I need to tell you what I've decided, and you're going to listen to both before you say anything. Understood?"

His eyes went wide and steady. "Understood."

She told him. The injector, the arcs, the fork, the six-day margin, the impossibility of return. She watched him do the math in real time — she could see it happening behind his face, the decay curve of the signal laid against the burn curve of the Kestrel, two lines converging on a window that was closing — and she watched him arrive at the same place Tam had arrived, and Junie, and she herself, hours ago.

"We might all die out there," she said plainly. "Not might. Statistically, there's a real chance this ship becomes a tomb with excellent acoustics. Tam will tell you the same if you ask her straight. So I'm giving you the out, Zhou. Right now. We're still past the point where turning back costs us anything but pride. Say the word and I'll burn us home, and you can hate me comfortably from a bar somewhere for the rest of your life."

He didn't answer immediately. His hands were trembling — she'd noticed before that they always trembled, and she'd assumed it was nerves, but watching him now she understood it was something else, something more like a current running through him that had never found ground.

"When I was young," he said finally, voice rough with sleep, "my grandmother told me that in Ashkari, the word for *coward* and the word for *survivor* share a root. Something about — the same stem, different futures attached." He laughed, short and joyless. "I spent ten years proving the grammar right. I survived beautifully, Captain Ferro. I am the finest survivor you will ever meet, and everything I love is dead because of it." He met her eyes. "I'm not asking you to turn around. I'm asking you not to make me responsible for your choice, because everything I'm responsible for is already buried."

"There it is," Ferro said softly.

"What?"

"Honesty. First I've heard out here in months." She stood. "Here's my choice, then, and you can carry your share of it or none of it, that's between you and your grandmother. We go on. Full burn, tuned injectors, six-day margin, and if the margin fails, we fail *there*, in reach of the people we came for, instead of *here*, in comfortable charted space where nobody needs us." She headed for the hatch, then stopped. "One more thing. That translation on your console. *Please be someone coming.*"

His face went white. "I didn't mean for anyone—"

"Tell them," Ferro said, "that someone is coming. Tell them today. Before the window narrows. Because I've spent twenty years learning what silence costs, and I'm done paying retail."

She walked forward through the ship, past Junie's medbay with its doubled trauma kits, past Tam's engine room where the injector sang its slightly-rich song, all the way to the flight deck, where she strapped into the command chair and took the ship off auto-cruise with her own hands.

"Burn schedule change," she announced over the intercom, voice level. "Full transit burn effective immediately. We're going to the relay. All the way. Ferro out."

A pause. Then Tam's voice, dry as vacuum: "Copy, captain. Tuning injector two now. For the record — I'm angry at you already."

"Good," Ferro said, and found, to her genuine surprise, that she was smiling. "Get in line."

The Kestrel leaned into her burn, and the stars ahead stretched by fractions, and behind them the charted universe fell away like a shore receding.

In the cargo bay, Odile knew, a linguist was already composing words in a language of the dead, words that would travel at lightspeed across the killing dark to a ship full of rationing air and counting children: *Someone is coming. Hold on. Count slower.*

And somewhere in the black between here and Relay 7-Delta, in a region no map had ever bothered to name, the message went out — and thirty seconds later, faint and cracked and impossibly young, an answer came back.

Ferro heard it over the open channel, Zhou's translation trailing it like a shadow:

*We hear you. We will count slower. But hurry — the little ones are asking why the sky has stopped answering.*

The window was narrowing faster than any of them had calculated.
