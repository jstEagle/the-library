## 13. Twenty-Two Days

The sabotage had been elegant, which was the worst part of it.

Ivo stood in the relay's translation bay and looked at the routing matrix the way a man looks at a wound he cannot stop touching. Someone — Voss's people, almost certainly, though they'd been careful not to sign their work — had gutted the automated cross-lingual processors. Not destroyed them; that would have been crude, and crude things could be replaced from spares. Instead they had severed the calibration buses, pulled the contextual memory cores, and scattered the components through the relay's guts like a child hiding pieces of a broken clock in different rooms of a house. The machine still breathed. It simply could no longer think.

Which meant that every signal passing through this station now had to be translated by hand. By mind. By him.

"Tell me again what we're looking at," Odile said over the comm, her voice arriving slightly flattened by the ring's failing infrastructure, as if even her words were being rationed.

"The relay can still receive," Ivo said. "It can still transmit. What it can't do is decide *what* anything means. Every packet from the seed-ship comes in raw Ashkari, gets dumped into a buffer, and sits there like a letter in a dead man's drawer until I open it and read it aloud to a console that only speaks Standard." He touched the edge of the manual interface, its keys worn smooth by decades of hands that weren't his. "Twenty-two days of reactor power. Twenty-two days of messages. And each one has to pass through me."

"Can you do it?"

The question was honest, not cruel. Odile Ferro did not waste cruelty on people she intended to keep.

"I did it for six years during the war," Ivo said. "Faster than this. With worse sleep."

He heard her silence, and understood it. She was doing the arithmetic everyone aboard eventually did — the war, the translations, the settlements that burned because one man's voice had made their coordinates legible to people with weapons. She kept him anyway. That was either forgiveness or faith, and Ivo had stopped trying to determine which, because both felt heavier than guilt.

"Sleep when you can," she said finally. "Tam's rigging you a cot in the bay."

"I don't need—"

"That wasn't a question, Zhou."

* * *

The cot appeared that evening, wedged between two humming capacitor banks that radiated a dry, feverish warmth. Tam had also left a thermos of something caffeinated and foul, and a note in her blocky handwriting: *Drink it or I'll start singing at you.* He drank it.

The work settled over him like water filling a hull.

Every six hours, the seed-ship transmitted. Every transmission took him between forty minutes and three hours to route manually — parsing the Ashkari, holding its meaning steady in his mind while he keyed it into Standard for the relay's logs and for Odile's command channel, then reversing the process for anything outbound. In between, there were consortium intercepts to triage, Voss's traffic to monitor, the endless administrative chatter of a station dying in slow motion. Junie brought him food and stood over him until he ate it. Tam brought him tools he didn't ask for and watched him work with an expression he couldn't parse — hunger, maybe, or grief.

He lost track of days by the third one. The relay ring had no windows worth the name, and the lights ran on a maintenance cycle tuned to nobody's circadian rhythm. Time became a corridor of identical hours, and at the end of the corridor, always, the same door: the next transmission, the next buffer full of a language that lived now only in the mouths of starving people and in the ruined architecture of his own memory.

On the fifth night — he called it night because his body insisted, trembling, that there must be one — he caught himself answering Sefa's message before he had finished reading it.

That had never happened before. Translation was supposed to be a corridor too: source on one side, target on the other, and the translator walking carefully between, never touching either wall. But somewhere around hour ninety of wakefulness, the walls came down. He read the child's careful glyphs — *the air recyclers sing differently today, is that a word for fear or a word for weather* — and his hands were already moving on the console, already composing the reply, and the reply was coming out in both languages at once, interleaved, the Ashkari and the Standard braided together like double-stranded thread, and for one vertiginous moment he could not have said which language was the original and which the echo.

He stared at what he'd written. Then, slowly, he separated the strands, checked them against each other, found them true, and sent the message.

*Both are correct,* he told Sefa. *Fear is a kind of weather.*

* * *

By the ninth day, the languages began to trade places.

It started small. He reached for the Standard word for *capacitor* and found the Ashkari sitting there instead — *vessh-kel*, vessel-that-holds-lightning — and had to dig beneath it, down through strata of forgetting, to retrieve the mundane human syllables. Then it happened with *door*. Then with *yes*. His inner monologue, that constant narrator every person carries, began switching registers mid-sentence without announcement, so that his thoughts sounded like a radio drifting between stations, one voice fading in as another faded out.

He should have been frightened. Some part of him registered that — the old clinical part, the linguist who knew what semantic attrition looked like, who had written papers once about code-switching under cognitive load. That part observed him from a great distance, taking notes. *Subject exhibits involuntary alternation. Subject exhibits lexical substitution under fatigue. Subject exhibits—*

But the larger part of him, the part that had spent eleven years in exile deliberately letting his mother's tongue go silent in his mouth because every syllable of it felt stolen, that part felt something else entirely.

It felt like a door unlocking in a house he'd boarded up.

He worked. The transmissions came. He routed them. Somewhere in the walls of the relay, the reactor counted down its twenty-two days with the patience of all doomed machinery, and Ivo Zhou sat in the warm dark translating a living language back into the world, one exhausted hour at a time.

Junie found him asleep at the console on the eleventh day — actually asleep, cheek on the manual interface, fingers still resting on the keys — and rather than wake him, she dimmed the lights, draped a thermal blanket over his shoulders, and sat down across from him to watch the buffers herself. He woke forty minutes later to find her frowning at a status display she clearly didn't understand, and something in his chest turned over, painful and warm, at the sight of her stubbornly guarding a language she couldn't read.

"You were talking in your sleep," she said.

"What did I say?"

"No idea. Didn't sound like anything." She paused. "Sounded like water, maybe. Like a river going somewhere fast."

*Ashkari was a riverine language,* some distant academic corner of him noted. *High sonority. Open vowels. The settlers of the Ashkar delta built their phonology around the sound of current over stone.* He had written that in a monograph. The monograph had been cited in the planning documents. He closed that corner of himself and got back to work.

* * *

The dream came on the fourteenth day, and it did not ask his permission.

He had fallen asleep mid-transmission — a sin he'd promised Odile he would never commit, and which exhaustion collected on with interest. One moment he was rendering Sefa's report on the seed-ship's fuel reserves; the next, the console was gone and he was standing on the banks of a river he had never seen but knew completely, because he had spent his childhood hearing it described in a language his mother had sworn to him was dying and his government had ensured that she was right.

The river spoke Ashkari. Not metaphorically — the water itself articulated, each ripple a morpheme, the current's grammar carrying meaning the way his own tongue carried breath. *Come home,* the river said, in the formal register reserved for elders addressing children, and Ivo wept in the dream, standing in the shallows of a language he had helped murder, being invited back into it as if he were eight years old and innocent.

In the dream, his mother sat on the bank mending a net. She did not look up. "You're late," she said, in Ashkari, and the words were not accusation but simply fact, the way tides are facts, the way distance is a fact. "We waited. Now work."

And he knelt beside her and took up the net, and the net was made of sentences, thousands of them, knotted together, and some of the knots were ones he had tied himself during the war — coordinates disguised as courtesies, settlement names folded inside polite diplomatic phrasing — and his task, in the way of dreams, was to unpick every knot he had tied without breaking the net entire. His mother handed him knots. The river supplied the patience. He worked until his fingers bled light instead of blood, and the net grew whole, and far downstream he could hear voices — many voices, children's voices, Sefa among them — calling out readings from the mesh, testing each repaired strand, laughing when one held.

He woke with wet eyes and the console alarm shrieking an overdue transmission, and for eleven full seconds he could not remember a single word of Standard.

Then it came back, the way shore comes back to a swimmer, gradually and then all at once, and he keyed the console with shaking hands and finished the routing, and did not tell anyone what had happened, because how could he? *I dreamed in the language I sold. The language let me in anyway.* There was no protocol for that confession. There was no grammar for it in any tongue.

But that night — his body's night, the arbitrary dark he now observed — he slept again, and dreamed again, and this time the dream was smaller and stranger: he stood in the Kestrel's galley teaching Tam the word for *ember*, *sesh-va*, and Tam repeated it perfectly, and then asked him, in flawless Ashkari, what the word was for *homecoming*, and he opened his mouth to answer and woke before the word arrived, hunting for it even as his eyes opened, reaching for it the way you reach for a rung in the dark.

*Selu-ma,* it was. *The road that returns.* He lay on the cot saying it silently, over and over, guarding it like a coal.

* * *

Day seventeen. Day eighteen. The boundary dissolved entirely, and Ivo stopped fighting it.

He learned to work in the dissolve — that was the truth of it, the thing no training had prepared him for and no manual described. There was a state past exhaustion where the two languages stopped being two things at all, where meaning floated free of its clothing and he could dress it in whichever tongue fit best, and in that state his routing speed nearly doubled. Odile noticed. She said nothing, but he saw her noticing, saw her recalculate whatever private ledger she kept on him, adding a column whose heading he couldn't read.

Junie started leaving him notes in phonetic Ashkari, spelled wrong, endearing beyond measure. *Yuo are stil humman. Eat.* He corrected her spelling gently and kept every note.

Tam began sitting with him during the long routings, saying nothing, just present, her shoulder a few centimeters from his, and sometimes she would ask, very quietly, *how do you say* — and fill in the blank with something small. *How do you say grandmother. How do you say the song my mother half-remembered. How do you say I'm sorry I forgot.* And Ivo, who had believed for eleven years that he deserved no students, taught her, and discovered that teaching was a kind of penance that actually paid out, that returned more than it took, that left the teacher richer in the thing he'd thought he'd forfeited forever.

The reactor reached ten days. Then nine. The seed-ship's transmissions grew shorter — power rationed, air rationed, hope rationed into careful portions — and Ivo routed each one with the tenderness of a man handling origami made of skin. Sefa's messages were the ones he saved for last each cycle, a dessert of dread and grace. *The little ones practice your alphabet. They call it the tall language. Will you tell them a story in it?*

He told her the story of a fox who stole fire from a government office. It was not a traditional Ashkari story. He suspected she would forgive him.

* * *

On the twentieth day, running on perhaps nine hours of sleep across four nights, Ivo hit the wall at full speed.

It happened during a routine consortium intercept — Voss's traffic, dull logistics, nothing sensitive — and his mind simply misfiled reality. For four minutes he read Standard text as Ashkari, genuinely, completely, the human letters resolving in his vision into delta-glyphs, and he produced a translation of a supply manifest that was, he realized only afterward, a fluent and coherent rendering of a completely different document: a fragment of Ashkari maritime law he hadn't consciously recalled in fifteen years, surfacing through his hands as if the language itself had decided to use him as a mouthpiece while his guard was down.

He stared at the output. His heart slammed. Because the legal fragment was real, and it was relevant — it concerned salvage rights over vessels carrying living passengers, and it said, in the old delta jurisprudence, that *a ship with breathing souls aboard belongs to those souls, and to no one who finds them*, and Voss's consortium was at this moment claiming salvage rights over a ship full of children—

His hands were shaking too hard to key the console. He laughed instead, a cracked, unhinged sound in the empty bay — laughed because the language had come back to him not as punishment but as *ammunition*, had surfaced precisely when he needed it, had put the exact precedent into his head at the exact hour, and if that was coincidence then coincidence had a sense of justice he had never before been granted.

He transcribed the fragment carefully, both tongues side by side, verified it three times through the fog of his exhaustion, and sent it to Odile marked with the highest priority flag the dying relay could manage.

Then he sat back, and the exhaustion took its toll all at once, and the last thing he registered before sleep took him sideways off the cot was the sound of the bay door opening and Junie's voice, sharp with purpose, saying words he was too far under to catch:

"—found where Voss keeps the archives. I'm going in tonight."

He dreamed, in two languages, of a locked room full of voices waiting to be let out.
