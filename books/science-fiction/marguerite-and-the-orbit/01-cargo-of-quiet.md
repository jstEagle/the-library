## 1. Cargo of Quiet

The ore sang to me.

Not literally — I want to be clear about that, because the last thing a man in my position needs is a reputation for hearing voices in his cargo. It was the resonance of the hold, four hundred tonnes of unrefined iridium slugs shifting against their restraints as *Marguerite* came around the night side of Meridian, and every bump and hum of it arrived in my skull already translated into something clean and geometric. A lattice. A slow bright architecture of stress points and harmonics that meant, if you knew how to read it — and I did, God help me — that my cargo was secure and my ship was sound and nothing in the universe required me to say a single word about it.

Three years ago I would have heard the same sounds as noise. Now noise was the foreign country.

"Freighter *Marguerite*, this is Meridian High Control, you are cleared for approach on vector seven, confirm your transponder code."

I keyed the comm. "High Control, *Marguerite*, transponder Kilo-Nine-Oh-Four-Tango, vector seven confirmed." My voice came out level, pleasant, professionally bored. It always did. That's the trick nobody tells you about losing yourself: you get very good at performing the version of you that everyone expects. The voice is easy. The voice is just another language, and languages are the one thing I've never had trouble with.

I have trouble with everything they're attached to.

* * *

Let me tell you how I sleep, since sleep is where the trouble keeps its real office. I lie down in the narrow bunk behind the cockpit of a thirty-year-old ore hauler, and I close my eyes, and within minutes I am not dreaming so much as *solving*. There are no words in it. No faces, no rooms from my childhood, no anxiety about invoices. What there is instead is pattern: nested spirals turning inside nested spirals, a grammar made of angles, meaning arriving the way water arrives — everywhere at once, no edges. When I wake, the feeling lingers for a few seconds like a scent, and then it goes, and I'm left lying in the dark doing the one thing I can no longer do, which is remember what it felt like to dream in English.

I've tried explaining this to the fleet psychiatrists, both fleets' psychiatrists, actually, because when your orbit is contested you get your head examined twice. They ask questions like "Do you experience distress?" and I say no, and that's true. The dreams aren't frightening. They're better than my old dreams, honestly. My old dreams were about my mother's kitchen and an exam I failed in 2087. The new ones are beautiful.

What's frightening is the daylight. What's frightening is standing in a docking bay bar while a loader operator tells me a long joke about a colonial quartermaster and a crate of contraband cheese, and realizing I followed every word, caught every beat, could reproduce the joke verbatim in three dialects — and felt absolutely nothing, the way you feel nothing reading a menu in a language you don't eat. Fluency without residence. I know what everything means. I don't live anywhere in it.

So I smile, and I laugh at the right syllable, and I buy the round, because that's what competent, well-adjusted Tamsin Oyelaran does, and competence is the best disguise ever invented. Nobody interrogates a man who is good at his job. They thank him. They hand him another contract. They leave him alone in his ship with his beautiful wordless nights, which is, I suspect, exactly what he wanted, though he'd have a hard time proving he wanted anything at all.

* * *

Meridian hung outside my viewport like a marble someone had loved too hard — swirled white and blue-green, one whole hemisphere wrapped in cloud, the terminator crawling with the lights of a colony that was now eleven years old and still arguing with itself about whether it was a frontier or a home. Above it, in the high orbits, sat the reason my life had developed a background hum of dread: two fleets.

The colonial fleet kept to the inner shells, gray-green hulls, running lights in Meridian's colors. The Earth fleet held the outer shells, older hulls, Earth-blue beacons, close enough to see with the naked eye when the sun hit them right, like knives laid out on a table by someone who insisted they were just organizing cutlery. Between them ran the traffic lanes, the jump corridor mouth beyond both, and a great deal of diplomatic language about "deconfliction protocols," which is what diplomats call it when two armed men are standing very still in a small room.

I hauled ore through all of it, contract by contract, because ore doesn't care whose flag is overhead and neither, officially, do I. Unofficially, I cared the way you care about weather. It was there. You dressed for it.

"*Marguerite*, be advised, priority traffic in your lane, adjust plus zero-point-three degrees."

"Adjusting, High Control." I nudged the stick. Somewhere below, a colonial patrol frigate — the *Vigilant*, or her sister, they all had the same silhouette, like identical stern aunts — slid across my original vector six kilometers down. Everyone pretended this was routine. It was routine. That was the problem. Three months ago patrol frigates hadn't crossed commercial lanes to remind freighters they existed. Now they did it weekly, then daily, a slow escalation of presence that both fleets called "posture" and I called, privately, in the geometric privacy of my own skull, a closing spiral. Everything lately looked like a closing spiral if you'd spent enough time inside the signal's grammar. Maybe that was vanity. Maybe the universe really did rhyme.

I docked at Ring Station Meridian, berth 214, with the smoothness of eleven hundred dockings, and began the unload, and that is when the morning stopped being quiet.

* * *

The knock on my airlock came forty minutes into offload, three raps, official cadence. Through the port I saw two figures in colonial fleet blacks and a third in station security gray, and my stomach performed a small maneuver that I chose not to interpret.

I cycled the lock. The lead officer was a woman with a face like a filed-down rivet and a tablet held the way priests hold scripture. Her name tape read OKONKWO.

"Tamsin Oyelaran? Master and owner, *Marguerite*?"

"That's me. If this is about my transponder lag last month, I filed the appeal—"

"This isn't about your transponder." She stepped aboard without being invited, which is a thing fleet officers can do and station security cannot, and I watched the gray uniform stay politely in the lock like a dog trained not to follow. "Routine hold inspection. New directive. All independent tonnage entering colonial space gets a deep scan."

"Deep scan." I leaned against my own bulkhead, casual, a man with nothing but time and iridium. "Since when? My last four dockings were standard sweep."

"Since Tuesday." She didn't look up from the tablet. Behind her, the second officer — young, freckled, trying very hard to look like furniture — was already running a scanner wand along my cargo cradle with the reverence of a boy touching his first engine.

I ran the arithmetic without letting any of it reach my face. Hold contents: iridium slugs, manifests stamped, bills of lading clean, nothing in the false panels except emergency water and a bottle of actual Scottish whisky I'd been saving for an occasion that kept failing to arrive. I was clean. I was so clean it was almost insulting that they were scanning me. Which meant the scan wasn't about me at all. Which meant Tuesday had happened, whatever Tuesday was, and nobody had told the freighters.

"Rough week?" I asked Okonkwo, mildly.

She looked up then, and gave me the flat once-over that officers give civilians they've decided are either harmless or interesting, categories she clearly hadn't finished sorting me into. "You could say that. Forty-three kids on the surface woke up at three in the morning Tuesday, all saying the same words. Words that don't exist. Command's a little jumpy."

The hold went very quiet around me. Or I went very quiet inside the hold; the distinction has never been crisp.

"Kids talk nonsense," I said. "It's practically their job description."

"Not in unison." She turned back to her tablet. "And not in a phonology that matches nothing in the linguistic databases. Not," she added, and here she glanced at me again, longer this time, "in anything on file anywhere."

Somewhere in my chest, a lattice shifted. I thought of spirals nested in spirals, of meaning arriving like water, of a survey ship called *Calliope* and a signal that had poured through my skull for nine hours while the rest of the crew slept, and of the three years since, in which I had told precisely no one the full truth, because the full truth sounded like madness and got certified xenolinguists pulled from flight status, and flight status was the only place left where I knew who I was supposed to be.

"Fascinating," I said, and my voice did its level, pleasant, professionally bored trick, and I hated it a little, the way you hate a mask that fits too well. "Well. Scan away. Unless the children have taken up smuggling iridium, you'll find I'm the most boring man in orbit."

Okonkwo almost smiled. Almost. "Boring men are usually hiding the least," she said, which was generous of her, and wrong, and we both let it stand.

The scan took twenty minutes. The wand chirped over my slugs, my water, my whisky — the freckled officer raised an eyebrow at that, and I raised one back, and a fragile détente was established — and over the empty bay three, where there was nothing, where there had never been anything, and where I stood with my hands loose at my sides while the scanner's pulse ticked against my skin like a second heartbeat asking questions in a language I understood perfectly and refused, on grounds of self-preservation, to answer.

Because here is the thing I have never said out loud, the thing I keep folded flat in the geometric country where I sleep: I recognized the shape of what Okonkwo described before she finished describing it. Children speaking in unison, in a phonology matching nothing on file. I could have told her what the syllables would sound like. I could have told her they'd come in rising triplets, that they'd feel less like speech than like orientation, the way a compass needle feels north. I could have told her that whatever was happening to those kids on the surface at three in the morning had happened to me first, alone, three years ago, in the silent dark between stars, and that it had not finished with me yet.

Instead I said, "Find anything?"

"Clean," she said, and sounded faintly disappointed, and I understood that too — disappointment is just curiosity wearing a uniform.

"Glad to bore you." I walked them to the lock. As Okonkwo crossed the threshold she paused, half-turned, and said, "Oyelaran. You're rated xenolinguistics, aren't you? Calliope program, before your — retirement."

"Certified, yes. Lapsed. I haul rocks now. Rocks are honest."

"Mm." She looked at me for one second longer than courtesy required, and in that second I watched her file me somewhere — harmless or interesting, the sorting unfinished — and then the lock cycled and they were gone, and I stood alone in my hold surrounded by singing ore and the smell of cold metal, fluent in everything except myself.

That night, at berth 214, with the station's lights strobing soft through the port and two fleets holding their knives very still above the world, I lay down in my bunk and closed my eyes and waited for the spirals.

They came. They always come. But tonight they came differently — tonight, nested in the turning geometry, was something new: a triplet of rising tones I recognized, repeating, patient, insistent, like a name being called down a very long corridor.

Forty-three children were dreaming my dream.

In the morning, I told myself, I would decide whether to tell anyone.

I already knew I wouldn't.
