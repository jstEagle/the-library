## 14. Departure Window

The survey ship had a name once. Vesper found it stenciled under three layers of grime on the hull's port flank when she ran her palm along the plating at dawn: *Kestrel's Mercy*. She didn't remember naming it. She remembered flying it—eleven years ago, the survey runs that mapped Mirabel's magnetosphere and charted the debris shoals off the southern shelf—but the name felt like something another woman had chosen, back when she still named things.

She left the grime where it was. Some things you don't clean before you leave; you leave them so there's something to come back to.

The pad smelled of cold dust and oxidizing metal, the particular perfume of every morning she'd spent alone in the high country. But this morning was not alone. Amara was already aboard, running preflight in the copilot's seat with the methodical patience of a woman who'd taught herself avionics from colony manuals because the manuals were all there was. Through the open hatch Vesper could hear her reading checklists aloud to herself, her voice carrying that classroom cadence, each item given its full weight as though the ship might be graded.

Vesper stood on the pad and looked east, toward the beacon.

It caught the early light the way it always did—a thin silver needle against the pale sky, patient as a held breath. Nineteen days ago she would have looked at it and seen only her work. Now she saw what the children saw when they dreamed: a corridor of blue light, a voice counting backward, eleven doors standing open in the dark of the cryo-hold below it. The beacon wasn't just transmitting anymore. It was listening. And somewhere past the edge of dead space, something faint and stubborn was answering.

Nine days until the window closed. Six days of transit if the old drives behaved. No margin for sentiment.

"Vesper." Sef's voice, from the base of the boarding ramp.

She turned. The boy stood with his hands behind his back in a way she recognized—she'd taught him that stance herself, two years ago, without meaning to. It was how she stood when she wanted to look like she belonged somewhere. He was twelve and small for it, with his mother's dark eyes and a habit of watching everything twice, and he carried the maintenance kit against his hip like it weighed nothing, which it did not, which was the point.

"You're early," Vesper said.

"You said the ritual starts before first light." He climbed the ramp without waiting to be invited, because she'd taught him that too—that the invitation was implied in the showing up. "I checked the phase coupler last night. Third harmonic's still drifting half a degree per cycle."

"It'll drift worse before we're back. You'll compensate."

"I know." He set the kit down inside the hatchway, then seemed to realize he couldn't bring it, and picked it up again, and put it down again, and finally just rested his hand on it. "I wrote it all down. Everything. The draw anomalies, the harmonic corrections, the—"

"Sef."

He stopped. His jaw did the thing jaws do when someone is determined not to let anything show through it. She knew that jaw. She wore that jaw.

"The manual is the ship," she said. "But the ship isn't the beacon. The beacon is—" She paused, because she had never once said aloud what the beacon was, not even to herself, not in twenty years of tending it. "The beacon is a promise somebody made before either of us was born. Your job isn't to keep it running. Your job is to keep the promise. Do you understand the difference?"

He thought about it. She watched him think about it, actually watch the thought move through him like weather, and she thought: *this is what it would have been like*, and then she thought: *this is exactly what it is like,* and the second thought hurt more than the first.

"The promise is the people," Sef said slowly. "Not the light."

"The light is for the people. The people are the point." She crouched to his level, which she almost never did, because crouching meant looking up at the sky and she preferred looking down at instruments. "Eleven people are asleep under that beacon. In nine days they'll either have a name or they won't. While I'm gone, nobody goes into the hold. Nobody touches the pods. Not Havel's people, not the council, not anyone who says I sent them, because I won't send anyone. If the dream comes to you—the corridor, the counting—you write down what number it stops at. That's all. Write down the number."

"Why?"

"Because numbers are how they talk." She held his eyes. "And because if the count reaches zero before I'm home, I need to know I wasn't lied to about how long I had."

Something flickered across his face—not fear exactly, but the older sibling's arithmetic, the calculation of who else needed protecting while the adults were away. There were nine dreaming children now. Ten, counting the new family that had arrived at the settlement last month, a girl named Idris who'd woken screaming the corridor-dream her first night in her bunk and had refused ever since to sleep with the lights off, which in a colony where power was rationed was its own kind of protest.

"They'll be okay," Sef said. It wasn't a question and it wasn't quite a statement. It was a boy deciding to make it true by saying it.

"They'll be better than okay," Vesper said. "They'll be the reason."

* * *

Amara had the flight plan laid out on the console when Vesper strapped into the pilot's seat: a great lazy arc out of Mirabel's gravity well, skimming the magnetotail for the slingshot boost, then the long silent fall toward the jump threshold. The route was drawn in Amara's neat teacher's hand, transferred to the display but also inked on actual paper, folded once, tucked under the console lip like a talisman.

"You plotted it on paper," Vesper said.

"In case the display dies. In case everything dies. Paper doesn't." Amara settled her harness and pulled it snug with a practiced yank. "Also, I wanted to see the shape of it. Where we're going. You can't feel a route off a screen."

Vesper looked at her. Amara Okonkwo, forty-some years old, who had come to Mirabel eleven years after Vesper did, who had taught three generations of colony children to read and none of them to ask fewer questions, who had stood in front of the council six days ago and said, in a voice that did not tremble: *You want to examine the Nystrom problem? Fine. Examine it. But you'll examine it with her in the room, because she is not a problem, she is a neighbor, and if you can't tell the difference this colony has bigger troubles than dreams.* Who had then walked out of the closed session, found Vesper in the equipment shed behind the water reclamation plant, and said, quietly, "I know what it is to arrive somewhere running from a name. Take me with you or don't, but decide fast."

Vesper had decided in four seconds. It was the fastest decision of her life and the easiest.

"Feel the route," Vester repeated. "That's navigator talk."

"That's grandmother talk. My grandmother flew coastal mail runs on a world I've never seen, and she swore the sea had a shape you could hold in your hand." Amara smiled, small and crooked. "I never believed her. Then I came out here and learned what dead space feels like from the inside of a comm blackout, and I wrote her an apology letter I couldn't send."

Vesper ran the preflight sequence. Her hands moved through it without consulting her, eleven years of muscle memory waking up like old dogs hearing a familiar step on the stairs. Fuel pressure nominal. Reaction mass at ninety-four percent. The Kestrel's Mercy groaned once, deep in its spine, the sound an old ship makes when it remembers it can fly.

"Board is green," Amara said. "Your board, I mean. Mine's green too. We're very green."

"We're very late," Vesper said, and keyed the comm. "Mirabel Ground, this is survey vessel Kestrel's Mercy, requesting departure clearance, vector seven-seven-niner, window opens in four minutes."

Static. Then, unexpectedly, a child's voice.

"Kestrel's Mercy, Mirabel Ground." A pause, the sound of paper being consulted. "You are cleared for departure. Um. Wind is calm. Gravity is normal. Have a good trip." Another pause, longer. "This is Sef. I'm using the ground relay. Don't tell the council."

Vesper closed her eyes for one second—one second she could not afford and spent anyway—and opened them.

"Ground, Kestrel's Mercy. Understood. Tell the council whatever keeps the pad clear." She throttled up. "We're coming back, Sef. Keep the light on."

"Always," the boy said, and cut the channel before his voice could do anything embarrassing, which was exactly what she would have done.

* * *

They rose through Mirabel's thin gold morning and the colony fell away beneath them like a coin dropped into deep water.

Vesper kept her eyes on the instruments, because that was discipline, because that was who she had been for twenty years. But the forward viewport was wide, and wide viewports are honest, and so she saw it whether she meant to or not: the settlement strung along the river terrace like beads on a wire, the water reclamation plant glinting, the school with its solar roof angled south, the market square emptying as people climbed, all of them climbing, up the eastern ridge.

They had come to watch her leave. That was the thing she could not make herself look at directly, the thing she approached the way she approached live circuits—with insulated gloves and held breath. Twenty years she had built her invisibility plank by plank, and in nineteen days the children's shared dream had torn it open like wet paper, and now the whole colony stood on a ridge in the cold to watch her go, and she did not know what was in their faces at this distance and was afraid to find out.

"Look at them," Amara said softly. Not a command. An offering.

Vesper looked.

The ridge was black with people. Not a crowd—the word was wrong, crowds pressed and surged—but a line, a human horizon, dozens of figures standing shoulder to shoulder against the dawn wind. And at the crest, at the exact highest point, small and unmistakable even from altitude, a cluster of children holding something up. It took her a moment to resolve it against the brightening sky.

Lights. Hand lights, work lamps, the little glow-globes from the schoolroom ceiling that Idris had demanded stay on while she slept. The children were holding up lights.

"They know," Amara said. "Havel told everyone the beacon was a hazard. Told them you were a danger, a stranger, a thing that shouldn't be here. So they came out to say—" She stopped, and Vesper heard her swallow. "I don't think any of them planned what to say. I think they just came."

Vesper flew. She banked the Kestrel's Mercy onto the departure vector, seven-seven-niner, and as she turned, the ship's belly swept across the ridge and for one heartbeat the whole assembled colony was framed in the viewport, every face tilted upward, and the lights the children held swung together in a slow arc, tracking her, following her, a constellation of small hands refusing to let her leave in the dark.

Her throat closed. She had prepared for this departure the way she prepared for everything—checklists, contingencies, fuel margins, a sealed letter to the council in case she didn't return. She had not prepared for being watched with love by people whose names she mostly didn't know.

"Vesper." Amara's hand, brief and warm, on her forearm. "Fly."

"I'm flying."

"You're crying and flying. Both is allowed. Grandmother's rule."

"I'm not—" Vesper wiped her face with the back of one gloved hand, keeping the other on the stick, because some disciplines survive even this. "Fine. Both is allowed."

* * *

Mirabel shrank to a blue-green marble, then a coin, then a speck with a needle of silver beside it—the beacon, visible even from here, still transmitting, still promising. Sef was down there somewhere with the maintenance kit and the written manual and the promise, and the strangest part, the part Vesper kept turning over like a stone with something alive under it, was that she felt no fear about leaving it in his hands. Twenty years of guarding everything alone, and she had handed the whole of it to a twelve-year-old, and it felt less like risk than like relief.

Trust, she thought, was just loneliness that had finally found somewhere to put itself down.

The hours unspooled. They crossed the terminator into night, and Mirabel's stars came out hard and close, and Amara navigated by grandmother's rules—by feel, by the shape of the dark, calling out corrections minutes before the instruments confirmed them. Somewhere past the fourth hour Vesper realized she was talking. Actually talking, in sentences, unprompted—about the survey runs, about the year she'd spent calibrating the beacon's first harmonics, about a storm on the southern shelf that had lasted nine days and taught her more about patience than any teacher ever had. Amara listened the way good teachers listen, which is to say she made silence feel like a room you were safe in.

"—and that's when I understood the power draw wasn't a fault," Vesper said, and stopped, because she heard herself, heard the door swinging open on rooms she'd kept locked for two decades. "Sorry. You didn't sign on for twenty years of my monologue."

"I signed on for exactly that," Amara said. "Keep going. What was the draw feeding?"

Vesper hesitated. Then, because the ridge was behind her and the whole colony had held up its lights:

"A shadow account. Power siphoned, a trickle at a time, into systems the manifest doesn't list. Someone built a second network inside the beacon's bones, and they hid it so well that I coexisted with it for twenty years without seeing it. It's been warming something. Preparing something. And the dreams started the same month the draw spiked." She flexed her hands on the stick. "Whoever commissioned that beacon didn't just build a lighthouse, Amara. They built a lighthouse with a cellar. And I've been living above the cellar my entire adult life."

"And your mother built it."

"My mother built it." Saying it aloud still felt like stepping off a ledge. Three days ago, in the reclamation plant's steam-warm machinery room, she had told Amara everything—the manifest of ghosts, the genetic signature, the name in the commissioning fragment that matched the name on a navigator's license issued thirty-one years ago to a woman who flew into dead space and never flew out. *Nystrom, E.* Her mother. A lighthouse for anyone the corporation might someday discard. *Including me.*

"Then she hid the cellar from you too," Amara said gently. "Or she hid it *for* you. There's a difference, and I suspect it matters."

"To whom?"

"To whoever wakes up in those pods." Amara nodded at the nav display, at the thin line of their route stretching out toward the jump threshold and the silent reach beyond it. "Nine days, Vesper. Whatever's down there has been dreaming for decades. When they wake, they won't wake into your story. They'll wake into their own. The question is whether anyone will have made room for it."

Vesper was quiet for a long time. The drive hummed. The stars held still.

"I used to think I was the caretaker," she said finally. "That the backups were cargo. Eleven copies of me, sleeping, and my job was to keep them cold and keep them secret and never ask why." She shook her head. "But the diverged one—the pod that changed—it asked for standing. *Let us argue our own case.* Not replacement. Standing. And I keep thinking about the ridge back there, all those people holding up lights for a woman they barely know, and I think—" Her voice caught. She let it. "I think maybe family isn't the people who share your face. Maybe it's the people who show up on the ridge."

Amara reached over and folded the paper flight plan into a smaller square, and a smaller one, and tucked it into her breast pocket, over her heart, like the talisman it was.

"Then we'd better get home," she said. "You've got a lot of ridge to answer for."

* * *

The jump threshold announced itself the way thresholds always do—not with a sight but with a silence. At 0300 ship-time, every ambient signal in the cockpit died at once: no carrier wave, no chatter, no whisper of Mirabel's beacon riding the long frequencies. The array went quiet as a held breath. Dead space began where the noise ended, and they crossed the line at 0304, and Vesper felt it in her teeth.

Amara felt it too. She sat up straighter. "There it is," she murmured. "Grandmother's sea."

For six days they would fly through the silent reach, where no beacon answers and no rescue follows and the only law is the one you carry with you. Vesper had crossed it twice before, both times alone, both times feeling like the last lit thing in the universe. This time the copilot's seat was warm and the paper flight plan rode over someone's heart and the colony's lights were still burning behind her, impossibly far away, tended by a twelve-year-old who kept promises.

She should have felt afraid. Instead she felt something she had no checklist for: the specific vertigo of leaving a place knowing, for the first time in her life, that she wanted to return to it. Wanting things was dangerous. Wanting things gave the universe leverage. She did it anyway.

On the sixth hour of the crossing, with Amara asleep in shifts and the cockpit lit only by instrument glow, the long-range array woke up.

Not with noise—with pattern. A carrier wave, faint as breath on glass, rising out of the directionless dark ahead: the relay, still transmitting, still counting down the arbitration window, its signal decayed to a thread. Vesper logged it, triangulated it, marked it. On schedule. Nine days minus six days' transit left them three days of margin, and the margin was the whole game.

Then, beneath the relay's thread-thin carrier, the array resolved something else.

A second signal. Deeper in the dark, farther out than the relay, moving. It wasn't broadcasting to them. It was broadcasting to the relay—or rather, it was *answering* the relay, call and response, a handshake protocol Vesper recognized because she had seen its signature buried in the beacon's hidden cellar network. Corporate encryption. Reclamation-class transponder codes.

Someone else was coming to the arbitration. Someone with paperwork.

Vesper sat very still in the quiet cockpit, watching the second signal crawl across her display, and did the arithmetic she had been avoiding since the day she decrypted the manifest. If the beacon had been commissioned by a corporation, then the corporation had records. Records of eleven backups flagged to a shadow account predating the colony charter. Property, on their ledgers. Property, expiring.

The signal pulsed again, patient and cold, and Vesper understood that the race had just changed shape. It was no longer only about reaching the window in time.

It was about who else was already reaching for it.

She keyed the intercom softly. "Amara. Wake up. We have company."
