## 3. Words Older Than Grief

The beacon woke her at 0347 station time, which was a fiction of a clock and a lie of a morning, but the beacon did not care. It had never cared about anything before.

Astrid Fairweather came out of sleep the way she always did—slowly, with one hand already reaching for the soil monitor on her nightstand, checking that Bay Four's nitrogen fixers hadn't thrown another tantrum—and it took her three full seconds to understand that the sound in her ears was not an alarm she knew.

It was singing.

Not music, exactly. A patterned tone, rising and falling in long patient phrases, like something enormous breathing through a flute. It came from everywhere at once because Verdance was small and the speakers were old and the station had only ever had one voice: hers.

"Okay," she said to the ceiling, to no one, as she said most things. "Okay, you're new."

She pulled on her coveralls without turning on more lights than she needed, which was a habit from her first year, when every shadow in the hydroponics bays had seemed like a person. Nine years later the shadows were just shadows, but the habit stayed, the way habits do—outliving their reasons like heirlooms nobody remembers the origin of.

The beacon chamber sat at the heart of the station, a cathedral of somebody else's architecture. The Founding Council had built Verdance around it the way medieval towns built around wells: everything else functional, replaceable, but this one thing sacred and untouchable. The beacon itself was a column of dark alloy taller than Astrid, older than her manuals, humming its single note into the dark for longer than anyone living could account for. Her job description—one page, laminated, written by people who had signed off and died or drifted away long before she ever arrived—said *maintain transmission integrity*. That was all. Keep the lighthouse lit. Don't ask what the ships are for.

The beacon was not humming tonight. The beacon was *talking*.

Astrid stood in the doorway with her hair uncombed and her heart doing something embarrassing, and watched the diagnostic displays scroll data in formats she'd never seen. The transmission log—the one that had recorded nothing but the same carrier pulse for nine years, 3,285 days of faithful monotony—was filling with entries. Outbound entries.

"It's replying," she said aloud, because saying things aloud was how she kept herself company, and because some part of her hoped the station would correct her if she was wrong.

Nothing corrected her. The beacon sang its long phrases into the black, paused, listened, and sang again. Call and response. Question and answer. She had spent nearly a decade tending a garden for people she'd never met, and now, in the smallest hours of a fictional morning, the station had started a conversation behind her back.

*With whom?*

That question had teeth. She felt them.

* * *

By 0600 she had made tea she didn't drink and pulled up every manual, schematic, and maintenance record the station possessed, which was not many. The beacon's technical documentation was thin in the way that redacted things are thin—not missing pages so much as pages with the middle scooped out, leaving intact beginnings and endings around a hollow. *Transmission protocols per Council Directive 7-something-something*, the files said, and then a smear of classification bars, and then *routine verification required quarterly.*

Nobody had verified anything quarterly in nine years. Nobody had told her there was anything to verify.

"You could have mentioned you were bilingual," she told the beacon. It sang on, serene, unbothered by accusation. She envied it.

The incoming signal—the thing it was replying *to*—was faint, arriving in bursts, riding frequencies she hadn't known the array could receive. She isolated a fragment and played it through the chamber speakers, and the sound filled the room: those long tonal phrases again, but rougher, degraded, a voice calling up from under deep water. Human-made. She was almost sure it was human-made. Machines didn't usually sound this *tired*.

And underneath the tiredness, something familiar. Not the words—she didn't have the words—but the shape of the language. The cadence of it snagged on something in her memory the way a thorn snags wool.

She went looking for the thorn.

* * *

The linguistic archives were her favorite guilty corner of the station database. Early in her tenure, lonely enough to be dangerous to herself, she had downloaded every cultural module the colony preparation package included—language primers, music libraries, histories, children's readers—all of it intended for the sleepers' education when they woke. She had justified it as curriculum review. Really, she had wanted voices. Any voices. She had learned fragments of six languages from modules designed for people who weren't born yet, reciting vocabulary to seedlings because the seedlings were better listeners than the walls.

One module was flagged differently from the rest. *Old Meridian*, the label read, followed by a classification bar and a note in cold administrative prose: *Heritage language. Retention optional. Instruction discouraged per Directive—* and then the smear again. Everything came back to the smears. The whole station was Swiss cheese of redaction, holes where meaning used to live.

She opened it anyway. Discouraged, she had learned over nine years, mostly meant *nobody will stop you, but we want it on record that we frowned.*

The primer loaded. A synthetic voice spoke its first lesson, and Astrid's arms broke out in gooseflesh.

It was the shape. The exact shape of the signal. The rising interrogative lilt, the long vowels held like held breath, the way sentences fell at the end like someone setting down something heavy. Old Meridian. The dead language of a dead polity, extinct since the Fracture—she knew that much from general history, everyone did; Old Meridian was the tongue of the before-times, taught in history holos the way you'd teach about dinosaurs. Beautiful, mournful, gone.

Except it wasn't gone. It was calling her beacon from somewhere in the dark, and her beacon, her mute, faithful, nine-years-silent beacon, was answering it fluently.

"Oh," she said softly. "Oh, you *liar*."

She meant the beacon. She possibly meant the Council. She possibly, in some small ashamed corner of herself, meant the whole tidy story she'd been telling herself for nine years—that she was a gardener, that this was a garden, that the universe was the size of her duties.

She got to work.

* * *

Decoding was slow, tender labor. The archived primer gave her phonemes and basic grammar; the signal gave her speed and grief. She sat cross-legged on the floor of the beacon chamber with a datapad balanced on her knee, cross-referencing phrase structures, building a lexicon the way she built anything: patiently, imperfectly, one piece at a time, talking to herself through the hard parts.

*"Kesh-vara,"* she sounded out. The primer glossed it as *still*—persistence, continuation, the verb you'd use for embers or hope. In the signal it appeared over and over, anchoring every transmission like a heartbeat. *Still. Still. Still alive.*

Her chest tightened. She wrote it down anyway, because the work was the work, and work was how she kept her hands from shaking.

*"Dua-meridian."* Second Meridian. But *meridian* in Old Meridian wasn't just a place-name—it meant *the crossing*, the passage between one state and another. And paired with *dua*—

She stared at the screen for a long moment.

Second ark.

There was no second ark. There was *Verdance*, there were her sleepers—eleven thousand pods in the deep vaults, her unborn orchard-people, the reason she pruned and logged and talked to herself—and the whole architecture of her purpose rested on the fact that they were the cargo, the only cargo, the precious and singular remnant. The histories were clear. The Fracture had scattered humanity; the Founding Council had gathered what it could; one ark, one destination, one chance. She was the keeper of the only seeds.

But the signal said *second ark* the way you'd say your own name. Familiarly. Desperately. *We are the dua-meridian. We are still. We are still.*

She pulled the next phrase apart letter by letter, and when it resolved she had to set the datapad down on the cold floor and press both palms flat against the alloy, because the floor was real and solid and the word was not.

*"Mersi."*

Mercy.

They were asking for mercy. From her beacon. From her station. From—her stomach turned slowly over—from *her*, although they couldn't know she existed, couldn't know anyone was listening. They were broadcasting into the dark in a forbidden language to a machine that should have been dead, asking the oldest question in any tongue: *is anyone there, and will they be kind?*

Astrid Fairweather, who had spent nine years being kind on schedule, who watered on Tuesdays and pruned on Thursdays and narrated weather reports to cryo-pods, found that she did not know the answer.

* * *

She worked until station noon without noticing, and it was the smell that finally broke her trance—soil and green, the greenhouse breathing its usual breath through the vents, reminding her that the living needed tending regardless of revelations. She went, because the tomatoes didn't care about archaeology, and because her hands needed something honest to do.

Bay Three was mid-harvest rotation. She moved down the rows on autopilot, checking moisture wicks, thumbing aphids off leaf undersides, and it was there, between the gene-tailored wheat and the dwarf citrus, that the awe finally curdled.

Because here was the thing she couldn't stop assembling, the picture building itself piece by piece whether she consented or not: if there was a second ark, then the story was wrong. Not embellished—*wrong*. The Council's history, the clean narrative she'd been hired into, the whole moral architecture of *we saved what we could*—it had a twin. A hidden sibling. And you didn't hide a shipload of survivors unless there was something about them somebody didn't want found.

And her beacon—her sweet, monotonous, nine-years-faithful beacon—had known. Had been *waiting*. Had kept the light burning not as a lighthouse but as a *door*, and had answered the knock the moment it came, without consulting her, without pausing, as if she were irrelevant. As if the gardener were furniture.

"I named a tomato after Pod 4471," she told the citrus tree, apropos of nothing, because that was the thought that surfaced and she had long since stopped editing herself for plants. "I talk to them. I tell them about the weather." The tree offered her a small unripe orange and no wisdom. "And apparently I've been working in a post office for a war I don't know about."

The word *war* dropped into her mind uninvited and sat there, heavy.

She didn't know that either. Mercy-seekers weren't necessarily enemies. Redactions weren't necessarily crimes. She was a woman standing in a greenhouse constructing entire catastrophes out of four decoded words, and she knew it, and she did it anyway, because the alternative was the silence, and after nine years she knew exactly how loud silence could get.

*Still alive. Second ark. Mercy.*

Three phrases. Enough to change everything, not enough to explain anything. It was like finding a stranger's letter half-burned in your own fireplace.

* * *

In the end she did the only responsible thing available to her, which was the thing she was worst at: she waited. She ran the signal analysis again, and again, confirming direction, strength, repetition interval. The source was distant—a long burn away, weeks even for a fast ship, and she had no fast ship. The transmissions repeated every eleven hours, each cycle identical except degrading slightly, a voice growing hoarse.

Eleven hours. Which meant she had eleven-hour intervals in which to decide what a person was supposed to do with a message from the drowned.

At 1900 she stood in the beacon chamber with her hand flat against the dark alloy column, feeling its hum through her palm like a sleeping animal's pulse. It had been transmitting for thirty-one hours. It showed no sign of stopping. Whatever directive drove it, it was patient and absolute, and it had never once, in nine years, asked her opinion.

"I'm not angry," she lied to it gently. "I'm just—recalibrating."

The beacon sang its reply into the dark. *Still. Still. Still.* Somewhere out there, something broken and human heard it and endured another eleven hours.

Astrid looked at the redaction bars smeared across her screen—Directive this, Council authorization that, the same institutional handwriting that had written her job description and buried her questions before she'd thought to ask them—and felt the first crack go all the way through, not in the files, but in her.

Nine years of trust is a structure. You don't hear it break. You just notice, afterward, that the room is colder.

She queued the signal logs for deeper decryption, wrapped her hands around a cup of tea gone bitter hours ago, and said, to the beacon, to the sleepers, to whoever was listening in the dark:

"Hold on. I'm coming to find out what you are."

Far overhead, at the edge of the system's charted dark, a relay node she had never been told about quietly forwarded Verdance's entire transmission log to coordinates that appeared in no manual she owned.

Somewhere, something began paying attention.
