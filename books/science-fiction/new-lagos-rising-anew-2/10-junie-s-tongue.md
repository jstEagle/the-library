## 10. Junie's Tongue

Junie worked the way she did everything else: with her sleeves pushed up, a stylus behind each ear, and a cup of tea going cold beside her because tea was for people who believed in finishing things.

She had claimed the corner of Verdance's medbay that Astrid had cleared for her—two days of moving seedling trays and apologizing to plants—and turned it into something between an archive office and a nest. Data slates fanned across the cot. Printouts, actual paper printouts that Okafor had grumbled about wasting toner on, were pinned to the wall in overlapping constellations. Red string would have been too much, but only just.

"Talk to me," Ilya said from the doorway. He had a habit of announcing himself with demands rather than greetings, which Astrid had learned was his version of shyness.

"I'm working."

"You've been working for eleven hours. You ate half a ration bar at hour six and then argued with it."

"It was dry." She didn't look up. Her fingers moved across the slate in quick, precise strokes, and her lips moved too, silently shaping syllables that belonged to no language anyone on Kestrel Dawn had spoken aloud in thirty years.

Ilya came in anyway, because of course he did, and sat on the edge of the cot with the careful weight of a man who knew better than to touch anything. "Okafor wants a status report by end of shift."

"Tell him the status is *shut up, I'm close*."

"That's not a status."

"It's the truest one I've got." She finally looked up, and Ilya saw what eleven hours had done to her: the dark circles, yes, but under them something lit, something that hadn't been there when they'd first heard the distress signal crackle out of Old Meridian like a voice from a drowned country. "The linguistic modules Astrid pulled are good but they're academic. Textbook Meridian. My grandmother's Meridian had elbows. It swore. It sang off-key. The beacon's reply used the textbook version, which means whoever built it learned the language secondhand—but the *distress archive*, the thing we're answering? That's native. That's home."

"You can read it."

"I can *almost* read it." She held up a hand before he could say anything. "Almost is doing heavy lifting. There's dialect drift—the ark's been transmitting for decades, maybe generations, and languages rot when nobody's around to correct you. But it's my grandmother's grammar underneath. You don't forget the shape of your mother's hands just because they get old."

Ilya was quiet a moment. "Your grandmother," he said. "You never talk about her."

Junie turned back to the slate. "Because she died teaching me this language in secret, in a storage closet on the ark, while the Council's loyalty officers ran literacy checks two decks up. Because the last thing she said to me before they put me under was *keep it alive, little bird, even if you're the only one*. And because if I start talking about her I will cry, and I am *this close*—" she pinched her thumb and forefinger a millimeter apart— "*this close* to understanding what these people have been trying to tell us for forty years."

*These people.* Not *the second ark*. Not *the rival ship*. Somewhere in the last eleven hours, Junie had stopped decoding a document and started listening to voices.

Ilya stood. "I'll bring you real food. And when you're ready—"

"I'm ready now." She said it fast, almost startled by herself. Then slower: "No. Wait. One more pass. Give me twenty minutes. If I'm right about the structure, the last segment isn't a distress call at all."

"What is it?"

"A census."

* * *

Astrid found them all crammed into the medbay an hour later—Okafor filling the doorway because Okafor always filled doorways, Ilya perched on the supply cabinet, and Junie standing at the center of her paper constellation with a marker in her hand like a conductor's baton.

She'd slept, or at least lain down for ninety minutes with her eyes closed, and it had steadied her. But there was a tremor in her voice when she began, and none of them pretended not to hear it.

"The Verdance beacon received its first transmission from the second ark forty-one years ago," Junie said. "Not a distress call. A *greeting*. They'd detected the beacon's carrier wave, matched it against their own commissioning records, and realized they weren't alone out here. Their message was basically: hello, sibling ship, we thought you were a story."

"And the beacon answered?" Astrid asked.

"The beacon answered with a denial. Standard Founding Council protocol fragment: *no second ark exists, cease transmission, your signals constitute contamination*." Junie let that sit. "They kept transmitting anyway. For four decades. Greetings, status reports, requests for coordination on the jump window. Every single one answered by the same automated refusal. Imagine knocking on your sister's door for forty years and having a machine tell you that you were never born."

Okafor's arms were crossed, his jaw working. "The distress ping we caught. The one that reached us through Verdance's relay."

"That one was different. That one was *aimed*. Someone aboard the second ark finally figured out how to route around the beacon's filter—bounced their signal off relay debris, disguised the header as maintenance chatter. It got through to us because *we* answered it, not the machine." Junie uncapped the marker. "And here's what they wanted us to know. Here's the census."

She wrote three numbers on the wall, large enough for everyone to see.

"Total souls aboard the second ark—she's called *Amadahy*, river-of-the-water in an older tongue still—as of last transmission: nine hundred and fourteen in cryosleep. Two hundred and six awake, running shifts, keeping the ship alive across the generations." The marker paused. "And sixty-one children born en route. Born awake. Never frozen. Never asked."

Nobody spoke. The greenhouse hummed faintly through the bulkhead, that sound Astrid had lived inside for nine years until it had become indistinguishable from silence.

"Born en route," Ilya said carefully. "That's—I woke up alone on a dead ship and I thought that was the worst hand the Fracture could deal. Those kids were *born* to the crossing. Whole childhoods spent in transit corridors."

"The Council had a word for them," Junie said, and her voice changed—not softer, harder, the way steel changes when it goes from forge to blade. "I found it in the beacon's own files, cross-referenced against the refusal protocols. The official designation for children conceived during transit on unauthorized arks." She wrote it on the wall beneath the census: **CONTAMINANT GENERATION**. "Their existence was classified as a genetic integrity risk. The Council's directive—which our beacon has been faithfully executing since before any of us were frozen—was to deny them, refuse them, and if necessary, ensure they never reach the new world. Because they weren't part of the chosen line. Because they were proof that humanity could survive without being *managed*."

"They're children," Astrid said. It came out barely above a whisper, and she hated how small it sounded, so she said it again, louder, with nine years of talking to sleepers behind it. "They're *children*. They were born on a ship in the dark and the crime they committed was being born."

"Yes," Junie said simply.

"And the Council wrote them off. Before they existed. As a category."

"As contamination." Junie's marker hand was shaking now, and she gripped it tighter. "My grandmother taught me our language in a closet because the Council decided our tongue was contamination too. Our names. Our songs. Anything that remembered who we were before the Founding Council decided who we should be. I used to think that was ancient history. Old grief. Something that happened to other people a long time ago." She looked around the room—at Okafor, at Ilya, last and longest at Astrid. "It's happening right now. It's been happening the whole time we've been arguing about fuel mass and orbital windows. We've been doing survival math about whether sixty-one children get to exist, and we didn't even know we were doing it."

* * *

The argument started, as their arguments tended to, with Okafor being practical.

He'd taken them to the mess—a real table, real chairs, the ritual of it mattering more than the food—and laid it out flat, captain-style, no varnish. "We have a window that fits one ship. We have two arks. Everything we decide flows from that, and pretending otherwise is how good crews die. So. Options."

"Take the second ark's fuel," Ilya said immediately. "Verdance's sleepers get through. Nine hundred plus souls versus two hundred awake and sixty-one kids. The math—"

"The math," Junie cut in, "is exactly what the Council wants you to do. Did you hear yourself? *Contaminant generation.* You just used their arithmetic."

"I used *orbital mechanics*—"

"You used their arithmetic wearing orbital mechanics' coat."

"Enough." Okafor said it quietly, which was worse than shouting. He rubbed his face with both hands. "I've run salvage fifteen years. I've made triage calls that keep me up at night, and I'll make more, because that's the job. But I want to be honest with myself about what we're choosing, so let's lay all three cards down. One: we take their fuel, save our sleepers, live with it. Two: we warn them, they burn for the window, they probably lose—slower ship, less fuel—and we watch it happen. Three: we stay silent, take the window ourselves, and let them drift until the Council's beacon forgets they exist. Which, apparently, it already has."

"Option four," Astrid said.

Every head turned. She hadn't meant to speak. The words had come up out of her the way water comes up through soil, slow and then all at once.

"There's no option four," Okafor said gently. "One window. One mass. That's physics, Astrid, and physics doesn't do sentiment."

"The beacon does." She stood, palms flat on the table. "I've been living with that machine for nine years. It draws power it shouldn't need. It holds commissioning records redacted so deep they're practically geological. It speaks a dead language fluently and lies in it. Whatever the beacon actually *is*, it is not a lighthouse. And a machine that lies about one thing—about *sixty-one children*—is lying about everything, including the window. Including what fits through it. I don't accept the premise. I won't. Not until I've read every file on that station with my own eyes."

Silence. Then Junie, softly: "The gardener's finally asking what the garden is *for*."

"The gardener's furious," Astrid said, "and fury is a much better fertilizer than despair."

That got a broken laugh out of Ilya, and even Okafor's mouth twitched before he mastered it. He looked around the table at his crew—at the medic who'd found her grandmother's voice in a dead machine's refusal, at the engineer who'd woken alone and couldn't bear the thought of doing it to others, at the woman who'd talked to seeds for nine years because she believed things deserved tending before they'd proven they deserved it.

"For the record," Okafor said, "the vote isn't today. Today we gather information. Tomorrow we decide. That's not cowardice, that's procedure." He pointed a thick finger at Astrid. "You get your files. All of them. Ilya goes with you—if that beacon's hiding something big enough to change the math, I want an engineer's eyes on it."

"And me?" Junie asked.

"You," Okafor said, "keep translating. Every word those people sent, I want it in front of me. If we're going to weigh lives, we're going to weigh *people*, not numbers. Names if we can get them."

Junie nodded once, and something passed over her face that Astrid recognized from her own mirror: the moment when grief stops being a weight you carry and becomes a job you do.

* * *

Later, past the hour the station's lights dimmed to night-simulation, Astrid found Junie in the greenhouse.

Not the medbay, not her paper constellation—the greenhouse itself, standing among the gene-tailored orchards with her hands loose at her sides, looking at the seedlings the way pilgrims look at relics.

"I name them after the pods," Astrid said, coming up the row. "It started as a joke, year two. Now it's just what they're called. This one's Pod 4471—her name is Mercy, according to the manifest. This one's Pod 2210. His name is Dawit."

"Dawit." Junie tried the shape of it. "That's a Meridian name."

"It's a lot of the manifests, actually. I never wondered why. I should have wondered why." Astrid stopped beside her. "Forty years of transmissions, Junie. Forty years of greetings, and the machine answered *no second ark exists*. What did the people send back? After the refusals. What do you send after forty years of that?"

Junie was quiet a long moment. In the grow-lights, her face looked younger and older at once.

"The last transmission—the one that got through to us—it ends with a phrase my grandmother used to sing. Literally sing, off-key, while she cooked. It means..." Junie's voice caught, steadied. "It means *we planted anyway*. No rain, no permission, no guarantee. We planted anyway. That's what they sent us. Sixty-one children and two hundred waking hands and a whole generation the Council called contamination, and their message to the universe is *we planted anyway*."

Astrid looked down the long green rows—nine years of her life, pod-named and pruned and loved in the specific fierce way you love things you may never see fulfilled—and felt something settle in her chest, hard and clear as a seed casing.

"Then we'd better find out what this station really is," she said, "before the window opens. Because I am not letting a machine decide who counts as harvest and who counts as weeds. Not ever again."

Behind them, deep in the station's bones, the beacon cycled—its carrier wave shifting pitch for a fraction of a second, like something vast turning over in its sleep.

Neither of them noticed.

Yet.
