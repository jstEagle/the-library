## 11. Fuel Is Blood

The derelict hung in the dark like a drowned cathedral.

Okafor had seen a hundred wrecks in his years on the salvage circuit, and he had a private taxonomy for them. There were *clean* wrecks—ships that died quick, systems dark, hulls intact, nothing aboard but vacuum and regret. There were *dirty* wrecks, full of tumbling debris and frozen coolant and the occasional corpse doing a slow, patient orbit of the bridge. And then there were wrecks like this one: the *Providence's Mercy*, a colonial fuel hauler, nine hundred meters of scarred plating and dead engines, still holding, against all reason and several laws of statistics, a whisper of atmosphere in its lower tanks.

"Say that again," Junie said over comms, "but slower, because I refuse to have heard you correctly."

"Residual volatiles in the transfer manifolds," Okafor said. He was in the cockpit of the Kestrel's boarding skiff, watching the Mercy's hull crawl past the viewport, pocked with micrometeorite scars, one cargo pod torn open like a sardine tin. "If the containment held even partially, there could be refined jump fuel in the reserve bladders. Enough to matter."

"Enough to matter," Ilya repeated, from the seat behind him, in the flat tone of a man doing arithmetic he didn't like. "We need four thousand liters to make both arks jump. You're telling me there might be—" he squinted at the sensor readout, "—maybe six hundred. Maybe. If the bladders didn't crystallize. If the seals didn't fail. If the ghosts of the original crew don't object."

"Then it's six hundred liters we didn't have yesterday," Okafor said. "We'll find the rest. We always find the rest."

"We have never once found the rest," Junie said. "We have found *some*. Some is not the rest."

Okafor smiled, because that was what a captain did, and because the alternative was letting them see the arithmetic he did in the dark hours, the one where four thousand liters stood between two arks full of sleeping strangers and a jump window that did not care about anyone's feelings.

"Skiff's going in," he said. "Junie, you're on the Kestrel, monitoring pressure and radiation. Ilya, you're with me."

"Why am I always with you on the creepy ones?"

"Because you complain the least."

"I complain constantly."

"You complain *quietly*. It's a gift. Come on."

* * *

The Mercy's airlock accepted Ilya's override code with a sigh that sounded, to Okafor's ear, almost relieved. Ships were like that. Left alone too long, they got lonely.

Inside, the hauler was a museum of somebody's interrupted life. A pressure suit hung in the docking bay, arms out, as if its owner had stepped out of it mid-sentence. A child's drawing was still taped to a locker door—a lopsided sun over a lopsided ship, crayon colors gone pale with radiation bleaching. Someone had written *BACK SOON* beneath it in a language Okafor didn't recognize, the letters soft and looping, and something in his chest went tight and stayed tight.

"Meridian script," Ilya said quietly, following his gaze. "Astrid's been teaching me. It says—hm. Roughly, *we go ahead to prepare the way.*"

"Prepare the way," Okafor echoed. "For who?"

"For us, maybe. Or people like us." Ilya's headlamp swept the bay. "The second ark's people came out of the same culture. Same language. Same exile." He paused. "Same Council, probably, before the Council decided they were contamination."

Okafor grunted. Nine years he'd run salvage out past the shipping lanes, and the whole time, history had been happening in the dark around him—two arks, one window, a secret war of bloodlines fought with paperwork and cryo-fluid. He was a man who fixed things. It offended him, deeply and personally, that the universe kept breaking in ways no welder could reach.

"Transfer deck's two levels down," he said. "Stay on the tether. If the bladders are intact, we pump and go. If they're not, we pump anyway and filter what we get. Either way, we're off this ship in four hours."

"Four hours," Ilya said. "You said three last time."

"Last time I was an optimist. I've grown."

They descended through the hauler's spine, hand over hand, boots magnetic on the deck plating. The ship groaned occasionally—a thermal tick, metal contracting as the skiff's lights warmed the compartments— and every time it did, Okafor felt Ilya go still beside him, the way a man does when a sound reminds his body of something his mind has agreed to forget. Waking alone on a dead ship. A year of it. Okafor didn't ask. Some debts you didn't collect.

The transfer deck was a cathedral of pipes. Fuel lines as thick as Okafor's torso ran the length of the compartment, feeding six reserve bladders the size of shuttlecraft, each one wrapped in insulation that had gone gray and brittle with age. His suit's scanner painted the bladders in soft false-color, and there—there it was—the third bladder lit up faint amber.

"Live volatiles," he breathed. "Ilya. Look at that. Look at that beautiful, stubborn, impossible bladder."

"I see it." And Okafor heard it, the crack in Ilya's voice, the thing underneath the flatness: hope, which was the most dangerous substance on any ship, more volatile than anything in the tanks.

They worked. Ilya spliced into the transfer manifold with hands that remembered this work from a lifetime and a cryo-sleep ago; Okafor ran the lines back to the skiff's external tank, checking seals twice, because a fuel leak in a dead ship's gut was how salvage crews became part of the wreck. The pump spun up with a whine that Okafor felt in his teeth. The gauge began, slowly, blessedly, to climb.

"Fifty liters," Ilya reported. "A hundred."

"Junie, you seeing this?"

"Loud and clear, Captain." Junie's voice came through warm with something that was almost laughter. "Radiation's quiet, pressure's quiet. Only thing noisy over here is me, not believing our luck."

"Don't jinx—"

The ship groaned again. Longer this time. Deeper.

Okafor froze with his hand on the pump housing. Beside him, Ilya had gone absolutely still, headlamp aimed at the overhead, where a hairline fracture in the deck plating had begun—very slowly, very politely, the way disasters do—to widen.

"Ilya."

"I see it."

"How long has that been there?"

"It wasn't."

The fracture ran along a structural seam, old damage, probably from the same event that killed the ship—stress the hull had carried for decades, held together by nothing but habit. And now the pump's vibration, the skiff's warmth, the small rude fact of their presence, was asking the seam a question it had been avoiding for thirty years.

"Junie," Okafor said, keeping his voice level, because a captain's voice was a tool like any other, "structural readout on the transfer deck. Now, please."

A pause. When Junie answered, the warmth was gone. "Captain, the whole compartment's under stress. It was holding. Your pump vibration is—" a breath— "it's resonating. You need to shut it down."

"If I shut it down, we lose the transfer. We're at three hundred liters."

"If you don't shut it down, you lose the compartment."

Okafor looked at the gauge. Three hundred and twelve liters, climbing. He looked at the fracture, a black thread in the overhead, thin as a hair, patient as a debt.

"We're close," he said. "Two thousand more liters and we can stop improvising. Two thousand more and nobody has to be a hero. Give me ten minutes."

"Captain—"

"Ten minutes, Junie."

There was a silence on the channel, and in it Okafor heard everything he would spend the next several days regretting: the sound of a good medic deciding to trust a stubborn captain, against her better judgment, because he had been right before.

"Ten minutes," Junie said. "Not one second more."

* * *

The bladder gave them nine hundred liters before it gave them everything else.

Okafor felt it before he heard it—a shudder through his boots, a wrongness in the deck, the ship's long-held breath finally, finally released. The seam let go with a crack like the world's largest knuckle, and the overhead came down in a slow-motion bloom of plating and frozen insulation, and the compartment's atmosphere—what little there was—went howling out through a wound three meters wide.

The decompression caught him at the pump. It didn't throw him; there wasn't enough air left to throw him. It simply *took*, the way vacuum takes, gentle and absolute, and suddenly Okafor was sliding across the deck toward the breach with his tether whipping behind him, and the last thing he saw before the plating edge caught his leg was Ilya, ten meters away, reaching for him with both hands and missing by the width of a prayer.

Then the plating edge caught him. Not his tether. *Him.*

The pain arrived a half-second later, enormous and white, and Okafor had been a salvage man for twenty years and had a whole vocabulary for pain, and this one used all of it. His suit's emergency sealant hissed against the gash in his thigh, doing its small, faithful work, and the vacuum pulled at him, patient, and somewhere very far away Ilya was shouting his name.

He grabbed a pipe. An old fuel line, dead, solid, bolted to the deck by people who had believed in permanence. He held on. It was, he would think later, the least heroic moment of his life: a captain clinging to a pipe in a dead ship's gut, leg torn open, breath fogging inside a helmet that was rapidly becoming his entire world, thinking not of the two arks, not of the window, not of the grand moral arithmetic of it all, but only—*only*—*hold on, hold on, hold on.*

The tether went taut. Ilya had anchored it to the skiff's hardpoint and was hauling, hand over hand, and the physics of it was simple and merciless and beautiful, and Okafor came back across the compartment meter by meter, leaving a smear on the deck that the sealant foam made look almost decorative, and then Ilya's hands were on his shoulders, and Ilya's voice was in his ears, and Ilya was saying, over and over, in a voice cracked clean through:

"I've got you. I've got you. I've got you."

"Junie," Okafor managed. "Tell Junie—"

"Shut up. Med bay. Now."

* * *

The Kestrel Dawn's med bay was a converted storage locker with a surgical frame, a fabricator, and a wall of adhesive bandages that Junie organized with the devotion other people reserved for religion. Okafor lay on the frame while she worked, and he watched her face, because her face was the diagnosis.

The gash ran from mid-thigh to knee. The plating edge had gone through the suit and into the muscle beneath, and the sealant had bought him the difference between a scar and a eulogy, but the muscle was chewed, and the leg would take weeks, and weeks was a currency they did not have.

"You're lucky," Junie said, not looking up.

"I know."

"You're not. You're not lucky, Okafor. Lucky is when the universe is kind to you. What happened is that the sealant worked and Ilya didn't let go and the pipe was where it was. That's not luck. That's three separate things going right in a row, and you don't get to spend three things going right in a row on *ten more minutes of pumping*." She tied off a suture with more force than the tissue strictly required, and Okafor hissed. "Good. Feel that. Feel it every time you do the math wrong."

"Junie—"

"Three hundred and ninety liters." She finally looked at him, and her eyes were wet and furious, which from Junie was worse than shouting. "We got three hundred and ninety liters and you nearly died, and I sat on this ship and *obeyed*, because you said ten minutes and I trusted you, and I want you to understand that I will never do that again. Not because I don't love you. Because I do."

The words landed in the little med bay and sat there. Okafor looked up at the overhead, at the scuffed plating he'd been meaning to replace for two years, and found that his throat didn't want to cooperate.

"I made the call," he said finally. "It was my call."

"Yes."

"And it was wrong."

"Yes." She pulled a blanket over his legs, gentler now, the fury banked but not out. "The worst kind of wrong, too. The kind that *almost works*. You'll want to do it again because it almost worked. Don't."

The med bay door opened and Ilya came in, still in his suit's underlayer, hair plastered flat, hands shaking—he saw Okafor notice and shoved them into his pockets, and Okafor pretended not to see the pretending. Nine hundred liters of fuel sat in the Kestrel's tank. Nine hundred liters bought with a captain's thigh and a medic's trust and an engineer's hands that hadn't stopped shaking since the pipe.

"How's the leg?" Ilya asked.

"Junie says I'll live long enough to be yelled at."

"She's generous."

They were quiet a moment. Somewhere in the ship, the fuel recycler hummed, counting its treasure.

"Astrid pinged," Ilya said. "While you were under. She wanted to know how the raid went." He hesitated. "I told her we got fuel. I didn't tell her about—" a gesture that took in the med bay, the leg, the whole bleeding shape of the day. "Figured that was yours to tell."

"Tell her everything," Okafor said. "She's about to make a choice bigger than mine. She should know what the small ones cost." He shifted, and the leg announced itself, and he breathed through it. "And Ilya. Thank you. For not letting go."

Ilya looked at him for a long moment, and something moved behind his eyes—the dead ship, the year alone, the arithmetic of a man who had once been the only one left to do the hauling. "I know what it is," he said simply, "to be the one nobody reaches. I wasn't going to watch it happen to somebody else. Not you."

He left. Junie stayed, monitoring vitals, and the Kestrel Dawn turned her nose toward Verdance with her tanks heavier and her captain lighter, and Okafor lay in the med bay doing the new arithmetic, the honest kind: nine hundred liters down, three thousand one hundred to go, one leg out of commission, one crew's confidence cracked like the Mercy's overhead seam.

And it was Junie, checking his drip, who found the message first—Astrid's follow-up ping, flagged urgent, sent while he'd been under the knife. She read it, and went still, and Okafor watched her face do something he had never seen it do.

"Captain," she said. "The garden's dying. All of it. Astrid says it started this morning, all at once, like something *switched it off*."
