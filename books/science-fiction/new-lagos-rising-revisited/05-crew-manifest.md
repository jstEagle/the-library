## 5. Crew Manifest

Dara Voss had learned to read a ship the way other people read faces.

The *Halcyon Drift* was forty-one years old and honest about most of her moods. A bearing on its way out sang in the portside coolant loop — a thin, rising note like a wineglass about to confess. The number three fuel cell ran hot when the ship thought no one was watching. And the gravity plating in the aft sections had always been a half-degree off true, a tilt so slight you only noticed it when you set a cup down and watched it drift a centimeter toward the bulkhead over an hour.

But tonight the ship was lying to her.

Dara stood in the engineering bay at 0300 ship's time, three days out of New Lagos, staring at a power draw that should not exist. It was small. That was what made it beautiful and terrible. Someone was siphoning four kilowatts — steady, disciplined, masked inside the life-support baseline with a skill that made Dara want to applaud and vomit in equal measure. She'd found it by accident, chasing a harmonic ghost in the reactor telemetry, and once she'd seen it she could not unsee it: a hidden circuit, routed through junction 12-C, running aft.

Aft. Toward cargo hold two.

She knew what was in cargo hold two. Officially: desalination membranes, seed stock, medical consumables for a colony that officially needed them. Unofficially — unofficially, Dara had told herself for six years — nothing. Just freight. Just the strange, heavy silence Ines Montrose carried whenever anyone mentioned the hold, the way they checked its seals twice per watch without ever logging why, the way the manifest listed its mass in round numbers that never quite matched the load cells.

Four kilowatts, running cold and constant, night after night, year after year. You didn't spend that kind of power on membranes.

Dara pulled up the crew manifest on her workbench display, mostly because she needed to look at something that wasn't the truth. Six names. Six years. Okafor-Sefu was still listed as dockmaster liaison, though he hadn't set foot aboard since the last run; Ines kept him on the books because paperwork was cheaper than sentiment and Ines had never once been sentimental where credits were concerned. Then there was Dara herself, engineer, second seat. Pilot Ade Okonkwo-Bell, who slept through everything and saw everything. Medic Halle Brandt, who asked no questions because questions were a kind of debt. And Ines Montrose, navigator, first seat, captain in every way that mattered and none of the ways that were legal.

Six names. And, if the power draw meant what it meant, some number of additional souls who appeared nowhere on any manifest, drew no rations, breathed no air, and weighed exactly as much as dead people weigh.

Dara closed the display. Her hands were steady. She noted that with a kind of clinical pride — *good, the tremor comes later, when there's time for it* — and then she did what she had been doing for a year now, ever since she'd first traced this same circuit to this same hold and sat in the dark of the engineering bay until her tea went cold, deciding.

She said nothing. Again.

But tonight she got up from her bench, and she went aft.

* * *

The corridor to hold two smelled of recycled air and old insulation, that particular sweetness of polymer slowly giving up its structural arguments. Dara walked it slowly, boots quiet, not because she was sneaking — it was her ship too, damn it, every rivet of it — but because some animal part of her brain insisted that whatever lived behind that hatch deserved the courtesy of approach.

The hold's outer lights were dimmed to maintenance level. The inner hatch — the second one, the one that wasn't on any schematic Dara had signed off on, which had been her first clue a year ago and the thing she'd spent twelve months pretending not to have noticed — was sealed. Status panel green. Power draw active. Cold running, just above cryo-idle.

And standing in front of it, one hand flat against the hatch like a person touching a door they weren't sure they had the right to knock on, was Ines Montrose.

They didn't startle. Dara had never once seen Ines startle; it was one of the qualities that made them unnervingly good at their job and difficult to be friends with. They simply turned, and the corridor's low light caught the gray coming in at their temples, and their face did something complicated and quick before settling into the navigator's mask — polite, neutral, faintly amused, a face designed to end conversations rather than begin them.

"Dara." A beat. "It's late."

"It is." Dara leaned against the opposite bulkhead, arms crossed, and let the silence do some work. Ines was better at silence than almost anyone Dara had ever met, but silence cost Ines something; Dara could see the price ticking up behind their eyes. "Junction 12-C," Dara said finally. "Four-point-two kilowatts, continuous, cold-looped through the life-support baseline. Routed to a hatch that doesn't exist on my schematics. I found it fourteen months ago."

Something moved through Ines then — not fear exactly, or not only fear. Relief, arriving uninvited, wearing fear's coat. Dara recognized it because she'd felt the same thing a year ago, alone at her bench: the awful lightness of no longer being the only one carrying it.

"You never said anything," Ines said.

"No."

"Why?"

"Because I wanted to see how long you'd let me pretend." Dara heard her own voice come out harder than she'd intended, and didn't soften it. "That was the experiment. I'm the engineer, Ines. My whole job is knowing what the systems are actually doing instead of what the readouts claim. And here's my own captain, running a hidden load through my ship, through *my* reactor, for six years, and trusting me so little that you built your lie straight through my department and assumed I'd never look." She pushed off the bulkhead. "I looked. Fourteen months ago. And then I stood watch over your secret like an idiot, waiting for you to trust me with it, and you never did, and I hated you a little more every month, and I *still* didn't say anything, and I would like someone to explain to me what that makes me."

The words came out in a rush, and once they were out Dara felt strangely hollowed, like a component stripped for inspection. Ines hadn't moved. Their hand was still on the hatch. When they spoke, their voice was low and even, and it was the evenness that frightened Dara more than shouting would have.

"What do you think is back there?"

"I think," Dara said, "that neural backup cradles draw between three and five kilowatts on cold idle. I think that's Compact-restricted technology that carries a mandatory report-and-forfeit order under the Identity Integrity Act. I think that if a patrol vessel ever scanned this ship properly instead of lazily, we'd all be looking at re-education, and I've known that for fourteen months, and I've slept anyway, somehow." She stopped. Made herself say the last part plainly, because it was the part that mattered. "I think there are people in that hold, Ines. Dead people. Being kept. And I think one of them has your face, because I've seen the manifest anomaly in the biometric logs — the duplicate pattern, three years stale, that you've been scrubbing manually every quarter and doing a *sloppy* job of it, which is how I know, because you're never sloppy about anything except the things that break your heart."

For a long moment neither of them spoke. Somewhere forward, the coolant bearing sang its thin note. The ship breathed around them.

"Her name is Ines," Ines said at last, and Dara realized with a small, cold shock that they'd given up. Just like that. Fourteen months of siege ended in a single sentence, and Dara couldn't tell if she'd won or lost. "Ines Prime. Me. The me that stayed on New Lagos during the evacuation order and ran the shelter at Pelican Row until the second wave came through. Three hundred and eleven people got out because of her. She didn't." Ines's thumb moved against the hatch, a small unconscious stroke, the way you'd touch a sleeping child. "There are others. Nine others. Colonists whose families paid smugglers before the schedules collapsed, and the smugglers took the money and the backups and vanished, and we — I — picked them up in drift caches over four runs. They're not contraband to me, Dara. They're passengers."

"They're evidence," Dara said quietly. "We're not a rescue ship. We're a getaway car."

"I know what we are."

"Do you? Because I've been trying to figure out what *I* am for a year. Accessory? Accomplice? Hostage?" Dara laughed, and it came out wrong, brittle. "You know the worst part? Finding out. Not the crime — the finding out. Because some part of me, some stupid loyal part, spent fourteen months constructing explanations. Salvaged data cores. Archival samples. Anything. And the whole time the truth was simpler and worse, and when it finally landed I felt—" She searched for it. "Relieved. That's the word. Relieved that I wasn't crazy, relieved that I finally knew, and betrayed in the same breath, and I don't know how both of those fit in one chest at the same time, but apparently they do, because here I am."

Ines nodded slowly. "Yes," they said. "That's exactly what it's like. Every day. For six years." They turned back to the hatch, and their voice dropped into something almost private. "You get good at holding both. That's the trick nobody warns you about. It doesn't resolve. You just get stronger hands."

"That's not a philosophy, Ines. That's a symptom."

"Probably."

Dara looked at the hatch. Behind it, ten human minds sat in the dark at four degrees above freezing, dreaming whatever the dead dream when someone pays to keep the dream running. She thought about the Identity Integrity Act, which she'd sworn an oath to uphold along with every other licensed engineer in Compact space — an oath that existed because of the Reconstitution Wars, because of the warlord who'd printed himself ten thousand times, because of things Dara had learned about in school and filed under *history*, under *impossible*. She thought about the fact that history was currently sleeping in her cargo hold with her captain's face.

"The flicker," Dara said.

Ines went very still. It was a small thing, a half-beat of held breath, but Dara had spent six years learning this ship and three days learning to watch Ines Montrose the way she watched pressure gauges, and she caught it.

"You felt it too," Dara said. "Nine seconds. Gravity stuttered across the whole system and every instrument on this ship lied to us at once, and you stood on the bridge and told Ade it was a plating resonance cascade, and you *lied beautifully*, and then you came down here and checked on them." She jerked her chin at the hatch. "Why? What does a gravity flicker have to do with your ghosts?"

"Nothing. Routine check."

"Ines."

"*Routine check.*"

And there it was — the wall going up, smooth and fast, the navigator's mask sliding home. Dara had seen it a thousand times across a card table, across a cargo negotiation, across the dinner table when Sefu's name came up. But she'd never had it turned on her while she was holding a piece of the truth, and now that she had, she understood something new and unwelcome: Ines wasn't protecting the backups from the Compact.

Ines was protecting them from the crew. From her.

"Okay," Dara said, and stepped back, palms open, surrendering the corridor. "Okay. Keep it. Keep all of it. But hear me, because I'm only going to say this once." Her voice steadied itself the way her hands had, clinical, precise. "I've said nothing for fourteen months, and I'll say nothing now, because those people in there deserve better than to become a tribunal exhibit, and because — God help me — I love this crew, and I'd rather be complicit than alone. But the lying stops expanding. You don't get to add me to the secret's weight-bearing structure without telling me what floor it goes up to. Something happened out there, Ines. Nine seconds of something. And you know what it was, and you're going to tell me, or one day soon I'm going to open that hatch myself, and neither of us wants to find out what happens when I stop being patient and start being curious."

Ines looked at her for a long moment. The mask was still up, but behind it Dara could see the arithmetic running — the same cold, careful arithmetic that had rerouted four kilowatts through her reactor for six years without her noticing, except now she noticed everything, and they knew she noticed, and the whole hidden architecture of the ship had changed shape around that single fact.

"You sound like her," Ines said quietly. "Prime. When she'd decided something."

"I'll take that as a compliment."

"Don't." Ines pushed off the hatch at last, and walked past Dara toward the forward section, and paused, just briefly, shoulder to shoulder in the dim corridor. "Tomorrow," they said. "After we dock. There are records on New Lagos — tidal data, array telemetry. If I'm right about what the flicker was, you'll want to see it before I tell you. Trust me on the sequencing."

"And if you're wrong?"

"Then I'll tell you that too." A ghost of the old dry humor. "I'm told I lie beautifully. Let's find out if I can manage the truth."

Ines walked away. Dara stood alone in the corridor a moment longer, listening to the ship breathe, feeling the four kilowatts flowing steadily aft through her bones like a pulse.

Then she turned and headed for the engineering bay, because there was work to do, and because if the sequencing really mattered — if Ines was right about what waited on New Lagos — then Dara Voss wanted to be the one who understood it first.

She didn't sleep. She pulled the flicker telemetry instead, all nine seconds of it, and began, very quietly, to take the universe apart.
