## 6. The Compact's Long Arithmetic

The hail came at 0347 ship-time, which was to say it came at the hour Ines had privately designated as the hour for bad news. Nothing good had ever arrived in the dark hours of a freighter's watch. Good news waited until morning like a civilized guest. Bad news knocked whenever it pleased.

"Incoming transmission," the console said in its bland synthetic voice. "Compact Naval Vessel *Adjudicator*, registry CN-7714. They're requesting a parley channel."

Ines set down the tea they'd been nursing — cold already, the way all their tea went cold these days — and looked at the signal profile for three full seconds before answering. The *Adjudicator* was sitting forty thousand kilometers off their bow, close enough to read the Halcyon Drift's hull markings with a child's telescope, far enough away to pretend courtesy. Patrol frigates didn't come out this deep on courtesy.

"Open the channel," Ines said. "Audio first."

The screen stayed dark. A voice came through instead, clipped and pleasant, the particular pleasantness of someone reading from a script they'd helped write. "Freighter *Halcyon Drift*, this is Lieutenant Commander Vashti Okonkwo of the Compact Naval Service. Please confirm your identity and current routing."

"This is Ines Montrose, master and navigator of the *Halcyon Drift*." The lie about being master sat so comfortably in their mouth now that Ines barely noticed it anymore. Captain Reyes preferred to sleep through hails; that arrangement had been in place for two years and had never once been questioned. "We're running standard supply circuit, outbound from Meridian Station, bound for New Lagos colony. Manifest is agricultural equipment, medical stock, and general freight. Do you need us to transmit it?"

A pause. Not long enough to be suspicious. Long enough to be deliberate.

"We have your manifest, Montrose. We're curious about your route." Another pause, this one shaped differently. "You've altered course twice in the last six days. Both alterations took you through low-traffic corridors. Can you account for that?"

Ines leaned back in the navigator's chair and let the silence do some work. This was the part of the job nobody taught you in certification school: the arithmetic of lying. Every lie cost something. A small lie cost a small thing — a detail you'd have to keep track of, a thread that could unravel later. A big lie cost more. And the price wasn't paid when you told the lie. It was paid later, always later, in installments, with interest.

"We had debris fields on the plotted route," Ines said. "Meridian's been sloppy about publishing its sweep data since the last budget cycle. I don't risk my hull on their bookkeeping." A shrug they could hear. "I reroute around anything anyone hasn't swept in ninety days. You can check my flight logs against the published hazard notices if you want. I'll transmit them."

That was the trick, or one of them. Never defend. Offer more than they asked for. A guilty person protects their records; an innocent one hands them over with a smile. Ines had learned this not from any manual but from six years of carrying cargo that would end their life if examined too closely, and the learning had settled into them the way silt settles into a riverbed — gradually, invisibly, permanently.

"Your logs will be reviewed," Okonkwo said, and Ines heard the faint click of something being recorded, filed, cross-referenced somewhere in a database that never slept. "One further question. Your vessel transited grid sector KL-9 on the fourteenth of this month. Is that correct?"

Ines's stomach performed a slow, controlled drop, the way it did during gravity anomalies, and they kept their voice level through sheer professional discipline. KL-9. The nine-second flicker. Of course. Someone had felt it too, and someone had gone looking for who else had been there to feel it.

"That's correct. Empty sector. Nothing there but dust and old survey buoys. Why?"

"Did you experience any navigational anomalies during transit?"

Here it was. The whole conversation pivoting on this point, everything before it just approach vectors. Ines thought about the nine seconds — the way the coffee had lifted off the galley table in slow silver beads, the way every instrument on the bridge had reported perfect conditions while their inner ear screamed that the universe had briefly forgotten how to hold things down. Nine seconds of the fundamental grammar of reality stuttering, and then nothing. No debris. No shockwave. No explanation. Just the stars, indifferent, and a crew that had laughed about it over dinner.

"We had a gravimetric flutter," Ines said. "Maybe two seconds. Instruments flagged it, then cleared themselves. I logged it as sensor ghosting — those old Meridian arrays throw false positives all the time." They paused, then added, with the precise weight of a man mentioning something he'd almost forgotten, "Did you feel it too? We've been wondering if anyone else picked it up. My engineer wanted confirmation she wasn't chasing a hardware fault."

Let him chew on that. Give him a reason to believe your version: because you wanted his. Liars who ask questions are believed; liars who only answer are remembered.

Another pause. Longer this time. Ines imagined Okonkwo on the other end of the channel — young, probably, or young-seeming, the way Compact officers were groomed to seem, polished and reasonable and absolutely certain that everything he did served order. Somewhere behind him there would be a superior officer listening, and behind that officer an analyst, and behind the analyst a system that had been asking questions along shipping lanes for weeks now, quietly, precisely, the way you tap the walls of a house to find where the hollow spaces are.

"Our instruments recorded a transient gravitational event in KL-9," Okonkwo said carefully. "Duration under ten seconds. Origin undetermined. The Compact is conducting a routine survey of the phenomenon."

Routine. Ines nearly smiled. There was no such thing as a routine survey of a weapon test. But he would say routine, because routine was what kept civilian traffic calm, and calm traffic was easier to search.

"Glad it wasn't my engineer's fault," Ines said lightly. "She'd have made me replace the whole array, and the Drift can't afford it. Anything else, Commander? We're on a schedule. New Lagos expects us inside the week, and the colony doesn't handle delays well. Short tempers, long winters."

"New Lagos." The name landed differently than the others had. Ines couldn't have said how — a fractional change in Okonkwo's cadence, the sound of a man reading a line he'd been told to emphasize. "You make regular runs there?"

"Six years. Somebody has to. The Compact stopped scheduling regular service after the war heated up, and the colonists still eat." Let a little edge into it. Freighters were allowed edge. Freighters were supposed to be slightly bitter, slightly tired, slightly mercenary. Bitterness was camouflage.

"The Compact has not forgotten New Lagos," Okonkwo said, and the sentence had the smooth, hollow ring of something rehearsed. "Supply allocation is determined by strategic priority. I'm sure you understand."

"I understand arithmetic," Ines said. "I don't have to like it."

"No," Okonkwo agreed, and for half a second something human flickered under the polish — or Ines wanted it to, which amounted to the same thing at 3 a.m. "Thank you for your cooperation, Montrose. Your logs will be reviewed. The *Adjudicator* may contact you again. Safe transit."

The channel closed. The bridge lights hummed. Ines sat very still in the navigator's chair and listened to the ship breathe — air cycling, water moving, the reactor's low patient heartbeat — and did the accounting they always did after a hail like that.

Cost of the morning: two falsified log entries confirmed and transmitted, which meant the falsifications were now load-bearing. One lie about the flicker's duration, which meant Dara's own instrument records — if the *Adjudicator* ever got hold of them — would show nine seconds where Ines had sworn two. One performance of innocence delivered with such fluency that Ines had caught themselves, halfway through, actually feeling innocent.

That was the worst part. Not the lying. The lying was a skill, and skills were neutral, like welding or suturing. The worst part was how good it felt to be believed. Somewhere in the last six years, Ines Montrose had become excellent at the exact thing that should have disgusted them, and excellence, unlike guilt, didn't fade with practice. It compounded.

*Small corruptions,* their mother used to say — though their mother had said it about sugar in tea, about rounding numbers on a market scale, about the tiny dishonesties that grease a life. *Watch the small ones, Ines. Nobody falls. They slide.* She'd died on Earth believing her child ran a clean ship between honest ports.

Ines keyed the intercom. "Dara. Bridge."

* * *

Dara Voss arrived smelling of engine coolant and the protein bars she lived on, hair tied back with what appeared to be a length of wire, which knowing Dara it probably was. She read Ines's face before she read the console.

"How bad?"

"They know about the flicker. They've got a name for it — transient gravitational event, origin undetermined — and they're surveying. And they asked about our route through KL-9 specifically, which means they've pulled transit records for the whole sector." Ines pulled up the flight logs on the main display. "I gave them doctored copies. Two-second anomaly, sensor ghosting, logged and dismissed."

Dara studied the entries for a long moment. Her jaw worked the way it did when she was deciding whether to say the thing she was thinking. With Dara, that decision usually went one direction.

"You lied well," she said finally.

"I lied beautifully. That's the problem."

"No, the problem—" She stopped. Started again, slower, the way she talked when she was working through a fault in a system she respected. "The problem is they're not doing a survey. I've been thinking about it since the flicker. A survey collects data passively. This—" she gestured at the comm log, at the transcript glowing between them, "—this is canvassing. They're interviewing witnesses. You interview witnesses when you're looking for something specific, and you look for something specific when you lost track of it." She tapped the entry for KL-9. "They tested something out there and they don't know if it worked. Or they know it worked and they don't know who saw it. Either way, we're not cargo to them anymore, Ines. We're a variable."

Ines looked at her. Dara Voss, who could rebuild a fusion injector with a multitool and a bad attitude, standing on the bridge at four in the morning talking like an intelligence analyst, and the terrible thing was that she was right, and the worse thing was that Ines had known she'd be right before the hail even ended.

"There's more," Ines said. "He asked about New Lagos. By name. Asked if we made regular runs."

Dara went still. "Nobody asks about New Lagos. That's the whole point of New Lagos. It's the place everyone agreed to stop looking at."

"Somebody's looking now."

The two of them stood in the green glow of the nav display, and Ines watched the same realization move across Dara's face that had been living in their own chest for days: that the flicker hadn't been random, that the questions hadn't been routine, that the quiet abandoned colony at the end of their route might be quiet for reasons that had nothing to do with neglect and everything to do with attention — the wrong kind, the kind that comes before a decision.

"If they're canvassing the shipping lanes," Dara said slowly, "they'll pull port records next. Dock manifests. Census data flows." She frowned. "Sefu's filings."

"Sefu's filings are clean."

"Ines." Dara gave them the flat look she reserved for people understating structural damage. "Nothing Sefu files is clean. He files whatever keeps the colony fed. If the Compact starts auditing New Lagos paperwork, we're not talking about a bad day. We're talking about the whole fiction coming apart — the census, the supply chain, the—"

She stopped herself. Ines watched her stop herself, watched the sentence die behind her teeth, and understood exactly which word she'd swallowed. *The hold.* The hold that dreamed. Thirty-one neural backups riding in shielded cradles under false manifest codes, including one labeled with a dead woman's name that happened to be Ines's own.

"The whole fiction," Ines finished quietly.

"Yeah." Dara rubbed her eyes with the back of a coolant-stained hand. "Okay. What do you want to do?"

What did they want to do. Run? To where — the Compact owned the shipping lanes, and the Concord owned the gaps between them, and both governments were currently performing the same pantomime of peace over the same secret war. Report the flicker honestly? Hand the truth to Okonkwo and his analysts and become either a witness to be managed or a loose end to be trimmed. Turn around? Abandon New Lagos mid-run, with the population halved and falling, with Sefu Okafor holding the docks together with falsified forms and stubbornness?

Every option had a price tag. That was the long arithmetic. You never got to choose whether to pay. Only what.

"We run the route," Ines said. "We deliver the cargo. We dock at New Lagos, we take on water and news, and we keep our heads down until we understand what they're actually looking for." They held up a hand before Dara could object. "And we get smarter. I want you going through the flicker data — the real data, the nine seconds, not what I sent Okonkwo. Duration, waveform, propagation. If it was a test, tests leave signatures. Find me the signature."

"And if I find it?"

"Then we'll know what we're carrying besides cargo." Ines killed the display. The bridge went dim, starlight and instrument light, the oldest lighting scheme in human history. "Dara. The lie I told today — the two-second ghosting. If anyone ever puts your sensor records next to my flight logs, we're both finished. You understand that. From this moment, your official records match mine."

Dara looked at them for a long time. Something moved behind her eyes — not anger, exactly. Accounting. She was doing her own arithmetic, Ines realized, weighing the cost of the lie she was being asked to co-sign against the cost of refusing, and finding, as they all eventually found, that the ledger only balanced one way.

"My records match yours," she said at last. Then, softer, almost to herself: "You know what my father used to say? Back on Ceres. He said the Compact doesn't beat you with soldiers. It beats you with paperwork. Every form you sign wrong becomes a wall. Eventually you're standing in a room made entirely of your own signatures, and there's no door, because doors require telling the truth to somebody."

She left. Ines stayed on the bridge and watched the stars do their slow wheeling, and thought about rooms without doors, and signed nothing else that night.

* * *

Two days later, with New Lagos a pale blue promise on the long-range scope, the *Adjudicator*'s channel opened again without warning.

Not Okonkwo this time. A different voice — older, unhurried, with the flat warmth of a man who had conducted a thousand interviews and enjoyed perhaps none of them. "Freighter *Halcyon Drift*, this is Captain Ede-Rios, Compact Naval Service. Commander Okonkwo sends his regards. I have a short list of follow-up questions regarding your transit of sector KL-9. Nothing alarming. Routine." A pause, delicately placed. "Though I confess I'm also curious about something else. Your ship has made eleven documented calls at New Lagos in six years. That's remarkable dedication to an unprofitable route. Most independents cut losses after two."

Ines looked at the blue dot growing in the viewport, at the colony that everyone had agreed to forget, and felt the familiar slide begin beneath their feet — the ground tilting by degrees, the way it had tilted for nine seconds in the dark of KL-9.

"Captain," they said, and heard their own voice come out warm, steady, and completely full of lies, "I grew up in places like New Lagos. Somebody has to remember them."

On the scope, the blue dot brightened. Behind it, somewhere in the dark, the *Adjudicator* adjusted course to follow.
