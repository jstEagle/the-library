## 13. Interdiction

The blockade announced itself the way bad news always does: quietly, then all at once.

Ines was running a fuel-slope calculation when the traffic channel lit up with eleven new contacts in under a minute. Eleven. New Lagos had never seen more than three ships in orbit at once in six years of runs — usually just the Drift and whatever tugs Sefu could keep breathing. Now the long-range plot bloomed with Compact hulls sliding into a lattice around the planet, each one taking up station like pieces of a lock assembling itself around a throat.

"Dara," they said into the comm, and their voice came out steadier than they felt. "Come look at this."

She arrived still wiping coolant residue off her hands, took one glance at the display, and said what Ines was already thinking: "That's not a patrol pattern. That's an interdiction cordon."

The Halcyon Drift hung forty thousand kilometers off New Lagos's night side, engines cold, riding the dark like a held breath. Below them, the colony's broadcasts were still going out — Sefu's voice, and Adeyemi's, and the raw archival audio of supply orders that had been cancelled by men who never signed their names. The whole planet had become a transmitter, and the Compact had come to put its hand over its mouth.

"Eleven ships," Dara murmured. "For a colony of what, nine thousand people? That's not containment math. That's siege math."

Ines watched the lattice close. Each ship burned precise, economical correction burns — no waste, no hesitation. They'd done this before. Maybe not here, but somewhere. Somewhere people had also gotten loud, and someone had decided that quiet was worth the cost of a fleet.

"We're inside it," Ines said. "We were inside it when it closed."

Dara looked at them. "How long before they notice us?"

"They noticed us the moment we crossed the line. They're deciding what we are."

* * *

The hail came ninety minutes later, on a tight-beam frequency the Drift hadn't used since its registry renewal. That was the message underneath the message: *we know exactly what you are.*

"Halcyon Drift, this is CCS Vigilant Order, Captain Ede-Roan commanding. You are within a declared security exclusion zone. State your cargo, your destination, and your business."

Ines sat in the comm chair and felt the old machinery spin up — the smoothness, the pleasant register, the lie-shaped sentences that fit together so cleanly. Six years of smuggling backups past customs drones had built this instrument inside them, and now, as always, they hated how well it played.

"Vigilant Order, Halcyon Drift. We're a contracted freighter out of Ceres Ring, carrying agricultural stock and medical consumables for New Lagos. We were mid-delivery when your cordon deployed. Requesting permission to complete delivery and depart."

A pause. Long enough to be deliberate.

"Your transponder shows you've made nineteen runs to this colony in six years, Drift. That's a lot of agricultural stock for a settlement your own manifests list as depopulating."

There it was. The census data. Sefu's careful falsifications, fed back to them like a mirror angled wrong. Someone had read the colony's fiction and found it wanting — or found it useful.

"Our manifests reflect colonial records," Ines said. "We deliver what we're paid to deliver."

Another pause. Then, softer, which was worse: "Hold position, Drift. A boarding assessment may be required. Do not alter your orbit. Compliance will be noted in your file."

The channel closed. Ines sat with their hands flat on the console, feeling the tremor they refused to let reach their fingers.

"They're going to board us," Dara said from the doorway. She'd heard all of it. "And when they do, they'll find the hold."

"Yes."

"And the hold is the only thing keeping everyone alive right now. The keys, the message, Prime—" She stopped herself, but the name had already landed between them like a dropped tool.

"I know what's in my hold, Dara."

"Do you?" She came forward, arms crossed, and there was no anger in her face, only exhaustion wearing anger's clothes. "Because I've been watching you treat those drives like cargo for three years. They're not cargo. They're witnesses. And we're sitting inside a cordon full of people who would very much like witnesses to stop existing."

Ines turned back to the plot. The lattice tightened another fraction. Below, New Lagos kept broadcasting, defiant and doomed-sounding, Sefu's voice reading supply cancellation orders into the dark like a man reciting names at a grave.

* * *

They ran the numbers for four hours. Every option died on the table.

Run for the cordon gap at the southern pole? The gap was a mouth; the two ships parked there were teeth. Burn for open space and trust the Drift's aging drive to outrun military interdiction boats? Dara laughed at that one, the short ugly laugh of an engineer hearing physics insulted. Surrender and submit to boarding? That meant the hold opened, the backups seized, the evidence — all of it, the archives, the decryption, the dead — swallowed into a Compact evidence locker that had never once released anything back into daylight.

"Or," Dara said finally, "we wait for her."

"Wren." Ines said the name like spitting out a seed.

"She shadowed us for two weeks. She offered asylum for the archive data. She knew about the backups before we ever told her, which means she has sources inside something, somewhere. And Concord stealth hulls have a documented habit of slipping through Compact cordons, because Compact sensor doctrine was written by people who assume nobody builds quiet ships." Dara shrugged. "I'm not saying I like her. I'm saying she's the only door in the room that isn't painted shut."

"The price is the backups, Dara. All of them. As *evidence*. She wants to parade our dead through Concord courts as proof of Compact crimes. Prime. The Okafor twins' mother. Everyone sleeping down there becomes a political exhibit."

"And if the Compact boards us first, they become a destroyed exhibit, because the Vigilant Order will not leave loose ends floating in a quarantine zone. You know that. I know that." Dara's voice dropped. "Every exit runs through somebody's hands, Ines. We just get to choose whose."

Ines looked at the hold's status display — green lights, stable temperatures, thirty-one sleepers dreaming their archived dreams — and felt the weight of it settle onto their shoulders like ballast. Loyalty to the living meant handing the dead to strangers. Loyalty to the dead meant burying the living along with them.

"Send her a whisper," they said. "Tight-beam, burst transmission, nothing traceable. Tell her we're caught. Tell her we want to talk terms."

* * *

Wren Ilyich answered in person, which was somehow exactly what Ines should have expected.

The Concord ship — no name given, just a hull signature reading *BC-quiet-7* — materialized off the Drift's port quarter at 0300 ship time, so close that Ines felt it before they saw it: a pressure change in the passive array, a whisper of drive plasma that shouldn't have been detectable and was, barely, because Dara had tuned the sensors herself and trusted nothing factory-standard.

"She's good," Dara admitted grudgingly. "Nobody gets that close to my array."

"Everyone gets that close to your array eventually. You just like pretending otherwise." Wren's face filled the comm screen, and she looked, infuriatingly, like she'd slept well. Dark hair cropped short, Concord fleet grays worn like they were a joke she was in on, eyes that catalogued everything behind a smile calibrated to seem effortless. "Captain Montrose. Or is it Navigator? Your file is admirably vague."

"You've read my file."

"I've written parts of it." She leaned back, casual as a friend dropping by. "Here's the situation as I see it. Eleven Compact hulls upstairs. A boarding party prepping downstairs — yes, we're reading their boat prep chatter, their comms discipline is a scandal. You have perhaps six hours before they knock. Inside your hold you're carrying the single most explosive secret in this war, and you're carrying it into the hands of the people who most need it buried." She spread her hands. "I'm offering you a door."

"At a price."

"At a price." No flinch. Ines gave her that much; she didn't pretend the word offended her. "The backups. Transferred to Concord custody, authenticated chain of custody, used as evidence in the identity-crimes tribunal we are formally opening against the Compact next month. Their neural harvesting programs. Their backup-quarantine protocols. What they did to New Lagos." Her smile thinned, and for half a second something real moved underneath it. "You think I'm collecting trophies. I'm building a case. There's a difference, and the difference is that cases end wars."

"Cases get people erased," Ines said. "You'd know. Your side wrote the quarantine order too — we've seen the signatures, Wren. Both governments. You're asking me to hand the dead to one executioner to testify against the other."

Something flickered across her face — fast, quickly filed away, but Ines had spent six years reading micro-expressions through customs cameras, and they caught it. Doubt. Real doubt, briefly surfaced.

"That order is being investigated internally," she said, and heard immediately how it sounded, because her jaw tightened. "Look. I'll be honest with you, because you're the only person in this system who seems to value honesty as a currency. I believed in this mission when I took it. I believed the Concord was the adult in the room. Then I started pulling threads on New Lagos and every thread led somewhere ugly, and some of them led home." She paused. "I'm not clean, Montrose. Neither are you. But right now I'm the only ship in this cordon that can get you out alive, and the tribunal is the only forum where those people in your hold get to *count* — publicly, legally, on the record. Isn't that what you want? For them to count?"

It was. That was the obscenity of it. It was exactly what Ines wanted, wrapped in exactly the betrayal they'd sworn against, delivered by a woman whose charm was so precisely engineered that Ines couldn't find the seam.

"Give me until the boarding party launches," Ines said. "Not a minute less. If I'm going to hand you thirty-one lives, I want to watch the alternative arrive first."

Wren studied them for a long moment. Whatever she saw seemed to satisfy her, or at least amuse her.

"Six hours," she said. "Then I make the choice easy for you, one way or the other. Sleep well, Navigator. Though I suspect neither of us will."

* * *

The channel closed, and Ines went down to the hold.

They told themselves it was to check the environmental systems. They were lying, and the hold knew it, and maybe that was why the air felt different tonight — heavier, attentive, like the sleepers had turned toward them in their dreams.

Thirty-one indicator lights. Thirty-one small green constellations in the dark. Ines walked the rows slowly, boots loud on the deck plating, and stopped at the rack they always stopped at.

"Ines Prime," they said aloud. Their voice sounded strange down here, unarmored. "The Compact is coming to take you. Or the Concord is. Or we burn the drives ourselves and let you die twice, properly this time, and I don't know which one is mercy anymore."

The light blinked its steady green. No answer. There never was, except the answer they carried around inside themselves like a splinter: *you already know what I'd say. You've known for three years. You just can't stand to hear it.*

Prime would say: *stop choosing between cages.* Prime had never once, in any of their conversations, accepted a premise she hadn't audited first. She'd say the Compact cage and the Concord cage were both cages, and that the only honest act left was the one that got everyone out of every cage at once, even if it killed them, especially if it killed them.

*Especially* — that was such a Prime word.

Footsteps on the ladder. Dara descended, and stood beside Ines in the green glow, and for a while neither of them spoke.

"Sefu's broadcasting again," she said finally. "He's reading the names of everyone who left. Everyone the colony lost. He's been at it for two hours. The Compact is jamming him, but he keeps switching frequencies faster than they can follow. Old smuggler trick." A pause. "Your trick, actually. He learned it from watching your approach vectors."

Ines almost smiled. Almost. "He's telling the whole system who we were."

"He's telling the whole system we *existed*. Different thing. Bigger thing." Dara turned to face them. "So. Six hours. Wren's offer. The Compact's boarding party. Talk to me, navigator. Where are we going?"

Ines looked down the long row of green lights — thirty-one lives folded into crystal and cold, the dead of New Lagos waiting on a decision that belonged, by every right Ines could name, to them. To the living. To the crew of one battered freighter caught between two fleets that had both, at one time or another, signed this colony's death warrant.

"Not to her," Ines said slowly, and felt the shape of the thought assemble itself as they spoke it, dangerous and obvious and terrifying. "And not to them. Dara — pull up the handshake decryption. All of it. The authentication keys, the broadcast architecture, everything Prime built."

"Why?"

"Because Wren wants the backups as *evidence*." Ines felt their heart begin to hammer, the old dread transmuting into something hotter. "But the message doesn't need the backups. It needs the *keys*. And the keys are already decrypted. We don't have to give anyone the dead." They turned toward the ladder, toward the bridge, toward the impossible arithmetic suddenly resolving into a third number nobody at the table had counted. "We just have to finish what they started. Wake Dara's decrypt team, get me Sefu on a jam-resistant channel, and tell him the colony's old deep-space array is about to carry a message from its dead."

Dara stared at them. "Through an active Compact interdiction cordon."

"Through an active Compact interdiction cordon," Ines agreed, already climbing. Above them, the alert board chimed — the Vigilant Order's boarding boat, launching early, six hours collapsed to four.

The countdown had begun without their permission. Fine. Ines had spent six years learning to navigate between the lines other people drew.

Time to draw one of their own.
