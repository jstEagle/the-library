## 14. The Message in the Dead

Dara Voss had a superstition about problems: they were never solved at the console. They were solved in the shower, in the galley, halfway through a cup of reconstituted coffee — solved by the part of the mind that worked while the rest of you pretended to rest. So when the handshake data refused to crack on the third day, she did what she always did. She walked away from it.

She made it as far as the corridor outside engineering before her own brain betrayed her.

*The array answered.*

She stopped walking. The corridor lights hummed their night-cycle amber around her, and she stood there like an idiot with a cooling mug in her hand while the thought finished assembling itself.

New Lagos's deep-space array had been decommissioned eleven years ago. Stripped, powered down, its uplink physically severed — she'd seen the maintenance records herself, had laughed at how thoroughly someone had killed that thing. And yet when the gravity flicker came, nine seconds of the universe stuttering like a skipped frame, the array's passive receivers had logged *something*. Sefu had pulled the logs for her. A response burst. Faint, garbled, but structured. An answer to a question nobody was supposed to be asking anymore.

Which meant the question hadn't been aimed at the array at all.

Dara set the mug down on a conduit housing, went back into engineering, and didn't sleep for thirty-one hours.

* * *

The handshake protocol was beautiful. That was the worst part. She'd expected brute force — Compact military crypto was usually a boot through a door, all sledgehammer and no subtlety. This was different. This was elegant in a way that made her palms sweat, because elegance meant time, and time meant resources, and resources meant this hadn't been built in some panic in a basement. Someone had spent years on it.

It wasn't a weapon signature. She'd confirmed that much by hour six. The flicker itself — the gravitational shear that had rattled every tooth in the system — was real, and it was terrifying, and it could absolutely crack a colony world like an egg if scaled up. But the flicker was only the carrier wave. The envelope. What rode inside it was a query.

A query formatted like a challenge-response authentication.

She stared at the structure until her eyes burned. Challenge, nonce, key request, verification window. It was the digital equivalent of knocking on a door and demanding to see papers. Except the papers it demanded weren't passwords or biometrics or Compact issue codes.

They were *memories*.

Specifically: the handshake required a distributed key — not stored anywhere, not transmitted anywhere, but *derived*, reconstructed piece by piece from fragments held by multiple parties. Sharded cryptography, the old paranoid kind, where no single person ever possesses the whole secret. Each fragment was a vector of neural-pattern data. Personality architecture. Memory topology. The kind of information that existed in exactly one class of artifact in the entire settled system.

Neural backups.

Dara sat back from the console so fast her chair banged the bulkhead. In the empty engineering bay, the sound rang like a shot.

"No," she said aloud, to nobody. "No, no, that's —"

But the math didn't care what she believed. The math was patient and it was cruel and it kept confirming itself. The key fragments in the handshake matched the encryption schema used by the backup cradles in the Halcyon Drift's hold — the illegal ones, the ones Ines pretended weren't there, the ones Dara had known about for a year and carried like a stone behind her sternum. Not identical. *Derived from the same root.* Whoever built the handshake had built it against the same foundational architecture the colonists' backups ran on. Had built it *from* that architecture, maybe, or from its source.

The weapon test hadn't just been a weapons test.

It had been a search.

Someone had fired a question across the system in the shape of a catastrophe, and the question was: *are you still out there? Do you still hold your pieces of the key?* And New Lagos's dead — the forty-some colonists sleeping in crystalline silence in the Drift's hold, including a dead version of the woman flying the ship — had answered without knowing it. The array's response burst. Passive receivers don't authenticate. But the cradles in the colony's archive vaults, the ones still buried under the governor's residence, still whispering to themselves in the dark — those would have responded. Those would have *had* to respond, if they were running the firmware Dara was looking at.

Every backup on New Lagos had just told whoever was listening: *here I am.*

Dara put her head in her hands.

* * *

She didn't tell anyone. Not yet. That was the coward's arithmetic and she knew it, but she needed to be sure, needed to run it again, needed to check whether she'd talked herself into a hallucination. Three more hours of verification. Then four. Then she stopped counting.

By the time she surfaced, the ship was deep in its second night cycle and she smelled like recycled air and fear. She went to find Ines.

She found them in the nav bay, which surprised her; she'd half expected the hold. Ines sat at the plotting table with a paper chart spread out — actual paper, an affectation Dara had never fully understood — marking positions with a grease pencil. Old habit from a navigator who trusted ink over instruments ever since the flicker.

"Dara." Ines looked up, and whatever they saw in her face made them go very still. "What is it."

"I cracked the handshake."

Ines set down the pencil. Carefully. Like it might go off. "And?"

"And it's not a weapon test." Dara pulled a chair over, sat, leaned forward. "Or it is, but that's not the point of it. The point of it is the payload. It's an authentication challenge. Distributed-key architecture. The key doesn't exist anywhere as a whole — it's sharded across multiple holders, and the challenge asks each holder to prove possession of their fragment."

"Prove it to whom?"

"That's the part I can't answer yet. The return address is masked behind three layers of relay. But here's the thing." Her mouth was dry. "The fragments aren't codes. They're not numbers somebody memorized. They're neural-pattern vectors. Personality data. Memory topology." She watched Ines's face do something complicated. "The key is people, Ines. The key is *minds*. Specific minds, with specific architectures, matching a schema that —" She stopped. Made herself say it. "That matches the backups in our hold. Same root architecture. Same lineage."

The nav bay was very quiet. Somewhere aft, the environmental system cycled, a long mechanical breath.

Ines spoke first, voice flat and careful. "You're saying someone tested a gravity weapon on this system as a way of asking our cargo a question."

"I'm saying someone tested a gravity weapon on this system as a way of asking *New Lagos* a question. Our hold just happens to carry copies of the same minds." Dara rubbed her eyes. "The array answered, Ines. Sefu's logs. Something responded to the challenge. Which means somewhere on that colony, in some vault, the original cradles are still live, still running, still holding their shards. And when the flicker hit, they answered."

"So whoever fired it now knows—"

"Now knows exactly which minds are down there. Exactly which fragments exist. Maybe even got partial verification back." Dara let that land. "It was a census, Ines. A census of the dead. Fired with a weapon, because apparently that's what it takes to get an honest answer out of a decommissioned colony."

Ines was staring at the paper chart without seeing it. Dara recognized the look — she'd seen it in mirrors during the war, the expression of someone recalculating everything they thought was solid.

"Why," Ines said finally. "Why build a key out of *people*? Why not just — data? Passwords? Why go to the obscene expense of backing up human minds just to —"

"Because minds can't be stolen quietly." Dara heard herself answering before she'd decided to. "Think about it. A password can be copied. A codebook can be photographed. But a neural fragment — a real one, tied to a living or once-living mind — if you take it, the person knows. Or the person's *dead*, and then taking it means building a cradle, running infrastructure, committing identity crime in the first degree. It's a key that fights back. It's a key that leaves witnesses." She laughed, and hated the sound of it. "Whoever designed this wanted a secret that couldn't be compromised without someone going to prison forever. Or being executed. Depending on whose law."

"And the colony." Ines's voice had dropped. "New Lagos. Half the population gone, supply schedules cut, everyone pretending it's just economics. You're telling me—"

"I'm telling you I don't have proof yet. But the shape of it—" Dara stood, started pacing, because sitting felt like drowning. "The shape of it is this: you've got a secret too valuable to store anywhere official, so you shard it across dozens of minds. You embed those minds — or their backups — in a remote colony. And then, to protect the secret, you make sure nobody looks at the colony. Ever. No supply runs worth noticing. No census anyone checks. No attention. Because attention is how keys get found." She turned to face Ines. "New Lagos wasn't forgotten, Ines. New Lagos was *buried*. Deliberately. The abandonment isn't neglect. It's camouflage. We've been ferrying supplies to a lockbox for six years and calling it charity."

The grease pencil rolled off the table. Neither of them moved to catch it.

* * *

She told Sefu next, over a delayed-comms link, watching his face pixelate and stutter in the Drift's comm bay while he sat in his office above the harbor on New Lagos. She gave him the technical summary first, clean and dry, and watched him absorb it the way dockmasters absorb bad news — silently, completely, with a slow settling of the shoulders like a ship taking on weight.

Then she watched him do something she didn't expect. He got up, checked his door seal, and turned off his camera.

When it came back on, he was closer to the lens, and his voice was lower.

"The archive vaults," he said. "Under the residence. I've known about them since I took this job. Governor's standing order: nobody enters, nobody audits, power draw comes out of a line item labeled 'climatic control.' I falsified around them for eight years, Dara. I thought they were the governor's private grief — backups of his dead wife, maybe, something sad and small." His image broke up, reformed. "You're telling me they're a cryptographic vault."

"I'm telling you they're *part* of one. The whole colony might be. Sefu — think about the population decline. Think about who left and who stayed. If the key is sharded across minds, and some of those minds died or emigrated—"

"Then the key changed." He closed his eyes. "God. Every death on this rock changes the lock. Every funeral is a security event." He opened his eyes and there was something terrible in them, something Dara had only ever seen in soldiers. "I catalogued the departures. For my own records, years ago, when I still believed the numbers were a mistake. Four hundred and eleven people left in six years. If even a fraction of them were carrying shards—"

"Then whoever built this has been tracking them. Or lost track, and that's why the flicker happened. A recount." Dara exhaled. "Sefu, I need access to the vault's external telemetry. Nothing invasive. Power signatures, thermal, anything the cradles broadcast passively. I want to confirm the architecture match before we say any of this out loud to anyone else."

"You'll have it within the hour." He paused. "Dara. If you're right — if this colony was erased on purpose, quarantined around a secret — then the Compact didn't abandon us. They *imprisoned* us. Six years of slow starvation dressed up as budget cuts, and every one of us thanked them for the freight." His laugh was worse than hers had been. "My mother stayed because she believed the Compact would come back for us. She died believing it. And now you're telling me the reason they never came back is that coming back would have meant *looking*."

"I'm telling you what the math says. I wish to God it said something else."

"No." Sefu shook his head, and his image steadied, and when he spoke again his voice had that quality Dara had heard from him exactly twice before — the voice of a man deciding. "No, don't wish. Math that says this is math we can use. Get me your confirmation. And Dara —" he leaned toward the lens, "— whatever you found, whoever fired that question at us. They're going to ask it again. Harder. Next time won't be nine seconds."

The link died. Dara sat alone in the comm bay with the carrier tone humming, and understood that he was right, and understood that she had maybe days.

* * *

She worked through what remained of the night cycle, and it was near shift-change when she found the last piece, the one she'd been dreading, the one the data had been saving for her like a trap.

The handshake's verification window. The window during which the challenged fragments were expected to respond.

Nine seconds.

Not approximately. Not coincidentally. The gravitational flicker that had knocked the whole system sideways — the flicker that had terrified Ines, that had scarred the water table, that Sefu had measured in the tides like a wound — lasted exactly as long as the protocol demanded an answer. The weapon and the question were the same event, timed to the millisecond. The physics hadn't malfunctioned. The physics had been *scheduled*.

And there was one more line in the metadata, buried so deep she almost missed it. A retry counter. Set to a maximum of three attempts.

Attempt one: complete.

Dara sat in the blue glow of the console, her hands flat on the desk to stop them shaking, and did the arithmetic she'd been avoiding since the corridor. Two attempts remaining. No way to know the interval between them. No way to know what a failed third attempt would trigger — whether the search would simply end, or whether the searcher would stop asking politely and come to collect the shards in person, with ships, with soldiers, with the full obscene machinery of a war that needed winning.

Either way, the countdown had already started, and the starting gun had sounded nine seconds of silence ago.

She saved the analysis in three places, encrypted it against Ines's personal key, and went to wake the crew.

On her way through the corridor, past the sealed hatch of the hold where forty-one dead colonists slept with pieces of a world-ending secret folded into the architecture of their minds, Dara Voss stopped, and pressed her palm flat against the cold metal, and whispered:

"We know why they left you now. God help us, we know."
