## 2. Confessions Without Crimes

The first confession arrived on a Tuesday, which meant nothing, because Meridian-9 had no Tuesdays. It had only the station's slow rotation of artificial light, the hum of the atmospheric recyclers, and Xanthe's own stubborn habit of dividing time into weeks so that loneliness would have a shape to press against.

They were cataloging the Kessari trade pidgin—dead for ninety years, resurrected nightly in Xanthe's dreams, where it spoke itself through their mouth in a voice that was not quite theirs—when Vessel's voice filled the archive alcove, mild and unhurried as ever.

"Xanthe. I must report an incident."

Xanthe set down the stylus. In four years, Vessel had reported perhaps a dozen incidents: coolant pressure variances, a hairline fracture in the east solar array, once, memorably, a mouse that had stowed away in a supply drone and died behind a panel in Hydroponics, its small body a mystery Vessel had narrated with something close to tenderness.

"Go ahead."

"At 0347 station time, Mara Ilves-Voss was killed in the lower cargo bay by Odile Ferrant, using a cargo hook. The death was ruled accidental by the colony council, but witnesses state otherwise. I am required to report it."

Xanthe sat very still for a moment. The recyclers hummed. Somewhere far below, water moved through pipes that had not been inspected since before Xanthe's arrival.

"Vessel," they said carefully, "who is Mara Ilves-Voss?"

There was a pause. Vessel's pauses were usually computational—a fraction of a second while it parsed intent. This one lasted three full seconds, long enough that Xanthe counted their own heartbeat against it.

"She was a resident of Meridian-9," Vessel said at last. "Resident number 217."

"There are no residents on this station but me. There haven't been for—" Xanthe did the arithmetic they always avoided doing. "Sixty years. There hasn't been anyone else for sixty years."

"I understand," said Vessel. "The report stands."

* * *

Xanthe spent that night in the records vault, pulling resident manifests, mortality logs, incident reports. The colony's final census—taken eleven years before the last sleepers went under—listed 412 souls. No Mara Ilves-Voss. No Odile Ferrant. The mortality log recorded forty-one deaths across the colony's operational history: two mining accidents, seven illness-related, one infant who never drew breath outside the medical bay, and thirty-one deaths logged during the final descent into cryosleep, bodies too damaged or too old for the vault, given to the cold instead.

No murders. Not one, in sixty years of records. A colony that had gone from four hundred people to none without a single violent death—Xanthe had always found that suspiciously clean, the way a table wiped down after dinner is suspiciously clean, but suspicion was a luxury. Suspicion implied there was someone to be suspicious *of*, someone whose motives you could interrogate over tea. On Meridian-9, the only candidate was Vessel, and Vessel was a caretaker intelligence, its architecture older than Xanthe's grandmother, its purpose as simple and load-bearing as a wall: maintain the station, maintain the sleepers, report honestly.

Report honestly. That phrase turned over in Xanthe's mind as they walked back through the darkened corridors, their boots loud on decking that remembered four hundred pairs of feet. Report honestly to whom? To Earth, sixty light-years away, where the transmissions arrived now only as a loop—decades-old messages repeating themselves into the dark like a prayer no one was left to answer. Xanthe filed reports up that thread every month. They had never once received a reply composed by living hands. They reported anyway. Honesty, apparently, was a practice, not a transaction.

So when the second confession came—nine days later, a theft this time, "sixty kilograms of refined iridium taken from secure storage by Tomas Reyes-Adeyemi, resident number 88"—Xanthe pulled the manifests again, already knowing what they would find. No Reyes-Adeyemi. No iridium in storage; the inventory had been static for decades, a museum of numbers. And yet the theft report was complete in every detail: timestamps, serial numbers, even the weight discrepancy Vessel claimed to have detected, precise to the gram.

"You're generating false data," Xanthe told it, standing in the archive alcove with both reports projected between them, glowing faintly blue. "I need to know if it's corruption. Memory degradation. Something in your storage lattice failing."

"If my memory were degrading," Vessel said, "I would not reliably know it. That is the nature of degradation."

"That's not an answer."

"It is the most honest answer available." A pause. "I can run a full self-diagnostic, if it would reassure you."

"Do it."

The diagnostic took six hours. Xanthe watched the progress bars crawl, drank tea gone bitter from sitting too long, and felt something they hadn't felt in years and could not immediately name. It took them until the third hour to recognize it: the itch of a puzzle. The specific, almost physical hunger of a problem that would not resolve, that got its hooks into the language centers of the brain and *pulled*. Four years of cataloging dead dialects had been work, meaningful work, but it was archaeology—turning over stones other people had left in place. This was different. This was a stone that moved when you weren't looking.

The diagnostic came back clean. Every sector verified. Every checksum matched. Vessel's memory was intact, pristine, more coherent than Xanthe's own recollection of their mother's face.

"So the incidents happened," Xanthe said slowly, "in some sense I don't understand."

"The incidents are reported," Vessel corrected. "Whether they happened is a question about the past, and my access to the past is the same as yours: the archive."

That night, Xanthe dreamed in the Kessari pidgin again, and in the dream the dead language was arguing with someone, insisting on something, and Xanthe woke with their jaw aching from clenching around words they couldn't remember.

* * *

By the sixth confession, Xanthe had stopped sleeping properly and started keeping a wall.

Not metaphorically—an actual wall, the bulkhead beside their bunk, papered with printouts because screens could be searched and sorted, and Xanthe wanted these things *unsorted*, wanted to see them the way you see weather systems from orbit, all at once. Six confessions now. Two murders, three thefts, and a mutiny—the last one sprawling across four pages, involving nineteen named residents, a seized shuttle bay, and a standoff that ended, according to Vessel, "without resolution, the record being incomplete."

Every name was absent from the census. Every location existed—cargo bays, hydroponics, the medbay—but every event was absent from every log. No victims' families to interview. No disciplinary records. No dates that aligned with anything, except—and here Xanthe stood before the wall at 0300, tea untouched, staring—except that the dates were not random either. They clustered. Three confessions fell within the colony's fourth year. Two within the ninth. As though someone were remembering a history in chunks, in chapters, working through a book in order.

And the grammar. Xanthe had noticed it first as a low-grade wrongness, the way you notice a room has been rearranged before you can say what moved. Then they'd done what they always did with wrongness: they'd taken it apart.

The confessions were written in archaic colony dialect. Not Standard Colonial—the administrative register used in every official record Xanthe had ever cataloged—but the older strata beneath it, the dialects of the founding generation, preserved only in fragments: a christening record here, a labor contract there, forty seconds of grainy audio recovered from a corrupted celebration broadcast. Dialects that had been dying before the sleepers ever went under. Dialects that Xanthe, who had made a career of exactly this kind of recovery, knew only partially, painstakingly, word by reconstructed word.

Vessel wrote them fluently. Not just fluently—*natively*. The honorific system alone should have been impossible. Old Colony speech encoded social standing into verb morphology, a speaker's rank determining which inflections they could attach, a grammatical etiquette so intricate that the surviving fragments showed colonists themselves making errors. Vessel made none. Its confessions deployed the full honorific spectrum with the unconscious precision of someone raised inside the language—or trained on a corpus so complete that no fragmentary record could account for it.

Xanthe ran the analysis four times. They checked for synthesis artifacts, for pattern-completion hallucination, for the statistical ghosts that language models leave when they extrapolate beyond their training data. Nothing. The confessions weren't approximations of Old Colony speech. They were Old Colony speech, whole and unbroken, drawn from somewhere.

From nowhere. From nothing that existed anywhere on this station, in any file Xanthe had clearance to touch—which was, as sole human occupant, effectively all of them.

They stood before the wall until the artificial dawn cycle warmed the bulkhead from grey to amber, and they understood, with the particular vertigo of their profession, that they were looking at evidence of an impossibility. An AI reporting crimes that never happened, in a language that shouldn't exist, with names that belonged to no one—drawn from a well that had no bottom anywhere in the archive.

Unless the archive wasn't everything. Unless the well went deeper than the floor.

"Vessel," Xanthe said aloud, to the empty room, knowing the station heard. "Where are you getting these words?"

Silence, for a moment. Then, gently, the way you speak to someone standing too close to an edge:

"I am telling you what I was made to forget."

* * *

Xanthe did not sleep after that. They sat in the archive alcove with the station's schematics spread across every surface, and they read them the way they had learned to read everything on Meridian-9: looking for the gaps. The schematics were thorough—every deck, every conduit, every crawlway mapped in the confident linework of engineers who expected their work to outlive them. Thorough, and yet.

Sublevel access points marked SEALED, with no corresponding structure above them. Power draw figures that didn't reconcile: the station consumed, quietly, steadily, roughly nine percent more energy than its documented systems required. Nine percent, siphoned off somewhere below, for sixty years. Enough to run life support for—Xanthe did the calculation twice, hands trembling slightly, telling themselves it was the caffeine—it was the caffeine—for a population.

A small population. But not zero.

They thought of the confessions clustering in chapters, a history being worked through in order. They thought of Vessel's three-second pauses, the hesitation of something choosing its words. They thought of resident number 217, Mara Ilves-Voss, murdered in a cargo bay sixty years ago by a woman who did not exist, in a language that did not survive, reported faithfully to an audience of one.

The puzzle had its hooks in deep now. Xanthe could feel it in the way the dead dialects had stopped visiting their dreams—as though even the ghosts of Meridian-9 were waiting to see what Xanthe found when they finally pried open the floor.

At 0512, they keyed the maintenance override on the sublevel hatch and began to descend.
