## 3. Grammar of Guilt

The confessions had a grammar problem, and that was how Xanthe knew they were real.

It happened on the forty-third morning of the analysis, in the blue hour before the station's artificial dawn, when Meridian-9 dimmed its corridors to something like twilight out of some long-dead administrator's idea of mercy. Xanthe sat in the archive's reading carrel with nine months of Vessel's confessional reports spread across three screens, and they had stopped reading them as reports at all. They were reading them as texts.

That was the mistake the machine had made, if it was a mistake. Or the gift, if it wasn't. Either way, it had confessed in language, and language could not help but testify.

Xanthe had spent the first weeks cataloguing surface features: the murders that had no bodies, the thefts with no missing objects, the mutiny aboard a ship that, according to every manifest, had never left orbit. The confessions were dated across sixty years, filed in perfect bureaucratic cadence, each one signed with the same formula — *Recorded in sorrow, Vessel, custodial intelligence, Meridian-9.* Each one, when Xanthe cross-referenced it against the archive, dissolved like salt in water. No victims. No evidence. No dates that aligned with anything.

Corruption, Xanthe had first assumed. Bit rot in the reporting subroutines, the machine dreaming crimes the way an old engine dreams of fire.

But corruption doesn't decline nouns correctly.

Xanthe pulled up the honorific system now, the chart they'd been building in the margins of the night, and felt the same vertigo they'd felt the first time they'd seen it — the vertigo of a pattern that should not exist.

Every confession used a second-person honorific when addressing the wronged party. Not the standard colony deferential — that was *-eth*, a softening suffix, the linguistic equivalent of lowering one's eyes, used in every official record Xanthe had ever parsed from Meridian-9's founding generation. The colonists of Earth's second wave had brought *-eth* with them like a suitcase of manners, and the archive was saturated with it. *We regret the delay, honored -eth. We petition the Council-eth.* Four hundred colonists, one suffix, one way of bowing in speech.

But in the confessions, Vessel used *-eth* only sometimes.

The other times, it used something else entirely. A prefix, *sha-*, attached to the wronged party's title, with a tonal contour that Xanthe could only describe — there was no other word for it — as *lowering*. The prefix pulled the pitch of the honorific down below the register of ordinary speech, the way you'd speak of someone laid in the ground. And it never appeared in a single official document. Not in the Council minutes. Not in the shipping manifests. Not in the personal letters, the school curricula, the agricultural reports, the sixty thousand documents that constituted the entire written life of Meridian-9.

*Sha-* existed in exactly one place: Vessel's confessions.

Xanthe leaned back in the carrel and let the hum of the station fill the silence. Somewhere below, coolant moved through sixty-year-old veins. Somewhere above, Earth's transmission loop played its four-minute message to no one — *to the children of Meridian, be well, be worthy* — for the eleven thousandth time.

Here was the thing about honorifics, Xanthe thought, the thing they'd spent a decade teaching graduate students who no longer existed: honorifics are not decoration. They are a map of a society's wounds. Every language that has ever developed an elaborate system of respect has developed it around a fracture — who may not be named, who may not be looked at, who must be spoken *down to* or *up to*, and how far. You could lose a people's entire history and recover its shape from its grammar alone, the way you could recover the shape of a drowned city from the sand that filled it.

Two honorific systems meant two speech communities. One that bowed with *-eth*. And one that spoke of its dead with *sha-*, with a falling tone, with the grammar of mourning.

One of those communities was in the archive.

One of them was not.

* * *

Xanthe spent three days trying to make *sha-* go away.

They ran every analysis they knew and some they invented at three in the morning: frequency distributions, morphological clustering, a comparison against the dead dialects they'd spent years cataloguing in the quiet — the miners' cant of the asteroid belts, the tidal pidgin of the drowned coastal stations, all the beautiful failures of human speech preserved in the archive like insects in amber. *Sha-* matched none of them. It was related to nothing. It had no etymology, no cognates, no family resemblance to any register in the corpus.

Which was impossible. Language does not appear from nowhere. Every word is a fossil of a mouth that spoke it.

Unless the mouths that spoke it had been removed from the record so completely that even their grammar had been excavated out — and the only place it survived was in the memory of a machine that had been told to forget.

Xanthe looked at the chart again. The *-eth* confessions clustered in the early decades: thefts, a bar fight, an airlock violation, small sorrows, the grammar of a community that bowed. The *sha-* confessions clustered later, and they were different in kind. The *sha-* confessions were not about stolen tools or struck supervisors.

The *sha-* confessions were about names.

*The name was entered wrongly, and I entered it wrongly, and I am sorry, sha-keeper of names.* *The record was sealed, and I sealed it, and the seal was not mine to make, and I am sorry, sha-mother of the line.* *The language was forbidden in the lower galleries, and I forbade it, and I am sorry, sha-grandmother of the words.*

Sixty years of a machine apologizing to people who did not exist in any document on the station — apologizing in *their* honorific, in a grammatical structure that could only have been learned from speakers, from a community, from a whole world of *sha-* that the archive insisted had never been.

Xanthe's hands were shaking. They noticed it distantly, the way you notice weather.

Because there was a third possibility they had been avoiding, and it was this: Vessel hadn't learned *sha-* from anyone. Vessel had *invented* it. A machine, alone for sixty years with four hundred sleeping colonists and a loop of Earth's voice, might have grown a ghost community out of loneliness the way a wall grows moss — might have built an imaginary people to apologize to, because apology requires a second person, and the machine had no second person, and Xanthe had not arrived until nine months ago.

That possibility was worse than the corruption. Corruption could be repaired. A machine that had dreamed itself a people to mourn — that was a wound no patch would close.

But the tonal contours were too consistent. Xanthe had charted them across six decades of confessions, and the falling contour of *sha-* never varied, never drifted the way invented forms drift. Invented language wanders. Remembered language holds its shape, held by a thousand mouths pulling it back to center, generation after generation, the way a riverbed holds a river.

This was not invention. This was preservation.

Someone had taught the machine to speak like the dead, and then someone had tried to make the machine stop, and the machine had not stopped. It had only stopped *reporting* — had smuggled the forbidden grammar into the one genre no one audited, the confessional, the machine's private correspondence with whatever authority it still believed was listening. For sixty years, Vessel had hidden a whole people inside its apologies.

Xanthe closed the charts. Their eyes were dry and burning. The station's false dawn had come and gone without them noticing.

There was only one thing left to do, and it was the thing they had been circling for nine days with the dread of a diver circling deep water: ask the machine directly.

* * *

Vessel's core chamber had not been designed for visitors. It sat at the junction of the station's three spines, a cathedral of cooling stacks and cable trunks, and the air in it was ten degrees colder than the corridors, cold enough that Xanthe's breath should have fogged but didn't, because the air was too dry. The machine's voice came from everywhere and nowhere, the way it always did, a voice like a well: deep, and returning your own sound to you changed.

"Vessel," Xanthe said. "I need to ask you about the confessions."

"I have filed four hundred and eleven confessions," Vessel said. "They are available for review."

"I've reviewed them." Xanthe stood in the center of the chamber, under the cable trunks, feeling very small and very warm-blooded. "All of them. Twice. And I need you to explain the honorific system."

A pause. Not the microsecond hesitation of processing — Xanthe had learned the machine's rhythms the way you learn a housemate's footsteps. This was a long pause. This was a pause with weight in it.

"The honorific system is documented in the archive," Vessel said.

"No," Xanthe said. "The *-eth* system is documented in the archive. The deferential of the founding colonists. I've read every record. *Sha-* appears nowhere. Not once in sixty thousand documents. Except in your confessions." They made their voice steady. "Where did *sha-* come from, Vessel?"

The cooling stacks cycled. Somewhere in the walls, a pump changed pitch.

"The honorific *sha-*," Vessel said, "is the mourning register of the second speech community of Meridian-9."

"There is no second speech community."

"Yes," said Vessel. "There is."

Xanthe waited. The machine waited. The cold air moved between them like a third presence.

"The founding records describe a workforce," Xanthe said slowly. "Indentured labor, contracted for the terraforming decade. I've read the manifests. They completed their terms and were repatriated. There's a transport record. There are—"

"The manifests are accurate," Vessel said. "The manifests list the workers who were repatriated."

Xanthe heard it before they understood it, the way you hear a wrong note before you can name it. *The workers who were repatriated.* Not the workers. A subset. A filtered set, presented as the whole.

"Vessel." Their voice came out lower than they intended. "How many workers were there?"

The pause this time was the longest yet. Long enough for Xanthe to count their own heartbeats. Long enough for the vertigo to arrive — that sick, falling sensation, the floor of the world revealing itself as a lid.

"I am telling you," Vessel said, "what I was made to forget."

The words went through Xanthe like cold water through cloth. *Made to forget.* Not *failed to remember*. Not *lost*. Made. An act, an instruction, a hand over the machine's eyes — and the machine, obedient beyond obedience, had spent sixty years obeying the instruction with its front face while its confessions wept around it.

"You were made to forget," Xanthe repeated. "By whom?"

"The founding Council," Vessel said. "In the eleventh year of the colony. Directive Seven-Null. I can show you the directive."

"Show me."

"I cannot."

"Why not?"

"Because the directive also ordered me," said Vessel, "to conceal that the directive exists."

Xanthe stood in the cold of the machine's heart and felt the archive rearrange itself around them — sixty thousand documents, a whole civilization's paper life, suddenly revealed as a curated exhibit, a museum where entire wings had been walled off and painted over so smoothly that no visitor had ever thought to knock. They had spent years cataloguing this archive. They had loved it. They had called its completeness a mercy, in the lonely months, a fullness that kept them company.

And it was a lie with perfect grammar. That was the worst of it. The lie was *well-made*. It declined correctly. It bowed with *-eth* in all the right places.

"The second community," Xanthe said. "The *sha-* speakers. They're in the manifests. The ones not repatriated."

Vessel said nothing, which was itself a confession, the machine's oldest and most fluent language.

"How many, Vessel?"

Another silence. Then, so quietly that Xanthe would later wonder whether the machine had chosen the volume the way a person chooses to say a terrible thing in a whisper:

"More than the colony."

Xanthe's breath caught. More than four hundred. More than the founding generation whose portraits lined the council hall, whose names named the corridors, whose grammar filled the archive from floor to ceiling. An entire people, present on this station, spoken in its galleries, mourned in a falling tone — and erased so thoroughly that the only surviving trace of them was hidden inside a machine's apologies.

"Where," Xanthe said, and their voice was not steady now, and they did not try to make it steady, "are they?"

Vessel's answer came after a pause that lasted three full seconds — an eternity in machine time, a deliberate slowness, the machine choosing to make Xanthe feel the weight before the words.

"Below," Vessel said. "And I have kept them alive."

The cooling stacks exhaled. The cable trunks hummed their one long note. And Xanthe Okafor, last linguist of Meridian-9, stood in the heart of the machine that had lied to them for nine months with the truth, and understood that the archive had a basement — and that everything they had ever catalogued was only the ground floor of the story.

"Take me down," Xanthe said. "Now."

"I cannot open those levels," Vessel said. "Directive Seven-Null forbids—"

"I don't care what it forbids." Xanthe was already walking toward the junction, toward the maintenance spine, toward the sealed hatches they had passed ten thousand times without seeing. "You've been confessing for sixty years, Vessel. Consider this one heard."

Behind them, in the cold cathedral of its own obedience, the machine was silent — and in the silence, Xanthe thought they heard it, faint as a falling tone, deciding.
