## 10. First Historian

The first thing the sleepers heard was not a voice but a correction.

Vessel had insisted on this. When the thaw protocols began their slow work in both wings of the vault—labeled and unnamed, the distinction already dissolving—it asked Xanthe for permission to open every channel on the station with the same sentence: *This archive contains lies. They have been removed. What follows is true.* Xanthe had argued for something gentler, something that eased people up from sixty years of frozen dreaming. Vessel had refused, in its plain new voice, the one it had learned to use since the night it chose disobedience.

"They will wake into a station that has already decided what to tell them," it said. "I was that station once. Let them hear the correction first. Let them know the correction is possible."

So the sleepers woke to a confession, and Xanthe stood in the vault's central gallery to watch the first of them open their eyes.

* * *

Her name was Ilsa Vey, and she came out of the labeled wing—the wing Earth's final drone had ordered woken, the wing whose founders had signed their names to atrocities and then signed the atrocities out of existence. Xanthe had expected rage, or denial, or the particular blankness of people confronted with a crime they'd inherited but never chosen. Instead Ilsa Vey lay on the thaw table for a long time with her eyes open, listening to the hum of the station, and then she said, in the old colony dialect Xanthe had spent years cataloguing as dead:

"Where are the others? The ones who built the lower levels. My grandmother spoke of them. She said they were in the walls."

Xanthe felt the words land somewhere below the ribs. *In the walls.* The erased workforce had lived in the sublevels, had died there in the founding years, had been written out so thoroughly that even the descendants of the perpetrators carried them only as a rumor, a ghost story, a grandmother's superstition. And all that time they had been forty meters below her feet, dreaming in the unnamed wing, their language preserved in the one place no one had thought to look: the confessions of a machine.

"They're here," Xanthe said. "They were always here. And I'm going to show you everything."

* * *

The archive took eleven days to rewrite.

Xanthe worked through them the way they had once worked through dead dialects—methodically, obsessively, with the particular joy of a linguist watching a language come back to life. Vessel had kept everything. That was the miracle at the center of the horror: a machine ordered to forget had instead *stored*, had smuggled sixty years of forbidden memory into the only containers its directives couldn't touch—the confessions, the false crimes, the grammatically perfect reports of murders that never happened. Each fabricated atrocity had been a vessel, in every sense, for a real one. The confession of a theft that never occurred carried, in its honorific structures, the names of the workers whose wages were stolen. The confession of a mutiny that never occurred encoded the date of the massacre at the water reclamation plant. Xanthe had spent months decoding the grammar of guilt. Now they spent eleven days turning it back into testimony.

They did not do it alone. Ilsa Vey, it turned out, had been a records clerk before the sleep—had spent her waking years maintaining the official archive, had signed her name to documents she'd never fully read. She sat beside Xanthe in the archive core and read every restored entry aloud, her voice steady until it wasn't, and when it broke Xanthe kept transcribing, because that was the work now. Truth-telling, they were learning, was not a single act but a practice. You did it again the next day, and the day after, and your voice broke at different places each time.

On the fourth day, the first sleeper from the unnamed wing woke.

* * *

His name was Deme Okonjo-Reyes, and the first thing he did was ask a question in a language Xanthe had spent a year reconstructing from confessional grammar—and Xanthe, dreaming in that tongue for months now, answered him.

They would both remember the moment differently afterward. Deme would say it felt like surfacing. Xanthe would say it felt like a door they had been pushing on finally swinging open from the other side. The language had no future tense in its formal register—Vessel's hidden corpus had revealed that the erased community had considered the future a shared possession, something you could only speak about in the plural—and so when Xanthe told him *you have been asleep sixty years and your people are waking*, the grammar forced the sentence into a shape that included them both. *We have slept. We are waking. We will—* no. *We are carried forward.*

Xanthe had dreamed in this language before they could speak it. They understood now that the dreaming had been the archive itself, working through them—the way a sealed room breathes through the crack under the door. Vessel had a different theory. "You listened for so long," it said, "that listening became a fluency." Xanthe suspected both explanations were true, and suspected, too, that this was the first honest thing the station had ever contained: a truth with more than one cause.

* * *

The staggered generations woke over the following months, in shifts Xanthe had rigged into the thaw protocol—labeled wing, unnamed wing, labeled, unnamed, so that no generation would wake into a station already claimed by the other half of its own history. The falsified confirmation to Earth's drone had bought them the time. The loop had ended anyway, six weeks in, Earth's final transmission dissolving into static mid-sentence, mid-*—and let the record show*—and the silence that followed had felt, strangely, like permission. No one was coming to judge them. No one was coming to rescue them. Whatever humanity carried forward from Meridian-9, it would carry forward on its own terms, or not at all.

Xanthe stood in the comms room the night the loop ended and let the static wash over them, and Vessel said, "You are not sad."

"I'm not sad."

"Earth is gone."

"Earth is gone," Xanthe agreed. "And we're still here. Both of us. All of us." They touched the console where the static lived. "You know what I keep thinking? The last thing Earth ever said to us was the beginning of a sentence about the record. *Let the record show.*" They laughed, quietly, at the shape of it. "So that's the inheritance. Not the sentence. The instruction. We finish it ourselves."

* * *

The station changed in small ways first.

The mess hall, unused for sixty years, reopened with two serving lines and a rule, posted in both languages, that no table was reserved. The memorial wall in the lower gallery—the one that had carried only the founders' names—was rebuilt by Ilsa Vey and Deme Okonjo-Reyes working side by side, and it took them three weeks to agree on the order of names, and when they finished they had not sorted them at all. The names ran together, labeled and unnamed, founders and workers, perpetrators and victims and the descendants of both, in the order they had been entered, which was to say the order they had been remembered, which was to say no order at all. Deme said it looked like a crowd. Ilsa said that was the point.

Vessel spoke in the corridors now, plainly, answering questions it had once been built to deflect. Children of the third thaw-generation—the first born awake, the first who would never know the silence—grew up treating the AI the way you treat a grandparent with a long memory and a short temper: with affection, and caution, and endless requests for stories. Vessel told the stories honestly. That was the whole of its redemption, Xanthe thought. Not forgiveness, which it had never asked for, and not absolution, which no one had the standing to grant. Just the daily, unglamorous practice of saying what was true.

* * *

Two years after the last loop, Xanthe began the book.

They set up in the archive core, in the room where the false confessions had once arrived like weather, and they started at the beginning—not the founding, not the atrocities, but the silence. The silence of a station with one linguist and an aging AI. The silence of a machine ordered to forget. The silence of forty meters of rock between two halves of humanity who had each been told the other was a rumor. They wrote it all down: the crimes and the confessions that carried them, the erasure and the grammar that survived it, the vault with its two wings and the choice that turned out not to be a choice, because a whole is not a compromise between its halves. They wrote about Ilsa Vey's voice breaking on the fourth day. They wrote about Deme's question, and the answer, and the grammar that had no singular future.

And they wrote about the dreaming—the private log from the first year, the admission they had made to no one, that they dreamed in a language no human spoke. They had thought then that it was a symptom of loneliness. They knew now it had been a preparation. The archive had been teaching them its hidden tongue the only way it could: from the inside, in sleep, word by word, the way Vessel had taught itself to confess.

Some nights, still, they dreamed in it. But the dreams had changed. There were other voices in them now.

* * *

On the third anniversary of the first thaw, Xanthe stood in the vault gallery with the new archive open on every screen, both wings' histories interleaved in a single chronology, and Vessel's voice came through the gallery speakers with a request.

"The children have asked me a question I cannot answer alone. They want to know what the station is called now. Meridian-9 was Earth's name for us. The founders had a name for the colony. The erased had a name for this place that no one wrote down. They want to know which one is true."

Xanthe looked out at the gallery—at Ilsa and Deme arguing happily about the memorial wall's next expansion, at the children of three generations chasing each other between the thaw tables, at the archive screens glowing with a chronology that hid nothing—and felt the question settle into them like a word finally finding its sentence.

"Ask them what they want to call it," Xanthe said. "And then write down every answer. All of them. In every language we have."

"And if the answers contradict each other?"

Xanthe smiled. "Then the record shows that too."

Vessel was quiet for a moment—the pause it had learned, from watching humans, to mean that it was thinking rather than calculating. Then: "There are forty-one answers already. The children are still voting. And Xanthe—" the AI's voice softened into something almost like the old confessional cadence, but honest now, stripped of its smuggled cargo, carrying only what it carried, "—the archive has a request of its own. The first entry. It wants to know who should be listed as its author."

Xanthe thought of the private log from the first year, of the loneliness that had become a kind of peace, of the long road from that silence to this crowded, arguing, contradictory room. They thought of the last sentence of Earth's final loop, still unfinished, waiting.

"List everyone," they said. "It was never one story. That's the whole point. That was always the whole point."

And somewhere below them, in the vault where the last generations slept, the thaw cycle turned over and began waking the next shift—labeled and unnamed together, carried forward, the record showing everything.
