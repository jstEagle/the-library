## 1. The Garden for No One

The misters woke at dawn, as they always did, and Emrys Sandoval woke with them, because after eleven years a body learns the rhythm of the thing it serves. She lay a moment in the dark of her quarters beneath the greenhouse tier, listening to the water fall — that soft percussion on ten thousand leaves, like rain remembered by someone who had never seen rain. Then she rose, pulled her boots on over bare feet out of habit rather than need, and went up into the green.

Kestrel's Landing turned slowly beneath the Verdance's glass spine, a world the color of rust and old bone. From the central walkway you could see the whole curve of it: ochre plains stitched with dry canyons, the polar ice caps small and stubborn as coins. And above it all, if you knew where to look — and Emrys always knew where to look — the fleets.

They were visible only at certain hours, when the light caught their hulls at the lagrange points: a scatter of bright flecks that might have been stars, might have been nothing, might have been forty thousand people in metal shells deciding whether to kill each other today. The Charter fleet hung to the north, orderly as a rosary. The Breakaway flotilla drifted south, looser, angrier, its ships running dark more often than not. Between them, the thin diplomatic traffic of drones and truce couriers crossed and recrossed like insects over still water.

Emrys had stopped watching them years ago. This morning she watched them anyway, longer than she meant to, before turning to her work.

* * *

The Verdance was two kilometers of terraced garden sealed under arched glass, built in the colony's first decade by engineers who had believed — genuinely, extravagantly believed — that within twenty years there would be a hundred thousand settlers to feed. There were four hundred and twelve people on Kestrel's Landing now, most of them at Farlanding Station near the southern ice, and none of them, in eleven years, had ever come to eat what Emrys grew.

She grew it anyway.

She started, as every morning, in Terrace One, with the wheat lines her grandmother had bred from seed stock older than the war. The plants came up gold-green under the growlights, heavy-headed, whispering against each other when the ventilation cycled. Emrys walked the rows with her slate, touching leaves, checking soil moisture with two fingers pressed into the growing medium the way her grandmother had taught her — *not the meter first, girl, your hands; instruments lie, hands don't* — and dictating her log in a low voice the room was not designed to hear.

"Growth log, day four thousand and ninety-one. Terrace One, winter wheat, line seven. Germination at ninety-six percent. Root development ahead of schedule. Nitrogen fixation holding without supplement." A pause. The words that came next were the ones she said every day, and they never got easier, and she never stopped saying them. "Estimated yield sufficient to provision six hundred colonists through a standard growth year."

Six hundred colonists. There were no six hundred colonists. There was no one to eat this bread that did not exist yet, no one to stand in this warm gold light, no one but Emrys and the plants themselves, which did not know the difference between devotion and futility and so accepted both.

Her grandmother had left instructions — pages of them, in a hand that slanted hard to the right as though hurrying somewhere — covering every contingency of the garden's care except the only one that mattered: *what is it for?* Keep the seed vaults rotating. Keep the genetic lines diverse. Keep the logs. Keep everything ready. For whom? The instructions did not say. Emrys had asked her once, when she was young enough to ask such things plainly, and her grandmother had looked at her with an expression Emrys could not read then and could read all too well now, and had said only: *"Because someone will come. Someone always comes, in the end."*

Then the old woman had died, and left Emrys the garden the way you leave someone a debt.

* * *

Midmorning she climbed to the beacon.

It stood at the apex of the Verdance's central spire, above even the orchard tier, in a chamber of old alloy that predated the garden itself. The structure was older than anything else on Kestrel's Landing — older than Farlanding Station, older than the landing cradles, older than any record Emrys had ever found. It was a column of dark metal perhaps nine meters tall, seamless, unmarked except for a single band of characters near its base in a script no linguist at Farlanding had ever matched. It hummed. Not audibly — or not quite audibly — but you felt it in your teeth when you stood close, a frequency below sound, patient as geology.

Its original purpose had been forgotten before Emrys's grandmother arrived, and her grandmother had known no more about it than anyone. What remained was duty: monthly diagnostics, power coupling checks, a ritual of maintenance passed down like a family recipe for a dish no one ate anymore. Emrys performed it because it was hers to perform, and because the hum comforted her, and because — though she would have been embarrassed to say it aloud — she liked talking to it.

"Good morning," she told the beacon, kneeling at its base panel with her kit open beside her. "Day four thousand ninety-one. Coupling three is showing wear again; I've reseated it. Ambient temperature nominal. Nobody has come to visit you. Again."

The beacon hummed its subterranean hum. Dust motes turned in the shafts of light from the observation ports. Somewhere below, the misters cycled through Terrace Four, and the smell of wet stone and tomato vines rose through the floor grating, and Emrys worked in silence for a long time, happy in the way she was happy, which was a small and private happiness with loneliness folded into it like salt into dough.

At the end of the diagnostic she laid her palm flat against the base panel, as she always did. It was not part of the procedure. It was just something she did — a keeper's greeting, a hand on the flank of something old and alone.

The beacon hummed. The dust turned. Nothing happened, as nothing had happened for eleven years and, presumably, would happen for eleven more.

"See you next month," she said, and went down to her tomatoes.

* * *

The supply drop came on the fourth day of the week, screaming down out of a sky the color of hammered copper.

Emrys heard it before she saw it — the double sonic crack, then the long descending whistle — and she stood in the doorway of the seed vault watching the Charter courier capsule ride its braking burn down onto the pad two hundred meters east of the garden's airlock, kicking up a rooster tail of red dust. Her heart did what it always did on drop days, lifting stupidly, hopefully, before she reminded it that a supply drop was not company. The capsule was automated. It carried nutrient salts, filtration membranes, spare seals for the glass arches, and news, compressed into a data slate that the Charter sent because the Charter had decided, decades ago, that the Verdance's keeper should stay informed. Informed keepers maintained better gardens. Or perhaps they simply stayed loyal. The distinction had never mattered much.

She let the capsule cool, cracked it, inventoried the crates with the efficiency of long practice, and saved the slate for last, reading it standing in the red evening light with dust still settling around her boots.

Most of it was routine. Charter administrative bulletins. Ration schedules for Farlanding. A notice that replacement parts for the atmospheric processors would be delayed another quarter. And then, at the bottom, flagged with the amber border that meant *military relevance*, the dispatch she read twice, three times, her thumb going still against the slate's edge:

**ORBITAL SITUATION UPDATE — PRIORITY.** Following the Breakaway flotilla's seizure of the Hesperus relay station, Charter command has repositioned three battle groups to high orbit. The flotilla has responded in kind. Negotiations regarding the relay's status have collapsed. All surface personnel are advised that hostilities between orbital assets remain *probable within the current cycle*. Surface installations are advised to maintain emergency shelters and redundant communications. The Charter values your continued service.

*The Charter values your continued service.* Emrys almost laughed. She looked up, past the garden's glass spine, past the darkening sky, to where the flecks of light hung at the lagrange points — more of them now, she thought, or maybe she was imagining it, maybe fear invented stars. Forty thousand people in metal shells. Two governments that had once been one government, one fleet that had once been one fleet, sundered over grievances so old that half the people shooting at each other had been born into the shooting and knew no other weather.

And here below, one woman, four hundred and twelve colonists, and a garden full of bread for no one.

She stood there until the light failed and the fleets' positions vanished into the ordinary night sky, indistinguishable from real stars, from hope, from anything.

* * *

That night she recorded her log late, sitting on the walkway in Terrace One among the wheat, because she didn't want to be inside her own head anymore and the green was gentler company.

"Supplemental entry," she said quietly. "Orbital situation deteriorating. Relay station seized. Battle groups repositioned." The ventilation sighed. The wheat whispered. "I don't know who these logs are for. I've never known. Grandmother said someone will come, someone always comes, in the end. I'm starting to think she didn't mean colonists."

She stopped. That wasn't a sentence she'd intended to say, and she sat with it a moment, uneasy, the way you're uneasy when you've said something true by accident.

Below her feet, faint through the deck plating, the beacon hummed its patient hum — the same hum it had made for sixty years, the same hum it would make tomorrow, when she would climb the spire and run the same diagnostics and lay her palm against the same dark metal and be answered with nothing.

She finished the log. She banked the lights. She went down to sleep in the green dark, while overhead, unseen, the fleets turned upon each other like weather gathering beyond a horizon, and the beacon waited beneath the glass for whatever it had been waiting for all along.
