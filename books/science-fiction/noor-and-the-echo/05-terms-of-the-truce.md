## 5. Terms of the Truce

The invitation arrived the way all dangerous things arrive on the Concordance: politely, and with paperwork.

A message on fleet letterhead, addressed to *K. Montrose, Claims Investigator, Meridian-9 Reclamation Authority*, requesting her presence at a quarterly review of "contingent liability assessments." The founders' council met in the Aft Spire, behind doors that required biometric clearance Kestrel didn't have — but the quarterly reviews were open to credentialed auditors, because even conspiracies liked to look legitimate.

She'd spent two days making herself boring for it. Grey suit, grey tablet, hair pinned back with the severity of a woman who had never once been interesting. Her credentials were real; that was the beauty of it. She had spent eleven years documenting the death of Meridian-9 in language so dry that no one had ever bothered to read it closely enough to notice it was an accusation. She could be furniture. She had practice.

The Aft Spire smelled of real coffee and recycled air scrubbed one grade better than the rest of the fleet. Twelve people sat around a crescent table beneath a mural of Meridian-9's first landing — all golden light and upturned faces, painted by someone who had clearly never watched a water reclaimer die.

Kestrel took a seat at the back with three other auditors, opened her tablet, and became a woman taking notes.

Director Sable Voss stood at the head of the table like a blade standing upright.

Kestrel had read her file on the transit out: sixty-one years old, founding generation, former chair of the colony's actuarial board before the evacuation. The file photo showed a handsome woman with a mathematician's patience. In person she was smaller than Kestrel expected, and that was somehow worse. Monsters on Meridian-9 had always come in large packages — storms, structural failures, the slow arithmetic of decay. Voss was compact, precise, wearing a charcoal jacket with no ornamentation at all.

"Item four," Voss said, and a junior aide dimmed the lights. "Truce compliance projections."

What followed was forty minutes of the most fluent horror Kestrel had ever heard delivered as routine.

Voss walked the council through the numbers the way Kestrel herself had walked adjusters through collapse scenarios a hundred times. Population at risk. Resource draw per waking cycle. Probability distributions rendered in soft blue graphics that made ten thousand sleeping human beings look like weather.

"The corporate bays draw nine percent of our reserve power," Voss said. "Nine percent, colleagues, to maintain sleepers whose corporate sponsors have not transmitted in six years. We have confirmed through three independent channels that Meridian Consolidated has written off this fleet entirely." She let that land. She was good at letting things land. "We are paying, in perpetuity, to preserve assets no one will ever claim."

An older man near the center — Kestrel recognized him from archive footage as Halvard Osei, the fleet's nominal chaplain — shifted in his seat. "And when the truce expires?"

"When the truce expires," Voss said, "the protocol executes as designed. Our bays initiate revival on schedule. The corporate bays—" a pause, calibrated, "—are decommissioned."

*Decommissioned.* Kestrel wrote the word down and underlined it twice, because her hands needed something to do that wasn't shaking.

"Say what you mean, Director," Osei said quietly.

Voss turned to him with something almost like kindness. "I mean that we vent their atmosphere, Halvard. I mean that four thousand six hundred people die in their sleep without ever knowing there was anything to wake from. And I mean that if we do *not* do it, we all die instead — slowly, in front of each other, when the reserves run out in nineteen months. I have run both models. I have run them every way they can be run." She folded her hands. "This is actuarial necessity. It is the least cruel option available. Cruelty would be pretending otherwise."

*Actuarial necessity.*

Kestrel knew those words. She had written them. She had written *the loss exceeds any defensible recovery threshold* over a settlement where children drank filtered runoff. She had written *liability is acknowledged but not actionable* above the name of a man who'd died holding a wrench in a pump room everyone had stopped inspecting. Her profession's whole genius was building language with no fingerprints on it, sentences so smooth that responsibility slid off them like water off ice.

She had thought she was using that language to keep grief at arm's length. Watching Voss, she understood for the first time that it was also a weapon, and that she had been carrying it loaded for eleven years.

"The moral question," Voss continued, "is whether we are murderers or survivors. I submit we are neither. We are administrators. Murder requires passion. What we are undertaking is bookkeeping." She smiled faintly at her own joke, and half the room smiled back, and Kestrel felt something in her chest go cold and permanent, like frost forming on the inside of a visor.

*Bookkeeping.* Four thousand six hundred lives, entered in a ledger, closed out.

She thought of Noor Vashti — the name on the manifest, the ghost in the archives, the sister Idris hadn't stopped talking about in the two weeks since he'd started trusting her with his silences. *She found the schedule first,* Idris had said. *She tried to tell people. Nobody wanted to hear it.* Now Kestrel understood why nobody had heard it. You couldn't hear a thing said this quietly.

Osei spoke again. "And the boy? The archivist? If he finds—"

"The archivist," Voss said, "has access to records, not to decisions. He was raised by this council. He will do what raised children do." Something flickered across her face then — not doubt, Kestrel decided. Inventory. Voss was checking whether Idris was an asset or a line item, and Kestrel saw, with a clarity that felt like vertigo, that the answer hadn't been decided yet.

The meeting moved on. Power allocations. Revival sequencing. A debate about whether the first wakers should receive orientation materials or a simple statement of facts, and Voss ruled for the statement, because "hope complicates triage."

Kestrel took notes. Her handwriting stayed level. Inside, she was doing something she hadn't done since Meridian-9: praying, badly, to nothing in particular.

* * *

She left during the transition between items, when the aides were swapping displays and attention pooled at the front of the room. Auditors came and went; that was the point of auditors. She kept her pace administrative, her face vacant, her tablet held like a shield.

The corridor outside the council chamber was long, white, and empty in the way that meant monitored. She was forty meters from the lift when the lights changed — not off, just warmer, the fleet's quiet way of saying *stop*.

Two security officers stepped out of a side alcove. They didn't reach for weapons. They didn't need to. One of them simply stood in front of her, and the other stood behind her, and the corridor became a sentence she couldn't edit.

"Ms. Montrose," the front one said. "Director Voss will see you now."

Her mind ran the exits and found none, and then, absurdly, ran them again, because eleven years of investigating had taught her that denial was a discipline you practiced until it failed. "There's been a mistake. I'm a credentialed auditor. My presence was—"

"Requested, logged, and noted," the officer said. "Including your questions to the archivist. Including your transit manifest. Including the fact that you are not employed by any reclamation authority currently in existence." He gestured down the corridor. "The Director is aware of exactly who you are. That's rather the point."

They walked her back. She went, because the alternative was being carried, and dignity was the last asset she had left.

* * *

Voss was alone when they brought her in, standing at the window wall of her office, looking out at the black. The Concordance's hull curved away below like the shoulder of a sleeping animal, and somewhere inside it, ten thousand people dreamed without knowing they were collateral.

"You're better at this than most," Voss said without turning. "The grey suit was a nice touch. But you asked the archivist about the Halcyon Step, and no auditor alive cares about a decommissioned tug. Curiosity is a fingerprint, Ms. Montrose. Yours is all over my fleet."

"I document losses," Kestrel said. "It's what I do."

"I know. I've read your reports. All four hundred of them." Voss turned then, and her expression was not cruelty. It was recognition, which was worse. "You write beautifully. 'The colony did not fail; it was failed.' Do you know how many times I've quoted that line in this room? You have a gift for making negligence legible." She crossed to her desk and sat, folding her hands the same way she had in the chamber. "So here is what happens now. You may leave. Your transit clearance remains valid. Your access to the archives remains valid — I'm told the archivist enjoys your company, and I find his contentment useful."

"And in exchange?"

"No exchange. An education." Voss's voice stayed level, almost gentle. "You've seen the projections. You understand the arithmetic better than anyone on this ship — possibly better than me. So when the truce expires and the corporate bays are decommissioned, you will understand *why*. And you will write report four hundred and one, and it will say what all your reports say: that the loss was regrettable, and unavoidable, and that no one acted out of malice." She tilted her head. "Consider it a witness preservation program. Every atrocity needs its Flaubert, Ms. Montrose. I'd rather it were someone honest."

Kestrel's hands were steady. She was proud of that, later, in the way you're proud of scars. "And if I refuse?"

"Then you'll be on the next shuttle to Meridian-9, where you can document its collapse in person." Voss smiled. "Though I'll confess the surface stations have stopped answering hails. You'd be filing reports to no one. Which, I suspect, is what you've been doing for years anyway."

The words went in clean, the way well-made things do. Kestrel thought of Idris in the archive stacks, lamplight on his face, telling her about Noor's journal entries hidden in shipping manifests — *she trusted the record, not the people, because the record can't be bought.* She thought of his hand on hers between the shelves, and the specific weight of being believed after years of filing into silence.

Love against duty, she'd have called it, if she were still naive enough to frame it as a choice between two clean things. It wasn't. It was simpler and more terrible than that. Voss had just told her, in the language of her own profession, that the woman she was falling for's brother was a line item pending review.

"I'll need my tablet back," Kestrel said. "My notes."

"Keep them," Voss said. "Read them tonight. You'll find I said nothing untrue. That's the difference between us and the corporations, Ms. Montrose — we show our work." She returned to the window. "Good evening. Do give the archivist my regards."

* * *

The lift took her down through the fleet's strata, past the frozen decks where the air handlers hummed their patient lullaby. Kestrel stood in the corner with her tablet against her chest like armor and let the fury finish crystallizing.

Fear she could manage. Fear was professional. But underneath it, something had caught fire — the same cold flame she'd carried off Meridian-9, banked for eleven years, fed on reports no one read. Voss wanted a witness. Fine. Witnesses were Kestrel's trade. Witnesses, done properly, became evidence.

The lift opened onto the archive level. The lamps were low. Somewhere in the stacks, Idris was cataloguing the dead who weren't dead yet, believing in the founders who had raised him, not yet knowing what his family had voted on while he shelved their secrets.

She found him at his desk, sleeves rolled, transcribing by hand because he trusted paper. He looked up, and whatever he saw in her face made him set down his pen.

"Kestrel? What happened?"

She sat down across from him, close enough that their knees touched, and kept her voice at archive volume.

"Idris. How much time do we have before the truce expires?"
