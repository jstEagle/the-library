## 6. What the Ice Remembers

The archive stacks ran the length of Deck Nine, a cathedral of cold storage and older sins. Idris worked nights there, when the fleet's hum dropped to its lowest register and the only light came from the amber status lamps above each cryo-bay — ten thousand small suns, one for every sleeper he'd sworn to keep.

Kestrel started coming after her third week aboard, ostensibly to cross-reference shipping manifests against the evacuation ledgers. Ostensibly.

"You're here again," Idris said, not looking up from his terminal. His voice carried the flat warmth of someone who had learned not to expect company and was quietly pleased by it anyway.

"Your records are incomplete."

"My records are the most complete thing on this fleet."

"Then your fleet is incomplete." She set her slate beside his, angled so he could see the discrepancy she'd flagged: forty-one crew transfers with no corresponding berth assignments. "People went somewhere, Idris. Forty-one someones."

He read it twice. Something in his jaw tightened — the look of a man watching a load-bearing wall develop a crack. "Maintenance levels," he said finally. "Sealed since the evacuation. Structural survey said uninhabitable."

"Structural surveys can be written by anyone with a credential."

"Yes," he said. "I know. I've read yours."

She should have been alarmed. Instead she felt something loosen in her chest, the strange relief of being seen accurately. "And?"

"And they're very good." He turned in his chair, and the amber light caught the tired lines of his face. "You document ruin for a living, Kestrel. So do I. The difference is you get paid."

"The difference is I file reports nobody reads. You keep records nobody's allowed to touch." She held his gaze. "We're both curators of things gone wrong."

Something passed between them then that neither of them named. It would take weeks to name it, and by then it would already be true.

* * *

They fell into a rhythm that had nothing to do with investigation and everything to do with hunger — the specific hunger of two people who had spent years being the only ones who remembered correctly.

She brought him real coffee, hoarded from her investigator's stipend. He taught her the archive's dead languages: the founder-era encryption shells, the corporate redaction protocols, the way Noor — his sister, his ghost — had once hidden messages inside checksum errors because she thought it was funny.

"Tell me something true," she said one night, three weeks in, sitting on the floor between the stacks with her shoulder against his knee. The bay lights cycled dim for sleep-shift, and the dark made confessions cheaper.

"I haven't slept more than four hours a night since the evacuation," he said. "I dream about the manifest numbers. Ten thousand, four hundred and six. I count them like sheep. If one goes missing I'll know before the system does."

"That's not a secret. That's a job description."

"Fine." A pause long enough that she heard the coolant pumps cycle twice. "I requested this posting. Nobody assigned me here. I asked to be the archivist because my sister was on the Halcyon Step, and if she ever came back through these doors, I wanted to be the first person she saw."

Kestrel went still. "Idris. The Halcyon Step never launched."

"I know what the registries say." His voice didn't waver, but his hand found hers in the dark and held on. "I also know my sister. And I know that whoever scrubbed those records missed one timestamp, and timestamps don't lie. People do."

She told him about the detention, the security officers who hadn't been surprised to see her, the questions they'd asked about who she'd contacted before boarding. She told him she'd found her own name in a surveillance log, flagged and color-coded, tracked across three decks.

"They're watching both of us," she said.

"They're watching everyone. The question is what we do while they watch." He turned her hand over, traced the callus on her palm where she gripped her stylus. "You could still walk away. Take your payout, catch the next transport, write your four hundred and first report."

"I don't want the transport."

"No?"

"No." She looked up at him, and whatever was in her face made him stop breathing for a second. "I want the truth. And apparently I want the archivist who keeps it."

He kissed her then, carefully at first, the way he handled the old data crystals — like something that could shatter and couldn't be replaced. Then less carefully. The stacks hummed around them, ten thousand sleepers dreaming their frozen dreams, and Kestrel thought: this is what the ice remembers. Not the cold. The warmth people brought to it, and left behind.

* * *

Afterward, lying among the archive crates with her head on his chest, listening to his heart slow, she said, "Show me the maintenance levels."

He tensed. "They're sealed."

"You said structural survey."

"I said what the survey said." He stared at the ceiling. "I've had the access codes for six years. I've never used them."

"Why not?"

"Because sealed doors are promises, Kestrel. You break one, you have to break all of them." He sat up slowly. "But my sister might be behind one of them. So."

* * *

The maintenance levels smelled of rust and recycled air that hadn't been breathed in years. They moved through corridors lit by emergency strips, past hydroponics bays gone feral — tomato vines climbing the walls like they were trying to escape — past a machine shop where someone had been living.

Someone *was* living. There was a bedroll, a water line tapped into the fleet's plumbing with ugly competence, walls papered with printouts. Hundreds of them. Ledger fragments, duty rosters, truce schedules, all annotated in a cramped, furious hand.

"Noor," Idris breathed.

A shape detached itself from the shadows near the far bulkhead — thin, quick, eyes that had adjusted to darkness the way deep-sea things adjust to pressure. She held a cutting torch the way other people held opinions.

"Don't move," Noor Vashti said. Her voice was rusted from disuse. "Either of you."

"Noor. It's me. It's Idris—"

"I know who you are." The torch didn't waver. "That's the problem. You're either with me or you're their archivist, and you can't be both anymore."

"I've never—" His voice cracked. Six years of counting sleepers in his sleep, and now. "Noor. Please."

Kestrel stepped forward slowly, hands visible. "My name is Kestrel Montrose. I'm an insurance investigator. I came looking for a passenger on a ship that never flew, and I found a fleet full of hostages instead. Your journal entries are embedded in the archive checksums. I've read eleven of them."

The torch dipped a centimeter. "You decrypted them?"

"Idris did. I provided the coffee."

Something flickered across Noor's face — not quite humor, but the memory of it, like a muscle remembering its shape. Then it hardened again. "An investigator. Corporate credentialed. You understand why that doesn't make me feel warm."

"I understand exactly what a corporate tool looks like," Kestrel said. "I file reports on dying colonies so boards can price the losses before they happen. Meridian-9 is on my desk right now. Four hundred reports, and every one of them filed into a void. I'm not here for them anymore."

"Everyone says that until it costs them something."

"Then cost me," Kestrel said. "Test me. Use me. I don't care. But you've been alone down here with the truth for years, and the truce clock is running, and you cannot vent-proof ten thousand sleepers by yourself."

Silence. The emergency strips buzzed. Somewhere in the walls, the fleet breathed its slow mechanical breath.

Noor looked at her brother — at the man who had counted her among the missing for six years — and then at the stranger with the corporate credentials and the steady hands.

"The founders rigged the schedule," she said at last, lowering the torch. "When the truce expires, their half wakes clean, and the corporate bays vent quiet. Sable Voss signed off on it herself. I have the order, her seal, everything." She pulled a battered data crystal from her shirt and held it up. "This is the murder weapon. The question is what we do with it."

"We take it to the council—" Idris started.

"They ARE the council." Noor laughed, and it was a terrible sound. "There is no 'them,' brother. There's just us, and ten thousand sleepers, and a countdown."

Kestrel stepped closer, into the torchlight, and met Noor's suspicious eyes without flinching.

"Then show me the schedule," she said. "Because I've spent my career pricing out disasters after they happen. I'd very much like to prevent one for once."

Noor studied her for a long moment — the investigator, the archivist, the ghost, three people standing in a dead corridor with a murder weapon between them.

"Days," Noor said finally. "We have days. Come inside before the thermal sensors pick us up. And Kestrel Montrose—" the torch came back up, briefly, pointing at her like an accusation or a blessing, "—if you betray us, I will show you what six years alone in the dark teaches a person."

"Noted," Kestrel said. "Fair warning: I document everything."

Behind them, faint through the bulkhead, the fleet's chime sounded the hour. Somewhere below, ten thousand hearts beat once a minute in the dark, waiting for morning — and the countdown to decide whose morning it would be.
