## 8. Countdown on the Concordance

The truce had thirty-one hours left on it, and the fleet smelled like a funeral.

Kestrel felt it in the air handlers first — a change in pressure, a faint ozone tang, as if the Concordance itself were holding its breath. Noor felt it in her bones. She'd spent six years learning the ship's rhythms from the inside, and now the ship was winding up for something.

"Venting sequence pre-stages twelve hours before the truce expires," she said, flat as a manifest. "Voss's people will start cycling atmosphere through the corporate bays on a test loop. After that, it's one switch."

"Then we take the switch before they touch it," Kestrel said.

They moved through Deck Nine's maintenance spine, single file, headlamps off, navigating by the emergency strips that Noor had rewired years ago to die in sequence — a private darkness only she could read. Idris carried the archive keys in a case strapped to his chest like a heart he'd finally decided to take out in public. Ten thousand lives, four kilograms of encrypted access.

Kestrel had done the math twice on the transit from the archive stacks to Master Control on Deck One. Eleven decks. Four checkpoints. Two hours if nothing went wrong.

Nothing went right.

* * *

The alarm didn't sound like an alarm. It sounded like a voice — Voss's voice, warm and reasonable, piped through every speaker on the maintenance levels.

"Idris. I know you're listening. You always listen."

Noor froze against the bulkhead. Kestrel watched her brother's face in the strip-light's dying amber, watched him age a decade in four seconds.

"You were the best archivist I ever had," the voice continued, gentle as a claims adjuster. "Meticulous. Loyal. I'm prepared to restore everything. Your quarters. Your clearance. Your sister's record, expunged — she becomes a ghost again, safe, unprosecuted. All you have to do is bring me the keys."

"He's lying about Noor," Kestrel breathed.

"Of course he is," Noor said. "But listen to how he says it. He's rehearsed. He's already planned the version where Idris says yes."

Idris closed his eyes. When he opened them, he looked — Kestrel thought — like a man reading the last page of a book he'd spent his whole life cataloging.

"I need to answer him," he said. "They're tracing the signal. If I don't, they'll flood the maintenance levels and search deck by deck, and you'll never reach Control."

"And if you do—"

"Then they know exactly where I am." He almost smiled. "Kestrel. You're the investigator. Tell me the play."

She hated him for asking. She hated herself more for knowing. "You run loud, we run quiet. You give them a chase that costs them twenty minutes, we take the ventral shaft to Deck One. They'll take you alive — you're worth more to Voss as a negotiator than a corpse."

"Comforting."

"It's the best actuarial assessment you're going to get."

He kissed her then, quick and hard, tasting of dust and archive paper, and Noor looked away with the expression of someone who had already lost everyone once and was rehearsing for a second time.

"Deck One," Idris said. "Don't be late."

* * *

They gave the security teams their twenty minutes.

Idris ran the archive corridors like a man who had built them — killing lights behind him, sealing bulkheads on staggered delays, forcing the pursuit to cut through sections he'd already flooded with coolant vapor. Kestrel and Noor went the other way, down the ventral shaft, climbing rungs in the dark while the fleet shuddered around them.

At Deck Six, Noor stopped.

"Listen."

Kestrel listened. Under the hum of the air recyclers: a new sound, thin and rhythmic. Valves cycling. Somewhere below them, in the corporate bays, the test loop had started early.

"She's not waiting for the truce," Noor said. "The pre-stage is live. We have hours, not a day."

They climbed faster.

* * *

They took Deck One's service corridor at a dead run and hit the last junction two hundred meters from Master Control — and the lights came up all at once, white and total, and Kestrel's night vision died screaming.

When she could see again, there were six of them. Carbines, nonlethal loads, the matte grey of Voss's personal security. And between them, hands cuffed in front of him, bruised at the jaw, stood Idris.

He was smiling. It was the worst thing Kestrel had ever seen.

"They didn't even interrogate me," he said. "I want that on the record. Your people are getting sloppy, Director."

"Idris." Noor stepped out of the shadow of the junction, hands open, empty. "Walk away. Please. For once in your life, walk away."

"I spent my whole life walking away," he said. "Into the archives. Into the records. Into other people's histories because ours was a lie." He lifted the case, still strapped to his chest, and the nearest guard's carbine tracked it. "You want the keys, take them. But you should know what they open. Not just Control. The archive. Every ledger, every order, every signature — including yours, Director. I've broadcast-ready copies in four locations, and the release codes died with my clearance. Kill me, and the fleet wakes up to the truth with no one left alive who can soften it."

"You'd burn everything," Voss's voice said, from a speaker above the corridor, because of course she wasn't there in person. She never was. "Ten thousand people, Idris. Chaos. Violence. You'd trade order for a gesture."

"I'd trade a lie for the truth. It's called an exchange rate. Look it up — you use it every time you price a human life."

The speaker was quiet for a moment. When Voss spoke again, she'd dropped the warmth entirely, and Kestrel heard, beneath the steel, something almost like respect.

"You were raised better than this."

"I was raised by liars. I'm the family disappointment."

"Last offer. The keys, and your old life. Refuse, and my people take them off your body, and your sister watches."

"Noor," Idris said, and his voice cracked down the middle, and he kept going anyway. "Don't negotiate for me. Go."

Kestrel was already moving.

It wasn't bravery, or not only. It was the oldest instinct she had, the one that had carried her through four hundred reports on a dying colony: when the numbers are all bad, you play the only line that hasn't been priced yet. Two guards turned toward her — she was the unknown variable, the off-fleet element, the one face their dossiers hadn't fully mapped — and for one and a half seconds, every carbine in the corridor pointed at a former insurance investigator with a stolen maintenance jack in her fist.

One and a half seconds was enough. Noor came off the wall like six years of feral survival, took the nearest guard's weapon hand and folded it the wrong way, and the corridor went to noise and white flashes and someone shouting *don't kill the archivist* — and Kestrel ran.

She ran past Idris. She didn't look at him. Looking would have stopped her.

"Kestrel—" he shouted, and then a carbine stock took him in the ribs and the shout became a grunt, and she ran.

* * *

Master Control's door recognized her stolen credentials for exactly as long as it took to open, and then the lock re-engaged behind her with a sound like a verdict.

The room was smaller than she'd imagined. A horseshoe of consoles, a wall of status displays, and at the center, glowing patient amber: the truce protocol, counting down. Twenty-six hours, eleven minutes. Below it, in red, the pre-stage indicator, cycling.

*VENTILATION TEST LOOP — BAYS C-1 THROUGH C-14 — ACTIVE*

Fourteen bays. Five thousand sleepers breathing recycled air on a loop that could become a purge with one authorization.

She sat down at the console and did what she had done for eleven years, what she was, in the end, for: she read the system. Claims history. Liability chains. Authorization hierarchies. Voss's fingerprints were all over the venting schedule, elegant and deniable, an actuarial necessity dressed in six layers of procedural cover — and underneath it, the override.

It needed two keys. Of course it did. Founders' paranoia, baked into the fleet's bones: no single person could wake the sleepers or vent them. Two keys, two hands, two signatures.

She had the archive keys. Idris had carried them to her, at the cost of everything.

She had one hand.

The console chimed. Internal channel. She accepted it because there was nothing left to protect.

"Ms. Montrose." Voss, live now, no warmth at all. "You're in my control room."

"Technically it's the colony's control room."

"You have the archive keys and no co-signatory. You can't wake them. You can't stop the loop. You can only die in an interesting room." A pause, and then, almost gently: "Bring me the keys and I'll let you file your report. All of it. I'll even sign it."

Kestrel looked at the countdown. Twenty-six hours. At the red cycling light. At the door, where she could hear boots in the corridor, and beneath the boots, faintly, Noor's voice buying seconds with a stolen carbine and pure fury.

And she thought: *two keys, two hands.*

"Director," she said, "do you know what an investigator does when both parties to a contract are in breach?"

"I imagine she files—"

"She voids the contract." Kestrel pulled up the revival protocol, all ten thousand sleepers, founders and corporate both, and began entering the archive key. "Simultaneous execution. Everyone wakes at once. No half-fleet, no hostages, no purge. Your truce doesn't expire, Voss. It terminates — with full disclosure."

"You can't authorize that alone."

"No," Kestrel said. "But I know someone who can. And you're about to hear him refuse you one more time."

The channel chimed. Incoming — not from the corridor, but from Deck Six, from a maintenance relay, from a man with cracked ribs and a broadcast console and the family talent for showing up in the record at the worst possible moment.

Kestrel looked at the second key field, empty and waiting.

"Idris," she said into the open channel. "I need your hand."
