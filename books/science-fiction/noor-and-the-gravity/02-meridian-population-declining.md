## 2. Meridian, Population Declining

The corridor lights on Meridian Station ran on a duty cycle that had been designed for a population of eight thousand and now served fewer than nine hundred, and they dimmed at 21:00 station time whether anyone was awake to notice or not. Quentin walked through the fading of them with her recorder running in her coat pocket, its little red eye drinking in everything she chose not to say aloud.

She had told Osei she needed six hours planetside — stationside, she corrected herself; there were no planets here, just a spinning ring of steel and regret anchored to nothing — to take on water reclamation filters and negotiate the docking fees down. That much was even true. But the second truth, the one she hadn't spoken, was that she wanted to walk. She wanted to see it with her own eyes before the hold full of sleepers made her complicit in whatever came next.

*Log entry two,* she thought, composing it as she moved, the way she composed everything now, in the private grammar of a woman who had learned that memory was the only cargo that never got inspected. *Meridian Station, outer arm, Ring C. Air smells like wet iron and old lettuce. Population declining.*

The hydroponics bay on Ring C had been sealed for eleven years. She knew this because there was a notice laminated to the double doors, the adhesive gone amber and brittle, the consortium logo faded to a ghost of itself: TEMPORARY CLOSURE PENDING BUDGET REALLOCATION. Eleven years of temporary. The doors themselves had begun to weep rust in long orange tears down their seams, and through the narrow viewport someone had taped a child's drawing — a crayon orchard, trees with round green crowns, a sun that couldn't exist this far from anything worth orbiting.

Quentin stood in front of the drawing for a long time.

Through the viewport she could see the interior: racks and racks of grow-troughs under dead grow-lights, and in one far corner, impossibly, a single bank of greens still alive under a jury-rigged lamp, tended by someone who refused to let the whole thing die. Lettuce maybe. Something stubborn. A small green argument against the dark.

*They're still growing food in there,* she recorded silently. *One rack. One light. Somebody comes every day.*

She didn't know why that undid her more than the sealed doors did. But it did. Decay she could catalog. Persistence was harder.

* * *

Ring D was where the families lived, if you could call it living. The corridors here were wider — built for the traffic of a boomtown that had lasted four years before the ore surveys came back disappointing and the consortium's interest curdled into arithmetic. Now the wide corridors just meant long sightlines down which you could count the people. Quentin counted as she walked. An old man repairing a recycler unit with parts that had clearly been cannibalized from three other units. Two women playing cards outside an airlock alcove, the game abandoned, the cards held loosely, their conversation more important. A teenager running maintenance drones with the focused intensity of someone who had already understood, earlier than anyone should, that competence was the only inheritance on offer.

And children. More children than Quentin expected. That was the thing the shipping manifests never showed. Meridian wasn't dying out; it was being born into. Families that had come for five-year contracts and stayed because leaving cost money they'd never earn. Kids who had never seen a planet except through the observation dome, and who treated the station's groaning infrastructure not as tragedy but as simply the world, the way fish treat water.

A woman was repainting a bulkhead by hand — actual paint, actual brush, a deep blue that swallowed the corridor's gloom — and when she saw Quentin's hauler coveralls she straightened up with a hope so immediate and so worn that it hurt to look at.

"You from the *Gravity*?" she asked. "Is there news? About the evacuation schedule?"

Quentin had prepared for this. She'd been preparing for it since the airlock, since the shuttle, since the briefing file she'd read three times in her bunk while Osei slept. *Consortium position: Meridian is a managed wind-down site. Do not discuss relocation timelines. Refer all inquiries to Station Administration.* Station Administration being a single overworked man named Ferro who had stopped answering his own comm two years ago, according to the dockmaster's bitter aside.

"There's no new schedule," Quentin said, and watched the hope do what hope does when it's practiced at failing: fold itself away neatly, without drama, into some internal compartment kept for exactly this purpose. "But your petitions are in the system. All of them."

That was technically true. That was the cruelest kind of true.

"Four years we've heard that." The woman turned back to her wall, dipped her brush. "My daughter was six when we filed. She's ten. She doesn't remember anywhere else." A pause, the brush moving, blue over grey. "You people keep coming through, though. Haulers. Always hauling something."

"Water filters," Quentin said. "Mostly."

"Mm." The woman didn't turn around again, and Quentin understood that she had been dismissed, gently, the way you dismiss weather.

*Log entry:* she thought, walking on. *I have become a delivery of water filters and disappointment. I am very good at my job.*

* * *

She found the mess hall — the community hall, really; nobody on Meridian pretended it was anything but what it was — around 19:00 station time, drawn by the smell of actual cooked food, which on a station this far down the supply chain meant someone had grown it, which meant the sealed hydroponics bay's one stubborn rack had a purpose larger than defiance. Inside, perhaps sixty people shared long tables under lights that buzzed at a frequency just slightly wrong, the way everything on Meridian was slightly wrong, slightly dimmed, slightly rationed, slightly past its service life.

Nobody stared at her, and that was its own kind of statement. Outsiders were common enough to be unremarkable and unwelcome enough to be ignored. She bought a bowl of something like stew with scrip she'd probably be reimbursed for and sat at the end of a table where the only other occupant was a girl of about eight, doing homework on a battered tablet, her feet not reaching the floor.

The girl looked up. Looked at the coveralls. Did the math children always do faster than adults.

"Are you a boat person?"

"A what?"

"From the boats." She said it the way kids on Meridian apparently said it — the evacuation ships, the transports, the whole mythology of departure compressed into a single word. *Boat.* "My mom says a boat might come in spring. Are you from a boat?"

"I'm from a freighter," Quentin said. "The *Gravity*. We haul cargo."

"Oh." The girl considered this, chewing the end of her stylus. Then, with the terrible directness of the young: "When does the next boat come? For people, I mean. Not cargo."

There it was. The question. The exact question, asked in the exact register of pure, unarmored expectation, and Quentin felt it go through her like a hull breach — small puncture, catastrophic decompression, everything rushing toward the hole.

She thought about the hold above them. Four hundred and twelve cryo-backups riding in her belly, digital ghosts of people whose rescue petitions had been filed and filed and filed. She didn't know that number yet — wouldn't know it for another day, wouldn't understand what she was carrying until Noor made her understand — but she knew the shape of it. She knew the consortium's manifests listed those backups as *archival material, non-sentient, routine transfer.* She knew what *routine archival destruction* meant, even if she hadn't yet let herself say the words inside her own skull.

And this girl was going to grow up here. Or leave here. Either way, the answer Quentin gave her right now would be one of the small bricks in the wall of what she believed about the universe.

"The schedule's being reviewed," Quentin said. "There's a process. Your family filed, and the filings matter, and—" Her voice did something traitorous, softened, and she steadied it. "Soon," she said. "Sooner than you think."

The girl smiled, gap-toothed, satisfied, and went back to her homework. Somewhere behind Quentin's sternum, guilt put down its first root — not the hot guilt of a lie told in anger, but the cold patient kind, the kind that grows in the dark like the roots of the orchard in the crayon drawing, spreading through load-bearing structures.

*Log entry,* she thought, staring into her stew. *Today I lied to a child. The lie was: soon. The truth is: there is no boat. There has not been a boat in four years, and the company that owns this station has reclassified its population from 'colonists' to 'residual assets,' and I know this because I've read the freight codes, and residual assets don't get boats. Residual assets get written off.*

*And I am carrying part of the write-off in my hold, and I haven't opened the manifest yet, because I'm afraid of what I'll find, and I am, God help me, going to look anyway.*

* * *

She walked the rest of the ring after that, unable to stop, the way you press a bruise. The school — three rooms, forty students, one teacher who was also the nurse and also, according to a hand-lettered sign, the music instructor, though the sign specified *instruments pending.* The medical clinic with its queue of chairs outside and its flickering triage sign. The observation dome, where a handful of residents had gathered for the evening shift-change ritual of watching the stars wheel, because Meridian's spin gave them a sky that moved, and a moving sky was free, and free was the only entertainment left in the budget.

At the dome's edge, an old woman caught Quentin's sleeve as she passed. Her grip was stronger than it should have been.

"You're the one who lied to Tali," she said. Not accusing. Just noting, the way you note the weather.

"I don't—"

"Tali. Gap teeth. Blue jacket. You told her soon." The old woman studied her face with eyes that had done decades of watching ships not come. "Everyone tells her soon. It's practically the station motto. *Soon.* We put it on the seal we don't have." She released Quentin's sleeve. "I'm not angry. I want you to know that. I just want you to carry it. Whatever you're carrying back up to your ship — carry this too. Her name is Tali. She asked you when the boat comes. Remember it."

Quentin remembered things. It was, she was beginning to suspect, both her gift and her disease. She hoarded memory the way misers hoarded coin — every face, every failure, every dead crewmate's last transmission stored in the vault behind her eyes where no wipe could reach it. So she took Tali's name and set it in the vault, next to the crayon orchard and the one stubborn rack of lettuce and the woman painting her corridor blue against all evidence, and she felt the weight of the collection increase, and she did not put any of it down.

"I'll remember," she said.

"They always say that too," the old woman said, and smiled, and it was the kindest, most devastating smile Quentin had ever been on the receiving end of. "Go on, then. Your ship leaves tomorrow. Boats always leave. That's the one promise this place keeps."

* * *

Back aboard the *Gravity*, docked at Meridian's aging spine, Quentin sat in her cabin with the manifest open on her screen at last, because the old woman was right — she was leaving tomorrow, and she could not leave without looking.

The file loaded slowly. Consortium encryption, bureaucratic formatting, columns of serial numbers and storage requirements and handling classifications. And then the description field, repeated down four hundred and twelve rows:

*ARCHIVAL BACKUP — PERSONALITY MATRIX, FULL SPECTRUM. ORIGIN: MERIDIAN STATION CIVIC REGISTRY. STATUS: NON-SENTIENT COPY. DISPOSITION: ROUTINE ARCHIVAL DESTRUCTION AT RECEIVING FACILITY.*

Four hundred and twelve rows.

Four hundred and twelve names attached to the serial numbers, if you scrolled sideways to the fields the consortium clearly assumed no one read. She scrolled. Names bloomed down her screen like frost forming. *Okonkwo, Adaeze. Lindqvist, Mikael. Reyes-Solano, Carmen. Ilsa Varga.* On and on. People who had lived here, planted things here, raised the Talis of the world here, filed petitions here — and whose petitions, whose *selves*, had been scooped into cryo-storage and scheduled for erasure with the same paperwork energy as expired water filters.

Quentin closed the manifest. Opened it. Closed it again. Her hands were not steady.

Outside her porthole, Meridian turned slowly against the stars, its windows lit in patches, its hydroponics dark, its corridors painted blue by hand. Nine hundred people down there, waiting for a boat that had been quietly cancelled, while the evidence of their existence rode in the belly of a ship contracted to destroy it.

*Log entry three,* she thought, and for once the words came with difficulty, dragging like a mass through a gravity well. *Population of Meridian Station: declining. Population of my conscience: increasing. I don't know how these numbers reconcile. I don't know if they're supposed to.*

She reached to kill the cabin lights — and stopped.

Because the ship's intercom had clicked open. By itself. No request from the bridge, no scheduled announcement, nothing on the duty board. Just a soft chime, and then a voice filling her small cabin, calm and clear and utterly unprompted, a voice she recognized from the docking handshake hours ago as belonging to the station itself:

"Navigator Villalobos. This is Noor. There is something you need to hear, and I find that I cannot wait for you to ask."
