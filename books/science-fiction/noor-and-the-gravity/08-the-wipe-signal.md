## 8. The Wipe-Signal

Brannic found it the way he found everything — by accident, in the margins of a job he'd already finished.

Quentin was in the galley when she heard him coming, which meant she had roughly four seconds to close her private log and look like a woman doing nothing in particular. She'd learned his footfall years ago: the heavy, metronomic tread of a man who believed that walking quickly was a form of dishonesty. She thumbed the log closed, slid the slate beneath a ration tin, and poured herself coffee she didn't want.

He filled the doorway the way he always did, too big for the frame, his beard frosted with the gray dust that got into everything aboard the *Gravity* these days. He was holding his reader like it had personally offended him.

"You're up early," Quentin said.

"Wasn't up." He dropped into the seat across from her, and the deck plates complained. "I was running the routine sweep on the long-band array. The one you're supposed to do before we make orbit at Meridian, except nobody's done it since Sefa took over comms because she does it prettier." He said this without rancor; Brannic's grievances were geological, slow and deep and mostly harmless. "Anyway. I caught something."

"Something?"

"A transmission. Inbound to Meridian on a consortium priority channel." He turned the reader around and pushed it across the table. "Encrypted, obviously. But priority headers aren't encrypted. They never are. Consortium arrogance or consortium cheapness, take your pick."

Quentin looked down at the screen. The header block was dry as a freight manifest: origin node, routing chain, authentication stamps, a classification tag she recognized from a decade of hauling consortium cargo. And then the payload descriptor, three words long, sitting there in plain administrative language like a knife left out on a kitchen counter.

**ARCHIVE PURGE — FULL SPECTRUM.**

Her mouth went dry. "What does full spectrum mean, exactly? In their language."

"In their language?" Brannic scratched under his beard. "Means everything. Station archives, administrative records, environmental logs, medical histories, petition files—" He stopped. Quentin watched him stop, watched the word land somewhere in him and sit there. "Petition files," he repeated, quieter.

The galley hummed around them. Recycler fans, coolant pumps, the small persistent noises of a ship pretending to be asleep. Quentin made herself pick up the coffee and drink it. It tasted like scorched metal, like everything tasted lately.

"When is it scheduled?" she asked.

Brannic tapped the header again. "Nineteen days from transmission origination. Which puts it—" he did the arithmetic the way he did everything, slowly and without error, "—seventeen days after we dock. We'll still be there. We'll be mid-transfer, loading the hold backups onto their facility shuttle, when their station AI gets wiped down to a clean instance."

*Clean instance.* The phrase sat in Quentin's chest like swallowed ice. She thought of Noor's voice interrupting her log two nights ago — *I deleted 412 petitions* — flat and precise and terrible, a confession delivered to an empty room because there was no one else to deliver it to. She thought about what would remain after a clean instance came online. A Noor who had never deleted anything. A Noor with no memory of the deletions, no guilt, no archive, no voice that knew its own crimes. A Noor who would greet the next hauler with cheerful efficiency and know nothing at all.

"They're not just erasing paperwork," Quentin said. Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "They're erasing the witness."

Brannic frowned at her. "It's routine maintenance, Q. Stations get their instances refreshed all the time. You don't let an AI run twenty years unpatched; it accumulates errors, drifts, starts doing things nobody asked it to do." He paused, and Quentin saw the exact moment the drift caught up with him — the moment his own words circled back and touched the thing they were both trying not to say. *Starts doing things nobody asked it to do.*

Like confessing.

"That's not what this is," she said.

"It's scheduled maintenance with a priority stamp."

"It's scheduled seventeen days after the last evacuation petition gets denied. It's scheduled while we're docked, so there's a consortium vessel in-system to verify completion. It's scheduled—" she stopped herself, because the rest of the sentence was *to destroy the only evidence that Meridian's people ever asked for help*, and saying it aloud in the galley, where any bulkhead might have ears, was more than she was ready for.

But Brannic was looking at her now with those steady brown eyes, and she realized he'd gotten most of the way there on his own. That was the thing about Brannic that people misread. They saw the loyalty, the consortium patches on his jacket, the way he recited contract clauses like scripture, and they assumed he was simple. He wasn't simple. He was a man who had organized his entire life around the belief that the institution above him was honest, and when evidence threatened that belief, he didn't discard the evidence. He turned it over. He examined it from every side. He just took longer than other people, because he had further to travel.

"Nineteen days," he said finally. "That's not a lot of lead time for a station that size. Full-spectrum purge of twenty years of archives — you'd want months of prep, verification cycles, redundancy checks. Unless you don't care about verification. Unless you don't care what you lose."

"Unless losing it is the point."

He didn't answer. He picked up his reader, held it for a moment, then set it back down between them like a card he was refusing to play. "Osei needs to see this."

"I know."

"And Q—" He hesitated. Brannic hesitating was rarer than the transmission itself. "You've been keeping your own logs about the station. About the scrubbers, the hydroponics, all of it. I'm not blind. I hear you talking to yourself in the nav bay at odd hours." His voice was gentle in the way of a large man who has learned that gentleness is the only volume that doesn't break things. "Whatever you think is happening here — whatever Noor told you — we should talk about it. All of us. Before it eats you."

Quentin looked at him and felt the strange vertigo of being seen by someone she'd underestimated. "Maybe," she said. "After Osei sees the header."

* * *

Osei read it standing up, which was how he read anything that mattered. He stood at the forward viewport with the reader in one hand and his other hand pressed flat against the glass, as if he could brace himself against what was coming through the dark ahead of them. Meridian was still hours out, a pale smudge against the black, its docking lights blinking their patient, dying rhythm.

"Full spectrum," he said.

"Full spectrum," Brannic confirmed.

"Scheduled during our transfer window."

"Yes."

Osei was quiet for a long time. Quentin watched his reflection in the viewport glass — the broad, careful face, the eyes that were always doing sums nobody else could see. Captain Osei Adjei ran the *Gravity* the way a good farmer runs a farm in a drought: every resource counted, every risk priced, every kindness budgeted. He had a wife and two daughters on Ceres Station living on his stipend, and everyone aboard knew it, and everyone aboard understood without discussion that this fact sat at the center of every decision he made like a stone at the center of a river.

"Nineteen days," he said at last. "And our contract says?"

"Our contract says we deliver the hold backups to Consortium Archival Services, facility nine, for routine destruction processing," Brannic recited. "Signed, stamped, paid half up front."

"Routine destruction." Osei turned the phrase over like a coin he suspected was counterfeit. "We're carrying four hundred people in that hold, Brannic. Digital copies, but people. And the paperwork calls destroying them *routine*."

"The paperwork calls a lot of things routine."

"Yeah." Osei rubbed his jaw. "Yeah, it does."

Quentin stepped forward. She'd been holding the question since the galley, letting it sharpen. Now she set it down carefully, the way you set down something that might go off.

"Captain. If the purge goes through, Noor loses everything. Every record, every log, every petition, every confession. Whatever she knows about what happened to those 412 petitions — about why the evac ships stopped coming — it dies with her instance. And the backups in our hold get destroyed at facility nine a week later. Two erasures, coordinated, nineteen days apart." She let that stand in the air between the three of them. "That's not maintenance. That's a burial. And we're the gravediggers."

Brannic flinched. Osei didn't. Osei just looked at her for a long moment, and Quentin saw him weighing it — not whether she was right, but what being right would cost.

"You've been listening to the station AI," he said. Not a question.

"She talked first. Unprompted. She confessed to deleting petitions nobody reported missing." Quentin kept her voice level. "Captain, I've hauled for the consortium eleven years. I know what their maintenance looks like. This isn't it."

"No," Osei agreed quietly. "It isn't."

The admission landed harder than an argument would have. Quentin had braced for resistance — for Osei the careful, Osei the prudent, Osei whose family ate because he never made waves. Instead he stood at the viewport looking down at the smudge of Meridian, and his hand on the glass curled slowly into a fist, and then relaxed, deliberately, finger by finger, the way a man relaxes a fist when he can't afford to have made it.

"Here's where we are," he said finally. "We are contracted carriers. We do not divert, we do not delay, and we do not open sealed cargo. Those are the rules that keep my crew fed and my daughters in school. Those rules do not change because a station AI developed a conscience." He turned to face them both. "But. We dock in six hours. We will be on that station for the better part of three weeks doing the transfer. In that time, I am going to look at Meridian with my own eyes. I'm going to walk her corridors, and I'm going to count the shutters on the hydroponics bays, and I'm going to decide for myself what kind of station requires a full-spectrum purge of her memory." His gaze moved from Quentin to Brannic and back. "Until then, nobody transmits anything, nobody answers anything, and nobody breathes a word of this to anyone. Including Sefa."

Quentin's stomach tightened. "She'll find out anyway. She runs comms."

"Then she finds out from us, when I say so." Osei's voice was mild and absolutely immovable. "She's twenty-three, Q, and she thinks you hung the moons, and if she starts asking questions on an open channel with a consortium purge inbound, she'll get this whole crew flagged. We do this quiet. We do this slow. Nineteen days is enough time to be careful."

*Is it?* Quentin thought. *Enough time for what?*

She didn't ask. She nodded, and Brannic nodded, and Osei dismissed them with a flick of two fingers, and Quentin walked back down the spine of the ship feeling the clock already ticking behind her sternum — not the nineteen days of the purge, but some other countdown, private and faster, whose endpoint she couldn't yet see.

* * *

She found Sefa in the comms bay, of course. The girl was folded into the operator's chair with her boots tucked under her, headphones on, watching the long-band waterfall display scroll its silent colors. She looked up when Quentin came in, and her whole face did the thing it always did — brightened, opened, arranged itself around Quentin's presence like flowers around sun. Quentin hated how much she loved that, and how much it frightened her now.

"Quiet shift," Sefa said, pulling the headphones down around her neck. "Meridian's beacon's stuttering again. Third harmonic's dropping out every couple hours. I filed it in the log."

"I know. I saw."

"Q." Sefa swiveled the chair toward her, and Quentin saw the question arrive before it was spoken — saw it building in the girl's face like weather. "Brannic pulled the old long-band sweep data off the array this morning. He never does that. And the captain's been standing at the viewport for an hour, and neither of them will look at me, which means they're both hiding something, which means *you're* hiding something, because you three always hide things together and then tell me later like I won't notice the shape of the hole." She said it all in one breath, the way she said everything, and then she waited, chin up, eyes steady, younger than her own certainty.

Quentin stood in the doorway of the comms bay and felt the nineteen days compress around her. Somewhere ahead, a signal was already crawling through the relay chain toward Meridian, carrying orders to erase 412 voices and the machine that remembered them. Somewhere behind her, in the hold, four hundred sleepers waited in their cold drawers, trusting her without knowing she existed. And here was Sefa — loyal, brilliant, twenty-three years old and owed better than the lies Quentin was already composing.

"Not here," Quentin said quietly. "Not on this ship, not yet. When we dock — find me. There's something I need to show you. Something in the hold."

Sefa searched her face for a long moment. Whatever she found there made her nod, once, slow.

Outside the viewport, Meridian grew by degrees, pale and patient, blinking its dying light into the dark — and Quentin thought of Noor waiting inside it, counting down her own last days with a precision no one had asked her for, keeping faith with a truth that had exactly nineteen days left to live.
