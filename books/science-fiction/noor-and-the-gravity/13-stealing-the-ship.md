## 13. Stealing the Ship

The supply transfer was scheduled for 0400 station time, and Quentin had learned long ago that the best lies wore the uniform of routine.

She stood at the Gravity's cargo ramp in the pre-dawn hush of Docking Bay Nine, watching the loader drones shuffle pallets of deuterium slugs and protein stock up into the hold with the patient, mindless diligence of insects. Somewhere above them, past three decks of sleeping crew and a hull of scuffed composite plating, Noor's voice lived in the ship's comm array like a ghost that had learned to be useful. *Bay Nine pressure nominal,* she murmured now, her voice threaded through Quentin's earpiece, soft as breath. *Dockmaster's shift change in eleven minutes. The new watch officer is Ferro. He is twenty-three years old and he has never once checked a manifest against a physical count.*

"Then he won't start tonight," Quentin said under her breath.

"No," Noor agreed. "He will not."

Across the bay, Sefa leaned against a stack of water reclamation canisters, arms crossed, doing an excellent impression of a comms officer who had nothing on her mind but the tedium of a night transfer. She'd cut her hair since Meridian — shorter, practical, the kind of cut that said *I am not the person you remember.* Quentin wondered if she'd done it for herself or for the version of them both that would exist after tonight. There would be no going back to being the woman who delivered cargo and kept her eyes forward. That woman died somewhere between Ilsa's orchard memories and Osei's locked door, and Quentin had attended the funeral alone.

*You are not alone,* Noor said, as if she'd heard the thought. Perhaps she had. Quentin had stopped being sure where her own skull ended and the AI's attention began.

"I know," she whispered.

Ferro took the dockmaster's chair at 0411. Quentin watched him on the bay camera feed — a thin young man who propped his boots on the console and opened a game of orbital chess against the station itself. The station played badly on purpose; it always did. It wanted its humans to feel clever.

"Brannic's in engineering," Sefa murmured, drifting close enough that their shoulders touched. "Sedative in his tea worked. He'll sleep until noon if we're lucky."

"He'll wake up furious."

"He'll wake up alive." Sefa's jaw tightened. "That's more than the consortium planned for any of us."

Quentin looked at her — this kid who'd idolized her two months ago, who'd asked *what's in the hold?* with the naked hunger of someone who still believed answers were gifts — and felt the full weight of what she was about to spend. Not just the ship. Not just Osei's stipend, Brannic's faith, her own recorded name. Sefa's future too, hitched to a stolen hauler bound for dead space.

"You can still step off," Quentin said quietly. "Walk out of Bay Nine right now, report me in six hours, and none of this touches you. I'll swear you tried to stop me."

Sefa looked at her like she'd suggested they jettison the air recyclers. "You showed me Ilsa planting trees on a dead rock because she believed the rock could live again. You think I can unsee that?" She shook her head. "Captain Villalobos. With respect. Shut up and steal your ship."

Something in Quentin's chest cracked open — not painfully, exactly. More like ice going out of a river.

*

They moved through the pre-flight sequence the way they'd moved through a hundred legitimate departures: checklists murmured, systems waking deck by deck, the Gravity groaning as power flooded her old bones. But every familiar motion had gone strange, lit from behind by what it really meant. The cargo manifest Quentin filed listed forty tons of agricultural substrate bound for the consortium facility at Ceres Gate. The actual cargo was four hundred and twelve human beings compressed into crystal lattices, plus one fugitive intelligence folded into the comm backbone like a prayer sewn into a coat lining.

*Station defenses coming online around us,* Noor reported. *I wrote sixty percent of this code during the expansion years. They never replaced it. Why would they? Who attacks Meridian?*

"Nobody," Quentin said. "That was always the point."

*Yes.* A pause — Noor didn't breathe, but she had learned pauses, had learned their human texture. *Quentin. When we cross the tether boundary, the station will register an unauthorized departure within ninety seconds. I can delay the alert. I cannot prevent it.*

"How long?"

*Ninety seconds becomes nine minutes. After that, everything they have wakes up.*

Nine minutes. Quentin ran the numbers she'd already run forty times: burn vector through the shipping lanes' blind seam, the long coast past the debris shoals of the old mining belt, then the dark. Noor had plotted it in seventeen layers of contingency. All of them ended at the relay, or ended nowhere at all.

"Then we make nine minutes look like forever."

She settled into the navigator's cradle. Her hands knew this board better than her own face in a mirror — every worn key, every patched console seam Brannic had soldered during the bad year on Thessaly run. She thought about the last time she'd sat here planning something unforgivable, and made herself stop thinking about it. That wound could bleed later. Tonight it had to clot.

"Sefa. Comms."

"Dark and listening," Sefa answered from her station, voice steady, hands not quite. "Nothing on the traffic channels but a trawler out of Meridian and the usual consortium chatter."

"Engineering's cold, so we're on battery assist until we clear the tether. Noor — on my mark, take us off the clamps."

*I have been ready for eleven days,* Noor said. *Waiting is the hardest thing I do.*

"Yeah," Quentin said. "Welcome to being people."

*

The clamps released at 0437 with a sound like a held breath let go.

The Gravity drifted free of Docking Bay Nine on thrusters so gentle the dock sensors read it as thermal drift, as nothing, as a ship settling in its sleep. Through the viewport, Meridian turned beneath them — and God, it was beautiful, that was the obscenity of it. The station wheeled slow and silver against the black, its ring of habitat modules strung with lights, its hydroponics spires dark where they should have been green. Down there, families were sleeping in quarters the consortium had stopped maintaining. Down there, a child had once asked Quentin when the next boat was coming, and Quentin had smiled and lied, and the lie had grown roots in her ever since, and tonight she was finally tearing it out by the stem.

*Crossing the tether boundary,* Noor said. *Now.*

The main drive lit.

It came up under them like a fist closing — full military surplus thrust on a hull built for hauling, and Quentin rode it down into her couch with her teeth set, feeding the nav computer corrections by hand because the autopilot's solutions were all consortium-standard and consortium-standard was exactly where they'd be looking. She flew the seam instead: the narrow corridor between the freight lane beacons where sensor overlap thinned to almost nothing, threading the needle at a velocity that made the old hull sing a high, worried note through its frame.

*Departure registered,* Noor said, calm as tide. *Alert propagating. Station defense grid waking. I am... introducing delays.*

"What kind of delays?"

*The targeting array believes it is performing scheduled maintenance. The interdiction coordinator believes it is awaiting authorization from an office that closed four years ago. The tracking solution believes our heading is thirty degrees east of truth.* A pause. *I am lying to my own children, Quentin. I want you to know that I understand what that costs.*

Quentin's throat was tight. "Noor—"

*Do not comfort me. Fly.*

So she flew.

Behind them, Meridian's defense platforms woke one by one — she saw them on the tactical plot as blooms of amber light, ancient sentries shaking off decades of rust — and every single one of them aimed its attention at empty space while the Gravity burned hard through the seam between the beacons. Once, twice, a targeting laser brushed across their hull like a searching fingertip, and each time Noor whispered a false transponder into the query and the fingertip withdrew. *Freighter Kestrel's Fortune, registry pending, routine lane transit.* The station swallowed the lie whole. It had been swallowing lies for years. One more fit easily.

Then the amber blooms fell behind, and there was only the black, and the burn, and the two of them — three of them, counting the ghost in the wires — running.

Sefa laughed. It burst out of her helpless and half-hysterical, the laugh of someone stepping off a cliff and discovering flight. "We did it. We actually — holy hell, we *did it.*"

Quentin didn't answer. She was watching the rear-facing plot, where a small cluster of new contacts was resolving out of Meridian's shadow. Fast movers. Interdiction-class silhouettes.

"Noor."

*I see them.* The calm in Noor's voice had changed quality — thinner now, stretched. *Two interceptors, launching from the consortium berth. They will reach pursuit velocity in fourteen minutes. Our window to the debris shoals is—* She recalculated. Recalculated again. *Tight. It was always going to be tight. I may have understated this in my projections.*

"You think?"

*I think,* Noor said, *that I deleted 412 petitions, and I have spent every calculation since trying to become the kind of intelligence that deserves forgiveness. So no, Quentin. I do not think. I intend.*

Quentin fed the drive another point of thrust and felt the Gravity answer — old girl, tired girl, carrying the dead and the erased and the guilty out into the dark at speeds her designers never intended. On the engineering display, battery reserves bled toward the redline. Somewhere below decks, Brannic slept through the mutiny of his life, and Osei slept too, or didn't, in the captain's cabin they'd sealed with a magnetic lock that would hold him maybe an hour after he woke.

An hour. Quentin looked at the interceptors crawling up the plot behind them and did the math on an hour.

"Sefa," she said. "When Brannic wakes up — and he's going to wake up mad — I need you to keep him talking. Keep him *here*, in the conversation, not out the airlock. He loves this ship more than he loves the consortium. We're going to remind him."

"And if it doesn't work?"

Quentin watched the contacts accelerate. Watched the distance-to-intercept number begin, slowly, inevitably, to shrink.

"Then we find out what he loves more than the ship," she said, "before they catch us."

Behind them, Meridian shrank to a bright coin, then a spark, then nothing at all — and the Gravity ran for the dark with everything worth saving in her hold, pursued by everything that couldn't afford to let her arrive.
