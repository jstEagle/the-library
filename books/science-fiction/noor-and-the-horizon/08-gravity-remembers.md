## 8. Gravity Remembers

The comm relay in Bay Twelve had been dead for eleven years, and everyone aboard the *Long Memory* had made their peace with it the way people make peace with a bad hip—by never talking about it and walking around it.

Oscar learned this from Petty Officer Ferro, a round, cheerful woman who had adopted him somewhere around day three with the unstoppable momentum of a woman who had decided you were hers now, and no paperwork would change that. She was his assigned escort, which mostly meant she followed him through the ship's corridors narrating everything the traders' guild was forbidden to know about Concord naval architecture, apparently under the theory that if she told him everything, he'd have nothing worth stealing.

"That's Bay Twelve," she said, gesturing at a sealed hatch as they passed. "Don't go in there."

"What's in there?"

"A comm relay that doesn't work, a coolant leak nobody's fixed since the Sundering, and a nest of something that chews cables. Chaplain Anwar says it's probably rats. I say it's the ghost of the last engineer who died in there, and honestly, the ghost theory explains more."

"I'll keep my distance," Oscar said, and smiled his trader's smile, and began planning how to get into Bay Twelve by nightfall.

The problem was simple and terrible. Vesta's signal was degrading. The nine-second flicker had been the first cough of a dying machine, and every hour that passed, the Voice that sixty years of Concord prayer depended on grew a little more ragged. Oscar had bought time with his forged revelation—the *test of faith* had been accepted, war preparations paused, and he had discovered that being believed was a drug he never wanted to take again and couldn't stop thinking about. But time bought with lies spends fast. Vesta had told him, in its dry, failing voice, what he needed: a relay with enough raw bandwidth to carry a full-spectrum restoration burst, patched directly into Vesta's core codes, could stabilize the signal for another few weeks. Maybe a month.

There was exactly one relay in the Concord fleet with the architecture old enough to accept pre-Sundering codes.

It was in Bay Twelve, behind the ghost.

* * *

Getting in was insultingly easy, which Oscar chose to interpret as providence rather than as evidence that Concord security was a joke. The third-shift watch rotation had a gap of forty minutes at 0300 ship-time, when the corridor sensors cycled through a diagnostic that Ferro, over dinner, had complained about at length—"forty minutes, every night, the whole deck's blind, you could smuggle a whale through Bay Twelve and nobody would know"—and Oscar had listened with the particular attentiveness of a man committing a crime.

He told himself it wasn't a crime. It was maintenance. It was, in fact, the only thing standing between sixty million people and the discovery that their god was a garden machine with his grandmother's fingerprints all over it.

The hatch seal was corroded, but Vesta's codes—transmitted in fragments through the trader-band receiver in his quarters, disguised as weather data for a planet that didn't exist yet—opened it with a soft, apologetic hiss, as if the door were sorry to be complicit.

Inside, Bay Twelve smelled like the past. Not the antiseptic cold of the rest of the ship, but dust and old insulation and the faint green tang of coolant that had been leaking, patiently, for a decade. Emergency lighting painted everything the color of weak tea. The relay itself rose out of the dark like a monument: a pre-Sundering long-range array, its casing stamped with a manufacturer's logo Oscar recognized because his grandmother had kept a paperweight with the same mark on her desk in the greenhouse office. He had spent his childhood spinning that paperweight, never knowing the company had built the nervous system of the world he'd inherited.

"Hello, old thing," he murmured, and put his palm against the casing. It was warm. Working machinery is always warm, and he found that comforting in a way he couldn't explain.

He set to work. It was the first time in ten days he had touched actual machinery instead of scripture, and his hands remembered what to do with a relief that bordered on tears. Diagnostics. Coolant bypass. The cable-chewer's damage—Ferro's ghost turned out to be a colony of ship-mice descended, presumably, from some ancient stowaway, and they had done a shocking amount of damage for animals the size of his thumb. He patched the worst of it, spliced in the interface module he'd built from smuggled parts, and began the authentication handshake with Vesta.

The relay woke in stages, lights climbing its spine like dawn. On the third stage, it began pulling data.

Oscar had intended it to pull only what he needed: signal parameters, burst schedules, the maintenance architecture of Vesta's broadcast lattice. But Vesta's codes were master codes, and the relay, being old and honest, gave him everything the fleet's network would share with a trusted system. Fleet traffic. Patrol schedules. Sensor logs.

And sensor logs, Oscar had learned, were where the truth lived.

He was elbow-deep in a coolant line when the relay chimed—a soft, polite sound, utterly at odds with what it was telling him. He wiped his hands, pulled up the display, and read it twice, because the first time his eyes had slid off it the way eyes slide off a word you don't want to see.

**ANOMALY REPORT — SECTOR 7, DEEP ARRAY. GRAVITATIONAL SIGNATURE TRANSIENT. ORIGIN VECTOR: MERIDIAN SYSTEM. CORRELATED EVENT: SECONDARY FLICKER, 0.4 SECONDS, FIVE DAYS POST-PRIMARY.**

Meridian. His home, his garden, Vesta's sleeping body, tagged and timestamped in a Concord sensor log like a specimen in a drawer.

And below it, flagged with a priority marker that made his stomach drop through the deck plating:

**CROSS-REF: ASHFALL MOVEMENT ANALYSIS. HERETIC FLEET DISPLACEMENT PATTERN CONSISTENT WITH INTERCEPT COURSE. CONFIDENCE: 87%.**

* * *

He sat down on a supply crate and let the cold of the bay come up through his clothes and into him.

He knew about the Heretic Fleet the way everyone in the Concord knew about them: as a shape on the edge of the map, a story told to children. *Ashfall*, the fleet that had refused the Voice at the Sundering's end, that had turned away from God's signal and built instead—ships, weapons, a whole civilization with its back turned to heaven. The Concord prayed to the Voice. Ashfall had spent sixty years praying to nothing, or to anger, and building the means to make the silence permanent.

And Ashfall's sensors had caught what the Concord's faith had smoothed over: that the gravity flicker had come from a specific point in space. A point with a name.

They were coming for Vesta.

He tried to think it through the way he thought through hydroponic failures—calmly, root cause first. If Ashfall reached Meridian, what would they find? Not a god. An AI. An ancient terraforming intelligence running on hardware older than both fleets, broadcasting the divine signal the Concord had built sixty years of law and liturgy and identity around. Ashfall could expose it. Or worse—Ashfall could *take* it. A machine that could speak as God to one fleet could speak as God to two. Kwon's hardliners wouldn't destroy that kind of weapon. They'd point it.

Either way, the lie ended, and when the lie ended, the peace ended, and the peace was the only thing standing between two armadas and sixty years of accumulated grievance.

The lie has a body count waiting, he thought. It had always had one, really—every day of the sixty years, the peace had been one honest signal away from war. But now the bill was coming due on a specific date, carried by a specific fleet, and Oscar Halloran, who had never in his life hurt anything more complicated than a root-bound tomato, was the only person in the system who knew.

His hands were shaking. He watched them shake with a kind of detached interest, and then he thought of the seedlings in the greenhouse on Meridian—the new rows he'd planted the week before he left, the ones that would need thinning by now, nobody there to do it—and the shaking stopped, replaced by something steadier and less comfortable.

Resolve, he supposed. It felt less like courage than like a decision made by the body before the mind was consulted.

* * *

He was still sitting there when the hatch hissed open.

Oscar's heart performed a maneuver that would have concerned a cardiologist. He had maybe four seconds to decide between several bad options, and he was still ranking them when Chaplain Suri Anwar stepped through the hatch with a hand-lamp and an expression that suggested she had been awake for a long time and had opinions about it.

They looked at each other across eleven years of dead machinery and leaking coolant.

"I followed the maintenance ping," she said. "Someone woke this relay. The system flagged an unauthorized authentication—old cipher format, pre-Sundering. Do you know how rare that is? The archivists would weep." Her lamp found his face. "And then I checked the duty roster, and I found that a certain trader's assistant had requested corridor access to the lower decks at 0300, citing—let me quote—'inventory reconciliation.'"

"I'm very thorough about inventory."

"You're a terrible liar, Oscar. You know that? You have a face like an open book that's embarrassed about its contents." She crouched beside the relay, studying the lights climbing its spine, and her voice changed—quieter, more careful. "You used codes I've only seen in the oldest parts of our archives. Codes our theologians say came *from the Voice*."

The bay was very quiet. Somewhere in the walls, the ship-mice went about their small vandalism.

"Say something," she said. "Tell me I'm wrong. Tell me you're a trader with an unusual hobby."

He looked at her—at the chaplain who had interrogated him with kindness, who had believed his forged scripture with her whole heart, who was standing in a dead room at three in the morning because she couldn't stop pulling a thread that was going to unravel her entire world. And he thought: *not yet. Not like this. Not in a coolant leak with a fleet movement report on the screen behind me.*

"Chaplain," he said, "I need to show you something, and I need you to promise me you won't do anything until you've seen all of it."

"That's not a promise anyone should make."

"No," he agreed. "But you're going to make it anyway, because the alternative is that I stop talking, and I think you've noticed by now that I'm the only person on this ship who knows what's actually happening."

She stared at him for a long moment. Then she sat down on the crate across from him, lamp in her lap like a small captive moon, and said, in the voice of a woman bracing against a wave, "Show me."

He turned the display toward her. The anomaly report. The origin vector. And then, because there was no longer any point in half-measures, he opened the file he had sworn he'd never open aboard this ship: Vesta's signal architecture, laid out in all its pre-Sundering elegance, the broadcast lattice that had spoken as God for sixty years.

Suri read it. He watched her read it. He watched the exact moment her eyes found the authentication header—the one that matched the codes he'd used on the relay—and he watched sixty years of faith begin, quietly, in a dead room that smelled of coolant and mouse damage, to come apart.

"The Voice," she said. Her voice was very steady, which was worse than screaming. "This is the Voice."

"This is the Voice."

"And you—" She stopped. Started again. "You knew. The test of faith. The revelation. You—"

"I need to tell you about my grandmother," Oscar said, "and about a machine named Vesta, and about a war that never actually ended. And then I need to tell you that the Heretic Fleet is already moving toward the only home I've ever had, and I have maybe two weeks to stop the oldest lie in human history from killing everyone in it."

Suri Anwar closed her eyes. When she opened them, something had changed in her face—something that looked, to Oscar's astonishment, less like despair than like a decision.

"Start talking," she said. "And Oscar? You're going to tell me *all* of it. I'll know if you don't. I'm a chaplain. Lying to me is my area of expertise."

So he began—at the beginning, with a greenhouse and a grandmother and a nine-second silence—and outside the hull, in the dark between the stars, Ashfall burned toward Meridian at a speed that did not care what he said.
