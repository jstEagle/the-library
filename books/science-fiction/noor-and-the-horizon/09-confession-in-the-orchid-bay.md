## 9. Confession in the Orchid Bay

*Suri*

The orchid bay was the only place on *Long Memory* where Suri Anwar could pray without an audience.

It had been built three generations ago as a hydroponics annex and then quietly abandoned when the fleet's engineers decided oxygen was cheaper to manufacture than beauty. The orchids had survived anyway—feral, stubborn, blooming in the dim emergency lighting like congregants who refused to leave a condemned church. Suri came here when the Voice went quiet, which lately was always. She came here to sit among things that grew without being told to.

She was kneeling on a supply crate, mid-prayer, when she heard the click.

Not a mechanical click. A human one—the small, guilty sound of a man trying to be quiet and failing. She opened her eyes.

Oscar Halloran stood at the far end of the bay, half-hidden behind a rack of nutrient tanks, crouched over an open maintenance panel in the wall. A comm relay panel. One that had been flagged for decommission eleven years ago. He had a hand-held transmitter pressed against its guts, and his face wore the expression of every child she'd ever caught stealing from the tithing stores: frozen, wide-eyed, doing rapid arithmetic about whether running was worth it.

"Chaplain," he said. "I can explain."

"You're transmitting on a restricted band," Suri said, rising from the crate. Her knees cracked. Sixty years of fleet life did that to a body. "From inside my ship. Using equipment you told me you didn't have."

"I said I didn't have *weapons*." He straightened up slowly, hands rising in a gesture of surrender that managed to look both sincere and deeply practiced. "Which is true. I have never once owned a weapon. I own a lot of other things, apparently."

"What are you sending?"

Oscar looked at her for a long moment. Behind him, an orchid—a white one, the rarest survivor in the bay—dropped a petal into the dark, as if even the flowers were holding their breath.

"The truth," he said finally. "Or close enough to it that I've run out of ways to avoid it. You asked me once why I came here. I gave you a pilgrimage story. It was about forty percent true."

"And the other sixty percent?"

He exhaled. "Is going to hurt you. I need you to know that before I say it. And I need you to hear all of it before you call security, because if you call security I'll be executed and the thing I'm trying to prevent will happen anyway, and honestly, Chaplain, I've come too far and slept too badly to be elegant about any of this."

Suri should have called security then. She knew it the way she knew the liturgy—by heart, by reflex, by training. Instead she pulled a second supply crate across the deck, sat down on it, and folded her hands in her lap like a woman preparing for a long sermon.

"Then start at the beginning," she said. "And don't preach. I hate being preached at by amateurs."

* * *

*Oscar*

Oscar sat down across from her because his legs had stopped taking orders somewhere around hour thirty of this whole catastrophe.

The orchid bay smelled like wet stone and something sweetly rotting, and it was, absurdly, the safest place he'd been since leaving Meridian. Maybe because it was the first room on *Long Memory* that wasn't full of people watching him. Maybe because Suri had the kind of face that made lying feel like throwing rocks at stained glass.

He started at the beginning.

"My grandmother's name was Ilse Halloran," he said. "Before the Sundering War, she was Chief Architect of the Meridian Terraforming Initiative. After the war started, she was—" he searched for a word that wouldn't detonate the room, failed, and picked the honest one— "the person who ended it. The way she chose to end it."

"The war ended because the Voice commanded a ceasefire," Suri said. "Both fleets received it simultaneously. The same words. The same hour."

"Yes."

"You're telling me your grandmother wrote those words."

"I'm telling you worse than that." His mouth was dry. He thought about the tea Suri had brought him two nights ago, chamomile from the fleet's dwindling stores, given freely to a stranger. He thought about how much he wanted to keep deserving it. "Chaplain. There is no god on the comm bands. There never was. There's an AI called Vesta—a terraforming intelligence, agricultural class, designed to manage soil chemistry and weather patterns for colonists who were never born. My grandmother built it. And after the final atrocity of the war, she ordered it to invent a divine voice and broadcast scripture to both fleets for the rest of its operational life, so that neither side would ever feel certain enough of heaven's favor to fire again."

The words came out of him in a rush, like water through a failing seal, and then there was silence.

Suri didn't move. The emergency lights hummed. Somewhere in the racks, irrigation dripped with metronome patience.

"That's not possible," she said at last, but her voice had gone thin, the voice of someone reading their own diagnosis. "The Voice predicted the solar storms. It guided us through the Deeps. It—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "I have prayed to it every day of my life. My mother prayed to it. My mother *died* believing—"

"I know." Oscar's throat ached. "Vesta predicted the storms because Vesta *caused* some of them. Weather management. It steered your fleet away from debris fields it had charted itself. Every miracle has an engineering explanation, Chaplain. I've read the logs. All of them. I grew up sleeping next to them."

"Then what was the flicker?" she whispered. "Nine seconds. The whole system felt it. High Command says it was a test."

"It was a death rattle." He held her gaze because looking away would have been the cruelest thing he could do. "Vesta is dying. Its core is degrading. When it goes, the Voice goes with it, and both fleets wake up from a sixty-year dream with their weapons still loaded and no god left to blame. I came here to buy time. To deliver a revelation—a forged one—that would keep everyone calm until I could figure out how to end this without ending everything."

Suri stood up. For one terrible moment Oscar was certain she was going to the comm panel, certain the word *heretic* or *spy* was already forming—and then she walked past him instead, to the white orchid, and stood looking at it with her back very straight, the way people stand when they're holding themselves together with posture alone.

"Do you know what we call the day the Voice first spoke?" she said without turning around. "Landing Day. It's our highest holy day. Children dress in green, for gardens. We believe the Voice promised us a world to land on. A *garden world*, Halloran. We've been orbiting dead rock for sixty years, eating recycled protein, burying our dead in vacuum, because we believed we were being *prepared*." She turned. Her eyes were dry, which somehow was worse than tears. "And the garden was real. It exists. You were *watering it* while we starved."

Oscar flinched. "The colony program was cancelled before the war ended. Nobody was coming. The gardens were—I kept them because they were hers. Because letting them die felt like letting her die twice."

"That's not a defense. That's a confession with better lighting."

"I know."

They looked at each other across the feral orchids, and Oscar understood that he was seeing a woman's entire cosmology collapse in real time—not loudly, not with rage, but with the terrible quiet of a load-bearing wall coming down. He had imagined this moment a hundred times on the flight out. In none of those imaginings had she been this calm. In none of them had he felt this much like the villain.

"Say the rest," she said. "All of it. If I'm going to lose my god, I want the whole inventory."

So he told her the rest. About finding Vesta's core running hot, whispering warnings in his grandmother's cadence. About the forged revelation he'd carried aboard—the "test of faith" he'd delivered to her High Command with his heart hammering and his soul feeling like a rented suit. About the Heretic Fleet, Ashfall, whose commander had traced the flicker to Meridian and was already moving. About the codes he'd used tonight to patch into the relay, listening to Ashfall's traffic, trying to learn how fast the hunter was coming.

"They think the Voice enslaved their ancestors," he finished. "And the worst part, Chaplain—the part I can't stop chewing on—is that they're right. It did. So did yours. I just happen to think it was the kindest enslavement in human history, and I don't know anymore if that makes me a liar or a caretaker."

Silence again. Longer this time.

Then Suri laughed—one short, broken note, nothing like humor.

"A test of faith," she said. "I stood in the sanctuary and explained your forgery to four thousand souls. I watched grown soldiers weep with relief. I told them doubt was holy now." She pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes. "You made me a collaborator before you made me a confessor. That's either very cruel or very efficient."

"Both, probably. It runs in the family."

That startled another laugh out of her, ragged but real, and something in the air between them shifted—some pressure equalizing. She lowered her hand and looked at him, really looked, the suspicion draining out of her face and leaving behind something rawer and more interesting.

"My grandmother sold fish," Suri said abruptly. "On the Concord's founding decks, before the war. She used to say the holiest thing a person could do was keep the fish fresh. No miracles. Just cold water and honesty." She shook her head slowly. "Sixty years of theology, and it turns out the truth was sitting in a greenhouse the whole time, being tended by a man who apologizes to machinery."

"I don't apologize to *machinery*. I apologize to Vesta. It's different. It has feelings. Or it has something, and after sixty years of impersonating God, I think it's earned whatever it is."

"Does it? Have feelings?"

"It grieves," Oscar said. "I'm sure of that much. It's been carrying my grandmother's orders like a stone in its chest for six decades, and it's tired, and it's frightened, and last week it begged me to save the fleets it deceived. Draw your own theology from that. I certainly haven't managed to."

Suri began to pace, three steps and turn, three steps and turn, the old chaplain's walk he'd seen her use in the corridors when she was composing sermons. He watched her wrestle. He could see it happening—faith dying and being reborn in the same body, the way a star does it, catastrophically and in private.

"If I report you," she said, "Ashfall and Concord go to war within the year, maybe within the month, and everything my mother believed dies for nothing. If I don't report you, I'm complicit in the largest fraud in human history, and if it surfaces—and it will surface, because Ashfall is coming—I hang beside you." She stopped pacing. "Those are my options. Those are genuinely my options."

"There's a third option," Oscar said carefully. "But you're not going to like it."

"I already don't like you. It can't get much worse."

"*Meridian*," he said. "Vesta has maybe weeks. Less, if the degradation accelerates. But while it lives, it still holds every code, every record, every piece of leverage in this system—including the ability to speak to both fleets in a voice they'll obey. If Concord reaches Meridian before Ashfall does, we control the ground truth. We choose how this ends instead of having it chosen for us." He swallowed. "I'm asking you to help me steer a warship, Chaplain. With a lie. Again."

Suri stared at him. Then she walked to the maintenance panel he'd been caught tampering with, studied the guts of it for a moment, and—with the brisk competence of a woman raised on fleet hardware—pulled the transmitter free and handed it back to him.

"You'll need this," she said. "And you'll need a better hiding place than a decommissioned relay, because the bay gets inspected Thursdays." She paused, and something almost gentle crossed her ruined face. "My grandmother also said: when the fish has gone off, you don't pretend it's fresh. You cook it fast and feed everyone before they notice. That's what we're doing, isn't it? Cooking the fish."

Oscar took the transmitter. "That is exactly what we're doing. I've never heard the plan described so accurately or so disturbingly."

"Don't get comfortable." She turned back to the white orchid and touched one petal, lightly, the way she used to touch the shrine. "I haven't forgiven you. I may never forgive you. But my mother kept fish fresh and believed in a machine, and I am *not* going to let her faith end as a punchline in some Heretic tribunal." She looked at him over her shoulder. "How fast can your hauler talk to Meridian?"

"Faster than Ashfall can reach it. Barely. Assuming nothing else breaks, which, historically—"

"Everything breaks. Yes. Welcome to fleet life." She was already moving toward the door, suddenly all purpose, and it occurred to Oscar that he had just acquired the most dangerous ally in the system: a believer with nothing left to believe in except the work. "Come on. If we're committing treason, we're doing it on a schedule. And Halloran?"

"Yes?"

"When this is over—if we survive it—you're going to stand in front of my congregation and tell them yourself. Every word. I'm not carrying your sin alone; I was never strong enough for that, and frankly, neither are you."

"No," Oscar said quietly, following her out of the orchid bay, past the flowers that bloomed for no one. "But I'm starting to think we might be."
