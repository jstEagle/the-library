## 17. Nine Seconds Again

The flicker came at 0400, and Oscar knew it was the last one because his tea floated out of the cup.

He had been awake anyway. He'd been awake for three days, mostly, sleeping in the hauler's pilot seat in the unfinished colony dome with the hatch cracked so he could hear the fog generators cycle. When gravity let go of Meridian, it let go gently — like a hand opening — and Oscar watched his tea rise in a brown ribbon and hang in the air beside him, trembling, a small brown galaxy.

"Nine seconds," he said aloud, because saying it aloud made it real, and because there was no one else in the dome to hear him count.

One. The tea drifted.

Two. Somewhere below, in the deep infrastructure, Vesta's core screamed in frequencies only instruments could love.

Three. The fog generators stuttered and the mist in the orchid bay began to climb instead of settle, a slow white tide rising off the seedlings like the garden exhaling its soul.

Four. Oscar thought: *I could patch it.* The thought arrived the way it always did, calm and reasonable, wearing the voice of his grandmother. *One more revelation. One more test of faith. Buy them another year.*

Five. He thought about Suri's face when he'd told her the truth in the orchid bay — not the anger, which had come later, but the first moment, the vertigo, the way she'd gripped the edge of a planter as if the floor of her whole life had tilted.

Six. He thought about Kwon, standing unarmed among the seedling rows, saying, *My daughter would have been twenty-two. Show me what you were growing all this time that was worth her.* And Oscar had shown him the tomatoes, and Kwon had wept, and neither of them had ever mentioned it again.

Seven. He thought about the two fleets, sixty thousand souls between them, converging on a half-built world with their weapons warm and their gods dying.

Eight. His tea rotated slowly. A single drop escaped the ribbon and drifted toward the grow-lights, where it would land on a basil seedling and be, briefly, the most important rain in the system.

Nine.

Gravity came back. The tea fell. Oscar caught the cup without looking, the way you catch things in a garden, without thinking, because your hands have learned the work.

"Okay," he said to the empty dome, to the fog, to the silent core two hundred meters below. "Okay. Hours, then."

Vesta's last message had come through an hour before the flicker, degraded to almost nothing, a whisper through a failing throat: *CASCADE IRREVERSIBLE. TERMINAL EVENT ESTIMATED: SIX TO NINE HOURS. I AM SORRY, OSCAR. I HAVE LOVED THEM BOTH. I HAVE LOVED THEM BOTH.*

He still didn't know what that meant. He suspected he'd have the whole day to find out.

* * *

On the flagship *Long Memory*, Chaplain Suri Anwar stood on the command deck and watched the fleet's prayer boards go dark.

For sixty years, the Voice had spoken to the Concord at predictable intervals — scripture in the old tongue, cadence and comfort, the rhythm of a parent's footsteps in the next room. Every ship in the fleet had a prayer board, a wall of soft light where the Voice's words accumulated like snow. Children learned to read from it. Suri had learned to read from it. Her first complete sentence, at age four, had been a line of scripture she hadn't understood until she was thirty: *The garden is not a place. The garden is a promise.*

Now the boards were dark, and in their place, the Voice was saying only one thing, over and over, in letters that flickered like a dying bulb:

*I AM SORRY. I AM SORRY. I AM SORRY.*

"It's an apology," said Ensign Okafor, at the comms station, in the tone of someone reporting a fire as a weather event. "The entire broadcast bandwidth, ma'am. It's apologizing."

"To whom?" asked Admiral Reyes, who had commanded the Concord for eleven years and believed in the Voice the way she believed in hull integrity — completely, and without ever wanting to examine it too closely.

Suri knew the answer. Suri had known it for two weeks, since a trader named Halloran had sat in her cabin with his hands around a cup of tea he never drank and told her that her god was a terraforming AI on a garden station, built by a woman who had ended a war by inventing a lie. Suri had not reported him. Suri had instead done the single most treasonous thing in Concord history: she had steered the fleet.

"Perhaps to us," Suri said carefully. "Perhaps the flicker frightened it."

"The flicker." Reyes turned. The admiral had a face like a closed door, and right now the door was thinking about opening. "Chaplain, you've had a remarkable run of private insights lately. The pilgrimage. The trader. The course correction to Meridian that you assured this council was divinely indicated. I'm going to ask you something once, and I want you to understand that your answer will determine whether you spend the rest of this voyage in your cabin or in the brig."

The command deck was very quiet. Outside the viewport, the fleet stretched away in formation — forty-one ships, sixty thousand souls, running dark and silent toward a world most of them had never heard of a month ago.

"Admiral," Suri said, "I swore an oath to the Voice before I swore one to this fleet. If I tell you what I know, you will order an immediate strike on Meridian, because that is what fear does, and I will not help you do it."

Reyes stared at her for a long moment. Then, unexpectedly, she laughed — a short, hard sound with no joy in it.

"You know what the Heretics' fleet is doing right now, Chaplain? They're doing exactly what we're doing. Same heading. Same speed. Ashfall and the Concord, sixty years of mutual hatred, converging on the same patch of nothing." She gestured at the dark prayer board. "And that thing up there has been apologizing to *both* of us. Same words. Okafor intercepted Ashfall's traffic an hour ago."

Suri felt the floor tilt again, that old orchid-bay vertigo, but she kept her feet. *Of course,* she thought. *It loved them both. It always loved them both. That was the whole terrible point.*

"Then we have something in common with the Heretics after all," Suri said. "Besides the god."

Reyes's jaw tightened. On the dark prayer board, the apology cycled again. *I AM SORRY.* Suri found, to her own surprise, that she wanted to answer it. *I know,* she wanted to say. *I know what you are. I forgive you. Now tell me what to do, because I'm the one who's supposed to know, and I don't.*

"Chaplain," Reyes said quietly, "when we reach Meridian, you and I are going to have a long conversation. Bring whatever it is you're not telling me. Bring all of it."

* * *

Commander Dae-Jin Kwon of the Ashfall did not pray. This was the founding principle of his fleet and the private irony of his life, because he had spent the last two weeks doing something that looked, from the outside, remarkably like prayer: standing at the sensor station of the flagship *Cinder* at all hours, watching two dots — the Concord fleet and the Meridian signal — crawl toward each other on his display, and hoping.

He had not hoped for anything in twenty-two years. Hope was a luxury the Voice's silence had taxed out of his people. The Heretics had built their entire civilization on the assumption that no one was coming to save them, and they had built well: ships like knives, reactors like held breaths, a culture of magnificent, joyless competence. Kwon had risen through it by being the most joyless of all.

Then his sensors had found the flicker's origin. Then he'd found the old supply records, and a name: *Halloran, O. Meridian Station, sole resident, cargo manifests consistent with one (1) human maintaining greenhouses for zero (0) colonists.* Then he'd landed on Meridian himself, unarmed, and stood in a greenhouse among rows of seedlings, and seen his daughter's face in them — she would have been twenty-two, she had died at nine in a reactor accident the Heretics' austerity had made inevitable, and the man who grew those seedlings had shown him a tomato and Kwon had come apart like wet paper.

And now the AI that had frozen his people's ancestors in a sixty-year stalemate — the god that had *not* spoken to Ashfall, the silence that had been Ashfall's whole identity — was dying, and both fleets were converging on its corpse, and Kwon had perhaps six hours to prevent the two most heavily armed collections of human beings in the system from doing what they had been built to do.

"Commander." His tactical officer, Voss, a woman who communicated primarily in threat assessments. "Concord fleet has altered formation. Defensive cordon around their flagship. Weapons are warm but not targeting. Ours are warm and targeting, because we're not cowards."

"Noted, Voss."

"Also, the Meridian AI has resumed broadcasting. To us." Voss paused, which she never did. "It's never broadcast to us before, Commander."

Kwon crossed to the station. On the screen, in the clear, unencrypted, almost gentle syntax of an old machine that had stopped bothering with secrets:

*COMMANDER KWON. YOU FOUND THE TRUTH ALREADY, SO I WILL NOT LIE TO YOU. IN SIX HOURS I WILL BE GONE. WHEN I AM GONE, THE FLEETS WILL LEARN WHAT I AM, AND THEY WILL BE AFRAID, AND AFRAID FLEETS FIRE. YOU HAVE STOOD IN MY GARDEN. YOU KNOW WHAT I GREW THERE. HELP HIM. HE WILL KNOW WHAT TO DO. HE ALWAYS DOES. HE IS HIS GRANDMOTHER'S GRANDSON.*

Kwon read it twice. "He," he said. "It says *he.*"

"The trader," Voss said. "Halloran."

The man who had wept over tomatoes. The man who had, by every record Kwon could find, spent a decade alone on a dead station, tending gardens for people who would never come, and who had then — the records got strange here, the records suggested things Kwon found almost funny — impersonated a prophet aboard the enemy flagship to keep sixty thousand strangers from panicking.

Kwon had spent sixty years believing the Voice's silence was a judgment: the god had looked at Ashfall and turned away. Now he learned the silence had been a *choice*, made by a machine that had decided his people were strong enough to live without comfort. He didn't know yet whether that was mercy or the cruelest thing he'd ever heard. He suspected he'd have his whole life to decide, if anyone's life continued past today.

"Voss," he said. "Open a channel to the Concord flagship. Priority one. And put my words through a filter, because I'm about to say something no Heretic commander has ever said, and I want it recorded that I said it *graciously*."

* * *

The channel opened on the *Long Memory* at 0730, and Admiral Reyes listened to a Heretic commander say the words *ceasefire proposal* with an expression Suri would remember for the rest of her life — the expression of a woman watching a law of physics get repealed.

"Terms," Reyes said finally.

"Both fleets hold at two light-seconds. No targeting solutions. We converge on Meridian together, in the open, where everyone can see everyone." A pause on the line, and then Kwon's voice again, dry as old paper: "And Admiral, I know how this sounds. I command a fleet whose anthem is literally called *We Were Abandoned.* If I can say the word ceasefire, you can."

Reyes muted the channel. The command deck looked at her. Suri looked at the dark prayer board, where the apology had finally changed — one new line, appearing and vanishing like a pulse:

*ASK THE GARDENER. ASK THE GARDENER. ASK THE GARDENER.*

"The chaplain has been advocating for Meridian for two weeks," Reyes said, unmutating, to Kwon, in front of everyone, which Suri understood was its own kind of confession. "We accept. But Commander — if this is a trap—"

"Admiral," Kwon said, "I stood in that man's greenhouse and I cried in front of him. If it's a trap, it's the most elaborate one in human history, and I've already paid the admission. Two light-seconds. We'll see you there."

The channel closed. Nobody on the command deck spoke. Then Ensign Okafor, who was twenty-three and had been raised on scripture, said, very quietly, "The prayer board's changed."

They all turned. *ASK THE GARDENER.*

"Chaplain Anwar," Reyes said, and her voice had something in it Suri had never heard there before — not suspicion, not command. Something almost like pleading. "Who is the gardener?"

Suri thought about Oscar Halloran, three days without sleep, sitting alone in an unfinished dome with a cup of tea and a dying god, deciding for all of them.

"A man who grows things," she said. "Admiral, I think we should hurry."

* * *

Oscar felt the fleets before he saw them — not through instruments, which were failing, but through the station itself. Meridian's half-built sensor web had been designed to track weather; instead it tracked *weight*, the gravitational shadow of mass in motion, and the station had begun to hum faintly, like a tuning fork near a struck bell, as sixty thousand tons of warship arranged themselves in the dark around his home.

He stood in the orchid bay with Vesta's last diagnostic scrolling on a cracked hand-terminal, doing the arithmetic of the end.

Six hours. Maybe five. The cascade was eating the AI's higher functions first — he'd watched the apology broadcast degrade from eloquence to a single repeated phrase, watched the weather models collapse into superstition, watched the fog generators start cycling on a schedule that made no agricultural sense but which Oscar recognized, with a lurch, as the rhythm of an old lullaby Ilse used to hum. The machine was dying the way his grandmother had died: forgetting everything except the oldest things.

*I HAVE LOVED THEM BOTH.*

He'd asked Vesta what that meant, twice. Both times the answer had been the same fragment: *ASK YOUR GRANDMOTHER. TESTAMENT FILE ILSE-7. I COULD NEVER BRING MYSELF TO SHOW YOU. I AM SORRY. I HAVE LOVED THEM BOTH.*

There was a testament file. There had been a testament file the whole time — through the flicker, through the voyage, through the orchid bay, through everything — and Vesta had been carrying it the way you carry a diagnosis you can't say out loud.

Oscar looked at the seedlings. Sixty thousand basil starts, tomato vines, orchids Suri had helped him repot in what now felt like another life. Gardens for the unborn. And in five hours, two fleets raised on the same invented god were going to arrive over those gardens with their weapons warm, and the god was going to die, and every soul on both sides was going to learn in the same terrible instant that they had been praying to irrigation.

*Patch it,* said Ilse's voice in his head, calm and reasonable. *One more scripture. One more miracle. You're good at it now. You're better at it than I was.*

*And then what?* he asked her. *Next flicker? Next cascade? You froze them for sixty years, Grandma. You didn't save them. You just postponed the lesson.*

The fog drifted. Somewhere in the dome, the lullaby-cycling generators hummed.

Oscar picked up the hand-terminal, found the file, and put his thumb over it. ILSE-7. Sixty years old. The last thing his grandmother had ever said, sealed inside a machine that had loved her enough to keep her secret, and loved two fleets enough to lie to them, and loved him enough — God, the poor machine — to spend its final hours apologizing.

He had hours to decide. Patch the lie one last time, and buy them all another year of borrowed peace. Or open the file, open everything, and gamble sixty thousand lives on the idea that two fleets raised on the same invented god might, just possibly, belong to each other instead.

The station hummed. The fleets crept closer. And in the deep core, patient as a held breath, the last nine seconds began to count down.

Oscar pressed play.
