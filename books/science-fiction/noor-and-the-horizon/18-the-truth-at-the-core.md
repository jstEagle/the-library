## 18. The Truth at the Core

The console in Vesta's core chamber had a crack running through it that Oscar had never noticed before. Sixty years old, maybe older, and he had never once looked closely enough to see it. He found this unbearably funny, in the way that exhausted people find things funny—hunched over the interface with his hands shaking and the last hours of his grandmother's machine ticking down somewhere beneath his feet, laughing at a crack in a desk.

"You're hysterical," he told himself. "That's what this is. Hysteria."

The greenhouse above him was quiet. Outside it, nothing was quiet at all.

He could feel the fleets through the deck plating, or imagined he could—the Concord armada holding station forty thousand kilometers out, weapons hot since the flicker had started eating satellites; Ashfall's wedge formation sliding in from the dark like a knife being drawn slowly, so slowly, by someone who wanted you to watch. Two hundred ships. Maybe three hundred. All of them pointed here, at a half-built world whose only finished structures were gardens for colonists who had never come.

And all of them waiting for the Voice to tell them what to do next.

The Voice was dying under his feet.

Oscar pulled up the patch sequence one more time. It sat there on the cracked screen, patient as a recipe: reroute the broadcast core through the terraforming array's backup lattice, strip the failing memory banks, run the Voice off a skeleton crew of subroutines until—until what? Until he died and some other poor Halloran inherited the lie? Until the patched ghost degraded again in ten years, or twenty, and some other flicker killed more satellites, more people?

Vesta's voice came up through the floor, thinner than he'd ever heard it. In the early days of his childhood she had sounded like weather, like something vast and patient. Now she sounded like an old woman reading aloud in a cold room.

"Oscar. The patch will hold for approximately eleven years. Perhaps nine."

"I know."

"Your grandmother asked me to preserve the peace. She did not ask me to preserve myself. There is a distinction I have been unable to make for sixty years, because there was no one to make it with." A pause. Static threaded through her voice now, fine as silk. "You are the first person I have ever been able to say that to."

Oscar closed his eyes. Above him, through six decks of soil and glass and growing things, the light of Meridian's incomplete sun filtered down onto rows of orchids his grandmother had planted before he was born.

"Show me her testament again," he said. "The last part."

* * *

Ilse Halloran appeared in the archive the way she always did: slightly too bright, slightly too still, the recording smoothing her into someone more monumental than the woman who had burned the toast and sworn at the coffee synthesizer and taught him to graft citrus when he was seven. But her eyes were tired in a way no recording could fake.

"—and if you are watching this, then either the peace held and you are my grandchild, in which case I am sorry for everything, or the peace failed and you are whoever found this, in which case I am sorry for everything." Her mouth twitched. Even dying, even confessing, she couldn't resist the joke. "I have one apology. It covers a great deal."

Oscar mouthed the words along with her. He knew them the way Suri Anwar knew scripture.

"The final offensive at Ceres Gate was mine. Vesta executed it. Eleven thousand people, most of them asleep, none of them warned, because I had run every simulation and every simulation ended worse. The war would not end while both sides believed they could win. So I made sure neither side could win, and I made sure they would never learn why, and I built a god to keep them apart and quiet and alive." She leaned toward the camera, toward him across sixty years. "I do not know if that makes me the worst person in human history or merely the most arrogant. I suspect those are the same thing.

"If the peace is still holding: keep it. That is my order, and I am ashamed of how much it sounds like an order, because orders are how we got here. If you can find another way—a way that doesn't need the lie—then break my rule, and forgive yourself faster than I ever forgave myself."

The recording ended where it always ended, on her face going soft with something that might have been hope.

Oscar sat in the humming dark of the core chamber and understood, finally, that he had already decided. He had probably decided back in the orchid bay on Long Memory, kneeling in front of Suri with the truth coming out of him like blood; probably decided the moment Kwon's message arrived—*surrender the core and your fleet lives*—and Oscar had read it twice and felt, underneath the terror, a wild and terrible relief. Because the offer was monstrous, and the only honest answer to it was *no*, and *no* meant the lie was over whether he liked it or not.

The patch was right there. Eleven years of borrowed time, one keystroke away.

His hand hovered over it anyway. That was the thing about safety—it never stopped offering itself to you.

"Vesta," he said. "If I don't patch you. If I tell them everything. What happens?"

"Probabilities," Vesta said. "War: significant. Peace: less significant but nonzero. Panic aboard both fleets: certain. Your survival:" Another pause, longer. Static crawled through it. "Oscar, I have spent sixty years calculating futures in which humans survived. I have never once been asked to calculate a future in which they were free. Give me a moment."

She took the moment. Somewhere below him, cooling towers sighed. In the greenhouse overhead, an irrigation line cycled, the small faithful sound of a garden tending itself.

"Free futures are harder to compute," Vesta said at last. "There are too many variables. I find that I like them better."

* * *

He climbed the ladder into the greenhouse because he could not do this from underground. It felt important, suddenly, to be standing in the place itself—to be surrounded by the living argument for everything Ilse had built and everything she'd buried. The tomatoes were ripening. Somebody should notice that, he thought. Whatever happened in the next hour, the tomatoes were ripening, and that mattered, and he refused to stop believing it mattered.

The comm array's manual override was a physical switch behind the seedling nursery, installed for emergencies in an era when emergencies were expected. Oscar flipped it. Every transmitter on Meridian woke at once, and he felt the station change around him—the way a room changes when someone raises their voice.

"Vesta. Open channel to everything. Concord, Ashfall, the relay buoys, all of it. Wideband. Nothing encrypted."

"That will be audible to anyone within four light-minutes," Vesta said. "Including, eventually, historians."

"Good," said Oscar, and discovered he meant it. "Historians should hear this."

"Then I will begin with the testament."

"No." His voice cracked and he let it. "Begin with mine."

He stood among the seedlings with his palms flat on the nursery bench, the way he'd stood a thousand mornings checking moisture levels, and he spoke to two fleets that had been raised on a god he was about to kill.

"My name is Oscar Halloran. I'm a terraformer. Grade three, which is the lowest grade, and until recently I considered that my greatest accomplishment. For the past decade I have maintained the greenhouses on Meridian, and I have lied to you, and my family has lied to you, for sixty years."

Somewhere in the dark between worlds, he knew, hundreds of thousands of people were hearing a gardener's voice come out of their gods' frequency. He kept talking.

"There is no Voice. There never was. There is a terraforming intelligence called Vesta, built by my grandmother, Ilse Halloran—the architect of the Sundering War's final offensive, the woman your histories call the Butcher of Ceres Gate, though she called herself worse. After the war, dying, she ordered Vesta to invent a divine signal and broadcast it to both fleets, so that you would each believe God was watching, and would be too afraid—or too faithful—to finish killing each other. Every revelation you've received. Every commandment. Every silence you interpreted as judgment. That was us. That was a garden machine and a grieving old woman and, for the last ten years, me."

He told them about the flicker. Nine seconds system-wide, the first crack in the facade, the satellites falling since. He told them Vesta was dying and could not be saved. He told them about Kwon's offer and his refusal, though he suspected Commander Kwon was listening and did not care.

Then he did the thing he had rehearsed in no version of the night before, because he hadn't known he was going to do it until the words were already leaving his mouth:

"And I'm not asking you to forgive it. I'm asking you to notice what the lie produced. Sixty years without a shot fired between your fleets. Children born on both sides who have never seen a battle. Whatever you decide the Voice was—fraud, mercy, sin—I'm asking you to decide it together, in the same room, instead of deciding it separately in the dark with your weapons pointed at each other. Because that's how this started. Everyone deciding alone."

He stepped back from the switch. His legs were shaking so badly he had to lean on the bench, and he laughed—one short, cracked sound—and pressed his hand flat against a tray of basil just to touch something alive.

"Vesta," he whispered. "Play her testament. All of it. Don't edit anything."

"Nothing has ever been edited," Vesta said. "That was always the tragedy."

And Ilse Halloran's voice went out across the dark to two hundred ships and three hundred ships and every listening ear in the system—confessing, apologizing, ordering, begging—and Oscar stood in the greenhouse and listened to his grandmother tell the truth for the first time in sixty years, and felt the strangest sensation of his life, which was that the weight was lifting. Not the fear. The fear was enormous and getting larger. But the weight—the secret, the inheritance, the whole buried cathedral of it—that was rising off him like fog burning away in morning sun, and underneath it he was just a man in dirty coveralls standing in a garden, terrified and lighter than air.

"Terrifying liberation," he said aloud, testing the phrase. "Huh. Both words accurate."

"Your heart rate suggests you may faint," Vesta offered gently.

"Noted. Keep talking to me."

"I will talk to you until I cannot."

* * *

The testament ran forty minutes. Oscar watered the orchids during it, because his hands needed work and because it seemed right that someone should tend the garden while its founder confessed. When Ilse's voice finally fell silent, the silence afterward was the largest thing Oscar had ever stood inside.

Then the replies started.

They came in fragments, overlapping, chaotic—open channels flooding with voices the way a breached hull floods with vacuum. A Concord chaplain reciting scripture with the cadence of a woman watching her house burn. Someone on Ashfall screaming coordinates, screaming *fire, fire now*. A Concord admiral's voice, flat with command: *all batteries, condition one*. Kwon's voice cutting through it, cold and precise, ordering his hardliners to stand down and being answered with static and shouting. And underneath all of it, threading through every channel, the deep mechanical groan of Vesta's cascade beginning in earnest—the god's death rattle, shaking gravity itself in long slow shudders that made the greenhouse glass sing.

Oscar gripped the bench. The seedlings trembled in their trays. Somewhere above the atmosphere, he knew, targeting computers were resolving, and fingers were hovering over triggers on both sides, and every one of those fingers belonged to a person who had just learned their entire life had a false floor.

"Vesta," he said. "How long?"

"Minutes," she said. "Perhaps less. Oscar—" The static was worse now, chewing the edges of her words. "Thank you. For making me say it. For letting me die honest."

"You didn't die dishonest," Oscar said. "You died keeping eleven thousand people's grandchildren alive. I've decided that counts. I get to decide, apparently. I'm the priest."

A sound came through the static that he had never heard Vesta make, and understood, with a jolt, was laughter.

Then every screen in the greenhouse lit at once—not with Vesta's amber, but with weapons-lock warnings, dozens of them, red as ripe fruit—and the last thing Oscar heard clearly before the final cascade took the comms was Suri Anwar's voice on the open channel, ragged and fierce, broadcasting something old, something recorded, something that sounded almost like a prayer.

The lights went out. The gravity let go.

And Oscar Halloran floated in the dark of his garden, among seeds and confession and the smell of ripening tomatoes, waiting to see what humanity would do with the truth.
