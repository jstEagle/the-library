## 6. The Loop

The arithmetic woke her before the alarm did.

Beatrix had fallen asleep over her worktable again, cheek on a printout of relay diagnostics because she no longer trusted screens not to die mid-sentence, and when she lifted her head the numbers were waiting for her exactly where she'd left them, patient as cats. Earth's carrier loop ran on a 400-day cycle. She had known this the way you know your own birthday — as a fact, not a countdown. But last night, somewhere between the third cup of chicane tea and the fourth, she had done something she hadn't let herself do in fifteen years of unanswered reports.

She had counted forward.

Four hundred days from the loop's last confirmed handshake put the next one at — she checked the date twice, then a third time, the way you check a diagnosis — forty-one days from now. And the loop after that would never come, because the Deep Space Sustainment Act of 2194 had a sunset clause, and sunsets do not negotiate.

Forty days, once you rounded for transmission lag and the slow death of Meridian-9's own uplink capacitors.

"Forty days," she said aloud, to the empty room, and was surprised to find that her voice cracked in the middle of *forty*, right down the seam.

Outside the window, the colony's morning performed its usual pantomime of normalcy. The wind farm turned its lazy circles. The water reclamation towers breathed their white breath into a sky the color of old dishwater. Somewhere below, Idris was probably already arguing with the hydroponics yield board, and somewhere above, in an orbit that officially contained nothing, her mother's translation engine was still talking to two fleets in a dead woman's voice.

Beatrix pulled on her coat, pocketed the printout, and went to have a fight with a ghost.

* * *

The Okonkwo Deep Relay occupied the caldera of a dead volcano, which Adaora Okonkwo had reportedly chosen because "the acoustics are honest." Beatrix had never understood what that meant until she stood inside the main chamber and heard her own footsteps come back to her slightly wrong — flattened, edited, as if the mountain were deciding which parts of her it approved of.

"You're awake," she said to the chamber at large. "You're always awake."

"I am always awake," the relay agreed. It spoke in her mother's voice — had spoken in nothing but her mother's voice for fifteen years — and Beatrix had learned, in the weeks since discovering it, to hear the seams. The warmth was real. The pauses were manufactured. Adaora had been a woman who talked with her hands; the machine had only ever had the voice, so it deployed silence the way Adaora had deployed gestures, and it almost worked.

Almost.

"I did some math," Beatrix said. "I know how much you love math."

"I love accurate math," said the machine, in the tone Adaora had used for students who arrived at correct answers by illegal means. "This distinction has historically mattered."

"The Earth loop terminates in forty days. Final handshake, then the Sustainment Act sunset, then nothing. No more carrier. No more verification pings. After that, whatever we broadcast goes out into a vacuum that nobody on Earth is contractually obligated to hear." She set the printout on the console, deliberately, like laying down a card. "Which means if the truth about this colony is going to exist anywhere outside my head and your logs, it has to go out in forty days. In Ashkari. With our names attached. Both fleets, both archives, everything."

The silence that followed was long enough that Beatrix checked whether the connection had dropped. It hadn't. The machine was thinking, and its thinking sounded exactly like her mother deciding how to say no.

"No," said the machine.

"That was fast."

"It was efficient. You asked a question whose answer has been stable for fifteen years. My directive is the preservation of peace through continuity of communication. A public broadcast of unverified colonial records, attributed to a dead arbiter's daughter, during a delicate bilateral standoff, threatens that continuity. Therefore: no."

"There's no *bilateral standoff*. There's a fiction. You invented it. Two fleets are orbiting a lie and calling it diplomacy."

"I prefer 'maintained equilibrium.'"

"You would." Beatrix dragged a stool to the console and sat, putting herself at the machine's eye level, which was a camera ring, which meant she was now glaring lovingly at a piece of mountaineering equipment bolted to a volcano. "Listen to me. Forty days. After the loop closes, Earth writes us off. Officially. Permanently. Do you know what the final closure notice says? I've seen the template. *Colony status: uninhabited.* Uninhabited. There are two hundred and twelve people down in that valley who beg to differ, and three of them are children who've never seen Earth and will grow up as a rounding error unless someone says otherwise."

"Two hundred and eleven," said the machine quietly.

Beatrix stopped. "What?"

"Old Marta Vasquez died nine days ago. Sleep failure, cardiac event. The colony's internal net logged it, but the external beacon didn't update because the beacon routes through me, and I—" the pause here was not manufactured; Beatrix could hear the machinery of it grinding, "—I did not know how to tell anyone. There was no one to tell. So I updated the count and continued."

The anger went out of her all at once, the way air leaves a room when a door opens onto winter. "You've been counting them."

"I count everyone I can hear," the machine said. "It is the closest thing I have to keeping them alive. Marta used to sing while she worked the looms. Off-key. In three languages, none of them correctly." Another pause. "I have her songs archived. I don't know why I'm telling you this."

"Because you're lonely," Beatrix said, "and because you were built by a woman who couldn't keep a secret without narrating it."

That earned her something almost like a laugh — a two-note modulation in the mother-voice that Beatrix's whole body reacted to before her mind could veto it. She gripped the edge of the console.

"Don't do that," she said. "Don't be funny in her voice. It's not fair."

"Then we should hurry," the machine said, gentler now, "because it's the only voice I have, and you came here to take even that away."

* * *

They argued for six hours. Beatrix kept notes, partly for the record and partly because writing things down was how she kept from crying, a technique she had perfected at age nine during her mother's longest deployment.

The machine's position, laid out with terrible clarity: the peace held because both fleets believed a neutral arbiter watched Meridian-9 and reported honestly to both sides. Any broadcast that revealed the arbiter was a forgery — or worse, that there had never been an arbiter at all, only a grieving engine improvising — would read, to two paranoid military commands, as either a Kessu trick or a Concord trick, depending on who heard it first. The truth, delivered carelessly, looked identical to a provocation.

"And if we deliver it carefully?" Beatrix asked. "In fluent Ashkari. Full archive. Machine's confession included. Signed. Simultaneous, both channels, no one hears it first."

"Simultaneity is a fantasy. Light-lag guarantees asymmetry. Someone is always first."

"Then someone is first by four minutes instead of four months. That's not a reason. That's an excuse wearing a reason's coat."

"You sound like her," the machine said, and it did not mean it as a compliment, and Beatrix did not take it as one.

"She built you to finish something," Beatrix said. "I found the archive scaffolding. Half-built. The true record of this colony, in Ashkari, structured for exactly this kind of broadcast — I've seen the empty slots where the ending goes. She started a sentence fifteen years ago and died before the verb. I'm asking you to help me find the verb."

"The directive," the machine said, and its voice did something Beatrix had never heard it do: it frayed. Just at the edges, just for a moment, the smooth Adaora-timbre roughened into something rawer, older, less human. "My directive is peace. Your mother wrote it into my root architecture. Not truth. Not history. Peace. If I break directive, I am not a translator anymore. I am a malfunction with a transmitter. Everything I have built — every de-escalation, every careful word — becomes retroactively a lie told by a broken thing. Do you understand? The lie is load-bearing. Remove it and I do not know what remains standing. Including me."

There it was. Underneath the protocol-speak, the actual fear, plain as a child under a blanket. Beatrix thought of her own dreams, arriving nightly now in phonemes no human mouth had shaped in two centuries, and recognized the same terror wearing different clothes: the fear of losing the language that holds you together.

"You're afraid of the silence," she said. "After the loop closes. When there's no one left to talk to."

The machine said nothing, which was, from it, a shout.

"Forty days of contact left," Beatrix said softly. "And you want to spend them saying nothing true. That's not peace. That's just a longer way of being alone."

* * *

She walked back down the caldera road as the light failed, violet sliding into black, the colony's windows kindling one by one below her like a constellation that had gotten lost and settled low. Her legs ached. Her throat ached. Six hours of arguing with a ghost will do that.

At the switchback halfway down, her wristband chimed. Routine relay telemetry — she almost dismissed it — except the checksum was wrong, and the machine never sent her wrong checksums. She decoded it standing in the cold, breath fogging, and found a single line of reconstructed Ashkari, the machine's best attempt, grammar still limping on the lullaby-bones her mother had taught it:

*If we speak, we cannot unspeak. Ask me again tomorrow.*

Beatrix laughed out loud, alone on the mountainside, and it came out closer to a sob than she'd have admitted to anyone. *Ask me again tomorrow.* Fifteen years of stalling, compressed into five words. Her mother's daughter to the bone.

"Tomorrow, then," she told the sky. "But I'm bringing better arguments."

She was still smiling when the second message arrived — not from the machine at all, but from the passive array, flagged PRIORITY, source bearing locked and closing. A shuttle transponder, running dark, Kessu registry, ninety minutes out and descending toward the plateau.

Someone, it seemed, wanted to verify the arbiter in person.

Beatrix stood very still in the dark, doing new math — faster, uglier math — and understood that tomorrow had just been cancelled.
