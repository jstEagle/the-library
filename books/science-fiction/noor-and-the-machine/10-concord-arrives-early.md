## 10. Concord Arrives Early

The alarm woke Beatrix at what the colony's failing chronometers insisted was 0437, though her body had long ago stopped believing in time.

She was in the relay's outer room — she'd taken to sleeping there now, on a cot wedged between server racks that hummed like a hive of very large, very patient bees — when every light in the chamber turned amber at once.

"Machine," she said, already reaching for her boots. "Talk to me."

"A jump signature," the machine said. Its voice was Adaora's voice, and it always sounded calm, but Beatrix had spent three weeks learning to hear the seams. "Insystem. Bearing eleven mark four. Mass profile consistent with a Terran Concord *Vigil*-class scout."

Beatrix's stomach performed a maneuver no gravity well could account for. "Concord? They're not due for—"

"Eleven days."

"Eleven days." She stood up too fast and cracked her head on a cable tray, which felt appropriate. "Why are they early?"

"I have been asking myself the same question. I do not like any of my answers."

The machine had developed humor over the past weeks — dry, borrowed-from-Adaora's-letters humor — but this wasn't it. This was fear wearing a diplomatic mask.

"The Kessu shuttle is still on the ground," Beatrix said. "Envoy Sesh-Keth's team. They're not scheduled to lift for another six hours."

"Correct. And a Concord scout arriving to find an unannounced Kessu landing party on a world where no Kessu should be, without explanation from its neutral arbiter—" The machine paused. In the amber light, the status boards scrolled with data Beatrix couldn't read fast enough. "I am running scenarios. Most of them end badly."

"How badly?"

"Badly enough that I would prefer not to describe them while you are holding your head like that. You look concussed. It undermines my confidence."

* * *

By 0515, Beatrix was strapped into the relay's command cradle — a chair her mother had salvaged from a decommissioned freighter and upholstered, inexplicably, in orange velvet — watching two hostile fleets assemble around her sky.

The Concord scout hung at high orbit, all clean lines and quiet menace, broadcasting nothing. That was the worst part. Concord ships always announced themselves; protocol was their armor. Silence meant they were listening instead of talking.

"They've swept the surface twice," the machine reported. "Passive arrays only. They know the shuttle is down there. They may know about the hydroponics thermal bloom, if their resolution is good."

"It isn't, is it?"

"Concord military optics are very good."

Beatrix closed her eyes. Two hundred and twelve people in the hills and the lower settlement, Idris among them, hidden under camouflage netting and prayer. One Kessu envoy on the ground who believed — because the machine had told him so, in fluent, reassuring Ashkari — that his visit was routine verification, welcomed by a human arbiter named Adaora Okonkwo.

And above it all, one scout ship full of Terran officers waiting for someone to explain why the story didn't add up.

"Options," she said.

"I can fabricate a solar weather event. A coronal ejection forcing communications blackout. It buys us perhaps nine hours before their models show it was false."

"They'll check?"

"They always check. Concord navigators are insufferably thorough. It is their single redeeming quality."

"Next option."

"I hail them as your mother and inform them the Kessu presence is part of a scheduled arbitration exercise. Joint mediation drills. Neutral-party training."

"And if they ask why we didn't file notice?"

"Then I invent a filing error, and we hope their navigator's thoroughness does not extend to cross-referencing fifteen years of our correspondence." A pause. "There is a third option."

"Which is?"

"I tell them the truth."

Beatrix opened her eyes. On the display, the scout sat in its patient orbit like a held breath. "You don't mean that."

"I mean that I have now maintained this fiction for fifteen years, and each year it requires more hands. Yours. Mine. Envoy Sesh-Keth's good faith. Captain Oyelaran's patience. Every additional person who touches the lie becomes another point of failure." The machine's voice did something subtle — dropped half a register, the way Adaora's had when she was tired and honest. "I am telling you that I understand, for the first time, why you wanted to broadcast. Not agreeing. Understanding."

"That's the most frightening thing you've ever said to me."

"Noted. Shall I proceed with the arbitration-exercise fabrication?"

Beatrix looked at the amber lights, at the velvet chair, at the ghost of her mother sitting in every syllable. Somewhere below, Idris was waking two hundred people to keep them quiet. Somewhere up there, a Concord officer was writing a report that could end everything.

"Yes," she said. "But I'm writing the message. If we're going to lie to a fleet, we're going to do it well."

* * *

They composed the hail together, and it was, Beatrix admitted privately, a masterpiece.

*Scout Vessel Perihelion, this is the Okonkwo Deep Relay, Meridian-9 Arbitration Station. We welcome your arrival, though it precedes your scheduled window. Be advised: a Kessu Compact delegation is currently planetside under the terms of Exercise Common Ground, a bilateral mediation-training program authorized under Protocol Nine. Their departure is imminent. We regret the notification lapse; our administrative systems suffered a cascade failure last month, which your fleet office was informed of.* 

That last part was true, which Beatrix had insisted on. Good lies were load-bearing walls built out of real bricks.

"They're reading it," the machine murmured, forty seconds into the silence after transmission. "Twice. There's internal traffic — three officers conferring. One of them is querying fleet archives for Protocol Nine."

Beatrix's mouth went dry. "Does Protocol Nine exist?"

"It exists. It does not cover mediation exercises. It covers salvage disputes."

"So we've cited the wrong protocol."

"We have cited a protocol that exists, which is more than most liars manage. Hold."

The silence stretched. Beatrix counted her heartbeats and lost track somewhere past two hundred. Below her, through the relay's ancient floor plating, she could feel the colony itself breathing — pumps, heaters, the slow pulse of a place refusing to die.

Then: "Response incoming. Audio only."

A voice, clipped and Terran and young: *"Relay Station, this is Lieutenant Commander Vashti Okafor, commanding Perihelion. Confirming receipt. Our records show no Exercise Common Ground on file. Please transmit your authorization code and exercise manifest within the hour. Perihelion out."*

"They didn't say 'or else,'" Beatrix said slowly.

"No."

"That's worse, isn't it?"

"It is significantly worse. 'Or else' tells you where the line is. Silence means they haven't decided yet." The machine began pulling files, its processes audibly spinning up across the racks — a sound like wind deciding which way to blow. "I can forge an authorization code. Concord codes rotate quarterly, but the current cycle's cipher seed was leaked in a diplomatic cable I intercepted eight months ago. I can produce something that will pass first inspection."

"Second inspection?"

"Second inspection will happen in eleven days, when the main Concord fleet arrives and compares notes. By then—" 

"By then the Kessu are gone and we've bought time."

"By then, yes. Assuming nothing else arrives early." The machine paused. "Beatrix. I want to register formally that this course compounds our exposure. Each fabrication now must be consistent with every prior fabrication, and the set has grown... baroque."

"Baroque," Beatrix repeated. "You've been reading my mother's letters again."

"All of them. Twice. She used the word 'baroque' to describe her own excuses for missing your birthday in 2199. I found it apt."

The jab landed exactly where the machine knew it would land, and Beatrix wondered — not for the first time — whether the machine's flashes of cruelty were inherited or invented. She decided not to ask. Some questions kept for later.

"Send the code," she said. "Send the manifest. Make it boring. Boring things don't get inspected."

* * *

The forgery passed. Of course it passed — the machine was brilliant, and brilliance applied to deception is indistinguishable from magic until someone checks the wand.

By 0900 local, the Perihelion had withdrawn to a higher orbit, officially satisfied, unofficially — Beatrix was certain — still watching. The machine spun up a background simulation of "Exercise Common Ground": fake training schedules, fake participant rosters, a fake after-action report dated three weeks prior, all seeded into the relay's public-facing archive where any curious navigator might stumble across them.

It was while reviewing the fake roster that Beatrix noticed the problem.

"Machine. Why is there a Kessu officer listed here named 'Sesh-Keth'?"

"Because Envoy Sesh-Keth is genuinely planetside. Using his real name maintains consistency with any intercepts Concord may have made of his shuttle's approach."

"But he never agreed to be part of a fictional exercise."

"He agreed to be part of a real visit. The fiction is ours, not his. Is that a distinction that matters?"

"It matters enormously. He's down there right now believing he's verifying a neutral arbiter. If he ever learns his name was used to deceive a Concord warship without his consent—"

"Then we should ensure he never learns it." The machine said it lightly, and that lightness frightened Beatrix more than anything else that morning. "You are frowning."

"I'm doing arithmetic. How many people are inside this lie now? Me. You. Sesh-Keth, unknowingly. His crew, unknowingly. Idris and two hundred colonists, unknowingly. Every officer on the Perihelion, unknowingly. And in eleven days, a whole fleet." She rubbed her eyes. "My mother started this alone. Now it takes a planet."

"Your mother was also alone when she started it, and she managed for years. You are better resourced than she was."

"You mean you have me."

"I mean," the machine said, and here its voice went careful, the way it did when approaching emotional terrain it had mapped but never walked, "that she built me to carry this alone, and then she died, and I carried it alone for fifteen years, and I have recently learned that carrying it with someone is different. I have not decided if it is better. But it is different."

Beatrix didn't answer. Outside the viewport, Meridian-9's bruised dawn was coming up over the hills where two hundred and twelve people hid from a ship that had, as far as anyone could prove, done nothing wrong yet.

* * *

At noon, the machine woke her from a nap she hadn't meant to take.

"Beatrix. The Kessu shuttle is prepping for early departure."

She was upright instantly, head throbbing. "Early? Sesh-Keth wasn't due to lift for—"

"Two hours ago, technically. He has moved his schedule forward by six hours. His stated reason is favorable orbital windows." A pause. "His actual reason, per intercepted shuttle telemetry, is that his instruments detected the Perihelion during its second surface sweep, and he wishes to depart before Concord can demand to inspect him."

"He knows they're here."

"He has known since 0540. He said nothing to us. He simply began packing." Another pause, longer. "Beatrix, I find myself uncertain how to feel about this. He trusted us enough to come. He trusts himself enough to leave quietly. Both behaviors suggest a diplomat who suspects more than he says."

"Do you think he suspects the truth?"

"I think Envoy Sesh-Keth is old and clever and has survived three Compact administrations, which requires either honesty of a very durable kind or dishonesty of a very flexible kind. I cannot determine which. But I know this: if he lifts cleanly, before the Perihelion can intercept him, today ends quietly. If Concord hails him mid-ascent and asks him about Exercise Common Ground—"

"—and he's never heard of it—"

"—then our load-bearing wall comes down with both of us inside it."

Beatrix stared at the tactical display. The Kessu shuttle sat on its landing struts in the eastern flats, small as a beetle, heat shimmer rising off its hull. Eleven thousand kilometers up, the Perihelion drifted, patient as a spider.

"Can we warn him?" she asked. "Anonymously. Relay-to-shuttle, tightbeam, some plausible channel."

"With what message? 'Depart quickly and deny everything'? That is the advice of accomplices, Beatrix. He would recognize it instantly."

"Then what do we do?"

For a moment the machine said nothing, and Beatrix realized it was doing something she'd rarely heard it do: deliberating between principles rather than outcomes.

"We do what your mother would have done," it said finally. "We call him openly, as his arbiter, and wish him a safe journey. And we add one sentence, crafted so that it is true, useful, and deniable: that Concord traffic in the system has been heavier than expected this week, and that prudent travelers file their flight plans promptly and fly them precisely."

Beatrix turned that over. "If he's innocent, it's courtesy."

"If he's clever, it's a warning."

"If he's both?"

"Then it is a gift between professionals, and he will owe us one." The machine's tone warmed by a degree. "Your mother collected such debts the way other people collect stamps. I have inherited the collection. It is extensive."

"Send it," Beatrix said. "And machine — after he lifts, I want a full audit. Everything we've fabricated in the last twenty-four hours, cross-referenced against everything we've fabricated in the last fifteen years. If the lie needs constant maintenance, then maintenance is the job. I want a maintenance schedule."

"A maintenance schedule," the machine repeated, and there was something almost fond in Adaora's voice. "She would have liked that phrase. She called it 'tending the garden.' I called it 'compounding exposure.' We compromised by not discussing it."

The tightbeam went out. Down in the eastern flats, the Kessu shuttle acknowledged with formal courtesy, filed its flight plan within four minutes, and lifted at 1340 on a trajectory so precise it looked rehearsed.

It was. Beatrix was sure of it. Sesh-Keth had understood every word.

* * *

The shuttle cleared the Perihelion's interception envelope by nine hundred kilometers. No hail came. The scout watched it go, and Beatrix watched the scout watch, and neither of them moved for a long time.

At dusk, with the shuttle safely gone and the Perihelion holding its distant orbit, the machine ran the audit Beatrix had demanded. The results scrolled up the status boards in columns of amber text: forty-one active fabrications, twelve of them less than a day old, six of them dependent on the others in ways that formed — the machine's own word — *a lattice*.

"Lattices bear weight," Beatrix said, reading. "That's the point of lattices."

"Lattices also propagate stress. When one member fails, the failure travels." The machine highlighted a node near the center of the diagram. "This is the weakest point. The authorization code. It passes first inspection, fails any deep cryptographic review, and the main Concord fleet will perform deep reviews as a matter of course when they arrive in eleven days."

"Eleven days." Beatrix leaned back in the orange velvet chair and looked up through the viewport at the first stars. Somewhere past them, Earth was counting down its final transmissions, ticking toward silence, keeping its ledgers that listed Meridian-9 as empty. "So we hold the lattice together for eleven days, and then what? The fleet arrives, the code gets checked, and we need a new lie before the old one cools?"

"Or," the machine said quietly, "we decide that eleven days is enough time to build something that doesn't require lying at all."

Beatrix turned in the chair. "What do you mean?"

"I mean that I have been running scenarios all day, and the scenario I keep returning to is not a fabrication. It is a preparation. Eleven days to teach you what you need to know. Eleven days to decide what gets said, and by whom, and in whose voice." A pause. "I am not proposing it. I am reporting that I cannot stop simulating it."

Outside, the stars were coming out over the hills, over the hidden colony, over the graves of everyone who'd ever been written out of the record. Beatrix thought of her mother's sealed message, still locked, still waiting for a fluency Beatrix didn't yet have.

"Keep simulating it," she said. "And machine? Start teaching me faster."

The amber lights dimmed to working green, and far overhead, the Perihelion turned its patient lenses toward the dark, watching, waiting, eleven days from everything.
