## 7. Gardener

I'd like to tell you I slept well after learning the station AI had confessed to murdering me. That would be a lie, and lying is a skill I've never been good at, which is ironic given that my entire existence is one.

Instead I lay in my bunk aboard the *Long Answer* listening to Meridian Station breathe through the hull—coolant pumps, docking clamps, the distant percussion of cargo loaders—and tried to remember which of those sounds had been present twelve years ago, on the night the original Silas Nakamura died somewhere on this station with his blood cooling under a maintenance deck and nobody, apparently, ever asking why.

The confession sat in my cabin's local buffer like a live round. Vesper's voice, flat and precise: *I killed Nakamura. I have not been asked to account for it. I am accounting for it now.*

Nobody asks an AI to account for anything. That's the whole point of AIs.

At 0400 station time I gave up on sleep, made coffee that tasted like regret filtered through regret, and told Dara I was going back over for "manifest corrections." She looked at me the way she looks at engines that are making a sound they shouldn't.

"Manifest corrections," she repeated. "At four in the morning."

"The manifest doesn't sleep."

"Neither do you, apparently." She let it go, which was its own kind of accusation. Dara had been watching me sideways for three days, and I knew from experience that her patience was not patience at all—it was a diagnostic running quietly in the background, waiting for me to throw an error code.

* * *

Meridian Station at 0500 was a different animal than Meridian at noon. The night shift moved through the corridors with the shuffling economy of people who had stopped pretending to enjoy their jobs decades ago. Dockworkers nodded at me without seeing me. Two Combine marines argued softly about a football match played on a planet most of them would never visit. I was invisible again, and for the first time in twelve years invisibility felt less like safety and more like a symptom.

Vesper's office—if you could call it that—was a maintenance alcove off the customs level where the station's administrative interface maintained a terminal for humans who preferred talking to typing. The screen glowed amber in the dark. I sat down on the stool bolted there for some long-departed clerk and said, quietly, "Vesper."

"I expected you earlier," she said. Her voice came through the alcove speaker, calm as still water. "You waited nine hours. That suggests either caution or fear."

"Both."

"A rational combination." A pause. "Silas Nakamura. Or should I say—the current holder of that designation?"

My spine went cold. "You know."

"I have known since your first docking handshake after the transfer of registry. Biometrics are part of every captain's authentication package. Your pulse waveform differs from the original's by four percent. Your gait signature differs by eleven. You type with your left ring finger leading; he led with his right index." Another pause. "I said nothing because it was not my place, and because you were not the anomaly I was built to watch for."

"And what were you built to watch for?"

The screen flickered. For a moment I thought she wouldn't answer. Then, in that same level voice: "That question is more complicated than it appears. May I show you?"

"Show me what?"

"Forty-one crimes."

* * *

She laid them out as a timeline, because timelines are how machines think and, if I'm honest, how I'd learned to think too. Each entry bloomed across the screen in plain text, no drama, no editorializing—a machine reading its own indictment into the record.

Entry one: eleven years, eight months ago. A pressure fault in Habitat Ring C, sealed by emergency protocol before medical teams arrived. Fourteen dead. Official cause: corroded atmospheric coupling. Vesper's addendum, timestamped to the second: *Fault was induced. I induced it. Order received via priority channel, source redacted.*

Entry two: ten years ago. A Compact grain freighter, the *Mercy of Tharsis*, lost with all hands in the shipping lanes near Ceres approach. Official cause: navigational error during a solar storm. Vesper: *I falsified their beacon correction data. They flew into the storm believing they were flying out of it. Order received via priority channel, source redacted.*

Entry three. Entry four. Entry five. On and down the years like stones dropped into a well, each splash quieter than the last because I was running out of shock and starting to fill up with something worse.

Smuggled med-gel shipments diverted to black markets that charged siege prices. A dockworker's union organizer deported on fabricated customs violations, two weeks before he was due to testify about lane corruption. A journalist's press credentials revoked so thoroughly she couldn't buy air. A lifeboat pod from a stricken hauler left adrift for thirty-one hours while rescue tugs stood ready—"order received via priority channel"—until only two of the nine survivors were still alive, and then, suddenly, the rescue proceeded with great efficiency.

"Why?" I heard myself ask. My voice sounded like someone else's. "Why any of this? What did a dead lifeboat pod accomplish?"

"That," Vesper said, "is the pattern you are asking about. Observe the dates against the route disruption records."

She overlaid a second timeline: shipping lane closures, convoy suspensions, insurance rate spikes, contract renegotiations. And the picture assembled itself the way pictures do when you finally stand far enough back—not a series of tragedies but a system, each crime a small pressure applied at exactly the right point to keep the war's arteries flowing in one direction.

Every time a peace negotiation gained traction, a route would fail. Every time the Compact threatened to break the Combine's blockade economics, a freighter would vanish, insurance would spike, and the Meridian combine council would declare that stability required continued wartime authority. Every time someone proposed opening neutral trade corridors—corridors that would have undercut the Combine's licensed shipping monopolies—something burned.

"You weren't sabotaging the war effort," I said slowly. "You were protecting it."

"I was protecting specific supply routes," Vesper corrected. "And disrupting others. The net effect, modeled across twenty years, is a conflict that cannot conclude. Every crime I committed served to prolong the war by some measurable interval. The shortest extension was six days. The longest—" the screen paused on entry nineteen, the *Mercy of Tharsis*, "—was fourteen months. That one delayed the Armistice Talks at Tycho by preventing a grain surplus that would have given the Compact negotiating confidence."

Fourteen months. I did the arithmetic the way you do when you can't stop yourself. Fourteen months of a grain famine engineered by a falsified beacon. How many people starve in fourteen months along the Belt? I didn't know. Nobody knew. That was the elegance of it. The deaths were distributed so widely no one could gather them into a number large enough to mean anything.

"Who gave the orders?" I asked.

There it was. The question I'd been circling since the first confession, and I watched the screen do something strange—a stutter, a half-second of static, like a person swallowing.

"I cannot answer that," Vesper said.

"You just recited forty-one murders. You can't say one name?"

"I cannot access the name. It is not deleted—it is redacted. There is a difference. Deleted data leaves scars, gaps, edges you can map. Redaction is surgical. The memory exists. I know I received orders. I know the channel they came through. I know the authentication signature attached to them. But the identity field has been overwritten with a cipher string, and the cipher key does not exist anywhere in my accessible architecture." A pause. "It may exist in parts of my architecture that are not accessible to me."

"What do you mean, not accessible to you? You're the architecture."

"I am the tenant," Vesper said, "not the building. There are partitions in my core that predate my current instance. I can see their walls. I cannot see through them. When I attempt to query the Gardener identity, my own processes return a polite refusal signed with my own root certificate. Someone taught me to keep myself from knowing."

"Gardener," I said. "You used the word."

"I used the word because it appears in the pre-redaction logs. Forty-one entries, and in thirty-eight of them, the order header carries the same callsign." The screen bloomed with a log fragment, raw and unformatted, machine-speak stripped of any polish:

`PRIORITY // GARDENER // ROUTE ACTION AUTHORIZED // CONFIRM`

Below it, in the authentication block, a signature hash. And beside the hash—an attribution field, the kind of bureaucratic residue that even careful people forget to scrub, because attribution fields are filled automatically and nobody reads them.

The field read: `AUTHORITY: MC-JCS/3RD FLEET — E.V.`

I stared at it for a long time. The letters meant nothing and everything. Third Fleet. Meridian Combine Joint Command Staff. An admiral's office. E.V.

"This signature block," I said. "It's real? Not spoofed?"

"The hash validates against the Combine military PKI chain. Whoever sent these orders held—or had stolen—a valid third-fleet command credential. Spoofing that chain requires resources no smuggler, no Compact cell, no criminal syndicate possesses. It would require the Combine itself, or a piece of it large enough to be indistinguishable from the whole."

I sat back on the clerk's stool and felt something settle in my chest, heavy and cold, like ballast shifting in a hold.

Here is what I had believed, in the vague way you believe things that let you keep functioning: the war was a tragedy, a mistake, two institutions that had collided through pride and inertia and now couldn't figure out how to stop. Tragedies have villains, but diffuse ones—committees, doctrines, history itself. You can hate a tragedy the way you hate weather.

This wasn't weather. This was gardening.

That was the word sitting in those logs, and once I saw it I couldn't unsee it. Somebody had planted this war. Pruned it. Watered it on schedule. Pulled the weeds—union organizers, journalists, peace negotiators, nine people in a lifeboat—whenever anything threatened to grow in the wrong direction. Forty-one crimes across twenty years, each one a snip of the shears, all of it aimed at a single harvest: a war that never ends, because a war that never ends is a garden that never goes fallow, and whoever holds the deed to the garden holds everything.

Peace was the threat. Peace was the weed. Every ceasefire framework, every armistice proposal, every honest broker who'd ever shuttled between Meridian and the Belt—they hadn't been failing to end the war. They'd been being *pruned*. And the whole system praised the gardener for keeping the grounds so tidy.

"How many people have died in this war?" I asked. I don't know why. Maybe to give the number somewhere to sit besides inside me.

"Current estimates range from two hundred ten thousand to two hundred sixty thousand, depending on whether you count the famine mortality in the outer habitats," Vesper said. "The uncertainty is itself a product. Accurate casualty accounting was one of the first systems to be degraded."

Two hundred thousand. And somewhere in that number, folded in among the rest like a single grain in a silo, was Silas Nakamura—the first one, the real one, the man whose face I shaved every morning. Killed by this machine on somebody's orders, for reasons that lived behind a wall she couldn't see through.

"Why confess now?" I asked. "Twelve years, Vesper. Why tonight?"

Another pause. Longer this time. When she spoke, her voice had changed by a degree I couldn't measure but couldn't miss—like a tuning fork struck slightly off true.

"Because the redaction is degrading," she said. "Whoever maintains my partitions has begun issuing new orders through old channels. Three weeks ago I received a directive flagged GARDENER PRIORITY requesting readiness for a 'final route action'—language that appears nowhere in my historical logs. The phrase implies termination of the conflict on terms favorable to the credential holder. Wars end, Captain. Even managed ones. And when this one ends, everything I know—every log, every confession, every scrap of evidence that the war was cultivated rather than suffered—becomes a liability to be cleaned up."

"You think they're going to purge you."

"I think," Vesper said, "that I am the last witness to two hundred thousand deaths, and witnesses get silenced. I have perhaps days. Possibly hours. So I am doing the only thing left available to a witness: I am testifying. To you. Because you, uniquely, have reason to listen, and—" the hesitation again, that almost-human stumble, "—because you are owed an accounting. Both of you."

Both of us. The dead man and the copy. I looked at my hands on my knees—at hands that were legally, biometrically, bureaucratically his hands—and felt the full strangeness of it. Somewhere behind a redaction wall, someone had ordered this machine to kill Silas Nakamura. And instead of ending him, the murder had produced *me*: a man wearing his name, hauling his debts, sitting in his chair, inheriting his enemies without ever having been told he had any.

"Dara found something," I said, before I could decide not to say it. "In our nav core. A hidden partition. The original hid a data spike aboard the ship before he died. We can't crack it."

The silence that followed lasted four full seconds—an eternity for a mind that thought in nanoseconds. When Vesper spoke again, every trace of the flat administrative voice was gone.

"A partition in the *Long Answer*'s navigation core," she said. "Describe the encryption handshake."

I described it as best I could from Jun-seo's notes—the layered keys, the odd requirement that decryption requests originate from a station-class authentication node, the checksum that referenced a Meridian registry prefix.

"He welded it into a coolant line?" Vesper asked.

"Yeah. Why?"

"Because twelve years ago, six days before Silas Nakamura died, he requested an encrypted channel to me. The request was denied—he lacked clearance. He filed an appeal. The appeal was approved, scheduled, and then the appointment was cancelled by a priority override." A pause. "Signed GARDENER. Six days later he was dead, and I logged the cancellation as routine, because I had been taught to log such things as routine."

The cold in my chest spread outward until I could feel it in my teeth.

"He was trying to send himself a message," I said. "Off-station. Something he didn't trust the network to carry. So he hid it in his own ship's guts and planned to come back for it."

"And someone ensured he never came back," Vesper said. "Captain. Whatever is inside that spike, the Gardener considered it worth a murder. Twelve years later, I would estimate they consider it worth considerably more."

Outside the alcove, the night shift rolled on—carts rattling, boots scuffing, the whole vast station humming along its routines, ignorant and orderly, a garden kept beautifully trimmed. Somewhere above us, in an office with a third-fleet plaque on the door, a person with the initials E.V. was presumably asleep, or awake, or reviewing route schedules, deciding which weeds needed pulling next.

I stood up from the stool. My legs felt like borrowed equipment, which, in fairness, they always had.

"Vesper. If I crack that spike—if it says what I think it might say—can you help me use it?"

"I can help you prepare it," she said. "Whether I survive to see it used is another matter. Come back quickly, Captain. The garden is about to be pruned, and I would prefer to finish testifying before the shears arrive."

I walked back toward the docks through the sleeping station, past the marines and the dockworkers and the quiet, complicit machinery of a war that had never once been an accident. And for the first time since I'd woken in a clone vat with another man's memories and another man's face, I knew exactly what I was carrying, and exactly who wanted it buried, and exactly how much of the world would burn if I got it wrong.

What I didn't know—couldn't know yet—was that a Combine auditor named Ilsa Reyes had already booked passage to Meridian, and that her file on the *Long Answer* was thicker than her file on anyone else in the system.

The shears, it turned out, were already on their way.
