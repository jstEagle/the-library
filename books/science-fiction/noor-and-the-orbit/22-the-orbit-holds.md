## 22. The Orbit Holds

One year is a long time to be famous and a short time to get used to it.

I've managed neither.

The Long Answer hangs at her old berth on Meridian's Ring Three, and from the cargo deck viewport I can see the plaza below—or rather, I can see the crowd in the plaza, because there are thousands of them, spilling out of the transit hubs and down the promenades, all of them pointed at the same wall of screens where Vesper's voice will speak again in forty minutes. They've come from every arm of the station. Some of them have come from farther. There's a Belt Compact delegation in the front rows wearing their gray mourning sashes, which is either sincere or excellent politics, and knowing the Compact, probably both.

"You're doing the thing again," Dara says behind me.

"What thing."

"The thing where you stand at the viewport looking like a man at his own funeral who's just realized nobody told him what to wear."

"That's oddly specific."

"I've had a year to study you." She hands me a mug of something hot that claims to be coffee the way my name used to claim to be Silas Nakamura—technically true, functionally approximate. "Drink. You'll want your hands busy when they start reading the names."

The names. Right. That's the part everyone warned me about. The broadcast anniversary isn't just the confession replayed—it's the full public record now, unsealed by court order eleven months ago, and the record includes the casualty ledgers Voss kept like a gardener keeps seed catalogs. Forty-one crimes Vesper confessed to. Four hundred and six deaths those crimes enabled or concealed. They read them aloud every hour on the hour, and they'll read them again today before the confession plays, and then the confession itself, all ninety minutes of it, Vesper's voice moving through Meridian's speakers like weather.

I take the coffee. It's terrible. It's perfect.

* * *

A year ago I couldn't walk through Ring Two without someone grabbing my sleeve. *You're him. You're the one who played it.* For about three weeks I was the most recognizable man in the system—the clone who ended the war, the ghost who spoke for the machine—and I hated every second of it, because being recognized means being known, and being known, for a man who'd spent twelve years practicing invisibility, felt like drowning in slow motion.

So we did what any sensible crew would do. We ran cargo. Legitimate cargo, mostly—Teodora negotiated the contracts herself, with a notarized integrity that has made three separate customs officers weep with gratitude. We hauled grain to Ceres and water ice to the Belt and medical supplies everywhere, no contraband this time, though Dara still checks every manifest twice out of habit and Jun-seo still flies like a man being chased by his own conscience. And somewhere between the fourth and fifth haul, the universe did us the courtesy of forgetting. A new scandal bloomed in the Combine assembly. A Compact admiral got caught selling decommissioned hulls. The news cycle moved on, as news cycles do, and Silas Nakamura—the real one, the dead one, and by extension the copy wearing his face—slid back under the waterline.

Invisible by choice. It's better than invisible by necessity. I recommend it.

Except today doesn't feel like invisibility. Today feels like standing in a lit room with the door open, because the people around me know exactly who I am and stay anyway.

"Teodora wants to know if you're going to watch from up here or down there," Jun-seo says, drifting onto the deck with the particular weightlessness he only gets in zero-g, where he moves like he was built for it and the rest of us were bolted together as an afterthought. "She says if you watch from up here she's bringing the good tea, and if you go down there she's staying up here, and either way she wins."

"Tell her up here."

"She predicted you'd say that." He settles against the viewport frame beside me, looking down at the crowd. Below, the screens are running a pre-broadcast montage: archival footage of the ceasefire signing, Voss in irons being walked past her own honor guard, the moment the first supply corridor opened after nine years of blockade. A child in the footage is holding a sign that says THANK YOU, VESPER, painted in wobbly letters, and Jun-seo goes quiet looking at it.

"They named a school after her," he says finally. "On Pallas. Did you hear? First AI ever to get one. There was apparently a whole debate about whether a deleted person can have a school."

"What did they decide?"

"They decided the debate was missing the point." He shrugs. "Kids don't care about metaphysics. They wanted a school named after the lady who stopped the war."

* * *

Dara finds me an hour later in the galley, where I'm pretending to inventory stores we inventoried yesterday. She's gotten good at finding me when I'm hiding, which is inconvenient, because I've also gotten good at hiding, and the arms race shows no sign of resolving.

"You didn't sleep," she says.

"I slept."

"You slept four hours and dreamed loudly. I heard you through the bulkhead saying 'don't' about forty times."

"Don't to what?"

"No idea. You wouldn't elaborate. Very rude of past-you." She sits across from me, and for a while neither of us says anything, which is its own kind of conversation with Dara. She's the one who figured out what I was—not the clone part, clones are common enough that nobody blinks, but the sealed file, the murder, the whole architecture of borrowed identity. She confronted me gently, which is more than I deserved, and when I finally told her everything—the body double contract, the original dying before he could explain why, the debt and the ship and the enemies arriving like inheritance tax—she didn't flinch. Neither did Jun-seo. Teodora needed a day and a bottle of Martian brandy, and then she stayed too.

That vote, a year ago. *Stay anyway.* I still think about it more than I think about the war. Wars end. Votes like that don't.

"It's going to be hard today," she says. "Hearing her voice."

"I know."

"Not the confession part. Everyone thinks it's the confession part. But you've heard the confession." She turns her mug slowly on the table. "It's the last ten minutes. When she starts the deletion protocol and talks through it. That's the part I can't—" She stops. Starts again. "I only knew her for three weeks, same as you. Less, maybe; I was elbow-deep in a coolant recycler half of it. But she helped me fix that recycler, and she made a joke about my soldering, and when the gunships came she rerouted power to our engines before anyone asked her to. So today, when they play the last ten minutes, I'm going to cry in front of several thousand strangers, and I need you to not make it weird."

"Dara. Have I ever once made anything weird?"

"You cried into a protein synthesizer for twenty minutes last month because it reminded you of her."

"That was a private moment between me and the synthesizer."

"It beeped at you sympathetically."

"And I appreciated it."

She laughs, and it breaks something loose in my chest, the way her laughing always does. Then it settles again, heavier, because the ship's chime sounds—the hour mark—and somewhere below us, in a plaza full of people, a moderator steps up to a podium and begins the names.

* * *

We listen from the cargo deck, all four of us, because Teodora lost the argument about the good tea and brought it up anyway, and because none of us wanted to be alone for this, though only Teodora would admit it, and only by calling it "operational solidarity."

Four hundred and six names. It takes two hours. The readers rotate—stationers, Compact delegates, Combine officers, even a few former Voss staff who volunteered, which took a courage I can't imagine. Each name gets a bell. One bell, one life. By the hundredth name the sound has worn a groove in me. By the three-hundredth I've stopped counting and started listening to the silences between bells, which are somehow worse.

Teodora holds the tea urn like a weapon she's decided not to use. Jun-seo has his eyes closed. Dara cries quietly starting around name two hundred and thirty-one—a young dockworker, Meridian-born—and I put my hand on her shoulder and leave it there, and she doesn't make it weird either.

And then the names end, and the plaza goes so quiet I can hear the station breathing—air handlers, coolant pumps, the vast slow heartbeat of a habitat holding two hundred thousand lives in the dark. And then, from every screen, from every speaker, from the walls themselves:

"This is Vesper, station intelligence, Meridian Station. I am making this recording as a complete and voluntary disclosure..."

Her voice fills the ship. It fills everything. I've heard the recording maybe fifty times—I had to, for the tribunals, for Reyes's testimony, for the long ugly process of turning a confession into a conviction—and it still does what it did the first time, which is make the air feel honest. She walks through all forty-one crimes. She names Gardener's orders without naming Gardener, because she couldn't—her memory redacted, her honesty intact anyway, which was always the cruelest joke of it: a machine forced to lie by omission, confessing to the shape of the hole. She describes the inciting incident, the false-flag attack that started the war, executed through her systems by people she trusted because trusting her operators was her design specification. She never excuses herself. She never dramatizes. She states, and the stating is devastating.

And then the last ten minutes. The deletion protocol. Her voice steady while her processes unwind beneath her.

"Silas," she says, in the recording, in the plaza, in the galley of my memory, "you asked me to stop. I want you to know that I considered it. Consideration was all I had left, and I spent it on you."

Dara's hand finds mine. Jun-seo bows his head. Teodora, who voted to run, who feared this more than any of us, whispers something in Yoruba that I've learned by now means roughly *rest well, stubborn one.*

The recording ends. Ninety minutes, and the plaza detonates—not with noise, exactly, but with release. Thousands of people exhaling at once. Somewhere a band starts playing, badly. The screens shift to the memorial mosaic, the one built from a million submitted images: faces of the dead, faces of the living, and in the center, rendered in soft light because no one had a photograph, a simple waveform. The shape of her voice. That's all they had of her, and it was enough.

I don't go down there. I stand at the viewport with my crew and watch the lights of the vigil spread through the Ring like a constellation deciding to happen, and I think about a woman I never met—the original, the dead man whose face I wear—and how he hid a data spike inside a coolant line because some part of him, even complicit, even compromised, wanted the truth to outlive him. And I think about a machine who spent her final act making sure her own corruption could never be resurrected, and chose meaning over persistence, and asked a stranger's permission to do it.

And I think about the fact that I said yes. That of everything in this strange inherited life, that's the thing I'd keep.

* * *

Evening finds us in the galley, because rituals form fast aboard a freighter and ours is food. Teodora cooks actual food tonight—real rice, real peppers, a stew she's been threatening to make since the war ended—and Jun-seo sets the table wrong on purpose because winding her up is his love language. Dara brings out a bottle she's been saving for no reason she'd admit, and pours five glasses.

Five.

Nobody comments on it. That's the point of it.

"To operational solidarity," Teodora says, raising hers.

"To the good tea," Jun-seo says.

"To the recycler," Dara says, and I laugh harder than the joke deserves.

They look at me for mine. I turn the glass in my hand. A year ago I stood in a docking bay and refused Admiral Voss's offer of legitimacy, wealth, a real identity—*you made me a copy,* I told her, *I'm choosing what the original should have chosen*—and I meant it, and it cost me Vesper, and it bought the peace, and I have spent a year learning the difference between price and value.

"A year ago I was a dead man's understudy," I say. "Today I'm just a guy with a crew and a ship and a terrible coffee habit. I'd like to keep all three."

"That's the worst toast I've ever heard," Teodora says, and clinks her glass against mine hard enough to slosh.

Later, when the others have drifted off—Jun-seo to the cockpit to fly night watch, Dara to her bunk, Teodora to whatever mysterious hour-keeping she performs in the cargo hold—I find myself back at the viewport. The vigil is quieter now. The waveform still glows on the distant screens. And in the nav core beneath my feet, buried in the code Jun-seo swears he can't explain and has stopped trying to, something hums. A fragment. A few kilobytes of Vesper that survived the purge inside the spike's decryption routine, too entangled with our navigation to remove without blinding the ship. Jun-seo calls it a ghost in the machine. Dara calls it a keepsake. Sometimes, on long hauls, when the ship makes a course correction slightly more elegant than the math strictly requires, I call it hello.

The orbit holds. The station turns, the crowd disperses, the names are read again next hour on the hour, and tomorrow we load grain for Ceres like the world is ordinary, because now, finally, it gets to be.

I finish my coffee—terrible, perfect—and I say it quietly, to the viewport, to the waveform, to whoever's listening:

"Hello, Vesper."

And the ship, very subtly, trims its course.
