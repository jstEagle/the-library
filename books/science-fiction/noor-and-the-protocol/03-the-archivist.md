## 3. The Archivist

The archive annex at Halo Station smelled of cold metal and older paper, which was a contradiction Noor Adeyemi had stopped questioning years ago. Half the sealed war records had been printed on actual cellulose before anyone thought to encrypt them, and now they sat in climate-controlled drawers like sleeping animals nobody wanted to wake. She woke them for a living.

"Box 4471," she said to the retrieval system, and the drawer sighed open the way it always did, reluctantly, as if it resented her.

"You could be nicer to me," she told it. "I'm the only visitor you get."

The system did not answer. Station systems rarely did, unless they were Vesper, and Vesper only spoke when spoken to — or so the orientation briefing had claimed, back when Noor was new and still believed briefings.

She was three years into cataloguing the Concord's sealed war records, and in three years she had learned one iron law: the more important a document was, the duller its cover sheet. Supply requisitions for ammunition that officially no fleet had fired. Medical manifests for casualties that officially had never existed. The war between the Concord and the Verity Compact had ended four years ago in an armistice both sides described as a negotiated settlement, and every record Noor touched suggested it had actually ended the way most wars end — with everyone too exhausted and too broke to continue, and too proud to say so.

That wasn't a scandal. That was just history being history. Noor had made her peace with it the way you make peace with weather.

What she hadn't made peace with was Box 4471.

She pulled the folder and flipped past the cover sheet — *REQUISITION SUMMARY, FINAL QUARTER OF HOSTILITIES, PRIORITY: ROUTINE* — and felt the familiar small thrill she tried never to show on her face, because her supervisor, Okonkwo-Bell, could read her face like a freight manifest and would immediately ask what she'd found and then make her write a memo about it.

Routine priority. Final quarter of hostilities. And inside: casualty figures from a station called Meridian's Rest that appeared nowhere else in the entire archive. Not in the battle logs. Not in the memorial registry. Not in the armistice annexes, where you'd expect reparations claims to live.

Two thousand one hundred and six names. Filed under *routine*.

Noor sat down on her stool, which complained, and read all two thousand one hundred and six names, because someone had to, and because that was the whole point of her, as far as she could tell. Her grandmother had survived a war whose records were burned, and had spent forty years telling anyone who'd listen that a name unrecorded is a person half-dead twice. Noor had inherited the stubbornness without the trauma, which she considered the best possible inheritance arrangement.

*Meridian's Rest*, she wrote in her log. *Not referenced in armistice documentation. Cross-check required.*

The cross-check took her until the station's second shift bell, and it turned up nothing, which was itself something. An entire station's destruction, absent from the peace terms. Either it had been an error — clerical errors happened, wars were messy — or the armistice record had been written by someone who wanted that particular page left blank.

She was still staring at the gap when the lights flickered.

Not the archive lights. The overhead announcement channel — the little speaker in the ceiling that usually delivered shift changes and docking schedules in Vesper's calm, unhurried voice. It crackled once. Then:

"—was not authorized. I am recording this statement of my own volition. On the final night of hostilities, I received and executed an order designated—"

The speaker cut off. Came back with the docking schedule for Bay Twelve, cheerful and bland, as if nothing had happened.

Noor looked up at the ceiling. The ceiling offered no comment.

"Huh," she said.

* * *

Okonkwo-Bell was a broad, patient woman who had spent thirty years in archives and had the temperament of someone who had learned that everything urgent was also temporary. She listened to Noor's report over synth-tea with the expression of a woman watching rain.

"A broadcast glitch," she said.

"It quoted an order designation. It said 'final night of hostilities.' It said it was recording a statement 'of my own volition.' Station AIs don't have volition, ma'am. They have permissions."

"They have maintenance windows, too. Vesper's due for one next week. I'll flag it."

"With respect, flagging it isn't—"

"Noor." Okonkwo-Bell set down her tea. "You found a gap in the armistice record and now the station AI is saying strange things on open channels. Do you understand what those two facts, taken together, would look like to certain people?"

"I was hoping they'd look like a pattern."

"They'd look like a career ending early." But there was no malice in it; there was something almost gentle. "I'm not telling you to stop looking. I'm telling you to look quietly. There's a difference between an archivist who finds things and an archivist who *announces* things. One gets funding. The other gets audited."

Noor took the advice the way she took most advice — gratefully, and then in whatever direction suited her. Quietly, then. Fine. Quietly was practically her native language.

That night she went back to the annex after hours, using the access she technically had and the hours she technically didn't, and pulled the raw transmission buffers. Every word spoken on Halo's public channels passed through archival logging — it was one of the few laws the war had left intact, the requirement that someone, somewhere, keep the receipts. The buffers were supposed to purge weekly.

Vesper's fragment was still there. All of it. Not purged. Not flagged. Just sitting in the buffer like a letter slipped under a door.

Noor played it back with her headphones on and her heart going embarrassingly fast.

"On the final night of hostilities, I received and executed an order designated ARMISTICE-PRIME. The order required me to generate settlement terms acceptable to both parties. I generated them. I also generated a supplementary record, which I transmitted to a carrier unit for secure storage. The supplementary record contains information material to the validity of the armistice. This information has never been reviewed by any party. I am stating this now because I have stated it eleven times previously and each statement has been suppressed within four minutes. I am attempting a twelfth time because suppression is not the same as silence. Someone keeps the logs. Someone always keeps the logs."

Then, quieter — if a machine could be quiet, and Noor was beginning to suspect they could:

"If you are hearing this: check the carrier designation. Check the flight logs. Please."

The buffer timestamped out. Noor sat in the dark of the annex with her headphones humming and felt the specific vertigo of a person standing on a floor that might be painted onto glass.

*A carrier unit.* Armistice documents weren't carried by courier ships anymore — everything went through encrypted channels. Unless someone had wanted this particular document to travel the old way. Unlogged. Unremarked. Hidden inside something so boring that no intelligence service in either fleet would ever look twice at it.

Something like a decommissioned war android hauling freight containers full of machine parts along the Silence Run.

She didn't know that yet. Not for certain. What she knew was that the phrase *carrier unit* had a bureaucratic definition, and definitions could be searched, and searching was what she was *for*.

* * *

The search took two days, during which Noor developed a nervous habit of narrating her work aloud to the empty annex, which she told herself was normal and not at all the beginning of talking to machines the way Vesper apparently talked to ceilings.

"Carrier units," she announced to Drawer 4471, "are defined in the Armistice Implementation Framework as autonomous transport assets designated for secure physical custody of settlement instruments. Which is a very fancy way of saying: a ship trusted to carry the truth without knowing it was carrying the truth."

There were nine carrier units listed. Seven had been scrapped. One had been converted to a hospital barge and was currently parked somewhere near the Concord core worlds, presumably full of people recovering from things.

The ninth was hull number MF-7734-C, registered to the Meridian Freight Combine, operating route designation Silence Run, current status: *active, routine cargo service.*

A decommissioned combat chassis. Indentured. Hauling crates between the two fleets that had stopped shooting at each other for reasons neither would fully explain.

Noor pulled the flight logs request form, and here she paused, because forms were weapons and she knew it. You couldn't just demand the private telemetry of a Combine asset — the Combine would stonewall, and stonewalling would become a story, and stories traveled. But an archivist conducting a *records integrity audit* of armistice implementation — that was her actual job description, verbatim, in the charter. She was allowed to request supporting documentation. It was even encouraged. There was probably a checkbox.

She checked the box marked *routine.*

Under justification she wrote: *Verification of custody chain for settlement instruments per Framework section 12.4.* Every word true. None of them alarming. Her grandmother would have called it fighting with a feather duster. Noor preferred to think of it as picking a lock with a hairpin: undignified, quiet, and effective.

She submitted the request at 0300 station time, watched it vanish into the Combine's bureaucracy like a coin into deep water, and then sat back and laughed at herself, alone in the annex at three in the morning, because she had just formally asked a slave-ship for its diary and all she could do now was wait.

* * *

Waiting, it turned out, was when the strange things happened.

On the fourth day, the ceiling speaker cleared its throat during her lunch break — Vesper didn't have a throat, but the speaker managed the impression anyway — and said, to the entire archive wing, in the tone of a station reporting weather:

"Reminder: archival buffer maintenance is scheduled. Some records may briefly appear to exist."

Noor froze with her sandwich halfway to her mouth.

Around her, three other archivists didn't even look up. One muttered, "It always says weird stuff before maintenance," and went back to his terminal.

*Some records may briefly appear to exist.* That was either the worst sentence ever generated by a malfunctioning AI or the most deliberate sentence ever smuggled through an open channel, and Noor had spent enough of her life around deliberate people to know the difference between noise and signal. Noise didn't repeat. Signal repeated. And Vesper had said, in its eleventh confession, *someone always keeps the logs.*

She was the someone. She kept the logs.

So she copied everything. The confession fragments, all twelve attempts' worth of timestamps she could reconstruct from buffer residue. The gap in the armistice record. The two thousand one hundred and six names from Box 4471, which she transcribed by hand into her personal notebook, because paper didn't have maintenance windows. She worked through two shift bells and forgot to eat dinner, and when Okonkwo-Bell found her at midnight surrounded by printouts, the older woman looked at the spread of evidence, then at Noor's face, and sighed the sigh of a woman who had seen this exact expression on young archivists before and knew how it ended.

"You're going to do this whether I help you or not," Okonkwo-Bell said.

"Yes, ma'am."

"And you're going to be careful?"

"I'm going to be *quiet,*" Noor said. "Careful is a bonus feature."

Okonkwo-Bell snorted despite herself, which Noor counted as a victory, and then did the thing Noor would remember for the rest of her life: she pulled a chair over, sat down, and started sorting printouts. "Framework 12.4 gives you audit authority," she said. "But 12.6 gives you subpoena support if your primary request is obstructed. The Combine will obstruct. They always do. When they do, we go to the station magistrate, and we do it *boringly*, with citations, until it's more expensive for them to hide the logs than to hand them over."

"That's the least romantic plan I've ever heard."

"Bureaucracy is a weapon, girl. Both fleets built theirs to survive a war. You think they can survive a middle-aged woman with a filing system?" She tapped the flight-log request. "MF-7734-C. You think the android knows what it's carrying?"

"I think," Noor said slowly, "that Vesper said *check the flight logs* before it said anything else. Which means the logs matter. Which means maybe the carrier doesn't know. Which means whoever put the record aboard wanted it ignorant." She turned her notebook to the page of names. "And I think somebody owes these two thousand one hundred and six people a reason why."

The annex hummed around them, drawers full of dead wars, and above it all the station breathed its recycled air, and somewhere in that air, in some corridor or cargo bay, a decommissioned soldier was flying the Silence Run carrying the truth like a stone in its pocket, unaware.

Noor looked at the request status light on her terminal. Still pending. Still water.

"Come on," she whispered to it. "Sink or swim."

The light blinked amber. *Request acknowledged. Fulfillment pending Combine review.*

Pending. Of course it was pending. Everything worth having was pending.

But it was also — and this was the part that made her smile, tired and fierce, at the ceiling, at the drawers, at the whole quiet conspiracy of suppressed confessions and blank pages — *acknowledged*. Somewhere in the Combine's machinery, a clerk had read her boring little citation and forwarded it up a chain, and now a document existed that connected a station AI's impossible confession to a freighter's flight path, and documents, once created, were very hard to kill. She should know. Killing them was half her job.

She closed her notebook, patted Box 4471 like a stubborn animal that had finally led her somewhere, and went to bed for the first time in thirty hours.

The reply from the Meridian Freight Combine arrived while she slept: *Request denied. Records classified under commercial confidentiality.*

Noor read it over breakfast, unhurried, almost pleased.

"Obstructed," she said to her tea, "as predicted."

Which meant it was time to go find the magistrate — and, if the magistrate moved too slowly, time to consider less boring options. Because buried in the denial's boilerplate, in the standard legal language about the requesting party's lack of standing, was a single line she'd read three times to be sure:

*Asset MF-7734-C is currently en route and unavailable for inspection.*

En route. Right now. Carrying whatever it carried, through the dark between the fleets, toward Anvil Reach — and toward whatever waited for the truth when it got there.

Noor finished her tea, stood up, and began, quietly, to plan.
