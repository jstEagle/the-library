## 2. Children of the Deep

The quarantine clamps closed around the *Vespera* like a fist deciding to be gentle about it.

Odile Ferro stood on her own bridge — technically no longer hers, since the moment the Meridian Navy's tugboats had taken them under tow eleven hours ago — and watched the docking arms of Station Meridian Prime reach for her ship with the slow, inevitable confidence of something that had never once been told no.

"Captain," said Ensign Okonkwo from the helm, "they're requesting we power down all non-essential systems."

"Tell them we already have. Tell them our non-essential systems are very tired." She rubbed her eyes and felt, again, the wrongness of her own face under her hands. Smooth. No crow's feet. The scar above her left eyebrow — earned at Cassini, of all places, a shard of bulkhead glass she'd carried for thirty-one years — was gone. Not faded. *Gone*, as if the skin had been given a fresh start and decided not to repeat its mistakes.

Forty years out. Sixty days back. And in between, whatever had happened in that black stretch of nothing where their chronometers had run true but their cells had run backward, they had come home with bodies twenty years younger and memories forty years longer than any medical officer would be able to reconcile.

She'd done the arithmetic herself, alone, in the captain's cabin, three times. It always came out the same: they were owed two decades they hadn't lived, or they'd lived two decades they couldn't prove. Either way, the paperwork was going to be a nightmare.

"Ensign," she said, "how do I look?"

Okonkwo turned, and Odile watched him perform the same calculation every one of her crew performed now when they looked at each other: *I know your face, but not this version of it.* He was twenty-two when they'd shipped out; he was nineteen now, and it sat on him badly, like a uniform cut for someone else.

"You look," he said carefully, "like you're about to tell someone off, Captain."

"Good. That's accurate. Power down the non-essentials."

* * *

The medical boarding party came aboard an hour after hard dock, six of them in sealed whites, moving through her corridors with the delicacy of people entering a crime scene. Their lead was a Commander Vashti, whose badge said Public Health and whose posture said Intelligence, and who looked at Odile's face for exactly one second too long before consulting her tablet instead.

"Captain Ferro. Welcome home. You've been listed as lost with all hands for—" she checked, though Odile was certain she didn't need to check, "—thirty-nine years."

"And yet here I am, rude as ever."

Vashti did not smile. "Your crew will need to submit to full biometric workups. Standard repatriation protocol."

"Of course." Odile folded her hands. Under the table, her thumb found the seam of her jacket lining, just below the left ribs, where forty hours ago she'd sat with a needle and surgical thread and sewn shut the most important cargo the *Vespera* had ever carried. The data core was the size of a cigarette case, warm from her body heat, and it weighed — she was certain of this, though she knew it was impossible — more than the ship. "Standard protocol. Bloodwork, scans, the lot. My crew has nothing to hide."

It was the kind of sentence that only sounds confident if you've never hidden anything. Vashti's eyes flicked up from the tablet, and Odile smiled the smile she'd used on customs officers, creditors, and one memorable pirate magistrate in the Verge: open, guileless, faintly bored.

"We'll begin with you, then."

"Naturally. I insist. Though I'll warn you, my blood pressure has always made doctors weep."

* * *

The thing nobody tells you about being de-aged, Odile thought, lying on a scanner bed while machines hummed over her with increasing confusion, is how much of yourself you'd stored in your body without knowing. Her knees had stopped aching sometime during the return transit, and she'd caught herself missing the ache — the way you miss a dog that followed you everywhere. Her hands were a stranger's hands. She'd spent thirty years reading her crew's health in the backs of those hands, the liver spots, the tremor in Chief Marisol Vega's grip that meant she needed sleep, and now everyone's hands were smooth and lying to her.

They ran the scan four times. On the fourth pass, the technician — a young man whose name tag read OYELARAN and whose expression had progressed from professional neutrality to quiet theological crisis — excused himself to consult with Commander Vashti, and Odile lay alone in the bay and listened to him whisper through the door:

"—bone density of a twenty-year-old, telomeres like a newborn, but the cataract surgery scarring is still *there*, sir, the lens replacement is intact, it's just — the tissue around it grew *young around it*—"

She closed her eyes. Outside the viewport, Meridian Prime's lights wheeled slowly past, and somewhere beyond them, the war she'd sailed away from was paused rather than ended, held by an armistice thinner than the hull of a courier skiff.

When Vashti returned, she had a new expression, which was the old expression plus hunger.

"Captain Ferro, I'm going to ask you to describe your final transit."

"I'd love to," Odile said, sitting up. "We were in the deep dark past the Halloway Shelf, running silent because the Shelf eats signals for breakfast. Chronometers normal. Then there was — I want to say a day, maybe two, where everything felt thick. Like wading through cold honey. Tam Rhee swears the reactor sang to him. Tam sings back to reactors, so I wouldn't put much weight on his testimony. Then the thickness lifted, and we were sixty days further along than we should have been, and we were—" she gestured at herself, at all of it, "—this."

"You felt no pain?"

"No."

"No visual phenomena?"

"The honey. I told you about the honey."

"Captain." Vashti set the tablet down, and for a moment the intelligence officer fell away and something almost human looked out. "Sixty-two people have come back from deep-space losses in the history of the Meridian fleets. Fifty-eight of them came back dead. Four came back mad. You came back *younger*. Do you understand what that means? There are people on this station who will burn cities to get inside your cells."

Odile held her gaze. "Then it's fortunate," she said, "that my crew and I are extremely flammable."

* * *

They kept the crew together — she made sure of that, invoked some obscure clause about unit cohesion during medical evaluation that she was fairly sure she was inventing on the spot, but which the harried quarantine administrator let stand because it was easier than arguing with a woman who had died thirty-nine years ago and filed no complaint. They gave the *Vespera*'s people a ward: twelve bunks, a mess area, a viewport looking out onto the station's gray flank, and guards at both doors who tried very hard to look like orderlies.

Her crew filled the ward the way water fills a hull breach.

Marisol Vega claimed the bunk nearest the coffee dispenser and began, within the hour, a campaign of charm against the orderly staff that would, Odile estimated, result in contraband espresso by morning. Tam Rhee — nineteen years old, gangly, with the same cowlick he'd had at nineteen the first time, which Odile found privately hilarious — took apart the ward's entertainment console "just to see," reassembled it better than he'd found it, and got scolded for it by a guard who clearly wanted to laugh. Old hands in young faces. Forty years of shared jokes compressed into bodies that could finally, cruelly, *do* things again — Marisol did a handstand in the corridor just to prove she could, and then had to sit down because her inner ear remembered being fifty even if her spine didn't.

And through all of it, Odile watched the doors.

Because here was what she knew, what she'd known since the second day out from the dark, when she'd pulled the core from the long-range array housing and understood what was written on it — or rather, understood that it was *written*, that it was language, that it was addressed to someone: the government that had sent them out did not send rescue ships for lost crews. It sent them for salvage. And the most valuable salvage in the galaxy, right now, was the answer to the question *what happened to you* — unless it was the answer to the question *what did you bring back*.

The core was both answers. She intended for it to stay that way until she chose otherwise.

"Captain." Tam dropped into the seat across from her at the ward's little table, keeping his voice under the ambient noise. His hands were steady. His eyes weren't. "They scanned the ship today. Whole hull, bow to stern. Took apart my engine room."

"Did they find anything?"

"They found my engine room immaculate, which confused them deeply." A ghost of a grin. Then it faded. "Captain, if they take us apart the way they took apart the ship—"

"They won't."

"How do you know?"

Because I'm going to make sure of it, she thought. Because I have spent forty years learning exactly one skill, which is keeping people alive in places designed to kill them, and this ward is just another place. Aloud, she said: "Because I know how bureaucracies work. They can't dissect sixty veterans on live armistice television without paperwork. Paperwork takes time. Time is ours."

"That's not reassuring, Captain."

"It wasn't meant to be. It was meant to be *true*." She leaned forward. "Tam. Listen to me. Whatever they offer you — treatment, answers, your years back — you come to me first. All of you. We came out of the dark together. We decide what happens next together. Nobody gets isolated, nobody gets charmed, nobody gets scared into a private conversation. Are we clear?"

He nodded slowly. "You think they'll try that. Private conversations."

"I know they will. It's the oldest move there is. Find the one who's most afraid, and make him feel special." She reached across and tapped the back of his hand — young skin, old calluses, the calluses hadn't faded yet either, thank God for small mercies. "You're not special, Rhee. You're mine. Remember the difference."

Something in his shoulders unknotted. "Yes, Captain."

* * *

Night cycle, such as it was under quarantine lighting, and Odile lay in her bunk doing the thing she did every night now: inventorying the jacket.

It hung on the hook by the door, where a guard had examined it twice and found nothing, because the lining was sewn flat and the core sat against the fabric over her left hip, indistinguishable from padding. Every night she checked the stitches. Every night she pressed her palm against it and felt the faint warmth of the thing and thought: *witness.*

That was the word. She wasn't a linguist — she was a freighter captain with a talent for surviving — but even she could feel it, the way you feel weather coming. The core wasn't encrypted the way human files were encrypted, locked boxes waiting for keys. It was more like... a letter. Something patient. Something that had waited however many decades in the dark of the Halloway Shelf for a ship to come along, and had chosen hers, and had ridden home inside her array humming a frequency that made Tam's teeth ache and gave Marisol dreams of falling upward.

Whatever it said, it wasn't for her. She was just the courier.

The problem with couriers, she thought, staring at the ceiling, is that governments shoot them.

A soft knock. She was across the room before the second knock landed, jacket in hand — habit now, wearing it everywhere — and opened the door on Marisol, who slipped in holding two cups of genuinely terrible coffee.

"Contraband already?" Odile said. "I underestimated you."

"I bribed a corporal with stories about the Verge. He thinks I'm fascinating." Marisol handed her a cup and lowered herself onto the end of the bunk with a sigh that belonged to a seventy-year-old woman, whatever her spine said. For a while neither spoke. The station hummed. Somewhere down the corridor, a guard changed shift.

"Odile." Marisol rarely used her name. "The young doctor today. The one doing my intake. She asked me what year I thought it was."

"What did you tell her?"

"The truth. She wrote it down like I was delusional." Marisol turned the cup in her hands. "My daughter would be forty-three now. If she lived. I keep doing the math and it keeps making me sick. And then I look at my hands—" she held them up, smooth, brown, absurdly young, "—and I think, well, at least I could meet her grandchildren looking like this. Isn't that a horrible thing to think?"

"It's a human thing to think."

"It's a *horrible* thing to think." But she almost smiled. Then the smile went away. "There are men outside that door, Odile, who look at us and see spare parts. Young parts. I've been a spacer forty years. I know what a hold looks like from the inside."

Odile drank her terrible coffee and said nothing, because there was nothing to say that wasn't either a lie or a promise she might not keep.

What she thought was: *I have a letter in my jacket that could end the war or start a worse one, and I don't know which, and I am the only person standing between it and every government in the system.* What she said was: "Get some sleep, Marisol. Tomorrow they'll want more blood, and I want you insufferable for it."

* * *

On the fifth day of quarantine, the summons came.

Not for the whole crew — for her, alone, which was itself an answer to a question she'd been asking. An escort of two, polite as undertakers, walking her through corridors that got cleaner and quieter the deeper they went, until they arrived at a briefing room with a viewport full of stars and a single man standing at it with his hands clasped behind his back.

He was older than her, now, which was a strange sensation. Silver at the temples, a Ministry pin at the collar, and a voice she recognized before his face finished turning: Minister-Adjunct Corvane, whom she'd last seen thirty-nine years and one impossible lifetime ago, seeing off the *Vespera* at this same station with a speech about duty.

"Captain Ferro." He smiled, and it was a good smile, practiced on crowds. "Welcome back from the dead. We have so much to discuss."

Behind him, on the room's display wall, hung a file with her name on it — and beneath her name, a designation she'd only ever heard whispered about in the worst corners of the service:

**PRIORITY ASSET — RECOVERY AND STUDY.**

Odile Ferro smiled back, and beneath her jacket, against her ribs, the core seemed to grow warmer.

"Well," she said. "Let's discuss."
