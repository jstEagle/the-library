## 3. Conscription Papers

The supply run was three days late, which on Kestrel-9 meant either a bureaucratic hiccup or the universe had finally noticed her and decided to do something about it.

Lena spent the delay productively, which is to say she argued with the beacon.

"Not that phoneme," she told it, tapping the maintenance console with a stylus worn smooth as an old spoon. "You've been drifting on the fourth harmonic again. I know you think I won't notice. I always notice."

The beacon, being a forty-year-old relay array bolted to a rock at the ass-end of the Meridian corridor, did not answer. But its diagnostic readout flickered in a way she chose to interpret as contrition, and she logged the recalibration aloud in Old Meridian Standard, then again in Keshti trade-tongue, then — because the regulations technically required only one language and she had long ago decided that technical compliance was a form of self-respect — in the clicking, tide-pulled grammar of the deep-water dialects of Thessaly-Prime, where nobody lived anymore but where somebody, once, had sung.

Her voice filled the station the way it always did: too big for the rooms. Eleven years on Kestrel-9 and she still hadn't learned to speak quietly. There was no one to be quiet for.

She was rehydrating something labeled STEW, ASSORTED when the proximity alarm sang — not the supply ship's polite two-tone chime but the full-throated klaxon reserved for unscheduled arrivals, which in eleven years had happened exactly twice, once for a meteor and once for a smuggler who'd stayed just long enough to ask directions and steal her coffee.

Lena stood very still in the galley, spoon halfway to her mouth, and watched the approach vector resolve on the wall display.

It was a Ministry cutter. Matte grey, government-issue, running lights in the austere configuration that said *we are here officially and would prefer you didn't enjoy it*.

"Well," she said to the stew. "Either they've come to arrest me or promote me."

She genuinely could not have told you which prospect frightened her more.

* * *

The cutter set down on Landing Pad One — the only landing pad — with the delicacy of a cat that has been assured it is welcome and intends to test that assurance thoroughly. Lena met it outside, because meeting people inside meant letting them into her home, and eleven years of solitude had hardened into her a single unbreakable rule: *the station is mine, the pad is neutral ground.*

The airlock cycled. A man descended the ramp.

He was not what she expected, though she couldn't have said what she had expected. Not a soldier, certainly. He wore civilian clothes cut with military precision — a charcoal coat, no insignia, gloves he did not remove despite the cold that could crack exposed skin in twenty minutes. Middle-aged, silvering at the temples, with the kind of face designed by committee to be forgettable and failing only slightly. His breath fogged. He smiled anyway, which struck Lena as either bravery or training.

"Dr. Quintana." His voice carried across the pad without effort. "Minister-Adjunct Corvane, Office of Linguistic Affairs." He produced credentials from his coat with the smoothness of a card trick. "I apologize for the irregular arrival. Your supply vessel was delayed at our request."

"Our request," Lena repeated. "Whose *our*?"

"The Ministry's." He tucked the credentials away. "May we speak inside? It's minus thirty-one degrees and my coat is rated for minus twenty-five, which tells you how confident I was about your hospitality."

Something in her — the part that had been alone so long it had begun to mistake loneliness for a personality — wanted to say yes just to hear another voice in her galley. The rest of her wanted to say no and watch him freeze politely on her pad like an expensive lawn ornament.

"You can speak out here," she said. "I'll keep it short."

A flicker crossed his face — amusement, maybe, or respect. "Very well. Dr. Quintana, you are hereby conscripted, under Emergency Provision 12 of the Armistice Accords, into temporary service of the Ministry as a specialist consultant. Your assignment is the decryption and interpretation of materials recovered by the salvage vessel *Vespera*, currently under quarantine at Meridian Station."

The words landed one at a time, like stones dropped down a well.

"I'm a beacon keeper," she said. "I file maintenance reports. In several languages, admittedly, but—"

"You are also the author of fourteen published papers on non-human signal structure, including the foundational taxonomy of recursive alien syntax that every xenolinguist under forty was trained on." He recited it without checking notes. "You are additionally the subject of—" he paused, and something in the pause made her stomach drop before he even continued, "—a certain amount of internal correspondence."

"Internal correspondence."

"Dr. Quintana." He exhaled, and the fog of it hung between them like a small ghost. "Three days ago a ship came back from deep space carrying a message that has defeated every decryption system the Ministry owns. Its crew came back biologically younger than the voyage permits. And the message's underlying structure matches, point for point, the anomalous signal patterns documented in your dreams."

The cold stopped mattering. Lena became aware of it only distantly, the way you become aware of your own heartbeat when someone says your name in a crowded room.

"I never reported my dreams," she said quietly.

"No," Corvane agreed. "You didn't. That's rather the point."

* * *

He talked her inside eventually — not with force, but with the terrible patience of a man who had all the time in the galaxy and knew it. She made tea because her hands needed something to do that wasn't shaking. He sat in her galley, in the chair that had been empty for eleven years, and declined sugar with a small apologetic gesture, and looked around at her walls of dream-journals and hand-labeled language charts with an expression she couldn't classify. Hunger, maybe. Or inventory.

"Show me the orders," she said. "The actual paper. Provision 12 requires written instrument, witnessed."

"You know the Accords better than most judges."

"I know the Accords better than *all* judges. They were written by linguists. Bad ones."

He passed a folio across the table. Official seal, official ribbon, official everything. Her name spelled correctly, which unsettled her more than any typo would have. She read the operative clause twice, looking for the trap, and found instead something worse: precision. Whoever drafted this had known exactly who she was.

"Why me?" she asked. "There are dozens of working xenolinguists. Hundreds. You have entire departments—"

"And every one of them approaches unknown languages as puzzles to be solved." Corvane turned his teacup slowly on its saucer, a quarter-turn at a time, like a man dialing a safe. "You're the only one on record who argues they should be treated as *speakers*. 'A signal isn't encrypted,' you wrote in 3141. 'Encryption hides meaning. Language *is* meaning, waiting.' The Ministry has spent two weeks throwing cryptographers at this cargo and gotten nothing. Because it isn't locked, Dr. Quintana. It's talking. And none of them know how to listen."

Lena set down her cup. Her hands were steady now, which surprised her; she'd have bet on shaking.

"That quote wasn't published," she said. "That was from my grant renewal appeal. Which was denied."

"Yes."

"So you've been reading my denied paperwork."

"We've been reading everything." He said it without apology, and that was somehow the most honest thing he'd said yet. Then he paused, and she watched him make a decision — watched it happen behind his eyes like weather changing. "In the interest of efficiency, since you'll see it within the hour regardless: there's a file."

"A file."

"On you. Maintained continuously since your university years. Flagged status: *passive asset, monitor, do not engage.*" He slid a second folio from his coat — thinner, worn at the corners, tabbed with colored markers that had faded unevenly, the way things fade when they're handled over many years by many hands. "You asked why you. The honest answer is that you were never forgotten, Dr. Quintana. You were stored."

She didn't want to open it. She opened it.

* * *

The first page was dated nineteen years ago. A student evaluation, hers, annotated in red ink by someone whose job it apparently was to notice that a graduate student named Lena Quintana had scored in the ninety-ninth percentile on pattern-recognition aptitude and had, per a footnote, "reported recurring auditory phenomena during sleep, subject dismisses as stress-related. Recommend follow-up."

There had been no follow-up. There had been a flag.

Page after page. Her first posting. Her papers, each one summarized in flat bureaucratic prose that somehow conveyed both total comprehension and total absence of feeling. Her transfer requests — denied, each one, and beside each denial a note in the same unhurried hand: *retain at current position.* Her application for the Meridian corridor survey, the one she'd dreamed about for a year, the one that would have taken her toward the signal's source: denied, with the annotation *asset value maximized at remote listening posts.*

Remote listening posts. Kestrel-9. Eleven years of wind and stew and a beacon that drifted on its harmonics, and all of it — every lonely rotation, every denied transfer, every night she'd woken with alien syllables dissolving off her tongue like snow — catalogued, cross-referenced, and filed under a heading she now read with her own name above it:

**QUINTANA, L. — ANOMALOUS RECEPTION CAPABILITY. STATUS: STORED.**

"They watched me dream," she said. Her voice came out level, which felt like a betrayal of everything happening inside her chest. "For nineteen years. They watched me dream and they wrote it down and they left me on a rock."

Corvane said nothing. He was watching her with that committee-built face carefully arranged into neutrality, and she realized suddenly that he was letting her be angry on purpose — that a calm witness was part of the procedure, that somewhere in some manual there was probably a section titled *Managing Asset Distress During Conscription Interviews.*

"Say something," she said.

"What would you like me to say?" He folded his gloved hands on the table. "That it was wrong? It may have been. That it was necessary? It may have been that too. I inherited this file four months ago along with an office and a mandate, and the first thing I did was read it cover to cover, and the second thing I did was wonder what kind of institution stores a human being like a spare part and then congratulates itself on its foresight." He turned the teacup another quarter-turn. "But I'll tell you what I told myself, Dr. Quintana, when I signed the order bringing you in. Whatever they intended by keeping you — whatever cold arithmetic justified those denials — the fact remains that you are the only person alive who has been rehearsing this language for nineteen years without knowing it. They stored you. Fine. But storage, it turns out, was preparation. You weren't forgotten. You were *kept*. And now whatever the *Vespera* brought back needs exactly what you are."

Lena looked down at the file. At the faded tabs. At nineteen years of her life rendered in other people's handwriting, and beneath it all, threaded through every page like a wire through wax, the thing she had never told anyone: that the dreams had changed pitch last week. That the signal she'd carried silently for two decades had shifted, subtly but unmistakably, the way a singer shifts key before the final verse — and that she had lain awake in the dark telling herself it was nothing, the way you tell yourself a cough is nothing, the way you tell yourself the supply ship is merely late.

"It's not a puzzle," she heard herself say. "Whatever's on that core. It's not a cipher. It's been *talking to me* for years and I never answered because I didn't know the conversation had started."

"Then you understand better than anyone why we need you." Corvane rose, smoothing his coat, businesslike again, the moment of honesty sealed back up like a letter. "Transport departs at 0600. Pack light. The quarantine facility at Meridian Station is comfortable enough, if you don't mind guards."

"Guards."

"For the cargo's protection," he said, and then, after a beat, with the faint dryness of a man who had decided she'd earned one true sentence: "And everyone else's."

At the airlock he paused, one hand on the frame, and glanced back at her walls — the journals, the charts, the eleven years of a life built small on purpose.

"For what it's worth," he said, "the file notes that you sing while you calibrate. In languages nobody speaks. The observers found it — and I'm quoting directly — '*anomalous but not threatening.*'" Something moved at the corner of his mouth, almost a smile. "I look forward to hearing whether the Ministry's assessment holds."

The airlock cycled shut behind him.

Lena stood alone in her galley, in the sudden enormous silence, surrounded by nineteen years of evidence that she had never been alone at all — only archived. Slowly, mechanically, she began pulling her dream-journals from their shelf, all forty-three of them, and packing them into a crate, and if her hands shook now, there was no one on Kestrel-9 to see it.

On the last page of the last journal, dated six days ago, she had written a single line in the dark, half-asleep, in a script that was only partly hers:

*They're almost done saying it. Someone has to be ready to answer.*

She closed the crate. Outside, the Ministry cutter's engines began their low, patient warming, like something vast clearing its throat.

At 0600, Lena Quintana left the only home she had left — and flew toward the de-aged strangers of the *Vespera*, carrying the one thing the Ministry's file had never managed to catalogue: the answer, forming word by word, in her sleep.
