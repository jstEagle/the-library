## 7. Loyalty Audit

The interrogation room on Meridian Station had been designed by someone who hated chairs.

Tam Rhee had spent forty years — or nineteen years, depending on which part of him you asked — fixing things that other people broke, and he recognized bad engineering when he sat in it. The chair was bolted at an angle that made his lower back file complaints within ten minutes. The table was too high. The light came from everywhere and nowhere, the kind of diffuse ceiling glow that left no shadows to hide in and no reflections to check your own face in. He'd have docked whoever built it a week's pay and made them sit in it for a shift.

He'd been sitting in it for three hours.

"Mr. Rhee." The woman across from him hadn't introduced herself in the first hour, and when she finally did, it was a rank without a name: *Ministry Auditor, Second Class*. She had the kind of face that would be forgettable in any crowd of more than four people, which Tam suspected was a professional qualification. "Let's go back to the transit logs again."

"We've done the transit logs twice."

"Third time's the charm, as my grandmother used to say."

"Your grandmother served on a deep-salvage rig?"

"She worked in customs." The auditor smiled. It was a good smile, calibrated, the kind you practiced against a mirror until it stopped meaning anything. "Everyone's grandmother worked somewhere useful, Mr. Rhee. That's rather the point of grandmothers."

Tam decided he disliked her enormously, and then corrected himself: he disliked her professionally. Personally she might be perfectly pleasant at a dinner party, provided nobody asked her about her work. This distinction mattered. Odile had taught him that. *You can hate what a man does all day long,* she said once, *and still share a bottle with him at night, if you keep the two ledgers separate.* Forty years of salvage contracts with people who'd cheerfully cheat you taught a crew to keep two ledgers.

"Transit logs," he said. "Vespera departed Meridian, standard survey loop, eleven months out. We went further than the loop because we found something worth going further for. That's the whole story. I've told it twice."

"And the something worth going further for?"

"Salvage," Tam said. "It's in the name of the ship. You may have noticed."

* * *

Here was the thing they didn't understand yet, the thing Tam kept carefully folded up small inside himself like a tool wrapped in cloth so it wouldn't rattle:

They thought they were interrogating a man who'd lost thirty years.

They were actually interrogating a man who'd lived forty-one of them, and whose body had been rewound to match a nineteen-year-old's, and who had spent every one of those forty-one years learning exactly how much pressure a person could take before something bent.

The de-aging had happened out past the Meridian Verge, in the shadow of a phenomenon the crew had privately named the Long Shallows — a region where space did things to time that the physics manuals described in footnotes and apologias. They'd come through it changed. Younger. Cells reset, scars smoothed away, old knee injuries healed over into knees that had never been injured. Tam had woken up in the med bay with the face from his first enlistment photo and hands that didn't ache in cold weather, and he'd cried about it for exactly one evening, alone in the head, and then never again.

Because here was the other thing: his memories hadn't shrunk to fit. He remembered all of it. The wedding he'd botched. The daughter he'd seen four times in twenty years. The thirty years of hauls and breakdowns and quiet watches beside Odile Ferro while the ship hummed around them like a sleeping animal. All of it lived in a head attached to a body that had never done any of it.

The Ministry doctors called it *temporal-biological desynchronization* and said they'd never seen anything like it outside theoretical papers. Tam called it being haunted by your own future, and mostly tried not to think about it, except now he was being asked to think about it constantly, by people with clipboards.

"Restorative treatment," the auditor said, sliding a slate across the too-high table. "I want to be clear that this is a genuine offer, Mr. Rhee. Not leverage. An offer."

Tam looked at the slate. On it was a medical summary written in the cheerful language of brochures: *full chronological realignment protocol*, neural mapping to restore the body's age-state to match the mind's, recovery time six weeks, success rate — and here they'd gotten honest — *variable*.

"Variable," Tam said aloud.

"Meaning what it means. The procedure is new. Your condition is unprecedented. There are risks." She folded her hands. "But there is also a version of your life where you get those thirty years back. Where your body catches up to your memory. Where you walk out of a clinic at fifty-two instead of walking around at nineteen pretending."

"I'm not pretending," Tam said. "I'm nineteen extremely well."

Something flickered across her face — amusement, quickly suppressed, which he chose to take as a point in her favor. Points in her favor were dangerous. He'd learned that in negotiations with port authorities: the moment you start liking them, you've already given something away.

"The offer stands regardless of your answers today," she said. "That's important to me. I don't want you thinking this is conditional."

"It's conditional," Tam said. "Everything's conditional. I've read enough contracts to know there's no such thing as an unconditional offer from an institution. Somewhere in the fine print there's a clause, and the clause has your name on it, and the name isn't yours."

The auditor regarded him for a long moment. Then, unexpectedly, she laughed — a short, real laugh, quickly strangled. "You're exactly what your service record says you are."

"What does it say?"

"*Obstinate. Thorough. Loyal to a fault.*" She tapped the table. "We're here to find the fault, Mr. Rhee."

* * *

They gave him a room after that, not a cell — a distinction the Ministry clearly considered generous and Tam considered architectural. A bunk, a washbasin, a window looking out onto the station's docking arm where the Vespera sat impounded under quarantine seals, floodlights on her hull like an accusation.

He could see her from the window. Her port-side thruster housing, the one he'd rebuilt twice, caught the light. Somebody else was maintaining her now. Ministry techs swarmed her decks in gray coveralls, running diagnostics on systems they didn't understand, and every time one of them opened a panel wrong — and they were all opening panels wrong; he could tell from the way the exterior lights cycled — something in Tam's chest pulled tight, like watching a stranger drive a stick shift.

His door wasn't locked. He tested it the first night, purely as an engineer, and found it swung open freely onto a corridor with two bored guards at the end. The message was elegant: *you could leave, but where would you go?* The whole station was a cell with better lighting.

On the third day they brought in a second interrogator, a man this time, warmer, avuncular, with a cardigan under his uniform jacket like a man who wanted you to know he had a family somewhere. He brought tea. Actual tea, decent tea, not the station swill, and he poured two cups and pushed one across the table and said, "Mr. Rhee, let's stop wasting each other's time."

"I'd love that," Tam said, and drank the tea, because it was good tea and he refused to make a point at his own expense.

"Captain Ferro is going to be charged. That's not a threat, it's arithmetic. A ship returns from a restricted region biologically altered, carrying an unregistered data core sewn into civilian clothing — you knew about the core, Mr. Rhee, please don't insult us both — and the captain of record claims she doesn't remember acquiring it. Charges will be filed. The only question is their severity. And severity, in cases like this, has historically correlated very strongly with cooperation."

Tam set down his cup. "You want her intent."

"We want to understand her intent."

"You want her intent so you can predict her, and you want to predict her so you can contain her, and you're asking me to hand you the map to a woman who pulled me out of a decompression event with her bare hands when I was twenty-six." He paused. "Or am. The tenses are complicated these days."

The warm man sighed, and for just a moment the cardigan fell away and Tam saw the auditor underneath, tired and institutional. "You love her."

"That's not a question."

"No. But it's the answer to most of mine, isn't it?" The man gathered his slates. "Think about the offer, Mr. Rhee. Thirty years. Think about your daughter."

And that was the low blow, and it landed, and Tam sat alone in the room afterward feeling it land, feeling it keep landing, ripple after ripple. His daughter, Ilse. Fifty-one now, if the math held. Older than him. Grandchildren he'd never met, children of a man who looked like a cadet. The restorative treatment wouldn't fix that — couldn't fix that, no procedure on any world could rewind the calendar — but it would let him stand next to her at some future wedding without strangers assuming she was his mother.

He thought about that for a long time, sitting in the bad chair, in the shadowless light.

Then he thought about Odile, in her holding ward two decks up, refusing to answer questions, refusing food that wasn't shared with her crew, sewing — he'd bet the ship on it — some new contingency into the lining of whatever garment they'd let her keep. He thought about the way she'd looked at all of them in the med bay after the Shallows, young faces in an old captain's eyes, and said, *Well. Nobody panic. Panic is for people with worse posture.*

He thought about the ledger.

* * *

On the fifth day, they changed tactics, which meant he was winning.

They moved him to a different room — one with a comfortable chair, which somehow frightened him more than the bad one — and the nameless auditor came back, alone, and sat down across from him without any slates at all.

"Tell me about the signal," she said.

Tam kept his face still. It cost him something. "What signal?"

"The one the xenolinguist keeps dreaming about. The one your data core is addressed to." She watched him with something almost like kindness. "Mr. Rhee, we've known about Lena Quintana for eleven years. We flagged her dreams before you ever left drydock. Do you understand? The core isn't a mystery to us. The linguist isn't a mystery to us. What's a mystery to us is why Captain Ferro risked everything to bring it back instead of selling it, and why her engineer — a man with a documented grievance against the universe — hasn't given us a single reason to go easy on him."

"Grievance against the universe," Tam repeated. "Is that in my file?"

"Metaphorically."

"Well, put it in literally. And add: grievance currently under negotiation." He leaned forward. "You want to know what Odile intends. Fine. Here's what I know, and I'll swear it under whatever oath you like. She intends to bring her crew home. Every one of us, alive, whole, and free. That's the entire manifesto. It's been the entire manifesto for forty years. Everything else is navigation."

"And the core?"

"Is cargo," Tam said. "And I'm the engineer. I don't ask cargo its opinions."

The auditor studied him for a while. Outside the window behind her, a supply shuttle crossed the docking arm, and beyond it the Vespera sat in her cage of floodlights, patient as a held breath.

"You're stalling," she said finally, without heat. "You've been stalling beautifully for five days, and I want you to know I respect the craftsmanship. But the treatment offer expires Friday, Mr. Rhee. Institutions are institutions. Even the good clauses have dates."

"Friday," Tam said. "Noted. I'll put it in the log."

She left. He stayed in the comfortable chair, which was worse than the bad one, because the bad one at least kept him angry, and anger was load-bearing.

* * *

That night he stood at the window a long time, looking at his ship.

Thirty years. He turned the number over the way he'd turn a corroded fitting, checking the threads. Thirty years of aches and arthritis and the slow betrayal of cartilage, erased. Thirty years of watching people he loved die on schedule, restored to him as open road. A body that matched the man inside it. He was not a saint. He wanted it. God help him, he wanted it the way a drowning man wants air — not nobly, not philosophically, just completely.

And underneath the wanting, steady as a keel, was the other thing. The thing Odile had never once said out loud in forty years but had demonstrated daily, in the grammar of shared watches and split rations and *nobody panic*: that a crew is a promise you make with your life, and that the promise doesn't have an exit clause, and that the people who look for one were never really crew.

Ilse would understand. That was the terrible, quiet truth of it. Ilse, who'd grown up waiting for a father the sea kept, had learned early that love and presence were different currencies, and had forgiven him in the only way that mattered, by building a life that didn't require him. She'd forgive this too. She'd probably say something dry about it. *Dad finally commits to something, and it's a jailbreak.*

He smiled at the window, at his reflection ghosted over the floodlit hull, a nineteen-year-old's face wearing forty years of expression.

Friday, then. Let Friday come.

He was still smiling when the corridor lights changed — shifted from white to amber, the station-wide tone that meant *movement restriction, all personnel* — and boots started hammering somewhere below, many boots, moving fast, and a klaxon began its low, rising note that Tam had heard maybe four times in his career and never once for anything good.

Down the corridor, one of the guards swore. Through the window, Tam watched a fire-suppression foam plume bloom from the far end of the docking arm — not from the Vespera, but from the relay pylon beyond her, the thin spire of Kestrel-9's feeder array, where the lights had just gone dark.

Where, if Tam's instincts were right — and his instincts had forty years of seniority — somebody had just decided that certain questions were easier to bury than to answer.

He pressed his palm flat against the glass, toward his ship, toward the smoke, and said, to nobody, in the voice of a much older man:

"Hold on, Captain. We're coming."
