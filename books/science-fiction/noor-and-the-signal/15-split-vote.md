## 15. Split Vote

The Vespera's galley had never been designed for democracy. It had been designed for six people to eat reconstituted stew without elbowing each other, and even that had been optimistic. Now it held the entire crew plus Lena Quintana plus a holographic projection of a dead man, and Tam Rhee stood at the back with his arms crossed, watching his family come apart at the seams.

"Say it again," Odile said. She was sitting on the counter because all the chairs were taken, her boots hooked on a drawer handle, her voice level in the way Tam had learned — over forty years of shared air — meant she was furious. "Slowly. For those of us who'd like to make sure we heard it right."

Minister-Adjunct Corvane's ghost flickered in the middle of the room, blue around the edges where the projector was losing its argument with the ship's aging power grid. He looked exactly as he had in the holding ward: gray at the temples, immaculate despite everything, wearing death like it was a well-tailored coat.

"The fleet codes are genuine," Corvane said. "Kestrel-9's backup array is still live under military encryption. Without my codes, you cannot broadcast from it. With them, you can. That's the whole offer."

"And the catch," Odile said.

"You let me control the broadcast."

The silence that followed had texture. Tam could feel it against his skin like static before a storm.

It was Sefa who broke it, because Sefa always broke things first — it was why they loved him. The pilot pushed back from the table hard enough to rattle the stew bowls. "Absolutely not. We did not run a cordon, lose Jaya's arm—" he gestured at Jaya, who raised the prosthetic in question and waggled the fingers, "—and bleed half our plasma supply so some Ministry suit could stand on the corpse of our evidence and take a bow."

"I'm not asking for a bow," Corvane said mildly.

"You're asking for the microphone."

"I'm asking for the *infrastructure*." Corvane turned, and the hologram's eyes found Tam across the room, which was a trick, because the projector didn't track anyone. It just happened to be facing him. It kept happening to be facing him. "The pirate relay option has been raised. I'd like to address it honestly."

"Oh, this'll be good," Sefa muttered.

"The Meridian Free Wave operates out of a converted ore hauler called the *Kindly Anvil*. Its transmitter is rated for maybe forty thousand kilometers of clean signal. Civilian channels in the inner system will pick up fragments. Fragments," Corvane repeated, "can be dismissed. Dismissed fragments protect the people who staged Cassini Station. A full broadcast through an authorized beacon cannot be dismissed, because it comes stamped with the authority of the very institution it indicts. You don't want volume. You want *legitimacy*."

"He's right," Lena said quietly.

Everyone looked at her. She was sitting in the corner with her dream-journals stacked beside her like sandbags, a mug of tea going cold in her hands. She looked, Tam thought, like someone who hadn't slept properly in eleven years and had recently stopped pretending otherwise.

"The archive archivists said the same thing," she went on. "Authentication isn't enough. Anyone can authenticate anything these days; that's the problem. It needs to come through a channel both governments can't claim is forged. Kestrel-9 is the only beacon left that qualifies." Her thumb moved over the rim of her mug, a small nervous orbit. "But that doesn't mean we hand him the wheel."

"No," Odile agreed. "It doesn't."

Corvane's image shimmered. "Captain Ferro. I died — publicly, expensively — saving your linguist from a sabotage team sent by my own ministry. Whatever you believe about my motives, understand that my career, my survival, and my entire remaining purpose now depend on this broadcast happening and being *undeniable*. I am the most motivated man in this system. Motivated men make reliable allies."

"Motivated men make excellent liars," Odile said. "I've been one."

Tam almost smiled. Almost.

* * *

They argued for two more hours.

Tam mostly listened. This was his role in the crew's ecosystem — not the quiet one, exactly, because he could talk the paint off a bulkhead when engineering came up, but the weighing one. When the Vespera's people needed to know if a thing would hold, they asked Tam. Right now the thing under stress was the crew itself, and he could hear the metal fatigue in every voice.

Sefa wanted the pirate relay. So did Jaya, though more quietly — Jaya trusted institutions the way she trusted gravity: as a force that had failed her before and probably would again. Priya, their medic, was torn, worried aloud about what a partial broadcast would do to the armistice — "a rumor of atrocity travels faster than proof, and rumors start wars" — which surprised no one who'd watched her triage decisions. Odile sat on the counter and absorbed everyone's positions like a hull absorbing micrometeorites, giving away nothing.

And Lena — Lena kept looking at Tam.

Not staring. Just checking. Every time the argument swung toward the pirate relay, her eyes drifted to him the way a helmsman checks a gauge. He realized, somewhere in the second hour, that she was waiting for him to say something, and he couldn't figure out why, and that bothered him more than the argument itself.

Finally Odile called it. "We vote. Everyone speaks, everyone counts, including our guest. Majority decides, and nobody sulks — Sefa."

"I never sulk."

"You invented sulking. You have a plaque."

They went around the table. Sefa: pirate relay, delivered with a speech about freedom that lasted four minutes and contained the phrase "the sky belongs to everybody" twice. Jaya: pirate relay, two words, "trust nothing," which Tam noted was technically three. Priya: abstained, visibly hating herself. Lena: Corvane's codes, reluctantly, "because a whisper won't save anyone, and I've spent ten years learning how big this message wants to be." Odile voted last among the crew, and when she did, she said only: "Codes. Under conditions we haven't negotiated yet." Which meant she'd already decided and was letting the room pretend otherwise, which was captaincy in a nutshell.

Four votes. Two and a fraction. It came down to Tam.

He felt the weight of it settle on his shoulders like a toolbelt full of lead.

* * *

Afterward — after the vote, after the groans and the shrugs and Sefa's theatrical departure to go sulk professionally in the cockpit — Odile caught him in the corridor outside the galley.

"A word, Mr. Rhee."

"Captain."

She leaned against the bulkhead. In the corridor's thin light she looked tired in a way that had nothing to do with her de-aged body. Her face was thirty years old now. Her exhaustion was older than that.

"You voted for Corvane," she said.

"You were winning anyway."

"That's not an answer."

Tam blew out a breath. Down the corridor, the ship hummed its old familiar hum, the sound he'd fallen asleep to for four decades, the sound his nineteen-year-old ears had relearned to love. "You want the real answer?"

"I always want the real answer. It's my worst quality."

"The real answer is that I met Corvane alone, in the holding ward, three days before the station burned." He watched her face carefully. "He offered me something. Restorative treatment. My years back. All I had to do was report on your intentions regarding the core."

Odile didn't flinch. He'd known she wouldn't. "And what did you tell him?"

"That you intended to do the right thing, whatever that turned out to be. Which was true, and useless, and he knew it." Tam rubbed the back of his neck. "I told you about the interrogation during the long silence. You gave me the core to guard afterward. I figured the ledger was balanced."

"It was balanced. Then you voted for him today, and I'm trying to decide whether that's balance or debt."

"Neither." He made himself hold her eyes. "It's arithmetic, Captain. Bad kind. The pirate relay gives us maybe a forty percent chance the truth reaches enough people to matter, and a ninety percent chance we die making the attempt, and a hundred percent chance that if we fail, Cassini stays a lie forever and the war starts back up in five years with better propaganda. Corvane's codes give us a real transmitter, a legitimate channel, and a man whose self-interest happens to point in our direction for once." He shrugged. "I'm an engineer. I don't fall in love with components. I ask what they do, and whether they do it reliably, and what happens when they fail."

"And if he fails?"

"Then I'm standing next to the core when he tries, and he knows it, and I made sure he knows it." Tam smiled thinly. "Trust isn't a feeling, Captain. It's a load-bearing calculation. You check the tolerances, you add redundancy, and you build the thing anyway. Sentiment is what you feel *after* it holds."

Odile studied him for a long moment. Then, unexpectedly, she laughed — short, rough, real. "Forty years ago you'd have voted with your heart."

"Forty years ago I *had* a heart. Now I have a nineteen-year-old's heart in a forty-year-old's head, and frankly the combination is insufferable." He pushed off the wall. "Get your conditions from him. Hard ones. Broadcast unedited or we walk. And Captain — put me on the detail that guards him during transmission. Not because I don't trust him."

"Because you don't."

"Because trust is a calculation," Tam said, "and I like to be the redundancy."

* * *

He found Lena on the observation deck an hour later, which was not a coincidence, because he'd checked the galley first, then the med bay, then the small cluttered space she'd claimed as a workspace, following the trail of cold tea mugs the way other people followed breadcrumbs.

She was pressed against the viewport, watching the minefield drift past at a respectful distance — the shattered hulks of it, the slow-turning wreckage of a war neither side would officially admit to mining. Her reflection looked ghostly against the stars.

"You were watching me in there," Tam said. "During the debate. Waiting for me to say something."

Lena didn't turn around. "You recognized my voice once. Did I ever tell you that? Thirty years ago — well. From your perspective, thirty years ago. You were an engineer on a relay station and you heard a xenolinguist reading maintenance logs in a language that didn't exist yet, and you remembered it." Now she turned. Her eyes were shadowed but steady. "You told me it was the first time in years the signal sounded like it was going somewhere. Like someone was building toward a sentence."

"I remember."

"So did I. Eventually. It took me a decade to understand that the sentence was testimony, and that I was the only mouth it had." She hugged her elbows. "When Corvane made his offer, everyone in that room was arguing about risk. Risk to the crew, risk to the mission, risk to themselves. And I kept thinking — there's one person on this ship who understands what it costs to be out of sync with your own life. One person who got his future stolen and chose, today, to bet the getting-it-back on a man who tried to buy him. I wanted to hear how you'd do that math. Because I'm going to have to do it too, soon. At the array. When he asks me for something."

"What makes you think he'll ask you for something?"

"Everyone asks the translator for something," Lena said simply. "That's what translators are *for*."

Tam leaned on the rail beside her. Outside, a derelict gunship tumbled end over end through the field, its hull scarred with the markings of a navy that no longer admitted owning it. Somewhere behind them, both fleets were hunting. Somewhere ahead, a ruined beacon waited with a dead man's codes and a live man's ambition.

"For what it's worth," Tam said, "the math worked out today. Probably. Ask me again when we're not inside a minefield."

Lena laughed — a small, startled sound, like she'd forgotten the machinery for it. "That's not reassuring, Engineer."

"Nothing on this ship is reassuring. That's why we keep each other." He nodded at the viewport, at the wreck-strewn dark between them and Kestrel-9. "Get some sleep, Dr. Quintana. If the signal's going to use your mouth, the least you can do is arrive rested."

She started to answer — and stopped. Went very still.

Tam knew that stillness. He'd seen it on her twice before, both times in the hours before she translated something terrible.

"Lena?"

Her voice came out flat and far away, reciting rather than speaking. "*'The witness arrives at the broken tower. The tower remembers its purpose.'*" She blinked, and the distance fell out of her eyes, and she looked at him with something close to apology. "It's been quiet for weeks. Since Halcyon Reach. I thought — I hoped it was finished with me."

"And now?"

"Now," Lena said, turning back to the viewport, to the dark, to the broken tower waiting somewhere inside it, "it's counting down."
