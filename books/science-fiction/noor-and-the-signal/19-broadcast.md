## 19. Broadcast

The array was dying the way old things die — not all at once, but in stutters, in coughs of static, in the smell of hot insulation that had no business smelling like anything except forty years of patient waiting.

Lena stood at the base of Kestrel-9's backup transmitter and put her palm flat against its housing. It was warm. It had never been warm before. Somewhere inside it, capacitors were giving everything they had left, and she found herself thinking, absurdly, *good boy. Hold on.*

"Talk to me, beacon," she murmured. "You've been talking to me my whole life. Return the favor."

Behind her, boots on grating. Tam Rhee came around the corner of the relay housing with the data core hugged against his chest like a football, which was how he'd carried it for six hours, through a firefight, down a maintenance shaft, and up a ladder that had tried to kill him twice. He was nineteen years old and moved like a man who'd buried friends, because he was.

"Corvane's people are pinned in the lower bay," he reported. "Odile says we have maybe twenty minutes before the fleet gets here, and she says it like twenty minutes is a generous gift from a rich aunt."

"Is it?"

"No. But Odile says if you're going to do the thing, do it now."

"The thing," Lena said. "Yes. The thing."

She had rehearsed this moment for ten years without knowing it. Every night, in her bunk on Kestrel-9, in her dreams, in the grammar that had colonized her sleep one syllable at a time — she had been rehearsing. The signal had spent a decade teaching her a language, and she had spent a decade resenting it, and now, standing in a ruined station with two navies converging and the air tasting of burnt copper, she understood: it hadn't been teaching her to translate. It had been teaching her to *speak*. There was a difference. Translation was a bridge between shores. Speaking was standing on your own shore and refusing to be quiet.

Tam set the core into the cradle they'd rigged from salvage and prayer. Its indicator light pulsed — slow, patient, the rhythm she now recognized as the alien equivalent of a held breath.

"Array status?" she asked.

"Honest answer or morale answer?"

"Morale answer."

"We're fine."

"And honest?"

"It'll hold long enough," Tam said. "Probably. Odile rerouted power from everything that isn't you. We have no gravity plating in the east wing, no comms except us, and the tea machine is dead, which I know because I asked about the tea machine first and got yelled at." He paused. "Also there are approximately four hundred warships entering the system, split evenly between governments who both want us very dead or very quiet."

"So a normal Tuesday at Kestrel-9."

"You really did live out here alone too long."

"I did," Lena said, and something in her chest went soft and strange, because it wasn't true anymore, and hadn't been since a supply run brought news of an impossible ship. She looked at Tam — nineteen, loyal, carrying decades he hadn't earned in a body that hadn't earned them either — and felt the kinship she'd felt the first day in the holding ward, when he'd said *I know your voice* and meant it across thirty impossible years. Two people out of sync with time, keeping each other company anyway.

"Go tell Odile I'm ready," she said.

"She wants to be here for it."

"She should be steering."

"She says steering is what the autopilot is for and being here is what captains are for, and then she used a word I'm not allowed to repeat in front of linguists."

Lena laughed — actually laughed, high and cracked, the laugh of someone standing at the edge of personal ruin discovering that ruin has good acoustics. "Fine. Let her stay. But if she touches anything—"

"She knows. She's known since the drydock. She says you're the only person aboard the Vespera scarier than her when focused, and she means it as a compliment."

* * *

They gathered in the transmitter bay because there was nowhere else and because, Lena suspected, none of them wanted to be anywhere else. Odile Ferro stood with her arms crossed, jacket still bearing the seam where a data core had lived against her heart for months. Vash, the pilot, leaned against a console that sparked occasionally, unbothered, the way she was unbothered by most things including atmospheric reentry. The others crowded the doorway — de-aged veterans with old eyes, the crew of a ship that had come back wrong and right at the same time.

And Corvane. Minister-Adjunct Corvane, who had faked his death, offered his codes, planned to edit the truth into a monument to himself, and been stopped at gunpoint by a nineteen-year-old engineer who had once nearly sold everyone out for the promise of his own life back. Corvane stood apart now, wrists cuffed with a cargo strap, watching Lena with the expression of a man doing rapid arithmetic and not liking any of the sums.

"You understand what you're about to do," he said. Not a threat. Worse — a plea. "Unedited? Both fleets will hear testimony neither government sanctioned. The armistice—"

"The armistice is a bandage over a wound nobody cleaned," Lena said. "I'm the cleaning. It's going to sting."

"There will be chaos."

"There already is. It's just organized chaos, wearing flags." She turned away from him, because looking at him too long made her want to argue instead of work, and arguing was a luxury of people with time. "Odile. Am I clear to transmit?"

Odile crossed the bay and stood beside her, close enough that their shoulders touched. For a moment the captain said nothing, just looked up at the great dish visible through the shattered observation port — Kestrel-9's backup array, commissioned by whistleblowers who were silenced before launch, maintained by a lonely woman who never knew she was someone else's contingency plan. Rage had lived in Lena for three days now, ever since she'd found the founding documents in the station's sealed archive. But rage, she'd learned, was just grief that had decided to be useful.

"When I was a girl," Odile said quietly, "my grandmother told me that some truths are like birds. You can keep them caged, but they lose weight every year until one day you open the cage and find nothing left." She nodded at the core. "That one's still heavy. Set it free while it can still fly."

"That's beautiful."

"I stole it from a recruiting poster. Doesn't make it less true."

* * *

Lena opened the channel.

Not the military channels — those were jammed, contested, screaming with challenge codes and threats. She opened everything. Civilian bands. Emergency frequencies. The old deep-relay lanes that freighter crews still monitored out of habit and superstition. The array groaned, found its voice, and pushed.

Then she began to speak.

She started with Standard, because it was the language of both fleets and both governments, and she gave them the testimony plainly, without ornament: *We watched. We were present at Cassini Station on the night of the massacre. We saw the telemetry planted, the signatures forged, the ships that fired on their own people while both governments pointed at each other. We have waited through your war. We are done waiting.*

In Standard it took ninety seconds. In the dream-language it took eleven.

Because that was the thing about the signal's grammar — the grammar that had replaced her dreams, syllable by syllable, decade by decade — it didn't say things so much as it *made them undeniable*. Where human languages built arguments, it built weather. You didn't hear the testimony in that tongue; you stood inside it. She spoke it now for the first time with a waking mouth, and felt it move through her chest like something with weight and wings, and heard her own voice double itself strangely in the monitoring speakers, harmonics she wasn't producing arriving anyway, as if the signal itself had decided that tonight it would speak through whatever mouth was willing.

*witness us,* the phrase translated, and she had translated it that way weeks ago, trembling in a dark lab, but now she understood it went both directions. They had witnessed the massacre. And now they demanded witnesses in return. All of them. Everyone. A whole galaxy made responsible by knowing.

She layered the languages. That was the trick she'd found at three in the morning, sleepless, staring at the translation key burned into her memory: the testimony wasn't one message in many tongues. It was many tongues braided into one message, each strand carrying what the others couldn't. Standard for the courts. Meridian trade-cant for the dockworkers and the spacers, who would believe a cant-speaker before they'd believe a minister. Old Cassini dialect for the survivors' descendants, so the words would land in the accent of home. And underneath all of it, the dream-language, vast and patient, saying the same thing in the only grammar that could hold its full weight.

Her voice was going raw by the fourth cycle. She kept going.

Somewhere behind her, Tam whispered, "It's spreading. Halcyon Reach is rebroadcasting. The archive moon — they're putting it on every channel they have." A pause. "So is somebody else. Unregistered relays. Dozens of them. People are—" His voice caught. "People are listening."

"Good," Lena rasped, and kept speaking.

* * *

The array failed at minute nineteen.

Not completely — it failed the way she was failing, partially, gloriously, shedding pieces of itself while continuing anyway. Warning lights she'd never seen lit up consoles that hadn't been touched since before the war. The dish's servos screamed through a frequency range they were never rated for. Heat shimmered off the transmitter housing, and the smell of burning insulation thickened until Vash cracked a seal and vented the bay, laughing at something — Lena would learn later it was the sight of a Ministry drone outside the viewport visibly hesitating, its targeting logic apparently unsure whether a lone woman telling the truth constituted a weapons threat.

It did not. That was the whole point. That had always been the whole point.

On the twelfth cycle, the first response came in. Not a threat. A question, on an open civilian band, from a ship whose transponder identified it as a hospital vessel out of Meridian space: *Say again. Say all of it again. Recording.*

Then another. Then nine. Then the bands filled with voices asking her to repeat, to slow down, to send the coordinates, to confirm the telemetry hashes — and Lena realized, with a sensation like vertigo inverted, that she was no longer broadcasting *at* anyone. She was broadcasting *with* them. The message had escaped. It was loose in the wires of civilization, replicating faster than any order could chase it, and somewhere in the last few minutes it had stopped belonging to her at all.

She sat down on the deck. Her legs had simply announced they were finished, and she'd agreed.

"Lena." Odile crouched beside her. Through the observation port, the stars wheeled slowly as the station's damaged attitude control drifted, and among the stars, close enough now to see running lights, the leading edge of the joint fleet. Hundreds of ships. Weapons hot, presumably. Orders incoming, certainly. And every bridge crew on every one of those ships hearing the same testimony their own governments had murdered to bury.

"They're going to fire," Corvane said from across the bay. He sounded almost gentle, which from him was its own kind of terrifying. "You should know that. Truth doesn't stop torpedoes."

"No," Lena said. Her throat was wrecked; the words came out as gravel and grace. "But truth stops the *reason* for torpedoes. Eventually. Maybe not today. Maybe today is just the day everybody finds out what they've been shooting for." She looked up at Odile. "Did I get it all? Did I miss anything?"

Odile was quiet a moment. Then she smiled — small, fierce, the smile of a captain watching her impossible crew do the impossible thing one more time.

"You missed nothing," she said. "You got it all, and then some, and you did it in a language nobody's spoken aloud in recorded history, and you did it while bleeding from the throat, and I have served with heroes for forty years and you are embarrassing all of them."

"Forty years," Tam said. "You're welcome, by the way. For the gunpoint thing."

"You're welcome," Lena told him, and meant all of it — the vote, the trust, the nineteen-year-old body holding a forty-year-old promise steady under fire.

The array shrieked. Sparks cascaded from the main conduit, and the great dish overhead shuddered through its final calibration, and Lena Quintana — xenolinguist, exile, contingency plan, mouth for the dead — put her hand on the transmitter one last time and felt it give everything it had left.

Outside, the fleet was coming. Inside, the silence after the broadcast rang louder than the transmission ever had.

Odile's comm crackled. She listened, and her face did something complicated.

"What is it?" Lena asked.

"The lead ship is hailing us," Odile said slowly. "Both flagships. Joint channel." She looked down at Lena, and for the first time since the drydock, since the theft, since the minefield and the betrayal and the long radio-dark — Captain Odile Ferro looked uncertain. "They're asking for the translator. By name. They want *you* to talk to them."

Lena laughed again, wetter this time, and wiped her eyes with the back of a hand that smelled like burnt copper and forty years of waiting finally, finally ending.

"Well," she said. "Tell them I'm coming. And tell them to put the tea machine back online. I've earned it."
