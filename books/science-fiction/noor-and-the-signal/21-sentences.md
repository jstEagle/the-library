## 21. Sentences

The tribunal chamber on Halcyon Reach had once been an archive reading room, and it hadn't quite committed to its new identity. The shelves were still there, bolted along the walls, though the archivists had emptied them of anything flammable and filled them instead with potted ferns that nobody watered enough. Tam Rhee sat at a folding table under a bank of grow-lights, wearing the only jacket he owned that still fit his nineteen-year-old body, and waited for the universe to decide what he was worth.

Three judges. Two from the Meridian Concord's provisional authority, one from the Republic delegation — a gesture of evenhandedness that everyone present understood to be theater, since both governments had spent the last four months trying very hard to pretend they'd never heard of the Vespera or anyone aboard her.

"Engineer First Class Tam Rhee." The middle judge, a Concord woman named Oyelaran with reading glasses she kept pushing up her nose, consulted a slate thicker than a hymnal. "You are charged with theft of a military vessel in drydock, unauthorized departure from a quarantine zone, flight from joint naval forces, discharge of weapons within a civilian corridor, and—" she paused, and something flickered behind the glasses that might have been sympathy or might have been indigestion, "—one count of 'general insubordination,' which I confess is not a standard charge."

"That was Captain Ferro's addition," Tam said. "She wanted the record complete."

"I see. And do you wish to contest any of these charges?"

Tam thought about it. He'd had four months in custody — comfortable custody, archive-moon custody, the kind where the guards brought you tea and asked you questions about deep-space engineering because they were genuinely curious — to think about what he wanted to say. He'd drafted answers. He'd rehearsed them in the shower, which was the only place on Halcyon Reach with decent acoustics.

"No, ma'am," he said. "I did all of it. I'd do most of it again. The weapons discharge in the civilian corridor I regret. We clipped a freighter's antenna array and they were very polite about it, which made it worse somehow."

Oyelaran wrote something down. The Republic judge, a heavy-jowled man named Doss who looked like he'd been carved out of a diplomatic communiqué, leaned forward.

"You understand," Doss said, "that the vessel you stole was your own ship. You served aboard the Vespera for eleven years before the incident."

"Twenty-two years," Tam said. "Give or take the ones my cells don't admit to."

The third judge — the youngest, a Concord officer whose name Tam had already forgotten because he'd decided early that she was decoration — looked up sharply at that. Right. Nobody had gotten used to it yet. Four months of tribunals and hearings and medical evaluations, and people still did a small double-take when the crew of the Vespera opened their mouths and out came voices aged forty years past their faces.

"The restorative treatment remains available," Doss said, and here he shifted, and Tam understood that this was the part the man had actually come to say. Everything else was procedure. This was policy. "The Concord's medical authority has confirmed viability. Full somatic restoration to your chronological age. Your service record would be reinstated. Your seniority preserved. In light of your cooperation during the broadcast crisis, the tribunal is authorized to offer it as part of any sentence reduction."

There it was. The offer they'd been dangling since the interrogation, back when it had been a bribe, dressed up now as mercy.

Tam folded his hands on the table. His hands were nineteen years old. He knew every scar that should have been on them and wasn't — the burn from the coolant line on the *Persephone*, the splinter he'd taken through the thumb during the Meridian blockade runs, the knuckle he'd broken punching a bulkhead the day the war started and Cassini Station burned on every screen in the fleet. Smooth skin. A stranger's map.

"Can I ask a question?" he said.

"You may."

"When you restore somebody — put the years back — does the memory go with them? The forty years we lived out there? Or do I just wake up forty-one with a gap where my life used to be?"

Doss glanced at Oyelaran. Oyelaran took off her glasses entirely, which Tam suspected was as close as she got to dropping the official mask.

"The treatment restores cellular age," she said carefully. "Memory integration is... incomplete. In trial subjects, the intervening period presents as dreamlike. Persistent but not reliably accessible."

"So I'd remember it like a story someone told me."

"More or less."

"And the parts of me that those forty years built — the parts that aren't memories exactly, just... load-bearing. The habits. The instincts. The reasons I flinch at certain frequencies." He tapped his temple. "Do those survive?"

Silence. The young judge looked down at her slate. Even Doss didn't have a line for that one.

"Yeah," Tam said. "That's what I figured."

* * *

They gave him three days to decide, which was two days more than he needed and one day less than he wanted, because wanting was the whole problem.

He spent the first evening in the crew quarters — the archivists had given the Vespera's people a wing of the old residential stacks, bookshelf bunks and shared galley, which Odile had pronounced "the best posting I've had in thirty years" with a straight face. He found her in the galley doing what she always did when a decision loomed: cleaning equipment that didn't need cleaning. She had a torque wrench disassembled into its component parts, laid out on a towel like surgery.

"They offered it to you too," Tam said. It wasn't a question. The offer had gone to all of them. That was the elegant cruelty of it — no punishment required, just temptation, evenly distributed.

"Forty-one years old," Odile said, turning a screw over in her fingers. "I could be younger than my own command record. Imagine the paperwork."

"Are you taking it?"

She set the screw down and looked at him for a long moment. Odile Ferro had a face built for silence; she could hold one the way other captains held a deck.

"I'm fifty-eight," she said finally. "Chronologically. Biologically, I'm somewhere around twenty-six and my knees haven't figured it out yet. Every morning I wake up and for about four seconds I don't know which body I'm in. And then I remember the war, and the long hauls, and the eleven people I've buried, and I know exactly which body I'm in, because this is the one that carries all of it." She picked the screw back up. "If they sand me down to my real age, where does it go? Where do they keep it?"

"That's not how they described it."

"That's not how they'd describe anything." She glanced at him. "You're going to refuse."

It wasn't a question either. He shrugged anyway.

"Part of me wants it so bad I can taste it," he said. "That's the honest answer. There's a version of me that walks out of here forty-one, gets his rank back, maybe a ship of his own someday, and never has to explain to another doctor why his reflexes are older than his ligaments. I want that guy's life. I've wanted it since the day we came back."

"But."

"But it's not mine." He sat down across from her, at the little table with the towel and the wrench. "You know what I figured out, in the interrogations? Corvane's people kept asking me what I'd lost. What the deep space took from me. And I kept trying to answer, and I couldn't, because the answer is: nothing I'd trade. The years out there were bad and cold and long, and I loved almost every minute, and if I let them file those minutes off smooth, I don't know what's left holding me up. I'd be forty-one years old with a stranger's spine."

Odile nodded slowly, and then, because she was Odile, she said: "Your spine's fine. I signed off on your last physical."

"My *real* last physical was twenty-two years ago, Captain."

"Then it's overdue. See? You need the years." But she was smiling, the small dry smile she rationed out like fuel, and she reached across the table and gripped his forearm — the grip of a captain confirming her engineer was solid, which was the closest thing to affection the fleet had ever taught either of them.

* * *

The second day, Lena came to see him.

She arrived with a box of tea — actual tea, smuggled from Kestrel-9's rebuilt commissary, where she apparently now held some kind of honorary position that involved talking to the beacon. She'd cut her hair short since the broadcast. She looked better than she had any right to, considering. Rested, even. She'd told him once that after the signal went quiet, she'd slept fourteen hours a night for a month, "making up for a decade," and she still had the slightly stunned look of a person discovering that mornings could be pleasant.

"I brought bribes," she said, setting the box on his bunk. "For the tribunal. You're supposed to slip them to the judges, right? That's how justice works?"

"I'll be sure to mention your counsel at my hearing."

"How *is* the hearing going? They're keeping you all in separate sessions, so we only get the rumors."

"Rumors are accurate so far. Theft, flight, insubordination. The usual rap sheet of a career criminal." He poured them both cups of the tea, because refusing good tea was against his religion. "How's the monument business?"

"Loud, actually. Tourists. They come out to Kestrel-9 to look at the beacon and ask me what the aliens sounded like." She wrapped her hands around her cup. "I tell them the truth, which is that they sounded like a sentence I'd been dreaming for ten years, and the sentence turned out to be *witness us*, and now everybody has, so. Case closed."

"Do you miss it? The signal?"

Lena considered this with the seriousness she gave everything. "I miss being sure of something before breakfast," she said. "But no. I don't miss it. It was a guest that stayed too long, and it left the place nicer than it found it." Then she looked at him directly, and he braced, because he'd learned that directness from Lena Quintana was never casual. "They offered you the restoration."

"News travels."

"Odile told me. She also told me what you're going to do, and she asked me to come talk to you anyway, because she said — and I quote — 'he'll want to argue with someone who isn't his commanding officer.'"

"She knows me disturbingly well."

"So argue." Lena set down her cup. "Tell me why I'm wrong to hope you'd take it. Because I keep thinking — Tam, you were nineteen when you went out. You never got the twenties. The bad apartments, the terrible relationships, the year you eat nothing but noodles. You could have all of it. A whole decade of mistakes you were owed."

And there it was, laid out plain: the life not lived. He felt it pull, low and constant, like a tide.

"I thought about that," he said. "A lot. And here's the thing — I can't have the twenties. Not really. The treatment doesn't give me the twenties. It gives me a forty-one-year-old body and a head full of forty-year-old memories filed under 'miscellaneous.' I'd walk into a bar full of twenty-somethings looking their age and feeling like a museum exhibit with the placard torn off." He shook his head. "The years I actually lived are the only ones that are mine. They're ugly and they're cold and half of them happened inside a hull smaller than this room, but they're *mine*. I'm not giving them back so I can pass a physical."

Lena was quiet for a while. Then she smiled — a real one, crooked, the kind she'd clearly been practicing since the dreams stopped.

"Okay," she said. "For the record, I hoped you'd take it. And for the record, I think you're right, and I hate it a little, because it means the universe doesn't fix things. It just makes you strong enough to carry them."

"That's the most depressing comforting thing anyone's ever said to me."

"I'm a xenolinguist. Comfort was never my language." She stood, drained her tea, and paused at the door. "For what it's worth — whatever they sentence you to, the crew's already decided it's too much. Odile's been negotiating with the archivists. Something about time served."

"We were in deep space for forty subjective years. Does that count as time served?"

"She's arguing it should count double."

* * *

On the third day, they sentenced him.

Oyelaran read it out in a voice pitched for fairness: theft of the Vespera, remitted on grounds that the vessel's legal ownership had been, per the armistice archives, "contested at the time of acquisition" — a phrase Tam intended to have engraved on something. Flight from naval forces, six months' supervised service, to be performed, at the crew's request and the archivists' insistence, in the maintenance of the Halcyon Reach archive itself. The weapons discharge drew a formal reprimand and an order of restitution to the freighter's owner, who had sent the tribunal a note asking only that the Vespera's crew "keep being interesting."

The general insubordination charge — Odile's addition — drew a suspended sentence, contingent on Tam's continued good behavior, defined by Oyelaran as "whatever Captain Ferro says it is."

"In other words," Oyelaran said, removing her glasses one final time, "you are sentenced to remain an engineer. Under her. Indefinitely."

"Yes, ma'am," Tam said. "I'll survive it."

Doss cleared his throat. "The offer of restorative treatment stands open for one further year. Should you change your mind."

"I won't," Tam said. "But thank you. For leaving the door open. Somebody should get to walk through it." He thought of the others — Vess, the comms officer, who'd already scheduled her restoration for the spring; Okonkwo, who was still deciding; the two who'd taken it immediately, without ceremony, and whom Tam missed like a limb and refused to resent. "Not everybody should refuse. It's not a test of character. It's just a door."

Oyelaran looked at him for a moment longer than procedure required. "No," she agreed quietly. "It isn't."

* * *

That evening, the whole crew crowded into the archive wing's common room — Lena and Odile and the nine who remained, plus a scattering of archivists who'd adopted them the way archivists adopt anything that generates paperwork worth keeping. Someone had produced a cake. It said CONGRATULATIONS ON YOUR CRIMES in icing, which Tam suspected was Lena's work, and which Odile pretended not to see while cutting the largest slice for herself.

"To the Vespera," Odile raised her cup, "and to sentences served in full."

"To time served," Tam said, "double rate, deep space."

The laughter went around the room, warm and tired, and Tam stood in the middle of it with his smooth young hands around a warm cup, forty years old in every way that counted, and let himself feel it: imperfect, unfinished, real.

He was still holding the cup when the comm panel chimed — the priority tone, the one that meant off-world traffic, the one nobody used for cake.

Odile answered it. Tam watched her face do something complicated.

"That was the Kestrel-9 relay," she said slowly. "Lena — they're saying your beacon just woke up. On its own. And it's transmitting."
