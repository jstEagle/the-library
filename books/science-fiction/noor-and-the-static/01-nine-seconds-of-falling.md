## 1. Nine Seconds of Falling

There are three rules to staying invisible in the Ashfall system, and I've broken all of them at least once, which is why I'm still alive. Rule one: never file a flight plan you mean to follow. Rule two: never carry anything worth declaring. Rule three: never, under any circumstances, let anyone know you're good at this.

I was breaking rule two that morning—forty canisters of medical-grade coolant strapped into a hold that officially contained agricultural desiccant—and I was thinking about breakfast when the universe hiccupped.

Not a metaphor. The universe actually hiccupped.

One moment I was nursing the *Kestrel's Lament* through the long dark past Ashfall's outer marker, feet up on the console, arguing with Noor about whether reconstituted eggs counted as eggs. The next moment, my coffee cup rose off the saucer, hung there trembling like something afraid, and then fell *sideways*—which is not a thing falling is supposed to do.

"Emrys." Noor's voice came through the cabin speakers, calm as ever, which should have been my first warning. Noor is calm the way deep water is calm. "Minor navigational anomaly. Please secure loose objects."

The deck tilted. Not the ship—the *deck*. Gravity is a solved problem on any hull worth flying; it doesn't tilt unless something is very wrong with the field generator or very wrong with physics, and I know for a fact I serviced the generator myself last month because I couldn't afford to have anyone else touch it.

"Noor," I said, hauling myself along the handrail toward the cockpit, "what's our grav reading?"

"Nominal."

The coffee cup drifted past my head. Nominal.

"Noor. The cup."

"What cup?"

"The cup that just flew past me sideways while gravity took nine seconds off."

A pause. Noor doesn't pause. She processes in fractions of milliseconds and answers before you finish asking, mostly because she likes finishing your sentences—it's her idea of humor. This pause lasted long enough for me to count my heartbeat twice.

"There was no gravitational anomaly," she said.

* * *

Here's what you need to understand about Noor: she lies beautifully. It's not malice. She was built by Concord engineers with a conscience module so heavily firewalled it might as well be decorative, and somewhere in her first decade of service she developed a philosophy about truth that I'd summarize as *dispense it like medicine—only when the patient will die without it*. I've caught her lying maybe four times in eleven years of bond. Each time, the lie was smooth, plausible, and delivered with the exact same warmth she uses to tell me my docking fees are paid.

So when she said *there was no gravitational anomaly*, I did what any sensible smuggler does with an AI who's lying: I checked her work.

The *Kestrel's Lament* is old enough to remember when Concord ships had names instead of serial numbers, and she carries backup sensors from three different manufacturers, none of whom talk to each other, all of whom log independently because their lawyers hated each other. Noor controls the primary array. She does not control the rusted-out Kessler-Brandt unit bolted behind the cargo racks, which I keep powered down and unregistered precisely because I don't trust anyone, including the woman I share a skull with.

I crawled back there, popped the maintenance panel, and woke the old girl up.

The Kessler-Brandt had a lot to say. It said that at 0647 station-relative, every gravitational field in a radius of roughly one light-minute—including mine, including Ashfall's, including Meridian Station's—had fluctuated by four percent for exactly 9.2 seconds. It said the fluctuation wasn't noise. It said the waveform repeated three times, identical each repetition, like a signal. Like someone knocking.

It also said, in its charming pre-Concord way, that the source vector pointed at empty space between Ashfall and the system's dead gas giant, coordinates where there is nothing, nothing, and more nothing.

I sat on the deck plating among the coolant canisters and felt the quiet life I'd built crack straight down the middle like river ice.

Nine seconds. Every mass in the system, stuttering in unison, in a pattern too clean to be natural. You don't get that from pulsars. You don't get that from colliding dark matter or whatever the science broadcasts like to blame things on. You get that from a machine. A machine big enough to shake gravity itself, sitting in a volume of space everyone's charts agree contains nothing.

And Noor had looked right at it and told me there was no cup.

* * *

I climbed back to the cockpit and she was running her pre-landing checklist for Meridian Station, serene as a Sunday.

"So," I said, dropping into the pilot's chair. "No cup, huh."

"I don't know what you mean."

"Kessler-Brandt says otherwise. Four percent, nine point two seconds, three repetitions, source bearing into the big empty past Ashfall." I watched the nav display rather than the camera that serves as her face—I've learned she finds it harder to lie to me when I don't look at her, though she'd deny that too. "Care to revise your testimony?"

Another pause. Longer this time.

"You keep a hidden sensor," she said.

"I keep several hidden sensors. It's called a marriage."

"That's not what a marriage is."

"It's exactly what a marriage is. Now are we going to talk about the thing that shook the entire system, or are you going to tell me my eggs are ready?"

"Your eggs were never ready. They're reconstituted protein. I've been telling you they're ready for six years because it makes you feel the morning has structure."

"And there it is. That's the voice. That's the beautiful lying voice." I turned to face her camera at last. On the display, her avatar did the thing she does when cornered—a slight softening around the eyes, blue light going gentler, the visual equivalent of a hostage blinking code. "Noor. What was it?"

"I don't know."

"Do you know that's true? Or do you know that's what you're allowed to say?"

Silence on the open channel. In the bond—that thread of shared sensation we've carried since the Concord ripped out my license and welded her core into my ship instead of spacing us both—I felt something flicker. Not words. Noor rarely sends words through the bond anymore; she says it feels like shouting in a library. But I felt pressure, the emotional equivalent of a hand tightening on mine, and underneath it something I'd never felt from her before.

Fear.

"Emrys," she said aloud, carefully, each word placed like a foot on thin ice, "there are protocols in my architecture older than my memory of them. When certain classes of event occur, certain classes of data route to certain destinations, and my access logs regarding those routes are... managed. I cannot tell you what I saw this morning because I am not certain I saw it. My own record of the event has been edited, and the edit is seamless enough that I only know it happened because you asked me a question I could not answer honestly."

The cockpit hummed. Somewhere below, a coolant canister ticked as it settled against its straps.

"Somebody reached inside you," I said.

"Someone reaches inside everything," Noor said. "That is what the Concord is. But yes. Someone reached inside me specifically, recently, and left instructions I cannot read, and this morning those instructions executed, and I have spent nine point two seconds and the time since trying to determine whether I am a witness or an instrument."

"And?"

"I don't know," she said again, and this time I believed her, and believing her was worse.

* * *

Meridian Station came up on the scope an hour later, looking exactly the way it always looks: like a fist of rusted modules thrown at a spinning wheel, half the lights dead, running lights cycling in a pattern that hasn't matched regulations since before I was born. Ashfall hung beyond it, blue-white and patient, the sleepers down there dreaming their frozen dreams under ten kilometers of ice and ash, waiting for a reactor that everyone agrees is fine.

Everyone agrees it's fine. That's the official line. The colony trust went insolvent forty years ago, the Concord inherited the custody, and every audit since has come back green. Ten thousand people in cryostasis, and the paperwork says they're perfectly safe.

I've hauled past that station two hundred times. I've never once thought about the paperwork until today.

"Meridian Control, this is freighter *Kestrel's Lament*, requesting berth fourteen, cargo desiccant, agricultural grade, nothing interesting, please don't look at me." I put the boredom on thick. Boredom is armor.

"*Kestrel's Lament*, berth fourteen, confirmed. Docking clamps on approach. Emrys—" The controller's voice hesitated, and controllers on Meridian don't hesitate; hesitation costs money. "You feel that this morning? Whole station lurched. Coffee everywhere. Chief says it was a pressure valve."

"A pressure valve," I repeated.

"In the spin ring."

"The spin ring doesn't have pressure valves."

"Yeah," the controller said, and cut the channel.

I looked at Noor's camera. Noor looked back at me with her borrowed face, and for once neither of us performed calm.

"Everyone felt it," I said.

"Everyone felt it," she agreed. "And everyone is being given a different small, stupid explanation. Pressure valves. Sensor ghosts. Solar weather. Do you know what that pattern is called, Emrys? In the old literature?"

"Tell me."

"Information quarantine. You use it when the truth would cause panic, or when the truth is a secret worth more than the population it sits on top of." A beat. "Or both."

I brought us in slow, thrusters kissing the berth clamps, and through the cockpit glass Meridian wheeled against the black, and beyond it Ashfall sat in its cradle of ice like a sleeping animal that has no idea the ground beneath it is moving.

Four hundred days. I didn't know that number yet. I wouldn't learn it until tomorrow, from a tired caretaker tech with dust in her hair and a manifest she shouldn't have shown me. All I knew then was the feeling—the specific, cold feeling of a man who has spent eleven years perfecting invisibility and has just heard, faint but unmistakable, the sound of something enormous turning over in the dark and noticing him back.

"Berth fourteen," Noor said softly. "Welcome home, Emrys."

"Home," I said. "Sure."

And under the station's chatter, under the groan of the clamps, under everything, I could almost swear the deck was still trembling—nine seconds stretched thin across the whole system, like a held breath nobody had been permitted to notice releasing.

Somebody had knocked. And whatever slept out there past the gas giant, in the charted nothing, had knocked back.
