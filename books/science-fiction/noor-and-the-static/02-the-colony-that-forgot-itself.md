## 2. The Colony That Forgot Itself

Meridian Station didn't so much orbit Ashfall as cling to it, the way a barnacle clings to a hull everyone's already written off. From a thousand klicks out it looked like a child's idea of a station: a spindle of rust-brown modules strung on a spine that had been white once, back when anyone cared enough to paint things. The solar arrays hung off it at angles that suggested they'd been adjusted by hand, badly, by someone who'd stopped believing in the work around year three.

"Approach vector's ugly," Noor said in my ear. "Their beacon is drifting eleven degrees off nominal."

"So correct for it."

"I could. Or I could let you earn your landing fee."

I eased the *Kestrel's Lament* around the station's shadow side, where the hull plating was pocked with micrometeorite scars and one long gouge I pretended not to recognize from the last four visits. "You're a contraband-hauling AI with a sense of humor about safety protocols. Remind me why I haven't sold you to a museum."

"Because museums don't pay in unmarked credit chits, and you'd miss me."

She wasn't wrong on either count. That was the trouble with Noor—she was rarely wrong, and she knew exactly how much I hated it.

The docking clamps caught with a sound like an old man clearing his throat. I ran through shutdown while the station's gravity cycled up to something approximating standard, and I felt that familiar half-second wrongness in my inner ear as the deck remembered which way was down. Somewhere below us, ten thousand people slept through their own funeral arrangements.

* * *

The corridor from Docking Bay Three smelled the way Meridian always smelled: recycled air past its warranty, machine oil, and underneath it all a faint sweetness like fruit going off slowly, forever. The lights came on in sections as I walked, motion-triggered, each one buzzing awake a beat after I passed under it, as if the station were remembering me just slightly too late.

Cryo-bay access was supposed to be restricted. It was supposed to be sealed behind Concord-grade encryption with biometric locks and a logging system that reported every entry to some office on some core world where someone with a real desk and real coffee would read it.

Instead the door hissed open at my knock because the lock had died sometime in the last decade and nobody had told it to stop pretending.

Inside, the cold hit first. Cryo-bays run cold—that's physics, not sentiment—but this was a different cold, a thin one, the kind that gets into your teeth. Ten thousand pods stretched away in ranks, tiered upward toward a ceiling lost in conduit and frost. Tier One sat closest to the reactor feeds, closest to the warm bloodlines of power. Tier Two climbed the walls like ivy, packed tighter, cheaper pods, older seals.

And along the near wall, mounted on a panel that should have been showing pod status in serene green rows, a display scrolled amber.

**POD 4471 — VITALS LOST**
**POD 4472 — VITALS LOST**
**POD 4473 — VITALS LOST**

I stood there longer than I should have. Long enough for the lights behind me to give up and go dark, then buzz back on when I shifted my weight.

Vitals lost. Not *pod failure*. Not *maintenance required*. Vitals *lost*, logged in amber, timestamped, filed—and going nowhere. I pulled up the log history with a flick of my wrist terminal. Four hundred and six entries in the last two years. Four hundred and six people who had gone into the ice expecting to wake up on a world worth waking up for, and instead had simply stopped, quietly, one by one, while the station logged their deaths with the enthusiasm of a filing cabinet.

No alert. No flag. No message queued for transmission to anywhere.

"You're not supposed to be in here."

The voice came from my left, from behind a maintenance scaffold, and it belonged to a small woman in a tech's jumpsuit two sizes too big, sleeves rolled to the elbow, holding a diagnostic wand like she'd been considering using it on me. Dark skin gone greyish under the bay's lighting, hair cropped short, eyes that did a full inventory of me before settling on my face.

"Sefa," I said. "Right? You fixed my scrubber array in March."

Her expression didn't change. "That was March. This is now. Now you're standing in a restricted bay reading a manifest you shouldn't have access to."

"The door opened."

"The door's broken."

"Then we agree on something." I nodded at the amber scroll. "Four hundred and six."

Something moved behind her eyes—not surprise. She'd known the number. Probably better than I did; probably to the decimal. What moved was the flinch of someone who keeps a wound dressed in private being asked to show it in public.

"Four hundred and nine," she said. "Pod 4512 went last night. Cardiac support lag. The seal heaters on Tier Two are drawing from a bus that browns out every time the reactor cycles. They die in their sleep, Emrys. Which I suppose is the one mercy left on this rock."

"And nobody reports it."

"To whom?" She said it without heat, which made it worse. "The colonial authority dissolved eleven years ago. The Concord lists Ashfall as a Class-F deferred asset. Deferred means *we'll get to it when we get to it*. There's a comms officer on the ring who files a quarterly status report, and do you know what it says?"

"Tell me."

"*All systems nominal.*" She smiled, and it was the worst smile I've ever seen—the smile of someone who has learned that grief, done daily, looks exactly like routine. "Because if it said anything else, the Concord would have to either send help or send auditors, and both cost more than ten thousand frozen laborers are worth on paper."

"Laborers," I repeated.

Her jaw tightened. "Tier Two. Yes. You noticed the geography of the bay, I see. Founders near the power. Workers near the walls." She turned and walked, and after a moment I followed, because what else was there to do—stand alone in the cold with a list of the dead?

* * *

The reactor gallery sat two decks down, behind a door that at least had the dignity of a working lock. Sefa keyed it open and led me onto a catwalk above the thing itself: a torus of dull metal wrapped in coolant lines, humming at a frequency I felt in my molars more than heard. My cargo—forty canisters of black-market coolant, sitting in my hold one deck up—suddenly felt less like merchandise and more like irony.

A display dominated the far wall. Reactor output curves, containment margins, fuel burn graphs—all of them trending in directions graphs aren't supposed to trend. And beneath them, large, patient, counting:

**ESTIMATED CONTAINMENT FAILURE: 400 DAYS, 6 HOURS, 12 MINUTES**

The seconds ticked down as I watched. 11. 10. 9.

Nine seconds. Something cold walked up my spine that had nothing to do with the cryo-bay.

"How long have you known?" I asked.

"Officially? Since the last audit found nothing." Sefa leaned on the rail. "Unofficially? Three years. The containment lattice is degrading faster than the models say it should. I've sent requests for replacement lattice stock forty-one times. I've received a response twice, and both times it was a form letter thanking me for my continued service to Concord expansion goals."

"Forty-one times."

"I keep count. It's the only honest arithmetic left on this station." She glanced at me sideways. "Why do you think you've been hauling coolant here every eight weeks for two years? You think I'm running a still?"

I thought about the canisters in my hold. Thought about the amber scroll. Thought about a gravity flicker nine hours ago that had lasted nine seconds and made my tea float, and about Noor telling me it was sensor ghosting, and about how Noor only lied when she was told to.

"Sefa," I said carefully, "when the flicker happened—did your instruments catch anything?"

Her head came around slowly. "What flicker?"

"About nine hours ago. System-wide gravity stutter. Nine seconds, give or take."

For the first time since I'd met her, Sefa looked genuinely afraid. Not of me. Of the question.

"Our gravimetric suite logged it," she said quietly. "Then the log deleted itself. All of it. Within four minutes. I only know it happened because I screenshot the raw feed out of habit, and habits are the only security system that never gets patched." She pulled her wrist terminal around and showed me: a jagged spike, a flatline, a recovery curve. Timestamped. Real. "Stations don't delete their own logs, Emrys. People delete logs. Which means someone was watching this station closely enough to clean up after whatever that was—and they're watching right now."

The countdown ticked. 399 days, 23 hours, 51 minutes.

Ten thousand sleepers. Four hundred days. A weapon test somebody wanted forgotten before the witnesses thawed out—or didn't.

"I need to talk to my ship," I said.

Sefa raised an eyebrow. "Your ship know something?"

"My ship knows everything," I said. "That's starting to look like the problem."

Behind us, deep in the torus, the reactor hummed on, patient as a held breath, and somewhere in its cooling veins a clock counted down the colony that had forgotten itself—one amber line, one silent death, one deleted log at a time.
