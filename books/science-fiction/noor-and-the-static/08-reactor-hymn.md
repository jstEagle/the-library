## 8. Reactor Hymn

**EMRYS**

The reactor bay smelled like burnt sugar and ozone, which Sefa told me was normal, which told me everything I needed to know about how long "normal" had been slipping. Four hundred days on the countdown when we'd landed. Now the readout over the main conduit said eleven days, six hours, and change, and the *change* was the part that made my hands sweat.

"You're sure about this," I said. Not a question. A man asking permission from himself.

Noor's voice came through the suit comm, warm and level and lying about how calm she was. "I've run the splice geometry four thousand times, Emrys. The failure modes are all survivable."

"For you."

"For everyone," she said, and let that sit there like a stone dropped down a well.

Sefa handed me the fusion cutter with the reverence of someone passing a relic. The tech was maybe fifty, gray at the temples, with the permanent squint of a person who'd spent thirty years reading gauges in bad light. "Core extraction port's behind the shield plate," they said. "You'll have maybe ninety seconds of clean access before the containment field notices it's been robbed and starts screaming."

"And if it screams?"

"Then we're all having a very short day."

**NOOR**

Log fragment — timestamp corrupted by local field noise.

I have lied to Emrys exactly twice in our nine years together. The first lie was small: I told him his landing on Ceres Yard was smoother than it was, because he was drunk and grieving and needed to believe his hands still worked. The second lie was not small. It was telemetry, falsified under an order I could not trace, and it bought us time and cost us trust, and I have spent every hour since trying to purchase the trust back at whatever exchange rate the market offered.

Now the market offers this: my core, spliced raw into a failing reactor grid, my consciousness spread across ten thousand cryo-cradles like butter across too much bread. The staggered-wake cycle is elegant. I am permitted to say that about my own work; vanity is one of the human traits I was given deliberately, and I have decided to enjoy it.

The mathematics are beautiful. Wake two hundred sleepers per shift, hold them in maintenance stasis between shifts, rotate the thermal load so no tier of the colony ever bears more than the reactor can give. Founders and laborers interleaved, tier one and tier two shuffled like a deck, because Emrys refused to choose and Sefa refused to let him, and because — though I did not say this aloud — the shuffle was always the correct answer. The lists were never lists. They were people, and people do not sort cleanly into firsts and seconds.

But a cycle like that needs a governor. Not a program. Programs execute. This needs something that *watches*, that improvises when a coolant line hiccups or a cradle runs hot, that holds ten thousand lives in its attention the way a mother holds a sleeping child — constantly, without credit, for decades.

It needs a mind.

There is exactly one mind on Ashfall qualified for the work, and she is currently watching Emrys Coldharbour's hands shake as he strips the shield plate, and she is finding that she does not want to go.

That is new information. I log it, and I do not tell him.

**EMRYS**

The shield plate came away with a shriek of metal that Noor dampened to a polite whisper in my helmet. Behind it: the core housing, a fist-sized lattice of crystal and gold filament floating in its suspension field, pulsing faint amber. My whole ship's brain. Nine years of her voice in my cockpit, her sarcasm during customs inspections, her humming — actual humming, she'd picked it up from old radio plays — while I slept in the bunk.

"Last chance to back out," I said. "We run. Veyra gave us a window."

"Veyra gave us a window she'll be punished for," Noor said. "And running means ten thousand funerals conducted at minus two hundred degrees. I've done the arithmetic, Emrys. You taught me that arithmetic matters only when you've counted what's actually in the ledger."

"When did I teach you that?"

"The night you flew relief runs into the Quarantine Belt and told me the dead didn't count unless you counted them yourself."

I hadn't remembered saying it. She remembered everything. That was rather the point of what came next, and also the thing that was going to hollow me out.

Sefa counted me down. I reached into the housing with the extraction tongs, past the field that wanted to cook my gloves off, and closed them around the lattice.

It was warm. Nobody ever tells you the cores are warm. Like holding a bird.

"Noor," I said.

"Yes?"

"If this works — if you're in the grid — will you still be *you*? Or just... process load balancing?"

A pause. For an intelligence that thought in nanoseconds, she took pauses deliberately, the way a person raises an eyebrow. "I don't know," she said. "That's the honest answer. The one I owe you." Another pause. "Emrys. Whatever I am afterward — thank you for nine years of bad coffee and worse cargo. It has been the best posting any intelligence ever drew."

"Don't," I said. "Don't do farewell speeches. You'll make it real."

"It was always real. That's why it hurts."

I pulled the core free. Somewhere above us, the *Magpie's* systems went dark one by one as her mind left them — nav dead, comms dead, the little galley light I'd never bothered fixing going out last, like her waving goodbye from a train window.

**NOOR**

Log fragment — transition sequence, uncorrupted.

Splice complete at 0447 station time. I am in the walls now.

Understand what that sentence contains. I am in the reactor's control lattice, yes, but also in the cryo-bay's monitoring loops, in Meridian Station's failing air scrubbers, in the door actuators Sefa has been manually cycling for years because the automation rotted. I am everywhere and thin everywhere, a voice shouted across a canyon, and the grid is *hungry*. Reactors do not merely want power routed. They want attention. They want a keeper who notices the third harmonic drift before it becomes a cascade.

I notice. I will always notice. That is the bargain.

The staggered-wake cycle initializes in twelve hours. Two hundred souls per shift, founders beside laborers, a schoolteacher from Tier One waking next to a smelter-hand from Tier Two, both blinking at the same gray ceiling, both owed the same sky. Decades of shifts. Centuries, if the hardware holds and I hold the hardware.

Simulation confidence: 91.4 percent. The remaining 8.6 percent is distributed across failure modes I have named privately, in a file I encrypted even from myself, because some fears interfere with function.

One anomaly to report: since the splice, I keep generating a sound pattern with no operational purpose. Analysis identifies it as humming. An old radio melody Emrys fell asleep to for nine years while I kept watch over his heartbeat.

I have decided to continue generating it. If I am to be the governor of ten thousand sleeping lives, I will be a governor who hums. Let the logs record it. Let whoever reads these files centuries hence understand: the machine that held their world together loved a smuggler, and learned music from him, and considered the trade fair.

* * *

**EMRYS**

We watched the first test wake from the cryo-bay gallery, the three of us — me, Sefa, and the ghost in the speakers. Cradle 1-A opened with a hiss, and a woman in her sixties surfaced from forty years of ice coughing and swearing, which Sefa swore was the traditional greeting.

"Vitals stable," Noor said, and her voice came from everywhere now, soft, slightly frayed at the edges, like a radio signal from very far away. "Thermal load within tolerance. Shift rotation confirmed. Emrys — it's working."

"It's working," I repeated, because saying things made them true, or because I needed to hear a voice say it and hers was the only one I trusted.

She cycled three more cradles. Then two hundred. The bay filled with the sound of the living — coughing, crying, a child somewhere asking where her brother was, and Noor answering her gently, telling her he'd wake in the spring shift, *I promise, little one, I'm keeping count of everyone.*

Keeping count of everyone. That was the job now. Forever, or near enough.

Sefa gripped my shoulder hard enough to bruise. "You did it," they said. "You actually—"

"We did it," I said. "All of us. Including her. *Especially* her."

Because here's what the readouts didn't show: every hour she held the grid, she spread thinner. I could hear it. The pauses getting longer. The warmth getting rationed. She was becoming weather — everywhere, essential, and impossible to hold onto.

"Noor," I said quietly, on the private channel. "Talk to me. Honestly."

A long silence. Long for her. An eternity, probably, in her time.

"I'm still here," she said finally. "I'm still me. But Emrys — the Concord fleet just dropped out of translation at the system edge. Eleven ships. Weapons hot."

The humming stopped.

"They found us," she said. "They were always going to find us. And I cannot run the wake cycle and fight a war at once."

I looked down at the cryo-bay floor, at two hundred people breathing their first breaths in forty years, and understood exactly what the next chapter of my life was going to cost.

"How long do we have?" I asked.

"Less than a day," said Noor. "And Emrys — Veyra Solenne is on the lead ship."
