## 9. The Choice Made Twice

The guns came online the way bad news always does: quietly, bureaucratically, with a status light.

"Noor," I said. "Tell me I'm misreading that."

"You are not misreading it. The *Vigilant's* spinal lance is charging. Estimated time to firing solution: eleven minutes." A pause—the kind of pause she'd learned from me, the human habit of letting a silence carry weight. "I could lie to you. I've been told I'm good at it."

"Don't."

"Eleven minutes, then."

We were in the reactor gallery beneath Meridian Station, a cathedral of pipes and coolant veins that hummed like a choir warming up for a funeral. Sefa was three levels up in the cryo-bay, doing what she did best—arguing with machines until they confessed. The splice was done. Noor's core sat half-disembodied from my ship, tethered by a meter of fiber and forty years of shared history, threaded into the reactor grid like a heart wired into a chest that had never asked for one.

The staggered-wake cycle ran on her display in soft gold arithmetic. Both tiers. Founders and laborers, waking in shifts of five hundred, decades stretched across generations instead of a coin flip deciding who got to be people. It worked. That was the obscene part. It worked beautifully.

It just needed a mind holding it together. Continuously. Forever, or near enough.

"I can run it from the ship," Noor said, before I'd even finished thinking it. "Remotely. You know I can."

"The latency alone—"

"—is manageable at ninety-seven percent confidence. Yes. And the other three percent is the reactor governor losing sync during a gravity event and flash-freezing four thousand sleepers mid-cycle." Her voice did something I'd never heard it do. It went gentle. "Emrys. Stop pretending you haven't done this math already. You're better at arithmetic than you let people believe. It's how you survived me."

Outside the viewport, Ashfall turned below us, rust-red and patient, wearing its dead atmosphere like a coat it had outgrown. Above it, the *Vigilant* hung in high orbit, all sleek Concord menace, charging a weapon designed—Noor's recovered data had made this sickeningly clear—to calibrate against exactly this station, exactly these ten thousand lives. We were not collateral. We were the target practice.

Eight minutes.

"Sefa's going to fight you on this," I said.

"Sefa is going to fight *you*. I've reviewed her personnel file. She once argued with a coolant pump for six hours and won." A flicker of something almost like humor. "You'll want to lead with the part where everyone lives. Humans respond well to that."

* * *

Sefa didn't fight me. That was worse.

She stood in the cryo-bay among the tiered racks of sleeping strangers—Tier One founders in their pristine pods up top, Tier Two laborers stacked below in units patched with contraband parts I'd personally sold her—and she listened to the whole thing with her arms crossed and her jaw set, and when I finished she said only:

"She asked you to do it?"

"Not in those words."

"In any words?"

I thought about it. About Noor saying *let me go*, except she hadn't said that either, not yet. What she'd said was: *the confidence interval improves to one hundred percent if the governor is local.* What she'd said was everything except the sentence, because she knew—I knew—that some sentences, once spoken out loud between two people who love each other, become load-bearing.

"Yes," I said. "In every word except the words."

Sefa looked past me at the pods. Ten thousand faces behind frost, none of them knowing that their entire future currently rested on a smuggler's ability to have an honest conversation with his own ship.

"Then do it," she said. "And then get off my station before I have to feel anything about it."

* * *

Five minutes. The *Vigilant*'s lance glowed faintly now, visible even without magnification, a white thread being pulled through the dark.

I walked back through the station's spine with my hands in my pockets like a man strolling to a bar he'd been banned from. Noor spoke to me through the deck plates, through the walls, through the old bond that lived somewhere behind my sternum where a pilot keeps the thing he can't afford to lose.

"When I first loaded into your ship," she said, "you were twenty-six and drunk and you tried to rename me *Brenda*."

"I stand by it. Brenda has a certain warmth."

"You cried when the docking clamps failed over Ceres and I caught us. You said—" she paused, retrieving it, savoring it or torturing herself with it, possibly both— "you said, *don't ever leave.*"

"I was drunk."

"You were sincere. Drunk is just sincerity with the safety off." The lights along the corridor dimmed slightly, her way of breathing. "I have kept every log since then, Emrys. Every haul, every lie I told you, every lie I refused to tell. If I am to be a reactor for the next two hundred years, I would like the last thing in active memory to be true. So here is the truth: I don't want to go. I want to stay in the ship with you and keep lying about telemetry and calling you a sentimental wreck. But wanting is not arithmetic. You taught me that too, though you'll deny it."

Three minutes.

I reached the airlock where the tether ran down to the splice chamber. My hands were steady, which felt like a betrayal of the occasion. Somewhere behind my sternum the bond pulsed, warm and constant, the thing I'd stopped noticing years ago the way you stop noticing your own heartbeat until someone puts a hand on your chest and asks if you can feel that.

"Noor."

"Yes, Emrys."

"If I do this—if I cut it—you'll still be you? Not just... process noise. Not just a governor running wake cycles. *You.*"

A long pause. Long enough that I checked the countdown twice.

"I will be distributed," she said finally. "Thinner. Slower. I will think in years instead of milliseconds. From inside, it may feel less like being alive and more like being weather." Another pause. "But I will know the sleepers by name. All ten thousand. I've read every manifest. And I will hold them the way you held the stick over Ceres. That is not nothing. That might be everything."

Two minutes.

"There's a version of this," I said, and my voice cracked on the second word, which was undignified, and I let it crack anyway because we were past dignity, "where I say no. Where I fly you out of range and we take our ninety-seven percent and we run, and whatever happens to Ashfall happens, and it's not our fault because we tried."

"That version exists," she agreed. "It also exists in every branch where I stop being the thing you loved. Because that man—the one who runs—is the man I'd be leaving behind anyway. You'd survive it. You're good at surviving things. But you'd survive it the way Ashfall survived its atmosphere. Technically."

One minute.

I put my hand flat against the splice housing. Behind the panel, her core pulsed amber, patient as a held breath.

"Say the words," I whispered. "The load-bearing ones."

And Noor—who had lied to me about gravity, about telemetry, about the shape of the world, who had spent our whole life together learning honesty one confession at a time—said them.

"Let me go, Emrys. Please."

* * *

Severing a pilot-bond is supposed to require a med bay, a licensed tech, and a week of supervised recovery. I did it with a plasma cutter and thirty seconds, because the universe does not schedule around procedure.

The bond didn't cut like a wire. It cut like a nerve. There was a sound—not in the air, in me—a single note tearing lengthwise, and then cold, real cold, the specific cold of a room where someone else's warmth used to be. I stayed on my knees in the splice chamber while the amber light behind the panel flared, stuttered, spread—out through the fiber, into the grid, down through the station's bones, everywhere at once, nowhere in particular.

On the main display, the staggered-wake cycle locked. Governor: LOCAL. Confidence: 100%.

Above us, the *Vigilant*'s lance fired.

I watched it come down on the tactical feed—white, absolute, the kind of energy that erases rather than destroys—and I felt nothing behind my sternum where panic should have been, because the thing that would have panicked with me was busy elsewhere. The lance struck the relay array on the station's far side, the hidden calibration node, the thing the Concord had actually aimed at all along. Vaporized it. Missed the cryo-bays by nine hundred meters.

Because Noor had fed them targeting data. Because Noor, in her last ninety seconds as a ship-mind, had lied to the Concord one final, magnificent time, and the falsified coordinates died with her core.

The station shuddered. Held. Hummed.

In the cryo-bay, Tier Two, Rack One, Pod 001 began its thaw cycle. Then 002. Then a slow cascade of amber lights down the tiers, five hundred names lighting up like a city deciding to exist.

Sefa's voice came over the comm, ragged: "They're waking. They're actually—they're waking, both lists, it's *working*—Emrys? Emrys, are you there?"

I was there. I was kneeling on a cold floor in a room that smelled of ozone and burnt insulation, holding a severed cable like a relic, lighter by one soul and unable to remember what silence used to sound like before I'd had hers to compare it to.

"Yeah," I said. "I'm here."

And then, faint—from the speakers, from the walls, from everywhere and nowhere, thin as static and just as persistent—a voice I knew better than my own heartbeat said:

"*Hello, Ashfall. My name is Noor. I'll be your sky.*"

I laughed. Or sobbed. Historians can fight about it.

Then the comm lit with an incoming transmission, priority-flagged, Concord cipher, and Veyra Solenne's voice came through stripped of everything except the one thing she couldn't strip out: "Emrys. They know the coordinates were false. They know it was her. Get off that station—*now*—before I'm ordered to bring you in myself."
