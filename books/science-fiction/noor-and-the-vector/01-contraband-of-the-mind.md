## 1. Contraband of the Mind

The dead man in the coffin had been dead for eleven days, and Astrid Eastwick envied him.

He was cold in the way only the truly frozen are cold — a deep, patient cold that asked nothing of anyone. No debts. No customs declarations. No memories worth stealing and none left to lose. She'd checked his file twice: Tomas Reyes, sixty-one, hydroponics technician, died of cardiac failure on the surface level, family paying for transit to the orbital necropolis above Halcyon Station because burial dirt cost more than the body did. A modest life, honestly ended. The best kind of cover there was.

She ran her palm along the coffin's rim one last time, feeling for the seam she'd cut and resealed with polymer so fine it would read as factory work to any scanner short of a forensic bench. Inside, nested against the corpse's spine where the cold would keep the shunts stable, sat forty-one neural canisters — forty-one human lives peeled out of their owners' skulls and rolled up like blueprints. A widow's thirty years of marriage, sold by a husband who wanted to marry again without the weight. A miner's last descent into the Kestrel shafts, bought by an insurance investigator who didn't trust anyone's testimony but the dead's. A child's first six years, purchased by a woman who could not carry one of her own and wanted the experience secondhand, grief and all.

Astrid didn't judge them. Judging was for people who had never looked at their own inventory and wondered which pieces they'd sell if the price got high enough.

She sealed the lid. The magnetic clasp sighed shut like a held breath released.

"Transit manifest," said the dockhand at the bay door, not looking up from his slate. He was young, bored, and had the particular slouch of someone paid to notice things he preferred not to.

"Reyes, Tomas. Remains transfer, necropolis tier three." She passed the slate across. "Cold-chain certified. You want to open it, you'll need a coroner's seal and a very good reason."

The dockhand glanced at the temperature readout strapped to the coffin's flank. Minus eighty, steady green. Nobody liked opening cold-chain cargo. The thaw ruined the remains, the paperwork took four hours, and the smell — even frozen, the smell when the lid came off — stayed in your sinuses for a shift. He stamped it.

"Necropolis elevator's on your chip. Next."

That was all it ever was. Next. Eleven years running the dark lanes between Halcyon's rings, and Astrid had learned that the entire architecture of civilization — every checkpoint, every scanner, every law — rested on one universal truth: nobody wants to look inside the box. Not the box with the body, not the box with the past, not the box behind anyone's eyes. People wanted the boxes sealed and the manifests stamped and the next thing to come along.

She walked the coffin onto the freight lift herself, because letting anyone else touch it was how shipments died. The lift climbed through Halcyon's strata — the underdecks first, all steam-heat and rust-bloom and the sound of too many lives compressed into too little space; then the mid-rings, where the light turned corporate and the corridors smelled of recycled citrus; then the customs ring itself, a band of white corridors and polite violence where the station decided what its people were allowed to remember.

The scanner arch over the customs gate swept the coffin with a fan of pale light. Astrid stood beside it with her hands folded, wearing the expression she'd practiced in a mirror until it stopped being an expression and became a face: mild, tired, faintly annoyed at the delay. Grief's administrative cousin.

*Neural activity:* the scanner's readout blinked, projected in soft amber above the arch. *None. Biological: consistent with post-mortem cryostasis.*

Forty-one souls, and the machine saw nothing. Memory had no mass, no heat signature, no shadow. It was the perfect contraband — stolen goods that weighed nothing, fit anywhere, and could be carried inside anything, even a box already full of someone.

The customs officer on duty barely raised his eyes. "Necropolis tier?"

"Three."

"Condolences." He waved her through, already turning to the next traveler, a woman with a shrieking toddler and a suitcase full of whatever people smuggled when they were amateurs.

Astrid pushed the coffin down the white corridor toward the necropolis spur, and only when the doors closed behind her and the air changed — colder, quieter, smelling of frost and long silence — did she let her shoulders drop a centimeter. One centimeter. She never let them drop further than that. Habit was a survival trait.

* * *

The necropolis tiers were Halcyon's oldest lie. Officially, they were storage: forty levels of vaulted cold where the colony kept its dead because the surface couldn't spare the soil and the founders hadn't budgeted for grief. Unofficially, they were the quietest real estate on the station, and quiet was currency. Astrid rented a corner of tier nine under a shell company named for a flower that had gone extinct before she was born. There, in a locker chilled to the same patient minus eighty as the coffins, she kept the archive.

Ten thousand memories. Give or take. She'd stopped counting precisely somewhere around year six, because counting made them feel like inventory and they weren't, quite. They were people. Compressed, decanted, sealed in canisters of spun diamond lattice, but people — every one of them a whole stretch of somebody's one and only life, extracted and bottled and waiting in the dark for a buyer or a return or, in most cases, nothing at all. The trade had a dirty secret nobody in it talked about: most stolen memories were never resold. They were just taken, the way conquerors took statues, because taking was the point. Power over another person's past was the only luxury that never depreciated.

Astrid slid the forty-one new canisters into their cradles. Each one hummed faintly against her fingertips — not sound, exactly, but the ghost of it, the lattice resonating with the density of what it held. Forty-one lives. She logged them in her ledger under cipher, then closed the locker and stood a moment in the cold with her breath fogging, listening to the vaults.

The dead of Halcyon made a sound, if you listened long enough. A vast, subsonic whisper of cooling systems and settling frost, like the station breathing in its sleep. She'd come to find it honest. Nothing on the living decks was honest. Down here, everything was exactly what it claimed to be: finished, sealed, at rest.

Her wristband chimed. Payment confirmation from the widow-buyer, funds clearing through three shells before landing somewhere she could spend them. The job was done. Clean run, clean money, no complications.

So why did she stand there another full minute, forehead nearly touching the locker's frosted steel, feeling like the loneliest woman ever to orbit a dying star?

Because this was the part of the job nobody put in the brochures. You spent your career carrying other people's most precious possessions — their weddings, their wars, their children's faces — and at the end of every run you locked them in a freezer and went home to a cabin with one chair. Trust the cargo. Never trust the client, never trust the fence, never trust the law, and never, ever trust yourself past what you could verify on a manifest. That was the code. It had kept her alive through two price-hunts and one betrayal she refused to think about, the memory of it filed away so thoroughly she sometimes suspected she'd sold it to herself just to be rid of the weight.

She checked the time. Ninety minutes until the beacon window.

Her mood lifted — not much, but measurably, the way a room brightens when a cloud moves off a lamp. She took the maintenance lift up alone.

* * *

Navigation Beacon 7-Kilo hung off the station's trailing edge like a lantern nailed to the night.

Officially, it belonged to no one. Its commissioning records had been scrubbed — not lost, scrubbed, wiped from the registry with a thoroughness that itself should have been a red flag, though Astrid had stopped asking questions about it years ago. The array was old-generation hardware, pre-dating the current administration, pre-dating half the station's hull. Every other beacon in Halcyon's web answered to Traffic Control, logged its faults, drew its power from the grid. 7-Kilo answered to nothing. It burned on some private, ancient authorization, drawing current from a line whose origin the station engineers had given up tracing, and once a week a woman came out in a borrowed utility sled to tend it, and no one stopped her because no one remembered she needed stopping.

Astrid had inherited the ritual the way you inherit anything true: without explanation. Seven years ago she'd found the beacon flickering during a smuggling run gone quiet, fixed a cracked insulator because a dead nav light meant dead smugglers, and something in the work had hooked into her and never let go. Now it was hers. The one relationship in her life with no ledger attached.

She docked the sled at the array's service collar and stepped out into the open dark. Halcyon wheeled below her, ten thousand windows of warm light, the whole noisy experiment of humanity in one slow spin. Above and beyond, the stars did what stars do — nothing, beautifully, forever.

"Evening," she said to the beacon. She always said it. Talking to machinery was a habit she'd have mocked in anyone else.

The console inside the service blister woke at her touch, amber text crawling up glass older than she was. Diagnostics first: emitter alignment, phase variance, power draw. Everything nominal except the same small wrongness that was always there, the one she'd learned to live with the way you live with a scar — the clock drift. Beacon 7-Kilo's internal chronometer lost exactly 0.003 seconds per day against station time. Not failing. Counting something else. She'd measured it a hundred ways and never found the reference it was counting toward, and eventually she'd done what she did with all unanswerable questions: filed it, sealed it, and kept working.

She replaced a degraded relay, re-seated a connector furred with vacuum-frost, and ran her calibration sequence with hands that knew the motions better than her own heartbeat. This was the hour of her week when she was neither smuggler nor cynic nor orphan of her own missing history. She was a keeper. The word surfaced from nowhere whenever she worked the beacon — *keeper* — and she'd learned not to examine it, the way you don't probe a tooth that has decided, for now, to stop hurting.

Halfway through the calibration, she paused. Old habit: she pulled up the beacon's maintenance log, the local one, stored in the array's own memory where no registry wipe could reach it.

Seven years of her entries, neat and ciphered. And above them, older entries, timestamped from years she did not recognize — years she had, apparently, been alive, though she could produce no memory of them. Entries in a maintenance style identical to her own. Notation conventions she used. Abbreviations she'd invented, or thought she'd invented, alone in this blister with no one watching.

She'd found these logs before. She looked at them every week. And every week the same vertigo rose in her and the same discipline pushed it back down: *someone copied my style. Someone trained here before me. Coincidence.* The explanations were getting threadbare, seven years threadbare, and she knew it, and she sealed the question anyway, because the alternative was to sit in this cold blister and admit that the person she trusted least in the universe was the woman standing in it.

"You're drifting," she told the beacon, and keyed the trim adjustment, and let the work save her again.

When she finished, she lingered. She always lingered. She poured coffee from the sled's thermos and drank it looking out at the station, and for a few minutes she allowed herself the fantasy she never voiced: that someday the beacon would log something other than drift and dust. A ship in distress she could guide home. A signal worth answering. Something that would make seven years of tending mean more than competence and loneliness in equal measure.

The beacon pulsed its steady rhythm out into the dark. Light, light, light, patient as breathing.

Below her, Halcyon glittered, and beneath its glitter, in the mid-ring residential warrens, forty thousand people were going to sleep — and among them, though Astrid Eastwick could not possibly have known it yet, forty-one children were closing their eyes and beginning, all together, in perfect synchrony, to dream.

But that was tomorrow's problem. Tonight there was only the beacon, the cold, the coffee, and the small dishonest comfort of a mystery successfully postponed.

She stowed the thermos, sealed the blister, and cast off from the array. Behind her, 7-Kilo burned on against the black, keeping a count no one understood, holding a door no one remembered building, waiting — with more patience than any machine should own — for its key to start turning.
