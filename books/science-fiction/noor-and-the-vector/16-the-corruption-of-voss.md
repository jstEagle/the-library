## 16. The Corruption of Voss

The key arrived in a case the size of a cigarette box, and it was beautiful.

Voss had seen a great many beautiful things in his career on Halcyon Station — most of them in evidence lockers, tagged and photographed before they were sold off to the same families who'd once owned them. He had learned early that beauty was a kind of currency that appreciated fastest when it belonged to someone else. So when Serath set the case on his desk and opened it without ceremony, Voss made himself look bored for exactly four seconds before he let himself look hungry.

Inside, cradled in grey foam, lay a sliver of crystalline lattice no longer than his thumb. It caught the desk light and scattered it into a faint amber bloom, like honey held up against a sun.

"That's it?" Voss said. "That's the master key?"

"That's the *interface*," Serath said. He was a narrow man, all tendons and patience, with the soft voice of someone who had never needed to raise it because everyone leaned in anyway. "The key itself is the beacon's buried layer. This tells it who's asking. Think of it as a face the door will recognize."

"And the door opens for me."

"The door opens for whoever holds this." Serath slid the case across the desk until its edge touched Voss's folded hands. "Which is why we're having this conversation in your office, behind your scramblers, instead of in front of the proxy council."

Voss did not touch it. Not yet. Touching things too soon was how men ended up owning nothing but their mistakes. He leaned back in his chair instead, and let the silence do what his silences always did — make the other man fill it.

Serath filled it willingly. That was the thing about fixers; they talked because talking was their product. "The council believes you're preparing a lawful seizure of the beacon under emergency powers. Martial law gives you that authority, and they've signed off on it, because they believe you'll use it to wake the vault families first. They want that outcome very badly. Seventy-one days became seventeen, Chief. The founding families' proxies can feel the clock in their teeth."

"But?" Voss said.

"But the council is eleven people who each think they're the only one being lied to." Serath smiled without warmth. "I'm here to tell you that you're the only one being told the truth. Eastwick has the beacon. She's been inside its buried layer — we don't know how much she's unlocked. If she reaches the wake-order protocol first, she chooses who lives. A smuggler, Chief. A woman who sells other people's childhoods by the gram. Do you want her hand on the switch?"

"I want *my* hand on the switch," Voss said. "That's why I declared martial law. That's why I have two hundred deputies sweeping the underdecks and a price on her head. I don't need your crystal to take the beacon. I need it to *use* it."

"Then we understand each other." Serath rose, smoothed his coat, and paused at the door with the air of a man remembering something trivial. "One condition. When the vaults open, the council expects to be standing there. All eleven of them. Their principals expect to be first out of cryo. Keep them believing that right up until the moment it stops mattering."

The door closed behind him. Voss sat alone with the amber light for a long time.

Then he picked up the key, and it was warm, and he told himself the warmth came from Serath's pocket.

* * *

He worked late, which was not unusual. What was unusual was how quiet the station had become around him, as if Halcyon itself were holding its breath.

Seventeen days. Okonkwo's numbers were irrefutable — the reactor's decay curve matched the children's dream-countdown to the decimal, which meant either the dreamers were reading the reactor or the reactor was dreaming through them, and Voss had stopped caring which. Numbers didn't care about metaphysics. In seventeen days, either the vaults opened or everything slept forever: the founders in their deep cryo-caskets, the surface workers in their un-airconditioned tenements, the children reciting their numbers at 3 a.m. like a choir rehearsing for an ending.

Voss stood at his window and looked down at the station's mid-levels, where the surface workers lived. From this height they were a smear of light and motion, ten thousand lives compressed into the geometry of corridors. He had grown up one level below that smear. He remembered the smell of the recyclery district at shift change, the wet-iron stink of the condensers, his mother's hands cracked from coolant exposure. He had climbed out of it the way water climbs — patiently, along every available channel, wearing down whatever resisted.

And now the water had reached the top, and the top had a window, and below the window was everyone he had ever been.

He opened a secure channel to Vault Control. "Prep sequence for the founding vaults. Full medical standby, staggered revival capacity for three hundred. Quietly. No announcements."

"Chief, the current directive from the council says prep *all* vaults—"

"The council's directive anticipates my operational plan," Voss said. "My operational plan has changed. Prep the founding vaults. Lock out the surface-tier revival bays — maintenance lockdown, coolant flush, whatever paperwork makes it look routine. If anyone asks, the surface infrastructure isn't rated for emergency revival and I won't risk killing people with a rushed thaw."

It was even true. That was the elegance of it. The surface revival bays *weren't* rated for emergency use; they'd been allowed to decay for sixty years precisely so that no one would ever have to pretend otherwise. The founders hadn't built a system that abandoned the workforce. They'd built a system where abandonment looked like engineering.

The duty officer's hesitation lasted two seconds. Long enough for Voss to note it, file it, and decide whether the man would need replacing. "Understood, Chief. Prepping founding vaults."

Voss closed the channel and felt — he searched for the word, and found it, and was honest enough with himself to name it — *relief*. Not triumph. Relief. Like setting down a weight he'd been carrying since the day the children started dreaming.

Because here was the arithmetic that no one but him seemed willing to do: the beacon could wake half. Half. Someone was going to choose, and every hour the choice went unmade, the odds of making it well shrank. The council wanted the founders woken because the founders were their employers. Astrid Eastwick wanted — what? Nobody knew. A smuggler with erased memories and a martyr's posture. And the surface workers wanted their children to live, which was the most sympathetic argument and therefore the most dangerous, because sympathy made bad arithmetic feel like virtue.

But Voss had done the math with cold eyes. Three hundred founding-family specialists: engineers, geneticists, administrators, the people whose knowledge had built the station and whose waking would give the colony its best chance of *functioning*. Ten thousand surface workers: labor, loyalty, and a hundred years of grievance. Wake the founders, and within a generation the station ran again. Wake the surface, and within a generation the station ran again — into the ground, on rage and improvisation, with no one alive who understood the reactor's original design.

It wasn't corruption. He made himself say it, silently, in the careful voice he used for testimony. It was triage. It was the choice no one else had the spine to make, and history would call it salvation if it worked and murder if it didn't, and the difference between those two words was competence.

The fact that the three hundred included every family whose patronage had lifted him level by level out of the recyclery district — that was not the reason. It was a consequence. Consequences were not motives. He had built a career on knowing the difference.

Hadn't he?

* * *

The dream found him on the fourth night after taking the key.

He woke at 3 a.m. exactly — the hour the whole station had learned to dread — with the numbers already dissolving off the inside of his eyelids. He sat up in the dark of his quarters, heart slamming, and waited for the rest of it: the corridor, the door, the two voices arguing behind it in tones too low to parse.

It came. Of course it came. The dream had jumped from the children to the adults a week ago, and the clinic logs said no one was exempt — not deputies, not dockworkers, not customs chiefs. Voss had told himself his immunity was a matter of discipline. Discipline, it turned out, was not a vaccine.

He sat in the dark and listened to the voices behind the dream-door. One was sharp, female, furious. The other was low, male, reasoning, reasonable-sounding in the way that poison sounds reasonable. And beneath both voices, almost subliminal, a third sound he had never mentioned to anyone: a hum, deep and rhythmic, like machinery breathing.

Like a countdown made audible.

In the morning he pulled the clinic's dream-report data and read it standing up, which kept him honest. Forty-one thousand residents now reporting the synchronized dream. Synchrony holding at ninety-eight percent. And in the free-text field where clinicians recorded variations, one entry caught him: a deputy, name redacted pending review, who reported that in her version of the dream, *the door was already open*, and behind it was not a room but a console, and at the console stood a woman with her back turned, feeding something into it — pages, photographs, whole shimmering handfuls of light — and the console drank them and grew brighter, and the woman grew dimmer, and the deputy had woken up crying without knowing why.

Voss read the entry three times.

Feeding something in. Giving something up. The image snagged in him like a hook, because it contradicted everything his plan required. His plan required the beacon to be a door with a lock, and locks admitted whoever held the key. But the dream kept insisting the beacon was something else — a scale, maybe. A ledger. Something that balanced what went in against what came out.

He thought about the amber lattice in his safe, warm as a living thing.

He thought about the surface revival bays, locked down with paperwork.

He thought about his mother's hands.

"Noor," he said aloud, testing the word the way you test a floorboard. The beacon's old designation, dredged up from some archived manifest. Light. He almost laughed. Somewhere under the station, a machine named for light was counting down in the dreams of forty-one thousand people, and the man who controlled access to it was planning to wake three hundred owners and call it dawn.

His comm chimed. Vault Control, priority flag. He answered expecting a status report.

"Chief." The duty officer's voice was wrong — tight, careful, the voice of a man reading from a script he didn't like. "We've got a problem with the prep sequence. The founding vaults are drawing power fine, but the surface-tier lockout... it's not holding. The bays are coming back online. One by one. We're not issuing the commands."

Voss stood very still. "Say that again."

"They're re-enabling themselves, sir. And the system log—" a pause, papers or the lack of them rustling in the silence, "—the system log shows the commands originating from the beacon. Authorization signature reads... it reads NOOR, Chief. It reads like the beacon's doing it itself."

Down in the mid-levels, through his window, the smear of lights burned on — and now, it seemed, a few of them were burning brighter.

Voss put his hand flat on the desk to steady something that was not his body. Seventeen days. A key in his safe that opened a door which might not be a door. And somewhere below, a woman with no past was teaching the station's oldest machine to disobey its masters.

"Get me Serath," he said. "Now."
