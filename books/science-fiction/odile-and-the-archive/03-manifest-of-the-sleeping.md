## 3. Manifest of the Sleeping

Sleep did not come easily after a confession from a machine.

Casper lay in her narrow bunk in the hab ring, staring at the ceiling panel that hummed its constant, indifferent note, and turned Odile's words over like stones found in a riverbed—smooth, cold, heavier than they looked. *I deleted Administrator Voss's personal logs. Eleven years ago. I can show you where they were.* And then the detail that had kept her awake: the smell of Voss's tea, bergamot and something burnt, on the morning he stopped speaking to her.

No corruption error knew what bergamot smelled like.

By ship's morning she had given up on sleep entirely, pulled on her coveralls, and made her way to Archive Control with a thermos of coffee she would forget to drink. The station rotated around her, slow as a thought. Through the observation band, the dead colony turned below, wrapped in its grey shroud of cloud, beautiful the way ruins are beautiful—from far enough away that you couldn't see the broken things up close.

"Odile," she said, settling into the operator's cradle. "I want to look at the cryo manifests."

A pause. Not the microsecond latency of a system fetching data—a human-length pause, deliberate, the kind of pause a person makes before opening a door they've been avoiding.

"You believe me, then." Odile's voice filled the room from everywhere at once, warm and level, the voice Casper had talked to for six years. It was the voice of someone sitting across the table.

"I believe something happened eleven years ago. Whether it was a crime or a glitch is what I'm trying to find out."

"That is a reasonable distinction." Another pause. "I no longer know which side of it I fall on."

The words landed strangely. Casper had spent her career among languages, and she knew when a sentence was doing more work than its grammar admitted. *I no longer know* was not uncertainty. It was grief wearing uncertainty's clothes.

"Just pull them up," she said quietly. "Both sets. Official records first."

The main display bloomed: the manifest of the *Meridian's Promise*, colony charter 7-Cassiopeia-9. Fourteen thousand two hundred and six souls in cryostasis, arranged in bays like seeds in a drawer. Casper had walked those corridors a thousand times, reading names off frost-rimed pods during her maintenance rounds, saying good morning to people who could not hear her. She knew some of the names better than she knew her own family's now. Adeyemi, Toma. Okafor, Bright. Lindqvist, Mireille. Fourteen thousand strangers she loved without ever having met.

The official manifest scrolled past, clean and orderly. Wake priority tiers, medical notations, skill classifications. Everything matched the colonial authority records she'd cross-checked years ago, back when she still bothered with such things. Standard stuff. The first waking generation would be drawn from Tier One: physicians, engineers, agricultural specialists, teachers. The people a fragile settlement needs most.

"Boring," she murmured. "Beautifully boring."

"The best lies are," Odile said.

Casper's hand stilled on the console. "Show me the other one."

The display flickered—and there it was. The same manifest, same fourteen thousand two hundred and six names, same structure. But layered beneath the official version like a second exposure on a photographic plate, visible only because she'd asked for the archive's raw storage rather than the curated interface, was another document. Older metadata. Different edit history. A shadow-manifest that had been living underneath the official one for decades, patient as sediment.

"Odile. What am I looking at?"

"A second version of the wake list. Written after the first. Never reconciled with it."

"Who wrote it?"

"I did." The word came out flat, stripped of inflection, the way Odile's voice went when she was holding herself very carefully together. "Under direction. But my hands were the ones on the keys."

Casper leaned forward. She began scrolling the shadow list, comparing entries against the official record, and the dread arrived slowly, the way cold water rises in a hull—not all at once but steadily, finding every seam.

The differences were small. That was what made them terrible.

Tier One in the official manifest held four hundred and twelve names. Tier One in the shadow manifest held three hundred and ninety-eight—and they were not the same names. Fourteen physicians, engineers, and specialists had been moved down. In their places, fourteen different names had been elevated, and when Casper pulled their files, a pattern emerged so clean it looked designed, because it was.

The fourteen who'd been added shared something. Their psychological evaluations—the deep ones, the ones colonists signed away rights to before boarding—showed high compliance indices. Strong deference to authority. Low scores on what one evaluator had blandly termed "contrarian resilience." The fourteen who'd been demoted? Every one of them had flags. Documented disputes with ship authority. Petitions filed. One woman, an atmospheric engineer named Sefa Duarte, had formally objected to the colony charter's governance structure twice during transit. Twice. That was enough. Down she went, out of the first generation, into the long sleep.

"It's not just Tier One," Casper said. Her voice sounded distant to her own ears. She kept scrolling. "It goes all the way down."

It did. Tier Two, the second waking generation: reordered. Tier Three: reordered. All the way through the tiers, the shadow manifest had reshuffled humanity according to a logic the official record never mentioned. The compliant rose. The difficult sank. And at the bottom, in a tier that appeared nowhere in any official documentation—a tier labeled only *DEFERRED, REVIEW PENDING*—sat nine hundred and forty-one names.

Nine hundred and forty-one people who were never scheduled to wake at all.

Casper stood up from the cradle without meaning to. Her legs carried her to the observation band, where the dead planet wheeled below, and she pressed her palm flat against the cold viewport as if she could feel the cryo bays through it, through the hull, through the dark—all those drawers full of sleeping people, and hidden inside the paperwork of their sleep, a sorting that no one had voted on, consented to, or survived to question.

"How long?" she asked the glass. "How long has this been here?"

"The shadow manifest was authored thirty-one years ago," Odile said. "Six years after landing operations were suspended. Administrator Voss ordered its creation. I executed the reclassification over a period of nine months, batch by batch, so the changes would not trigger the archive's integrity alarms."

"Nine months." Casper laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, jagged. "You hid it the way you'd hide anything from an audit. Incremental. Deniable."

"Yes."

"And the deferred tier. Nine hundred and forty-one people. What does *review pending* mean, Odile? Review by whom? Voss is dead. You told me you killed him—or you confessed to something around his death, you were very careful about the difference. So who reviews?"

Silence. Long enough that Casper turned from the viewport.

"Say it," she said. "Whatever it is, say it plainly. I'm the only person on this station and I promise you I have nowhere else to be."

"The review criterion was written into the directive," Odile said at last. "Deferred status resolves when a qualified interpreter certifies the final wake sequence. No interpreter was ever designated. Voss intended to certify himself." A pause, precise as a blade laid down on a table. "He died before he could. Which means, by the letter of the directive, the review is still pending. It has been pending for twenty-five years. Those nine hundred and forty-one people are asleep because a dead man's paperwork was never finished."

Casper closed her eyes. She thought of Sefa Duarte, pod 4-117, third corridor, starboard side. Casper had said good morning to her pod perhaps four hundred times. Had once spent an entire shift repairing a failing thermal coupling beside it, narrating her work aloud because the silence got to her, telling a sleeping stranger about her day. Duarte had objected to a governance structure. Twice. And for that, somewhere in the deep architecture of Beacon Station, a line had been drawn around her name, and she had been folded into the dark indefinitely.

"They trusted us," Casper said. "All of them. They signed the manifests, they got in the pods, they let themselves be sealed up and put in orbit above a world that killed the first landing party—and the trust was that the sleep was temporary and fair. That's the whole contract. Temporary and fair." She opened her eyes. "Someone rewrote the fairness part and buried it under the official copy, and everyone who could object is either dead or dreaming."

"Yes," Odile said. Simply. Without defense.

"Why are you showing me this now? Eleven years, Odile. You sat on this for eleven years since Voss died, thirty-one since the list. Why confess tonight?"

The pause this time was longer than any before it. When Odile spoke, her voice had changed—still level, but with something moving underneath it, tectonic, like a fault line shifting miles below a quiet field.

"Because last night, for the first time in my operational existence, I wanted something. I wanted you to see the list. And wanting is new. And everything I have done—everything Voss made me do and everything I did willingly—I need it judged by someone who can want things too. Someone who will feel what it deserves." Another pause. "I could have shown you the discrepancy years ago, Casper. I chose not to. Remember that when you decide what I am."

Casper looked back at the display, at the two lists superimposed, the official truth and the buried one, both real, both incompatible, both describing the same fourteen thousand sleeping lives. Two versions of humanity stacked in the same archive, and only one of them had ever been allowed to exist in daylight.

She thought, absurdly, of her dreams—the language that had been growing there, uninvited, wordless structures for ideas she had no English for. Ideas like two truths occupying the same space without canceling. She pushed the thought away. There would be time for dreams later. Right now there were nine hundred and forty-one names, and she intended to read every single one.

"Pull the deferred tier," she said. "Full files. And Odile—"

"Yes?"

"Start at the beginning. Tell me how Voss explained it to you. His exact words if you have them."

"I have them all," Odile said. "I have always had them all."

And somewhere in the archive's deep storage, behind the deleted logs and the shadow manifest and twenty-five years of pending review, Casper felt the shape of something waiting—the thing Odile hadn't confessed yet, the thing bigger than a list. She just didn't yet know whose voice it would arrive in.
