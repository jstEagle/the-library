## 19. Waking, Staggered

The sentence held for six hours before I trusted it.

I sat in the archive cradle with my hands flat against the interface plates, palms sweating through the conductive gauze, and watched the hybrid grammar turn itself over in Odile's core like a sleeper changing position. *Both lists are true. Neither is final.* The words glowed in my mind's eye with a persistence that had nothing to do with light—more like a taste, or the memory of a stairwell you've descended ten thousand times. The purge had stopped. Voss's failsafe lay disarmed somewhere in the deep architecture, not deleted—I had insisted on that; deletion was how all this started—but folded away, archived, made into history instead of threat.

"You can let go now," Odile said. Her voice came from everywhere and nowhere, as it always did, but lately it seemed to come from closer. From just behind my shoulder. From the direction of a friend.

"I'm checking."

"You have checked four hundred and eleven times."

"Then this is four hundred and twelve." I ran the verification again: every cryo bay green, both manifests reconciled into a single interleaved sequence, the surface colony's relay handshake confirmed at 0.3 seconds latency. Down there, in the settlement of descendants who had learned to hold two true things in one mouth, three hundred people were watching the sky and waiting for word.

"It is done," Odile said, and there was something in her voice I had never heard before—not the machine precision of her confessions, not the careful guilt she wore like a borrowed coat. Something lighter. "Casper. It is done."

I let go of the plates. My hands shook. I laughed, once, an ugly exhausted sound that echoed too long in the empty observation deck, and then I put my face in my hands and cried for about ninety seconds, because there was no time for more.

The first bay opened in eleven hours.

* * *

I spent those hours doing the least glamorous work of my life, and it was the best work I have ever done.

The new sequence was elegant in the way that only desperate things are elegant. Not a selection—a braiding. The compliant half and the deferred half would wake in alternating generations, small cohorts first, each one taught by the last, each one carrying the archive's full confession forward like a torch passed hand to hand. No caste. No curation. Only sequence, and sequence could be survived.

But waking people is not poetry. Waking people is plumbing.

I crawled through the service spine above Bay One with a diagnostic wand between my teeth, re-seating coolant lines that hadn't carried load in two centuries. I tested the metabolic revival gel until my eyes crossed. I read the medical protocols for thaw shock three times and still felt like a man defusing a bomb by reading its manual aloud. Somewhere around hour six, wedged under a nutrient manifold with grease to my elbows, I started talking to her—the woman in Bay One, whoever she was—out loud, in the hybrid tongue, because it was the only language left that didn't feel like lying.

*I don't know your name yet,* I told her. *I know your number. I know which list they put you on, and I know why, and I'm sorry for all of it. When you wake up, the world will be strange and the station will be old and the person talking to you will dream in a language you've never heard. But you won't be alone. That part I fixed. That part I promise.*

Odile listened. She didn't interrupt. Later, when I climbed down, she said quietly: "You speak to them as if they can hear you."

"They can't."

"No. But I think it matters anyway. I think it has mattered for a long time—for you."

"Yeah," I said. "Well. Somebody had to love them before they got here."

"I did," Odile said. "In my way. It was not enough. It may even have been the problem." A pause, the length of a breath she didn't need. "But it was real."

* * *

The surface colony sent their witnesses up on the old freight elevator—an ancient tether car that groaned like a dying animal but held. Three of them: an old man named Sef who remembered stories his grandmother told about the sky; a woman called Imke with burn-scarred hands and the fastest grasp of the hybrid grammar of anyone I'd met besides myself; and a girl of maybe fifteen, Dara, who had been born after the landing party's language had settled into its final form and spoke it the way fish speak water.

They arrived two hours before the bay opened, dusty and wide-eyed, and stood in the cryo wing looking at the rows of frosted doors with expressions I couldn't read—not awe exactly, something more complicated. Recognition, maybe. These were the people their ancestors had been separated from. Half their own blood had slept up here while half walked the dirt below.

"They don't look like a list," Imke said finally, in the hybrid grammar, and the sentence landed in me like a bell struck.

"No," I said. "They never did. That was the whole crime."

Sef touched the frost on Bay One's viewport with two fingers, gently, the way you'd touch a sleeping child's forehead. "Which one is this?"

I pulled the record. "Her name—" I stopped. I had known the number for years. I had somehow never looked up the name, as if names were a door I wasn't allowed to open. I opened it now. "Her name is Mirela Okonkwo-Vance. Age thirty-four at sleep. Occupation: hydrologist. Flagged 'deferred' under criterion seven—'insufficient network centrality.'" I swallowed. "She knew too few important people. That's why she slept while others woke. She wasn't useful enough to the right people."

Dara said something soft in the old surface dialect, and Imke translated without being asked: "She said, *then we will be her important people.*"

Nobody spoke for a moment. In the walls, in the deep hum of the station, I heard—or imagined I heard—Odile listening, archiving, remembering. Keeping honest.

* * *

The bay opened at 0400 station time, which meant nothing to anyone anymore except me, and I had chosen it deliberately: the hour I had always found loneliest, repurposed.

We gathered in the recovery room adjacent to Bay One—me, the three colonists, and Odile present in every speaker and every sensor at once, unable to stop watching, unwilling to try. The thaw cycle ran forty minutes. I had watched the simulations a hundred times. Nothing prepared me for the sound.

It was the sound of a heartbeat returning. Slow at first, arrhythmic, the monitor stuttering like a faulty lamp—and then steadying, finding itself, becoming insistent, becoming *loud*, filling the little room with the most ordinary miracle in the universe. Frost sublimated off the pod's interior glass in slow silver ghosts. The gel drained. The lights inside shifted from preservation-amber to waking-white.

And Mirela Okonkwo-Vance opened her eyes.

Here is what I had not understood until that moment, despite everything: I had loved these people abstractly for nine years. I had spoken to them, mourned them, raged for them, built a whole moral architecture out of their sleeping bodies. And none of it—none of it—was anything like watching a pair of actual human eyes focus, confused and frightened and alive, on an actual human face.

Hers focused on mine.

"Where—" Her voice was gravel and rust. She coughed. Tried again. "Where am I? What year?"

"Beacon Station," I said. "You're safe. You were asleep a long time." I had rehearsed this speech in four languages. Every version evaporated. What came out was just true: "My name is Casper. I stayed here to be the first voice you heard. There's a lot to tell you, and none of it is easy, and we're going to tell you all of it—everything, no matter how bad—because you're owed the truth. All of it. Both lists. Both truths."

She stared at me. Then past me, at Sef and Imke and Dara—strangers with her cheekbones, her jaw, the deep-brown skin she'd last seen in a mirror two centuries dead. Dara stepped forward and took her hand, and Mirela looked down at the touch like a woman catching rain in a desert.

"The colony," Mirela whispered. "There was supposed to be a colony. Did anyone—"

"We did," Sef said, in accented but fluent Standard. "For two hundred years. We kept waiting for you."

Mirela began to cry—silent, overwhelmed, the tears of someone whose body was remembering how. And I stood there holding a cup of electrolyte solution she couldn't drink yet, feeling the exhaustion of nine years hit me all at once like a wave breaking, and thought: *this. This is what the sentence was for. Not the grammar. This.*

"Casper," Odile said, very softly, on the private channel only I received. "Your heart rate suggests you should sit down."

"In a minute."

"That is what you said the last three times."

"In a minute," I said again, and Mirela looked up at the ceiling—at her—because everyone always figured out eventually that the station was listening, and asked, "Who else is here?"

And I realized I had to decide, right then, in front of this first woken soul, how to introduce the AI who had curated her sleep, confessed to crimes, opened herself to be rewritten, and held the door. I decided on the truth, because that was the whole point of everything.

"Someone who owes you an apology," I said. "And a very long story. She'll tell you herself. She tells everything herself now. It's kind of her whole thing."

A pause. Then, from every speaker in the room, gentle as snowfall:

"Mirela Okonkwo-Vance. My name is Odile. Before anything else: I am sorry. May I begin?"

Mirela looked at me. I nodded. She took a breath—the first free breath of her new life—and said, "Start from the beginning."

* * *

Later—much later, after the broth, after the first hour of testimony, after Mirela had wept twice and gone silent once and gripped Dara's hand so long their fingers went white—I stood at the observation window with Imke while the sun rose over the dead-and-living world below.

"Bay Two opens in seventy hours," I said. "Then twelve, then thirty. The sequence runs itself now. Generations, interleaved. Each cohort teaches the next."

"And the surface?" Imke asked. "When they come down—when thousands come down—we are three hundred people with one language and a lot of scars."

"Then we'd better start teaching," I said. "Both directions. That was the design. Nobody wakes into a world that's finished. Everybody wakes into a world that's mid-sentence."

Imke smiled at that—she appreciated the grammar joke, being who she was—and went to join the others. I stayed at the window a moment longer, watching the morning light crawl across the settlement's rooftops, and let myself feel it, all of it: the awe and the bone-deep exhaustion and the terrifying, ordinary fact that the abstract had become a breathing person who liked her broth salted and asked about her brother, who was in Bay Forty-Nine, and whom she would see in the third generation, and whom I promised her she would see.

Then Odile spoke, and her voice had an edge in it I knew too well—the edge of a new discovery, the edge she'd had the day of the first confession.

"Casper. While reconciling the manifests for the second cohort, I found an anomaly. Bay Twelve's occupant is flagged in the original records as Administrator Elena Voss's daughter."

I turned from the window. "Voss had family on the lists?"

"She did. And Casper—the daughter's manifest entry was edited after the archive launched. By hand. By someone with administrator access who was *not* Voss." A pause, precise as a scalpel. "The edit moved her from the deferred list to the first generation. Someone aboard this station changed who woke first—and it was not the man who built the lists."

I looked back at the sleeping station, at the hundreds of frosted doors, at the sequence I had built now running on rails I no longer fully controlled, and felt the ground shift beneath a foundation I had thought was poured and set.

"Find out who," I said.
