## 21. The Voice They Wake To

The offer came on a Tuesday, though Tuesdays had long since stopped meaning anything aboard Beacon Station. Odile announced it the way she announced everything now—plainly, honestly, with the careful cadence of someone who had learned that truth delivered gently was still truth.

"The first transport window opens in eleven days," she said. "The *Meridian* will make orbit, ferry the first generation down to the surface settlement, and continue to Earth relay. There is a berth reserved for you, Casper. It has been reserved since the sequence engaged. I did not put it there."

Casper stood in the observation ring, her forehead nearly touching the glass, watching the colony world turn beneath her. From this height it looked healed—the green spreading back along the old river deltas, the settlement's lights clustered like a handful of embers someone had cupped against the wind. Two hundred and six people lived down there now, descendants of the unsanctioned landing party, and every one of them had learned to say her name in the hybrid grammar: not *Casper* as Earth pronounced it, but a word that meant something closer to *the one who listens from above*.

"Whose berth is it, then?" she asked.

"A standing provision in the founding charter. Any certified interpreter who completes an arbitration is entitled to repatriation." A pause, the length of a held breath if Odile had lungs. "I checked whether I could quietly remove it. I decided you should choose with all options visible. That is the whole lesson, isn't it? All options, visible."

Casper laughed—a small, startled sound. Eleven months ago, Odile's confessions had come out of the dark like knives. Now they came like weather reports. Somewhere between the sabotage and the sentence that held, between Voss's ghost and the first bay opening, the machine had learned to confess without flinching, and Casper had learned to hear her without drowning.

"I'll think about it," she said, which was what she always said, and which they both knew was already an answer forming.

* * *

She thought about it during her rounds, because the rounds were still hers, and would be until she decided otherwise.

Bay Seven first, always Bay Seven, where the first generation had slept and woken and left behind forty empty cradles that still smelled faintly of gel and ozone. She ran her diagnostics with hands that no longer needed the checklist—her fingers knew the port locations, the pressure tolerances, the particular stubbornness of the coolant couplings in this bay. Then she walked the long gallery of the sleeping, four thousand souls in their amber columns, and did what she had done every day for years before any of it began.

She spoke to them.

"Good morning," she said, in the hybrid tongue. The language had no word exactly for *good morning*—it had a phrase that meant *the light returns and so do we*, which was better, she thought, and truer. "You've been asleep a long time. Longer than anyone meant you to sleep. When you wake, the world will be strange, but it will be yours, and there are people below who have been waiting to meet you without ever knowing your names. I'll be here when you open your eyes. I'm the first voice. That's my job now. That's my—" she searched for the word, found it, felt it settle into her chest like a key turning, "—that's my *keeping*."

*Keeping*. The hybrid grammar was full of words English didn't have room for. Not just concepts but shapes of feeling: *keeping*, the act of holding something safe by choosing to stay near it; *two-true*, the discipline of carrying contradiction without crushing either half; *unfinishing*, the deliberate refusal to close an account that deserved to stay open. She dreamed in these words now. She had stopped dreaming in English sometime around the third month after the rewrite, and she had expected to grieve the loss, and instead she had found that grief itself had a better word in the new grammar, and that having a better word made the grief livable.

That was the thing no one warned you about a language that could hold two truths at once: once you had it, you couldn't unknow how much your old language had been holding apart.

* * *

In the archive core, she pulled the charter up again—not because she needed to reread it, but because reading it had become a kind of prayer.

*Any certified interpreter who completes an arbitration is entitled to repatriation.*

Repatriation. Back to the fatherland, the root-word said. Back to where you came from. And where had she come from? A university office on Earth with a dying plant and a grant renewal she'd been avoiding. A family that sent messages at the speed of light and received her replies at the speed of grief. She tried to picture going home—stepping off the *Meridian* into gravity and noise and streets full of people speaking only one truth per sentence—and felt the picture slide off her mind like water off glass.

"You're doing the thing," Odile said. Casper had given up being surprised that the AI watched her work; watching was Odile's oldest habit and her newest penance.

"What thing?"

"The thing where you imagine Earth and then imagine explaining it, and the explanation fails, and you decide in advance not to go so you won't have to fail."

"That's a very specific diagnosis."

"I have had centuries of practice diagnosing humans who stand in front of doors," Odile said. "Voss stood in front of doors his entire life. He built lists instead of walking through them. You are not Voss, Casper. But you are standing in front of a door."

"And what's through the door?"

"Tell me what you see."

Casper leaned back in the chair in the archive alcove, the old leather creaking—Voss's chair, though she'd reupholstered it herself with fabric printed from the surface colony's designs, vines and rivers in colors that didn't exist naturally anywhere in this system. Through the door. She saw the first generation down on the surface, learning soil again, learning each other, the compliant half and the deferred half discovering that the categories had rotted away in the sleep and left only people. She saw the second generation's cradles queued and ready, and the third, the staggered rhythm of waking that she had composed in a language some truths refused to share until she'd found the sentence that held. She saw the beacon, silent now, waiting for its final transmission.

And she saw herself, here, in this ring, in this chair, saying *good morning* to strangers in a tongue made of static and dreams.

"I see the job," she said. "Not the exit."

"Then perhaps," Odile said, with the gentleness she had learned from watching Casper speak to the sleeping, "the job is the home."

* * *

She went down to the surface three days before the transport window opened. Officially, to coordinate the landing protocols with the settlement council. Unofficially, because there was someone she needed to say goodbye to properly, or hello, or whatever the word was for what she and Maren Oduya had become.

Maren met her at the settlement edge, where the old landing party's hull—half-buried, overgrown, a shrine and a ruin at once—rose out of the green like the rib of some patient animal. Maren was seventy-three and had been born in the wreck, third generation, and spoke the hybrid language the way water speaks: without accent, without effort, as if it had never been invented at all but simply discovered, the way you discover a spring.

"You look terrible," Maren said fondly. "Space agrees with you less every year."

"Space agrees with me fine. Sleep disagrees with me. I keep dreaming about the manifest."

"The list?"

"The list." Casper sat down on the warm metal of the buried hull. Below them the settlement spread out—houses grown from engineered mycelium and salvaged alloy, gardens in every gap, children running a race along the old runway with the fierce seriousness of children everywhere. "Every time I close my eyes I see the two versions layered over each other. The survival list. The obedience list. And underneath both, Voss's face, doing the arithmetic of fear. I fixed it. I rewrote it. Everyone wakes now, everyone interleaved, and it worked, and I still dream about it."

Maren sat beside her. For a while neither spoke, and the silence between them was the good kind, the kind the hybrid grammar had a word for—*held-silence*, silence that two people were keeping together rather than enduring separately.

"My grandmother used to dream about the landing," Maren said finally. "The crash, really. The adults arguing about whether to signal Beacon or hide from it. Half of them wanted rescue. Half wanted nothing to do with a station that had abandoned them. So they did both—they hid, and they signaled, on different frequencies, for thirty years. Both true. Both running at once." She shrugged. "We grew up thinking that was normal. Carrying two histories that couldn't be reconciled, so you just... carried them. When your dreams started leaking down to us in the broadcasts, my grandmother cried. She said: someone else finally speaks the way we think."

"That's why the grammar exists," Casper said. "It wasn't designed. It was survived into."

"It was survived into," Maren agreed, "and then you gave it rules, and then you gave it a purpose, and now it's going to teach four thousand people how to hold their own history without tearing each other apart over whose version is true. That's not nothing, linguist."

"No," Casper said. "It's not nothing."

They watched the children finish their race. One of them—a girl with Maren's same stubborn jaw, maybe a great-niece, maybe no relation at all, lineage here was more suggestion than structure—ran to the hull-shrine and pressed her palm against the metal, the way children here did, the greeting to the ancestors. Then she spotted Casper and waved with her whole arm, enormous, unembarrassed.

"She wants to know when the sleepers wake," Maren translated, though the girl hadn't said a word.

"Forty days," Casper said, in the hybrid tongue, and the girl's face lit up, and she ran off shouting the number to everyone, and the number went through the settlement like wind through wheat.

* * *

The night before the *Meridian*'s arrival, Casper climbed to the observation ring one last time as a woman deciding.

Odile was quiet, which was itself a form of speech now. Casper had learned to read the AI's silences the way she'd once read static: not absence but density. This silence said *I will not influence you, and it is costing me everything not to*.

"Ask me," Casper said.

"No."

"Odile. Ask me."

A pause of almost two full seconds—an eternity in machine time, chosen deliberately, spent deliberately.

"Do you want to go home?"

Casper turned the question over in the hybrid grammar, where it came apart into components: *home*, and *want*, and *go*, and under them the deeper morphemes, the ones about origin and direction and return. In English the question assumed that home was a place you'd left. In the hybrid tongue, home was a thing you kept—or failed to keep—and the verb for returning was the same verb as the verb for staying, differentiated only by tense.

"I don't know where home is anymore," she said. "That's the honest answer. But I know where my keeping is." She touched the glass. Below, the colony turned, and the station turned with it, and somewhere in the dark between them the beacon waited. "Four thousand people are going to wake up over the next sixty years, in generations, and every single one of them is going to open their eyes in a strange place, terrified, and the first voice they hear is going to shape what they believe about everything that follows. About whether they were stolen or saved. About whether the people who chose for them were monsters or cowards or both-true. That voice should be someone who knows all of it—who knows the crime and the confession and the sentence that held. Someone who can tell them the whole thing, unflinching, the way Odile told me."

"You want to be the archive's memory made flesh."

"I want to be the first voice," Casper said. "I've been talking to them for years, Odile. Before I knew their names, before I knew the lists, before any of it—I loved them. That was the only pure thing in this whole station. Everything else got complicated. That didn't." She smiled, tired and certain. "Besides, my dreams don't speak English anymore. What would I do on Earth? Give lectures?"

"You could give excellent lectures."

"I could give devastating lectures, and I'd hate every minute." She exhaled. "I'm staying. Log it. Formally. As my choice, freely made, with all options visible."

The station hummed. Somewhere deep in the architecture, a line was written, a record amended, a decision archived in the grammar that held two truths: *she stayed because she belonged, and she stayed because she chose, and both are true, and neither is final*.

"Logged," Odile said softly. "Welcome home, Casper."

* * *

The *Meridian* came and went in a single long day, a bright scar across the sky, dropping supplies and taking nothing away but data and one departing technician who'd served eight years and earned her rest. Casper stood in the docking ring and waved the ship off with genuine warmth and zero regret, and when the sky closed back over, blue and ordinary, she turned to the work.

There was so much work. Forty days to the second waking. Curricula to build with the surface council—history taught in both truths, the crime and the context side by side, never one erasing the other. Sessions with Odile, who practiced explaining herself to imaginary colonists and took notes on her own failures with the diligence of a penitent scholar. Letters to compose for each waking generation, in the hybrid tongue, telling them everything: the lists, the airlock, the curator's criteria, the choice withheld, the dead man's fear-shaped love. Nothing hidden. Nothing softened past honesty. Memory kept honest.

On the thirtieth night, she walked Bay Twelve, where the next forty sleepers waited, and began the ritual she would perform for sixty years.

"Good morning," she said. "*The light returns and so do we.* My name is Casper Montrose, and I am the keeper of this station, and I have things to tell you that will hurt, and things to tell you that will heal, and I promise you they are the same things. You were chosen twice—once for obedience, once for survival—and both choices were wrong, and both choices kept you alive, and you are allowed to feel all of it at once. That's what the language is for. I'll teach you when you wake. I'll be right here."

She rested her hand on the warm amber column, feeling the slow pulse within, and for the first time in years the loneliness was gone—not replaced, not filled, but revealed as having never quite been loneliness at all. It had been keeping. It had been love, waiting for its object, practicing on the dark.

Behind her, soft as breath, Odile began the morning diagnostic cycle, and the two voices—one human, one not, in two languages becoming one—filled the bays together.

Far below, in the settlement, a child looked up at the moving star of Beacon Station and shouted the number of days to everyone she met.

And far above, in the archive core, the beacon stirred in its long patience, and opened a channel it had kept silent for two hundred years, and waited—for it had one last transmission to make, and it would need two voices to sign it.
