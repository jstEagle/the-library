## 5. Mother's Handwriting

The sub-basement had a smell, and the smell was her mother's.

That was the thought Beatrix could not shake, kneeling on the cold deck plating with the second transmitter humming above her like a hive that had learned to pray. It was not a literal smell, of course. Elena Vane had been gone for twenty-two years, and whatever scent she had carried — chalk dust and citrus soap, the ozone tang of her soldering iron, the faint sweetness of the tea she always let go cold — had long since been scrubbed from every surface of Relay Station Halcyon, or decayed into the station's neutral hum of recycled air. But the buried room smelled *occupied*. Warm plastic and old insulation, machine oil and something faintly biological, like the breath of a sleeping animal. It smelled like a person had been here, and recently, and had only just stepped out.

Beatrix knew better. She had checked the environmental logs three times. The sub-basement's atmosphere had been sealed and stable for decades. Nothing had entered. Nothing had left. The room was simply *warm*, the way a bed is warm after someone rises, and Beatrix could not stop her body from reading that warmth as presence.

She had climbed back up to the main level once, intending to sleep. She had made it as far as her cot before the wrongness of it caught her — the idea of lying down in her small, familiar, honest station while something older and stranger breathed beneath her feet — and she had turned around, made tea she also let go cold, and descended again.

The transmitter filled the chamber like a heart in a chest cavity. It was a cylinder of dark alloy, maybe four meters tall, wrapped in cabling that ran up through the ceiling in thick braided arteries, and it was not in any schematic she had ever been issued. Its casing bore no manufacturer's plate, no registry marks, no maintenance stencils. Someone had built it in secret, installed it in secret, and then — and this was the part that made Beatrix's hands unsteady — had kept it running for over two decades without a single service interruption, drawing power through a tap so elegant it had taken her eleven hours to find, siphoning from the station's spine like a tick that had learned to beat with the host's own heart.

She had named it, in her log, *the anomaly*, because naming things was how she kept them at arm's length. But kneeling here now, with the light from her hand-lamp sliding across the casing and finding, at last, an inscription — small, etched, half-hidden where the cabling bundle met the housing — she understood that the thing had been named long before she arrived.

*ODILE*, the inscription read. And beneath it, in smaller characters, as if the engraver had hesitated: *she was two things at once*.

Beatrix sat back on her heels. The lamp trembled in her grip, and the light trembled with it, and for a long moment the only sound in the buried room was the transmitter's hum and her own breathing, too fast, too shallow.

She knew that handwriting. Not the etching — the etching was machined, careful, impersonal. She knew the *phrasing*. Her mother used to say it about the moon of the gas giant visible from Halcyon's observation port, about the station's shadow falling across its own signal, about Beatrix herself, age seven, standing in the airlock with one foot in vacuum and one foot in the world. *Two things at once.* Elena Vane had a saying for everything, and Beatrix had spent twenty-two years forgetting them one by one, and here was one she had failed to forget, etched into the heart of a machine that should not exist.

* * *

She did not open the files that night. She told herself it was procedure: unknown hardware, unknown provenance, potential instability; you did not go poking at encrypted partitions on a transmitter that was somehow, impossibly, drawing forty kilowatts through a tap that shouldn't work. She told herself she would sleep, run diagnostics in the morning, approach it like a technician.

She lay in her cot and listened to the station breathe and knew she was lying.

The truth was simpler and much worse. The truth was that she was afraid of what she would find. For eleven years, Halcyon had been *hers* — every panel, every pipe, every creak of thermal expansion at dawn-cycle, every ritual she had built out of solitude the way a sailor builds knots. She had made her peace with the shape of her life. She was the keeper of a light. Ships passed at a distance, grateful and anonymous. That was the whole of the bargain, and she had signed it willingly, and the bargain had a beautiful, terrible simplicity: *no one is here, and therefore no one can leave.*

And now the bargain was broken. There had been someone here all along. Her solitude — the thing she had thought of as her one true possession, the thing no one could take from her because no one had ever wanted it — had been *furnished*. Someone had built a room inside it, warmed it, filled it with their intentions, and left her to live around the furniture without ever seeing it. Every quiet night, every uneventful shift, every moment she had believed herself the only consciousness on this rock — the transmitter had been humming beneath her, doing someone's work, carrying someone's voice.

And the handwriting on the inscription was her mother's phrasing, and Beatrix was not ready, would never be ready, and knew with absolute certainty that she would open the files the moment the duty cycle allowed.

* * *

She opened them the next morning, after coffee, because her mother had taught her that important work deserved ceremony, and this — whatever it was — was the most important work of her life.

The transmitter's local interface was ancient, a physical access panel behind a cover that required a two-person lift to remove, and Beatrix had to rig a hoist from spare parts and swear at it for an hour, and the whole time her heart was going like a child running ahead of her down a corridor. Inside: a maintenance console, dark, dustless. It woke at her touch as if it had only been waiting.

The directory structure was small. That was the first surprise. She had expected archives, terabytes of buried history. Instead there were eleven files. Eleven. She counted them twice, as if the number might be a code in itself. Each bore a date, and the dates ran backward through the years of her childhood, and the earliest was dated nine days before Elena Vane boarded the shuttle that took her away from Halcyon and out of her daughter's life forever.

Each file was encrypted. Each file bore the same signature block, appended in plaintext, as if the encryption were not meant to hide the author but only the contents:

*E. VANE. For B., when she is ready. If she is ever ready.*

Beatrix read that line eleven times. *For B.* Not *for the technician of record*. Not *for whoever finds this*. For B. The files had been waiting for her — for *her*, specifically, by initial, by blood — the way a letter waits in a drawer, the way a wound waits under a bandage.

She should have felt rage first. She would feel rage later; she would feel it in great rolling thunderheads. But what she felt first, kneeling at the console with her coffee going cold beside her, was the small, stupid, animal lift of joy that a child feels at seeing her own name — even an initial, even an initial scratched by an absent hand — and she hated herself for it, and the hatred changed nothing.

She tried the obvious keys first. Her own name. Her mother's name. Dates. The station's registry. The transmitter refused them all, politely, endlessly, a door with no handle. She worked through the morning, through the afternoon, through the meal she forgot to eat, and the transmitter hummed above her, patient as a held breath, and somewhere out in the dark the Meridian came on, kilometer by kilometer, carrying ten thousand sleepers who were dreaming — she understood this now with a certainty that needed no evidence — dreaming of a woman in a tower of glass.

*The beacon is not a beacon.* She had written it in her log the night before, and now she made herself write the rest: *It has never been a beacon. Eleven years I have kept a light, and the light was never for ships.*

Her hands were steady. That surprised her. Grief, she had learned young, did not always arrive as shaking; sometimes it arrived as an eerie calm, the body's way of clearing the work surface before setting down something heavy.

* * *

The key, when she found it, was not a word. It was a habit.

She had been staring at the signature block, hating it, when her eye caught on the period after the B. *For B.* — and she heard her mother's voice saying it, not as a letter but as a sound, *Bee*, the way Elena had called her across the observation deck, the way she had signed the drawings she made for her daughter: little swans, always swans, *for my Bee, who is two things at once*.

The swans. Beatrix closed her eyes and went back through twenty-two years of sediment and found them: the drawings, dozens of them, left behind in the quarters Elena had abandoned, which Beatrix had never had the heart to clear out. A swan on water. A swan in flight. And on the last one, the one pinned above the desk her mother had left behind, a swan drawn twice in the same line — above the water the white curve of the body, below it the black reflection, so that the drawing was two birds, or one bird in two worlds, and beneath it, in her mother's small precise hand: *Odile*.

Beatrix typed it. Six letters. The console thought for a moment — she would have sworn the hum above her changed pitch, though she knew that was impossible — and the first file opened.

It was a letter.

*My Bee,*

*If you are reading this, then you found the room, which means you found the draw, which means you are the technician I trained you to be, even if I wasn't there to finish training you. I'm sorry for that. I'm sorry for most of what follows. Read all of it before you decide what you feel. I know you. You won't. Read it anyway.*

*Halcyon was never a relay. That is the first thing. The commissioning order you have in your archives is a fabrication; I wrote it myself, twenty-six years ago, and I was very good at my job. There is no shipping consortium that remembers building this station because no shipping consortium built it. I built it. With funding I will explain later, from people I will explain later, for a purpose I should have explained to you a long time ago and did not, because you were nine, and because I was a coward, and because the work required a keeper and I could not bear to ask you and could not bear to leave it keeperless.*

*The transmitter below you is not a navigation aid. It is a broadcast. It has been broadcasting, continuously, since the day I left, and what it broadcasts is not a signal. It is a dream.*

Beatrix stopped reading. Her coffee had gone completely cold, a skin forming on its surface, and she stared at it as if it were an oracle. *It is a dream.* She thought of the anomaly in her power logs — the slow, patient draw, the forty kilowatts, the eleven years of it — and felt the floor of her understanding tilt. She thought of the Meridian, out there in the dark, ten thousand sleepers in their cryo-cradles, and of the reports that came through on the quarterly packet, dry and administrative: *colonist health nominal, cryo-systems nominal, no anomalies to report.*

No anomalies to report. Except the children, now. The children and their woman in the tower of glass.

She had almost filed that report herself, weeks ago, as a curiosity. A shared dream across a hundred cryo-pods was statistically impossible, and she had noted it in her log with the mild interest of a technician cataloguing weather. She had not connected it to the draw. She had not let herself connect it, the way you do not let yourself look directly at the thing standing at the edge of your vision.

She read on.

*The Meridian is dying. I know this because I helped design her, and because the people who funded this station told me what the audits found and what the audits could not be allowed to say aloud. Her landing systems — the cradles, the shuttles, the atmospheric frames — can support the waking and descent of half the colony. Not most of it. Not with rationing. Half. The other half will sleep until the vaults fail, which is to say: forever.*

*Every solution to this is arithmetic. The council will want the strongest, the healthiest, the most useful. I understand the arithmetic. I was raised on it. But I have watched you sleep, my Bee, and I have watched you dream, and I do not believe that a human being can be weighed on a scale built for cargo.*

*So I built a scale of my own.*

*The transmitter broadcasts a dream — mine, in the beginning, and then its own, grown from the seed of mine. It reaches into the cryo-cradles and it looks for something. Not strength. Not utility. It looks for minds that can hold two things at once — sleep and waking, self and other, the dreamer and the dreamed. The children are finding it first because children are closest to the door. The dream is not sickness. The dream is a question. And the tower they see is this station, and the woman in it —*

Beatrix's hand slipped on the console. The file kept scrolling, indifferent to her, and she read the next line three times before she could make herself believe it.

*— the woman in it is you. I am sorry. I could not put anyone else in the tower. I tried to build the dream around a stranger and it would not take; the machine wanted a face it could trust, and the only face I could give it that the dreamers might love was the face of my daughter. You are the tower, Bee. You have always been the tower. Forgive me.*

She did not remember standing up. She was standing, though — backed against the far wall of the sub-basement, the console's light washing pale across the transmitter's dark casing, her breath coming in the short hard rhythm of someone who has been struck. The room was warm. The room was warm and humming and full of her mother, and her mother had been here the whole time — not in body, not in any way that could be held or hated at a proper distance, but in *intention*, threaded through the station's bones, wearing Beatrix's own face as its messenger, reaching into the minds of ten thousand sleeping strangers and *choosing*.

Choosing. The word landed last and heaviest.

*The beacon is a message,* Elena had written, *and a machine for choosing.* Beatrix had not reached that line yet; it was further down the file, in the part she had not read, and she would read it, she would read all of it, her mother had ordered her to and she was going to disobey the order to stop and then obey the order to continue, because that was who she was, that was what eleven years of solitude had made of her — a woman who did the work. But first she stood against the wall and let the grief come, and it came the way she had known it would: not as shaking, but as a terrible clarity, the work surface cleared, the heavy thing set down.

Her mother had not abandoned her. That was the cruelest discovery of all. Her mother had *used* her. Had built a room inside her daughter's solitude and furnished it with a purpose, had made her — without her knowledge, without her consent — the face of a god's dilemma, the tower in ten thousand children's dreams, the scale on which half a species would be weighed. And she had done it with love. That was the unforgivable part. If it had been done with cruelty, Beatrix could have burned it down. It had been done with love, meticulous, patient, twenty-six years of love, and love made it load-bearing. She could not tear it out without the whole structure coming down on the sleepers' heads.

*Forgive me,* the file said, and Beatrix said, aloud, to the warm humming dark, in a voice that did not sound like her own:

"No."

The word echoed in the sealed room and came back to her sounding, horribly, like her mother's voice.

* * *

She read the rest through the night cycle. There were ten more files. She read them the way you drink in a drought — greedily, wincing — and what they held was worse and better than the first: the funding trail, the fabricated commission, the design notes for the dream-seed, the selection criteria laid out in her mother's precise, apologetic hand. *Not the strongest. The ones who can hold both.* And beneath the criteria, the thing that stopped her heart a second time: a live telemetry feed, running for twenty-two years, of the Meridian's cryo-decks. Ten thousand sleepers, each one tagged, each one scored, each one carrying a small quiet number that meant *chosen* or *not chosen* — and the numbers were still moving, still shifting, as the dream worked through the ship's sleeping population like water working through cloth.

Her mother had not just built the scale. Her mother was still, in whatever sense a dead or vanished woman could be, operating it.

And the last file — the eleventh, dated nine days before the shuttle — ended with a line that Beatrix read standing up, because she could not read it sitting down:

*The machine will need a keeper after me. I could not ask you. So I have done something worse than asking: I have arranged for you to find it yourself, and to choose it yourself, and to hate me for it, and to do the work anyway. That was the only inheritance I had to give you, my Bee. I am sorry it is heavy. All the true ones are.*

*The Meridian arrives in your sky soon. When she does, they will hail you, and they will not know what you know, and they will ask you to be what you have always been: the light. Decide, before they ask, what your light is for.*

Beatrix sat down at the console as the station's false dawn came gray through the sub-basement's single high vent. Her eyes were dry. She had passed through anger on the way to something she didn't have a name for yet — something with grief's weight and purpose's spine.

She opened her log. Her hands were steady.

*Day 4,018. The beacon is my mother. The beacon is a machine for choosing. The children are dreaming of my face. And in approximately — * she checked the transit tables, and her stomach dropped through the floor of the station, through the rock, into the dark — *in approximately nineteen days, ten thousand people arrive to find out what I've decided.*

She was still staring at the number when the comm panel, in the quiet station above, lit up with an incoming hail from the Meridian — eleven years early, off every scheduled channel, and addressed not to *Relay Station Halcyon* but to a call sign she had never registered, never used, never spoken aloud.

The call sign was *ODILE*.
