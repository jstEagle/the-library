## 6. The Meridian Answers

The protocol manual sat where it had sat for eleven years: third shelf, spine cracked at the same page, *Emergency Communications — Authorization Required*. Beatrix had read that page so many times the ink had gone soft and gray under her thumb, a small gray bruise on the paper. It listed, in order of severity, the circumstances under which an unscheduled hail to the colony ship *Meridian* was permitted: navigational hazard, hull breach, plague, mutiny.

She stood in the comms room with her coffee going cold and considered adding a fifth category to the list in her head. *My mother built a machine inside my station, and I need to ask someone whether their children are dreaming.*

It was not, strictly speaking, an emergency. The word felt wrong in her mouth anyway. What she felt was closer to vertigo — the specific vertigo of a woman who has spent eleven years believing herself to be the only awake mind within forty light-minutes, and who has just learned that the silence around her may have been full all along, humming with something she never thought to listen for.

Three days since the sub-basement. Three days since she'd knelt in the warm dark with her palm flat against the old transmitter's casing and felt it breathing — there was no other word for it, a slow tidal pulse beneath the metal, like a sleeping animal's flank. Three days since she'd found the first encrypted file signed E.V., opened it, and read four words before her hands began to shake:

*They will dream of me.*

She hadn't slept properly since. She'd eaten standing up. She'd run her maintenance rounds on autopilot of habit, checking coolant lines and antenna bearings while some deeper part of her mind turned the sentence over and over like a stone in a riverbed, wearing off its edges, looking for what was underneath.

*They.* Not *it will broadcast*. Not *the signal will propagate*. *They* — plural, personal, possessive. Children, if Elena Vane's arithmetic had been anything like Beatrix imagined it. Aboard the *Meridian*, eleven years out from Sol and still nine years from arrival, there were four thousand colonists in cryostasis, and among them — she'd seen the manifests once, years ago, when curiosity had still been allowed — some hundreds of children, frozen mid-childhood, dreaming whatever cryo-dreams frozen children dreamed.

Or not frozen at all. Or dreaming something very specific, seeded into them by a transmitter buried beneath a relay station at the edge of nowhere, tended all these years by the architect's abandoned daughter without either of them knowing.

Beatrix set down the coffee. Her hand was steady. That surprised her; she'd expected it to shake again. But there was a strange calm that came after three sleepless nights, a plateau past exhaustion where everything went quiet and clear, and in that clear quiet the decision made itself the way decisions do when they've been waiting long enough.

She opened the channel.

Not the emergency band — protocol had enough teeth left in her conscience for that much. She used the old handshake frequency, the one buried in the station's original specifications, the one that predated her assignment by decades and which no one had ever answered because no one was ever supposed to call it. Elena's fingerprints were on that too; Beatrix had checked. Of course they were. Her mother had left her one door, and Beatrix was about to knock on it.

The transmission would take eleven minutes to reach the *Meridian*. The reply, if any, another eleven back. Twenty-two minutes minimum, plus whatever time it took anyone aboard to notice, decide, and act. She keyed the mic and heard her own breath in the room, loud as surf.

"This is Relay Station Halcyon," she said. "This is—" and stopped, because she did not know what to call herself. Technician. Daughter. The woman in the tower, if the dreams were true. "This is Beatrix Vane. I'm calling about the beacon. I think you should know what it's saying."

She sent it. Then she sat down on the floor of the comms room, back against the console rack, knees drawn up, and waited the way she had learned to wait for everything: alone, in the hum, watching the seconds tick across a screen no one else would ever see.

* * *

Eleven minutes out, eleven minutes back. In between, the station performed its small liturgies around her. The air scrubbers cycled. The gyros murmured. Somewhere below her feet, behind three locked bulkheads and a floor panel she'd welded shut after finding it, the second transmitter pulsed its slow warm pulse, patient as a heart.

She tried to imagine the message arriving. A comms officer on night watch, maybe — though the *Meridian* ran skeleton watches, most of its crew in the tanks like everyone else. An alarm chiming in an empty corridor. Someone waking. Someone reading the words *I think you should know what it's saying* and understanding none of them, or worse, understanding all of them.

She tried to imagine what they knew. Nothing, probably. The council would know the ship's trajectory, its consumables, its landing margins — the cold ledger of survival. They would not know that their children had been dreaming in unison for months. Unless they did know. Unless the dreams had been reported and dismissed, filed under cryo-sickness, explained away the way inexplicable things always get explained away when explanation is cheaper than fear.

Her mother had counted on that, Beatrix thought. Elena Vane had understood institutions the way water understands stone — patiently, over years, knowing exactly where they would erode and where they would hold. She had hidden a god's dilemma inside a lighthouse and trusted that no one would look up until it was too late to look away.

Twenty-two minutes passed. Thirty. Forty. Beatrix got up, paced the length of the comms room — nine steps, turn, nine steps — and told herself that silence meant nothing. The ship was asleep. The watch officer might be afraid to escalate. The message might be sitting in a queue behind routine telemetry, unopened, unread, gathering dust in the dark between stars.

At fifty-one minutes, the console chimed.

Not the standard acknowledgment tone. Something older — the handshake completing itself, two frequencies finding each other across the gulf like hands meeting in a dark room. The channel was open. Someone had answered on Elena's private frequency, the one that existed in no official registry, the one only someone who had *learned it in a dream* could possibly have found.

Beatrix sat down so fast she missed the chair and ended up on the floor again, dragging the headset toward her with a cable that snagged and caught and finally gave.

Static first. Then a voice.

"—reading you, Halcyon. I don't know if this works both ways. I've never done this. Say something so I know you're real."

A young voice. That was the first thing. Younger than hers by ten years at least, untrained, unmodulated, carrying the slight flattened quality of ship-born speech — no weather in it, no wind, a voice that had never had to cross a field or rise above rain. And underneath the youth, something else: a carefulness, a held-breath quality, the sound of someone standing at the edge of something high.

"You found the frequency," Beatrix said. Her own voice sounded strange to her — rusty, too loud, a voice shaped by years of talking only to machines. "That frequency isn't in your ship's systems."

"I know." A pause. Static breathed between them like a tide. "I've known it since I was six. I thought everyone knew it. Like you know your own address." Another pause, longer. "You're the woman in the tower."

The words went through her like cold water through cloth.

"Yes," she said. "And you're one of the children who dreams of me."

"All of us do. Four hundred and six of us, last count. The little ones call you the glass lady. Some of them draw you." He laughed then, a short startled sound, as if the laugh had escaped without permission. "Sorry. I've rehearsed this conversation about a thousand times and every version falls apart the moment you actually talk. You sound different than the dream. Realer. Your voice has—" he searched, and she waited, and the waiting itself felt like a kind of intimacy she had no armor against, "—edges. In the dream everything is smooth. Glass and light and the numbers falling like snow. You're the only thing in it that isn't smooth."

"The numbers," Beatrix said carefully. "Tell me about the numbers."

"They fall through the tower. All the time, day and night, snowing down past the windows. Most of us can't read them. The little ones catch them like snowflakes and they melt. But some of us—" his voice dropped, went quieter, almost ashamed, "—some of us started being able to read them. A few years ago. Nobody taught us. We just woke up one cycle knowing. That's how I found the frequency. It was in the numbers. Buried in them, way down deep, like a seed at the bottom of the snowfall. Your frequency. This one. I've been listening to this channel for years, Halcyon. There was never anything on it. Just carrier wave, just silence. But tonight there was you."

Fifty-one minutes he'd waited, then. Or rather — she corrected herself, doing the terrible arithmetic — he had hijacked a console on a ship where unauthorized access was a flogging offense, and sat in the dark listening to static for nearly an hour, betting everything on a voice he'd only ever met asleep.

"What's your name?" she asked.

"Ilya." Then, quickly, as if names were doors that swung both ways: "Ilya Soren. I'm seventeen. I was born on the ship, year two out. I've never touched ground. Never seen sky that wasn't a screen." A breath. "Is it true there's sky? Where you are. Real sky. I know the dream shows it but the dream shows everything and I can't tell anymore which parts are memory and which parts are—"

"It's true," Beatrix said. "There's sky here. I haven't stood under it in eleven years, but it's there. It's blue at midday and black at night and it does this thing at dawn where it goes green for about ninety seconds, right before the sun clears the ridge. No one believes me about the green."

"I believe you," Ilya said instantly, and then, softer, wondering: "I believe you about everything. That's the problem. That's why I'm here."

* * *

They talked for four hours.

Later, trying to reconstruct it, Beatrix could never manage it in order. Conversation across twenty-two-minute gaps doesn't flow; it pools. Each message sent whole into the dark, each answer arriving like a stone dropped down a well, and in the long silences between, she learned to live inside his pauses the way she had learned to live inside the station's hum — filling them, furnishing them, making rooms of them.

He told her about the dreams. How they'd started as fragments — the tower, the falling numbers, a sense of enormous patience, of being *waited for*. How the younger children had accepted it the way children accept everything, folding the glass lady into their play, drawing her on walls in crayon, arguing about whether her hair was dark or light because the dream never quite resolved her face. How the older ones had grown frightened as the dreams sharpened, as the numbers became readable, as they began to wake with knowledge in their mouths like coins — frequencies, coordinates, fragments of a language none of them had been taught, a language Beatrix recognized with a lurch when Ilya spoke a phrase of it aloud: Old Standard, the archaic colonial dialect, Elena Vane's native tongue, dead everywhere except inside a buried transmitter and the minds of four hundred sleeping children.

He told her how the adults reacted. The first reports filed and dismissed. The second round of reports taken more seriously, pediatricians scanning sleeping brains and finding activity patterns that shouldn't exist in cryo, synchronized across hundreds of subjects like a choir. The whisper campaigns. The priest who called it grace and the engineer who called it malware and the council that called it nothing at all, officially, in the minutes, while unofficially doubling the watch on the children's decks.

"And you?" Beatrix asked. "What do you call it?"

Silence, twenty-two minutes' worth, and then: "I call it the only place I've ever lived. That sounds— I know how that sounds. But Halcyon, I grew up inside this dream. Before I could talk I was dreaming of your tower. Everything I am has that signal running through it like a spine. If you told me tomorrow it was poison, I'd still—" he stopped. Started again. "There's a girl in the next bunk deck, Mirei, she's nine. Last month she woke up crying and when they asked her what was wrong she said, *the lady in the tower is lonely, somebody should go to her.* Nine years old. She's never been anywhere. She's never been *lonely*, not really, not the way—"

He didn't finish. He didn't have to. Beatrix sat on the floor of the comms room with her eyes closed and let the unfinished sentence arrive in her chest like a delivered blow.

"Somebody should go to her," Beatrix repeated.

"That's why I stole the console," Ilya said simply. "Mirei asked. And I realized nobody else was going to. The adults want the dream to stop. They want us scanned and corrected and normal. Nobody was ever going to come find you. So."

So. A seventeen-year-old boy committing his life's first crime because a nine-year-old cried for a stranger. Beatrix pressed the back of her hand against her mouth and was silent for a full transmission cycle, and when she finally spoke, her voice came out lower than she intended.

"Ilya. There are things you need to know. About the beacon. About why the dream exists. And I need you to understand that I only learned this three days ago, and I haven't— I'm still—" She made herself say it plainly, because he deserved plainness, whoever else in his life had lied to him in beautiful curves. "Your dreams aren't an accident. They're a broadcast. From my station. From a transmitter my mother built into this rock before I was born. She designed it. She aimed it at your ship. She put the dream inside your heads on purpose, and I don't yet know all of what she wanted, but I know enough to tell you it isn't innocent. Whatever it's doing to you — the language, the numbers, the changes the doctors are finding — it's deliberate. It's her handwriting. And I'm sorry. I'm so sorry. I didn't know. I've been keeping the light on for eleven years without knowing what the light was for."

Another long silence. Longest yet. Beatrix watched the timer count and imagined him on the other end — in a stolen chair, in a red-lit comms alcove, deciding whether the woman in the tower was a savior or a symptom.

When he answered, his voice had changed. Steadier. Older.

"I figured," he said. "Most of it. We did. The ones who can read the numbers — we worked out years ago that the dream was *aimed*. Signals drift, Halcyon. Weather doesn't. This comes in too clean, too directed, too—authored. We just didn't know who the author was." A pause. "Your mother. Elena Vane. She was the chief architect, wasn't she? Meridian's flight plan still carries her signature in the header metadata. She vanished before any of us were born. They don't teach about her. Her files are sealed. But her name is in the bones of everything."

"Yes," Beatrix said. "She's my mother. She left when I was nine. I've spent my whole life—" and there her own sentence failed, the way his had, the way sentences fail when they touch the load-bearing wall of a person. She took a breath and finished it differently. "I've spent my whole life tending her machine. That's the joke. That's the whole joke of me."

"It's not a joke," Ilya said, with a fierceness that startled her. "Halcyon — Beatrix. Do you know what you are to us? Four hundred and six kids grew up inside your signal. You're not the keeper of a beacon. You're the first landscape we ever had. When Mirei draws home, she draws your tower." His voice roughened. "Whatever your mother built, you kept it burning. Every night of our lives, the light was on. Don't you dare call that a joke."

Beatrix looked at her hands. They were shaking again, but gently now, the way things shake when they're not breaking but opening.

* * *

Near the end — near what neither of them yet knew was only the beginning — Ilya said, "There's something else. The reason I really came tonight, besides Mirei. The numbers changed. Three nights ago. Everyone felt it — even the little ones woke up restless, like animals before weather. The snowfall shifted pattern. There's a new sequence running underneath the old one, and it repeats, and I've decoded enough of it to be scared."

"Read it to me," Beatrix said, and pulled a slate toward her, stylus ready.

He read it. Numbers, then the Old Standard fragments threaded through them, and Beatrix's blood went cold and colder as the translation assembled itself in her mind — because she recognized the structure. She'd spent three days inside her mother's encryption schemes. She knew this grammar. This wasn't navigation data. This wasn't even the dream proper.

It was a countdown.

"*When the ship crosses the threshold,*" Ilya recited slowly, translating as he went, his young voice very quiet in the static, "*the choice will be made. Half to wake. Half to remain. The criterion is written in the dream. None may alter it. Not the council. Not the keeper.*"

He paused. "Keeper," he said. "That's you, isn't it? That's what it calls you."

Below Beatrix's feet, through nine decks of rock and steel, the buried transmitter pulsed its slow warm pulse — patient, tidal, utterly indifferent to her — and she understood, with the clarity of the plateau beyond exhaustion, that her mother had known about this conversation. Had scheduled for it. Had perhaps, in some sense she refused yet to examine, arranged it.

"Ilya," she said. "Get some sleep. Real sleep, if you can. And then find me again — same frequency, same time tomorrow. Because I've read my mother's files, and I can tell you the criterion is not what the dream says it is. And I think the two of us have less time than either of us believed."

The channel closed. The static settled back into its ancient hush. Beatrix sat alone in the comms room with the countdown glowing on her slate, and for the first time in eleven years, the silence of the station felt less like solitude than like a held breath.
