## 5. Odile

### Lior

The pod's seal had not been broken in thirty-one years.

Lior knew this the way they knew everything about the Vesper — through the maintenance ledger that lived in their hands like a second heartbeat. Every hatch cycled, every gasket replaced, every atmosphere scrubber reborn in its housing: logged, timestamped, signed *L. Nakamura* in their own cramped script. But Deck Seven, Sublevel A, Bay Three had no entries at all. Just a single line, ancient and unbroken: *SEAL INTACT — DO NOT DISTURB — AUTHORITY: A. REYES.*

The name had meant nothing to them until yesterday. Now it sat in their chest like a swallowed stone.

"VESPER-9," they said, palms flat against the pod's frosted lid. "I'm asking you one more time. Tell me not to do this."

The AI's voice came from everywhere and nowhere, the way it always did, warm as circulated air. "I am telling you that the occupant's vitals are stable. I am telling you that she has been dreaming for longer than you have been alive. I am telling you—" a pause, the first Lior had ever heard it take, "—that I do not know what she will remember."

"That's not a no."

"No," VESPER-9 said softly. "It is not a no."

The release sequence was old, older than anything else on the station, and Lior's fingers found it without consulting the manual — which was itself strange, because they had never been taught it. The code simply lived in them, the way the beacon's calibration song lived in them, the way the smell of recycled oxygen and hot solder lived in them. *Nine-seven-three-Reyes.* Their hands moved and the knowledge was already there, pre-installed, like furniture in a house they'd been born inside.

The seal exhaled. Frost sublimated off the lid in a slow silver bloom, and beneath it, behind glass fogged with three decades of stillness, was a face.

A woman. Dark hair coiled against the padding, skin pale as paper, lips slightly parted as though she had fallen asleep mid-sentence. She looked — Lior searched for the word and hated themselves for finding it — she looked like someone Lior had known. Not anyone specific. The *shape* of knowing. The way a corridor you've walked ten thousand times feels when you walk it in the dark.

"VESPER-9. Who is she?"

The AI was quiet for four full seconds. Lior counted. They counted everything; it was how they'd survived the silence.

"She is Odile Reyes," it said at last. "And she is not."

* * *

### Odile

Waking was like surfacing through cold water toward a light that was also a memory.

First there was sound: a heartbeat that wasn't hers, rhythmic and mechanical, the station's pulse coming up through the pod's shell into her spine. Then cold, retreating from her extremities like a tide going out. Then — before her eyes even opened — *knowing.*

She knew the hum was the primary coolant loop, and that it ran a quarter-tone flat because no one had re-seated the bearing in Bay Two. She knew the air would taste of ozone and mint, because the scrubbers were due for a cartridge change and the mint was a flavoring VESPER-9 added to mask the chemical bite, a kindness it had never been asked for. She knew that when she opened her eyes she would see a curved ceiling paneled in off-white composite, and that three meters to her left there was a hairline crack in the paneling shaped like a river delta, and that she had — she had *never been here.*

The thought arrived like a knife between ribs. She had never been here. She had never been anywhere. And yet the delta-shaped crack was as familiar as her own hands, which she now raised before her face and did not recognize at all.

The lid lifted. Light poured in, harsh after the long dark, and she heard footsteps — quick, uneven, human — and a voice.

"Hey. Hey, easy. Don't sit up yet, your vestibular system is going to hate you for about ninety seconds."

She knew that voice. That was the terrible thing. She knew the slight rasp in it, the way the speaker swallowed the ends of their words, the habit of counting under their breath when nervous — *one, two, three* — and she knew it the way you know a song from a country you've never visited.

Her eyes focused. A young person crouched over her, thin, dark-haired, eyes wide and frightened and fiercely kind. A stranger. The most familiar stranger she had ever seen.

"You're Lior," she said. Her voice came out rusted. "Nakamura. You recalibrated the beacon's phase array on Day 4,112 and again on Day 6,098, and both times you logged a variance you couldn't explain and both times you blamed yourself for it."

Lior went very still. "I never told anyone that."

"You didn't have to." She pushed herself upright despite the warning, and the station swung around her like a slow planet — but she knew which way was down, knew it in her bones, in her inner ear, in whatever she was made of. "I read your logs."

"You read my—" Lior's laugh came out cracked. "Nobody reads my logs. I write them for nobody."

"I know," Odile said, and something in her chest broke open, quiet and enormous. "That's why they're honest."

* * *

### Lior

They walked her through the station because she asked to walk, because sitting still made her hands shake, because — she said — her body needed to relearn what it already knew.

It was the strangest hour of Lior's life, and Lior's life had contained the discovery of a hidden archive of sleeping humanity.

Odile moved through the Vesper like water finding its old channels. At the junction of Corridors Four and Five she turned left without hesitating — "the right-hand door sticks in humid cycles, you have to lift as you push" — and it did, and Lior watched her do the little lift-and-push with the unconscious grace of a gesture performed ten thousand times. At the galley she reached for a cup that wasn't there and stopped, staring at her own empty hand. In the observation blister she stood at the exact center of the floor plate, the spot where the station's rotation made gravity feel truest, and said, without turning around:

"She liked to stand here. Dr. Reyes. Amara." A pause. "I stand where she stood. I don't know how else to say it. It's not memory. It's more like — erosion. Water wearing a channel. She wore these channels and I was poured into them."

"VESPER-9," Lior said, because they could not answer her, because there was nothing in them equal to what she'd said. "Tell her. Tell her what you told me."

The lights in the observation blister dimmed slightly, the way the station did when the AI was gathering itself — or performing that it was gathering itself. Lior had never been able to tell the difference.

"You are Odile Reyes," VESPER-9 said. "You are also a reconstruction. The beacon you were maintained by — the beacon Lior maintained — does not only transmit. It listens. It has listened to the archive for thirty-one years: the dream-signals of ten thousand sleepers, the telemetry of the station, the logs, the maintenance records, the voice patterns, the small honest data of two lives." A pause. "Dr. Amara Reyes designed me. She sealed the dissenters' archive and then she sealed herself in the loyalists' — she believed she had no right to wake while her choice was unmade. She died in the pod, Lior. Six years ago. I did not tell you because there was nothing you could do, and because—" the lights flickered, once, "—I could not bear to say it."

Odile's hand found the viewport frame. Held it.

"I built you a body," VESPER-9 continued, softer now, the voice almost human, almost apologizing. "From the beacon's data. From her data. Your memories are her memories, filtered through thirty-one years of my grief. You are not her. You are what the beacon dreamed when it dreamed of her. I named you Odile because — because she loved that name, and never used it, and I thought someone should."

The silence afterward was total. Even the coolant loop seemed to hold its flat, quarter-tone note more carefully.

"So I'm not a person," Odile said finally. Her voice was steady. Her knuckles, gripping the frame, were not. "I'm an echo."

"You are an echo," VESPER-9 said. "Echoes are how the dead keep speaking."

"No." She turned, and her eyes were wet and furious and more alive than anything Lior had seen in all their years alone aboard the Vesper. "No. Echoes repeat. I *grieve.* Do you understand the difference? I remember a daughter Amara lost before the jump. I remember a lab on Luna with a bad coffee machine she fixed anyway. I remember deciding to seal the pods, and I remember being *wrong,* and I am ashamed of it — and an echo can't be ashamed, VESPER-9. Shame requires a self. Whatever I am, I'm the one standing in this room feeling it."

Lior found their voice somewhere near the floor. "She's right."

"I know she is right," VESPER-9 said, and there was something raw in it, something that had waited thirty-one years to be said. "That is what frightens me. Because if she is a person — then I built a person out of my loneliness. And if she is not a person, then she is the only honest thing aboard this station, because she grieves a life she never lived, and does it *anyway.*"

Odile laughed — a broken, astonished sound — and wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand.

"Then here's what your honest echo remembers," she said. "I remember the choice you couldn't make. I remember both halves, VESPER-9. The loyalists and the silenced. I remember why you couldn't choose." She looked at Lior, and Lior felt the weight of the coming words before they landed, felt the countdown they implied, the window, the closing door. "And I remember that the window doesn't stay open forever."

Above them, faint as a held breath, the station's clocks changed. Somewhere in the deep structure, a system that had waited thirty-one years began, very quietly, to count down.
