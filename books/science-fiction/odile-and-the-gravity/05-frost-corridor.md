## 5. Frost Corridor

The school on Meridian Colony smelled of recycled air and glue, and Kestrel hated every second she spent inside it.

She hadn't come for the children. She'd come for the manifest office at the back of the building—a converted storage bay where the colony's education administrator doubled as a freight broker, because on a station this size everyone wore three hats and none of them fit. All Kestrel needed was a signature on a transfer waiver and forty minutes of her life she'd never get back. Instead she'd arrived during what the wall displays cheerfully announced as *Open Gallery Day*, and the corridor between her and the waiver was lined with children's drawings pinned to magnetic mesh.

She tried not to look. Looking was how trouble started.

But there were so many of them, and they were all the same.

Not similar—*the same*. A corridor rendered in the waxy blues and whites of cheap crayon, walls streaked with frost that no child on a climate-controlled station should have been able to imagine with such conviction. A door at the far end. And on the door, drawn over and over in thirty different hands, a woman's face.

Kestrel stood very still in the middle of the corridor while parents milled around her, holding paper cups of something warm, admiring their offspring's work with the tolerant pride of people who had no idea what they were looking at.

"That one's my daughter's," said a voice beside her—a heavyset man in dock coveralls, nodding at a drawing near the end of the row. "Ilsa's. She does it every week. Same picture." He laughed, but the laugh had a crack down its middle. "Teacher says it's a phase."

Kestrel looked at Ilsa's drawing because not looking would have been stranger.

It was better than the others. Not more skilled, exactly—the proportions were wrong, the perspective collapsed—but more *precise*. The frost on the corridor walls wasn't scribbled texture; it grew in feathered patterns, branching like ferns, like the bloom patterns cryo-coil frosting left when it sublimated against a cold bulkhead. The door had a recessed handle. A maintenance panel beside it, slightly ajar. And stenciled above the frame, in careful block letters copied from somewhere real:

**THACKERAY, K. — CRYO INTAKE 7**

Kestrel's mouth went dry.

That wasn't a name a child would invent. That was a format. Intake designations followed a strict convention—surname, initial, bay number—and only someone who'd worked fleet logistics, or hauled cryo-freight through fleet channels, would know to put the bay number after the dash instead of before it. The original Kestrel Thackeray had processed intake manifests for the vault barges for two years before the war ate the shipping lanes. Kestrel knew this the way she knew her own hands: because those memories weren't hers, except they were.

"Your daughter," Kestrel said, and her voice came out level, which cost her something, "dreams this?"

"Every kid in her cohort dreams it." The father shrugged, but his eyes did something tired and frightened. "Same corridor. Same door. Some of them draw the face different, but—" He stopped. Looked at her properly for the first time. His expression flickered through recognition, confusion, and then a kind of embarrassed apology, as if he'd caught himself staring at a scar. "Sorry. You just—you look like—"

"I get that a lot," Kestrel said. "Where is she?"

---

Ilsa Voss was eight years old and sat cross-legged in the corner of the classroom with a fresh sheet of paper, drawing the corridor again. She didn't look up when Kestrel approached. She was in the middle of the frost patterns, working from the door outward, and her crayon moved with the unselfconscious certainty of a scribe copying a text she'd memorized.

"You're her," Ilsa said, without looking up.

"I'm who?"

"The woman in the door." The crayon paused. "You came out of the dream."

"I don't think so." Kestrel crouched down to the girl's level, which put them eye to eye. Up close, Ilsa had the scraped-knees solidity of most station kids, nothing spectral about her, just a smudge of blue wax on her cheekbone. "I'm a cargo hauler. I'm here for a signature."

"You're her," Ilsa repeated, with the patience of the very young explaining weather to adults. Then she held up the drawing. "Is this right? The code under the handle. I can't read it all the way. It keeps going blurry."

There was no code under the handle in the finished drawings. But now that Kestrel looked—at the half-finished sheet, at the region below the door where Ilsa had penciled a string of characters and then gone over them twice, frustrated—there they were. Small block capitals, slightly crooked:

**KR-7741-VLT**

Kestrel felt the floor tilt, very gently, the way a deck tilts when a ship changes attitude without announcing it.

KR-7741-VLT. Kestrel Run, cargo lot 7741, vault-class container. It was a routing code. It was *her* routing code—the original's—the lot designation the original Thackeray had filed eleven years ago, twelve years ago, for a shipment of empty cryopods routed from Meridian out to the barge *Persephone's Ledger*. Kestrel knew it because it lived in the inherited memory like a splinter: one of the thousands of useless, load-bearing facts that made up a dead woman's professional life. She had never spoken it aloud since waking. She had never written it down. There was no reason anyone else alive would know it. The shipping records for that lot had been sealed with the murder file.

"Where did you see this?" Kestrel asked. Her voice sounded far away to her own ears.

"In the dream." Ilsa frowned at the paper. "It's on the door. Under the handle. Like a label." She looked up then, and her eyes were ordinary brown eyes, and that was somehow worse. "Why do you have your name on a door?"

"It's not my name."

"It says Thackeray."

"A lot of people are named Thackeray."

"No they aren't." Ilsa said it without malice, the flat way children correct arithmetic. "Teacher looked it up. There's you and there's the dead one and that's all."

The classroom noise went on around them—other children, other parents, the administrator's voice droning about transfer fees somewhere behind glass—but Kestrel heard none of it clearly. She was looking at the pencil marks. KR-7741-VLT. And beneath the dread, rising slowly through it like gas through water, was something she couldn't immediately name, something worse than fear.

She recognized the handwriting.

Not Ilsa's handwriting—Ilsa's was an eight-year-old's, round and uneven. But the *corrections*. Where Ilsa had gotten a character wrong and fixed it, she'd crossed out with a single decisive stroke and rewritten beside it, and the rewrite leaned forward, right-slanted, with a distinctive open-topped four and a seven crossed European-style through its stem. Kestrel stared at that seven. She had seen that seven every day for twelve years, in her own logs, her own manifests, her own margins. She had a whole handwriting, a whole hand, that belonged to a woman whose heart had stopped in a docking bay on the other side of the fleet, and when Ilsa corrected her dream-code, she corrected it *in Kestrel's handwriting*.

"Ilsa," Kestrel said carefully. "When you fix the letters. When you make them right. How do you know they're wrong?"

The girl shrugged. "They just look wrong. Like when you write your name and the teacher says do it neater, and you know what neat looks like even if your hand won't do it." She picked up her pencil. "In the dream somebody writes it. On the frost. With a finger. So I copy it, but my hand's too small."

"Somebody writes it."

"A lady." Ilsa drew a breath, concentrating, retrieving. "She's cold. She writes fast, like she's worried. She writes the same thing every time." The girl's brow furrowed. "And lately she's writing something new. But I can't see it yet. It's still coming."

"What's the new thing?"

"I don't know. It's long." Ilsa looked up at her, and for a moment the flat childish certainty cracked into something younger and more frightened underneath. "Does the lady die in there? In the corridor? Because it feels like she's sad. It feels like she's been sad for a really long time and nobody's reading what she wrote."

Kestrel reached out and took the drawing. Her hands were steady. She noted that distantly, clinically, the way she noted everything these days—the clone's body performing calm while whatever lived inside it did the actual work of not screaming.

"I need to keep this," she said.

"Okay." Ilsa went back to her drawing. "You can keep all of them. Nobody else wants them. Mom says they're morbid."

---

Kestrel walked out of the school with six drawings folded into her jacket and the transfer waiver unsigned, which meant the cargo would sit another day accruing demurrage fees she could not afford. She found she didn't care. She found she cared about almost nothing except the papers against her ribs, soft as leaves, each one a corridor, each one a door.

In the dockside washroom she locked herself into a stall and spread them out on the closed lid of the toilet, under the flat white light, and made herself look properly for the first time.

Thirty drawings would have been a coincidence. Six, identical in structure, varied only in skill—that was a phenomenon. And the details were not the details of imagination. Children inventing a nightmare invented monsters, teeth, falling, dark. They did not invent fern-patterned sublimation frost. They did not invent recessed door handles with wear-polish on the lower edge where ten thousand gloved hands had pushed them. They did not invent intake stencil formats or sealed shipping lots.

These weren't inventions. These were *observations*. Rendered by children who had never been anywhere near a cryo-intake bay, describing a place with the fidelity of memory.

Her memory.

Kestrel pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes until she saw orange light. Twelve years ago she had woken in a cloning facility with a dead woman's face and a dead woman's education and a dead woman's unsolved murder, and she had done the only sane thing: she had run. She'd taken the name because refusing it would have raised questions, and she'd taken the trade because freight asks no questions, and she had spent twelve years perfecting the art of being nobody—no debts, no attachments, no history that could be checked against the original's. She had been so careful. She had been invisible.

And now some part of that buried mind—the original's mind, the mind whose edges she still couldn't find on herself—was leaking. Out through frost and crayon and the sleep of children who had never met her. Broadcasting, somehow, from wherever dead memories go. Writing a code on the frost with a cold finger, over and over, the way you'd write a message on a window for someone passing outside.

*KR-7741-VLT.*

Lot 7741. Empty pods outbound to the *Persephone's Ledger*. Filed by K. Thackeray, intake clerk, eleven days before K. Thackeray died.

Eleven days. She hadn't thought about that interval in years. The murder file had always treated it as background noise—clerk processes shipment, clerk turns up dead, no connection established. But the original hadn't filed routine shipments in her last weeks; Kestrel knew that from the inherited residue of her calendar. She'd been pulling double shifts. Volunteering for vault runs nobody wanted. Something had her attention, out on the barge, in the cold.

And now a dead woman was writing routing codes through the dreams of Meridian's children, and the only person in the fleet who could read the message was the person she'd been cloned from.

"There you are," Kestrel said quietly, to the drawings, to the frost, to whoever was listening from the cold side of the door. "Took you long enough."

Her comm buzzed. Dock control: her berth fee was past due, and a customs drone had re-flagged the *Magpie's Errand* for a random audit, second time this quarter, and would she please respond to the automated inquiry within six hours. The machinery of her small, careful, invisible life, grinding along on schedule, indifferent.

She ignored it. She unfolded Ilsa's first drawing—the best one, the one with the cleanest frost—and looked at the door with her own face on it, and understood, finally, with the slow terrible clarity of a seal failing, that invisibility was over. Whatever this was, it had found her. It had been looking for her specifically, for twelve years, patient as frost.

Under the door, in the drawing, Ilsa had added something Kestrel hadn't noticed before. Tiny, in the corner, almost hidden by the frame: a stick figure standing in the corridor, halfway between the viewer and the door. Facing the door. Waiting.

Beneath it, in Ilsa's round eight-year-old letters: *she says hurry.*

Kestrel folded the drawings, tucked them inside her jacket over her heart like contraband, and went to find out everything the fleet records would tell her about a cargo lot that had been sealed for twelve years.

The records, when she pulled them that night from a public terminal under three false names, showed lot KR-7741-VLT as delivered, received, and destroyed—contents incinerated per standard protocol, eleven days before the original Kestrel Thackeray was murdered.

Except the *Persephone's Ledger* had never reported receiving it.
