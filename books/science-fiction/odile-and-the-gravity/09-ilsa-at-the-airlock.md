## 9. Ilsa at the Airlock

The *Odile's Wake* smelled of recycled air and old coffee, and Kestrel loved it the way you love a body that has never once betrayed you.

She ran her pre-flight checks with the door sealed and the dock chatter bleeding faintly through the hull—Meridian Colony at 0400 station time, the hour when the corridors went quiet and the cargo cranes hummed their low, patient song. She liked this part best: the ship awake before anyone could ask anything of it. A manifest to file, a burn window in ninety minutes, forty tons of desalination membrane bound for the outer habitats. Nobody's business but hers.

She was halfway through the coolant pressure log when she heard the sound.

Small. Deliberate. The particular scrape of a child trying very hard not to make noise, which is the loudest kind of scraping there is.

Kestrel didn't turn around. She set down the stylus, let her hand drift—not obviously—to the panel by her knee where the cabin lockdown lived, and said, in the flat voice she used for customs drones and other things that should not be aboard her ship:

"You have ten seconds to tell me why I shouldn't open the inner airlock and let the vacuum finish whatever you started."

A pause. Then, from behind the stacked crates of membrane spools, a small voice:

"It took me two hours to get past Dock Nine security. If you open the airlock that's really wasteful."

Kestrel closed her eyes for one full second. Then she turned.

The girl was maybe eleven, small for it, wearing an oversized thermal jacket with the Meridian Colony crease still sharp on the sleeves—borrowed, then, or stolen from someone bigger. Dark hair cut short in the uneven way of a child who had done it herself with a dull blade. Her face was pale and set in an expression Kestrel recognized with a jolt she refused to name: the look of someone who has decided something enormous and is holding onto the decision with both hands because letting go would mean falling.

"I know you," the girl said.

"No," Kestrel said. "You don't."

"You're the woman from the door."

* * *

Her name was Ilsa Voss, though it took twenty minutes and one threat to call dock security before she surrendered it. She sat wedged into the copilot's chair with her feet not quite touching the deck, hands folded in her lap like a child at a hearing, and she did not cry, which Kestrel found more alarming than tears would have been.

Kestrel had considered every option during those twenty minutes. Call security: the girl gets returned to her parents, Kestrel gets flagged again, another entry in a file that was already fraying at the edges. Space her out the airlock as a bluff: the girl would probably call it, and Kestrel would have to live with having threatened a child, which was a debt she wasn't willing to carry even at interest-free rates. Put her off at the next habitat: two days away, and by then—

By then what? That was the question she kept circling. By then what?

"Say it again," Kestrel said. "Slowly. From the beginning."

Ilsa took a breath like a diver. "Everyone dreams it. Me and Priya and the Okonkwo twins and about thirty others—we counted. The corridor with the frost on the walls, going down and down, and at the end there's a big round door, and your face is on it." She frowned. "Not exactly your face. Older. But the same face."

"The same face," Kestrel repeated.

"Yours," Ilsa said, with the terrible simplicity of children. "And last week the dream changed. The door opened a little. And there was a voice, and it said—" her brow furrowed, retrieving it exactly, word for word, the way children retrieve things, whole and unedited—"it said *the counter needs her hands*. And everyone woke up except me, and I stayed in the dream, and I asked the corridor who the counter was, and the frost made a shape like a woman walking, and I followed it, and when I woke up I knew I had to find you." She unfolded her hands and spread them, done, case closed. "So here I am."

Here she was. Stowed away on a freighter at four in the morning, having beaten Dock Nine security—which Kestrel knew for a fact was bribed, lazy, and thorough in that order—with nothing but a dream and two hours of stubbornness.

"How did you get aboard?"

"Airlock maintenance crawlway, aft of berth twelve. It's supposed to be locked but the seal code hasn't been changed since the last inspection cycle. My dad works docks; he complains about it all the time." A beat. "Don't report him. He doesn't know I know."

"I'm not reporting anyone. I'm putting you on the next shuttle back."

"You don't have a shuttle."

"I'll *buy* a shuttle."

"You won't." Ilsa said it without malice, the way you'd note that water was wet. "You're leaving in eighty-one minutes. I watched you file the manifest. And if you put me off, you have to explain to someone why you had a stowaway, and you hate explaining things. You checked three times that the dock cameras were on the maintenance loop before you came up the ramp. I watched that too."

Kestrel looked at her for a long moment. The girl looked back, unblinking, and behind her eyes something moved that was not entirely an eleven-year-old's patience. It was older than that. It was patient the way ice is patient.

"Where are your parents right now?" Kestrel asked quietly.

"Asleep. They work second shift." Ilsa's chin lifted a fraction. "They think the dream is a phase. My mom cried about it once when she thought I was asleep. She thinks I don't remember things like that. Everyone thinks that."

"And so you climbed through a crawlway in the dark to find a stranger because a frost corridor told you to."

"Yes."

"That's insane."

"Yes," Ilsa agreed, agreeably. "But it worked."

* * *

Kestrel made coffee she didn't drink and gave the girl a bulb of warm milk from the galley stores, mostly to have something to do with her hands. The *Wake*'s cabin felt smaller with a child in it. Ships were calibrated to their crews the way gloves were calibrated to hands, and Ilsa Voss did not fit any glove Kestrel owned.

She ran the girl's story against herself while pretending to review the flight plan. Coincidence was possible. Children talked; nightmares propagated through schoolyards like any contagion; a shared image of a frost corridor could be nothing more than a rumor wearing sleep as a costume. Meridian's education feeds carried old archive footage—colony promotional material from decades back, including, she happened to know, exterior shots of the deep-storage barges. Frost on metal. Round doors. A child's mind could assemble the rest.

Except for the cargo code. Except for the handwriting.

She hadn't told anyone about that. Three nights ago, Ilsa's drawing had circulated through the colony's parent channels—a child's rendering of the dream, charcoal on salvaged paper, passed hand to hand with the uneasy laughter adults use to bury fear—and Kestrel had looked at it for exactly four seconds before closing her terminal and sitting in the dark for an hour. Because along the bottom edge of the drawing, in careful block letters no eleven-year-old would invent, was a handling code: *KT-114-DELTA-FROST*. A cold-chain routing prefix. One she had used herself, twice, hauling medical cryo on contract runs six years ago. A code that existed nowhere public, nowhere teachable. A code that had belonged to the original Kestrel Thackeray's early manifests, back when the woman whose name Kestrel wore had still been alive to write it.

And the letters were in her own handwriting. Not similar. Identical—the same cramped delta, the same way the crossbar of the T hooked left. She had stood in front of a child's drawing and recognized her own hand, and the hand was not hers, and both of those statements were true at once, and she had poured a drink and decided firmly not to think about it.

Decisions like that, she was learning, had a shelf life.

"Why me?" she asked aloud. "Out of forty thousand people on this rock. Why does the dream send you to me?"

Ilsa wrapped both hands around the milk bulb. "Because you're the woman in the door."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only answer there is." The girl shrugged. "Priya thinks the dream means we're supposed to find whoever the door belongs to. Marcus thinks it's a ghost. My mom says it's stress from the truce countdown, everyone's parents say that, they say it louder every day." She looked up. "But the frost lady walked toward your ship. I didn't decide that. I just followed."

"Frost lady."

"In the dream. After the door opened. She's made of the frost that comes off the walls, sort of, if the frost was a person. She doesn't talk. She walks, and you follow, and everything she walks past remembers being cold." Ilsa sipped her milk. "She looks like you too. But sad."

Kestrel set down the coffee. Her hands were steady, which took effort, and the effort showed, and Ilsa saw it, because children see everything and understand almost none of it correctly, which might be mercy's cruelest design.

"What happens in the dream after you follow her?"

"I haven't gotten there yet. The dream ends before I catch up. Every night it ends a little later, though." Ilsa's voice dropped, and for the first time the composure flickered, just at the edges, like a light with a failing ballast. "Last night I got close enough to see that she's counting. On her fingers. Like she's counting us. And when she finished counting she looked right at me and held up one finger, like—like wait. Like my number wasn't called yet." Her knuckles whitened on the bulb. "I don't want to be counted. I want to know what the counting is *for*."

The cabin hummed. Somewhere below them, Dock Nine went on with its indifferent machinery. Forty days—that was the number the whole fleet was chewing on, the truce clock, the countdown to war footing that hung over every conversation like weather. People said *forty days* the way sailors once said *storm coming*, meaninglessly, constantly, as if saying it enough might change the arithmetic.

Forty days until the factions stopped pretending. And now a child was telling her that something in the dark was counting heads.

"I'm taking you back," Kestrel said. "Today. Before I cast off."

"You'll miss your window."

"I'll find another window. Windows are cheap. Kidnapping charges aren't."

"I stowed away. You didn't kidnap me. That's literally the opposite."

"Do you argue with everyone, or am I special?"

"You're special," Ilsa said solemnly. "Most adults give up faster."

* * *

Kestrel walked the ship to clear her head, which meant walking away from the problem in a confined space until the problem was behind her at all times. Past the cargo bay with its strapped-down membrane spools. Past the engine housing, warm and steady as a sleeping animal. Past the aft lock, its maintenance crawlway hanging open like a pulled tooth, the seal code blinking amber—unchanged since the last inspection cycle, exactly as the girl had said, exactly as her father complained.

That was the detail that snagged her. Not the dream. Dreams were weather. But the crawlway code, the camera loop, the timing—Ilsa Voss had planned this. An eleven-year-old had surveilled a stranger's ship, learned its rhythms, and executed an infiltration that would have embarrassed half the freelancers Kestrel had ever hired. Children did not do that because of a nightmare. Children did that because of certainty, and certainty in a child that age came from one of two places: desperation, or instruction.

She stood in the aft corridor and let herself think the thought she'd been refusing since the drawing.

*Someone taught her. Or something did.*

Twelve years ago, a woman with her face had died with an open murder file and a head full of memories that had been copied, imperfectly, into a vat-grown body that woke up owing a debt it never agreed to. Twelve years ago, somewhere in the fleet's records, a line had been drawn between the original Kestrel Thackeray and the copy, and everything above the line was inheritance and everything below was hers, and she had spent twelve years keeping the two halves from touching. Clean record. Quiet runs. Freight asks no questions.

And now the line was bleeding. Through children's dreams, through a dead woman's cargo codes appearing in a living child's charcoal, through a frost-shaped woman walking out of a round door with Kestrel's face, counting.

She went back to the cabin.

Ilsa hadn't moved. She was asleep in the copilot's chair, finally, curled sideways with the empty milk bulb still in her hands, and in sleep her face lost its armor and became simply what it was: a tired kid who had crossed a dark dock alone because nobody bigger would listen. Her breathing was slow and even. No nightmare. Whatever haunted her waited until proper sleep, deeper down, where Kestrel couldn't follow.

Kestrel stood in the doorway for a long time.

There was a version of this where she shook the girl awake, walked her to the dock shuttle, paid the fare, and filed the incident report, and the fraying record frayed a little more, and the frost lady stayed a rumor, and the door stayed shut, and Kestrel Thackeray the second went on being invisible until invisibility was all that was left of her. That version was safe. That version was twelve years long already and she knew every step of it by heart.

There was another version. In that one, the girl in the chair was a message that had finally gotten through every filter Kestrel had built, and messages like that didn't come twice, and turning it away meant something specific. It meant becoming one of the people who hear a child say *nobody listens to me* and respond with procedure. It meant standing on the far side of a locked door from someone freezing in a corridor, and calling the lock safety.

She hated that arithmetic. She hated more that it was correct.

"Damn it," she said softly, to no one, to the original, to whoever was counting.

She crossed the cabin, lifted the girl—light, alarmingly light, all elbows and borrowed jacket—and carried her to the bunk, where Ilsa subsided into the mattress without waking, one hand curling instinctively around the blanket's edge. Kestrel stood over her for a moment. Then she went to the comm panel, opened a channel to Meridian dock control, and canceled her departure window with a lie about a coolant fault that would cost her the contract and possibly the client.

Then she sat down across from the sleeping child, pulled up the colony's parent-channel archive, and opened the drawing again. *KT-114-DELTA-FROST.* Her own dead hand, writing from beyond its grave.

The dream had changed last week, Ilsa said. The door had opened a little. A voice had said *the counter needs her hands.*

Whose hands, Kestrel wondered, staring at the frozen image. Hers? The original's? Or the thing wearing both their faces?

On the bunk, Isla stirred and murmured something in her sleep—two words, clear enough to catch:

"She's waiting."

Kestrel looked at the girl, then at the drawing, then at the amber blink of the unlocked crawlway seal, and understood that her window had already closed. Whatever came next, it was coming aboard either way.

She keyed the comm. "Meridian, this is *Odile's Wake*, new departure request—first available, priority flag." A pause, static, the dock controller's bored acknowledgment.

Priority flag. There it was: her clean record, lighting up like a flare.

Behind her, in her bunk, the child slept on, dreaming of frost, and somewhere down that long cold corridor, something with Kestrel's face turned another page of its count.
