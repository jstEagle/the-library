## 11. The Vault Barge

The derelict hung in the dark like a drowned cathedral.

It had drifted three days since the patrol skirmish, tumbling in a slow, patient rotation that made its running lights wink at them through the frost-fogged viewport—green, green, dark; green, green, dark—like a lighthouse for a coast that no longer existed. Kestrel had spent those three days telling herself she was going to report it and move on. She'd even drafted the transmission twice, both times deleting it before it reached the relay.

Derelicts were salvage claims. Salvage claims were paperwork. Paperwork had names on it, and her name was already fraying at the edges.

So instead she found herself cutting thrust a hundred meters off the barge's dorsal hull, watching the thing turn against the stars, while Ilsa pressed her forehead to the glass beside her and breathed fog onto it.

"It's bigger than the dream," the girl said.

"The dream isn't real."

Ilsa didn't argue. That was the worst part of her—the not-arguing. She just watched the lights cycle their dead green rhythm and said, "Green, green, dark. It's counting."

Kestrel's hands went cold on the yoke. She hadn't told the child about the lights' pattern. Hadn't described the derelict at all beyond *a hulk in the DMZ*. But there it was, the same cadence Ilsa had drawn in crayon on the back of a cargo manifest: two lights, then nothing. Two lights, then nothing. A corridor of frost, measured out in heartbeats.

"Stay on the ship," Kestrel said.

"You said that about Meridian too."

"And you stayed on Meridian until you stowed away in my coolant bay, which nearly cooked you, which is why you're going to stay on the ship now."

"I'm coming." Ilsa said it the way children say things they have already decided are true, with the serene authority of someone who has never once been overruled by reality. "The dream says the door opens for two. One to knock, one to answer." She paused. "You're the one who knocks."

*The woman in the door.* Kestrel looked at her own reflection ghosted over the turning hulk—pale, hollow-eyed, wearing the original's face like a coat that no longer fit—and felt something in her chest tilt, slow and structural, like cargo shifting in a rough burn.

She should have locked the girl in an airlock and flown home. Instead she ran the boarding checklist.

* * *

The barge's spine was a kilometer of rust-brown alloy, pocked with micrometeorite scarring, its registry plates burned away so cleanly it looked deliberate—not scavenged but *edited*. Kestrel's suit lamp swept across the hull and found the docking collar still pressurized, still cycling, its status board blinking amber with the patient idiocy of machinery that did not know it had been abandoned.

"That's wrong," she murmured.

"What is?"

"A derelict doesn't keep its air." She keyed the collar's diagnostic. "Atmosphere's at seventy-one percent standard. Someone's maintaining it. Or something is."

The inner lock opened with a hiss that sounded, in the silence, almost like an inhalation.

Inside, the cold hit first—dry and mineral, the cold of deep storage—and then the smell: ozone, antiseptic, and underneath it something faintly sweet and organic that Kestrel's hindbrain identified as *cryofluid* before her conscious mind caught up. Her mouth went dry. She knew that smell from training sims, from emergency-response manuals, from exactly one other place: the medical wing where she had woken twelve years ago, naked and new, with another woman's memories settling into her like sediment into water.

The corridor ahead stretched away under strips of failing light. Frost furred the walls in long white feathers. Somewhere far down the dark, a pump cycled. Green, green, dark.

"This is it," Ilsa whispered. Not a question. She wasn't even pretending to be afraid, which frightened Kestrel more than anything else on the ship.

They walked.

* * *

The pods began at the first junction.

They lined the corridor in double ranks, hundreds of them, coffin-shaped and frosted to opacity, each one hooked into the ceiling's umbilical veins by cables thick as Kestrel's arm. Her lamp moved down the rows and the frost gave back her own reflection, warped and multiplied—a hundred pale faces floating in the dark, all hers, none of them quite.

"Status board," she said aloud, because saying things aloud kept them ordinary. She found the master panel at the junction, cracked its ice skin with the heel of her glove, and woke the display.

The screen stuttered, thought about it, and came up amber.

**POD COMPLEMENT: 4,112**
**ACTIVE CRYOSTASIS: 2,047**
**TERMINATED/EMPTY: 2,065**

Kestrel stared at the number. Half. Half the pods empty. She scrolled for the maintenance log, expecting decay, expecting failure—expecting the slow arithmetic of a ship dying alone in the dark.

What she got was a ledger.

**POD 0117 — OCCUPANT RELEASED — AUTH: VAULT ADMIN — DATE: YR 12, DAY 44**
**POD 0118 — OCCUPANT RELEASED — AUTH: VAULT ADMIN — DATE: YR 12, DAY 51**
**POD 0121 — OCCUPANT RELEASED — AUTH: VAULT ADMIN — DATE: YR 12, DAY 58**

Down, and down, and down. Thousands of entries. Regular as tide tables. Every seventh day or so, sometimes two, sometimes five, always the same authorization string: **VAULT ADMIN**, followed by a checksum that resolved, when she queried it, to a council-grade encryption key.

"They were released," she said. Her voice came out flat, strange, like someone else's. "Not failed. *Released.* Somebody's been waking them up. For years."

"How many years?" Ilsa asked.

Kestrel pulled the earliest entry. The date made her stomach drop through the deck plating: eleven years, nine months ago. Three months after the original Kestrel Thackeray died.

The case file had gone cold three months after the murder. And this ledger had warmed up right on schedule.

"Kess." Ilsa tugged her sleeve. "Look."

The girl was standing at the nearest pod, wiping frost from the observation port with her bare palm—Kestrel snapped a reprimand halfway out and swallowed it, because inside the port there was nothing to protect. The pod was empty. The gel had been drained, the restraint couch stripped, the occupant cradle folded up neat as a made bed. Taped to the inside of the port, facing outward, was a printed label:

**POD 0113 — MANIFEST ENTRY REVOKED — SEE AMENDMENT 88-C**

"Revoked," Kestrel repeated. She moved down the row. Pod after pod, the same label, the same amendment number. She found a terminal recessed between pods 130 and 131, jacked her slate into it, and pulled Amendment 88-C.

It was beautiful work. That was the horror of it—it was *beautiful*. Whoever wrote it understood manifests the way surgeons understand flesh. Occupants released under emergency medical authority. Records consolidated. Names struck from the vault complement and cross-filed into transit-loss categories in the colonial registry—*lost in transit*, the phrase every spacer used for the people who went out an airlock or missed a jump window, the phrase that meant *grieved briefly, investigated never*. Each revocation carried a reason code. Medical futility. Custodial transfer. Administrative duplication.

Administrative duplication. Two thousand human beings, filed under the same heading as a double-printed shipping form.

Kestrel stood very still in the frost-light and let the cold settle into her bones, because if she let herself feel what was actually moving through her chest, she would start breaking things, and breaking things would get Ilsa killed.

"Are they alive?" Ilsa asked quietly. "The ones who were released?"

"I don't know." It was the truth, and the truth was worse than either answer. If they were dead, it was murder on an industrial scale. If they were alive—if they'd been woken and moved somewhere, quietly, one every few days for twelve years—then somewhere in the fleet there were two thousand people walking around with no records, no names, no legal existence at all. Ghosts. Like her. Except she had chosen her ghosthood, more or less, and these people had been *assigned* theirs by an algorithm with a council seal.

She thought of Chapter Six—the patrol ships trading fire over this very hulk, both factions claiming salvage, both willing to shoot for it. They hadn't been fighting over scrap metal. They'd been fighting over *this*: four thousand sleepers, half of them already unmade on paper, the rest still breathing in the dark. Leverage. Labor. Votes. Bodies.

"Okay," she said. "We're copying everything."

* * *

The copy took four hours, and it was during the fourth hour, wedged in the junction with her slate tethered to the archive core while Ilsa dozed against a pod's warm flank, that Kestrel found the anomaly that turned dread into certainty.

The release schedule wasn't random. She'd assumed it was bureaucratic—whenever a review board convened, whenever a quota cleared. But sorted chronologically, the releases clustered. Always within days of faction summits. Always within days of council votes on fleet resource allocation. Always, in the last two years, within days of truce-renewal negotiations.

Two thousand people, woken and erased in installments, timed to the political calendar like deliveries.

And there was one more thing. Buried in the checksum structure of the VAULT ADMIN key, wrapped so deep in the encryption padding that only someone who'd spent twelve years reading other people's freight codes would ever think to look: a secondary signature. Not a name. A mnemonic hash—the kind of private stamp a paranoid administrator uses to sign work they can't afford to have traced back to them.

She copied it. Committed it to memory, character by character, the way she memorized customs rotations and patrol schedules. Then she wiped her access trail, pulled the slate, and sat in the humming dark with her heart slamming.

Because she recognized the hash. Not the person behind it—but the *format*. Council-grade. Inner circle. The kind of signature that belonged to someone who never had to explain themselves to anyone, because everyone who could ask was either bought or buried.

Someone who never sleeps, the original's papers had said. She'd read that line a hundred times since the estate documents cracked open, and it had always sounded like poetry. Sitting here among the drained pods, it sounded like a job description.

A soft weight settled against her shoulder. Ilsa, half-asleep, cheek cold through the suit fabric.

"In the dream," the girl mumbled, "there's a room past the pods. With a desk. And on the desk there's a book that isn't finished yet."

"There's no desk, Ilsa. There's no book."

"Not yet," Ilsa agreed, and closed her eyes.

Kestrel should have laughed. Instead she looked down the corridor, past the ranks of frosted coffins receding into the dark, and saw—at the far end, where her lamp couldn't reach—a shape that might have been a bulkhead door, and might have been something else, standing very still.

Her lamp flickered. Green, green, dark.

When the light steadied, the shape was gone, and the master board at the junction had changed. New text, amber and patient, scrolling across the bottom of the screen beneath four thousand names:

**QUERY LOGGED. WELCOME BACK, ADMINISTRATOR.**

Kestrel's blood went to ice water. She hadn't logged in as anyone. She hadn't entered credentials. The system had looked at her face—or her biosignature, or her genetic tag, indistinguishable from the original's—and known her.

The board blinked. A single line appeared, waiting, cursor pulsing like a heartbeat:

**WOULD YOU LIKE TO CONTINUE WHERE SHE LEFT OFF?**

Behind her, Ilsa stirred and said, in a voice gone suddenly clear and awake: "That's the knocking. Now somebody answers."
