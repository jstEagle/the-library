## 19. Rhade Closes In

The trouble with archives, Councilor Dev Rhade had learned across thirty years of governance, was that they remembered everything and understood nothing. A ledger did not know which entries mattered. A database could not tell the difference between a clerical error and a confession. For twelve years he had trusted the colony's records the way a man trusts a well-trained dog—loyal, obedient, incapable of betrayal.

Then, on the morning of the forty-first day before truce expiry, his private terminal flagged an anomaly in the anomaly reports.

Someone else was looking for them.

He read it twice, standing at the window of his quarters while Meridian's artificial dawn crawled up the habitat ring. The system that watched for watchers—a modest piece of software he'd commissioned a decade ago and never needed until now—had caught a search pattern threading through the sleep-record databases. Not a breach. Nothing so crude. Someone with legitimate archivist credentials was pulling fragments: erased entries, routing logs, authorization chains. Pulling them slowly. Carefully. In an order that suggested the person already suspected what they would find.

Rhade poured his tea and let it cool untouched. He did not need to check the credentials. There were only four people in the colony with access depth sufficient to find those seams, and three of them were loyal to him in ways that had been expensive to arrange.

Tomas Vane.

He sat down and called up the young man's file with the fondness one reserves for a promising student. Twenty-nine years old. Third-generation fleet, parents lost in the transit accidents—or what the manifests called transit accidents, though Rhade knew better than anyone how few of those losses were accidents. Vane had clawed his way up through the archive hierarchy on sheer meticulousness, the kind of clerk who corrected other people's footnotes without being asked. He had applied twice for a council research posting. Both applications sat unanswered in Rhade's own queue, because Rhade had read them personally and recognized the appetite underneath the polish.

Ambition. That was all this was, probably. A hungry man digging through old dirt hoping to find something shiny enough to trade for advancement.

Probably.

But *probably* was a word that had killed better men than Tomas Vane, and Rhade had not survived this long by letting probably live.

* * *

He summoned Vane at midday, through official channels, with a pretext so bland it insulted them both: a routine audit of pre-truce record integrity, ahead of the faction negotiations. Let the boy sweat over whether the summons meant anything. Fear sharpened some minds and scattered others, and watching which way a man broke was itself a kind of intelligence.

Vane arrived at the council offices precisely on time, which told Rhade he hadn't yet decided to run. A guilty man who'd made his choice either came early or not at all.

"Councilor." Vane stood at parade rest in front of the desk, hands folded, eyes steady. Young face, unremarkable, the kind you forgot in a crowd—which was exactly why Rhade had once marked him as useful. Inconspicuous men made excellent instruments.

"Sit, sit." Rhade gestured with genuine warmth. "Tea?"

"Thank you, no."

"Nonsense. You look like a man who's been up all night." He poured anyway, sliding the cup across the desk, watching Vane's throat move as he swallowed. "Record integrity is going to matter enormously in the coming weeks. When the factions sit down to negotiate, every claim either side makes will rest on documentation. Who was where. What was promised. What was lost." He smiled. "I want our house in order before anyone else starts looking through our windows."

"Of course, Councilor."

"You've been doing some housekeeping yourself, I understand."

It was beautiful, really, how still Vane became. Not frozen—the boy was too disciplined for that—but a stillness like deep water, everything moving underneath. "Routine maintenance," Vane said. "Legacy systems degrade. I've been flagging corrupted sectors."

"Mm." Rhade opened a folder on his desk—not the real file, a decoy he'd prepared, thin and dull—and turned it so Vane could see the label. AUDIT: SLEEP RECORDS, CYCLES 4400–4500. The years around the murder. "Corrupted sectors," he repeated pleasantly. "In those particular cycles. Isn't that interesting. Data loss tends to cluster, doesn't it? Around events. Around stress on the system." He closed the folder. "Tell me, Archivist. When you find corruption, what do you do with it?"

"I log it. Report it through channels."

"Through channels." Rhade leaned back, lacing his fingers over his vest. "And if the channel itself were corrupt? If the corruption weren't degradation at all but deletion—deliberate, authorized, necessary deletion?" He held Vane's gaze. "What would you do then, Tomas? May I call you Tomas?"

A long pause. The tea steamed between them like a small surrender.

"I'd take it to someone above the corruption," Vane said finally.

"Yes." Rhade beamed. "That's exactly the right answer. And here's the thing, Tomas—I am above the corruption. Whatever you found, whatever you think you found, I can tell you its context, its history, its necessity. Things that aren't written anywhere because writing them down would have endangered the colony." He spread his hands. "There are decisions from those years that kept two hundred thousand people alive and fed and breathing, and they required sacrifices that no tribunal would ever bless and no archive should ever preserve. You're a historian by instinct. You know what happens when populations learn their survival was purchased with their neighbors. It doesn't make them grateful. It makes them cruel."

Vane said nothing. His stillness had changed quality; Rhade had spent enough decades reading rooms to know the difference between a man listening and a man calculating exits.

"So here is my offer," Rhade continued, warm as a hearth. "Bring me everything. Every fragment, every copy, every note in your own hand. Bring it to me privately, and I will give you what you actually want—which was never the truth, Tomas, because you didn't come looking for truth, you came looking for significance. I'll give you a seat at the negotiation table. Your name on the record-integrity commission. A career with weight to it." He softened his voice. "You've been invisible your whole life in that basement. I can make you seen."

For a moment—just a moment—something flickered across Vane's face that looked almost like temptation, and Rhade catalogued it with satisfaction. Then the boy said, "I'll consider it, Councilor," and rose, and thanked him for the tea, and left with his spine straight and his footsteps even.

Rhade sat alone for a long time after the door sealed.

Then he pressed the contact stud beneath his desk. "Warrantis. Quiet arrest package. One name." He gave it. "No announcement. No tribunal paperwork. Take him during second shift rotation, when the archive corridors are empty. Hold him in Sublevel Four, not the regular cells." A pause. "And sweep his quarters before anyone else touches them. I want to know if there are copies, and if so, where they went."

He set the tea aside, finally cold, and looked out at the habitat ring turning through its false morning. Somewhere below, in the vault barge's failing dark, half of humanity slept and dreamed, and the dreams leaked upward into children's heads like water through a cracked ceiling. Somewhere in the docks, a woman wearing a dead woman's name was asking questions she shouldn't have known to ask.

Two threads now. He'd only planned for one.

* * *

The invitation to Kestrel Thackeray went out that evening, handwritten on actual paper because paper disarmed people in ways screens never did. *Dinner aboard the Council Barge, tomorrow, 1900 hours. I find myself wanting to talk about cargo routes and old times. —D.R.*

Old times. He almost laughed at himself. There were no old times; he'd met the original Kestrel Thackeray perhaps six times, all business, all of it ending the night she died with her investigation half-finished and her killer—himself—standing in the frost-light of a corridor he had since scrubbed from every record. But the clone wouldn't know that. The clone had inherited the memories, or enough of them, and memory was a country where anyone who claimed citizenship got believed.

She arrived punctually, dressed in her worn dockside jacket as if to make a point, and he received her with the full ceremony of the barge's small dining room: real candles, real wine, a view of the turning colony through armored glass.

"Councilor." She took the seat he offered, back straight, eyes doing that quick inventory of exits he'd seen in every smuggler and soldier he'd ever fed.

"Dev, please. We're off duty." He lifted the wine. "You know, I dined with the other Kestrel Thackeray once. Years ago. She ordered the same vintage and complained it tasted like committee meetings." He smiled. "Do you share her palate, or has the intervening time changed things?"

"I wouldn't know what she liked," Kestrel said. "I'm not her."

"No," Rhade agreed smoothly. "Of course not. Legally distinct persons. Though the courts were never quite clear on the inheritance question, were they? Name, ship, memories—" he waved the thought away like smoke. "Philosophy. I invited you for practicalities."

"Practicalities."

"The truce expires in forty days. You run cargo through contested space more often than anyone alive, which means you see things the council doesn't. Patrol movements. Faction postures. Which depots are arming." He refilled her glass unasked. "I'm assembling an informal intelligence circle ahead of the negotiations. People outside the council structure. People whose loyalties are to the colony rather than to any faction." He met her eyes. "People who are very good at not being noticed."

"And in exchange?"

"Immunity." He let the word sit between them, fat and glistening. "Full retroactive amnesty for every manifest irregularity in your shipping history. And I mean every one, Kestrel—we both know your holds have carried things the customs drones were mysteriously blind to. One signature from me and none of it ever existed. You'd be the cleanest ghost in the fleet."

She turned the glass slowly on the cloth. "That's generous."

"I'm a generous man. Ask anyone." He leaned forward, and here it came, the teeth inside the mercy, delivered gently as a gift. "There's one small matter first. An archivist named Tomas Vane has been accessing restricted records—records concerning the cryovault, the selection protocol, events from twelve years ago. He's taken copies. Private copies, unsecured, unencrypted by the sound of it." He sighed, genuinely regretful. "If those fragments circulate before the negotiations—if the factions learn the vault's history from rumor instead of from us—it won't just endanger the talks. It'll endanger the sleepers themselves. Panic does terrible things to life-support priorities. You of all people understand what's stored on that barge."

Kestrel's face gave him nothing. He admired that; the original had been easier to read.

"He trusts you," Rhade continued softly. "I don't know why—he's careful, and you're a stranger—but my sources say he's been asking after you at Dock Nine. Deliver him to me. Unharmed, comfortable, willing. Do that, and the immunity stands, plus a finder's fee in fuel credits that would keep you flying for a decade." He raised his glass. "Consider it mercy, Kestrel. His and yours. Because if my security teams find him first—and they will—they won't be gentle, and you'll have traded a friend's safety for nothing at all."

Silence. The candles bent in the ventilation current. Through the glass, the colony turned, and somewhere beneath them the vault barge hung in its slow orbit like a held breath.

"You've thought about this," she said at last.

"Every hour for twelve years," Rhade said, and let her hear the exhaustion in it, the counterfeit of conscience. "That's what office is, Kestrel. Deciding who carries the weight so everyone else can sleep. I'd hoped never to ask this of you."

She set down her glass, stood, and looked at him for a long moment with those inherited eyes—eyes that had once, in another skull, watched him lie with the same warm smile.

"Dinner was good," she said. "I'll consider your offer."

"That's all I can ask."

She left. He watched the airlock cycle behind her, then sat alone among the candles and opened a secure line.

"She didn't say yes," reported the voice on the other end.

"No. She said *consider*, which means she's already decided against me and is buying time." Rhade swirled his wine, untroubled. "Follow her. Not close—she'll feel a tail. Just the ship. Wherever the Thackeray goes, watch what comes to her." He paused, watching the candle flame hold perfectly still. "Because it will. The archivist, the evidence, something. Ghosts attract ghosts."

He finished the wine and thought about the original Kestrel Thackeray, and how she too had smiled at him across a table once, right before she started digging where the digging gets people killed.

History, he reflected, was not repeating itself. It was simply refusing to stay buried—like everything else in his colony that he had buried badly.

Forty days. He had buried larger problems in less time.

But as he extinguished the candles one by one, Rhade allowed himself, for the first time in twelve years, a flicker of something adjacent to doubt—because the last woman who'd sat in that chair had also considered his offer, and had chosen instead to die asking questions.

And this one, he suspected, was built from exactly the same stubborn material.
