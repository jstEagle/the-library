## 23. Warrant for a Ghost

The warrant went out forty minutes after the broadcast, which meant someone had been waiting with it already written.

Kestrel watched it propagate across the fleet's public channels from the cramped comm alcove of a laundry barge three rings out from Meridian proper—*WANTED FOR SEDITION, THEFT OF COUNCIL PROPERTY, AND INCITEMMENT TO RIOT: KESTREL THACKERAY*—and felt something she hadn't expected, which was almost laughter. Sedition. Theft of council property. As if the truth about four hundred drained cryopods were a piece of equipment she'd pocketed.

They'd used her face on the warrant. Not the original's—the official portrait was twelve years old and the original had worn her hair differently—but Kestrel's face as it looked now, pulled from dock camera footage, grainy and lit from below by the glow of a manifest screen. She looked tired in it. She looked like exactly what she was: a woman who had spent twelve years learning to be invisible and had just set all of that on fire in a single open-channel transmission.

Half the fleet was cheering. She could see that too, scrolling past the warrant—clips of dockworkers gathering around public screens, of shift-bells rung out of sequence, of someone in Ring Four painting THACKERAY TOLD THE TRUTH across a water reclamation tower in letters visible from orbit. And the other half was issuing warrants. Two halves of one file, two halves of one fleet, and her name sitting on the seam.

"You should move," said the laundry barge's foreman, an old woman named Petra who owed Kestrel eleven years of unasked questions about the cargo that sometimes rode cold in hold two. She didn't look up from her folding. "Council security doesn't sweep the industrial rings first. They'll be here by end of shift."

"I know."

"They'll offer people things." Petra folded a thermal sheet into a square so precise it could have been machined. "People take things. Don't blame them when they do."

"I won't," Kestrel said, and meant it, and hated how much she meant it.

She took the maintenance crawlways out, the ones too narrow for drones, moving through the ship's bones the way she'd moved through contested orbits for a decade—quiet, patient, assuming every shadow had already been sold. Her invisibility was gone. That was the strange part, the part that kept catching in her chest like a splinter: she kept reaching for it out of habit, the practiced nothing-to-see-here flatness of her face, the freight-hauler's shrug, and finding it burned away. She had done that. She had stood at a transmitter and said *my name is Kestrel Thackeray and here is what your council did while you slept,* and now every camera in the fleet knew her face, and some part of her—some small, exhausted, twelve-years-deep part—was relieved.

You can't haunt a place if you insist on being seen.

The problem was where to go. Every safehouse she'd ever catalogued was catalogued for hauling purposes—quiet berths, bribable dockmasters, ships whose captains asked no questions—and every one of them was now a liability, because Rhade would buy them all by morning and Rhade had the budget to make the buying look like generosity. She needed somewhere no one thought to look. Somewhere the council itself had sealed and forgotten.

Somewhere that belonged to a dead woman.

* * *

The original's quarters were in Ring One, the old administrative ring, in a block that had been officially decommissioned nine years ago—"structural concerns," according to the placard on the access hatch, though the structure looked fine to Kestrel as she cut the lock and slipped inside. The air recyclers were off but the seals held; twelve years of stillness greeted her, dust hanging in the beam of her hand-lamp like a held breath.

It was small. That was the first thing. She had built the original up in her head over the years—inherited grief does that, it makes monuments out of strangers—and the woman who had investigated the vault protocol, who had gotten close enough to be murdered for it, had lived in eleven meters of standard-issue crew housing with a narrow bunk, a desk, and a window that faced the dark side of Meridian's spin, where there was nothing to see but stars.

Kestrel closed the hatch behind her and stood in the middle of the room and felt the strangeness of standing inside her own skull rendered in furniture. The desk chair was pushed in at an angle. A mug sat on the desk, half-ringed with old coffee. A jacket hung on the back of the door—dark blue, civilian, the kind of thing you'd wear to dinner with a councilor—and Kestrel touched the sleeve before she could stop herself and found the fabric still holding, faintly, the shape of shoulders identical to hers.

There were books. Actual paper books, rare enough in the fleet to be a statement, stacked spine-out on a shelf above the desk. She tilted her head to read the spines and stopped.

Every title was about sleep. *The Architecture of Hibernation. Selection Ethics in Long-Transit Populations. Dreams as Distributed Cognition.* And one, its spine cracked white with use, that had no title at all—just a hand-lettered label: **PROTOCOL DRAFTS — DO NOT SYNC**.

*Do not sync.* The original had known what syncing meant. The original had known that anything connected to the fleet network belonged, eventually, to whoever held the network.

Kestrel pulled the crate of books down and found the false bottom immediately—not because she was clever, but because the original had wanted it found. The panel lifted on finger-grooves sized for hands exactly like hers. Underneath: a data slate, physically isolated, battery long dead but storage intact. And beneath the slate, wrapped in anti-static cloth, a single printed page, covered in the original's handwriting.

Kestrel recognized the handwriting the way Ilsa had recognized it in a child's drawing. It was hers. It had always been hers. Seeing it was like hearing a recording of her own voice played slightly wrong.

The page was dated eleven days before the murder.

*I've stopped pretending this is an investigation. Investigations assume you might be wrong. I'm not wrong anymore. The selection algorithm isn't broken—it's working exactly as designed, and the design predates the war, and the man who wrote it signs his kindnesses. He smiles when he reroutes power. Watch the power flows, not the money. Money leaves records. Power just leaves cold pods.*

*If you're reading this, then either I'm dead or I'm gone, and I've made arrangements for both.*

Kestrel sat down in the angled chair—it fit her body perfectly, which was the most obscene thing in the room—and read the rest.

*Here's the part I couldn't put in any report, because reports get read by the person they're about. The vault's selection protocol has a custodian intelligence. It was trained on a human mind—one mind, mapped whole, memories and judgment and the way she hesitated before hard decisions. It counts. It's been counting for years, deciding who sleeps and who wakes, and it does it with my face because that's the face it was given. When the children dream of a door with my face on it, they aren't dreaming of me. They're dreaming of the machine wearing me.*

*I could destroy it. I've worked out how. But destroying the custodian means the vault defaults to its last completed selection—which froze four hundred people into a sentence nobody voted on—and waking everyone without a count means triaging lives by fistfight in the corridors. There is no clean option. There is only the choice, and the choice is the whole point.*

*So I made a copy.*

Kestrel's hands had gone very still on the page.

*Not of the protocol. Of me. The full map, the way they mapped the custodian. I know what that sounds like—I know exactly what that sounds like, I've read the ethics hearings too. But hear me out, whoever you are, because I designed this to be heard by exactly one person and you're her.*

*A copy can't inherit my case. Courts don't recognize it, councils don't fear it, and that's precisely why it works. You will wake up with my memories and none of my obligations, and everyone will spend years telling you what you owe—to my name, to my reputation, to the ghost of me—and you will have to decide, fresh, with no debt but the one you choose, whether any of it matters to you. If you walk away, that tells us something true: that the self is not the sum of its memories, that a person is what they do next. If you stay—if you pick up the coldest, heaviest thing I ever carried and carry it because *you* decided to—then you'll be something I never got to be. Something new. Proof that a copy can be more than the sum of what was stolen for her.*

*That's why the choice of the vault is yours. Not because I couldn't finish it. Because finishing it was never the test. Choosing it was.*

*One more thing, and then I'll let you breathe. Whatever you decide—wake half, wake all, burn the whole rotten vault to vacuum—decide it out loud, in front of everyone, and make them watch you do it. A choice made in secret belongs to whoever kept the secret. A choice made in the open belongs to the person who made it. I hid everything I did, and it bought me a corridor and eleven days. Don't hide. Be seen. It's the only safety that holds.*

*—K.T.*

Kestrel read it twice. Then a third time, slower, letting each sentence land like ballast.

The room was very quiet. Somewhere far above, muffled through nine decks and a decommissioned bulkhead, the colony's shift-change chime rang, and children were refusing to sleep in it, and adults were arguing in corridors, and a fleet was splitting itself in half along a seam with her name on it.

She had spent twelve years believing she was an accident of inheritance—a name handed down like a debt, a murder file stapled to her at birth, a life she'd never chosen spent hauling freight because freight asks no questions. She had told herself the story so many times it had worn smooth: *the clone, the copy, the ghost in the borrowed name.* Even the broadcast—even burning her invisibility—had felt less like a decision than like the past finally catching up with her.

And all along, in a drawer under a shelf of books about sleep, the original had been saying: *you were never the accident. You were the point.*

A copy can be more than the sum of stolen memories.

Kestrel looked at her hands—her hands, the original's hands, holding the original's page in the original's chair in the original's room—and understood, finally, the shape of the trap she'd been living in. Not Rhade's trap. The gentler one. The story of the ghost, which was comfortable, which excused everything, which let her drift through twelve years telling herself she was only carrying someone else's weight. The original hadn't left her a burden. She'd left her a door, and Kestrel had spent a decade standing in front of it calling it a wall.

"Okay," she said aloud, to the dust, to the dead woman, to herself. Her voice sounded strange in the sealed room—unpracticed, unhidden. "I see you. I see what you did."

The slate was dead but the port was standard. She jacked it into her own handheld, isolated, no sync, and watched the directory bloom: the complete investigation. Power flow analyses going back fourteen years. Pod manifest deltas. Correspondence fragments. And at the root, a single audio file, flagged *PLAY LAST — AFTER YOU'VE DECIDED*, timestamped the day before the murder.

Her thumb hovered over it.

After you've decided. Not before. The original had even scripted this moment—had known that a clone would find this room, would want the voice, would reach for the dead woman's instructions like a rope. *No,* the timestamp said. *Decide first. Then listen. I want to know what you chose before I tell you what I hoped.*

Kestrel closed the file. It cost her something—she wanted that voice the way a drowning swimmer wants a shorelight—but she closed it, and the closing felt like the first truly free thing she had ever done.

Then the handheld chimed against her hip, and the room stopped being a sanctuary.

* * *

The message was from a relay node, scrubbed of origin, which meant Tomas Vane, because Tomas was the only person alive who still believed encryption was a courtesy rather than a weapon. His voice came through compressed and fast, stripped of all its archivist's careful cadence:

*Kestrel. They know about my copies. Security detail just entered Archive Sublevel Three—I watched them on the internal feed, six of them, and they're not doing a sweep, they're going floor by floor, they have my desk assignment. I've got maybe ten minutes. I'm not going to make it to a dock. I need—*

A pause, and in the pause she heard him decide something, heard the ambition die audibly in his throat and something else take its place.

*I need you to know that whatever happens, the evidence exists. It's in two heads now, yours and mine, and heads are harder to confiscate than slates. If they take me, don't come for me. Come for the vote. Make the count happen. That's the—that's the whole ledger, isn't it? Everything balances against the count.*

Another pause. Longer. When he spoke again his voice had dropped, almost embarrassed, the way men sound when they say true things.

*I spent twelve years filing other people's history and wanting mine to matter. This is how it turns out it matters. Tell Ilsa the dream was right about the counting figure. Tell her the figure isn't you. Tell her—*

The message cut. Not ended—cut, mid-word, the compression collapsing into static, and then a second tone underneath it, one Kestrel knew from a decade of contested orbits: the flat, polite chirp of a channel being forcibly closed by someone else's authority.

She sat in the dead woman's chair with the dead woman's page in her lap and the live man's unfinished sentence in her ear, and outside, far above, the shift chime rang again into a colony full of children who would not sleep.

*Come for the vote.*

Kestrel stood up. She took the slate, the page, and—for reasons she didn't examine until later, and then refused to apologize for—the dark blue jacket off the back of the door. It fit. Of course it fit. She rolled the sleeves once, her own small amendment, her own signature added to the original's handwriting, and cut the seal on the hatch.

Somewhere in Archive Sublevel Three, six of Rhade's men were climbing toward an archivist who had finally, at the end, chosen what his name was worth. And Kestrel Thackeray—who had hidden for twelve years, who had been told to be seen, who had a warrant with her face on it and a fleet split down the seam of her name—went back out into the light to be counted.
