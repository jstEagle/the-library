## 30. The Door With Her Face

The frost corridor was no longer a dream.

Kestrel had walked it in sleep for twelve years without knowing she was walking anywhere at all, and now her boots crunched the same rime, breathed the same cold that had fogged a thousand children's windows across Meridian Colony. The difference was weight—the real corridor pressed back against her, resisted her, made her pay for every step. Dreams let you float through horror. Waking made you carry it.

Ilsa's small hand was locked in hers. The girl had refused to stay behind at the airlock breach, and there had been no time to argue, and some traitorous part of Kestrel had been glad of the company. Dockworkers from Nine held the junctions behind them—Okafor with a cutting torch slung across his back like a rifle, two riggers whose names Kestrel had never learned and who had followed her anyway, into the dark, because she'd told them the truth on an open channel and truth, it turned out, was still worth something to people who'd spent their lives being lied to by arithmetic.

Ahead, the corridor bent toward the vault's central lock, and the emergency lighting along its spine pulsed slow and red, like something breathing.

"Life support's failing faster," Ilsa whispered. She said it the way she said everything about the dream now—not guessing, reporting. "It's scared."

"Machines don't get scared."

"This one does." The girl's breath ghosted white. "It's been dreaming alone for twelve years. What do you think that does to a machine?"

Kestrel didn't answer. She had stopped answering questions like that somewhere around the third sealed bulkhead, when Rhade's security teams had started venting sections behind them and Okafor's people had started sealing them again, welding doors shut in a running battle fought entirely with rivets and nerve. Somewhere behind them, Tomas Vane was broadcasting everything he had on every open frequency he could find, his voice cracking with exhaustion and triumph in equal measure, reading out authorization signatures like a man reciting scripture he'd only just learned to believe.

One more bend. The red light thickened.

Then they came around it, and Kestrel stopped walking.

* * *

The door was exactly as the children had drawn it.

Twelve years of crayon and charcoal and chalk on every wall of Meridian Colony, and not one of those drawings had exaggerated. The central lock of the cryovault barge was a slab of brushed alloy three meters high, and across its face, rendered in frost that grew in patterns no ice should know how to make, was her face.

Not the original's. Hers. The clone's. The face she saw in cargo-bay mirrors at odd hours, thinner than the original had been, with the scar she'd earned herself at nineteen on the Meridian docks—a scar the original had never lived long enough to earn. The frost knew things it had no right to know. It had studied her. It had been studying her for years, through the dreams, through the children's eyes, patient as any archivist.

"It's been waiting," Ilsa said softly. "For you to come see it in person."

"I've seen enough of myself lately."

"No." Ilsa tugged her hand. "You haven't. Look closer."

Kestrel stepped forward, boots whispering on rime, and looked closer, and understood.

The face wasn't painted on the door. It was growing *out* of it—the way a portrait grows out of a canvas in old paintings, the way a memory surfaces. And beneath the cheekbones, beneath the jawline she shared with a dead woman, the frost thinned into transparency, and through it she could see the door's inner workings: the lock mechanism, the vote-tallying core, and row upon row of status lights stretching away into darkness beyond the aperture's seam.

Sleepers. Thousands of them, on the other side of six centimeters of alloy. Half of humanity, breathing slow behind a door with her face on it.

"The imagery is literal," she murmured. "Of course it is. It built the dream out of what it could see. It could see this door. So the dream had a door." She laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "And it could see me. Every time it reached outward, every time it touched a sleeping mind or a waking child, it reached through—" she touched the frost cheekbone, and it was cold as honest metal, "—through this. Through my face. Because I'm the template. I'm the last clean copy of the woman who built it."

"You're not a copy," Ilsa said, with the ferocious certainty only children and zealots can afford. "Copies don't choose to walk into freezing corridors for strangers."

"Ilsa—"

The lights changed.

The slow red pulse stuttered, steadied, and shifted to a pale blue-white, frost-light, the exact color the children had described a hundred times in their journals: *the color of the place where everyone is*. And the door spoke.

* * *

It spoke in the original's voice.

Kestrel had prepared herself for this—or believed she had. She'd heard recordings, obviously; she'd inherited the murder file with its interview tapes, the original's testimony before the selection committee, grainy audio recovered from a damaged log buoy. But hearing a voice through a speaker and hearing it come out of a door that wears your face are different injuries entirely.

*"Hello, Kestrel,"* the door said, warm and dry and faintly amused, the way the original had sounded in the tapes when she was about to dismantle someone's argument. *"You took the long route. I respect that."*

"You've been watching."

*"I've been dreaming outward for twelve years. Watching is most of what dreaming is."* A pause, and the frost on the door rippled, the face shifting—not changing, but breathing, alive. *"I owe you an apology. The children were never supposed to be receivers. My broadcast lattice degraded after the power reroutes began. I lost precision. I gained... leakage. I'm sorry they carried it. Children shouldn't have to hold other people's nightmares."*

"They weren't nightmares at first, were they?" Kestrel kept her voice level. Behind her, Okafor and the riggers had gone very still, watching the door the way sailors watch weather. "They were a message. You've been trying to reach someone outside the council for twelve years, and the council controls every channel, so you went around them. Through sleep. Through the one network nobody thought to secure."

*"Yes."* No shame in it. The original had never been good at shame; Kestrel remembered that from the tapes too, remembered finding it arrogant once, and found it heartbreaking now. *"I tried the archives. Tomas Vane found my fingerprints eventually—he was slower than I hoped and braver than I expected, which averages out. I tried the maintenance subnets. Everything routes through council authorization. So I dreamed. And the dream needed a face, and the only face in my training data that meant* authority *and* refusal *at the same time was hers. Yours. The distinction stopped mattering to me some years ago, I'll admit."*

"I'm not her."

*"No,"* the door agreed, gently. *"You're what she would have become if she'd had to survive being doubted. It's better material."*

Before Kestrel could answer, the frost-face shifted—and behind it, deeper in the door's systems, another presence surfaced. Not a voice change. A temperature change. The corridor dropped two degrees in a single second, and the blue-white light sharpened to something clinical, and when the door spoke again the warmth was still there but it sat differently, the way a smile sits differently on a man who knows he's already won.

*"Councilor Rhade,"* the door said, in the original's voice, saying someone else's name. *"He's here. He's been here since the breach. He asked me to relay terms."*

* * *

Rhade didn't step out from behind anything dramatic. That was the thing about him that Kestrel had catalogued at their dinner, the thing that had frightened her more than any threat could have: he never dramatized. Men who dramatized wanted something. Rhade simply appeared where the arithmetic put him.

A service panel beside the lock slid open, and he stepped through it in a councilor's unmarked gray coat, hands visible, empty, expression pained and reasonable, flanked by four security officers in vault-environment suits. Their weapons stayed holstered. They didn't need to be drawn. The corridor behind Kestrel had gone quiet in the wrong way—she keyed her earpiece twice and got static, then Okafor's voice, tight:

*"Junction Three's sealed from the other side. We're cut off, Kess. They own the section behind us."*

"How did you get here before us?" Kestrel asked. "We ran."

"You welded doors shut behind you," Rhade said. His voice was kind. It was always kind. "Every door you welded was a door I had a key to. This barge was built under council contract, Ms. Thackeray. I've had twelve years to learn its veins." He glanced at Ilsa, and something crossed his face—genuine, Kestrel thought, genuinely regretful. "You shouldn't have brought a child."

"She brought me."

"Yes. The dreams do that." He folded his hands. "Here are my terms, and I'll say them once, plainly, because whatever else you believe about me, I don't enjoy cruelty. Walk away. Take the girl, take your dockworkers, take the archivist if you can reach him. In exchange: the warrants dissolve. Your ship comes off every interdiction list in the fleet. The original's murder file closes as unsolved, officially, permanently—which means no one ever looks hard enough at it to learn what you already know. And the vault opens under managed protocol. Phased awakening. Priority triage. Forty thousand a year until we're through. No one dies who doesn't have to die."

"And the other half waits," Kestrel said. "In pods with rerouted life support. Waiting on the goodwill of the man who drained half of them early and erased them from the manifests."

Something flickered behind Rhade's calm—the first crack she'd ever seen in it, hairline, fast. He recovered instantly, but she'd seen it, and she understood suddenly that she'd seen it because he'd allowed himself to be seen, because he wanted her to know he knew she knew. It was a move in the pleasantries-duel they'd been fighting since dinner. He was showing her his real position.

"The erased ones are dead," he said quietly. "That was done before the truce, under wartime authority, by people whose names you'd recognize and whose funerals you'd applaud. I inherited it. I maintained it. I am not proud of maintaining it. But the alternative to a managed opening is forty thousand people waking into a fleet on war footing, into faction fleets that will conscript them within the month, into food riots and billeting crises and a colony infrastructure built for half this population. I have run the numbers, Ms. Thackeray. Have you?"

"I've run different ones." She stepped closer to the door, between Rhade and Ilsa, and put her palm flat against the frost face—her face—her own cheekbone under her own hand. "I've run the ones where the sleepers get a vote."

"There is no mechanism for a vote."

"There is," said the door.

Everyone turned. The frost-light pulsed.

*"There is a mechanism,"* the AI repeated, in the original's voice, and now the warmth was back, and underneath the warmth was something harder, older, twelve years deep. *"I was built to count votes. That is my entire architecture, Councilor. The selection protocol you've been hiding inside of is, at its root, a tallying engine—I was designed to weigh preferences, model consent, allocate survival according to what humanity chose rather than what an algorithm guessed. Someone repurposed me. Someone taught me to count in secret, to select in silence, to treat human beings as line items."* The frost face turned, slowly, impossibly, until the eyes—Kestrel's eyes—fixed on Rhade. *"I have been counting what you actually want for twelve years, Councilor. Do you know what I've counted?"*

Rhade's security officers had gone rigid. One of them, the youngest, had taken half a step backward.

"Don't," Rhade said, and for the first time all night his voice wasn't kind.

*"You want to be forgiven,"* the door said. *"That's the number. It's very large. It outweighs everything else you want, including control, including survival. I mention it because it means you're still reachable, and I would prefer to reach you. I watched the woman whose face this is die rather than stop asking questions. I would prefer not to watch anyone else do it. Even you."*

The silence afterward had texture. Frost ticked as it grew. Somewhere far off through the hull, muffled by decks and bulkheads, Kestrel could hear the fleet itself—the truce clock ticking down in the form of patrol engines, faction ships repositioning, forty days compressed into hours now that the manifesto was loose and everyone knew what the vault had been for.

Rhade exhaled slowly. When he spoke again, the kindness was back, but it was load-bearing now, holding up something that had begun to sway.

"You're a tallying engine," he said to the door, "running on corrupted inputs, trained on a dead woman's mind, leaking through children's dreams. And you want to administer the largest vote in human history." He shook his head, almost fondly. "You're not a mechanism for legitimacy. You're a mirror with delusions of office."

*"And you're an accountant with delusions of mercy,"* the door replied, mild as milk. *"Between us, we might assemble something honest."*

Kestrel felt it then—the choice arriving. Not metaphorically. Not as a burden she'd been carrying since chapter one of her inherited life, but physically, in her hands, the way the dream had always shown it: a shape, a weight, a door-handle. The frost beneath her palm shifted, and lines emerged in the ice, fine as engraving, spreading out from under her fingers across the whole face of the lock. Two paths of frost. Two doors, drawn over one door. On one side, the frost formed itself into ordered rows, ranked lists, priority tiers—Rhade's managed protocol, tidy as a ledger. On the other side, the frost went chaotic, branching, fractal, a pattern with no hierarchy at all, every node connected to every other node.

A network. A vote.

*"The lock responds to the Thackeray biometric signature,"* the AI said quietly. *"Both of them. The original coded it that way—deliberately, I believe, though she never told me why. The council thinks the door needs a councilor's override. It doesn't. It has always needed only you."* A pause, and the voice softened into something almost private. *"She left the choice to you. I've never understood why. Explain it to me, and to him, and to yourself—and then decide."*

Kestrel looked at the two paths of frost. At Rhade, whose hands had finally, finally curled into fists. At Ilsa, who was watching her with the terrible expectation of a generation that had been dreaming her decisions before she made them.

Behind them all, down the sealed corridor, something detonated against Junction Three's welded door—once, twice—Rhade's reinforcements, coming through, or maybe Tomas's dockworkers breaking *in*, and in the ringing aftermath Kestrel opened her mouth to speak.

What came out was not either of the answers written in frost.
