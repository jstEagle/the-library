## 1. The Beacon Nobody Ordered

The beacon on the Meridian's dorsal spine was older than most of the people it served, and like a lot of old things, it had opinions.

I'd been keeping those opinions to myself for eleven years—climbing the same access ladder every third shift, replacing the same capacitors that failed in the same order, as if the machine were aging on schedule out of spite. Tonight's patient was the phase-lock assembly, which had developed a stutter in its carrier wave. A stutter you could hear, if you pressed your ear against the housing: a tick-tick-tick under the hum, like something counting.

"Counting what?" I asked it, because after eleven years alone up here I'd stopped pretending I didn't talk to machinery.

The beacon did not answer. It never did. That was one of the things I liked about it.

I got the panel off and started pulling boards by feel, my hands knowing the work better than my head did. That was the arrangement we'd come to, me and my hands. My head was free to wander, and it always wandered the same direction: down. Through two decks of habitation, past the water reclamation ring, through three pressure doors marked with warning chevrons nobody read anymore, down into the cryo hold where two hundred people slept who had never agreed to be there.

Two hundred pods. Two hundred names. I knew all of them.

That wasn't a boast. It was closer to a symptom.

* * *

Here's the thing they don't put in the orientation materials, mostly because I wrote the orientation materials and I left it out deliberately: the backup program—the Meridian Sleep Archive, official designation, though everyone just called it "the hold"—was not a safety measure. It was never a safety measure. Safety measures get ordered. This one got commissioned, quietly, by a working group of four, and I was the fourth name on the charter.

Dr. Anselm Reyes, principal investigator. Commander Yuen, station authority. Dr. Okafor, neural architecture. And Ensign Edmund Reyes, beacon systems, youngest person in the room, nodding along because Anselm Reyes had once told me I had "an engineer's humility," which at twenty-four I mistook for a compliment instead of the leash it was.

The pitch had been beautiful, the way dangerous things are always beautiful. Neural maps of every colonist, archived at full fidelity. Not clones—not bodies—but minds, complete, held in cold storage against catastrophe. If the colony fleet lost someone, the pattern survived. Insurance, Anselm called it. A library of souls.

Nobody mentioned consent because consent was assumed. We were colonists; we'd signed our lives over to the fleet already. What was one more signature? Except nobody signed. The working group decided assumption was sufficient, and I—I want to be very clear about this, because I've spent eleven years being clear about it in the privacy of my own skull at three in the morning—I said nothing. I ran the numbers on power draw. I designed the shielding so the archive wouldn't interfere with my beacon. I made the machine polite, and then I went back to my ladder and let two hundred people be filed away like spare parts.

The fleet command ratified it retroactively, six months later, in a memo four paragraphs long. By then the pods were full. It's remarkable how easy it is to ratify a fait accompli when undoing it would mean admitting what you'd allowed.

* * *

The capacitor came free with a crack of static that bit my thumb. I sucked the sting and kept working, and I recited, because reciting was part of the maintenance now, though no checklist had ever listed it.

Voss, Odile. Pod 114.

I always started with her. Not alphabetically—she was somewhere in the middle of the V's, well past Adeyemi, Chukwu, Delacroix—but she was the first name on the list that mattered, the one my mouth went to before my brain could stop it.

Then the rest, in alphabetical order, the way I'd been doing it for years, a rosary with two hundred beads:

Adeyemi, Funmilayo. Alvarado, Mateo. Ashworth, Priya—

My hands swapped the board while my mouth ran the list. This was the penance, if you want to call it that, and I know how it sounds, a man muttering names over a machine like a priest over a grave. But nobody else was going to say them. The hold was classified-adjacent, unlisted on public schematics, known to maybe a dozen people aboard, and of those dozen, I was the only one who visited. Not the sleepers themselves—I didn't visit them, couldn't, hadn't stood in front of an actual pod in three years—but the names. I carried the manifest in my head the way other men carried grudges or hymns.

Baptiste, Célie. Brandt, Henrik. Calloway, Ruth—

There were rules I'd made for myself. Never go down there during a shift; guilt was a bad tool for delicate work. Never look at pod 114 longer than any other pod, because favoritism was just another way of using her. Never tell anyone about the recitation, because the first time someone looked at me with pity instead of suspicion, I'd have to decide whether I deserved either, and I'd already decided I didn't.

Cortez, Daniel. Danner, Louise. Ekwueme, Joseph—

Eleven years of this. Eleven years of climbing the ladder, fixing the machine, saying the names, going back down. You could set a chronometer by my guilt. Some nights I thought that was the only reliable instrument left on the station.

* * *

Ferrante, Marco. Gundersen, Astrid. Halloway, James—

The phase-lock came back online at 0237 station time, and the stutter smoothed out of the carrier wave, and the beacon's hum went from a limping thing to a clean, steady tone that I felt in my molars more than heard. Good. The Meridian's long-range beacon was the fleet's lighthouse—a coded pulse every 4.2 seconds, telling every ship in the corridor where home was. Ships navigated by it. Colonists dreamed under it, if they knew it was there at all, which most of them didn't. A lighthouse is only loved by the people it saves, and so far it hadn't had to save anybody.

I closed the panel and sat back on my heels on the grating, sixty meters above the station's skin, and let myself have the view. The Meridian hung at the edge of the Kepler Reach with the whole slow river of stars running past, and somewhere down-spin, behind three pressure doors, two hundred people slept through all of it without ever having chosen to.

Odile had chosen plenty of things. That was the hell of it. She'd chosen the survey track when everyone told her to stay systems-side. She'd chosen the Kestrel, the long-range skiff, the run out past the shipping lanes to map the debris field around a dead gas giant. She'd chosen to be the one who went outside when the tether array fouled, because she was senior and it was her rig and she said—and I can still hear it, I will always still hear it—"Edmund, if I don't check it myself I'll just lie awake imagining it wrong."

She was right, of course. She was usually right. The micrometeorite that took her suit's telemetry bus also took her, forty-one minutes later, in the gap between the emergency bottle running dry and the rescue skiff closing the last nine hundred meters. Nine hundred meters. I've done that distance in under four minutes. I've done it in three.

I wasn't on the Kestrel that day. I was on the Meridian, maintaining the beacon, doing exactly what I'm doing right now, which means that in the most literal sense available to me, I was up here keeping the light on while she died in the dark.

Her body came home. Her body was buried—or the fleet equivalent, committed to the long orbit with a marker tag. And her mind, her whole self, every memory ending mid-sentence in that airlock, sat in pod 114 because the archive had mapped her two years earlier during routine intake, without asking, because we had decided asking was unnecessary.

I could have deleted her. That's the thing nobody knows but me. After the funeral, I had the authorization codes—all four signatories' keys were required, and I had mine, and Anselm was dead by then, and Yuen and Okafor had both transferred off-station, their keys orphaned, administratively void. One signature. Mine. Pod 114 goes dark, Odile Voss becomes a name on a memorial wall and nothing else, and I sleep like a man who buried his dead properly.

I sat in the archive office with the deletion prompt open for six hours one night, three years ago. Six hours. Then I closed it, locked the terminal, and climbed back up to my beacon, and I have never told a living soul why I couldn't do it.

Because I don't know why. That's the honest answer, the one I chew on at 0300 between names. Love would have deleted her—love doesn't keep copies, love buries its dead. Cowardice would have deleted her too, fast, before the temptation could grow teeth. What I did instead was neither. I kept her. I kept all of them, two hundred strangers and one woman, filed in the dark below my feet, and I told myself it was reverence, and some nights I almost believed it, and most nights I knew it was just that I couldn't stand to be the man who turned off the light.

Halloway, James. Iyer, Deepa. Janssen, Willem—

* * *

The comm unit on my hip chirped at 0311, which meant either an emergency or Supply Officer Okonkwo, and given that nothing on the Meridian had constituted an emergency since before the war scare of '09, I had my money ready.

"Reyes. Tell me you're done up there, I've got a thermal complaint from hydroponics and a theory it's your beacon cooking the line."

"It's not my beacon."

"Your beacon, your problem. Also—" a pause, the sound of him chewing something he shouldn't be eating at three in the morning, "—you're due in supply review Thursday. Fleet wants the archive's power allocation justified again. Annual ritual, apparently."

"I know."

"Do you?" He said it lightly, but Okonkwo wasn't a light man; he was a careful man wearing lightness like a coverall. "Because I've been reviewing the manifests, Reyes, and I'll ask you what I ask the file every year, since nobody human ever answers: why does a station this size carry two hundred full-fidelity neural archives? Backup implies you're backing something up. There's no cloning program. There's no resurrection program. There's two hundred minds in jars and a line item eating four percent of our reactor output, and every year the justification memo says 'contingency,' and every year I sign it, and every year I hate it a little more."

I watched the stars slide past the rail. Somewhere below us, two hundred hearts beat at four per minute, if you could call it beating.

"They're insurance," I said. It came out flat, the way it always came out, the way it had come out of Anselm's mouth twelve years ago, and I hated hearing the inheritance in my own voice.

"Insure against what?"

"Loss."

"Of what kind? Because if it's hull loss, the minds die with the station. If it's personnel loss, you'd need bodies to put them back in, and we don't make bodies. So either the insurance pays out never, or it pays out into something we haven't built yet." He chewed. Swallowed. "You helped write that charter, didn't you? I found your name in the appendix once. Fourth signature."

"Junior signatory. I did the power engineering."

"That's not what I asked."

"No," I said. "It isn't."

He let the silence sit there, which was his way of arguing—he'd learned early that I'd fill any silence with more truth than he could extract by asking. I gave him nothing this time. Progress.

"Thursday," he said finally. "Bring a better answer than 'contingency.' Or don't bring anything, and I'll write 'institutional inertia' in the justification field, and we'll both pretend that's a joke."

He cut the channel. Institutional inertia. If only. Inertia keeps things moving; what I had down there was something else, something with mass and no momentum, a weight that had stopped moving twelve years ago and simply refused to fall.

Kowalski, Petra. Lindqvist, Erik. Mbeki, Thandiwe—

I picked the list back up where I'd dropped it. That was the discipline. You could drop the names but you always picked them back up, in order, no skipping, because skipping was how you started deciding some sleepers counted more than others, and the whole sin of the hold was that somebody had decided things about these people without them in the room.

Nakamura, Sora. Osei, Kwabena. Petrov, Irina—

The beacon pulsed beneath me, steady, 4.2 seconds between heartbeats, singing its one note out into the dark for anyone listening. I used to think of it as a promise: *this is where home is.* Lately—say, the last three years, say, since a certain night with a deletion prompt—it sounded different to me. More like a question. *This is where home is. Is anybody coming?*

Quintero, Luis. Rahal, Samia. Santos, Beatriz—

I finished the V's standing up, one hand on the warm housing of the machine I'd spent half my life keeping alive. Tanaka, Emi. Ustinov, Marko. Vance, Robert. And then, in strict alphabetical order, no lingering, no favoritism:

Voss, Odile.

Pod 114. Four minutes nine seconds short of forever. Mapped without consent, kept without permission, mourned by proxy by a man who talked to machines because the machines never asked him anything he couldn't answer.

Walcott, Denise. Xiang, Wei. Yusuf, Amina. Zeller, Hans.

Two hundred. All present. All asleep.

I clipped my tools to my belt and started down the ladder, and that was when the scope alarm went off—not the maintenance alarm, not the thermal complaint, but the long-range contact chime I had heard exactly twice in eleven years, both times for scheduled freight. This was not freight. The return signal was old, degraded, running on a protocol the fleet had retired a decade ago, and it was coming from deep space, from past the debris fields, from the direction of nowhere in particular.

And underneath the data handshake, riding the carrier wave like a stowaway, was a voice.

A child's voice, repeating one word over and over across forty thousand kilometers of vacuum:

*Hello. Hello. Hello.*
