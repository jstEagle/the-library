## 4. The Girl Who Was Captain

The first thing you learn when your body betrays you is that everyone else becomes a traitor too. Not on purpose. That's the worst of it. They look at me—Captain Ilsa Moreau, twenty-two years in the deep survey fleet, holder of the Meridian Cross, the woman who threaded the Kestrel Shoals with a hull breach and no navigation computer—and they see a child in an oversized uniform jacket, and their faces do this terrible soft thing, like I'm a photograph of someone they loved.

I have taken to wearing my hair pulled back so tight it hurts. It's a small cruelty I can administer to myself, and it keeps my face from looking soft. Faces matter. I learned that at the Academy, of all places, from a logistics instructor who said that command begins at the jawline. He was being glib. He was also right.

* * *

The Halcyon docked at Meridian Station nine days ago, and I have spent every one of those days fighting a war on two fronts: the war for my ship, and the war against the word "sweetheart."

The docking itself was a humiliation I intend never to describe to anyone who wasn't there. Our comms officer, Vashti Okonkwo, thirty-one years old and biologically eight, had to be lifted into her chair by a station crew member because her arms were too short to reach the console properly. I stood on a crate to address the receiving officer. A crate. I want that recorded somewhere official: Captain Ilsa Moreau commanded the return of the survey vessel Halcyon from a crate.

"Ma'am," the receiving officer said, and then his eyes did the soft thing, and he added, "where's your captain, sweetheart?"

"I'm standing on her," I said. "She's very tired."

He didn't laugh. None of them ever laugh, which tells you everything about how bad it looks from the outside. If I'd been joking, they'd have known. The fact that nobody laughs means everybody understands, on some level beneath language, that this is real and irreversible and happening to thirty people who used to be adults.

We are not "used to be" anything. That's the other front. Medical keeps using phrases like "recovery" and "restoration protocol," as if we're data files that got corrupted, as if there's a clean original version of us stored somewhere and we just need the right checksum. There isn't. There's me, eleven years old, with forty-one years of memory stacked behind eyes that haven't finished growing, and a body that falls asleep at nine o'clock whether I order it to or not.

The body wins arguments I don't even know we're having. Yesterday I fell asleep during my own briefing. Mid-sentence. My executive officer—a man I once watched talk down a mutinous engineering crew through sheer vocal authority, now nine years old and missing two front teeth—finished the briefing for me. He did it well. I've promoted him twice in my head.

* * *

Let me tell you what happened out there, because the station medical staff keep asking me questions designed for children—"Do you remember your name? Can you count backward from ten?"—and because there is exactly one person aboard this station qualified to explain it, and she is currently four foot six and being offered juice boxes.

We were fourteen months into the survey arc, past the Cartographer's Reach, in a stretch of nothing the charts call the Vellum Expanse because somebody with a sense of humor decided empty space should be named after blank paper. We were mapping gravitational shear for the colony lane extension. Routine. Beautiful, even—the kind of deep quiet where the ship hums and the stars don't move and you remember why humans left home in the first place.

Then the clocks started lying.

Not breaking. Lying. Ship's chronometer said 0300; the reactor log said 0314; my own wrist unit said 0257. Three honest instruments, three answers, all self-consistent internally, all disagreeing with each other. Okonkwo ran diagnostics for six hours before she came to me pale and said, "Captain, they're all right."

"They can't all be right."

"Time's running at different rates in different parts of the ship." She held up her hands, which were steady, because Vashti's hands are always steady, whatever size they are. "Forward sections are running fast. Aft sections slow. The gradient is smooth. It's not a malfunction, Captain. It's a field."

A field. In open space, light-years from any gravity well worth the name, our ship was sitting inside something that treated time as a medium—something with texture, with direction, with opinion.

We tried to leave. That's the part the reports will sanitize, so let me be precise: we tried to leave, and the expanse did not want us to go. Not dramatically. No tractor beam, no wall of fire. Just this: every course we plotted out of the region, when flown, curved us gently back toward its center, as though space itself had a preferred shape and we were water finding a drain. Chief Engineer Brandt called it "sailing uphill in a bowl." Brandt is seven now. She handled it better than most of us.

For nineteen days we sat in the bowl, taking measurements, because if you cannot escape a phenomenon, you document it, and documentation is the only immortality the fleet actually believes in.

Here is what we found, and I want this on the record in my own words, because the medical debriefers keep writing "temporal anomaly, origin unknown" in their notes, and "unknown" is a coward's word:

The anomaly was artificial.

I know what I saw. I know what my instruments saw, and my instruments were built by people smarter than either of us. The field had structure. Not the structure of a natural process—natural processes are messy at the edges, turbulent, indifferent. This had edges like a drawing. Concentric shells of temporal gradient, nested with a regularity that made my teeth ache. At the center, a point source. Something small and patient, broadcasting time the way a lighthouse broadcasts light.

And here is the detail I have now repeated to four committees, two medical boards, and one very polite Fleet intelligence officer who kept glancing at his notes as if hoping I'd stop: the shells pulsed. On a cycle. A long one—hours between pulses—but regular, disciplined, deliberate. Natural phenomena do not keep schedule. Storms do not check their watches.

"Something made this," I told them. "Something is running it. And whatever it is, it's been running long enough to get good at it."

They wrote it down. Then they asked me if I wanted to color.

* * *

I'm being unfair. Mostly. The station staff are frightened, and frightened people reach for the tools they have, and for a child-sized captain the only tool they have is kindness. But kindness, aimed wrong, is just condescension with good manners, and I have earned better than that. We all have.

The worst moments aren't the big ones. They're the small ones. Lieutenant Commander Adeyemi, our science lead, physically ten, was refused entry to her own laboratory until she produced a guardian's signature. She produced mine. I signed it CAPTAIN, I. M., HALCYON, in letters large enough to read from orbit, and the security officer looked at the signature, looked at me, and let her through, and I could see him deciding, in real time, whether he'd been tricked or whether the world had simply come apart in a way his training hadn't covered. The world has come apart. His training will catch up or it won't.

My own cabin is a special circle of embarrassment. The station assigned me quarters in family housing. There are glow-stars on the ceiling. Someone left a stuffed animal on the bunk—an absurd round creature with one eye—and I have not removed it, because removing it would mean admitting it affects me, and I refuse to give the universe the satisfaction. Its name is Admiral Pib. I did not choose the name. The name arrived fully formed, the way names do, and I have decided to allow it. Command is about knowing which battles to delegate.

At night, sometimes, the memories get loud. Forty-one years of them, sitting in a skull built for maybe eleven more. Doctors say the brain is remarkably elastic, that it's holding, that the ratio of memory to tissue is unprecedented but stable. Stable. They say it like a weather report. What it feels like is a library that burned down and rebuilt itself overnight in a smaller building, with every book intact and nowhere to put them, so they're all just—stacked. In the halls. In my chest. Some nights I wake up remembering my mother's funeral and my mother's face with equal clarity, and both feel like they happened to someone else, because in every way that matters, they did.

But here is what I hold onto, and I hold onto it the way Brandt held onto her wrench through the worst of it: the mind survived. Whatever that thing in the Vellum Expanse did to us, it took the body and left the self. Thirty minds, whole and continuous, walking around in borrowed childhoods. That's not a tragedy. That's a message. Somebody built a machine that treats human aging as a parameter, and machines with parameters can be argued with.

You just have to speak its language.

* * *

Which brings me to today, and the meeting I forced, and the reason I'm writing this instead of sleeping, which my body is filing formal complaints about.

Fleet Command convened a session on the Halcyon condition. I was invited. Invited, in the way you invite a survivor to a memorial—not as a participant but as evidence. I attended anyway, via the station's conference uplink, seated on three phone books so the camera could find my face. I am not ashamed of the phone books. I am going to be ashamed of what happened next, on behalf of everyone else in that room.

They presented the medical findings. De-aging, uniform across the crew, arrested at ages corresponding to—I want this exact—"a mean of approximately ten point five years." Arrested. As if it were a crime scene. Then the epidemiologist said the sentence I have been turning over ever since:

"The condition appears stable, which suggests the causal agent may still be active, possibly maintaining the affected crew in a steady state."

Maintaining. Steady state. And every head in that room nodded gravely, and someone said "so the priority is containment," and I felt the temperature of my blood change.

Containment. They were talking about containing us. Thirty officers of the Meridian survey fleet, reduced in the space of one briefing from casualties to hazards. And underneath it, worse: if the agent is still active, still maintaining us, then the agent is still broadcasting. Still reaching. The Vellum Expanse is months away at survey speeds—but signals are not ships. Signals travel at the speed of consequence.

So I unmuted my channel, and I said, in the voice I once used to talk down mutinous engineers, which comes out higher now but loses none of its edge:

"You're containment-planning the wrong object. The Halcyon isn't the event. The Halcyon is the receipt. Something out there sent a signal that rewrites cellular time, and it reached us across open space, which means distance is not protection—it's delay. You should be asking what else that signal has already touched. Your beacon. Your archives. Anything on this station old enough to have been listening when the broadcast began."

Silence. The particular silence of important people recalculating.

"And for the record," I added, because dignity is a discipline, "the crew of the Halcyon request to be classified as experts, not patients. Nobody on this station knows more about what that signal does than we do. We are the data. Treat us accordingly."

An older gentleman in Fleet gray—Reyes, the tag said, though not the Reyes, I'd learn later, or maybe exactly the Reyes, the records on that family are a maze—leaned forward and asked, gently, "Captain, how certain are you that the anomaly was artificial?"

"As certain as I've ever been of anything," I said. "It had architecture. It had a schedule. And it had intent, because it caught us, examined us"—because it did, those nineteen days, the gradients shifting around our hull like fingers turning us over—"and let us go. Changed. Nothing natural catches you, studies you, and releases you back into the wild. That's not weather. That's a hand."

He wrote that down. I watched him write it. And I saw the moment his pen slowed—the moment my words stopped being the testimony of a traumatized child and became, for him, a problem with numbers attached. Because if the signal is still active, and if it reaches anything on this station that shares its architecture—

I didn't know then what he knew. I know now that this station keeps two hundred sleepers in its belly, and that its beacon is older than anyone wants to admit, and that old machines and strange signals recognize each other the way dogs recognize thunder.

* * *

It's late. Admiral Pib is watching me with his one eye. My body is staging its nightly coup, and for once I'm inclined to let it win, because tomorrow I begin the work that matters: teaching this station what I know. They've finally agreed—Commander Okafor fought for it, bless her—to let the Halcyon crew consult formally. Experts, not patients. We won that much.

Thirty old minds in young bodies, and the one thing we have that nobody else in human history has ever had: we know what it feels like from the inside when time flows wrong. That knowledge is going to matter. I can feel it coming the way I felt the bowl closing around us—gradually, then all at once.

Because this afternoon, before the meeting, a medical tech mentioned in passing that the station's long-range beacon had been "acting odd." Running warm. Broadcasting something extra, maybe, though the technician laughed it off, said the old thing probably just had ghosts in its code.

I did not laugh.

I have heard a machine keep schedule before. Out in the dark, in the blank pages of the Vellum Expanse, I listened to one pulse for nineteen days, patient as a lighthouse, and I know exactly what it sounds like when something artificial decides to start talking.

If this station's beacon has picked up the accent, then the hand that built the lighthouse has been here all along.

And I intend to find out whose hand it is—even if I have to stand on a crate to do it.
