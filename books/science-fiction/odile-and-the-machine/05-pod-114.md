## 5. Pod 114

The order came down at 0400 station time, which is the hour command reserves for orders nobody wants to receive and nobody wants to sign. I know because I've been on both ends of that arithmetic.

*Directive 7-C: All cryogenic backup units aboard Meridian to be scanned for anomaly-immunity markers. Full neural map extraction authorized. Priority: absolute.*

Priority: absolute. In my experience, "absolute" is what bureaucrats write when they want you to stop asking whether the thing is a good idea. Nobody writes *priority: absolute* about a nice idea. Nice ideas get memos with exclamation points.

I read the directive three times in the beacon room, coffee going cold beside me, the old machine humming its one long note behind my shoulder like a colleague who never learned small talk. The Halcyon's crew had been aboard for six days now—thirty adults in children's bodies, thirty minds doing their best to be patient while medicine poked at them and found nothing wrong except everything. And somewhere in those six days, someone in Fleet research had finally asked the right question, which was: *if the anomaly rewrites cellular time, does anything aboard resist it?*

And the answer, apparently, was maybe. Maybe the backups did. Because the backup process—the one I had helped commission, the one I'd spent nine years not thinking about—predated the anomaly by two decades. Whatever architecture lived inside those pods, whatever signal grammar the engram recorders used to fold a whole human mind into cold storage, it was older than the thing that had unmade the Halcyon. Older meant different. Different sometimes meant immune.

Or it meant related. That was the thought I kept circling like a sore tooth. Related, the way cousins are related. The way my beacon and whatever happened out in the dark might be related.

I pulled up the backup system's original specifications for the first time in years. Anselm Reyes's name sat at the top of the design document like a signature on a confession. My mentor. Possibly my kin—the family tree on Mars was tangled enough that we'd stopped checking generations ago, and he used to joke that Reyes men were born knowing how to build machines that outlive their explanations. I'd laughed then. At 0400, alone, reading his handwriting in the margins of a spec sheet—*the recorder must speak the same language as the carrier wave; this is non-negotiable*—I didn't laugh.

*The same language as the carrier wave.* The carrier wave. My beacon. The machine I maintained, calibrated, loved in the way you love a thing that has never lied to you. Anselm had built the backups to speak its language. Which meant either he'd built them around the beacon, or—and here the tooth throbbed—he'd built them as part of the same project, two organs of one body, and only ever told me about one of them.

I closed the file. I made more coffee I wouldn't drink. And then I did what I always do when a decision gets too heavy to hold standing up: I went down to the hold.

* * *

Two hundred pods. Two hundred names. I've recited them so many times over the years that they've worn grooves in me, litany-style, the way water wears stone without meaning to. It started as penance and became habit and now I honestly can't tell you which it is, which is probably the most honest description of guilt anyone's ever managed.

Aguilar, Tomás. Adeyemi, Funmi. Okafor-Bell, Ruth. I walked the rows with my hands in my pockets because if my hands weren't occupied they'd do something embarrassing, like rest on the glass.

"Reyes." Supply Officer Dhawan caught up with me at the row's end, datapad under her arm, expression set to diplomatic. She'd been asking questions all week—the kind of polite, persistent questions that supply officers ask because they count everything, and two hundred sleepers who consume power and produce nothing are, from her desk, a line item with a bad ratio. "Can I ask you something off the record?"

"You can ask. Off the record I'm allowed to deflect."

"Why do they exist?" She said it gently, which somehow made it worse. "Everyone gives me a different answer. Strategic reserve. Cultural archive. Insurance. But nobody gives me the same answer twice, and you—" she nodded at my hands in my pockets, at the row of glass behind me, "—you talk to them."

"I inventory them," I said. "Out loud. It's an audit technique."

"It's a rosary."

Dhawan had grown up on a ship where religion was contraband and metaphor wasn't, so she got away with things like that. I gave her the official answer—continuity of persons, redundancy against colony loss, standard deep-fleet protocol—and watched her decide, visibly, not to push. She was good at her job. Her job included knowing which doors were load-bearing.

But after she left I stayed, because the honest answer was the one I couldn't give her: they exist because twenty-two years ago a brilliant old man convinced a tired junior engineer that archiving people was just filing, just data, just prudence—and the tired junior engineer signed the feasibility study, and the study became the program, and the program became two hundred people sleeping in a basement who had never been asked. Not one of them consented. There hadn't been a consent framework yet; there'd been a war, and then a scramble, and Anselm saying *we can save them, Edmund, we can save everyone, isn't that worth the paperwork later?*

It was worth the paperwork later. That was the trap of it. It was genuinely, measurably worth it, and I have spent nine years unable to decide whether that makes it better or infinitely worse.

Row twelve. Pod 114.

I don't always stop there. Stopping there is a discipline I practice the way recovering drinkers pass bars—sometimes you walk past fast, sometimes you stand outside and look through the window, and once in a while you go in and sit down and ruin a week. Tonight I stood outside and looked through the window.

Odile Voss slept the way she'd done everything: efficiently, without fuss, as if unconsciousness were a task she'd been assigned and intended to complete ahead of schedule. Face relaxed. Hands folded. The pod's frost-light made her look underwater, some diver paused mid-descent into a sea four years deep.

She died on the Kestrel survey. I keep the details in a sealed compartment and open it maybe twice a year, and each time the contents are heavier than I remembered. A pressure fault in a lava-tube survey habitat on Thessaly-4c. Eleven minutes of decompression protocol that should have worked. Odile staying in the tube to hand-seal a failing valve because the remote actuator was fried and someone had to stay and that someone was always, always her, because she was the fastest and the calmest and she had decided years before I met her that being those things was simply what she was *for*. The valve held. The Kestrel's crew lived. She didn't. Decompression took her in the last ninety seconds, quietly, mid-sentence on the comm—I know because I've listened to the recording exactly once, and the sentence she didn't finish was about dinner.

She'd been dead eleven days when the backup program's first full cycle completed. Anselm brought me the manifest himself, put a gentle hand on my shoulder, and showed me the entry: VOSS, ODILE M., neural capture complete, fidelity 99.2%. He said something kind. I don't remember what. What I remember is the number. 99.2%. I remember thinking: *two percent of her is missing,* and then thinking: *no, ninety-nine point two percent of her is present,* and then understanding, with a clarity I would pay real money to unlearn, that both thoughts were true forever and neither one was bearable.

I requested she be transferred to Meridian. Officially: proximity to fleet archives, chain-of-custody convenience. Unofficially: I could not stand the idea of her sleeping somewhere I'd never see, and I could not stand the idea of her sleeping somewhere I would, and I chose the second unbearable thing because at least it was mine.

Love or cowardice. Nine years, no verdict. Some mornings I'm sure keeping her is the last decent thing I'll ever do. Other mornings I understand that a copy is not a candle kept lit—it's a photograph I refuse to let fade, and refusing is not the same as remembering, and a woman frozen at 99.2% fidelity in a basement is not being honored, she's being *withheld.* From life. From whatever comes next. From herself.

Tonight, though, none of that philosophy mattered, because Directive 7-C had come down with *priority: absolute*, and the scanning order was already scheduled, and somewhere in the queue between Aguilar and Adeyemi was pod 114, and the scan was only the beginning. I'd read the annexes. Scanning was phase one. Phase two, flagged *contingent on findings*: selective revival of high-relevance subjects for interactive analysis.

Interactive analysis. They were going to wake one of them. Maybe several. And every part of me that had spent nine years dreading this exact moment stood up and pointed at the manifest and said, with terrible calm: *you know whose name is highest relevance. You knew before you finished reading the directive.*

Who else? The backup process itself was the lead—the signal architecture, the shared language, Anselm's marginalia. And who aboard understood the backup process from the inside? Who had helped commission it, tested it, argued with Anselm about buffer rates until three in the morning while laughing about something neither of them would remember later? Whose neural map would carry, folded into its structure, the exact patterns the researchers needed?

Hers. Of course hers. The universe has a sense of humor like a boot has treads.

* * *

Command convened at 1300, and I sat through forty minutes of epidemiology and signal analysis with my hands flat on the table like a man steadying himself on furniture. Dr. Ferro presented the scan results from the first fifty pods. Clean, controlled, and damning: every backup's engram encoding carried harmonic structures matching the Halcyon crew's cellular degradation signatures. Not identical. *Homologous.* Same grammar, different sentences.

"The implication," Ferro said, in the tone of a woman delivering news she'd rehearsed hating, "is that the anomaly and our own archival technology derive from a common source. We're not looking at immunity so much as—we're looking at fluency. The backups speak the same language the anomaly speaks. Which means the fastest path to decoding the phenomenon isn't external analysis. It's a native speaker."

"A sleeper," said Commander Osei.

"One sleeper," Ferro agreed. "Revived, cognitively intact, and technically literate enough to work with the captured signal data. The neural maps show engineering backgrounds in several subjects. One in particular."

She didn't look at me. Command staff had been briefed on the program's history; they knew I'd signed the feasibility study; they had the courtesy, or the cruelty, of not making me the exhibit. Instead the manifest came up on the main display, alphabetical, and scrolled, and stopped.

VOSS, ODILE M. — POD 114. RELEVANCE: CRITICAL.

There it was. The choice I had dreaded for nine years, arriving not as temptation, not as weakness, not as some private failure of nerve—but dressed in a uniform, with a directive number, wearing duty's face. That's how these things come for you. Never as the thing you feared. Always as the thing you'd have agreed to anyway, so that afterward you can't even claim you were forced. You were *asked.* You said yes. The yes was correct. The yes will still cost you everything.

"Mr. Reyes." Osei again. "You commissioned this program. Your assessment of revival risk?"

My mouth was dry. Somewhere below us, two hundred people slept through their own futures being decided. "Fidelity holds at 99.2% on her last diagnostic," I heard myself say, in the level voice I use for calibrations. "Memory continuity should be intact up to the point of original neural capture. Post-capture events—her death, the intervening years—she won't have them. She'll wake four years behind the universe, into a body that's been on ice, with a stranger's memories of her own funeral." I paused. "Cognitively, low risk. Every other kind of risk, I can't model."

"And your personal recommendation?"

Every eye on me. The beacon humming somewhere above us, one deck up, faithful and possibly complicit. Anselm's marginalia glowing in my memory: *same language, non-negotiable.*

"Wake her," I said. "If anyone aboard can decode that signal, it's her. Was her. Is—" I stopped, restarted. "Wake her."

Osei slid the authorization across the table. Wake authorization, form 22-K, the bureaucratic equivalent of a defibrillator. One signature. I picked up the stylus and my hand shook—not a tremor, a full honest shake, the kind you can't disguise as adjusting your grip—and I watched my own name assemble itself letter by letter beneath the words *revival hereby authorized,* and I thought, absurdly, of the unfinished sentence about dinner, and I thought: *she'd laugh at me for hesitating,* and I thought: *no. The woman I knew would laugh. This one won't know why she should.*

Because that was the truth underneath the shaking, the truth I finally let myself look at straight-on: waking her wasn't saving her. Waking her was ending the version of her I'd kept, the safe archived grief, the photograph that never faded—and meeting whoever stepped out of the pod instead. A woman with her face, her mind, her sharpness, and none of her history with me. A stranger wearing the most beloved voice I know. And I had just signed the paper inviting her into a universe that had already used her once—as a backup, as a data point, as infrastructure—and might use her again.

"Thank you, Mr. Reyes," Osei said. "Revival scheduled for 0600 tomorrow. Medical will handle induction. You're listed as program liaison—you'll be present."

Of course I would. Duty, dressing me again, buttoning my collar with its cold efficient fingers.

I rode the lift down afterward and stood in front of pod 114 while the maintenance systems murmured their pre-thaw checks, frost-light rippling, and I recited her name once instead of the full litany, just hers, just this time. Then I pressed my palm flat against the glass, which I had never once done in nine years, and said—to her, or to myself, or to the two percent that was missing, I genuinely don't know—

"They're going to wake you tomorrow. I'm sorry. I'm sorry it's me telling you, and I'm sorry it's not your choice, and I'm sorry that the first thing you'll need to forgive in this new life is the man standing here."

The pod answered with a soft chime: thaw sequence armed.

Six hours. In six hours, the choice I dreaded arrives wearing my own signature—and the woman behind that glass opens her eyes, and looks at me, and doesn't know me at all.
