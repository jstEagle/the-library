## 9. Children of the Deep

I was forty-six years old when I last commanded a bridge. I was eleven years old when I did it again this morning.

The Halcyon's bridge has not shrunk. I have measured it twice with a surveyor's laser because I refused to believe my own eyes. The consoles sit exactly where they sat when I had to stand on a step-stool to read the atmospheric telemetry—except now the step-stool is unnecessary and the telemetry is a humiliation written in numbers. My officers used to rise when I entered. Now they rise too, out of habit, and then don't know what to do with their faces, so they arrange them into expressions of professional concern that fool no one, least of all me.

"Captain," said Lieutenant Okafor yesterday—he is nine now, and his voice cracks in the middle of ranks the way it never cracked at thirty-four—"the long-range array is recalibrated. Mostly."

"Mostly."

"The upper frequencies are... high for me now." He said it without self-pity, which I appreciated, and then he ruined it by adding, "I can't reach the manual release levers without climbing on the console."

"Then climb on the console, Lieutenant. You're small. It's the one advantage we've been given."

He grinned. It was a child's grin coming off an adult's relief, and I felt something in my chest turn over—not grief, exactly, though grief was in the neighborhood. Something adjacent. Something I don't have a rank for.

* * *

Let me tell you what the medical staff will not say plainly, because they are kind people and kindness makes cowards of the articulate: there is no reversal protocol. Dr. Senn has tried hormonal cascades, telomere induction, a borrowed regenerative suite from the Meridian's medbay that cost more per hour than my ship earned in a year. The biology holds its new line like a garrison. Whatever happened to us in that region of space didn't damage our cells. It *renegotiated* with them, and the cells signed.

So we are thirty adults wearing childhoods like ill-fitting pressure suits, and the Meridian's command structure has begun using a word I despise: *casualties*. It appears in the reports. "The thirty casualties of the Halcyon incident." As if we died. As if what remains is wreckage to be catalogued.

I intend to make their lives difficult about this.

* * *

It started, as most mutinies do, with a meal.

The Meridian's galley crew, meaning well, began sending up children's portions. Soft foods. Bright trays. Someone—God help me—sent juice boxes. I stood in the officers' mess holding a juice box with a cartoon whale on it and felt forty-six years of command experience compress into a single point of pure fury.

I called a meeting. All hands who were able—all thirty of us, which sounds like nothing until you remember that the Halcyon's full complement was thirty-one, and Ensign Varga's pod failed to protect her, and she came back to us aged backward past memory, past language, an infant who does not know her own name. She sleeps in the Meridian's pediatric ward now. We do not speak of her casually. We speak of her the way you speak of a wound: carefully, and often.

At the meeting I put the juice box on the table.

"This," I said, "is the enemy."

Okafor laughed before he could stop himself. Chen, our engineer, currently eight years old and furious about the height of every workbench on the station, said, "You want to declare war on a beverage."

"I want to declare war on being managed," I said. "Listen to me. Command thinks we are casualties. Casualties get fed, housed, and filed. They do not get consulted. They do not get asked what they know about the thing that did this to them—and we are the only human beings in this system who have been inside that anomaly and returned with our minds intact. Our minds, people. That is not a tragedy. That is a strategic asset wearing a very small uniform."

Silence. Then Chen said, "What are you proposing?"

"A society," I said. "Small, temporary, heavily armed with data. We take care of each other, because nobody else knows how. And we make ourselves indispensable, because indispensability is the only currency command respects."

Vasquez—ten years old, formerly our xenolinguist—raised her hand like a schoolgirl, realized what she'd done, and converted it into adjusting her hair with tremendous dignity. "First order of business?"

"Chairs," said Chen. "Everything on this station is built for people a meter taller than us. I want fabrication access and I want it today."

And that, more or less, is how the Children of the Deep—the name Vasquez coined, and yes, we let her, because a name you choose yourself is armor—began.

* * *

Here is what nobody warned me about: it works.

We fabricated risers and platform shoes and extendable tools. We reorganized the watch rotations around our new bodies' treacheries—I fall asleep at 2100 whether I will it or not, some deep hypothalamic decree, and I have learned to schedule my hardest decisions before dinner. We built a ladder system through the Halcyon's corridors that would embarrass a playground designer and function better than half the station's original fittings.

But the bonds—that's the part I didn't plan, and couldn't have.

There is a particular intimacy in being diminished together. Okafor and Chen, who barely spoke aboard the old Halcyon beyond professional courtesies, now share a bunk room and a running argument about stellar cartography that has lasted eleven days. Vasquez has taken charge of teaching the two youngest of us—Bram, seven, and Ilse, six—how to hold onto themselves. Not memories; those are intact. *Continuity.* The feeling that the person in the mirror is the person who has lived your life. She does it with games. Naming games. "Tell me about the Kestrel relay failure," she'll say to Bram, who was our comms specialist and is now small enough to nap in a supply crate, and he'll recite the whole incident report in a seven-year-old's voice with a specialist's precision, and by the end he is standing straighter.

I sit in on these sessions sometimes. Officially, to observe. Unofficially, because when Vasquez asks me to tell the story of the anomaly, and I tell it, and thirty-some years of command look out of an eleven-year-old's face while I do it—I feel, for a few minutes, like myself. The whole of myself. Not the body. The through-line.

Last night Bram fell asleep mid-sentence during a session, the way children do, dropping from expert analysis into unconsciousness between one word and the next. Vasquez looked at him and said, quietly, "We should keep doing this even after they fix us. If they fix us."

"Do you think they'll fix us?" I asked.

She considered it with the seriousness she'd have given a first-contact translation problem. "I think," she said, "that whatever we become next, I'd like it to include these people. Is that strange?"

"No," I said. "That's the least strange thing aboard this station."

* * *

Now the part I have to deliver to command, and deliver hard, because they are not listening fast enough.

The anomaly is moving.

Not drifting—moving. With intent, if a region of spacetime can be said to have intent, and after ninety hours inside it I am no longer willing to rule that out. When the Halcyon encountered it, the boundary sat at coordinates we logged and named. I have reviewed the Meridian's own sensor sweeps since our return, cross-referenced against our original position fixes, and the boundary has expanded. Steadily. Along a vector.

Toward the colony lane.

I presented this to Commander Adeyemi's staff three days ago. I dressed in my full dress uniform, which now fits like a costume from a school play, and I stood on a platform so the council could see me over their consoles, and I gave them the numbers. Expansion rate. Vector. Projected intersection with the main colony traffic corridor in—by my estimate—somewhere between four and seven months, depending on whether the expansion accelerates.

A junior analyst thanked me for my "recovery progress" before responding.

I let the silence do my work for me. Silence is a weapon that scales down well; I've lost the voice for shouting and gained a stillness that makes people uncomfortable, and uncomfortable people concede ground.

"My recovery progress," I said finally, "is irrelevant to the projection. So is my height. What is relevant is that a phenomenon capable of rewriting human development across an entire crew is expanding toward the route your colony fleet will use, and your response so far has been to quarantine my crew and study our bloodwork."

"We're taking the matter seriously, Captain Moreau—"

"You are taking *me* seriously as a patient. You are treating my testimony as a symptom. I am telling you the anomaly behaves like a system, not an event. It has structure. It has—forgive me—a *behavior*. Something out there is either growing or being grown, and either way, the question you should be asking is not 'what happened to the Halcyon' but 'who built the thing that happened to the Halcyon.'"

That landed. I watched it land. Adeyemi's chief of staff stopped writing.

Because here is what I have not said publicly, what I have only told my own thirty: the region was not natural. I've flown deep space for twenty years. I know what gravitational lensing looks like, what pulsar wash looks like, what a dying star's turbulence looks like. This was none of those. This was *engineered*. There were harmonics in it—regularities, repetitions, a periodicity that nature doesn't produce and technology does. Vasquez heard it first, in the comm static. She called it "a signal pretending to be weather."

And one more thing, which I saved for last, watching their faces:

"Our bodies were rewritten along a gradient. The closer we were to the center, the younger we came back. That means the effect is directional, calibrated, and controllable by whoever controls the source. It also means—" and here I made myself slow down, because the next sentence was the one that mattered— "that somewhere in this system, someone may already possess the mathematics to reverse it. Or aim it. Or both. Find them before the field finds your colony lane. Because when it arrives, you will not get a warning in an adult voice. You will get it in voices like mine."

* * *

Afterward, Okafor found me in the observation bay, where I go to look at the debris field without anyone checking my posture.

"They didn't believe you," he said.

"Some of them believed me. Belief travels slowly through bureaucracies. It has to pass through committees, and committees metabolize belief into memos."

"What do we do?"

I thought about it. Below us, the Meridian's hull curved away toward the cryo hold, where two hundred sleepers wait in their pods—two hundred people who, if my projections are right, lie directly in the path of everything I survived. Two hundred minds that have never been consulted about anything, including whether they wanted to exist as copies. I thought about Ensign Varga in the pediatric ward, sleeping a sleep that isn't hers, and I thought: *no one gets to decide for the sleeping again. Not on my watch.*

"We do what ships' companies have always done," I said. "We keep records. We log everything—the field readings, the harmonics, the expansion vectors, all of it, timestamped and witnessed. If command won't act on our data, we make sure the data survives command. And we train."

"Train for what?"

"For the day the field reaches this station. Because it will, Okafor. Maybe months, maybe less. And when it comes, the people on this station will need to know what we know: how to hold onto yourself, how to work in a body that betrays you, how to stay a crew." I looked at him—nine years old, lieutenant's bearing, my best officer. "They'll need teachers who've already drowned."

He nodded slowly. "The Children of the Deep."

"Every expedition needs a name," I said. "Ours just happens to be accurate."

He left to draft the training rotation, and I stayed at the glass a while longer, watching the debris field turn in the dark.

That was when the station's general alert tone sounded—soft, administrative, the tone they use for routine notices. I almost ignored it. Then I saw the priority flag appended to it, and the routing: direct from Fleet Science Directorate to the Meridian's medical division.

Subject: comprehensive neural scan, all backup sleepers, hold deck two. Authorization pending.

I read it twice. A cold moved through me that had nothing to do with the observation bay's temperature, because I knew those pods, I'd reviewed those manifests, and I knew which name sat at number 114 on the roster.

Odile Voss. Dead four years. Sleeping in a copy nobody asked her permission to make.

And somebody upstairs had just decided her mind was worth scanning—as if they already suspected what I'd suspected alone in the dark for weeks: that whatever built the thing that unmade my crew had left its fingerprints somewhere closer to home.

I keyed the channel to my thirty.

"All hands," I said. "Change of plans. We're going to need to be louder."
