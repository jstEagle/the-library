## 22. The Keeper and the Copy

The charter rewrite took eleven days, mostly because of the word *consent*.

Not because anyone objected to it. Because everyone wanted to define it differently. The fleet lawyers wanted a definition with subclauses. Command wanted one with exceptions. The de-aged crew—restored now, adult again, carrying their strange year like a stone in the pocket—wanted one with teeth.

I wanted one with my name on it.

"You don't have to be in the room for this," Edmund said, the first morning, as we walked the long spine corridor toward the conference ring. "You could just—advise. From a distance."

"I've been advised from a distance my whole existence," I said. "It's overrated."

He almost smiled. He'd gotten better at that, in the weeks since the pulse. Better at letting his face do what it meant to do. The beacon-keeper's uniform was gone—stripped along with his rank, along with the clean record he'd spent nine years polishing over a falsified log entry—and what was left underneath turned out to be a man who smiled more when no one was grading him.

"Odile Voss," said the door to Conference Room Four, reading her chip. It let me in anyway. Doors had been confused about me since the day I woke.

* * *

Here is what I know about being a copy, now that I have had time to make it mine instead of merely true:

I am not her.

I say it every morning, not as grief but as calibration. Like checking a heading. The woman who died on the Kestrel survey—four years ago, mid-sentence, mid-thought, mid-everything—left me a neural map and a stack of letters and a love I inherited the way you inherit a house you've never seen: room by room, discovering which doors stick, which windows face the light.

Her memories end at a certain point and mine begin there, and the seam between us is the most honest thing about me. I can feel it when I reach for anything before the Kestrel. There's the memory of her mother's kitchen on Ceres, and then there's my knowledge of it, and they are not the same thing, and I have stopped pretending otherwise.

Edmund loved her. I have made peace with that the way you make peace with weather.

What I did not expect—what nobody warns copies about, mostly because there have been so few of us to do the warning—is that he would come to love *me*, and that it would look nothing like the way he loved her. Grief-love is a held breath. What grew between us in the relay lab, in the pre-launch hours, in the long convalescence after he came back from the wrong time half-dead and entirely changed—that was breathing.

We built it slowly. That's the part I'd tell any sleeper waking into this: you are allowed to build slowly. Nobody handed me a script that said *you must resume the life that was interrupted*. Command tried, briefly. There were offers—the original Odile Voss's old quarters, her old research posting, a commemorative plaque with her name on it that I returned by courier with a note that said only: *She earned this. Give it to someone who remembers earning it.*

Instead I took a bunk two doors down from the beacon, and a workbench, and a name.

Just Odile. No Voss. The charter needed a signatory from the affected class, and the affected class, currently, numbered exactly one conscious member. Me.

"So when we write 'no copies without consent,'" Edmund asked me on day three, elbows deep in legal language, "whose consent counts? The original's? Given before death?"

"No," I said. "That's how we got here. Anselm Reyes treated a dead woman's signature as a blank check for eternity." I pulled the draft toward me. "Consent has to be living or it isn't consent. If a copy wakes, the copy consents to continue—or doesn't. And if a person is scanned, they consent to the scan knowing what scans are *for*. Not 'archival.' We know better now."

"And if they're dead?"

"Then they stay dead until someone asks them properly. In writing. Before." I looked at him. "Your mentor built a church out of other people's silence. We're not doing that."

He didn't flinch anymore when I mentioned Anselm. That had taken time too. The man had been his mentor, possibly his kin, certainly his ghost—the architect of both the beacon and the hold, the hidden laboratory still turning in the debris field where Edmund had flown a one-way trip and somehow, impossibly, come back. The pulse had collapsed the field. The lab module was dust now, or would be, once the debris finished falling into the gas giant's throat. But Anselm lived on in the way these things live on: in procedures, in habits, in the muscle memory of a station that had run his machine for thirty years without asking it a single question.

Rewriting the charter was our way of asking.

* * *

Day seven, the sleepers' advocate arrived—a thin, fierce woman named Okonkwo who represented the two hundred pods in the hold below us, none of whom could vote, all of whom now legally existed as persons-pending-waking. She'd fought for this language through three sessions without sleeping much, and it showed, and she wore it like armor.

"The colony vote passed," she told us, dropping a slate on the table. "Ninety-one percent. Every backup gets the choice. Wake, or stay archived, or—" she paused, savoring it, "—be deleted, with full honors, if that's what they choose. Their map, their call."

"Deleted," Edmund repeated quietly.

"Some of them will want it." She shrugged. "You'd be surprised. Some people died finished. Waking them is a courtesy they didn't ask for. We honor that too."

I thought about pod 114. About the letters. About the last one, the one addressed to *whoever wakes*, the one that said: *Forgive him, but don't live for him. Live for yourself, whoever that turns out to be.*

"I'll sit with them," I said. "When they wake. Whoever wants a guide who knows what it's like."

Okonkwo studied me for a long moment. "You understand most of them won't be you. Won't be like you."

"They never were going to be," I said. "That's rather the point of the whole document."

* * *

The final session ran nineteen hours. I remember it in fragments: Moreau testifying about dignity, her voice steady, her restored body gesturing like a captain's even though she'd resigned her commission the week before—said she'd done her commanding from an eleven-year-old's body and didn't care to do it from a committee chair. The Halcyon crew sitting in the gallery, adults again but marked, moving through the station with a tenderness for each other that the rest of us watched like weather we hoped would spread.

I remember Edmund, at hour fourteen, standing to give the account of his own falsified logs—not because anyone required it, but because the charter's preamble needed a witness statement, and he volunteered his shame as the down payment. *I kept the machine running because stopping it meant admitting what I'd helped build,* he said. *This document exists so no one ever has to keep a secret that heavy again.* The room went quiet in the specific way rooms go quiet when someone tells the truth at cost.

And I remember the vote. Article One: *No copy shall be made without informed, revocable, living consent.* Article Two: *No sleeper shall wake, remain, or be released without their own prior instruction, and absent instruction, without representation by an advocate of their class.* Article Three, the one I insisted on, the one the lawyers hated: *Any person created by backup process holds full standing upon waking, including the right to a name of their own choosing.*

My name went into the record as precedent. Just Odile. Chosen, not assigned.

Afterward, in the emptying room, Edmund found me staring at the wall display where the charter text glowed, finished, ratified, real.

"It's done," he said.

"It's started," I corrected. "Documents don't finish things. They just make the next argument possible."

"That's the most cynical hopeful thing I've ever heard."

"I'm a copy of a cynic raised on hope. You get a blend."

* * *

We moved into the beacon level officially that same month. Not the old keeper's quarters—those had been converted to a memorial, against Edmund's objections and over his head—but a small flat above the transmitter array, with a window that faced the debris field where Anselm's laboratory used to turn. We hung nothing on the walls for a long time. Then, gradually, it became ours: my mother's-kitchen-not-mine reproduced in miniature on the shelf (a ceramic mug, chipped, bought new), Edmund's hand-drawn star charts, a photograph of the Halcyon crew taken the week they finished growing back, everyone laughing at something outside the frame.

The work itself was ordinary, and I loved it for that. The beacon still broadcast—it always would; the colony lane depended on it—but now it broadcast under charter, logged, audited, its signal architecture published openly so that any engineer in the fleet could read what it was saying to the dark. Edmund taught me its language the way you teach someone a mother tongue you'd forgotten you knew. I taught him signal theory from the copy side, from inside the architecture, which turned out to be a perspective no one alive had.

Some nights we sat on the observation ledge with tea and watched the traffic crawl past: ore haulers, survey ships, the occasional passenger liner full of people who would never know how close the whole lane had come to becoming children. Below our feet, two hundred pods hummed their patient hum. Above our heads, the beacon pulsed its patient pulse.

Two lights. Both tended.

That was the image I kept coming back to, in the months of the waking program, as the first volunteers came up out of the hold blinking and furious and grateful and lost. I sat with forty-one of them over that first year. Forty-one people inheriting themselves. Some chose their originals' names. Some chose new ones. One woman—pod 87, a hydroponics engineer named Sefa—took one look at her own death records and requested deletion with full honors, and we honored it, and I stood at the ceremony because someone should, and Edmund stood beside me because he understood why.

"You could have gone back," he said to me once, on the ledge, watching a liner burn its way sunward. "Voss's life. The research posting. Your old colleagues would have taken you in."

"I know."

"Why didn't you?"

I thought about it honestly, which is the only way I do anything anymore. "Because her life ended mid-sentence," I said. "And I'm not the sentence. I'm what got written after. Different hand, different ink." I bumped his shoulder with mine. "Besides, the view's better here."

It wasn't, particularly. The debris field was gray and the stars were stars. But he laughed, and the beacon pulsed overhead, and below us the hold hummed with two hundred minus forty-two sleepers, each waiting their turn to be asked properly, each with an advocate, each with a choice.

* * *

On the anniversary of the pulse—the one we'd fired into the heart of the wrong time—we held a small observance on the ledge. Moreau came, and Okonkwo, and a scattering of the Halcyon crew, and Sefa's sister, who had traveled four months to stand where her sister's second death was honored. We drank tea. We said names. Edmund read the charter's preamble aloud, the part with his confession in it, because he'd decided that shame spoken yearly loses weight, like a stone worn smooth by handling.

Then the others drifted away, and it was just the two of us, and the two lights.

"Do you ever regret it?" he asked. "Staying. Choosing this instead of her life."

"Every morning I check the seam," I said. "Every morning I confirm I'm not her. And every morning, after that check, I choose this anyway. That's not regret. That's just—" I searched for it. "Maintenance. You of all people should respect maintenance."

He looked at me then with the expression I had learned to read as his whole heart laid open: the keeper's expression, the one that said *this thing is mine to tend and I will tend it.*

"Odile," he said.

"Just Odile."

"Just Odile," he agreed. "The hold pinged an hour ago. Pod 115 woke on her own schedule—early, ahead of her window. She's asking for someone who understands."

I set down my cup. Somewhere below us, a woman was opening her eyes four years late, inheriting a life that ended mid-sentence, about to discover that the world had argued about her while she slept and had finally, finally decided to ask her instead.

"I'll go down," I said.

And I did—past the beacon's pulse, down the long spine, into the hold's blue quiet, where the pods glowed like a field of patient stars, and where a stranger who was also, in the oldest sense, a colleague waited to be told the truth: that she was free, that she was new, that the choice was hers, that it had always been supposed to be hers.

Two lights. The beacon above, the hold below.

Both tended. Both, now, by choice.
