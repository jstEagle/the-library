## 4. The Truce Clock

The broadcast came through at 0400 ship-time, which is the hour the universe reserves for news that ruins you.

I was awake anyway. I was always awake at 0400, because that's when the dreams let go of me — not gently, the way a tide releases a hull, but with a kind of bureaucratic finality, like a stamp coming down on a form marked *insufficient*. I'd been lying in my bunk in the dark, listening to the Pale Meridian tick and settle around me, doing the thing I do instead of sleeping, which is cataloguing the sounds of my own ship until they become a language I can read fluently. Coolant pump in the port wall, slightly arrhythmic, has been for three years. Hull expansion as we drift out of Ceres's shadow. The particular silence of a cargo hold full of agricultural substrate that nobody on this route will ever admit is fertilizer.

Then the comm panel chimed the priority tone. Not the urgent one — Fleet doesn't use urgent anymore, they learned during the war that urgent makes people panic and panic makes people ask questions. Priority is worse. Priority means *we have decided you need to know this, calmly, whether you want to or not.*

I floated out of the bunk and pulled up the feed.

The face that resolved out of the static belonged to Admiral Elandra Serevan, and I felt something in my chest do a thing I didn't have a name for. Not recognition exactly. More like the body remembering a smell before the mind remembers where it comes from.

She looked older than the recruitment holos, but they'd kept the voice. That was deliberate; I knew enough about broadcast psychology to know it was deliberate. Serevan's voice had been engineered into the public consciousness for twenty years — measured, warm at the edges, carrying underneath it the specific gravity of a woman who has never once needed to raise it. She was standing in front of a flag I half-recognized, wearing dress grays with a minimum of decoration, because Serevan understood that the most powerful costume is the one that looks like it's trying least.

"Citizens of the Concord," she said, "in ninety days, the Armistice of Vessh Reach expires."

* * *

> **FLEET COMMUNIQUÉ 4471-C // PRIORITY DISTRIBUTION // ALL REGISTERED VESSELS**
>
> By order of Fleet Command, all licensed operators are advised that the Armistice of Vessh Reach, in effect since the cessation of hostilities, will lapse in ninety (90) standard days unless renewed by mutual accord.
>
> Operators are reminded that shipping lanes adjacent to the Vessh Remnant corridor remain subject to advisory status. No change to insurance classifications is anticipated at this time.
>
> Fleet Command thanks you for your continued service to stability.

*Stability.* There's a word that gets used the way anesthesia gets used. You don't feel the operation happening; you just wake up missing something and are told it was never there.

I sat down slowly on the edge of the bunk and watched the rest of the address, because some part of me — the part I keep locked in the hold with the fertilizer — wanted to hear what she'd say next.

"The armistice was never meant to be permanent," Serevan said, and her tone did the thing where it became almost intimate, almost apologetic, a mother explaining why the world can't stay soft forever. "It was a pause. A chance for both our peoples to breathe, to rebuild, to remember who we are apart from each other. Ninety days from today, we will either renew that covenant as equals — or we will meet the future as strangers. Fleet Command prefers the former. Fleet Command prepares for the latter."

There it was. The sentence inside the sentence. *We prefer peace, and we are loading the guns.*

She went on for another four minutes about readiness postures and diplomatic channels and the "unfortunate rhetoric" emanating from certain colonial assemblies, and every word of it was polished to such a high gloss that you could see your own frightened reflection in it. At the end she said, "Be not afraid. Vigilance is not fear." And then the flag behind her did a slow digital ripple, and the screen offered me a choice of follow-up content, none of which I chose.

I sat in the dark for a long time after that.

Here's the thing about the Calyx Massacre — the one that officially never happened, the one I have spent nine years not thinking about with the discipline of a monastic order: everyone agreed to forget it, but forgetting is a maintenance task. It isn't a door you close once. It's a garden you have to weed, daily, forever, because the truth grows back. And the truce expiring meant the garden was about to be bulldozed, and everything anyone had buried was going to come up with the roots attached.

Ninety days.

I got up, made coffee that tasted like hot cardboard and regret, and went forward to the cockpit to check my route math, because when the universe hands you dread, the correct response is to do arithmetic.

* * *

> **FLEET COMMUNIQUÉ 4473-C // PRIORITY DISTRIBUTION // ALL REGISTERED VESSELS**
>
> Effective immediately, operators transiting sectors bordering the Vessh Remnant corridor must file flight plans no less than seventy-two (72) hours in advance of departure. Random inspection protocols may be instituted without prior notice.
>
> These measures are precautionary. Fleet Command reiterates its commitment to peaceful coexistence.

*Precautionary.* My father used to say that word too, back on Lagos Station, whenever the station authority announced a new curfew or a new tax or a new list of things we weren't allowed to say near the airlock cameras. Precautionary measures are how institutions flirt with the things they're planning to do to you.

The Pale Meridian wasn't going anywhere near the corridor. My route ran the long way around — the Long Route, the haulers called it, the loop through the dead ports and the silent stations where the only traffic was ore barges and people running from their own names. It paid badly and cost me nothing but time, and time was the one currency I had in surplus, because spending years was the whole point. Every year on the Long Route was a year the past couldn't find me.

Except now the past had apparently started filing paperwork.

I thought about the manifest again. I'd been trying not to think about it for two days, with limited success, the way you try not to think about a tooth. The Meridian. Destroyed — except the records say she never flew, so *destroyed* is technically a synonym for *never existed*, which is the kind of linguistic sleight of hand that would have earned a failing grade in any of my old seminars. And her crew manifest, surfacing out of some corrupted archive like a body rising in a flooded mine, with a name on it I hadn't answered to in nine years.

A name that fit me like a uniform.

And now this. Now the clock. Because that's what the expiration really was — not a deadline for diplomats, but a countdown for everyone else. In ninety days, either someone renewed the armistice or the two fleets drifted into firing range of each other again, and when that happened, every buried record, every erased name, every testimony that had been sanded out of existence would become suddenly, catastrophically relevant. Wars run on narratives the way ships run on fuel, and right now the official narrative was running clean and light, and I knew — I knew with the sick certainty of a woman who has read the engine logs — that somewhere under the deck plates of that narrative there was a leak nobody had patched.

I finished my coffee. I did the arithmetic. The Long Route would put me at Ceres Anchorage in six days, then out along the rim for a standard haul, then back. Ninety days was fourteen loops' worth of margin. Plenty of time to stay invisible while the adults decided whether to burn the sky again.

That was the plan. I want it on the record that that was the plan.

* * *

> **FLEET COMMUNIQUÉ 4479-C // PRIORITY DISTRIBUTION // ALL REGISTERED VESSELS**
>
> Fleet Command announces the commencement of renewal negotiations with accredited representatives of the Vessh Remnant, to be hosted at Meridian Station.

I read that line four times.

Meridian Station. Of course. Of course they'd host it there — or rather, of course the archive corruption would surface a ship named Meridian in the same season Fleet chose a station named Meridian for the negotiation that would decide whether the last twenty years of peace were real. Either the universe had a sense of humor, or somebody in Fleet Command's signals division had a sense of humor, and I genuinely could not decide which possibility I liked less.

The communiqué continued with the usual boilerplate — good faith, mutual security, the shared inheritance of the Reach — and then, tucked into paragraph six like a razor blade in a birthday cake:

> Operators are further advised that historical records pertaining to the conflict period remain subject to archival review. Requests for access should be directed through proper channels.

*Historical records remain subject to archival review.*

I laughed out loud, alone in my cockpit at 0500, and the laugh came out wrong, with edges on it. Subject to review. As if history were cargo that might be misdeclared. As if the past were sitting in a bonded warehouse somewhere waiting for customs officials to decide what it weighed.

I had spent nine years telling myself that what happened at Calyx stayed buried because everyone wanted it buried. That was the comfortable version. The comfortable version says: we all agreed, we all signed, the silence is load-bearing and shared. But communiqués like this one suggested a different architecture. They suggested that the silence wasn't shared — it was administered. That somewhere there were people whose job it was to maintain the forgetting, and that those people were, right now, in the ninety days before the truce expired, doing maintenance.

And if they were doing maintenance, then the manifest that surfaced with my name on it wasn't an accident. It was either a mistake they hadn't caught yet —

— or bait.

* * *

The next few days I flew and listened. That's most of hauling, honestly: flying and listening. The Long Route is quiet, but quiet isn't empty. Quiet is full of other people's transmissions bleeding across the bands — station chatter, ore barge complaints, the lonely personal calls of long-haul crews talking to spouses eleven light-hours away. On a normal loop, the background hum of human loneliness is the closest thing I have to company.

This loop, the hum had changed pitch.

On the open channels, the talk had gone strange. Not panicked — Fleet wouldn't allow panic — but taut. Haulers who usually gossiped about freight rates were gossiping about fleet movements. Someone on the rim relay claimed to have counted escort wings over a station that officially required no escorts. A woman hauling ice out of the belt told anyone who'd listen that her son had been recalled to active reserve, and that his orders had come with a confidentiality rider, and that he'd sounded scared on the call, and that scared wasn't a word her son used.

And underneath all of it, threaded through the chatter like a hairline crack through glass, the same phrase kept recurring, passed from mouth to mouth with the superstitious care people give words they don't fully understand:

*Ninety days. Ninety days. Clock's running.*

I caught myself doing something I hadn't done in years. Somewhere past the third day, alone on the night watch with the autopilot holding us steady through a stretch of nothing, I found my lips moving, shaping syllables in a language with no living native speakers. Not words, exactly — the Vessh language didn't work in words the way human languages do; it worked more in chords, meanings stacked on meanings, a single utterance carrying three or four propositions braided together. What my mouth was doing was the equivalent of humming. An old habit surfacing under stress, the way sailors whistle.

I stopped. I looked at my own hands on the console like they belonged to a stranger.

Because here's what I haven't said yet, and what I will say eventually, to someone, though not tonight: I dream in that language. Have for nine years. Dreams I can't translate, dreams that leave me waking with the shape of a meaning in my mouth and nothing to set it in. My own private theory — the theory I use to get through the mornings — is that it's scar tissue. That the language got into me during training, during the years when I was being shaped into an instrument of understanding between species, and that when the instrument was decommissioned, the tuning stayed. Brains are haunted houses. Mine just happens to be haunted in a dead tongue.

But lately — since the manifest, since the broadcast — the dreams had changed register. Less haunting, more insistent. The same chord repeating, night after night, a meaning I couldn't parse pressing against the inside of my skull like weather against a window.

And I am a xenolinguist, whatever my tax filings say. I know what repetition means. Repetition is how any speaker says *listen to me* when they're afraid they aren't being heard.

* * *

> **FLEET COMMUNIQUÉ 4486-C // PRIORITY DISTRIBUTION // ALL REGISTERED VESSELS**
>
> Fleet Command notes with concern recent incidents of unauthorized speculation regarding historical events. Operators are reminded that dissemination of unverified archival material constitutes a violation of information security statutes.
>
> Violations will be prosecuted.
>
> Fleet Command thanks you for your continued service to stability.

They sent that one eighty-one days out. I remember the number because I'd started counting, the way you start counting when you realize the clock in your chest and the clock on the broadcast are the same clock.

*Violations will be prosecuted.* Somebody, somewhere, had noticed the leaks. Somebody had noticed that the sealed past was seeping, and their response was not to answer questions but to criminalize them. And I sat with that communiqué glowing on my console and felt the paranoia settle over me like a harness — the specific, well-fitted harness of a person who knows exactly how much trouble she's in and cannot yet see the shape of the trap.

Because I had the manifest. Corrupted, fragmentary, deniable — but I had it, copied and encrypted in a partition of my ship's memory that officially didn't exist, much like the massacre itself. And possession of unverified archival material was now, as of eighty-one days out, a prosecutable offense. Which meant that the moment I'd pulled that file — the moment I'd looked twice at a name I'd spent nine years burying — I had already crossed a line drawn by people who knew exactly where the line needed to be drawn to protect whatever it protected.

They'd listed my buried name on a ghost ship's crew roster. And then they'd made looking into ghost ships a crime.

You don't have to be a xenolinguist to parse that syntax. But it helped.

* * *

Seventy-six days out, I made orbit at Ceres Anchorage, and the Anchorage was wrong.

Not dramatically wrong. Wrong the way a room is wrong when someone has moved one piece of furniture two centimeters. The dock queues were longer than they should have been. There were two Fleet cutters moored at the military berths where normally there'd be one, or none, and their hulls were too clean, freshly painted, recently commissioned. The station announcer's voice, when it cycled through the arrival litany, had a new clause in it — something about reporting suspicious activity to station security — delivered in exactly the same bored tone as the clause about duty-free liquor.

And in the queue for the fuel desk, ahead of me, a man in a civilian jacket with a military haircut was asking the attendant quiet questions, and the attendant's answers had the careful flatness of someone being polite to a person they'd been told to be polite to.

I kept my head down. I bought my fuel. I told myself the story I always tell: you are no one, you are a freighter captain with bad taste in routes and worse taste in coffee, nobody thinks about you at all.

But walking back along the ring concourse, past the shuttered tea shop and the shrine to the war dead that nobody maintained and everybody avoided, I passed the station's public archive terminal — a battered kiosk most haulers used to look up tide tables and docking fees — and it was powered down. Out of service. A printed notice taped to the screen:

**ARCHIVAL ACCESS SUSPENDED PENDING SYSTEMS REVIEW. WE APOLOGIZE FOR THE INCONVENIENCE.**

Pending systems review. Eighty-one days after the truce clock started, on a station at the far end of nowhere, the past had been switched off for maintenance.

I stood in front of that dead terminal for maybe ten seconds. Ten seconds too long, probably. Because when I turned back toward my berth, I caught it — the small, professional stillness of a man by the tea shop window who had been watching me and now very deliberately wasn't. Civilian jacket. Military haircut. Or possibly a different man entirely; at this distance, paranoia and perception wear the same coat.

I walked back to the Pale Meridian at a pace I calibrated to look unhurried, ran pre-departure with hands that were almost steady, and cast off from Ceres Anchorage into the dark.

* * *

That night, in my bunk, the dream came again, and this time it brought the chord with teeth.

I was standing — dreaming-standing — in a corridor I recognized and refused to recognize, white walls, the particular institutional lighting of a Fleet research deck, and somewhere ahead of me a voice was speaking Vessh. Not singing, not chanting — *begging*, though begging wasn't quite the word either, because the Vessh word carried a proposition braided inside it that English has no shelf for: it meant *stop, and also witness, and also remember this*, all at once, one sound.

And in the dream I understood it perfectly. That was the worst part. In the dream, I understood it perfectly, and I answered — I heard myself answer — in Fleet Standard, calm and procedural, saying words that were not what I meant, translating my refusal into compliance, and the voice ahead of me went quiet in the way that voices go quiet when it is already too late for stopping and too early for mourning.

I woke at 0400, as always, heart slamming, the chord still ringing in my inner ear like a struck bell that had forgotten how to decay.

I lay in the dark and did the arithmetic again, because arithmetic is my rosary. Seventy-five days. Fourteen loops' worth of margin, if I flew the Long Route and touched nothing and asked nothing and stayed no one.

But the dreams had started keeping count too. And they were counting down faster than the broadcast was.

I got up, made the cardboard coffee, and pulled up my navigation plot. My next scheduled stop was a fueling station out along the rim — Kestrel's Rest, a place so minor it appeared on charts mainly as a warning. Nothing waited for me there. Nothing ever waited for me anywhere. That was the entire architecture of my life, and I had built it myself, joint by joint, and I was proud of it the way you're proud of a wall you built to keep something out.

The transit computer flagged an anomaly while I was watching. A request for rendezvous — unscheduled, unsanctioned, routed through a civilian band by someone who clearly knew how such requests were supposed to look and had disguised theirs just well enough to seem harmless. Origin point: Kestrel's Rest. Destination: the Pale Meridian.

Message text, eleven words:

*Captain Okafor — or whichever name you're using now. We should talk.*

I stared at it for a long time. Outside the viewport, the rim dark turned slowly, indifferent and enormous, and somewhere behind me, in the direction of the Reach, a clock neither of us could see was ticking down from seventy-four.

Whoever they were, they knew the name I'd buried. Which meant invisibility hadn't failed gradually, the way I'd always feared — worn thin by accident, breached by coincidence.

It had failed all at once, on purpose, by design.

I keyed the transmitter.
