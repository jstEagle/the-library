## 6. What the Records Say

The archive terminal at Ceres Anchorage occupied a corner of the public library deck that nobody visited anymore, wedged between a shuttered tea house and a memorial wall to the Calyx dead—which is to say, a wall with no names on it, because officially there were no dead. Someone had taped a paper sign over the terminal's flickering status light: OUT OF ORDER. USE KIOSK 4.

Kiosk 4 had been out of order for three years. The paper sign was the only working interface in the room.

I peeled it off, sat down, and fed the terminal my credentials—the boring ones, the freighter-captain ones, the ones that said *Ansel Okafor, hauler, unremarkable, please ignore*. If someone was watching archive access patterns, and someone was always watching archive access patterns, I wanted them bored too.

"Query," I murmured, and the terminal woke with the enthusiasm of an old dog pretending not to hear you. "Vessel registry: Meridian. Fleet hull number FS-2211."

The answer came back in under a second, which was the first wrong thing. Deep registry pulls took time. They made you wait because waiting made you feel the weight of what you were asking for. This came back instant and clean and cheerful, like the question had never once been difficult:

**No vessel designated MERIDIAN exists in Fleet registry. Hull designation FS-2211 unassigned.**

I sat back. My heart did something complicated that I chose to interpret as indigestion from station noodles.

"Okay," I said to the empty room. "Okay. Try this. Query: incident report, Calyx Station, any date."

**No incidents recorded at Calyx Station. Calyx Station decommissioned following structural failure, standard salvage protocol. No casualties reported.**

Structural failure. I had seen Calyx Station from four kilometers out on a cargo run six years ago, close enough to count the scorch blooms across its spine, close enough to know that structure doesn't fail outward. Structure fails inward. Those scars were written on the outside of the hull like a scream written on the outside of a throat.

"No casualties," I repeated. The terminal, having no sense of irony, confirmed it.

Here is the thing about being a xenolinguist turned cargo hauler: you spend your career learning how meaning gets built, and then you spend the rest of it noticing how it gets unbuilt. A lie isn't a wall. It's a translation, and every translation leaves seams. You just have to know where to press your thumb.

I pressed.

"Query: crew manifest, Meridian, all personnel." Let it say the ship didn't exist again. Let me watch it say it twice, because the second time is where the seam shows—when a system has to be consistent with its own fiction, and consistency is hard work.

**No crew manifest on file. Vessel MERIDIAN does not exist.**

There it was. Not even a hesitation. Not the half-second stutter of a database reaching into sealed tiers and finding nothing, which is what a genuine absence looks like. This was smooth. Rehearsed. Somebody hadn't deleted the Meridian; somebody had taught the archive a story, and the archive told it fluently.

Fluently. And there was the word that made my hands go cold on the console, because fluency was my whole life, first as a trade and then as a curse, and I knew better than anyone that fluency is not truth. Fluency is practice.

* * *

I worked for six hours. I bought a tea from the one vendor still open on the library deck—a kid who looked at me like I was a ghost haunting a building he'd been told was empty—and I drank it while the terminal lied to me in forty different elegant ways.

The Meridian never flew. Fine. Then why did the fleet's own logistics sub-ledgers—buried two tiers down, in requisition records nobody bothers to falsify because nobody reads them—show a fuel allocation for a long-range survey vessel departing Titan Yards eleven years ago, tonnage class S-3, destination codes that resolved to nothing but whose *format* matched deep-orbit survey runs? Ghosts don't burn deuterium. Something flew, and something refueled, and then the paperwork forgot.

No massacre occurred. Fine. Then why did the medical exchange network—the civilian one, the one that predates Fleet's information hygiene reforms—still carry a vaccination-and-trauma supply surge to Ceres Anchorage dated nine years ago, flagged *mass casualty contingency*, with a note in the margin: **capacity planning per Fleet request, Calyx eventuality**? Eventuality. Like a storm. Like weather. Like something everyone agreed to call by a softer name before it happened so that afterward they could say they'd only ever prepared for weather.

And no one named Okafor served. That was the one I saved for last, the way you save the worst corridor of a burning house for last, telling yourself you might not go in.

I typed it small, in my head, though the query itself was the same size as all the others: *Ansel Okafor, linguistic specialist, service record.*

**No service record found.**

Of course not. I'd known it would say that. I'd have bet the Pale Meridian on it. But knowing a thing is coming doesn't stop it from landing, and when it landed it landed in the strange place—not grief exactly, not fear exactly, but that vertigo you get when you step off a stair that isn't where your body insists it should be. Nine years I had built my life on the assumption that I had erased myself. Carefully, patiently, one forged transit log at a time, I had sanded myself down to a woman who hauled cargo and dreamed badly and asked no questions. And now the archive was telling me I'd never needed to sand at all. The wood had never been there. There had never been an Ansel Okafor in the Fleet. There had never been a girl who spent four years learning to think in a language with no word for *alone*.

Somebody had gotten there first. Somebody had done my erasing for me, thoroughly, professionally, and—I understood this only gradually, sitting in the cold blue light of a terminal that was lying to me with excellent grammar—they had done it *for their own reasons*, not mine. Which meant my name wasn't hidden. It was *held*.

There's a difference. Hidden things want to stay lost. Held things are collateral. Held things are leverage, waiting for the day someone needs them.

A name on a manifest. Mine, under the old spelling, the birth-name spelling I'd buried twice—once in the records and once in the mirror. Whoever sent that manifest hadn't found me. They'd *unlocked* me.

* * *

Around hour five, I stopped querying forward and started querying sideways, which is the oldest trick in archival forensics and the reason I'm glad I spent my twenties learning dead languages instead of useful ones. You can't ask the archive about the thing. But you can ask it about the things *around* the thing, the innocent neighbors, the context—and watch which questions come back fast and which ones come back slow, because speed is a confession.

"Query: commendation citations, Fleet Survey Command, year of record minus eleven."

Fast. Hundreds of them. Medals for everything—navigation excellence, reactor innovation, a citation for someone's garden on Titan Yards, I swear on my mother's grave, a *garden*.

"Query: disciplinary actions, same command, same year."

Slow. Four seconds. Then: **Two records found. Both minor.**

Two minor disciplinary actions in a command division of eleven thousand people, in a year when the fuel logs show a deep-survey vessel burning through allocations like it was fleeing something. In my experience, when a database offers you two minor records where there should be dozens, the dozens haven't been lost. They've been *consolidated*. Somewhere there was a file where all those messy human failures got gathered up and rewritten into a single clean narrative, and the narrative was: nothing happened here.

"Query: personnel transfers out of Survey Command, year minus ten to year minus eight."

This one didn't come back slow. This one came back *wrong*. The terminal displayed a progress bar, filled it halfway, and then simply reset, polite as a waiter clearing a plate you hadn't finished. **Query timed out. Please refine parameters.**

I refined nothing. I sat very still and listened to the station breathe around me—the ventilation hum, the distant clank of cargo arms, the tea kid stacking cups somewhere behind me—and I thought about what a timeout actually means. A timeout means the request went somewhere it wasn't supposed to go, and something at the other end decided whether to answer, and the deciding took longer than the lying usually takes. There was a person, or a process wearing a person's clothes, on the far side of that progress bar. And it had looked at my query—*who left Survey Command after the thing that never happened*—and it had hesitated.

You don't hesitate over nothing. Nothing answers instantly. Nothing has been answering instantly all night.

* * *

By hour seven I had a pattern, and the pattern was worse than any single lie. It wasn't that the records contradicted each other—good forgeries don't contradict, that's amateur work. It was that the records *agreed too much*. Every layer, every tier, every cross-reference sang the same note: no ship, no massacre, no casualties, no Okafor. Perfect unison. And I kept thinking about the Vessh, about the one thing about their language I'd understood before I understood anything else, the thing that had made my instructors call me gifted right before they called me compromised: the Vessh don't trust a voice that never varies. A chorus where every singer hits the same pitch isn't harmony to them. It's a warning. Real voices drift. Real records drift. Consistency at this scale is manufactured, and manufacturing takes intent, and intent takes someone who decided, in advance, exactly what the truth was going to be allowed to say.

I closed my eyes and let the vertigo have me for a moment. This is what it must be like, I thought, to be a person who trusts the archive. To grow up believing the record is the world, and then to pull one thread and find the whole weave was loomed backward—pattern first, facts fitted in after. How do you know anything? How do you know your own history, your wars, your heroes, your one good admiral on every commemorative stamp? How do you know the truce is real, or what it ended, or what it cost?

You don't. You know what you were given. And someone decides what you're given.

My hands were shaking. I noticed it the way you notice rain—gradually, then all at once—and I pressed them flat against the console until the tremor transferred into the metal and, presumably, into whatever surveillance layer was watching this terminal, a little gift of biometric data for the collection. Let them see my pulse spike. Let them know the held thing was rattling its cage.

Because that was the other thing I'd figured out, somewhere around the sixth cup of terrible tea: if my erasure was deliberate, maintained, *active*—then tonight's queries were visible. Maybe not attributed yet. Maybe just an anomaly flag, a blip on some analyst's dashboard: *hauler with clean record pulling Survey Command ghosts.* But the manifest had already named me. The clock was already running. I had crossed, sometime in the last six hours, from invisible to observed, and observed things in Fleet space had a way of becoming either assets or debris.

* * *

I was gathering my coat when the terminal chimed. Unprompted. Nobody chimes an archive terminal; they're designed to be as responsive as furniture.

One line on the screen:

**Your session has been archived. Reference code: CALYX-EVENTUALITY / ANNEX 7. Have a pleasant cycle.**

Have a pleasant cycle.

I stared at it for a long time. The reference code wasn't a system string—system strings are numbers and hyphens, ugly and functional. This was words. Someone had *named* my search session, filed it under the massacre's softest euphemism, and left the label showing like a note pinned to a door. It could be a threat. It could be an invitation. With Fleet Intelligence the two were always the same document printed on different stock.

Annex 7. I turned the phrase over all the way back through the docking ring, past the tea kid who wished me a quiet night in a voice that suggested he wished everyone a quiet night, forever, professionally. Annex 7. Somewhere in the architecture of the lie there was a folder with my evening in it, shelved next to—what? The fuel logs? The medical surge? The two minor disciplinary actions standing guard over a graveyard of redactions?

Back aboard the Pale Meridian I ran pre-flight checks I didn't need to run, because my hands wanted occupation and my mind wanted a locked door. The station's night cycle dimmed the ports to amber. Somewhere out there, a manifest with my buried name on it was sitting in someone's queue, and somewhere in a sealed tier of the archive, a folder labeled with a euphemism had just grown heavier by one session.

I should sleep. I knew I should sleep. Instead I stood in the dark of my own galley, drinking water I didn't want, and felt the old reflex rise—the one from training, from the years I don't have records of—the reflex that says *when the surface language lies, go deeper, go to the language underneath.*

And that was when it happened, the thing I would later understand was the true end of my invisibility, more than the manifest, more than the reference code: I caught myself translating the archive's denials into Vessh. Automatically. *No vessel exists* became, in the tongue I only speak asleep, a construction that meant roughly *the naming has been withdrawn but the named persists*—because the Vessh, bless them, had no verb for deletion. Things in their language were never destroyed. They were *unsung*. And unsung things could always, always be sung again.

I stood there with cold water in my hand and my own fluency dripping out of me like a leak I'd patched nine years ago finally giving way, and I understood that the records weren't the only thing in this system that had been edited.

Something in me had been edited too. And it was starting to remember the original text.
