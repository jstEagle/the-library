## 7. Dreams in Vessh

The first thing they teach you at the Meridian Linguistics Institute is that you will fail.

Not as encouragement. As policy. Director Quist stands at the front of the amphitheater on intake day—Mara Quist, thirty-something and sharp as a folded knife—and tells two hundred of the fleet's brightest language prodigies that ninety percent of us will wash out before the second year, and the ones who remain will spend their careers translating threats, because that is what xenolinguistics is for now.

"Nobody learns an alien language to say hello," she says. "You learn it because somebody has to know what they're saying when they point at you."

I was twenty-two and I thought this was honesty. I thought it was even a little romantic. I had grown up on Ceres Anchorage reading dead Earth languages for fun—Akkadian, Linear B, the cuneiform receipts of merchants who'd been dead four thousand years and still wanted someone to know exactly how many jars of beer they'd shipped—and I believed languages were doors. I believed that if you learned enough of them, one of them would eventually open onto somewhere you belonged.

I want to be clear, looking back: I was not wrong about the door. I was wrong about which side of it I'd end up standing on.

* * *

The Vessh do not have words the way we have words.

That took me four months to understand and another eight to forgive myself for not understanding it sooner. Human pedagogy wants everything to be a word—a discrete unit with edges, something you can put in a box and stack in a warehouse of meaning. The Vessh language—which the translators' guild insisted on calling *Vesshi*, though the Vessh themselves called it nothing at all, because naming your own tongue would be like biting your own teeth—did not work in boxes. It worked in weather.

Their basic unit wasn't the word but what our analysts called, with the confidence of people who had never once been right about anything, a *phrase-cluster*: a braided structure of sound, posture, skin-flush, and subsonic pulse that carried meaning the way a chord carries meaning—not by its parts but by their relationship. A single cluster could hold six things at once. Grief and warning. Invitation and boundary. The Vessh could tell you they loved you and that you were standing too close in the same breath, and neither statement canceled the other out, and this was not considered contradiction. It was considered accuracy.

We were the ones who were imprecise. We just didn't know it yet.

My cohort called the training tank "the aquarium," which was disrespectful and also inevitable, because twenty-two-year-olds will nickname anything, including the end of the world. The aquarium was a chamber where a Vessh chorus-member named Selveth conducted sessions through a wall of smart-glass, teaching us the way you'd teach a very promising dog: patiently, with treats, and with a private opinion of us that we were never permitted to see.

Selveth was my instructor from the third month on. I don't know how they chose me. Later I would invent reasons—that my accent clusters were cleanest, that my error rates were lowest—but the truth is probably dumber and kinder: I was the only one who stopped trying to make the language be human. The others kept asking questions like "but what does it *mean*," demanding one meaning per utterance, and Selveth would flush a color our spectrum analysts logged as "amber-4" and give them the same answer again, more slowly, the way you'd speak louder to a deaf man instead of writing him a note.

I asked different questions. I asked what amber-4 felt like from the inside. I asked whether a cluster could mean something its speaker didn't know it meant. I asked, once, in a badly formed cluster that made Selveth's mantle ripple with what I later understood was laughter, whether the Vessh dreamed.

They answered me. That should have been the first warning sign, that they bothered answering. Languages get taught to enemies all the time; attention gets spent on enemies too. But Selveth stayed after session. Selveth flushed a slow blue-green—the color our logs called "calm," and which I would eventually learn meant something closer to *recognition*—and pulsed back at me, in careful, simplified clusters:

*Yes. We dream. In dreams, the clusters braid themselves. This is how new meanings are born.*

Then, after a pause long enough that I almost turned away:

*What do humans dream?*

"In whatever language we think in," I said, and then, because I was twenty-two and honest in the way that gets people hurt, "mostly in the language we're afraid in."

Selveth held that for a long moment. The glass between us hummed faintly with their subsonics, a frequency I felt in my sternum rather than heard.

*We are afraid in this language too,* they said. *That is why we teach it.*

* * *

Here is what nobody told us, in those bright early years of the truce: the Vessh weren't teaching us their language out of generosity. And we weren't learning it out of curiosity. Both species had fought a war eleven years earlier that neither side could accurately describe afterward, because most of the fighting had happened in misread signals—clusters translated as threats that were pleas, pleas translated as surrender terms that were curses. The Calyx Accords ended the shooting, but the misunderstanding industry stayed open for business. Every translator trained at the Institute was, functionally, a munition. We just came packaged as scholarships.

I knew this abstractly. Everyone did. What I didn't know—what none of us knew—was that some of the funding flowing into the Institute came from a program office whose name never appeared on any syllabus, and that the office's director was a rising star in Fleet Command named Serevan, who had concluded, with the tidy logic of a man who has never been inside a language but only ever outside one, that if language could cause wars, language could win them.

But that comes later. That night, the night of the dream, there was no Serevan in my head. There was only the aquarium, and Selveth, and the last session of my second year.

* * *

Second-year finals were conducted live: forty minutes of unscripted exchange with a chorus-member you hadn't trained with, graded on cluster fidelity, register control, and what the rubric called "relational maintenance"—which was Institute-speak for *can you keep talking to them without insulting anyone*. I drew a Vessh named Ossuary-Braid, an elder whose mantle had gone the deep iron-gray of age, and who spoke in clusters so dense that half the time I was translating structure rather than content, feeling for the grammar the way you'd feel for bones under skin.

Forty minutes. I remember maybe ten seconds of it clearly: the moment near the end when Ossuary-Braid asked me—in a cluster braided so tight it took me three full seconds to unpick—why I had chosen this work.

And I answered honestly, in my best mid-register cluster, the one I'd drilled until it lived in my hands: *Because I wanted to go through the door. Because every language I learned was a room and I kept thinking the next one would have a window.*

Ossuary-Braid went still. Then gray flushed toward violet, and the violet said something I did not have the vocabulary for yet, something I would spend years failing to translate, and the session ended, and I passed with the highest relational-maintenance score in cohort history, and Director Quist bought me a drink at the staff bar and told me I was the best student she'd had in nine years and that I should be careful, because the best students always got noticed by the wrong people first.

"Which people?" I asked.

"The ones who think a language is a lockpick," she said, and didn't elaborate, and I was twenty-three and drunk on passing, so I let it go.

That night I slept in my cell in the cadet wing—cells, we called them, though they were just narrow rooms with a bunk and a desk and a window that looked out on the Institute's interior courtyard, because windows that faced actual space were considered a morale risk—and I dreamed.

* * *

I need to describe the dream, and I need to do it carefully, because for nine years afterward I described it wrong, and describing it wrong nearly killed me.

It was not a dream *in* Vessh. That's what I told the intake psychologists, later, when they asked; that's what went in the file. A dream featuring Vessh elements, clusters surfacing in the usual dream-static, no big deal, bilinguals report this constantly. There's a whole literature on it. L2 intrusion in REM states. Perfectly normal.

That is what I said. Here is what happened.

In the dream I was in the courtyard, except the courtyard was also the aquarium, except it was also a place I had never been, a shoreline under a sky the color of amber-4, and Selveth was there, or something shaped like Selveth, and we were not speaking. We were *braiding*. Meaning was happening between us without either of us producing it, the way heat happens between bodies, and I understood—I understood with the total, frictionless comprehension that dreams grant and waking refuses—that I was dreaming in a language I had studied for two years but had never once *thought* in, and that the dream was not using my knowledge of Vessh.

It was using theirs.

Because here is the thing I lied about in the psych interview, the thing I left out of the file, the thing I have never said aloud until this sentence: in the dream, I was not the student. I was the one being studied. I was the strange creature behind the glass, flushing colors I couldn't control, pulsing meanings I didn't know I contained, and on the other side of the glass—patient, kind, taking notes—was Selveth. Or something wearing Selveth the way I wore my cadet uniform. And it looked at me with recognition, calm blue-green flooding its mantle, and it said, in perfect, effortless, native clusters:

*Now you know how it feels. Now we can begin.*

I woke up crying, which embarrassed me, because I hadn't cried since my mother died, and I sat on the edge of the bunk in the dark with my heart going like a cargo loader and tried to reconstruct the dream in English and found that I couldn't. Not wouldn't—couldn't. The memory was stored in the wrong format. When I reached for it in human language, it slipped, the way a name slips, the way a word on the tip of the tongue retreats forever the moment you grab at it.

But when I stopped reaching, when I lay back down and let my mind go quiet and unguarded, the clusters were still there. Whole. Warm. Waiting.

I lay awake until morning shift siren, and I did not sleep again, and I did not report the dream, and I have been deciding, ever since, whether that silence was the first honest thing I ever did or the first cowardly one. Probably both. Most things are both, if you carry them long enough.

* * *

Quist noticed anyway. Of course she did. You don't become the best relational-maintenance score in cohort history without changing, and I changed overnight—everybody saw it. My clusters got deeper. My register control got eerie. I started answering questions before they finished being asked, which sounds like a party trick and was actually a symptom: I was hearing the braids underneath the speech, the meanings people didn't know they were transmitting, and responding to those instead of the surface. My classmates found it unsettling. One of them, a sweet, doomed boy named Ferro who washed out in the third year, told me I'd started "listening like a predator."

Quist pulled me into her office—a real office, with paper books, which at the Institute was either a sign of seniority or eccentricity, and in her case both—and shut the door and looked at me for a long moment with those folded-knife eyes.

"You're dreaming in it," she said. Not a question.

"I'm studying hard," I said.

"Ansel." She never used my first name. The use of it now was itself a cluster, and I heard the braid under it: caution, affection, fear. "I've run this program for nine years. Four students have crossed the threshold you've crossed. Do you know where they are now?"

"Field postings?"

"Two are in Fleet Intelligence, doing work I'm not cleared to know about. One resigned and won't return calls. And one—" she paused, and the pause was the loudest thing in the room, "—one is a case file."

"A case file."

"Dreams that don't stop. Identity drift. She started signing her reports in cluster-form and couldn't explain why. Last I heard she was in a facility on Titan learning, and I quote her treatment plan, 'to re-establish primary-language dominance.'" She leaned forward. "The mind is a load-bearing structure, Ansel. You don't get to renovate it for free. So I'm going to ask you once, and then I'm going to pretend I never asked, and what you answer determines what I write down tonight. Are you dreaming in Vessh?"

And here is where I should tell you I told the truth. Here is where a better person's story turns.

I looked at her—at the woman who had bought me a drink, who had warned me about lockpicks, who was offering me, right now, in the only currency she had, a chance to stay safe—and I lied. Fluently. In two languages at once, though she could only hear one of them.

"No, Director. I dream in Standard, same as always. Sometimes I review clusters before bed. That's all it is."

Something went out of her face. Relief, maybe. Or disappointment. With Quist the two shared a color, the way grief and warning share a braid, and I was fluent enough by then to read it and arrogant enough to think reading it meant I'd won.

"All right," she said, and wrote something down, and I have spent nine years wondering what it said. "Keep your grades up. And Ansel—if the dreams change, if they start *asking* you things—you come to me. Before anyone else. Promise me."

"I promise," I said, and meant it, and broke it within the year, because the dreams did start asking, and the person they sent to collect my answers wore an admiral's uniform and smiled like a commemorative plaque.

* * *

The last thing I remember clearly from the Institute years is the night before my field assignment was announced. Third year, spring term. I stood at my window looking down at the courtyard where Ossuary-Braid had gone violet at me, and I practiced a cluster under my breath—an old one, one of the first Selveth ever taught me, a greeting-braid that meant roughly *I see you; I am glad the seeing costs me nothing.*

Except it didn't mean that anymore. Somewhere in the last year, without my noticing, the cluster had deepened. The way the Vessh used it among themselves, I'd learned, carried a second layer, a shadow-meaning braided beneath the greeting: *I see you; and I accept that seeing changes me; and I stay anyway.*

*I stay anyway.* That was the part the war had nearly bred out of both our species. That was the part the truce existed to protect. I said it to the empty courtyard like a prayer in a church built by strangers, and I went to bed, and I dreamed—

—and this time I remembered all of it, every braid, in the morning, in perfect clarity, which should have frightened me more than it did.

In the dream, Selveth stood on the amber shore and showed me a door. Not a metaphor-door. A door, ordinary, metal, fleet-issue, the kind that seals against vacuum. And beyond it, muffled, I could hear voices—human voices, shouting, and Vessh voices, and the two braids tangling into something that was neither plea nor threat but was being translated as both, and Selveth looked at me with calm blue-green recognition and said:

*One day soon, you will stand in front of a door like this. And everyone you trust will tell you what is on the other side. And you will hear something else.*

*When that day comes,* the dream said, *remember which language you were afraid in.*

I woke up with the cluster still warm in my chest. *I see you. I stay anyway.*

Three weeks later, my field posting came through. Deep-range assignment, Meridian corridor, attached to a joint operations command I'd never heard of, under a flag officer whose name I only knew from the truce commemorations.

Admiral Serevan. Hero of Calyx. Architect of the peace.

The orders were signed in his hand.
