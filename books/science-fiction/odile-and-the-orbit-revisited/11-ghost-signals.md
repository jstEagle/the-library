## 11. Ghost Signals

The dead don't sleep, and neither did I.

I'd told Ilo I needed six hours to run the recovery pass on the audio cores we'd pulled from the Calyx wreck, which was true in the technical sense that the pass takes six hours and a lie in every other sense, because what I actually needed was six hours to decide whether I wanted to hear what was on them. There is a difference between a task and a willingness, and freighter work had taught me to respect that difference the way sailors respect weather. You can't argue with it. You can only wait it out or get drowned.

So I made tea I didn't drink. I recalibrated a sensor array that didn't need it. I alphabetized a spice rack that had never known order and would never keep it. And then, because avoidance is a debt that collects interest, I sat down at the console with the three data cores cradled in foam like eggs from a bird nobody has ever seen alive, and I started the pass.

The *Pale Meridian* hummed around me, her old reactor doing its patient work. Outside the viewport, Calyx Station turned slowly against the dark, a broken ring of hull and scaffold and frozen atmosphere vented decades ago into crystals that still caught the light of the distant primary and threw it back in long pale spears. It should have been beautiful. Everything terrible is beautiful if you stand far enough back; that's practically the definition of distance.

The progress bar crawled. I watched it the way you watch a kettle, willing it not to boil.

Here is what I knew about those cores before I pressed anything: they were Vessh chorus-cores, recovered from the station's central archive node, which meant they hadn't been recording crew chatter or cargo manifests. Chorus-cores recorded everything. The Vessh kept continuous acoustic memory of their own spaces—the way some old Earth cultures kept oral epics—because for them, a room without its voices wasn't a room anymore. It was just geometry. Every corridor of Calyx Station had been singing to itself for years: footsteps, arguments, lullabies, the particular sound of two hundred people deciding, over and over, to share air.

And then one night, per the official record, nothing happened at all, because officially there was no night and no station and no two hundred people, and I had never been aboard, and my name had never been in any crew manifest, and the language I dreamed in was simply a hobby I couldn't explain.

The bar hit ninety percent. My hands were cold. I noticed this with the detached irritation of someone cataloguing symptoms in a stranger.

*Ansel,* I said to myself, *you have spent nine years being no one, and it has worked beautifully, and the price was only everything.*

The pass completed. The files resolved into a directory tree so corrupted it looked like a tree struck by lightning—fragments everywhere, most branches dead, a few stubborn limbs still holding signal. I ran integrity analysis. Of an estimated eleven thousand hours of archived chorus-recordings, maybe forty minutes were recoverable, scattered across nine years of station life like teeth after a fight.

Forty minutes out of nine years. That was what the massacre had left of itself, acoustically. I sat with that arithmetic for a while. It didn't improve.

I could play them in any order. Chronological seemed kindest—start with the living station, ease toward whatever came after. Kindness, I've learned, is usually just fear wearing a cardigan.

I put on the headphones because Ilo was asleep in the aft cabin and because some things you shouldn't make another person hear unless they ask. Then I opened the earliest fragment, timestamped four years before the end.

* * *

The first thing I heard was a market.

That sounds strange—a market inside a research station at the edge of contested space—but the Vessh had built Calyx as a joint facility, half human science, half Vessh archive, and where the two halves met there'd grown something no planner had designed: stalls, traders, a weekly exchange of goods and gossip conducted in six languages and one shared system of gestures. I heard it now through the chorus-core's patient ear: the clatter of crates, a human woman laughing, a Vessh voice doing that thing they do where a single speaker produces harmony with themselves, layered tones braided together like a chord learning to walk.

I knew that voice.

Not the individual—I never learned their name, or the name is somewhere in me behind a door I can't find—but the *style* of it, the cadence of Vessh speech as I'd been taught it, drilled into me across three years of training until it lived in my mouth the way my mother's accent lived in hers. I had spent nine years hearing that cadence only in dreams, muffled, underwater, always slightly wrong, like a song remembered by someone who'd only ever read the sheet music.

Now it was in my ears, awake, uncompressed, alive. A person was haggling over fruit protein yields four years before they died, and their voice was warm and annoyed and funny, and I gripped the edge of the console because grief arrived ahead of schedule, uninvited, for someone I'd never met.

*Get a hold of yourself,* I told myself. *You're a xenolinguist. You've processed harder material than a market scene.* This was true and also irrelevant. Processing is what you do afterward. First comes the part where the sound goes through you like a spear through water, disturbing everything, changing nothing on the surface.

Fragment two: a classroom. Human children—station families, the ones the histories never mention because joint facilities weren't supposed to have families—and a Vessh instructor teaching them phonemes through games. I could hear the little ones mangling the harmonic intervals, laughing at themselves, trying again. One child had a gift; you could hear it, the way you can hear a future in a voice. The instructor said something in Vessh that the core rendered cleanly, and I translated it automatically, the way you breathe:

*"This one will carry our words farther than we can."*

I paused the playback. Sat very still.

Somewhere in the wreck outside, in the vacuum-dark, that child's voice existed in these fragments too, or had existed, in the hours the corruption had eaten. Nine years of recordings, and the universe had let me keep forty minutes, and I found myself praying—not to anything, I'm not a praying animal, but the way you bargain with traffic—that the corruption had taken the worst of it and spared the ordinary days. Let the markets survive. Let the classrooms survive. Let the last thing preserved be someone laughing.

I unpaused, because mercy delayed is mercy denied, and cowardice dressed as pacing is still cowardice.

* * *

Fragments three through six were more of the living station. A maintenance argument. A poetry recital, bilingual, the audience responding in a ripple of both languages at once—which shouldn't work, harmonically, and yet did, the way a good translation works, not by matching but by answering. I listened to all of it with my eyes closed and my chest aching, hoarding it, because I understood now that I was doing exactly what the Vessh did: keeping the voices so the rooms wouldn't become geometry.

Then fragment seven. Timestamp: the night.

There is no gentle way to move from a recital to a scream, so I won't pretend I managed one gracefully. I'll tell you what the recording held, in order, because ordering things is how I keep from drowning.

First: alarms. Not human alarms—Vessh ones, tonal, rising through intervals that are, in their signaling grammar, the equivalent of a word I know and wish I didn't. *Seal. Seal. Seal.* Something was happening to the air or the hull or both.

Second: running. Hundreds of feet, dozens of voices, humans shouting in Standard and Vessh calling in layered tones, and underneath it all a low structural groan that my hindbrain identified before my training did: *breach, decompression, deck failure.* The station was coming apart around them and everyone in the recording knew it.

Third: the thing I will spend the rest of my life not explaining adequately. The Vessh voices stopped scattering and began to converge—acoustically, deliberately—into chorus. When Vessh face death, they don't pray alone. They merge their voices so that no one dies unheard, so that the room keeps them, so that whoever finds the recording later—and they design their archives expecting someone will—will have to carry all of them, together, indivisible. It is the most beautiful practice I have ever studied and I have hated knowing it since that night.

Two hundred voices braided into one. Screaming, yes, some of them screaming, and others singing, and others saying names—human names, Vessh names, the names of children—and all of it woven into a single sound that the chorus-core captured with terrible fidelity, because fidelity was the entire point of the technology.

I tore the headphones off.

The *Pale Meridian*'s cabin was silent. My hands were shaking. Outside, Calyx turned its slow broken turn, keeping its secrets, and I sat there breathing like a woman who'd surfaced too fast, waiting for the bends to take me.

Because here is the thing. Here is the thing I have to say even though saying it makes it real.

Underneath the chorus, in the last thirty seconds of fragment seven, there was a human voice. Close to the core's pickup—close enough that the recording caught breath, caught the small wet sounds of a throat working against panic. A young woman. Reading aloud, in flawless Vessh, the standard emergency litany: *We hear you. We carry you. Your words are not lost.*

A translator's duty. The oldest protocol in joint-service training: when the dying speak, the living translate, so the record holds both languages, so no one crosses over unwitnessed.

I knew the litany. I had written part of it—revised it, during training, tightened the phrasing until it sang. And the voice reading it, young and terrified and steady anyway, doing her job while a station died around her—

It was mine.

* * *

I played it again. That was a mistake. I played it a third time, which was a bigger mistake, and by the fourth time I had stopped pretending it was investigation and admitted it was compulsion, the way you press a bruise to confirm it's yours.

It was my voice. Not similar. Not suggestive. *Mine*—the specific instrument of it, the slight flatness on sustained tones that three vocal coaches failed to fix, the way I swallow the ends of phrases when I'm frightened. Twenty-six years old, give or take. Saying words I had helped write, in a language I dreamed in, in a place I had never been, on a night that officially never occurred, witnessed by no one, including me.

The horror isn't that I might have faked it. The horror is that I couldn't have. You cannot counterfeit your own voice under that kind of stress; every actor knows it, every liar knows it. Whatever else was true, that was me, at Calyx, on the last night, performing the translator's duty while two hundred people braided themselves into a single dying chord.

And I remembered none of it.

I want to be precise about what "none" means, because precision is the last tool I trust. It's not that the memory felt distant or dreamlike. It's that there was a shape where the memory should be—a negative space, memory-shaped, the way a missing tooth leaves a hole your tongue keeps finding. I could feel the edges of it. I could feel that something had been *removed*, cleanly, surgically, the way Fleet redacts a file: not destroyed, excised, with the wound closed over so smoothly you'd never know text had been there unless you already knew what text was.

I knew what text was. I was a linguist. My entire life was text.

Which raised a question I sat with for a long time, in the humming dark, with the headphones lying in my lap like a dead thing: who edits a mind? Files get edited by people with clearance. Memories get edited by people with *more*. And either answer—either I had done something so terrible that someone decided I needed to forget it, or something had been done to me so thoroughly that my own past was classified—meant that the woman I had spent nine years building, the careful invisible cargo-hauler with the spice rack and the tea she didn't drink, was constructed on top of a hole.

You can live on a foundation you don't inspect. People do it constantly. But once you've heard the hollow sound under the floorboards—

Fragment eight was the last one. Ninety seconds. Timestamp: after the end. After the chorus had stopped, which means after everyone in it had stopped.

It was mostly silence. Real silence, acoustic silence, the kind vacuum makes when it presses against a microphone that used to belong to a room full of people. But near the end, faint, distorted, half-eaten by corruption, there was one more voice. Human. Male. Giving orders in Standard, calm, unhurried, the tone of a man managing a logistics problem rather than a massacre.

I couldn't recover enough of it to identify him. Three words survived intact, and I have turned them over so many times they've worn smooth:

*"Secure the linguist."*

I sat in the dark for a long time after that. Long enough that the tea went fully cold, long enough that the viewport frost crept another centimeter, long enough that I heard Ilo moving in the aft cabin and had time to decide—quickly, badly, the way all my decisions were going now—not to tell him yet. Not because he'd betrayed me. Because I needed one more hour of being the only person in the universe who knew, before the knowledge became a thing with witnesses, and witnesses make things permanent.

Nine years I'd thought my ghosts were linguistic. Dreams in a dead language, a career in corpses of speech, a talent for talking to the gone. I'd built a whole identity on being haunted by *grammar*.

But the dead of Calyx hadn't been haunting me. They'd been *waiting* for me. Patiently, in forty minutes of surviving signal, holding their chorus, holding my own young voice like a returned letter, waiting for me to come close enough to listen.

And someone—some man with a calm voice and a fleet's worth of authority—had once ordered me secured, and I had evidently not stayed secured, because here I was, free, armed with nothing but a recovered fragment and a name on a manifest, drifting in the debris field of the crime I'd witnessed and couldn't remember.

The comm board chimed.

Not the *Pale Meridian*'s standard hail frequency. A tight-beam, military-grade, encrypted, aimed at us with the precision of someone who knew exactly where we were and had known for some time. The sender ID resolved, and I stared at it, and my stomach performed a maneuver no pilot training ever covers.

The identifier belonged to a training director I hadn't seen in nine years. Mara Quist. The woman who had signed off on my final evaluation, who had taught me the emergency litany, who had—my mind supplied, unhelpfully, with the new and terrible clarity of a woman listening to her own ghost—been the last person to see me before the hole in my memory began.

The message preview was one line long.

*Ansel. We need to talk about what you've heard.*

I hadn't transmitted anything. I hadn't played a single sound on open speakers. Which meant she didn't need to guess what I'd heard.

She already knew.
