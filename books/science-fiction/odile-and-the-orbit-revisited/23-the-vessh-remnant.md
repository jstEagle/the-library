## 23. The Vessh Remnant

Ilo said no.

He said it the way people say things they've already lost the argument on—loudly, twice, and then with a kind of resigned silence that meant he was already calculating how to survive my decision rather than prevent it. He stood in the Pale Meridian's cramped galley with his arms crossed, which is a posture that means "I am reasonable" in every human language I know, and possibly a few I don't.

"You can't just open a channel to the chorus-fleet," he said. "They're not a port authority. They're not even a government, exactly. They're—" he gestured vaguely, as if the Vessh could be summarized with hand movements, "—them."

"Them," I agreed. "Very helpful."

"You know what I mean. The remnant doesn't negotiate. They don't answer hails from Fleet. They sank three survey drones last month without so much as a courtesy ping. And you want to what—ring their doorbell?"

"I want to say something true to someone who might understand it," I said. "Which, yes, historically has gone badly for me."

He looked at me for a long moment. There was something almost gentle in it, which was worse than anger. Anger I could work with. Gentleness made me feel like a specimen.

"The truce expires in thirty days," he said quietly. "If you hail them and they read it as provocation—if Fleet reads your hail as contact with a hostile power—you won't just lose your immunity, Ansel. You'll give Serevan exactly the casus belli he's been shopping for. A human xenolinguist, trained under Chorus, opening unauthorized channels to the enemy? He couldn't write better propaganda if he tried."

"He's already written it," I said. "It's called the official record."

"That's not funny."

"No," I said. "It isn't. It's also not a reason."

Ilo exhaled through his nose and uncrossed his arms, which in Ilo-language meant surrender of the verbal kind only. "At least let me be on the bridge when you do it. If they answer, someone should witness who isn't dreaming in their language. Someone whose sanity I can vouch for."

"Your sanity or mine?"

"Yes," he said.

* * *

The thing nobody tells you about speaking a dead language is that it isn't dead. It's just sleeping, and it sleeps in *you*, and some nights it turns over and murmurs things you'd rather it didn't.

I had spent nine years treating the Vessh tongue like a locked room in my own head. I didn't translate it anymore. I didn't practice it. When phrases surfaced in dreams—and they always did, tide-like, indifferent to my preferences—I woke and wrote nothing down and told myself the forgetting was a kind of hygiene.

But since Calyx Station, since the ghost signals, since the counteragent cracked open nine sealed years like an egg against a counter, the language had stopped being content to sleep. It sat up. It looked around. It had opinions.

So when I sat down at the comm console on the third watch, alone except for Ilo pretending to read a maintenance manual two meters away, the words were already there. Waiting. They had been waiting, I understood now, for a very long time.

The chorus-fleet's last known position was a drifting coordinate cluster past the Hesper Shelf—a region of space where navigation charts went politely vague, the way maps used to say *here be dragons*. The Vessh didn't hide there. They simply existed somewhere Fleet's instruments kept failing to agree on, which is its own kind of armor.

I routed the transmission through the long-band array, stripped the encryption, and left the channel raw. No Fleet protocols. No authentication headers. Just voice, unencrypted, in a language that officially did not exist aboard any registered human vessel.

My hands were steady. That surprised me. Everything else—the pulse tapping at my throat, the sweat along my spine, the small animal part of my brain screaming *prey, prey, run*—was not steady at all. But my hands remembered this work. Nine years of cargo runs and my hands still knew how to open a channel like a door you intend to hold.

I pressed transmit.

And I spoke.

* * *

What I said isn't translatable into anything I could put in a report, which is precisely why I said it in Vessh. Human languages are built for claims. Vessh is built for something older than claiming—it's built for *standing inside a statement together*, the way you stand inside a room. You don't assert a truth in Vessh. You offer it a place to live, and if it's true, it stays.

I said: *I am the one who was there.*

I said: *I carried your voices without knowing I carried them, and I set them down, and I have spent nine years walking around the empty place where they were.*

I said: *I remember now. Not all of it. Enough.*

I said: *I am not asking forgiveness. I am asking to be heard by those who were never given the courtesy of being heard at all.*

Then I did the hardest thing, the thing my training director Mara Quist would have flinched at, the thing the whole architecture of Chorus had been designed to prevent: I stopped talking and left the channel open. Silence, in Vessh communication, is not absence. It is invitation. You leave the door of the sentence open and you wait to see what walks in.

The silence lasted four minutes. I counted. Ilo had stopped pretending to read; the manual lay face-down on his knee like a bird playing dead.

Then the channel breathed.

Not answered—*breathed*. A sound came through the speakers that I felt in my sternum before I parsed it with my ears: the low harmonic hum of Vessh choral speech, dozens of voices braided into one sustained tone, the way they spoke when they spoke as a fleet rather than as individuals. Humans who'd heard recordings described it as eerie. Descriptions are always written by people who weren't being spoken *to*.

To me it sounded like a house I'd been locked out of for nine years, with the lights on inside.

The chorus resolved into distinct registers, and one voice rose above the others—not louder, but clearer, the way a soloist emerges without breaking the chord. The voice was old. Vessh voices don't age the way ours do; they deepen toward something like gravel wrapped in velvet. This one had decades in it.

And it said my name.

Not Okafor. Not the name on my current registry, the alias I'd worn like a coat for nearly a decade. My *other* name. The buried one. The birth name that appeared on a manifest for a ship that officially never flew, the name I'd drowned in paperwork and distance and deliberate forgetting.

It said my name the way the Vessh say names—which is to say, it said the name *and everything attached to it*. In Vessh, a name isn't a label. It's a compressed history. When they speak your name, they speak the shape of what you did, and I stood on the bridge of my own ship and heard myself described back to me in syllables I hadn't consciously spoken in nine years:

*the small translator who wept in the corridor and translated anyway.*

Ilo made a sound beside me. I think it was meant to be a word.

"They know you," he whispered. "Ansel—they *know* you."

"Of course they know me," I said, and my voice came out strange, thin, like it belonged to the girl I'd been before. "I was there."

* * *

Here is what I had forgotten—or sealed, or buried, or whatever verb fits best for what was done to my memory with chemistry and consent-that-wasn't: the Vessh do not forget voices. Ever. Their cognition doesn't store language the way ours does, in fragments and approximations. They store *voices*, whole, the way we store faces of people we love. A Vessh who has heard you speak once carries you forever. It's why their mourning practices center on recitation—grief is literally the act of keeping voices alive by using them.

Which means that somewhere in that chorus-fleet, for nine years, my voice had been carried. Spoken in mourning cycles. Kept alive by people I had helped destroy.

I asked them—I remember my hands shaking now, finally, when it was too late for shaking to matter—*why keep my voice? Why not let it die with the others?*

The old voice answered, and the answer rearranged something in my chest that I don't think will ever sit the same way again.

*Because you were the only one who asked us questions in our own tongue while the order was being given,* it said. *Because you said "I'm sorry" in Vessh eleven times, and each time it was true, and the eleventh time you also said "I will remember," and then you were taken away and made to forget. We kept your voice because a promise was made in it. Promises live in the voice that made them. We have been keeping your promise for you.*

*I have been keeping your promise for you,* it said again, softer, and the chorus behind it shifted into a minor register that I recognized, from a lifetime ago, as the sound Vessh make when they grieve in company.

I put my hand flat on the console because the alternative was putting it over my mouth, and neither of us needed that.

"I'm here now," I said aloud, in both languages at once, which I didn't know I could do until I did it. "The promise is mine again. Tell me what you kept."

* * *

They told me over six hours. I recorded all of it. Ilo sat with me the entire time, and I will grant him this: he never once suggested we cut the channel short, even when the fleet's traffic monitors would have been logging every second, even when every minute of open contact was another nail in whatever coffin my anonymity was going to be buried in.

The Vessh remnant—forty-one ships, what remained of a civilization that had once sung across three systems—had done something in the aftermath of Calyx that no one in Fleet Command had ever bothered to find out, because no one in Fleet Command had ever bothered to ask.

They had preserved the record.

Not data, exactly. Not logs. The Vessh don't archive the way we do, cold and indexed. They *carry* things. Every survivor of Calyx Station—every Vessh crew member who escaped the station before Serevan's experiment cascaded, every voice that heard the final transmissions—had spent nine years reciting the record in rotation, a living chain of custody, passed voice to voice so that no single carrier's death could break it. They had the timeline of the massacre. They had the frequencies of Project Chorus's signal weapon. They had the moment of Serevan's order, spoken in his own clipped command-register, captured by a Vessh sensor suite that Fleet's analysts had dismissed as debris noise.

And they had me. Young Ansel, twenty-four years old, translating Chorus's technical demands to a Vessh engineering crew who understood perfectly well what was being built and were begging—not resisting, *begging*, in careful, courteous, terrified Vessh—for someone to explain to the humans that the device could not be tested safely, that the resonance cascade was not hypothetical, that the mathematics sang its warning to anyone fluent enough to hear it.

They had my answers. My translations. The ones where I took their pleas and rendered them, faithfully, accurately, into Fleet-standard syntax—and handed them up the chain to officers who filed them and proceeded anyway.

That was the part I hadn't remembered. Under the counteragent, in Mara's safehouse, I had recovered the begging. I had recovered the corridor and the weeping. What I had not recovered—what my mind had apparently walled off even from the chemical unsealing—was my own complicity in the translation itself. Not falsified. Not betrayed. Just... delivered. Accurately. Professionally. The way a good xenolinguist delivers bad news: clearly, completely, and then standing aside while other people decide what the words are worth.

The Vessh had kept that too. All of it. Without editing. Without softening. They had carried my worst hour with the same fidelity as my eleven apologies, because to them the record wasn't a weapon or a shield. It was a debt, and debts are carried whole.

"Why?" I asked, near the end, when the six hours had hollowed me out to a rind. "Why keep all of it? You could have kept only the parts that condemn him. Only the parts that acquit me. Records can be curated. Yours weren't."

The old voice was quiet a moment. When it answered, the chorus behind it had gone very still, the way a room goes still when something important is being said.

*Because a record that serves a purpose is a story,* it said. *We have watched your species tell stories about us for nine years. Stories are how you prepare a war. We did not want to answer your stories with stories of our own. We wanted to answer with what happened.*

*And,* it added, after a pause that contained multitudes, *because you asked us to remember, and we are not a people who break promises—even promises made by the frightened.*

* * *

Ilo didn't speak until the channel closed. Then he sat back in the copilot's chair and rubbed his face with both hands, and when he lowered them his eyes were wet, which he would deny later, and which I will never mention to him, including here.

"They've had the evidence," he said. "This whole time. Nine years. They've had Serevan's order on record, authenticated by their own sensory architecture, and they just—held it. Waiting."

"For what?"

"For you," he said. "Or someone like you. Someone who could carry it back across in a form both species would have to hear." He laughed, short and bitter. "Do you understand what you're holding? The Vessh copy plus the original logs—that's not testimony anymore. That's checkmate. If we can get it authenticated, if Quist's keys—"

"If," I said.

"And there's the other thing." He turned his chair to face me fully. "You realize what just happened on that channel, right? From Fleet's perspective? Long-band transmission, unencrypted, in Vessh, from an unregistered route, sustained for six hours. The moment Serevan's signals people finish triangulating—and they will, they're probably already doing it—you're not a ghost anymore. You're a collaborator. Or an asset. Whichever word plays better in the briefing."

"I know."

"And knowing that, you'd do it again."

I thought about lying to him. I thought about the version of the answer that was strategically wise, the one that acknowledged risk and hedged and kept my options dressed and ready. But Ilo had sat through six hours of my worst self being read back to me in a dead language, and he hadn't looked away once, and some debts you pay in honesty.

"Yes," I said. "I'd do it again. I spent nine years being no one, Ilo. It turns out 'no one' was just a name the guilty gave themselves. They remember my voice. After everything, after what I did—they kept it, and they kept the promise I made in it, and they handed it back to me like it was never in question." I looked at the dark viewport, at the stars that didn't care about any of this. "I'm not going back to being no one. Whatever it costs."

Ilo nodded slowly. Then he pulled his handheld from his jacket, thumbed it awake, and turned the screen toward me.

"My faction needs to move within seventy-two hours," he said. "Seize the Central Archive before the truce expires. Force is on the table. It was always on the table." His jaw worked. "But after tonight—after hearing *that*—I have to ask you something, and I need the truth, because I'm about to either green-light an operation that could start a war or burn the only allies I have left."

"Ask."

He leaned forward. "Can the truth be delivered without force? Because if it can't—if Serevan's wall is too thick, if the only way through is a breach—then tell me now, Ansel. Tell me what you'd spend to be heard."

Outside the viewport, far off past the Hesper Shelf, something moved against the stars. Forty-one ships, holding position, holding a promise, waiting to see what I would do with the voice they'd carried for me.

I opened my mouth to answer.

And the comm console lit up—an incoming priority signal, Fleet authentication codes, flagged urgent, flagged *direct*—with a sender ID I recognized from a lifetime ago and had hoped never to see again.

Admiral Serevan was calling.
