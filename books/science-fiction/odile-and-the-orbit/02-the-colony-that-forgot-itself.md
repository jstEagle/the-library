## 2. The Colony That Forgot Itself

The morning bell had not rung in four years. Tamsin Almeida still woke to its absence every day, her body keeping time with a ritual the colony had voted out of existence in a single tired evening session, and she lay for a while in the grey light that seeped through the habitat's polymer shutters, listening to Odile breathe.

It breathed through the vents. That was all. The great air processors down in Sublevel Two cycled the atmosphere on a forty-minute rhythm, and if you lay very still you could hear it—the long inhale through the eastern ducts, the slower exhale along the floor grates, warm and mineral-smelling, like a sleeping animal too old to dream. Seven years ago she had found the sound unbearable. Now it was the only voice she trusted to tell her the world was still there.

She rose, dressed in the layered wool the weavers' co-op made from hydroponic flax, and pinned her hair back with the same cracked bone comb she'd carried from Earth-orbit, from a life that felt less like memory than like something she'd read about in someone else's book. On the wall beside her cot hung the recording rig: a lattice of salvaged sensor filaments shaped like a moth's wing, its fibers tuned to the resonances of human speech. She touched one filament, lightly, the way you'd touch a sleeping child's cheek, and went out into the colony.

* * *

Odile had been named by people who expected it to be beautiful.

That was the first thing they told you when you arrived, back when arrivals were things that happened: the name came from an old survey poet, some cartographer with more lyricism than data, who had looked at the long amber twilight of the gas giant hanging over this moon and thought of a woman in a painting, turning away. The first colonists had landed believing in that beauty. They'd planted orchards in the regolith domes. They'd carved the settlement's founding lintel with words in eleven languages—*we come to stay*—and for eleven years, they stayed.

Then the fleets stopped coming. Then the resupply contracts lapsed. Then the jump corridor itself was rerouted, quietly, by parties who never sent so much as an apology, and Odile became what the shipping charts called it now: a notation. A hazard marker. *Colony presumed nonviable.*

Two hundred and fourteen people remained. Tamsin knew because she counted them—not their bodies, though she could have, but their voices. She kept a ledger, handwritten in three notebooks that were themselves becoming artifacts, of every living speaker on the moon: what language they dreamed in, what songs they remembered from before, what stories they told their children or had decided not to. It was, she supposed, a census of a kind. But censuses counted people as present. What Tamsin was doing felt closer to counting the last light in a room before the sun finished setting.

She began her walk at the eastern arcologies, where the oldest colonists lived, because the morning was kindest there and she liked to collect kindness early, the way other people collected firewood.

Mama Ijeoma was already awake, of course. She was ninety-one and had been awake, she claimed, since the evacuation order that never quite arrived. Her quarters smelled of dried bitterleaf and machine oil, an old woman's smell, a working smell, and she sat by her window grinding grain with a stone she'd brought across nine hundred light-years because, she said, flour that came from a machine tasted like nothing, and she had eaten enough of nothing to last a lifetime.

"You're walking again," Mama Ijeoma said, without looking up. "Recording?"

"Listening," Tamsin said. "There's a difference."

"Hm." The stone turned. "In my mother's village there was a man who listened to walls. They said he heard the dead talking in the mortar." She glanced up then, one eye bright and terrible with amusement. "They also said he was mad. You want tea?"

"I want your story. The one about the crossing."

"The crossing." The stone stilled. "You have it already. Twice."

"Once more. Please. The last version was better—you added the part about the singing."

"There was no singing."

"There is now."

The old woman laughed—a dry rustle, like wind through the flax fields—and Tamsin opened the recorder's wing of filaments above her shoulder and let it drink. This was the work. Not the extraction of facts; anyone could inventory a life. The work was the *adding*. Every telling changed the story slightly, grew it a new room, and Tamsin had learned—first as a scholar, then as something humbler, something closer to a daughter—that the changes were not corruption. They were the language breathing. A story that never varied was already dead; it just hadn't lain down yet.

Outside Mama Ijeoma's window, the amber giant filled a quarter of the sky, banded and patient, turning its slow face toward them. Odile's sun was a small fierce star behind it, and the light that reached the habitats was always secondhand, always reflected, always the color of old honey and old grief.

* * *

Tamsin had not chosen exile. That was the sentence she used, in the rare moments anyone asked, and it was true the way a knife is true.

Before Odile there had been the Meridian Linguistic Institute, orbiting Meridian Prime itself, all glass and ambition, and before that there had been the work: the Kessuari corpus, the great dead language of a species that had gone silent two centuries before humanity learned to listen. Tamsin had been the youngest senior fellow ever appointed to the reconstruction project. She had published the grammar that made her name. She had stood at a podium and said, to a hall of ten thousand, *a language dies only when no one dreams in it, and I intend to keep this one dreaming.*

And then she had done the thing.

Even now, seven years later, she circled the memory the way her tongue circulated a broken tooth. The Institute had wanted the Kessuari archive sealed—classified under cultural property law, access restricted to credentialed researchers, the dead language locked in a vault where it could decay politely without embarrassing anyone. There had been a dispute over a fragment. A disputed translation. Tamsin had believed—had *known*, with the arrogant certainty of thirty-one years and one famous book—that the Institute's reading was wrong, that the fragment proved the Kessuari had not died out but departed, deliberately, and left instructions. And so she had copied the full corpus—all of it, every phoneme, every glyph, every half-recovered prayer—and released it to the open networks, unencrypted, unattributed, unasked.

The scandal had lasted eleven months. The tribunal had lasted three days. Exile to a nonviable colony was technically a mercy; the alternative had been prison, and prison would have meant silence, and silence, for Tamsin Almeida, was the only sentence she truly feared.

So they sent her here, to the end of the rerouted corridor, to a colony the charts had already buried. And she had arrived furious, and stayed furious for a year, and then one evening in the hydroponics bay an old engineer named Devi had started humming a song in a language Tamsin didn't know—a real one, a living one, a human tongue carried here from some drowned coast on Earth—and Tamsin had felt something turn over in her chest like a ship changing heading.

No one was recording these people. No institute claimed them. Their languages were small, unwritten, held in throats and memories and the particular way a grandmother said a child's name—and when the colony finally failed, when the last generator coughed and the last vent went quiet, everything they carried would go with them, unwitnessed, into the dark.

She had built the first recorder from salvage in six weeks. She had been building the archive ever since.

Not for the fleets. Not for history, whatever history was anymore. For whoever might someday pass close enough to catch a whisper—for a message no one may ever receive, addressed the way all messages to the dead are addressed: in faith rather than expectation. *We were here. We spoke. Listen.*

* * *

Midmorning, she crossed the central commons, and the commons broke her heart on schedule, as it did every day.

It had been designed for four thousand. The plaza's tessellated paving ran half a kilometer in each direction, ringed by arcology towers whose upper floors had been sealed off years ago, their windows dark, their balconies furred with frost-lichen that glowed faintly violet in the reflected light of the giant. In the center stood the founding lintel, salvaged and re-erected by the second generation: *WE COME TO STAY*, in eleven scripts, the letters pitted by micrometeorite spray from the years the dome had leaked. Children played soccer against the lintel's base, using it as a goal wall, their shouts ricocheting up the dead towers. Two hundred and fourteen souls in a city built for twenty times their number, rattling around inside it like seeds in a gourd.

Tamsin walked the perimeter and did her count.

The weavers' co-op, six members, working the flax looms by hand because the automated mills had eaten their last spare part two winters ago—she noted the click and slap of the shuttles, a rhythm she'd recorded under the label *industry, domestic, hopeful*. The schoolroom in Tower Four, where Nkechi Okafor taught nineteen children everything from arithmetic to orbital mechanics with a patience Tamsin marveled at daily; through the door she caught the tail of a recitation, children chanting multiplication tables in three languages at once, braiding them together the way children do, unconscious of the borders adults draw around tongues. The medbay, where Dr. Ferro kept two hundred years of obsolete equipment alive through what he called "aggressive friendship." The kitchens, the water reclamation gardens, the little shrine-room where the Muslim and Christian and traditionalist families had negotiated, over five difficult years, a shared schedule of silences.

And everywhere, the empty places. Whole residential arcs sealed and cold. The transit tunnels closed at Junction Nine after the cave-in nobody had the power budget to clear. The orchard domes, abandoned in her third year, where the trees had gone strange and wonderful without pruning—frozen mid-gesture, branches raised like the arms of dancers surprised by music.

A language, Tamsin thought, pausing at the edge of the orchard dome, breath fogging in the chill. That was what a dying colony was. Not the buildings—buildings were only grammar, structure, the scaffolding of meaning. The language was the people, the living speech of them, and it was thinning. She could hear it thinning. Each year the stories got shorter, the songs fewer, the old words replaced by fleet-standard pidgin because pidgin was easier and the young were tired and who could blame them. Mama Ijeoma's generation held perhaps six hundred distinct lexical items that existed nowhere else in human space. When they went, those six hundred words would go, and no one would ever again be able to say *that specific quality of light on water at dusk, the kind that makes you homesick for a place you've never been*—because that word existed only here, only in one throat, and Tamsin had recorded it twice and needed to record it ten more times before winter.

She pressed her palm flat against the orchard dome's cold glass and watched the frozen trees.

*This is why,* she reminded herself. *This is what the exile is for.* Punishment transmuted, by stubbornness, into vocation. The Institute had meant to bury her somewhere her voice wouldn't matter. Instead they had handed her the most important archive of her career and dared her, by omission, to care about it.

She cared. God help her, she cared so much it frightened Sefa sometimes—she could see it frighten him, the ferocity of it, the way she'd kneel in the dust of the lower tunnels for hours to capture the echo signature of a room no one would ever use again.

Sefa.

She turned away from the orchard and walked toward the engineering levels, and tried, as she did every day, not to think about him, and failed, as she did every day.

* * *

They had come on the same transport, seven years ago. That was the joke neither of them told anymore: the exile ship, population twelve, and the two of them seated across from each other in the mess for the entire nine-month crossing, arguing about gravity.

He was the engineer; she was the linguist; the argument was about whether the ship's artificial spin constituted a *language*—whether systems had grammar, whether machines spoke. He'd said no, obviously, machines don't mean anything. She'd said meaning was just pattern plus stakes, and the spin system's patterns kept them all alive, which seemed like stakes enough. He'd called her a romantic. She'd called him a mechanic. By the time they made orbit around Odile they were inseparable, and everyone on the transport had seen it coming except, apparently, them.

Three years. Three years of shared quarters and shared work and the particular intimacy of two people building something together in a place the universe had forgotten—he keeping the lights on, she keeping the record—and then the slow estrangement, which had no villain in it, which was the worst part. Just divergence. He looked at the failing reactors and saw a problem to be solved by negotiation with the outside; she looked at the dying voices and saw a debt that couldn't be paid by rescue. He wanted to petition the fleets, break the silence, beg for the corridor to be reopened. She wanted the colony to stay invisible, because she had read the shipping politics, because she knew what both armadas did to unclaimed assets, and because—though she'd never said this plainly, even to herself—some stubborn cell in her body believed that being written off had been the colony's salvation. Invisible things survived.

They had fought about it for two years, gently at first, then not gently, and then one night eighteen months ago Sefa had moved his tools to the engineering level and not moved them back, and now they passed each other in the corridors like colleagues, courteous and precise, two halves of one archive refusing to share a table.

She missed him in the specific way you miss a language you used to dream in.

* * *

The afternoon took her to the western terraces, where the elders gathered when the light was good.

There were eleven of them today, wrapped in woven blankets, sitting in a semicircle on benches facing the great window that framed the amber giant. Old Tomas was telling the flood story—the one about the year the ice aquifer breached and half of Sublevel Three drowned, before Tamsin's time—and the others punctuated him with the low choral sounds that were, Tamsin suspected, the oldest thing on the moon: older than the colony, older than the ships, carried here in the bodies of people whose grandmothers' grandmothers had sung the same notes over other floods, other losses, on a blue world none of them would ever see.

She sat at the edge of the circle, opened the recorder's wing, and let it run.

Idris was there, of course. The boy was always there. Fourteen years old, born on Odile, sharp-faced and serious, with his mother's gift for questions that went through you like needles. He sat cross-legged at Tomas's feet, and when the story ended he asked it—"What happened to the family in Bay Twelve? Did they rebuild? Where did they sleep after?"—and Tomas answered, and Tamsin watched the boy's face and understood that he was doing exactly what she did, in miniature, with none of her defenses. Collecting. Refusing to let the flood take even the memory of the flood.

Their eyes met across the circle. The boy nodded at her recorder, solemn as a colleague. She nodded back.

Later, walking home through the dusk shift, when the habitat lights dimmed to simulate evening and the vents exhaled their long warm breath, Tamsin climbed to the observation blister at the top of Tower One—an extravagance of glass and steel the founders had built for stargazing, maintained now by a single heating element and Tamsin's private stubbornness—and looked out at the dark beyond the giant.

The stars here were wrong. That was the thing no one warned you about deep space: the constellations dissolved, the familiar shapes scattered, and the sky became a field of strangers. Somewhere out there, past all those stranger-stars, the two fleets maneuvered in their slow cold chess, and the corridor that had once connected Odile to everything lay rerouted and empty, and no signal worth the name had come down that corridor in seven years.

She thought about the archive. Forty thousand hours of it now. Voices, songs, arguments, jokes, the sound of the looms, the sound of the vents, the sound of rain they manufactured in the reclamation gardens because the children had asked what rain sounded like. All of it encoded, triplicated, stored in shielded cores beneath the tower—addressed to no one, preserved for everyone, a letter mailed into a darkness with no known address.

*A message no one may ever receive.* She had made her peace with that. She had thought she had.

Because tonight, looking out at the indifferent stars, Tamsin felt the old unease stir again—the one she'd been carrying for weeks without naming it, a pressure behind the eyes like weather coming. The colony was too quiet lately. Not the peaceful quiet of a place at rest, but the held-breath quiet of a place being listened to. The relay dishes on the northern ridge had been cycling oddly. The power draw readings Sefa muttered about had margins that wouldn't resolve. Small things. Nothing. Probably nothing.

Probably.

She pulled her blanket tighter and stayed in the blister until the simulated night deepened, watching the stranger-stars, counting them like names, like the 214, like everything she was afraid to lose—and below her, in the darkened commons, the vents breathed in, and breathed out, and the colony slept on, unaware of how closely the dark might be listening.
