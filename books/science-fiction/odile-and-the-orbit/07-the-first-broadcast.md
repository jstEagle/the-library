## 7. The First Broadcast

The vote was held in the old assembly hall, the one with the cracked dome where rain had been coming through for three years, dripping into a bucket nobody emptied because the sound had become part of the colony's breathing. Two hundred and fourteen people. One hundred and nine raised their hands for the lie. Ninety-eight against. Seven abstained, and Tamsin understood every one of those seven better than she understood herself.

She stood at the front with Sefa and did not look at her while the hands went up. She looked instead at the bucket, at the slow dark drip, at the way a single drop of water could keep time for an entire room of people deciding whether to gamble their lives on a fiction.

"It's close," Sefa said afterward, in the corridor outside, her voice carefully level. "Close enough that we treat it as unanimous. Nobody gets to say later that they weren't part of this."

"That's not how unanimity works."

"That's exactly how survival works." Sefa stopped walking and turned to face her, and in the dim amber light of the corridor her face was all planes and exhaustion, the beautiful stubborn architecture of it worn down to something rawer. "Tamsin. I need you to hear me. If this fails—if they come and find nothing—we die as liars. But if we don't try, we die as honest people, and the difference between those two deaths is nothing but a word in someone else's report."

"You've been thinking about my reports."

"I've been thinking about you for seven years. It's a habit I can't break."

Tamsin wanted to answer that with something true, and found she couldn't afford truth anymore, not even the small domestic kind. That was the thing about the lie they'd voted into being: it didn't stay contained. It leaked. It got into the water supply of everything.

* * *

The dead comms array sat on the ridge above the original landing site like the ribcage of something enormous that had lain down there to die a century ago. Its dishes were furred with lichen the color of old copper. Its cables ran down into the rock and terminated, most of them, in nothing—Sefa had traced them years ago, mapping the colony's nervous system back to its severed spine.

For three days they worked inside it, and Tamsin learned things about Sefa's hands that seven years of estrangement had let her forget: how they moved when they were certain, quick and economical, no wasted gesture; how they hesitated only when Sefa was about to do something she considered morally interesting. The hesitation came now, as Sefa spliced a live power line into a junction that hadn't carried current since before Idris was born.

"Once we wake this up," Sefa said, not looking at her, "it's visible. Everything we send from here is a shout. Seven years of quiet, gone in one transmission."

"I know what a broadcast is, Sefa."

"Do you?" She turned then, and her eyes were very tired and very kind, which was the worst combination. "Because I don't think you've fully priced what we're asking you to do. You're going to speak in a language you invented. You spent your whole career—" she gestured vaguely at Tamsin, at all of her, "—transcribing. Faithfully. You used to correct people's memories, do you remember? At dinner, on the *Meridian* transport out here, you corrected a woman's memory of her own wedding. Gently. But you corrected it."

"She thanked me for it."

"She did. Because you were right." Sefa picked up the splice tool again, held it like a cigarette she'd decided not to smoke. "And now I'm asking you to be wrong. On purpose. In your own voice. To two armadas."

Tamsin said nothing. Outside, through the array's broken lattice, Odile's sun was going down behind the hydroponics bay, and the light came through the structure in long amber bars that lay across the floor like fallen masts.

* * *

Here is what Tamsin knew about lies, professionally: that every language has one word for the deliberate falsehood and a dozen words for the adjacent crimes—the exaggeration, the flattery, the polite fiction, the white mercy. That in the dead language she dreamed in—the language of the first colonists' founders, preserved in forty thousand hours of recordings that might never be heard by anyone—the distinction was even finer. They had a word, *senneth*, that meant specifically: a false statement made to protect a listener who cannot yet bear the truth. And they had another word, *senneth-va*, which meant the same thing except spoken by someone who would carry the burden of it alone, forever, so the listener never had to know there had been a burden at all.

She had transcribed the distinction eleven years ago, sitting in a university office on a world she was no longer permitted to name, and she had written in the margin of her notes: *a culture that grammaticalizes mercy is a culture that has suffered*. Her supervisor had underlined it twice and written *beautiful* beside it, and six months later he had signed the order that put her on the transport to Odile, because she had recorded the wrong conversation, or recorded the right one too faithfully, and fidelity itself had become the crime.

So she knew about lies. What she had never done was author one.

* * *

They built the handshake over four nights.

A jump-anchor, when it woke, spoke a specific grammar: a carrier pulse, a self-identification string, a calibration sequence that told any listening fleet *I am real, I am stable, I am worth crossing a war to reach*. Sefa had the protocols memorized from her service years—she'd helped install anchors once, back when she'd still believed in institutions—and she reconstructed the handshake from memory, from salvaged manuals, from fragments in the dead array's own firmware, which had been designed to *listen* for such things and therefore contained, buried in its code, the shape of the conversation it was built to overhear.

"We don't have to fake the physics," Sefa explained, drawing on the dust of the array floor with her finger. "We just have to fake the *voice*. A sensor three systems out doesn't feel the anchor. It hears it. So we make the array sing the right song, and we let their imaginations do the rest. Fleets are desperate, Tamsin. Desperate listeners hear what they need."

"And if one of them sends a ship to touch it?"

"Then we buy the months between the song and the touch. That's all this is. Months. Enough time to—" She stopped. Neither of them had said aloud what the time was for. Evacuation was a word that meant scattering, and scattering was a word that meant admitting the colony was already dead and just hadn't finished.

"The handshake needs a speaker," Sefa said finally. "Not a machine voice. Their linguists will profile it. It has to sound like a person who believes what she's saying."

"And you want me."

"I want the only person on this world whose voice I'd believe."

* * *

On the fifth night, Tamsin sat alone in the array's control room with a script Sefa had drafted and a microphone older than the colony, and she could not begin.

The script was good. That was the problem. Sefa had written it in careful, plausible technical language—a maintenance crew reporting a dormant anchor reactivating, requesting verification on standard channels, apologizing for the delay with a colonist's rough politeness. Every sentence was a stone laid across a river. And Tamsin read it four times and understood that she could deliver it perfectly and it would still fail, because fleets did not trust perfect. Perfect was what machines produced, and machines could be spoofed. What fleets trusted was texture. Error. The human grain.

So she set the script aside, and she did the thing her training had spent twenty years teaching her never to do.

She improvised.

She thought about the woman whose wedding memory she had once corrected. She thought about the bucket in the assembly hall, keeping time. She thought about Idris, who had asked her last week whether the stars he'd never visited were louder than the ones over Odile, and she had told him yes, and hated herself for the small dishonesty of comfort. She thought about *senneth-va*: the burden carried alone.

Then she opened the channel and spoke—not Sefa's script but a life. She spoke as a maintenance chief named Alsen Vey, whom she invented in real time, giving him a wife with a bad hip and a daughter studying power systems, giving him twenty-six years on Odile and a tired man's impatience with protocol. She reported the anchor waking in language that was ninety percent true—the array really was old, the power grid really was failing, the delays she apologized for were real delays—and she wove the ten percent of fiction through it so finely that even she, reading the transcript back afterward, had to hunt for the seams.

It took eleven minutes. When she closed the channel, her hands were shaking, and the shaking was not fear. It was the specific tremor of a body that has done something irreversible. She had spent her career making records that other people could trust absolutely, and she had just made a record designed to be believed and to be false, and both fleets, somewhere out in the dark, were already leaning toward it like plants toward a light.

Sefa found her there an hour later, still sitting in the same chair, the transcript glowing on the screen between them like something caught.

"You changed it," Sefa said.

"It needed errors."

"Tamsin." Sefa crouched beside the chair, and after a moment she took Tamsin's hand, which neither of them had done in longer than either could count. "Listen to me. What you just made—it's the best forgery in the history of forgery precisely because it isn't one. There's more truth in it than lie. Anyone analyzing it will find a real woman's cadence, real fatigue, real love for a real place. They'll find you."

"Then they'll find a liar."

"They'll find a translator." Sefa's grip tightened. "That's what you did tonight. You translated us. From a dying colony into a language fleets understand. That's all translation ever is—making one world legible to another. You just chose which world survives the rendering."

Tamsin looked at the transcript. Eleven minutes of a man who did not exist, loving a place that did, in words that were mostly true. Somewhere in the middle of it she had given Alsen Vey a line about his daughter—*she thinks the anchor sings in its sleep, and maybe she's right, and maybe that's the only prayer I know*—and she had said it without thinking, and now it sat in the record like a splinter of her actual heart lodged in the body of the lie.

"Sefa," she said. "If this works. If they come. What happens to Alsen Vey?"

"He retires," Sefa said gently. "He fades into the background. Forgeries get simpler over time, not harder. We'll age him out."

"No." Tamsin shook her head slowly. "He can't fade. He has to *live*. He has to keep having opinions, keep complaining about his hip, keep worrying about his daughter. Because the day Alsen Vey stops being a person is the day someone notices he was never one. A lie isn't a wall, Sefa. It's a garden. You don't build it once. You tend it until the war is over or you're dead."

Sefa was quiet for a long moment. Then she laughed—a short, exhausted, admiring sound, the laugh of a woman watching her own plan grow teeth she hadn't anticipated.

"Then we tend it together," she said.

* * *

The reply came thirty-one hours later.

It came on a frequency the Concord fleet had used for diplomatic traffic in the years before the schism, which meant it was old, which meant it was automated, which meant somewhere far away a machine had heard Alsen Vey's tired voice and flagged it for attention, and somewhere beyond that machine a human being had read the flag and decided, at minimum, to listen.

The message was short. Tamsin translated it standing up, refusing to sit, refusing to give her body the option of collapse:

*UNREGISTERED ANCHOR SIGNAL DETECTED — SECTOR ODILE. VERIFICATION PROTOCOLS TO FOLLOW. MAINTAIN CURRENT STATUS. DO NOT ATTEMPT JUMP CALIBRATION PENDING INSPECTION.*

Do not attempt jump calibration. Which meant: *we believe you*. Which meant: *we are coming*.

She read it three times. Then she walked down from the ridge to the settlement, through the cold blue hour before dawn, past the hydroponics bay where nine seconds of falling had started all of this, past the assembly hall where the bucket still dripped its patient timekeeping, and she stood outside the door of the engineering office until Sefa opened it, hair uncombed, eyes already awake, because Sefa had been awake too—everyone had been awake, the whole colony lying in the dark feeling the sky change weight.

"Well?" Sefa asked.

"They believe us." Tamsin heard her own voice come out flat and strange, a stranger's voice. "Verification protocols. An inspection team. We bought our credibility, Sefa. Now we find out what it costs."

Sefa exhaled, long and slow, and for a moment she leaned her forehead against the doorframe, an inch from Tamsin's shoulder, close enough that Tamsin could feel the warmth of her, the seven years and the three days and the eleven minutes all compressed into that small distance.

"Then we start tending the garden," Sefa said. "Alsen Vey's daughter needs a name. And Tamsin—" she lifted her head, and her expression was grim and tender at once, "—whoever they send to inspect us is going to want to talk to him. In person. You understand that. The voice they heard is the voice they'll trust. You may have to *be* him."

Tamsin nodded, because there was nothing else to nod. And beneath the dread, beneath the vertigo of the fiction she had authored and could now never disown, she felt something else stirring—something she recognized from her dreams, from the dead language, from the recordings of people long gone who had also stood on this ground and lied to powers greater than themselves in order that someone, someday, might live to tell the truth.

In her dream that night, for the first time in months, the language came to her unprompted. And the word it brought her, over and over, spoken in voices she could not see, was *senneth-va*.

The burden, carried alone.

Except she was beginning to suspect she would not carry it alone. Because when she woke, there was a message waiting in the array's log—a second reply, buried beneath the Concord's official traffic, encrypted on a frequency no Concord ship should have known existed.

Someone else had heard Alsen Vey too.
