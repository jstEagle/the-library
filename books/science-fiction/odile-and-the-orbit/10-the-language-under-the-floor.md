## 10. The Language Under the Floor

The Concord linguist arrived on Odile the way a fever arrives—announced by symptoms before anyone named it.

Tamsin noticed her first in the mess hall, three days after the vanguard ship *Cautious Meridian* had settled into its polite orbit. A tall woman with close-cropped silver hair and the particular stillness of someone who listened for a living. She sat apart from the other Concord officers, eating the colony's protein ration without complaint, and her eyes moved around the room the way Tamsin's own did: not scanning for threats but *reading*, cataloguing gesture and inflection, building a grammar of the room.

Her name was Vess Andrade. Sefa had mentioned it in passing, along with the rest of the verification team's roster, delivered in the flat voice she used when she was frightened and refusing to show it. "Andrade handles first-contact protocols. Xenolinguistics. She'll want to interview people."

"Then she'll interview me," Tamsin had said, and Sefa had looked at her with an expression that was half warning, half plea, and said nothing.

But Andrade didn't request an interview. Not through official channels, anyway. Instead, over four days, Tamsin kept catching fragments of her: standing too long at the memorial wall in Habitat Two where the names of the dead were etched into salvaged hull plating; lingering near the schoolroom where Idris's cohort recited multiplication tables in accented Standard; once, memorably, in the corridor outside the archive room itself, apparently studying the door seal as though it were a text she hadn't finished parsing.

Tamsin told herself it was professional curiosity. She told herself a great many things during those four days, most of them lies, and she knew they were lies because lying had recently become a discipline she practiced daily—the forged handshake still humming in the dead array like a swallowed stone—and a liar learns to hear the shape of her own falsehoods.

On the fifth day, the linguist came to the hydroponics bay.

* * *

Tamsin went there every morning now, out of ritual rather than necessity. It was where the nine seconds had happened, where water had hung in the air like a held breath, and she found she needed to stand in the place where the universe had first admitted it was watching them. The bay had recovered—new seedlings rising in their trays, the nutrient mist cycling on schedule—but there was a patch near the eastern wall where the vines grew differently, twisted toward some memory of weightlessness, and Tamsin liked to put her hand against those leaves while she ran her morning recordings of ambient colony sound. The hum of the pumps. The drip of condensation. The small, stubborn noises of things deciding to live.

She heard the bay door cycle and did not turn around. She'd learned that much from seven years of listening: what approaches you tells you more if you don't watch it arrive.

"You're recording," Vess Andrade said. Her voice was low, unhurried, carrying the faint lilt of somewhere in the old Meridian corridors—no, Tamsin corrected herself, somewhere in the *Concord*'s founding habitats, the ones built from the same bones. The two fleets shared ancestry the way estranged siblings share a face.

"I record everything," Tamsin said. "It's my job. Or it was."

"And now?"

"Now it's my habit." She turned. Andrade stood among the vine racks with her hands folded behind her back, uniform jacket unzipped—not quite regulation, a small deliberate informality—and studied Tamsin with eyes the color of wet slate. "Commander Andrade."

"Linguist Andrade. The rank is administrative." A thin smile. "I've read your file, Dr. Almeida. Or what survives of it. The Concord Academy revoked your credentials seven years ago, which means our database contains your entire publication history, annotated with disapproval." She tilted her head. "Your paper on prosodic drift in isolated speech communities is still taught, incidentally. They just don't say your name when they teach it."

"They never do."

"No. They don't." Andrade stepped closer, stopping at a respectful distance, and lowered her voice to something meant only for the space between them. "May I ask you something off the record? Not as fleet business. As—" she paused, searching, and Tamsin watched her search, watched a woman whose profession was precision fail to find the precise word, "—as one listener to another."

"You may ask. Whether I answer is a separate question."

"Fair." Andrade glanced up at the grow-lights, at the mist drifting down through their amber glow, and when she spoke again her voice had changed—stripped of its diplomatic polish, down to something rawer. "Three days ago I was running spectral analysis on Odile's background transmissions. Routine. Ambient electromagnetic survey, part of the verification protocol. And buried under the power grid harmonics, under the life-support chatter, there was a signal pattern I recognized." Her slate eyes came back to Tamsin and did not move away. "A tonal system with a pentatonic base register and stress-shift morphology. A language with no living native speakers, reconstructed from fragmentary survey data forty years ago, classified extinct by every academy in human space. Except that it isn't extinct, Dr. Almeida. Because you've been broadcasting it into this colony's internal network for years. Low power. Encrypted. Almost invisible." She let the silence sit. "Almost."

Tamsin felt the floor tilt—or imagined she did, which amounted to the same thing. Seven years of work. Seven years of whispering a dead tongue into the dark, careful enough that she'd believed no one would ever hear it, and here stood a stranger quoting its phonology back to her like a psalm.

"It's a private project," she said. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her.

"It's a resurrection," Andrade said. "You've been speaking it aloud. In your recordings—I pulled the audio layers apart—you don't just transcribe it. You *converse* in it. With yourself. With whoever taught you." Something flickered across her face then, something that was not analysis at all. "There are maybe six people in human space who can do that. I'm one of them."

* * *

They walked, afterward, because Andrade asked to see the archive and Tamsin—against every instinct Sefa had drilled into her, against the cold arithmetic of the lie they were all now living inside—said yes.

Not all of it. She showed Andrade the outer room only: the racks of storage cores labeled in her own handwriting, the playback station with its worn headphones, the wall where she'd pinned printouts of waveform analyses like a scholar's shrine. She kept the deep archive locked, the one holding the elders' stories, the children's voices, Idris's careful questions, the whole breathing testament of 214 lives. That was not for a Concord officer, however gentle her voice.

But Andrade barely looked at the hardware. She stood in the doorway and breathed, actually breathed, the way pilgrims breathe entering a temple, and said, "May I?"

"May you what?"

"Say something to you. In it. In the language." Her hands had come unfolded now, hanging loose at her sides, and Tamsin saw that they trembled slightly—a woman afraid, and not of Tamsin. "I've spoken it alone in my quarters for eleven years. To recordings. To ghosts. I have never once said a word of it to another living person who could answer."

The request landed in Tamsin's chest like a seed finding soil. She thought of her dreams, the ones that had thinned and faded until the language lived in them only as echo. She thought of exile, of the Academy tribunal reading her research findings aloud in flat bureaucratic Standard, stripping every syllable of its music, declaring her conclusions dangerous and her methods unsound and her presence on the core worlds untenable. She thought of how she had boarded the transport to Odile carrying nothing but recording equipment and a grief so large she'd mistaken it for relief.

"Say it," Tamsin whispered.

Andrade closed her eyes. And spoke.

The sounds filled the little room like water filling a dry basin. It was the greeting-formula, the dawn-phrase, the words that in the old survey fragments opened every recorded exchange—*the light returns to the field, and the field remembers*—and hearing them in a living voice, pitched and stressed correctly, the vowels rounded with the exact breath-shape the reconstruction tables could only approximate, Tamsin felt her knees nearly buckle. Seven years she had been the last speaker. Seven years of talking to the dark and calling it conversation.

She answered. The response-phrase, the one that completed the circuit: *and the rememberer is remembered.*

Andrade opened her eyes and they were bright with tears she made no move to hide. "Eleven years," she said again, unsteadily. "Do you know what it's like? To carry a whole way of thinking inside you and have nowhere to set it down?"

"Yes," Tamsin said. "That's why I'm here."

* * *

They talked for two hours. Tamsin kept the recorder running—she could no more stop recording than stop breathing—but she told herself it was different now, this wasn't intelligence, this was communion, and she believed it, mostly, the way you believe anything warm when you have been cold long enough.

Andrade had learned the language the hard way: from the fragmentary survey data too, but from the Concord side, through a mentor who had died before the classification orders came down, leaving her with a stolen corpus and a forbidden fluency. "They made it a controlled artifact," she said bitterly. "A dead language with a security classification. Do you understand that? We killed everyone who spoke it and then we locked the corpse in a vault." She laughed, a short broken sound. "I requested access to study it officially, twice. Denied both times. So I studied it illegally, at night, in a room with the sensors covered. Like a criminal. Like a lover."

"Why?" Tamsin asked. "Why carry something that heavy for no reward?"

Andrade was quiet for a long moment. Outside, the colony's day-cycle dimmed toward evening, and the amber light through the archive's single window went copper, then rust.

"Because of how it thinks," she said finally. "You know this better than anyone alive. The evidentiality system—the way every statement has to mark whether you witnessed it, inherited it, or inferred it. There's no way to speak it without being honest about where your knowledge comes from. You can't say anything in that language without saying *how you know*." Her slate eyes fixed on Tamsin. "I grew up in an institution that punished uncertainty and rewarded confidence. Learning a language where certainty itself must be marked, confessed, qualified—it saved something in me. Or it showed me what had already been lost and let me mourn it properly. Mourning needs a vocabulary. That language is the best vocabulary for mourning I've ever found."

Tamsin's throat ached. She thought about the forged handshake sleeping in the dead array. She thought about the vote, narrow and grim, and Sefa's face afterward, and the sentence she had spoken into the transmitter knowing it was false, constructed in a language designed to make lying structurally impossible.

"The colony speaks it too," she heard herself say. "Badly. Partially. The elders remember fragments from their grandparents. The children learn what I can teach." She gestured at the wall of cores. "That's what the archive is. Not just my work. Theirs. Every voice on this rock that agreed to be remembered."

"Two hundred and fourteen," Andrade said softly. "I know the number. Everyone on that ship knows the number. It's in the briefing files." She turned back to Tamsin, and here it came, the thing beneath the thing, the current under the surface Tamsin had been feeling all afternoon. "Dr. Almeida. May I tell you what I actually think is happening here? Not the verification protocol. Not the anchor question. What I think."

"You may."

"I think this colony is going to be a casualty of a war that hasn't started yet. I think both fleets have decided what Odile means before anyone bothered to ask what Odile *is*. And I think—" her voice dropped, and she stepped closer, close enough that Tamsin could see the fine lines at the corners of her eyes, exhaustion and conviction braided together, "—I think that whatever happens in the next weeks, the archive in this room is worth more than any jump-anchor ever built. More than the corridor. More than the fleets themselves, possibly, because fleets end and languages outlive them if someone carries them." She exhaled. "I am telling you this in a room that is probably monitored. I am aware of that. I am telling you anyway."

Tamsin searched her face for the trap. She wanted, badly, not to find one. That wanting was itself the danger, and she knew it—knew it the way Sefa knew load tolerances, instantly and viscerally. A lonely woman hearing her own loneliness echoed back in perfect pitch. A mirror was exactly the shape a threat would choose.

"Why are you telling me?" Tamsin asked quietly. "What do you want, Linguist Andrade?"

"To help you finish it," Andrade said simply. "The archive. Whatever it's for—transmission, preservation, testimony, I don't need to know. Let me help. My corpus has material yours doesn't. Survey fragments the Concord confiscated, recordings from the last speakers before the end, dialect variants you've only hypothesized. I could—" she stopped, and her composure cracked clean through, and underneath it Tamsin saw not an officer or an asset or a threat but a person starving. "I could spend the rest of my life on this and consider it the only worthwhile thing I ever did."

The room hummed around them. Somewhere below, Sefa was rerouting power through the dead anchor's frame, making a corpse read warm, feeding a lie to an armada. Somewhere above, the *Cautious Meridian* held its polite orbit, full of people who would burn this world if ordered. And here, between those two pressures, stood a woman offering treason wrapped in scholarship, or scholarship wrapped in treason, and Tamsin could not yet tell which direction the wrapping went.

"I can't give you an answer tonight," Tamsin said.

"I know." Andrade straightened, reassembling her diplomatic calm piece by piece, but her eyes stayed open, stayed human. "I'll be aboard ship tomorrow, running the acoustic surveys. But I'll come back. I'd like to meet the elders. The children, if they're willing." At the door she paused, hand on the seal, and said one more phrase in the old tongue—softly, almost shyly. The farewell-formula. *The road forgets the walker, but the walker keeps the road.*

Then she was gone, and Tamsin stood alone in the amber light with the recorder still turning, capturing the silence Andrade left behind, and understood that something had entered the colony's story that she could neither translate nor control.

She played the recording back twice that night. On the second pass, she caught it: beneath Andrade's farewell, faint but unmistakable, a second voice on the channel—an open comm line, still live, transmitting from the archive room to somewhere off-world. Someone had been listening to all of it.

The signature code belonged to the *Cautious Meridian*'s command deck.

Tamsin sat very still in the dark, holding the evidence of her own carelessness like a hot coal, and began to calculate how much of the truth she had just handed to the enemy—and what, if anything, she could now do about it.
