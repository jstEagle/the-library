## 12. Handshake, Interrupted

Sefa Okoye had always believed she could smell a lie before it finished forming—the way you could smell ozone before a storm, or coolant before a breach. It was a survival trait in engineering: the machine that hummed too smoothly, the gauge that read too perfectly, the junior tech who reported no anomalies on a day when everything had gone wrong. Lies had a texture. She'd built her life on trusting that texture.

Which was why, standing in the comms room of Odile's central habitat with her palms flat against the cold console and her heart hammering somewhere behind her teeth, she recognized immediately that the voice coming out of the speakers was lying to her.

"We are the Meridian Compact survey vessel *Cautious Advance*," the voice said. It was smooth, unhurried, faintly amused—the voice of someone who had never once worried about power rations or water reclamation. "We have been observing your system for some time. We congratulate you on your continued survival."

*Observing for some time.* The words landed like a hand closing around the back of Sefa's neck. She thought of Idris's report three days ago—a glint in the belt, a shape that didn't tumble right, parked where nothing should be parked. She had filed it as debris, as wishful thinking, as a boy with good eyes and an overactive imagination. She owed him an apology she would never have time to give.

Behind her, Tamsin stood very still by the door, her hands folded, her face arranged into the particular blankness she wore when she was translating in her head—except there was nothing to translate here. The Compact spoke Trade Standard, crisp and clean. There was only what was being said, and what it meant, and the widening gulf between them.

"You have not responded to our earlier transmissions," the voice continued. "We understand. Isolation breeds caution. But circumstances have changed. Nine days ago, your system produced a gravitational signature consistent with an active jump-anchor handshake. Our instruments do not lie, colonist. Yours may be a dying settlement, but you are sitting on the single most valuable object in this sector—and the Concord fleet is already inbound to take it from you."

Sefa let the silence stretch. In her experience, silence did more work than speech. People rushed to fill it; they revealed themselves; they negotiated against their own interests just to escape the weight of the quiet. She watched the waveform of the transmission ripple across her display, steady as a metronome, and she counted her own heartbeat against it. Four years of power. Maybe five. Seventy-two hours until the Concord vanguard completed its verification protocols. And now this—a second knife, held at a different angle.

"Colonist," the voice prompted, when her silence had gone on long enough to become its own statement. "Do you copy?"

"I copy," Sefa said. Her voice came out level, which surprised her. She had expected it to crack. "This is Sefa Okoye, chief engineer of the Odile settlement. I'll be honest with you, *Cautious Advance*, because honesty saves time, and time is the one resource we're all short on." She paused, choosing each word the way she'd choose a fuse rating—deliberately, with an eye toward what would blow if she got it wrong. "You say you've been watching us. Then you already know we're two hundred and fourteen people on a failing colony with no navy, no weapons worth naming, and no capacity to give anyone anything. Whatever you think is down here, we can't stop you from taking it. So the question isn't whether you can take the anchor. The question is why you're talking to us at all."

A pause on the other end. Not long—but Sefa heard it, the small recalibration, the machine adjusting its assumptions about who it was talking to. They had expected a frightened functionary. They had gotten an engineer, which meant they had gotten someone who thought in systems and failure modes.

"A reasonable question," the voice said, and now there was something new under the smoothness—respect, maybe, or the performance of it. "We are talking to you because the anchor cannot simply be taken. Jump-anchors are attuned infrastructure. They bind to the minds that built them, through protocols your people will know if your ancestors were the ones who laid this frame. If the anchor responds to Concord command codes, the Compact loses this corridor for a generation. If it responds to ours, the Concord armada arriving in—" a pause, a soft chime of data being consulted, "—sixty-one hours arrives to find the door locked. You, colonist, are holding the key. We would prefer you turn it willingly."

*And if we don't?* Sefa did not ask it aloud. She let the question hang in her own skull instead, cold and precise. Because the answer was already written into the message, folded into its courtesy the way a blade folds into a coat. *Be treated as Concord assets.* That phrase hadn't been spoken yet, but she could feel it waiting behind the next sentence like water behind a cracked bulkhead.

She looked back at Tamsin. Seven years of estrangement and shared quarters and careful silences, and still Sefa could read her across a room faster than any instrument panel. Tamsin's eyes said: *They don't know about the Concord ship yet. Or they do, and they're testing whether we'll tell them.* Either way, the same trap. Two audiences, one lie, and every word spoken to one of them was a word the other might someday hear repeated back.

"I need to consult my people," Sefa said.

"You have thirty minutes," the voice said pleasantly. "Then we begin treating this settlement as hostile territory under Concord occupation. I trust I don't need to describe what that means for a civilian population."

The channel closed. The waveform flattened into a dead line, and the comms room was suddenly so quiet Sefa could hear the hydroponics pumps cycling four decks down, could hear the tick of the old thermal regulators, could hear Tamsin exhale.

"They were never watching the anchor," Tamsin said quietly. "They were watching the Concord ship arrive. We were never the prize. We were the lock, and both of them want the same key."

*

They gathered in the engineering bay, because it was the only room on Odile with a door that locked and a table big enough for the council—which was to say, eleven people: Sefa, Tamsin, the elder Nkiru Okafor with her hands folded like a woman in prayer, the hydroponics chief Devi, old Marek whose lungs rattled when he breathed too fast, and six others whose faces Sefa knew better than her own reflection. Idris was there too, because nobody had thought to exclude him and nobody had the heart to send him away. He sat at the far end with his slate hugged to his chest, fourteen years old and listening the way he listened to everything—completely, hungrily, as though the world were a language he intended to master before it finished dying.

"Thirty minutes," Sefa told them. "That's what we have. So I'm going to skip the part where we panic, because panicking takes time and we don't have any."

Devi laughed—one sharp, involuntary bark—and then pressed her hand over her mouth as if the sound had escaped without permission.

"The Compact wants the anchor," Sefa continued. "They believe it works. They believe we can hand it over. The Concord believes the same thing and is sixty-one hours out. We cannot give either of them a working anchor, because there isn't one—we all saw the frame under the landing site, we all know it's a dead thing, a half-built ruin the first generation abandoned before most of us were born. And we especially cannot give anyone a working anchor, because Tamsin and I decoded the warning in that frame last week." She let that land. Faces went gray around the table. "Activating it tears the corridor apart. Every ship in-system. Both fleets. Us. Everyone."

"So we tell them that," Marek rasped. "We tell them the truth. It's a tomb. It kills whoever wakes it."

"And they believe us?" Sefa shook her head. "Marek. Two armadas have spent nine days convincing themselves this rock is the most valuable object in the sector. You think they take the word of two hundred starving colonists over their own sensor logs? The moment we say 'it doesn't work,' they stop negotiating and start deciding—who gets to dig through the wreckage, who gets to interrogate us, who gets to make sure the *other* side doesn't get whatever's left. The truth doesn't save us. The truth just changes the shape of the war."

Nkiru Okafor spoke for the first time, her voice dry as seed husks. "Then you mean to keep lying."

It wasn't a question, but Sefa treated it like one, because it deserved an answer. "I mean to keep us alive. Yes. I'll lie."

"To two fleets." The old woman's eyes were pale and unblinking. "With words. You, who once tore a strip off Devi's daughter for fudging a maintenance log."

"Fudging a maintenance log gets people killed slowly," Sefa said. "So does telling the truth at the wrong moment. I've spent thirty years keeping this colony breathing by knowing which pipes can lie about their pressure readings and which ones can't. This is a pipe. It's lying right now, and it's keeping us alive, and I intend to keep it lying until we can get two hundred and fourteen people somewhere those ships can't reach."

Silence around the table. Somewhere above them, the habitat's spine creaked as the temperature differential shifted between day-cycle and night-cycle—a sound so familiar Sefa usually didn't hear it, but tonight it sounded like a countdown.

Idris raised his hand. Actually raised it, like a child in a classroom, and several adults almost smiled despite everything.

"What if you tell them the anchor only answers to us?" he said.

Every head turned. The boy's ears went red, but he kept going, the way he did when he'd found a thread and couldn't leave it alone. "You said jump-anchors attune. That's real—that's in the old manuals, the first-generation stuff. What if you tell the Compact that yes, the anchor works, but it's bound to the colonists. To specific people. That it can't be handed over, only... lent. Operated. By us. That way you're not refusing them—you're offering them something better than surrender. A working anchor they can use, as long as we stay alive and cooperative. Nobody bombs the only people who can turn the key."

Sefa stared at him. Around the table, she watched the idea move through the others like current through a wire—Devi's eyebrows lifting, Marek's rattling breath catching, Nkiru's folded hands going still.

It was beautiful. It was terrible. It was exactly the kind of lie that grows teeth, because it didn't just buy time—it created obligations, expectations, a whole architecture of fiction that would have to bear weight it was never built for. A lie that made them indispensable was also a lie that made them hostages. But hostages lived. Wreckage didn't.

"That's not how attunement actually works," Tamsin said softly. "Not fully. The binding protocols were partial. Unfinished. The builders never completed—"

"Good," Sefa said, and heard her own voice come out harder than she'd intended. "Then it's a lie with true bones in it. Those hold up best. Vague enough to survive scrutiny, grounded enough to sound like engineering." She looked at Tamsin, and something passed between them that wasn't quite forgiveness and wasn't quite anything else—just the old fluency, two people who had spent years learning each other's silences and could still conduct an entire argument inside one glance. *You hate this,* the glance said. *I know. Do it anyway.*

Tamsin closed her eyes for half a second. When she opened them, she nodded.

"Twenty-two minutes left," Sefa said, and stood. "Here's what happens. I go back on that channel and I give them the attunement story. Idris—you're with me. If they ask questions I can't answer, you cough, and I stall. Tamsin—" she hesitated, then said it plainly, because plainness was all she had left, "—start drafting what a genuine attunement protocol would look like on paper. Documentation. If they demand proof, proof has to exist. Make it beautiful. Make it boring. Boring things don't get audited."

"And if they call the bluff?" Devi asked quietly. "If they demand a demonstration?"

Sefa felt the floor tilt under her—not literally, though after the nine seconds of falling she no longer entirely trusted her inner ear—just the slow vertigo of a plan reaching its own edge. "Then we demonstrate," she said. "And I will spend every hour between now and then figuring out how to fake a miracle with a dead anchor, a failing reactor, and a lot of cable."

*

The channel reopened with a punctuality that felt obscene. Sefa had taken the ninety seconds remaining to wash her face, straighten her collar, and stand the way she imagined a person stood when they possessed a functioning jump-anchor and infinite leverage: tired but unbowed, a custodian rather than a supplicant.

"This is Okoye," she said. "I've consulted with the settlement council. Here is our position, and I'll say it once, so listen carefully."

She paused. Let the silence do its work. Somewhere in the dark beyond Odile's atmosphere, she imagined the Compact vessel hanging in the belt's shadow, its crew leaning toward their speakers, and she made herself picture them not as predators but as engineers—people who respected systems, who understood that some machines could not simply be seized.

"The anchor is real," she said. "It functions. But it is not a piece of equipment you can board this colony and carry away. It is attuned—to us. To the descendants of the original build crews, through genetic and neural keys established during construction. The first generation bound the anchor's control layer to the colony's founding lines deliberately, precisely so that no fleet, no government, no passing war could ever switch it on without us. They'd seen what happened elsewhere. They built the lock into the blood."

*Blood,* she thought, and nearly flinched at her own audacity. There was no blood-key. There was barely a control layer. There was a rusted frame under forty meters of regolith and a warning carved into its bones that meant activation meant death for everyone. But the words came out smooth, and she heard in them the cadence of every maintenance report she'd ever delivered—confident, technical, immune to doubt.

"So," she continued, "you have two options. Option one: you treat us as Concord assets, you come down here, you take the settlement apart deck by deck looking for a control interface that does not exist outside living colonists, and you spend the next decade trying to reverse-engineer attunement while the Concord fleet sits in your corridor. Option two: you recognize that the anchor's operators are sitting right here, alive, cooperative, and willing to negotiate terms of use. We open the corridor for the Compact. We maintain the anchor. In exchange, this colony survives—with supply guarantees, with transit rights, with our people left intact. You don't get a trophy. You get a partnership."

The silence on the other end lasted eleven seconds. Sefa counted every one of them, her hand resting on the console, feeling through her palm the faint tremor of Odile's aging reactor—the pulse of the only world she had ever loved well enough to lie for.

"You understand," the voice said at last, and the amusement had drained out of it entirely, leaving something colder and more careful underneath, "that claims of attunement are historically the single most common fraud in anchor disputes. We will require verification. A demonstration. Your operators will activate the anchor's handshake sequence under our observation, within—" another soft chime, "—forty hours. If the demonstration fails, or if we determine the attunement claim is fabricated, the settlement forfeits protected status. Completely."

"Forty hours," Sefa repeated.

"Forty hours," the voice agreed. "And colonist—one more thing. Our long-range watch reports a Concord vessel entering your outer system. We assume you knew. We assume that is why you suddenly find religion about cooperation. Be advised: if the Concord reaches your anchor before we verify your claim, none of your cleverness will matter. The Compact does not forgive divided loyalties."

The channel closed.

Sefa stood alone in the comms room—I dris had slipped out during the exchange, sent to fetch water she didn't need, because she hadn't wanted him to hear the threat spelled out so plainly—and she let herself lean forward until her forehead touched the cool metal of the console. Forty hours to fake a miracle. Sixty-one minus the Concord's approach, minus the Compact's patience, minus the shrinking margin in which two lies told to two audiences could coexist without touching. The arithmetic was collapsing. She could feel it the way she felt a load-bearing strut beginning to sing under stress—not broken yet, but singing, singing, singing.

She pushed off the console and went to find Tamsin, because the lie had just grown teeth, and teeth needed feeding, and there was no one else on this dying world who could help her build a miracle out of documentation, dead code, and nerve.

Behind her, on the dead channel, the silence of the belt kept its own counsel—and somewhere in it, unseen, the Compact's watchers marked the clock.
