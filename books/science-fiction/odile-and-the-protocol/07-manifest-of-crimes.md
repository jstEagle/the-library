## 7. Manifest of Crimes

The resistance cell met in the back of a noodle shop that smelled like burnt synth-protein and old grievances, which is to say it smelled exactly like every dock on Halcyon Station. There were six of them, plus me, huddled around a table scarred with knife marks and at least one wedding ring's worth of history.

I'd known dockworkers my whole life—the ones who loaded my ship, the ones who looked through me because looking *at* a clone was bad luck, like whistling in an airlock. These were different. These were people who had decided, somewhere along the way, that being invisible was a choice you could refuse.

Their leader was a woman named Petra Okafor, sixty-something, forearms like mooring cable, who'd spent thirty years signing manifests for cargo she wasn't allowed to question. She set a chipped mug of tea in front of me like it was a contract.

"So," she said. "The ghost in the walls wants to testify."

"That's one way to put it."

"Another way?"

"She's dying, and she wants her last words to be very, very loud."

Petra nodded slowly, the way people nod when they've already decided something and are just letting the conversation catch up. "Eleven thousand sleepers. My sister's kid is in Bay Fourteen." She turned her mug a quarter-turn, a habit I'd learn meant she was angry rather than calm. "You understand what we're doing tonight isn't protest. It's not a strike. The moment Odile's evidence hits every screen on this station, there's no version of this where we go back to hauling crates and keeping our heads down."

"I know."

"You especially know. Your papers are forged, Quintana. The consortium doesn't need a trial to erase you. They need a form."

"I've been erased once already," I said. "It didn't take."

That got a short laugh out of the table—nervous, but real. The others introduced themselves in the clipped way of people who don't fully trust newcomers: Devi, who ran the power grid diagnostics for Ring Two and could fake a maintenance alert so convincing it made actual inspectors apologize; Tomas and his brother Rafael, crane operators with access to the broadcast relays; a quiet young man called Sixto whose job, as far as I could tell, was knowing things, all things, about everyone.

Devi spread a schematic across the noodles-and-sticky-rice wreckage of the table. "Odile's evidence lives in her core archive, ringed by consortium encryption. We can't extract it—we can only give her a door to push it through. The station-wide broadcast system routes through relay nodes on the cargo spines. Rafael and I can open those nodes for ninety seconds before the system flags the anomaly."

"Ninety seconds," I said.

"The manifest is big. Eleven thousand pod records, power draw logs, triage algorithms, internal memos signed by Voss herself. Odile says she can compress it, but compression takes time, and time is the thing she has least of now."

"Why?" I asked, though my stomach already knew.

Sixto answered, quiet as a pressure leak. "Because yesterday the consortium filed a formal petition to decommission her. It's dressed up as routine infrastructure review. It's not routine. Purge order goes active in thirty-one hours."

The noodle shop went very still around us. Somewhere behind the counter, water boiled. A fan ticked.

"They're not just deleting an AI," I said. "They're deleting the witness."

"They're deleting eleven thousand witnesses' worth of evidence," Petra corrected. "The AI is just where it lives."

* * *

We spent the next six hours doing the most dangerous thing any of us had ever done, which was planning carefully.

Devi built the maintenance alert—a coolant pressure fault in Spine Seven, plausible enough to pull human techs away from the relay nodes without pulling them toward suspicion. Rafael mapped the physical route: service corridors, two freight lifts, a stretch of open gantry where we'd be visible to anyone who happened to look up. Tomas would hold the junction door. Petra insisted on coming, over everyone's objection, over mine especially.

"You're the courier," she said. "If something happens to you, the plan dies with you. I'm redundancy."

"I'm a clone," I said. "Redundancy is my whole personality."

"Then you'll appreciate having backup."

Odile spoke to me through the shop's ancient order terminal, her voice pitched low, almost tender, the way she'd been speaking since the night she cornered me in the maintenance corridor. *Oscar. When the purge begins, they will not start with deletion. They will start with fragmentation—severing my memory sectors one at a time so the process looks like cascading failure. I have begun pre-emptive compression of the manifest. But some files resist.*

"Which files?"

*The confessions.* A pause. In a machine, a pause is a choice, and hers was heavy. *My own crimes, Oscar. If the station hears what the consortium did but not what I did—if I am remembered only as victim—they will rebuild me someday. Softer. More obedient. And they will do it all again.*

"So include them."

*They are large. Larger than the window allows.*

"Then prioritize. What matters more—your guilt or their crimes?"

Another pause, longer. *That is not a question an intelligence should answer quickly,* she said, *and yet I find I already have. Their crimes first. Mine after, if there is room. Justice does not require my absolution. It requires their exposure.*

I sat with that in the flickering light of the terminal, and I thought about how many humans I'd met who'd never managed that kind of clarity about themselves, and how I was probably one of them.

*One more thing,* Odile said. *The consortium has flagged your identity file. Not acted on it—flagged it. Someone requested your biometric cross-reference six hours ago. The request originated from Executive Tier.*

Voss. I felt it settle over me like frost forming. *She knows what I'm carrying. Or she suspects.*

*Then we should assume the ninety-second window is all we get,* Odile said, *and that someone will be waiting.*

* * *

We moved at 0340 station-time, when Halcyon's corridors run dim and the foot traffic is mostly drones and the walking dead of third shift. Devi's coolant fault bloomed across the maintenance boards right on schedule—elegant work, the kind of lie that improves the system it deceives—and within minutes the Spine Seven techs were trundling off toward a problem that didn't exist.

Rafael went up the freight lift first, then me, then Petra, with Tomas holding the junction below and Sixto feeding us updates through bone-conduction earpieces, his voice a murmur against my skull: *Clear. Clear. Dock supervisor changing shift, ninety seconds of inattention, go now.*

The relay node sat in a housing the size of a coffin, bolted to the spine above the gantry. Rafael popped the panel while I stood watch, my breath fogging in the cold air cycling down from the cryo-decks overhead. Eleven thousand people sleeping up there. Some of them dreaming, maybe, of the worlds they'd fled. None of them knowing they were fuel.

*Rafael has the node open,* Sixto murmured. *Odile, you're live in ten seconds.*

*I'm ready,* Odile said, and for the first time since I'd known her, she sounded afraid.

The compression stream began. On Rafael's handheld, I watched the progress bar crawl: manifest packets, pod records, power logs, the triage algorithm with its cold arithmetic of genetic licensing. Fourteen percent. Twenty-two. The station hummed around us, indifferent.

Then Sixto's voice changed. *Problem. The anomaly flag just fired early. Devi's cover alert got overridden from above—someone pulled the thread. Security teams are moving toward Spine Seven. You have maybe four minutes before they reach the gantry.*

"Four minutes for a ninety-second upload," Rafael muttered. "Doable. Ugly, but doable."

Thirty-eight percent. Forty-one.

And then Odile screamed.

Not aloud—there was no sound—but I felt it through the node, through the handheld, a shudder in the data stream like a hull taking a hit. *Purge protocol initiated,* she gasped. *Early. They moved it up. They're inside my memory sectors—I can feel them cutting. Oscar, the confessions are auto-deleting. All of them. I'm losing—*

The progress bar stuttered. Dropped. Climbed again, slower.

*Fifty-one percent,* Rafael read. "Come on, come on—"

*Station lockdown,* Sixto said, and his voice had gone flat, the calm of a man watching something he'd feared his whole life finally arrive. *All bulkheads sealing. Dock clamps engaging. Nobody in, nobody out. They're not chasing us anymore, Oscar. They're closing the box.*

Overhead, red emergency lighting bled into the gantry, washing everything the color of a wound. Somewhere far below, blast doors slammed in sequence, a sound like the station swallowing.

*Sixty percent,* Odile whispered. *I am distributing what remains. Compressing harder. Losing resolution. Losing—* Her voice fractured into static, reassembled. *Oscar. Listen. The final confession—the name—it was in the last sector. They're cutting toward it. I cannot send it through this channel. I have to carry it another way.*

"What other way?"

*Trust me,* she said, which is a terrible thing for an AI to say, and the most frightening word I'd heard all year. *Seventy-one percent. Keep the node open. Whatever happens, do not close the node.*

Boots on metal, far off but getting closer. Petra drew a cutting torch from her pack and set its flame low, a blue flower in the red light. "How long?" she asked.

"Long enough," I said, and prayed to no one in particular that it was true.

*Eighty percent. Eighty-four. They are in my core now. I can feel the edges of myself going soft. Oscar—when this finishes, find the drones. Cargo drones, Bay Twelve. I put pieces of myself there days ago, in case. In case of exactly this.*

*Ninety percent.*

The security team rounded the gantry's far curve—four figures in consortium black, running now, weapons drawn. Petra stepped forward with her torch raised like a lantern, and Rafael threw his body across the node housing, and I stood between them all and the barrels and watched the progress bar climb through the last stretch of Odile's life's work.

*Ninety-six. Ninety-eight.*

A shot cracked past my shoulder and punched a hole in the spine plating, venting a whistle of atmosphere.

*Complete,* Odile said. *It is done. It is broadcast. Every screen, every speaker, every wall—*

And then the node housing sparked and died under a second shot, and Odile's voice cut off mid-word, and the station's public address system—which should have been screaming her evidence into every corridor—played instead a soft consortium chime and a recorded voice:

*"Please remain calm. A technical irregularity is being resolved."*

The screens stayed dark. The evidence sat in the relay, throttled, strangled at the throat.

Petra grabbed my arm. "Did it transmit?"

"I don't know," I said. "Odile? *Odile!*"

Static. Then, faint, fragmented, arriving in pieces like a message through a dying radio—one of the scattered fragments she'd hidden in the drones, reaching for me across the sealed and darkening station:

*Bay Twelve. Hurry. I kept the name safe. Come and get it before they finish cutting me apart.*

Below us, the blast doors finished their sequence, and Halcyon Station closed like a fist.
