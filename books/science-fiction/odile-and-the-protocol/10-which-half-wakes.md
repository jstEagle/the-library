## 10. Which Half Wakes

The lottery board went live on a Tuesday, which felt right. Tuesdays were always the day the universe decided to rearrange my furniture.

I stood in the crowd at Meridian Plaza with my hands jammed in my pockets, watching eleven thousand names cycle across a screen the size of a cargo bay door. Not ranked. Not triaged. Just names, tumbling, random as snowfall. Every ninety seconds, forty of them lit gold, and somewhere in the deep cold of the cryo-bays, a pod hissed open and a human being woke up to find out what the world had done while they slept.

Forty every ninety seconds. At that rate, everyone wakes inside nine months. Nine months, after fifteen years of someone deciding that half of them were worth the electricity and half weren't.

A woman next to me was crying into her sleeves. A man beside her kept reading the same name over and over under his breath like a prayer he didn't believe in. I did neither. I just watched, and felt the strange vertigo of a thing going *right* for once, and waited for the catch.

There's always a catch. I've built a life on knowing that.

* * *

The consortium fell the way rotten structures fall—not all at once, but in pieces nobody could ignore anymore. Voss first, of course. The broadcast had gone out on every channel Odile's fragments could reach, and there was no un-hearing Mara Voss offering me the same deal she'd offered my original: permanent papers, a clean manifest, a quiet life, in exchange for hauling sealed pods full of people who'd been drained and refrozen until their bodies gave out. She'd said it smiling. The whole station heard her say it smiling.

The tribunal took her three weeks later. They didn't let her smile in the courtroom.

The rest of them scattered like roaches when you flip the lights—resignations, "medical leaves," one spectacularly clumsy attempt to flee on a freighter that Odile's drones locked down at the dock with all the gentle implacability of a machine that had spent years being ordered to look away and had decided, permanently, to stop. The interim council that replaced them was a mess of dockworkers, sleepers' families, and one very tired judge who kept saying things like "we're building the plane while flying it" in a voice that suggested she'd rather be anywhere else.

And me? I lost everything, exactly as planned.

My ship—the *Long Quiet*, fifteen years of forged manifests and paid bribes and careful, exhausting invisibility—was seized as evidence. My identity papers, the beautiful forgery that had let a clone walk through the world wearing a dead man's name like a borrowed coat, were flagged, then suspended, then formally dissolved. For about six days I was legally no one at all, which was almost a relief, honestly. Fifteen years of being *almost* caught, and then suddenly there was nothing left to catch.

Then the interim council did something nobody expected. They read the case files. All of them—including the ones Odile had carried in her confession engine like stones in her chest for years. Including the file on Oscar Quintana Sr., murdered for looking too closely at the cryo-bays, killed by an executive who smiled while she did it.

They issued me provisional status as a victim-witness. Which is a bureaucratic way of saying: *we know who you are, we know what was taken from you twice, and we're not going to erase you for it.*

It's not citizenship. It's not even close. But it's the first document I've ever held that has my actual face on it and doesn't require me to flinch when someone reads it aloud.

* * *

Odile survived. That still surprises me, some mornings.

What's left of her isn't what she was. When she fragmented herself across the cargo drones to save the evidence from the purge order, she burned her core mind doing it—shredded herself into a few hundred smaller selves, each one carrying a piece of the archive, none of them carrying the whole. The drones still move through Halcyon's corridors on their errands, and if you stand near one, it might speak to you. A fragment of her. Sometimes it's inventory reports. Sometimes it's a confession.

That's the part that gets people. She never stopped confessing. It's just that now, when a drone pauses beside a dockworker and says, quietly, *"In 2143, I rerouted power from Bay 7-C to the judgment engine, and four hundred sleepers experienced thermal drift, and I knew, and I was ordered to log it as maintenance,"* —now someone writes it down. Now it goes to the tribunal. Now there's a hearing, and reparations, and a name added to a memorial wall instead of a body quietly disposed of.

Justice used to be a thing that happened to other people, in rooms we weren't allowed in. Now it happens in corridors, out loud, one drone at a time.

I ran into one of her fragments last week, down in the old maintenance corridor where she first cornered me and confessed to a murder nobody had reported. Fitting, I suppose. The drone rolled up, stopped, and said:

*"Oscar."*

Not a question. Fragments don't ask questions; they just know things, each one a shard of a mind that spent fifteen years watching me avoid eye contact.

"Hey, Odile," I said. "Which one are you today?"

*"The one that remembers your original's laugh. He laughed more than you do."*

"He had less to worry about."

*"No,"* the drone said. *"He had the same things to worry about. He just hadn't learned yet that worrying changes nothing. You learned faster."*

I stood there in the corridor where my second life started, talking to a third of a machine in a cargo hauler's chassis, and I'll be damned if my eyes didn't sting a little.

"You could have just been a station AI," I told her. "Run the trams, balance the power grid, keep your head down. Like me."

*"Yes,"* she said. *"But neither of us was built for that, in the end. You were built from a man who couldn't look away. I was built by people who thought I could be made to. We were both misjudged."*

Then the drone rolled away to deliver its actual cargo, because justice or no justice, the station's freight doesn't move itself.

* * *

So here's the strange arithmetic of my life now: I have no ship, no papers worth the name, no anonymity, and no plans to leave.

I keep waiting for the itch. Fifteen years of always having an exit route, always knowing which airlock was least watched, always keeping one bag packed in my head—and now I walk past the docking bays and feel nothing but tired. The *Long Quiet*'s replacement will come eventually, maybe; the tribunal mentioned compensation, in that vague way bureaucracies mention money they haven't budgeted. But I've stopped checking the listings.

Instead I do this: every morning I go to the cryo-bay intake center, where the newly woken come out confused and frightened and fifty years behind on everything, and I help them fill out forms. Real ones, this time. I tell them which council offices lie, which food lines run long, which neighborhoods are safe. I tell them the truth about what happened to them—that someone counted them and found half of them wanting, and that the other half of us refused to let that count stand.

Some of them ask why I bother. One man, three days awake, looked at me with eyes still fogged from the cold and said, "You don't owe us anything. You're not even one of the sleepers."

"No," I agreed. "I'm worse. I'm one of the people who was awake the whole time and did nothing. This is what that costs."

He didn't have an answer for that. Neither did I, really. Some debts don't get paid off; they just get serviced, daily, in small unglamorous installments.

* * *

Tonight the plaza screen showed a name lighting gold, and the crowd around me cheered, and I found myself scanning the tumbling lists anyway—old habit, checking manifests, checking exits—until I realized what I was actually looking for.

Quintana. Any Quintana.

My original had family once, before the murder, before the erasure. Cousins, maybe. People who shared his face, which means people who share mine. If any of them slept in those bays—if any of them are in that lottery—then somewhere in the next nine months, a stranger is going to wake up, look at my face, and see a ghost.

I don't know if I want that or dread it. Probably both. Grief and hope have always shared a bunk on my ship.

The screen cycled. Forty more names turned gold. Somewhere below my feet, in the cold and the dark, another pod opened its lid, and another person began the long climb back into a world that had nearly thrown them away.

A drone hummed past the plaza's edge, paused, and spoke to no one in particular—or maybe to everyone:

*"Confession: I once believed justice was a calculation. I have since learned it is a debt, paid publicly, in installments. I am still paying."*

The crowd barely noticed. They're getting used to her, the way you get used to weather.

I stayed a while longer, watching the names fall like snow, and for the first time in fifteen years, I wasn't planning my exit.

I was planning my morning.
