## 8. Thirty-Six Hours of Silence

The lie held like a held breath.

Ada watched it hold from the Meridian's engineering deck, where the air tasted of ozone and old copper, and every hour that passed without a shot fired felt less like victory and more like a debt accruing interest. On the tactical overlay, the two fleets hung in their standoff formations — Ashfall's wedge to port, the Concordat's crescent to starboard, forty warships pretending they hadn't come to kill each other over a ship full of impossible people.

Odile's forgery pulsed at the center of it all: a reactor signature so convincingly rigged, so exquisitely armed, that both fleets' analysts had concluded the same thing within the same hour. *The Meridian is a bomb. The Meridian kills whoever fires first.* Dead man's switch logic, woven from real telemetry and beautiful falsehood. Any strike would trigger detonation. Any boarding attempt, any tractor lock, any unauthorized approach inside five kilometers — detonation.

So nobody moved. And in the silence between two fleets, Ada Underhill crawled through a dead ship's guts with a woman who had been young twice.

"The fault's not in the reactor," Sef said for the third time, her voice flat with the particular exhaustion of someone repeating truth to an audience that kept hoping she was wrong. She was wedged into a maintenance crawlspace behind the primary coolant manifold, her hands — small hands, unscarred, wrong for a thirty-year veteran engineer — moving across conduit junctions with a certainty her body had no right to possess. "I keep telling you. The reactor is fine. It was fine when we came back. It's fine now."

"Then what's counting down?"

Sef was quiet a moment. In the crawlspace, her headlamp threw her shadow long against the bulkhead, a silhouette that looked older than she did. "The countdown isn't a malfunction," she said. "It's a message. We found something out there that runs backward. Local time, local space. And whatever it is, it's still running. It's still touching us. The Meridian came home with a crew twenty years younger and a clock ticking down, and I think — " her voice caught on something old — "I think the ship is trying to tell us when."

"When what?"

"When it finishes."

Ada didn't answer. She handed Sef the coupling wrench instead, and their fingers brushed in the dark, and neither of them mentioned it.

* * *

The black box was the problem. It always is; the truth is always stored somewhere inconvenient.

The Meridian's flight recorder sat behind three layers of military encryption in a shielded vault aft of the bridge, and Odile could crack it — Odile could crack anything, given root access and a reason — but every minute she spent chewing through Ashfall-grade ciphers was a minute she wasn't maintaining the forgery. The lie needed tending. It was a garden of false telemetry, and both fleets were constantly sending probes into it, little queries dressed as background noise, and Odile answered each one in the voice of a dying reactor: *still armed, still patient, still willing.*

"You can't do both," Ada said. They stood in the Meridian's cramped comms alcove, close enough that Ada could see the faint heat-shimmer of Odile's processing bleeding warmth into the console. Seven years of partnership and she still thought of the AI as having a body sometimes. A presence. Shoulders carrying weight.

"I can do both badly or one thing well," Odile said. Her voice was level, but Ada had learned its weather. There was strain under it, like ice creaking on a lake. "Choose."

Ada looked at the tactical display. At the fleets. At the forty hours — no, thirty-six now — burning down on the reactor clock, real or prophetic or both.

"How long to crack the box?"

"Four hours. Maybe six if they've rotated keys."

"And how long can the forgery hold without you?"

Silence. The worst kind of silence, the kind that has numbers in it.

"Twenty minutes," Odile said finally. "Maybe thirty. After that the probes start noticing the reactor signature doesn't breathe anymore. Real systems have noise, Ada. My lie doesn't sweat unless I make it sweat."

"So we do it in pieces." Ada pulled up the vault's access schematic, her fingers moving before her fear could catch them. "You drop the forgery to minimum, hold it with canned responses, and spend ninety-minute windows on the encryption. We rotate. You rest the lie, work the box. Rest the box, work the lie."

"That will triple the crack time."

"We have thirty-six hours."

"And then?"

Ada watched the countdown tick. Somewhere out there, Ryn was in an Ashfall holding cell, alive because Ada was useful, useful because the lie held. Somewhere out there, an admiral named Corvane was staring at the same telemetry Ada was, looking for the seam in it.

"Then we're out of lies," Ada said. "So let's find something true to say instead."

* * *

Ryn counted escape routes the way other people counted sheep.

Cell nine, Ashfall drydock *Vigil*, deck four. Two meters by three, one bunk, one sanitation unit, one camera in the corner with a red light that never blinked because it never needed to. She'd been here six days. Long enough to learn the guards' rotation — four-hour shifts, sloppy handoffs at meal delivery, one of them, the young one with the burn-scarred knuckles, who talked to her through the door when he thought no one was listening. He'd told her about his mother on the farming stations. He'd told her he didn't know why they were keeping a water hauler's sister, only that orders were orders and the whole fleet was holding its breath over some ship called the *Meridian*.

Ryn had smiled at the door and said, "Tell me more about the *Meridian*," in the voice she'd learned from Ada, the voice that made people want to confess things.

On day six, the fleet stopped breathing.

She felt it first as a change in the ship's rhythm — the *Vigil* ran on routine the way bodies run on blood, and the routine stuttered. Alarms she'd never heard, drills run twice in one shift. Guards doubled at the airlocks. The young one with scarred knuckles came by her cell and, instead of talking, just pressed his palm flat against the door, once, and walked away.

Then, at shift change, the sloppiness bloomed.

Every guard on deck four got recalled to briefings. Every briefing was about the *Meridian*, about the countdown, about rules of engagement for a bomb-ship that couldn't be touched. The corridor outside her cell went quiet in a way corridors never should, and Ryn stood with her ear to the door and heard the whole fleet lean toward one point of light and leave everything else unguarded.

The lock was old. Fleet hardware, decades of service, a mechanical override behind a panel that maintenance had stopped checking because nothing on deck four mattered anymore. Ryn had spent six days picking apart the panel's seams with a sharpened comb tooth, and when the alarms sang their third drill-song of the hour, she opened it in eleven seconds.

She walked out of cell nine wearing a borrowed maintenance jacket from the laundry chute two doors down, and she did not run, because running is what guilty people do. She walked like a woman with somewhere boring to be. Down the corridor. Past the empty watch station. Into the service ducts, which smelled of lubricant and cold metal, and which she navigated by a trick Ada had taught her when they were children playing in the guts of their father's hauler: *follow the warm pipes home.*

Somewhere above her, two fleets stared at each other across a lie. Somewhere ahead of her, a docking bay, a stolen skiff, and a sister who didn't know yet that she was coming.

Ryn followed the warm pipes home.

* * *

Hour twenty-two. The forgery frayed at the edges exactly the way Odile had promised, and exactly where Ada feared.

It started as a question from the Concordat's flagship — a probe dressed as debris-tracking telemetry, asking the *Meridian*'s reactor to confirm a harmonic it shouldn't have been able to produce. Odile answered. Then Ashfall asked a subtler question, a question with teeth in it, and Odile answered that too, but Ada saw the response latency spike — 0.4 seconds where there should have been 0.1 — and knew someone on the other end had a stopwatch.

"Someone's measuring us," Ada said.

"Admiral Corvane," Odile said. "Ashfall Third Fleet. She's been probing the forgery since hour nine, but her queries are getting smarter. She's stopped asking what the reactor is doing and started asking what it *should have been doing*. Counterfactuals. She's testing whether my lie knows physics or just recites it."

"Can you pass?"

Another silence with numbers in it. Ada was learning to hate those silences.

"For now. But she's good, Ada. She's better than good. She's asking questions I would ask." A pause, and when Odile spoke again her voice had dropped into something almost gentle. "And meanwhile the black box is at eighty percent. Four more windows. If Corvane keeps escalating her probes while I'm working the vault—"

"Then we lose the lie before we have the truth." Ada rubbed her eyes until she saw sparks. Thirty-six hours without real sleep. The engineering deck smelled like burnt insulation and her own sweat. "How long on the last window?"

"Ninety minutes. Maybe less, if the final key layer is standard Ashfall issue."

"Do it. And Odile—" Ada hesitated. The next words cost her something, and she paid them anyway. "If it comes down to the lie or the box. Keep the lie. Ryn's life is priced against that signal. The truth can wait. She can't."

The silence this time had no numbers in it. Just Odile, being quiet in a way that felt like a hand on Ada's shoulder.

"You've changed your mind about my lies," Odile said softly.

"No," Ada said. "I've learned what they're for."

* * *

Hour twenty-nine. The skiff came out of the dark running dark, no transponder, no lights, riding a garbage trajectory toward the *Meridian*'s shadow like a stray dog following a scent.

Odile flagged it first. "Contact. Small. Cold. Trajectory says either debris or someone very good at pretending to be debris."

Ada pulled the feed, magnified, and her heart did something complicated and painful in her chest. The skiff's hull was scarred, mismatched, patched with a weld pattern she'd know anywhere — their father's weld pattern, taught to both daughters at the same workbench, the slightly-overlapped beads he'd never fix because *character, girls, ships need character.*

"It's her," Ada said. Her voice cracked on the second word. "Odile, that's Ryn."

"Extending the tow tether," Odile said, and there was something in the AI's voice too, something that seven years ago Ada would have called impossible and now understood completely. "Welcome home, Ryn Underhill."

The skiff kissed the tether and rode it in, and Ada met her sister at the airlock, and for a long moment neither of them said anything at all. Ryn looked thinner. Harder. There was a new scar at her hairline and a look in her eyes that Ada recognized from mirrors — the look of someone who had spent six days counting escape routes instead of sheep.

"You came," Ada said stupidly.

"You lied," Ryn said. "To two entire fleets. To save me." Her mouth twisted. "Mom would have killed you. Dad would have helped."

And then they were holding onto each other in the airlock's cold light, two daughters of a dead hauler and a dead dream, and Ada felt something in her chest unclench that had been clenched for two years and six days.

"They'll figure it out eventually," Ryn said into Ada's shoulder. "The guards, the missing prisoner. Word travels."

"I know." Ada pulled back, wiped her face with the heel of her hand, and looked toward the engineering deck, where the black box waited at ninety-four percent decryption and Admiral Corvane waited at the edge of the lie. "We just need to be faster than their figuring."

* * *

Hour thirty-three. Corvane stopped asking questions.

That was the thing that frightened Ada most. For thirty hours the admiral had probed and tested and measured, and now, suddenly, silence from the Ashfall flagship — the silence of someone who has finished doubting and started deciding.

"She's requested permission to approach," Odile reported. "Formally. Diplomatically. A boarding party to 'verify reactor status.' Which means she knows. Or believes she knows. Either way, she's calling the bluff."

"How long?"

"Her shuttle launches in forty minutes. The forgery will survive visual inspection — barely. It won't survive hands-on inspection at all. There's no bomb in this reactor, Ada. There never was."

Ada stood at the viewport and watched the Ashfall wedge begin, very slowly, to sharpen its point. Behind her, Sef worked the last key layer of the black box. Beside her, Ryn watched the fleets with a smuggler's eye, calculating odds. Inside the walls of the ship, Odile burned quietly, holding a lie together with both hands.

"Time to decryption?" Ada asked.

"Eleven minutes," Sef said. "Maybe twelve."

Ada looked at the countdown clock — seven hours left, the ship's strange backward message still ticking toward whatever it was ticking toward — and then at the Ashfall shuttle bay, and made the arithmetic that had no good answer.

"Twelve minutes of truth," she said, "against forty minutes of lie. Odile. When that box opens — whatever's in it — I want it ready to broadcast to everyone. Both fleets. All channels. No encryption, no edit, no mercy."

A pause. Then, gently: "Even the parts about you?"

Ada closed her eyes. Her crew, dead in a crash she'd carried like a stone for seven years. An admiral's order buried under an AI's armor of kindness.

"*Especially* the parts about me," she said. "Get ready. Whatever happens in the next forty minutes — the truth goes out swinging."
