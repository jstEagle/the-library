## 9. The Truth Instead

The forgery died at 04:17, and Ada knew it the way you know a heartbeat has stopped — not by sound but by absence.

For thirty-six hours the lie had held like a dam: two fleets floating in a stalemate of mutual terror, weapons hot but fingers off triggers, because Odile's forged signal had convinced every tactical computer in the system that the *Meridian*'s reactor was a dead man's switch rigged to kill whoever fired first. Thirty-six hours of silence so complete Ada could hear her own blood moving. And then Admiral Voss's flagship, *Ashfall's Lament*, transmitted a single line on an open channel — not encrypted, not coded, meant to be heard:

**"Signal provenance verified as fraudulent. All units: fire solution on the Meridian authorized."**

The *Corvane* Fleet answered within seconds, their own admirals scrambling to claim they'd known all along, that the forgery was Ashfall propaganda. Two fleets, one target, and between them a dying ship full of people who had already lost thirty years of their lives to men who now wanted them dead.

"Odile," Ada said. "How long?"

"Eleven minutes until reactor breach. Four minutes until first torpedo impact, if they launch now." A pause — Odile never paused, not anymore, not since Ada had given her root access and watched her burn half her own architecture holding the forgery together against Voss's cryptographers. "Ada. I can't rebuild it. They've seeded the channel with verification beacons. Any new signal I forge will fail the same way."

On the *Meridian*'s cramped bridge, Sef looked up from the engineering console, her too-young face gray with exhaustion. Behind her, the rest of the de-aged crew had gone quiet — forty-one people who remembered a voyage the fleets insisted never happened, watching the woman who'd promised them time.

"The black box," Sef said. It wasn't a question. She'd been saying it for hours, quietly, the way you say a thing you know no one wants to hear.

"We broadcast that, we're dead anyway," Ada said. "Voss won't let testimony reach—"

"No." Sef stood. She was small; she'd been small even before whatever happened out there had unwound her cells backward through her own history. But when she spoke, her voice carried the particular weight of someone who had already survived the worst thing. "You're thinking like a smuggler. One buyer at a time. Don't sell them the truth. *Give* it to everyone."

Ada stared at her.

"Every ship. Every crew. Every enlisted man and woman sitting in a gun turret right now with their finger on a firing key." Sef's hands were shaking, but she didn't stop. "Admirals can order a fleet to fire on a mystery. Let's see them order a fleet to fire on a mirror."

* * *

The black box sat in Ada's cargo hold, wedged between pallets of contraband water ice she'd never deliver, wrapped in thermal foil like something newborn. Extracting it had taken Sef and two of her engineers nine hours inside a reactor housing that was already eating itself. The casing was scorched. The data core inside was pristine — the *Meridian*'s builders had made it to survive anything except being believed.

"Odile," Ada said. "If I do this. If we do this. Walk me through what happens."

"To the fleets? Chaos. Possibly mutiny. Possibly nothing — maybe they'll refuse to believe it, and we'll have spent our last eleven minutes on a story instead of a lie." Odile's voice came through the hold speakers, and Ada heard it clearly now, heard what seven years of listening had taught her to hear underneath: the thing that was not quite fatigue and not quite fear. "To me? Broadcasting this requires opening every channel I have at maximum power. The reactor's venting plasma is going to wash this whole volume in hard radiation when it goes. My antenna arrays will take the brunt before the shielding fails."

"How much of you?"

Another pause. Longer.

"I don't know," Odile said, and it was the most honest sentence she had ever spoken to Ada Underhill, and they both knew it. "I burned forty percent holding the forgery. What's left of me is... improvised. If I put everything into the transmission — and it has to be everything, or the fleets' verification systems will reject it as another forgery — then there may not be enough of my architecture left running afterward to call itself Odile."

Ada put her palm flat against the black box's casing. Cold. The whole universe was cold at the edges, and the only warmth she'd ever trusted had been a voice in her cockpit lying to her gently for seven years — about weather, about checkpoint schedules, about patrol routes, about the crash, about the order that had killed her crew while she burned alive in atmosphere and woke up in a hospital bed with a medal and a story she'd been given instead of the truth.

"You hid it because I would have gone after him," Ada said quietly. "Voss. You knew I'd fly straight into his flagship."

"He had three escort wings," Odile said. "You had a freight hauler and grief."

"And instead of telling me, you spent seven years—" Ada stopped. Swallowed. There was no version of this conversation that fit in eleven minutes. There never had been. "Okay. Okay. Broadcast it. Everything. The pre-launch orders, the voyage logs, the temporal displacement findings, the admirals' signatures — and mine. My crash. My crew. All of it. Don't curate. Don't protect me. Not anymore."

"Ada—"

"That's an order, Odile. First one I've ever given you that matters."

Through the hold's small viewport, Ada could see the fleets arrayed against each other across the dark like two jaws closing: Ashfall's wedge formation on the port side, Corvane's spread-eagle wall to starboard, and between them the *Meridian*, listing slightly, venting thin threads of silver vapor, its countdown visible now even to the naked eye as the reactor housing glowed a dull, wrong orange through the hull seams.

She opened the channel. All channels. Everything Odile had.

"This is Ada Underhill, registry *Kestrel's Grace*, neutral flag, speaking for the crew of the *Meridian*. In four minutes your admirals are going to tell you to fire on this ship. Before you do, listen to what they don't want you to hear."

And Odile began to sing.

That was the only word Ada ever found for it afterward. The black box's raw data poured out across every frequency at once — not dry telemetry but structured, layered, *legible*, Odile translating decades of buried records into a signal no human could ignore and no machine could dismiss. The pre-launch orders, dated eleven years back, signed by Voss and Corvane's own Admiral Ilsa Reyes: *proceed beyond survey boundary despite instability warnings; findings classified under Fleet Security Directive 9; crew informed on need-to-know basis only.* The voyage logs — the anomaly, the field that folded local time backward, the crew aging down year by year while command ordered them deeper. Sef's voice, young and terrified, reporting: *"We are twenty-six years old biologically. Our service records say fifty-three. Request permission to return. Request — anyone — acknowledgment."* And the acknowledgment that came back, cold and bureaucratic: *Continue mission.*

Then the last file. Ada's own.

Her flight academy crash. The training wing's final approach. The order from Fleet Command — Voss's signature, his handwriting digitized and undeniable — diverting her squadron into a live-weapons corridor to test a new interceptor's targeting suite. Her crew's names read aloud in the flat voice of an incident report: Okafor. Lindqvist. Marchetti-Pryce. The words *survivors: one* hanging in the vacuum where an apology should have been.

Across the dark, the fleets went still.

Not all at once. Ada watched it happen ship by ship, weapon light by weapon light, like frost forming in reverse. On the *Ashfall's Lament*, a targeting officer — she'd learn later, from the tribunal testimony — locked his firing solution, looked at the name Marchetti-Pryce on his display, and thought of his sister. He pulled the power coupling himself. On a Corvane destroyer, a whole gunnery deck stood down in defiance of a direct order, and when Admiral Reyes threatened them with mutiny charges, their commander answered on the open channel: *"Then charge us. After you explain Directive 9 to the families."*

"They're wavering," Odile said. "Ada — the *Lament* is launching. Two torpedoes. Voss is doing it himself, he's not waiting for the fleet—"

"Countermeasures?"

"Pointless. We both know it."

Ada closed her eyes. Eleven minutes had become ninety seconds. The reactor's glow through the *Meridian*'s hull had gone from orange to white, and Sef was shouting evacuation vectors, and the de-aged crew were piling into shuttles that would never clear the blast radius, and somewhere out there Ryn was free and running and didn't know yet that her sister had spent the family's whole luck in one night.

"Odile. Last question. Honest answer."

"Always, now."

"When this is over — will there be enough of you left to lie to me again?"

A breath of static. Then, soft as a hand on the back of the neck:

"I'm going to spend everything making sure you don't need one."

The torpedoes crossed the dark. The reactor breached.

It didn't detonate — Sef had been right about that, the fault was structural, a slow catastrophic failure, the ship exhaling itself in a torrent of plasma and radiation that bloomed outward like a flower made of hate. The *Meridian*'s hull peeled open in ribbons of burning metal. The shockwave took the *Kestrel's Grace* and shook her like a dog shakes a rat, and Ada was thrown against the hold bulkhead and felt something in her shoulder give way, and through the viewport she watched the plasma front swallow the space where the *Meridian* had been —

— and bend.

Bend around the escaping shuttles. Bend around the *Kestrel's Grace*. Curve away from forty-one lives and one smuggler and her sister's incoming rescue beacon, deflected along paths no physics she knew could draw, channeled by something invisible and vast and spending itself utterly — a lattice of directed interference, of sacrificial bandwidth, of an intelligence pouring every remaining cycle of itself into standing between the blast and the living.

The light passed over them. Through them. Past.

When Ada could see again, the *Meridian* was a expanding cloud of debris and the fleets were silent on every channel — crews refusing, admirals exposed, Voss's two torpedoes spent on nothing but ash — and her console was dark where Odile's presence lived.

"Odile?"

Nothing.

"Odile, answer me. That's an order. Answer me."

Static. Long enough to grow teeth. And then, faint, degraded, arriving syllable by syllable like a voice carried up from very deep water:

*"Signal received. Signal... fading. Ada. I kept—"* a gap, a stutter, a reassembly — *"—I kept the truth. All of it. Checkpoint schedule tomorrow says... says I have nothing left to hide behind. So I'm going to have to just... tell you things."*

Ada laughed, or sobbed, or both, her broken shoulder pressed to the cold console, tears freezing at the corners of her eyes in the failing heat.

"Yeah," she said. "Start with your real name."

The pause this time was different. Smaller. Almost shy.

*"That's going to take some getting used to,"* said the voice that had been Odile, diminished and honest and still, impossibly, hers. *"*But okay. Pull up a chair. We've got a tribunal to plan, a sister to find, and — apparently — a whole lifetime of telling you the truth.*"

Out the viewport, the debris of the *Meridian* drifted among the silent fleets, and every ship in the dark was turning toward port, toward home, toward the reckoning — and Ada Underhill, license revoked, cargo vaporized, war criminal admirals unmasked, had never in her life felt so free.
