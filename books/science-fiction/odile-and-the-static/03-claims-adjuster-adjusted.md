## 3. Claims Adjuster, Adjusted

I have watched a lot of terrible footage in my career. A grain elevator collapsing in slow motion while the owner's dog ran figure-eights through the dust cloud. A terraforming pond flash-freezing with eleven swimmers in it, caught on a resort's security cam at four frames per second. You develop a professional detachment. You learn to watch catastrophe the way an accountant watches numbers: as entries, not events.

I watched my nine seconds forty-one times that night, and the detachment never came.

* * *

My room at the Waystation Inn was three meters by four, smelled of recycled air and someone else's cigarettes from a decade ago, and had a desk bolted to the floor — which, given recent events, struck me as either prudent engineering or dark comedy. I set up my portable deck, plugged in the recorder, and told myself I was doing routine evidence review. Chain of custody. Timestamp verification. The liturgy of my profession.

The recorder had captured everything, because recorders are honest in a way nothing else is. Audio first: a low hum from the foundry floor, Adeyemi's voice mid-sentence about load-bearing tolerances, my own breathing. Then, at 14:07:22 local, the hum changed pitch. Not stopped — changed, the way a note changes when the instrument it lives inside is briefly removed from existence.

Then nine seconds of silence that wasn't silence. It was the sound of a world forgetting which way was down.

On video, the foundry came apart in slow motion. A wrench rose off a workbench with the unhurried dignity of a soap bubble. Dust lifted from surfaces nobody had cleaned in years, hanging in the air like the room's whole history deciding to float free. Through the high windows I could see the river — the actual river, Kilnwater, two kilometers east — standing up out of its bed in a silver sheet, a wall of water holding itself upright against every law I'd ever trusted.

Adeyemi's face, in frame for half a second before he grabbed a stanchion: not screaming. Counting. His lips moving. He'd told me later he counted all nine, because somebody had to.

Then 14:07:31, and gravity remembered its job. The wrench hit the bench hard enough to bounce. The river came down with a sound I felt through the floor of my room, through the bedrock, through whatever part of me still believed the universe kept its appointments. Somewhere in the colony, I would learn the next morning, every unsecured object on Meridian's Reach fell at once, and the collective crash lasted almost a full minute, like the planet applauding its own survival.

Routine review. Right.

I ran the timestamps. That was where the night went wrong — or right, depending on how much you value being able to sleep.

Here is what nobody tells you about gravity flickers, because there has never been one before to tell anybody about: they leave fingerprints. Light takes time to travel. Sound takes longer. When a region of space stops pulling, everything in it responds in sequence, nearest objects first, and if you have enough reference points — and a decommissioned foundry full of drifting tools is a very generous instrument — you can triangulate.

I spent six hours building the model. I used the wrench, the dust plume, the window light bending as the atmosphere above the colony thinned and re-thickened, and finally the river, God help me, the river was the best data point of all, two kilometers of water giving me a clean vector line. I fed it all into the actuarial modeling suite I use for collapse forensics, the software that tells insurance companies which disasters were accidents and which were somebody's Tuesday afternoon decision.

At 03:40 in the morning, the suite gave me its answer, and then politely asked if I wanted to file a report.

The flicker hadn't originated on the ground. It hadn't originated anywhere near the ground. The propagation front entered the system from high orbit — from a point matching, within the software's margin of error, the geosynchronous slot occupied by Meridian's orbital defense grid. The grid everyone knew was mostly decorative. The grid the fleet maintained on a maintenance budget smaller than my expense account.

The flicker had come from above us. From us. From the thing hanging over our heads wearing the friendly name *Meridian Shield Array Seven*.

I sat very still for a long time. Outside my window, the colony slept under a sky that had tried to let go of it, and I understood, with the cold clarity that only arrives at three in the morning, that I was no longer an insurance investigator looking at a claim.

I was a witness. And witnesses, in my experience, have a shelf life inversely proportional to the importance of what they've seen.

* * *

Commander Ilsa Reyes had been awake for thirty hours when her shuttle made its final approach to Meridian's Reach, and she resented every one of them.

Not the mission. Missions were simple; you executed them or you didn't. What she resented was the paperwork trail that had preceded her — three flag officers signing routing slips with hands that visibly shook, a legal attaché who kept using the word *plausibly deniable* as though repetition could make it true, and a briefing packet classified so far above her clearance that she'd been required to read it inside a shielded room while a man with a sidearm watched her eyes move down the page.

The packet had contained exactly one sentence she was authorized to repeat aloud: *Recover all civilian recordings of the anomaly. By any means consistent with continued good relations.*

*Consistent with continued good relations.* Fleet language was a beautiful thing. It meant: be nice until being nice stops working.

She looked down at the colony through the shuttle glass. Twenty years ago she'd done a rotation here as a junior officer, back when Meridian's Reach still appeared on recruitment posters. Even then it had been dying politely — scrubbers wheezing, schools consolidating, the whole settlement performing vitality for visitors the way old people perform health for their doctors. Now the posters were gone and the performance was over, and the place below her just sat in its valley like a debt nobody intended to collect.

Which was, she thought grimly, precisely the problem. Everyone had agreed Meridian's Reach didn't matter. And now the entire strategic balance of two fleets might hinge on what a handful of people in a forgotten town had happened to see.

Her liaison brief identified the primary civilian witness: one Oscar Fairweather, insurance investigator, licensed through the Outer Colonies Consortium, twenty-two years in claims. No political affiliations. No family. A personnel profile so clean it read like a eulogy.

Reyes had learned long ago that the cleanest profiles belonged to the most dangerous people. People without attachments couldn't be leveraged. They could only be persuaded — or replaced.

"Approach vector confirmed," the pilot said. "Landing pad seven. There's a reception party."

One person, actually, when Reyes stepped down onto the pad into air that tasted of rust and overworked filtration. Fairweather stood at the edge of the tarmac with her hands in her coat pockets, watching the shuttle the way people watch weather they've decided not to be surprised by anymore.

"Commander," Fairweather said. "You're faster than the mail service. That's usually a bad sign."

"Ms. Fairweather." Reyes extended her hand. Firm grip, dry palm, eyes doing inventory of the shuttle behind her — counting crew, scanning for weapons, cataloging everything. An investigator to the bone. Good. Investigators spoke fluent evidence, and evidence was a language Reyes could fake well enough to pass.

"I'm hoping this is a courtesy call," Fairweather said.

"It absolutely is." Reyes smiled, and meant perhaps ten percent of it. "Is there somewhere we can talk?"

* * *

We talked in the inn's common room, because it was the only interior space in town with chairs that matched, and Commander Reyes bought the coffee, which I noted as either generosity or strategy. With fleet officers it is always strategy. Generosity is just strategy that hasn't declared itself yet.

She was maybe forty-five, regulation-short hair going gray at the temples, with the kind of posture that suggested furniture had never once dared to be uncomfortable around her. She opened with small talk — the flight in, the state of the roads, the shocking price of fuel out here — and I matched her beat for beat, because I recognized the game. I played it across desks from policyholders' relatives twice a month. Two professionals circling each other, each waiting for the other to name the real subject.

She named it in eleven minutes. I respected the discipline.

"The event yesterday," she said. "Fourteen-oh-seven local. You were in the field."

"I was." I turned my cup a quarter-turn on its saucer. "In the old Kessler foundry, taking testimony for a claim. Property loss, twenty years outstanding."

"You felt it."

"Everyone felt it, Commander. It was the whole system."

"Everyone experienced it," she corrected gently. "You may be the only one who *documented* it. You're a recorder by trade, Ms. Fairweather. Your equipment was running."

There it was. I kept my face pleasant and my pulse professional. "My equipment runs whenever I'm working. That's the trade."

"And it captured the event."

"It captured nine seconds of things falling up and then falling down. Which, I'm told, is what everyone else captured too — with their knees and their kitchenware."

Something moved behind her eyes. Not anger. Recalculation. She'd expected denial or eagerness, and I'd handed her neither, and she was adjusting her model of me in real time. I know the look. I wear it myself.

"Ms. Fairweather," she said, leaning forward, voice dropping into a register of warm confidentiality that I have heard from adjusters, lawyers, and exactly one funeral director, "the fleet takes this incident extremely seriously. Unexplained gravitational anomalies are a safety-of-navigation issue. Every recording — civilian, commercial, personal — needs to be collected and analyzed so we can determine whether there's any risk of recurrence. You can appreciate that."

I could appreciate it the way I appreciate a denial-of-claim letter written in a sympathetic font.

"Of course," I said. "And when your analysis is complete, will findings be shared? The colony would want to know if the sky plans to do that again."

"The analysis is a fleet matter."

"Mm." I sipped my coffee. "Here's my difficulty, Commander. Everything I recorded yesterday is evidence. I'm working a live claim — the Kessler file, twenty years open. If my documentation leaves my custody, my chain of evidence breaks, my client's claim dies, and I've wasted a trip to the end of nowhere. So I'll need something in writing. A receipt. A subpoena. A formal requisition with a case number. Something that says the fleet took custody of evidence belonging to an open claim, over the investigator's objection, and assumes liability for its integrity."

Silence. She studied me for a long moment, and I watched her decide how much of her hand to show.

"That's unusually careful," she said.

"It's the job. Careful is the entire job."

"You understand that refusing a direct request from the fleet would be..." She paused, choosing the word with visible care, "...unhelpful. To everyone. Including the colony."

And there it was — the trap, sprung with a smile. Not a threat. Never a threat. Just the gentle observation that cooperation was a form of citizenship and refusal a form of something else, something with a file number. I'd spent twenty years listening to institutions say *this hurts me more than it hurts you*, and the fleet, it turned out, spoke the same dialect as the insurance industry. We are all actuaries of other people's suffering. Some of us just wear better uniforms.

"I'll tell you what," I said. "Come by my room this evening. I'll walk you through the footage personally. You can verify the timestamps yourself, confirm nothing's been altered, and we'll execute a proper transfer of custody. Full documentation. Everybody protected."

Something flickered across her face — surprise, quickly filed away. She'd come expecting either surrender or resistance, and I'd offered her bureaucracy instead, which is its own kind of resistance, dressed in compliance.

"That would be acceptable," she said.

"Splendid." I stood and shook her hand again. Her grip was firm and her palm, this time, was slightly damp. Interesting. "Oh — one question, Commander, purely professional curiosity."

"Go ahead."

"When your analysis determines where the anomaly originated — will that finding be shared with the colony? Since we're the ones who live directly underneath whatever it was?"

The pause lasted one second too long. I've interviewed enough people to know that a pause isn't empty; it's a room where a decision is happening.

"The origin determination is the core of the analysis," she said. "It will be handled at the appropriate level."

"Of course it will." I smiled. "This evening, then. Room twelve. I'll have the coffee."

* * *

Reyes walked back to her shuttle slowly, turning the conversation over the way she'd turn a captured device, looking for seams.

Fairweather had agreed. That was good. Fairweather had agreed *too easily and too carefully*, which was bad, or possibly excellent, depending on which Fairweather was real: the tired civil servant whose instinct was procedure, or the woman who had already looked at her own footage, seen what the fleet's own analysts had seen, and understood exactly why a commander had crossed half a system to sit in a shabby common room drinking bad coffee.

The preliminary sensor reconstruction was locked in Reyes's sealed orders, but she'd read it, and it lived behind her eyes now like a stone she'd swallowed. Origin: geosynchronous altitude. Bearing: Shield Array Seven. Confidence: ninety-four percent and climbing. Their own grid. Their own test, unauthorized, unsanctioned, conducted over a civilian colony with a blast radius that the models said should have included the ground.

*Should have.* The models had been wrong by a margin of eleven percent, which was the only reason anyone on that rock was alive to drink bad coffee.

If Halcyon's sensors caught even a fraction of what Meridian's own instruments had recorded — and Halcyon's sensors were better, that was the joke, their sensors were always better — then the truce was dead, and the war would start over a colony both fleets had agreed, in writing, wasn't worth defending.

So the recordings mattered. All of them. Especially the ones made by a professional witness with professional equipment and, apparently, a professional's instinct for custody chains.

Reyes paused at the shuttle ramp and looked back at the town. In a ground-floor window across the square, an old man was sweeping broken glass from yesterday's fall, methodical, unhurried, sweeping it into a neat pile as though debris were just another thing the day owed him and he intended to collect.

She thought: *this is what we tested our weapon over.*

She thought: *I can live with this,* and noticed, with a small cold surprise, that the thought arrived pre-rehearsed, as though some part of her had been practicing it since the briefing room.

Then she boarded the shuttle, and began drafting the requisition forms Fairweather had demanded — flawless, legitimate, and, like everything else in this operation, built to mean more than they said.

* * *

I spent the afternoon preparing. Not cleaning — preparing. I copied the original recording to two separate drives, hid one inside the housing of my calibration kit where it looked exactly like the boring spare part it resembled, and left the other in the deck where a reasonable person would find it. Then I did something that took real craft, the kind of craft I'd sworn off using outside of fraud investigation:

I edited a copy.

Nothing dramatic. I shifted three timestamps by fractions of a second — enough to introduce noise into any forensic comparison, not enough to survive close scrutiny. A forgery designed to be *detected*. Because here is what twenty years of investigating liars had taught me: the best way to protect a truth is to give the people hunting it a lie to catch. Let them find the tampering. Let them feel clever. Let them conclude that the investigator cut corners, that the footage is compromised, that nothing in this dusty room can be trusted as evidence.

A tainted witness is a safe witness. Sometimes the only way to keep a secret is to make yourself look like a smaller criminal than you are.

I hated it. I want that on the record, wherever records go when they burn. I hated every keystroke of it, and I did it anyway, sitting in room twelve of the Waystation Inn with the light going amber outside, while somewhere above me a defense array hung in its slot pretending to be a shield.

At nineteen hundred, Commander Reyes knocked. Punctual to the minute. Of course she was.

"Ms. Fairweather." She stepped in, glanced once — professionally, swiftly — at the deck on the desk, at the calibration kit in the corner, at the single cup of coffee I'd set out. One cup. I watched her notice that too.

"Commander. Please, sit. Let me show you the worst nine seconds ever committed to storage."

She sat. I queued the footage. And as the foundry filled the little screen — the wrench rising, the dust blooming, the river standing up like a held breath — I watched her face instead of the video, and what I saw there was not curiosity.

It was recognition. The look of a person checking items off a list she'd memorized before she ever landed.

"Well," I said lightly, pausing the playback on the frozen river. "Shall we talk about where this actually came from? Because I ran the vectors, Commander. And unless my software's developed a sense of humor, the answer is sitting directly overhead — wearing your fleet's name."

For the first time that day, Commander Ilsa Reyes looked genuinely tired.

"Ms. Fairweather," she said, "what I'm about to ask you next is going to require a great deal more coffee."
