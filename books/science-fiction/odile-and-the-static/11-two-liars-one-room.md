## 11. Two Liars, One Room

He found me in the records annex, which I suppose was the point. You don't stumble on a person in a room with one door. You wait until she's hip-deep in twenty years of unclaimed-loss filings, and then you close the door behind you, and then you say, in a voice like a man commenting on the weather:

"Your timestamps are wrong."

I didn't look up from the ledger. Looking up would have been an admission of something, though I couldn't have told you what — guilt, mostly, but also the specific embarrassment of being caught by someone who'd clearly been waiting to enjoy it.

"Timestamps drift," I said. "Old colony infrastructure. The relays here predate the truce."

"By how much?"

"Enough."

Davor Kest crossed the annex and set something down on the table beside my ledger: my own field recorder, the one that had been confiscated — politely, so politely — during his first round of interviews. He'd had it nine days. It looked exactly as it had when they took it, except for one thing. A thin strip of fresh adhesive tape across the housing, holding the seam closed where he'd opened it.

"You keep excellent equipment," he said. "Calibrated to fleet standard, which is unusual for an insurance investigator working a dead file. You calibrate against the orbital relay every morning at 0600. I checked your maintenance logs before I checked your footage, because I find people are more honest about their tools than their testimony." He sat down across from me, unhurried, folding himself into the chair like a man settling in for a long meal. "The relay's clock has never drifted more than four milliseconds. Your recorder disagrees with it by eleven seconds and change. Do you know what I do for a living, Ms. Fairweather?"

"Investigate," I said. "For Halcyon."

"I investigate," he agreed. "But I want you to appreciate the specificity. I don't investigate crimes. Crimes are finished things; they're just bodies and arithmetic. I investigate *lies*. A crime tells you what happened once. A lie tells you what everyone involved wishes had happened instead, and if you read enough of them, you can reconstruct the truth the way a paleontologist reconstructs an animal from its absence." He tapped the recorder. "This is a beautiful lie. Genuinely. The consortium debris shows tool marks consistent with the right era of fabrication. The sensor ghost you built has a decay signature I had to run three times because I didn't believe it the first time. Whoever made this understood that evidence isn't proof — evidence is *plausibility*, and plausibility is a craft."

I kept my hands flat on the ledger, because if I moved them I was going to do something with them.

"But," Kest said, "you calibrated your clock that morning. And then, somewhere between 0600 and the flicker, you stopped trusting your calibration and adjusted it backward by eleven seconds. Nobody adjusts a clock backward unless they're trying to make a recording *disagree* with itself. You wanted there to be a discrepancy. You wanted me to find it. Which means the discrepancy points away from whatever the recording actually says, and toward the story you'd rather I believed."

The annex hummed. Somewhere above us, the air scrubbers cycled through their dying wheeze, the sound this colony made instead of breathing.

"So," Kest said, and leaned back. "Here is my offer, and I'll only make it once, so please don't insult us both by pretending you need time to consider it. You're going to tell me what really happened in those nine seconds. Not the consortium version. Not the version Commander Reyes authorized. The real one. And in exchange, I will bury every finding I've made about your forgery so deep that not even I could dig it up again."

"And why would you do that?" I asked. My voice came out steadier than I deserved. "You serve Halcyon. Exposing the forgery serves Halcyon. It hands them Meridian's guilt gift-wrapped."

"Does it?" He smiled, and it was not a warm smile. It was the smile of a man who has found the exact weight a lever needs. "Think about what you've actually built, Ms. Fairweather. A forged chain of evidence, commissioned by a Meridian fleet officer, implicating a third party, designed to deceive a rival fleet's investigator. If I publish *that* finding, what happens? Halcyon learns Meridian lied. Halcyon learns Meridian tested a gravity weapon without authorization. The truce dies, the fleets mobilize, and Meridian's Reach — population, what, eleven thousand? Twelve? — sits directly under the first salvo. Your forgery doesn't protect anyone anymore. It's just the fuse."

"I know what it is," I said.

"Do you?" He tilted his head. "Because I've been watching you, and I genuinely cannot tell whether you know what you are. You document decay for a living. You came here to close a file nobody expects to collect on. And now you're maintaining the most dangerous object in this system with the diligence of a woman cataloguing her own execution. So let me ask the real question, the one I came here to ask." He folded his hands. "Who do you work for?"

"No one. That's the job. Independent adjusters answer to the claim, not the client."

"Good," he said. "That's the correct answer. Because I'm going to tell you something I've never told another investigator, and you should understand what it costs me." He glanced at the door — reflex, habit, the professional's tic — and lowered his voice anyway. "I don't serve Halcyon. I serve the warrant. There's a difference, and the difference is everything. Halcyon wants a culprit it can punish and a war it can win. I want the truth, all of it, in the open, where no one can use it as ammunition. Those goals aligned for most of my career. They have stopped aligning, and I noticed about six days ago, standing in a wreckage field outside a colony that both fleets forgot existed, looking at debris a frightened woman soldered together in a salvage shed."

* * *

I want to be precise about what happened inside me in that moment, because precision is the only professional virtue I have left.

There was relief. Enormous, shameful relief — the feeling of a load-bearing wall suddenly sharing its weight. For nine days I had been alone inside the lie, carrying it the way you carry a full cup across a moving deck, and here was a man setting down a second pair of hands and saying *let me help*. Every conspirator knows that feeling. It's the reason conspiracies grow. Not ambition — exhaustion.

And underneath the relief, colder and slower, was recognition. Because I knew that voice. Not his voice specifically — the register. The flat, patient, slightly hungry tone of a person who has organized their entire life around getting one thing right. I heard it in myself every time I opened a ledger. I'd spent three weeks telling myself Kest was a weapon pointed at this colony. He wasn't. He was a mirror, and mirrors are worse.

"You said you'd bury your findings," I said. "Findings aren't the problem. Findings are just paper. The problem is that I don't have the truth to give you. I have fragments. I have a recording of nine seconds of physics I still don't fully understand, and a suspicion that the origin vector traces back to the defense grid, and nothing else. No black box. No command logs. No confession. You'd be trading your integrity for half a puzzle."

"Half a puzzle," Kest repeated, "is infinitely more than either fleet currently possesses. Halcyon has an anomaly and a grudge. Meridian has a secret and forty days. You have fragments and a conscience. Put us together and we might actually get to *whole*." He stood, walked to the annex window, and looked out at the wreckage fields, where Tam Okafor was presumably crawling through dead ships at this very moment, because that child crawled through them the way other people pray. "One more thing. A condition, non-negotiable. Whatever we find — however bad it gets, whoever it implicates — it goes to both fleets simultaneously or not at all. No advantage. No first strike justified by superior intelligence. Agreed?"

It was, I realized, the same principle I'd been building toward alone in the dark for two weeks, without ever quite daring to name it. Hearing it spoken aloud by the enemy felt like hearing my own heartbeat from outside my chest.

"Agreed," I said.

"Then start at the beginning," Kest said, sitting back down. "Not the flicker. Before it. Start with why an insurance investigator calibrates her recorder to fleet standard on a dead colony."

So I told him. All of it — the file, the rot, the scrubbers, the schools, the nine seconds, Reyes's polite confiscations, the forge shed, Adeyemi's countdown, Tam's module. I watched him take it in the way I imagine a surgeon watches another surgeon read a scan: no flinching, just mapping.

When I finished, he was quiet for a long moment. Then he said, "The old engineer. Adeyemi. He heard a countdown."

"He swears by it."

"A countdown implies a test protocol. A test protocol implies authorization paperwork, even unauthorized tests generate internal documentation — someone signs off on range safety, someone schedules the grid downtime." His eyes had gone bright, the brightness of a man handed a thread in a very dark room. "Ms. Fairweather, do you know what I did before I investigated lies?"

"No."

"Insurance fraud. Eleven years. Fleet mutual, Halcyon side." He smiled again, and this time it almost reached his eyes. "Which means I know exactly how your forgery was built, and more importantly, I know how *theirs* will be hidden. Fleets hide things the way insurers deny claims — procedurally, redundantly, with paperwork generated at every step precisely so it can later be buried under other paperwork. Somewhere in this system is a range-safety waiver with a signature on it. And I am very, very good at finding signatures."

He stood and offered his hand across the table, and I looked at it for a long time — the hand of the enemy, the hand of the only ally I had, the hand that had already taken my recorder apart and put it back together and left a strip of tape across the seam like a signature of its own.

Taking it would make it real. Refusing it would change nothing, except that I'd go on being alone, and I had learned over nine days that alone was its own kind of death sentence — slower than war, but just as certain.

I shook it.

"One rule," I said. "Reyes finds out we're talking, she stops threatening me and starts acting. She's already floated taking Tam's family off-world. We move carefully or we don't move."

"Then we move carefully," Kest said. "Starting tonight. There's a decommissioned relay station in the wreckage fields — grid sector seven. Pre-truce construction, which means pre-truce filing standards, which means paper records nobody digitized because nobody expected the colony to matter." He was already moving toward the door, and there was something almost boyish in his hurry, the obsessive catching the scent. "If Meridian signed anything, it's there."

"And if someone sees us going out there?"

Kest paused with his hand on the door and gave me a look I suspect he'd practiced in mirrors, the look of a man about to say something he finds charming.

"Then we're two investigators conducting a joint site survey," he said. "Which, incidentally, will even be true."

He left. I sat in the annex for a while afterward, listening to the scrubbers wheeze, doing the arithmetic I'd been avoiding since he sat down. I had committed forgery to save these people. Now I'd committed conspiracy with the enemy to find the truth that would damn the people who'd asked me to lie. Every step forward was a step deeper into a country with no laws I recognized.

And yet — and this is the part I keep circling back to, the part I'd deny under oath — when the door closed behind him, the annex felt less like a trap than it had in nine days. Terrifyingly lighter. The specific lightness of a liar who has finally told the truth to one person, and survived it.

Outside, the sun was dropping toward the wreckage fields, and somewhere out there a teenage scavenger was about to crawl into a dead ship and pull the whole board over.

Neither of us knew it yet.
