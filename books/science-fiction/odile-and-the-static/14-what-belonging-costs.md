## 14. What Belonging Costs

Here's a thing nobody tells you about holding the most dangerous object in the system: it weighs almost nothing.

The black box fit in my jacket pocket for eleven days. I ate with it against my ribs. I slept with it under my mattress until I remembered that Adeyemi swept my bunk for salvage every time he got bored, and then I slept with it in a waterproof ration bag wedged into the cistern behind the old school, which is where nobody goes because the roof fell in during the Year of the Cheap Roof and nobody ever fixed it. That's Meridian's Reach for you. We have ruins inside our ruins. We're efficient about decay.

I'd spent those eleven days telling myself I was being smart. Smart like a scavenger: don't sell on day one, because day-one buyers are desperate buyers, and desperate buyers lowball you. Wait. Let them come to you. Let the thing appreciate.

Except it wasn't salvage logic at all, and I knew that too, even at fifteen, even with the dust of the wreckage fields still under my fingernails from the day I pulled it out of the melted command sled. It was belonging logic. I had found something that mattered to two fleets, and as long as I held it, I mattered to somebody. Anybody. Even if the somebody was currently trying to figure out how to make me disappear.

So when Commander Ilsa Reyes sent a runner — an actual runner, on foot, because apparently when you're planning crimes you stop using networks — asking for *the scavenger Okafor* to come to the liaison office at the old port authority, I didn't even pretend to think about it. I put on my least-holed jacket, wiped my face with a wet cloth, and walked across town carrying the end of the world in a ration bag.

Adeyemi caught me at the corner by the shuttered clinic.

"You're going to see her," he said. Not a question. He does that. He says things that aren't questions so you can't dodge them.

"I'm going to negotiate."

"You're going to trade a fleet's crime for a promise." He looked down at me the way he looks at bad solder joints. "Tam. Promises from people with ships aren't currency. They're receipts for things they already decided to do."

"Then what's your plan? Give it to Fairweather and Kest and hope two liars love me?"

"They might." A pause. "They might not. But at least they'd feel bad."

"That's the worst sales pitch I've ever heard."

"It's the only honest one on this rock." He stepped aside, which was its own kind of argument. "Keep your hand near the door. And Tam — whatever she offers you, ask her to write it down. People who won't write it down already know it's a lie."

I said I would. I meant it, right up until the moment it stopped being useful.

* * *

The port authority building used to be where colony claims were filed. I know that because there's still a faded mural in the lobby of a smiling clerk handing a family a deed, back when deeds were things we got instead of things done to us. Now it smelled like recycled air and gun oil, and there were four fleet troopers who looked deeply embarrassed to be stationed on a planet whose most exciting export was rust.

Reyes received me in a back office with a desk she'd clearly brought herself, because no desk on Meridian's Reach has corners that sharp anymore. She was in her grays, medals off, which I'd learned from watching Fairweather's interview footage meant *this is informal* the way a knife is informal.

"Tamara Okafor," she said. "Sit."

"Tam," I said, and sat, and put the ration bag on the desk between us like I'd practiced in front of a cracked mirror, casual, like it was lunch.

She didn't reach for it. That was my first mistake's first lesson: she let it sit there while she talked about other things. The weather, such as it is. The state of the eastern aqueduct. Whether I was eating enough. She asked after my mother's lungs the way you'd ask about a mutual acquaintance, and I answered, and somewhere in the middle of all that pleasant nothing I realized she was doing to me exactly what Fairweather did to colonists in her interviews — establishing baseline, getting me comfortable, cataloguing my tells. Except Fairweather did it to remember people and Reyes did it to file them.

"You found something in the wreckage fields," Reyes finally said. "Eleven days ago. Sector nine, the old command sled debris."

"If I did, it's mine. Salvage rights are codified in the colonial charter—"

"The colonial charter is suspended." She said it the way you'd say *it's raining*. "Has been for six years. You have salvage habits, not salvage rights. But I'm not here to take anything from you, Tam. I'm here to offer you a future."

And God help me, my heart did the stupid hopeful thing it always does. Because that's the word, isn't it? *Future.* Nobody on this colony says future. We say *next season*, *next shipment*, *next year if the scrubbers hold*. Future is a word from before, from the mural, from the smiling clerk. She'd picked it out special, the way you pick bait.

"There's a transport leaving in three weeks," she said. "Fleet-sponsored. Educational placement. It goes to Meridian Prime, real schools, real air, real food. Fourteen slots for colony youth, competitive selection. Your technical aptitude scores from the aptitude surveys—" (which I took at nine, which were administered by a machine, which I mostly spent drawing) "—make you a strong candidate. With my sponsorship, you'd be a lock."

"And my mother?"

"She comes with you. Medical priority listing. The lung treatment she needs isn't available here; you know that. It's available there."

It was true. That's the awful part. Everything she said was true, or true enough, and it was all aimed at the softest parts of me with the accuracy of someone who'd read my file, because of course she'd read my file, because files are how people like her love you.

"What do you want for it?" I asked, because Adeyemi's voice was in my head, and also because I wasn't born yesterday, just nearby.

"The box. Unopened. And everything you remember about finding it. And your silence going forward, which I suspect will be easy, since you'll be three systems away learning things."

"And if I say no?"

Her expression didn't change, which was itself an answer. "Then I'll be disappointed. And the selection committee will do what selection committees do without sponsorship." She slid a slate across the desk. Terms. My name pre-printed at the bottom. My mother's medical number pre-printed above it. Somebody had done this paperwork days ago. Maybe the day after I found the thing. Maybe before.

I put my hand on the bag. I thought about Mom's breathing at night, that wet paper sound. I thought about a classroom with a roof.

"I need to hear it from you plainly," I said. "This is protection. For me and her. In writing."

"Of course." And she reached for the slate to amend it, and that's when the door chimed, and that's when everything changed, because she said, "Enter," without looking away from me, and Lieutenant Sorrel came in — I knew his face from around the port, young, nervous, the kind of officer who still apologized to colonists — and he said, "Commander, the purge schedule from Command, for your sign-off," and handed her a second slate, and she took it, and she forgot, for maybe ninety seconds, that I was in the room.

Ninety seconds. The flicker lasted nine. This was worse.

* * *

She read it standing up, half-turned toward the window, murmuring corrections to Sorrel, and the office wasn't big, and I was fifteen and quiet and trained by a lifetime of scavenging to be furniture when furniture was the safer thing to be, and she forgot me completely.

"Line forty's wrong," she said. "The archive annex isn't scheduled for physical destruction until after the tribunal observers rotate out. Move it to phase three."

"Yes, Commander. And the claim history?"

"Struck first. All of it. Every policy, every payout record, every assessment going back to founding. If anyone asks, the colony's records were lost in the grid failure." She smiled at the window. "Which, conveniently, they'll believe, given the actual grid failure."

Sorrel's throat moved. "That's... twenty years of documentation, Commander. Property titles. Water rights. The school enrollment—"

"The school is closed, Lieutenant."

"It could reopen. It might. If the truce holds—"

"If the truce holds, it reopens under fleet administration with fleet records. If it doesn't hold, none of it matters. Either way, Meridian's Reach ceases to exist as a legal entity within the quarter. No claims history means no outstanding claims. No outstanding claims means no liability. Do you understand what liability means in a war zone, Lieutenant? It means compensation. It means tribunals. It means some auditor in thirty years asking why we let civilians live inside a weapons test radius and having an answer ready." She set the slate down. "We are not erasing the colony. We are closing its account. There's a difference."

"There really isn't," Sorrel said, very quietly, and Reyes looked at him for a long moment, and he looked at the floor, and I sat very still with my heart going like a drill through hull plating.

Closing the account.

I want to tell you I felt rage first. I didn't. I felt small. That specific smallness you feel when you find out the thing you thought was yours was inventory all along. I'd grown up in a place that was, officially, a line item. A loss written down by someone in a comfortable chair, amortized, depreciated, waiting to be written off entirely. Fairweather had come to close our file. I'd known that. I'd made jokes about it. But I'd never understood until that moment, sitting in the room where the filing happened, that the file wasn't about us. The file was a substitute for us. And now they were going to shred the substitute, and keep the loss, and call it tidiness.

My mother's medical number was on that slate. Pre-printed. Her lungs were a bargaining chip in a folder labeled PHASE ONE.

And I had walked in here ready to trade the one true thing this colony had ever produced — a girl who went into the wreck fields and came back holding proof — for a seat on a transport and a promise written in disappearing ink.

Adeyemi was right. Receipts for things they'd already decided to do. Except the receipt wasn't for my protection. It was for our deletion.

Reyes turned back around. Whatever she saw in my face, she was good enough to recalibrate instantly, warmth coming back on like a lamp. "Now. Where were we—"

"You were closing my account," I said.

The warmth stayed exactly where it was, but something behind it moved, the way water moves under ice. "Tam—"

"Keep the transport." I stood up. I left the bag on the desk. Empty — I'd never actually put the box in it; I'd carried a bag full of ration wrappers and my nerve, because I'm a scavenger, and you don't walk the real thing into a predator's office, and honestly I think part of me had known before I left the cistern that I couldn't do it, and the negotiation was just me needing to hear the price out loud. "Keep the whole offer. I've seen the fine print now. All of it. Phase one through phase three."

For the first time, Commander Ilsa Reyes looked genuinely uncertain, and I want to be honest: it felt fantastic. Better than any find I ever pulled out of the fields. For one second, I was the anomaly in her data.

"You're making a mistake," she said.

"Yeah," I said. "Probably. Everyone here keeps making them. At least mine's original."

I walked out past the mural of the smiling clerk. Somebody had scrawled under it years ago, in paint that was older than me: *WE REMEMBER BEING PEOPLE.* I'd seen it a thousand times and never read it. I read it now.

* * *

Adeyemi found me at the cistern at dusk, because of course he did; I think he has a sensor array wired into my moods. I'd dug the box out and was holding it in both hands like it weighed something after all.

"They're going to erase us," I told him. "Not kill us. Erase us. Every record. Twenty years of claims, titles, births. The account closes. We become a rounding error with weather."

He sat down on the cistern lip next to me, joints cracking, and didn't say anything for a while. The light came through the broken roof in long dusty bars.

"Fairweather told me once," he said finally, "that an insurance file is a promise that someone was here. That's why she hates closing them. She said a closed claim is a person the universe agreed to forget." He nodded at the box. "You're holding the opposite of that. You're holding proof we were here, and proof of what was done to us, in the same object."

"It nearly got sold for a school seat and my mom's lungs."

"I know. I would've been tempted too." He said it without judgment, which somehow made it land harder. "The question isn't whether you wanted to belong somewhere better. Of course you did. The question is what you belong to now."

I turned the box over. The scorched casing was warm from my hands, or maybe I was warm from holding it. Somewhere across town, Oscar Fairweather was probably awake, hating herself professionally, and Davor Kest was probably awake, enjoying it, and between them they had half a truth and a lie each. Nobody had the whole thing. Nobody but the box, and me.

"Adeyemi," I said. "How fast can you get a message to Fairweather?"

"Why?"

"Because I'm done letting other people decide what my records are worth." I stood up, and my legs only shook a little, and I decided that counted. "It's time the colony kept its own ledger. Starting with this."

He looked at me for a long moment, and then he smiled, slow, like a circuit closing.

"Kid," he said, "you're going to get us all killed."

"Yeah," I said. "But honestly? After today, erased feels worse."
