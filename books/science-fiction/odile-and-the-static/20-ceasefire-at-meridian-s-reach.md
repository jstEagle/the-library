## 20. Ceasefire at Meridian's Reach

*Oscar*

The broadcast array had a heartbeat. I'd learned that in my first week on Meridian's Reach, twenty years after anyone bothered to maintain it — a low, arrhythmic thrum through the floor of the relay station, like something enormous sleeping badly. Adeyemi said it was the coolant pumps. Tam said it was the station dreaming. I'd recorded both explanations in my ledger and flagged neither as verified.

Now the heartbeat was the only sound in the world, because everything else had stopped.

I sat on the floor of the relay station with my back against a console older than the colony's charter, watching two fleets' worth of tactical displays bleed across every working screen we'd spliced into the system. Kest had done the splicing. He did it with the serene competence of a man committing his fourth felony of the day, humming something that might have been a Halcyon naval hymn or might have been a drinking song about an admiral's wife. I chose not to ask.

The data was out. All of it. The black box. My original recordings from the foundry, timestamp anomalies and all. And the confession — mine, signed, notarized by Adeyemi with a stamp he'd liberated from a defunct fish cannery, which felt appropriately Meridian's Reach. Nine seconds of truth, plus twenty years of context, beamed simultaneously to the Meridian fleet command channel and the Halcyon equivalent, encrypted so plainly that decryption was less a barrier than a formality. Kest called it "broadcasting in the open." I called it shouting into both ears at once so neither could claim it hadn't heard.

For eleven minutes, nothing happened.

I counted the minutes the way I counted everything now — against the truce clock, which stood at nineteen hours when I pressed transmit. Nineteen hours until two armadas were legally permitted to stop pretending they weren't aiming at each other.

"Silence is good," Kest said, without conviction.

"Silence is what happens before either bad thing," I said. "Ask any actuary."

"You're a ray of light, Fairweather."

"I investigate collapses for a living. Light is above my pay grade."

Tam was up in the maintenance crawlspace with her ear against a conduit, which was how she listened to things too big to fit in rooms. She came down eventually, face smudged, eyes too bright. "Both fleets are talking," she reported. "Not to each other. To themselves. Lots of channels going quiet and then lots of channels going loud."

"That's either war councils or someone's cafeteria caught fire," Adeyemi said.

Nobody laughed. We'd used up our laughter somewhere around hour three of waiting, and now we just sat in the station's heartbeat and watched screens that showed us nothing but fleet icons holding position, holding position, holding position.

Then one icon moved. Then forty.

*Reyes*

Commander Ilsa Reyes had been awake for thirty-one hours, and she had spent most of them doing arithmetic she hated.

The numbers were simple, which was the obscenity of them. Meridian fleet: ninety-one combat vessels in blockade formation. Halcyon: eighty-eight, plus the vanguard squadrons already trading fire over the colony — six confirmed dead, four of them colonist-born crew who had signed on with Halcyon because Meridian's Reach couldn't feed its own children, let alone employ them. Six dead over a weapons test that had never been authorized, never been acknowledged, and nearly collapsed the gravity well under a civilian population of eleven thousand people.

Her own people. Her own order. She had signed the test authorization routing herself, back when it was a theoretical exercise with a probability table attached, back before the probability table turned out to have been optimistic.

The broadcast had reached *Meridian Ascendant* at 0417. Admiral Voss had read it in silence, all of it — the black box telemetry, the confession, the testimony of a teenage scavenger and an old engineer and an insurance investigator whose name Reyes had come to hate and then, in some chamber of herself she refused to inspect, to respect. Then Voss had looked up at his war council and asked one question:

"How many of you were born down there?"

Four hands went up. Out of eleven officers. Reyes hadn't known that. In twenty years of service she had never once thought to ask where her officers came from, because the fleet taught you that origins were ballast — jettison them and rise.

Voss had dismissed the council. That was the other obscene part. No orders. Just dismissal, and a man sitting alone with a screen full of his own crime, and a ship full of colonist-born crew who could all read.

Now she stood in the corridor outside the wardroom, listening to the ship talk to itself. Not the official channels — those were silent, frozen, waiting for an admiral to become an admiral again. The unofficial ones. The galley channel. The maintenance nets. The private frequencies where enlisted crew talked when they thought no one senior was listening, except that everyone senior had been enlisted once and remembered the frequencies.

They weren't angry. That was what undid her. She had prepared for anger — mutiny even, the clean drama of it, something she could answer with authority. Instead they were ashamed. She heard a petty officer named Okonkwo, colonist-born, third generation, say to his shift: "My mother's house is in the blast radius. They knew. It's in the file. They *knew*, and they ran the test anyway, and we were going to go die protecting them for it."

And another voice, Halcyon-born, a transfer: "We were going to bomb them for it."

Silence on the channel. The specific silence of people discovering they are on the same side of a shame.

Reyes leaned her forehead against the bulkhead. Somewhere below her, eleven thousand civilians were going about their morning — hauling water, bartering salvage, teaching children in buildings with failing scrubbers — while above them two fleets discovered, in real time, that there was no version of this story in which either of them got to be the hero. That was the design. That was Fairweather's design. Broadcast identical truth to identical audiences and let symmetry do the work that law couldn't. Neither fleet could claim advantage. Neither could claim innocence. There was no first-mover benefit in mutual disgrace.

It was elegant. It was the kind of solution Reyes would have proposed herself, if she hadn't spent nine months trying to murder it.

The alert tone sounded. Fleet-wide. She straightened, heart doing something unsoldierly.

Admiral Voss's voice, raw and unpolished, the voice of a man reading rather than commanding:

"All Meridian fleet units: stand down from blockade posture. Repeat, stand down. Vanguard squadrons, cease fire and hold. This is not a drill, and it is not a trick — Halcyon has transmitted matching stand-down confirmation on the open channel. Effective immediately, Commander Ilsa Reyes is relieved of duty and remanded to fleet security pending charges under articles—" and here his voice caught, actually caught, "—under articles I signed."

The two Marines who took her arm were polite about it. One of them, she noticed, was colonist-born. He wouldn't meet her eyes, but he also didn't grip harder than regulation required, and she understood that too. That was the sentence the colony would pass on all of them, eventually: not hatred, just the refusal to look away.

As they walked her toward detention, the ship's public address carried the last thing she heard for a long time: the galley channel, open, unencrypted, a hundred voices not cheering, not mourning, just breathing — the collective exhale of ninety-one ships' worth of people who had spent nineteen hours expecting to die and had instead been handed something worse and better.

The truth. Unmanaged. Shared.

Reyes had spent her whole career believing information was ammunition. Standing in the corridor, wrists cuffed, she finally understood the flaw in the doctrine: ammunition gets spent. Truth just sits there, in everyone's hands at once, heavy as gravity, steady as gravity, refusing to take sides.

She almost laughed. She didn't. The Marine might have misunderstood it as remorse, and she wasn't ready to find out whether it was.

*Kest*

Davor Kest had built a career on the principle that everyone lies, and that the lie is always more interesting than the truth. It had made him famous, wealthy by investigator standards, and entirely alone, all of which he'd considered a fair exchange rate.

So it was disorienting, standing in the relay station amid stolen equipment and cannery notarization stamps, to watch the truth work exactly as advertised. No leverage. No angle. Just the raw file, delivered to both sides at once, doing the one thing no forged evidence could ever do: making lying pointless.

He'd spent twenty years proving lies. He had never once seen a truth land cleanly. He filed the experience under *anomalies requiring further study* and allowed himself, briefly, to enjoy it.

"Kest." Oscar's voice, hoarse. "Halcyon's moving."

His stomach dropped — and then the display resolved, and he saw what she meant. Not the vanguard pressing the attack. The whole line, eighty-eight ships, rotating out of blockade geometry into something looser, wider, the posture of a fleet that had stopped expecting to fight and hadn't yet decided what to do with its hands. On the open channel, Halcyon Command transmitted a single line, unencrypted, addressed to no one and therefore to everyone:

*"Stand-down confirmed. Our crews include your children. There will be no war over a grave we dug together."*

Kest read it twice. Sentimental. Unprofessional. Almost certainly drafted by committee. It was also, he suspected, the most honest document Halcyon Command had produced in a decade, and it had taken an insurance fraud and a teenage scavenger to extract it.

"They're stalling," Tam said from the crawlspace hatch. "War councils are still running. Nobody's declared anything. They're just... standing there."

"Of course they're standing there," Kest said. "You've presented two governments with a problem their entire architecture can't process. Every protocol they have assumes an adversary. You've deleted the adversary. Now they have to invent a new category from scratch, live, in front of their crews." He smiled despite himself. "Bureaucracy has saved more lives than heroism ever has. Today it's earning its keep."

Oscar was staring at the display, at the two fleets hanging in the dark above the colony like weather that had forgotten how to break. "It's not over," she said. "Stalling isn't peace. Someone down here still has to account for me. For the forgery. For all of it."

"Yes," Kest agreed pleasantly. "Someone does. Fortunately, I've prepared extensive documentation of your crimes. Very thorough. Practically a confession."

"You kept the file on me?"

"Fairweather. I'm an investigator. Keeping files is the only prayer I know." He tapped the console. "Besides — whatever tribunal ends up with you, they'll want my corroboration. And I intend to give it. Fully. Against you, where warranted, and for you, where earned. Which is, I believe, the entire point of what you did."

She looked at him then — exhausted, ash-faced, twenty years of careful professional distance burned off her like paint in atmosphere — and Kest recognized the expression, because he'd worn it once himself, long ago, in a hearing room on a world he'd helped save and lost anyway.

Relief. The terrible kind. The kind that arrives only after you've stopped being able to carry something alone, and discovers you no longer have to.

Above them, the fleets held position. Below them, the river ran. Between them, the truth sat in every hand at once, and no one — not yet — knew what to do with a weapon that couldn't be fired.

* * *

*Tam*

Tam Okafor watched the sky from the wreckage fields, because that was where you went when you needed to see something clearly. The fields had taught her everything about seeing: what a hull looked like when it failed honestly, what a lie looked like welded over a stress fracture, what a whole history looked like when nobody wrote it down.

Two fleets hung overhead. She could see them as faint moving stars, wrong-colored, too many, arranged in patterns no salvage field had ever shown her. Nineteen hours ago those stars had been aimed at her home. Now they were just... there. Holding. Like the whole sky was holding its breath along with everybody else.

Adeyemi came out and stood beside her, and for a while neither of them said anything, because the fields didn't require talking. That was the best thing about the fields.

"My mum's going to want to know why I'm not packing," Tam said finally. "Everyone down in the settlement's packing. Half of them think the ceasefire's a trick."

"It might be," Adeyemi said comfortably. "Ceasefires usually are, at first. Then enough people decide not to be tricked, and they stop being tricks and start being Tuesdays."

Tam snorted. But she kept looking up, and what she felt wasn't hope exactly — hope was a young person's word and she was fifteen and had already learned to price it accordingly. What she felt was weight. The black box was gone from her hiding place, given away, and somehow she felt lighter and heavier both, the way you feel after you set down something you've carried so long your spine grew around it.

She'd found it in the wreckage because she belonged to the wreckage. That was the thing nobody up in those fleets understood, the thing the file had proven and the file still couldn't contain: the colony had always kept its own records. Scorched modules. Countdown memories. A scavenger's catalog of every failure the insurers logged and then abandoned. Two fleets had fought over who owned the truth, and the whole time it had been growing wild out here, free, in the dirt, in a teenager's lockbox.

Below the hill, the settlement lights were coming on for evening. Ordinary lights. Lights that assumed tomorrow.

"Come on," Adeyemi said. "Fairweather will want statements. Apparently we're witnesses now instead of salvage."

"Is that a promotion?"

"In this economy? Absolutely."

They walked down through the fields together, past the hulls that had failed honestly, past the module that had started everything, and overhead the two fleets hung motionless in the dark — not at peace yet, not at war anymore, just two enormous, armed, embarrassed institutions waiting to find out what happened next.

And down in the settlement, in a relay station with a heartbeat, an insurance investigator opened her ledger to a fresh page and began, carefully, to write down what the colony remembered.
