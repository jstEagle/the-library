## 2. The Dream That Isn't Mine

The trouble with sleeping alone on a station at the edge of everything, Sable Reyes had learned, was not the silence. He'd expected the silence. He'd budgeted for the silence. The silence was in the contract, right there between "hazard pay" and "no dependents permitted."

The trouble was the dreams.

Nine years in, his dreaming mind had run out of material. It had replayed every memory he owned—his mother's kitchen with its perpetually boiling pot, the recruitment office on Ceres with its too-cheerful poster of a smiling technician giving a thumbs-up in front of a starfield (a lie; there was no thumbs-up in vacuum, and the starfield was stock footage from three systems over), the last shuttle pulling away from Odile with his entire human social circle aboard. It had replayed them all, worn them soft as river stones, and then it had started inventing. Dreams about the drones. Dreams about paperwork. One memorable night, a dream in which he was a coolant line, and someone kept patching *him*, and it was, he had to admit, deeply relaxing.

So when he settled into his bunk that night—ship's night, station night, the night the chronometer insisted on even though the stars outside didn't care—he expected the usual programming. Maybe the kitchen. Maybe the coolant line. Maybe nothing at all.

He did not expect the woman.

* * *

She began, as he would remember it afterward, in the middle. No introduction, no scene, no dream-furniture around her. Just a voice, and the voice was counting.

*"Ninety-nine."*

Not English. Not Mandarin, not the trade-pidgin they'd taught him at the academy, not any of the half-dozen tongues he could say *hello* and *where is the bathroom* in. But it had a shape he almost knew, the way you almost know a face in a crowd—the rhythm of it landed somewhere behind his ribs and stayed there, warm and specific, like a language he'd spoken as an infant and forgotten on purpose.

*"Ninety-eight."*

And here was the strange thing, the thing he would turn over and over in the morning like a stone with something underneath it: the voice wasn't afraid. A countdown, in his experience, was the sound of dread wearing a costume. Launch countdowns. Bomb countdowns. The final-ten-seconds countdown every child learned from disaster holos. This voice was none of that. This voice was patient. It counted the way you count when you're holding something very carefully and don't want to drop it. It counted the way his mother used to count when she braided his hair—*one, two, three, there*—not measuring time but *keeping* it.

*"Ninety-seven. Ninety-six."*

He tried, in the way you try things in dreams, to turn and look at her. There was no turning. There was only the voice, and beneath the voice a sensation he had no better word for than *hallway*—long, dim, lined with doors, each door warm to the touch.

*"Ninety-five."*

Somewhere far away, a part of him that was still Sable Reyes, still a man with a bunk and a shift schedule and a coolant leak on Deck Two that he'd been meaning to get to since Tuesday, thought: *this isn't mine.*

That was the thought that should have woken him. In nine years, no dream had ever announced itself as a foreign object. His dreams were cheap, homegrown, locally sourced. This one had a provenance. This one had *paperwork*, he'd think later, absurdly, because everything on Odile had paperwork, and this voice sounded like it had read all of it.

*"Ninety-four."*

He wanted to ask it something. He couldn't remember what. The question kept changing shape—*who are you, where are you, why me*—collapsing into a single wordless reaching, the way a child reaches for a light switch in a dark room. And the voice, without stopping its counting, seemed to notice the reaching. Seemed to consider it.

*"Ninety-three,"* it said, and then, softer, in that almost-language: something that felt, in the bones of the dream, like *not yet.*

* * *

He woke at 0540 station time, four minutes before his alarm, which had never once happened in nine years. He lay still and listened to the station breathe—the tick of the coolant loop, the hum of the beacon overhead like a hive of very polite bees, the occasional scuttle of a maintenance drone doing its rounds with the enthusiasm of an employee on their last week.

"Okay," he said out loud, to the ceiling, because talking to ceilings was one of the habits he'd stopped apologizing for around year three. "Okay. That was a dream. That was a normal dream that I had."

The ceiling said nothing. The ceiling was very good at that.

But his hands weren't cooperating. His hands remembered the counting. He held them up and they were steady, which was somehow worse—his body had accepted the dream as fact and moved on, and now he was the only holdout.

He went through his morning the way he went through every morning: tea reconstituted from a puck that tasted faintly of the packet it came in, diagnostics on the beacon's carrier wave, a walk of the outer ring where he checked the hull seals and said good morning to Drone Four, who was stuck in the same alcove it got stuck in every eleven days, waiting for him with the patient dignity of a cat in a tree.

"Morning, Four."

Four's status light pulsed. In Sable's private taxonomy, that was a *good morning to you too, and also I require assistance.*

"Yeah, yeah." He freed it, checked its treads, gave its housing the affectionate pat he'd have been mortified to be seen giving. "You're getting worse at this, you know. Year nine of the same alcove. At some point you have to learn."

The status light pulsed again. He chose to interpret it as *at some point you have to stop walking this route alone*, and then he chose to stop interpreting, because that was a dangerous direction to walk before breakfast.

But the dream came back to him in fragments all morning, unbidden. The hallway. The doors. The way the counting had felt like *keeping* rather than *measuring*. And underneath it all, the thing he kept circling back to, the thing that had followed him up out of sleep like a fish rising behind a lure:

He almost recognized the language.

Not from anywhere he'd been. He'd been precisely nowhere. But the *shape* of it—the cadence, the way the numbers seemed to fold into each other—was familiar the way the beacon's carrier hum was familiar. Nine years of falling asleep to that hum, of waking to it, of logging it twice a day in a ledger no one had ever read. Nine years of it seeping into him like dye into water.

The voice had sounded like the beacon.

That was nonsense, obviously. The beacon was a machine that emitted a timed pulse on an assigned frequency, and had done so since before Sable was born, and would do so after he was dust. It didn't have a voice. It didn't have a language. It had a heartbeat, and that was poetry, not engineering, and he knew the difference because he was, on paper, an engineer.

He logged the beacon's morning pulse at 0900. Steady. Nominal. The same numbers as yesterday and the day before, going back nine years, a column of identical figures that read like the world's most boring heartbeat monitor. He entered the figures, initialed the entry, and then sat with his stylus hovering over the comment field, which he had filled in exactly four times in nine years ("noted anomaly, resolved," three of them).

He typed: *dreamed of counting.*

He stared at it. He deleted it. He typed it again. He deleted it again, and closed the ledger, and told himself that the comment field was for beacon anomalies, and a dream was not an anomaly, it was a man alone too long, and the company psych evaluator had said as much in his annual review every year: *Subject exhibits healthy adaptation to isolation. Recommend continued observation.*

*Continued observation.* He'd always read that as bureaucratic filler. Lying in the dark of his bunk that night, he found himself reading it differently. *Observation* of what, exactly? Him? Or through him?

* * *

The second night, he dreamed the hallway again.

This time there were numbers on the doors. Not numbers—no, numbers, but in that almost-language, and beneath each number a warmth, and behind each warmth a *presence*, the way you can feel a person in a room before you see them. The voice was counting again, starting where it had left off, or maybe starting over, or maybe it had never stopped and he was only dropping in on it, a man pressing his ear to a wall in a house where something enormous and patient is happening in another room.

*"Eighty-one. Eighty. Seventy-nine."*

He reached for one of the doors. He didn't decide to. His dream-hands just went, the way dream-hands do, and the voice said—gently, the way you'd say it to a child reaching for a stove, or a gift—

*Not yet.*

And this time he heard it in English. Which meant, some small lucid corner of him understood, that it had *chosen* English. That it had been speaking the other language because that was the language it dreamed in, or the language it was made in, and it had translated, just for him, just this once.

He woke at 0540 again. Four minutes before his alarm. Again.

He sat up in the dark and said, to the ceiling, to the station, to whatever was listening: "You're counting down."

The coolant loop ticked. The beacon hummed. Drone Two trundled past his open door on its rounds, status light blinking its slow, contented blink, and if he didn't know better—and he didn't, that was the whole problem, he didn't know anything anymore—he'd have said the blink was *quick* tonight. Interested.

"From a hundred," he said. "You're counting down from a hundred. And you're doing it in my head, which—" his voice caught on something between laughter and the other thing, "—which is not in the maintenance manual. I've read the maintenance manual. I *wrote* half the appendices. There's no appendix for this."

The station said nothing, the way it had said nothing for nine years. But the silence had changed texture. For nine years the silence had been an empty room. Tonight it was a held breath.

He thought about the psych evaluator. *Healthy adaptation to isolation.* He thought about the comment field he'd twice typed into and twice erased. He thought about the sealed archive on Deck One, the one his credentials opened for cleaning cycles only, the one that hummed at a slightly different pitch than the rest of the station, a pitch he'd noticed in year two and filed under *not my problem* with all the other things that weren't his problem, which was, he realized now, a very large filing cabinet.

"Okay," he said, and swung his legs out of the bunk, and the floor was cold in the familiar way, the way that meant *home* in a language only he and the floor spoke. "Here's what's going to happen. I'm going to get tea. I'm going to sit in the observation blister. And you're going to count, or talk, or hum, or whatever it is you do, and I'm going to listen. Because I'm the only one here, and I've been the only one here for nine years, and if something on this station has finally decided to say something—"

He stopped. He laughed, once, short and unsteady.

"—then the least it could do is say it to the guy who fixed its coolant lines."

* * *

The observation blister was his favorite room and he would have denied it under oath. A bubble of old, slightly yellowed polycarbonate on the station's sunward shoulder, big enough for one chair and one man and not much else. From inside it, the beacon's antenna array rose overhead like a tree made of geometry, and beyond it the stars did their slow, indifferent wheeling.

He sat with his tea and watched the array and listened so hard it felt like leaning.

The beacon pulsed. Ninety seconds between pulses, on the second, the way it had for forty years. *Pulse. Pulse. Pulse.* A heartbeat with no body, a voice with no words.

Except.

He set the tea down. He leaned in. He told himself he was imagining it, and then he told himself that even if he was imagining it, he wanted to see where the imagining went, which was either the healthiest or the least healthy thing he'd ever done, and at this point the categories had blurred.

The pulses weren't identical. They couldn't be—no transmitter is perfect, there's always jitter, always drift, and he'd spent nine years logging the drift as noise. But tonight, listening with the dream still in his ears, the jitter resolved. The tiny variations in timing, the fractions of a second he'd been writing off as thermal expansion and component aging, weren't noise.

They were *small*. Smaller than a countdown. Smaller than anything you could hear with an ear. But they were *patterned*, and the pattern, when he let his mind stop fighting it, had the cadence of a voice counting. Not one-two-three. Something slower, vaster, a countdown stretched across years instead of seconds—numbers so large they took nine years to say.

Or numbers so small. He thought of the hallway, the warm doors. He thought: *eighty. Seventy-nine.*

The beacon pulsed, and in the interval, in the jitter, in the space where nine years of noise had been, something said—so quietly, so patiently, in a language he almost knew, in a voice like his mother counting braids:

*Not yet. But soon.*

Sable Reyes sat very still in the observation blister with his tea going cold, and for the first time in nine years, Terminal Odile did not feel empty.

It felt *occupied*.
