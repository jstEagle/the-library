## 11. Voss Descends

The Vigilant came out of the dark like a rumor made official.

Warden Ilsa Voss watched it happen on the approach display—Terminal Odile swelling from a pinprick to a splinter to a place, a real place where a real man had spent nine years of his one life. The station was ugly in the way old things were ugly: patched hull plating, coolant lines wrapped in mismatched insulation, a beacon array that had been repaired so many times it looked less like equipment and more like a scar that had learned to function.

"Six days of transit," said Lieutenant Okonkwo at her shoulder, "and I still don't know what we're sanitizing."

"You don't need to know," Voss said. "You need to stand where I tell you to stand."

"Yes, Warden." A pause. "For the record, I'd like to know."

"For the record, denied." She almost smiled. Okonkwo had been asking her some version of that question for six days, and she had been giving him some version of that answer, and they both understood this was not insubordination but a kind of ritual—the crew testing whether she trusted them, her demonstrating that trust was a luxury above her pay grade.

She did trust them. That was the problem. You could not sanitize a station with people you trusted unless you were very careful about which parts of yourself you brought aboard.

The briefing packet sat on her private terminal, sealed with Halcyon Orbital's corporate watermark: a stylized sun rising over a stylized planet, as if the sun needed the planet's permission. She had read it eleven times across six days. It was short. Corporate orders usually were; brevity was how you knew someone had paid lawyers to make them that way.

*Sanitize Relay Terminal Odile. Decommission residual intelligence assets. Secure witness Reyes under standard severance protocol. Log all anomalies as equipment failure.*

*Standard severance protocol.* She knew what that meant. Everyone at her level knew what that meant, and everyone below her level was carefully not told, and the whole architecture of Halcyon rested on that gradient of knowing.

"Approach complete," the helm called. "Station is answering our hails. Audio only."

"Put it through."

A crackle, then a voice—male, middle-aged, unhurried, with the particular calm of a man who had talked to no one but machines for nearly a decade and had therefore lost all fear of talking to people.

*"Vigilant, this is Terminal Odile. Sable Reyes, station keeper. You're early. Your approach window wasn't for another—" a pause, the sound of someone checking something, "—well. You're early. Do you need a berth? The number two clamp still works if you're gentle with it."*

Voss blinked. In nineteen years of enforcement work, she had been hailed with terror, with defiance, with desperate lies, once with gunfire. Never with an offer of a gentle berth.

"Reyes. This is Warden Voss, Halcyon Enforcement Division. We're coming aboard. Have your station logs ready for transfer."

*"Sure,"* he said, agreeably. *"I'll put the kettle on. We don't have tea, exactly, but I have something that used to be near tea."*

She cut the channel. Okonkwo was looking at her with the expression of a man trying very hard not to say *I told you something was weird out here.*

"Prep the boarding party," she said. "Full kit, sidearms stowed. We are not soldiers today. We are auditors."

"And if the audit goes badly?"

"Then we were never here, and neither was he."

* * *

The airlock cycled open onto warmth.

That was the first wrong thing. Enforcement vessels ran cold; stations ran colder; abandoned relay terminals in the deep dark should have been running on fumes and frost. Instead the corridor beyond the lock was a comfortable eighteen degrees, lit in soft amber, smelling faintly of machine oil and—she checked, because she didn't believe it—yes, actual brewed something. Tea-adjacent.

Sable Reyes was waiting for them at the end of the corridor, and Voss's first thought was that the briefing photos had lied by omission. They'd shown her a thin man in a gray jumpsuit, station-issue, forgettable. They had not shown her the eyes: steady, tired, and appraising, the eyes of someone who had spent nine years noticing everything because there was nothing else to do.

"Warden Voss," he said, extending a hand. His grip was dry and firm. "You have a long walk ahead of you. The station likes to be introduced to new people. It's a quirk. Humor it."

Behind him, four maintenance drones hovered in a loose formation, their sensor lights blinking in what Voss would have sworn, against all professional judgment, was curiosity.

"Your drones will remain clear of my team," she said.

"Oh, sure. They're just nosy. Bort here hasn't seen a new face since the last supply run, and that was—" he counted silently, lips moving, "—four years ago. Bort, back up. Give the nice armed auditors room."

One of the drones—a squat unit with a dented shell and a name that was clearly invented—retreated perhaps half a meter, radiating sulkiness.

Voss filed all of it away. The warmth. The drones. The man who named his machines. None of it matched the profile. The profile said: isolated technician, psychologically brittle, manageable. What stood in front of her was not brittle. What stood in front of her was a host who had decided, for reasons of his own, to be gracious, and gracious hosts were the hardest kind to process.

"This way," Reyes said. "Conference room's through here. Best view on the station. Well. Only view."

* * *

The conference room was a converted storage bay with a viewport looking out onto nothing in particular—deep space, the slow wheel of distant stars—and a table that had clearly been built by hand from salvaged paneling. He had set out cups. Six cups, for a team of five, plus himself. One of the cups was chipped and had been turned so the chip faced away from its intended drinker.

Small kindnesses, arranged deliberately. Voss had seen that before, in hostage negotiations and in hospice wards, and it always meant the same thing: *I know why you're here, and I'm going to make you look at me while you do it.*

"Reyes," she began, "you know why we've come."

"I have theories," he said pleasantly, pouring. "Most of them involve the beacon. Some of them involve paperwork. All of them involve Halcyon being unhappy about something I found or might find. Would any of those theories benefit from correction?"

"The beacon is being decommissioned. Effective immediately upon your signature." She slid the order across the table—a physical flimsi, because paper made things feel final in a way screens never did. "Along with it, your contract concludes. Severance package: full back pay, nine years' interest, relocation assistance, a housing stipend on Ceres or anywhere in the Inner Belt. Medical coverage for life. You will never work again unless you want to."

He looked at the flimsi without touching it. "And in exchange?"

"In exchange, you sign a nondisclosure annex. Standard form. You saw nothing, heard nothing, transmitted nothing. The station's records become Halcyon property. You go home a wealthy man."

"That's it? Sign and sail?"

"That's it."

He picked up his cup, blew on it, took a sip, and grimaced with genuine feeling. "Yeah, that's not tea anymore. It's achieved tea's final form, which is sadness." He set it down. "Warden, can I ask you something honest?"

"You can ask."

"Do you believe me when I say I haven't done anything yet?" He leaned forward slightly. "Because I want you to understand—I haven't broadcast anything. I haven't decrypted anything. I've been sitting on this station for nine years keeping a light on, and last month the light started talking back, and I have been *extremely* patient about it. Whatever you think I am, I'm currently just a man with questions."

"And the questions are the problem," Voss said. She kept her voice level, almost gentle. This was the part of the job she was good at, the part that had gotten her promoted past better officers: the part where you made the cage feel like a favor. "Questions, answered, become knowledge. Knowledge becomes liability. Not yours—ours. Yours too, eventually. Liability has a way of finding people, Mr. Reyes. It finds them years later, in their homes, on their comm lines. It finds their families."

Something moved behind his eyes—not fear. Recognition, maybe. The look of a man hearing a truth he'd already deduced, confirmed aloud.

"You have footage," he said quietly. "Of witnesses who refused."

Voss held his gaze. Then she nodded to Okonkwo, who produced a tablet and set it on the table between them.

"We don't show this to everyone," she said. "Consider it a courtesy. Most people get the summary. You strike me as someone who reads the full report."

She thumbed it on.

The footage was old—grainy, red-tinted by bad archival compression—but legible. A woman in a habitat module on Ganymede, a former Halcyon contractor, standing in her kitchen while enforcement officers waited politely outside her door. She had refused a severance annex eleven years ago over a mining-safety cover-up. The footage showed what happened next, in the Halcyon way: not violence, never violence. Her credit accounts frozen mid-purchase, groceries left in the checkout lane. Her medical coverage revoked during a routine procedure, the surgery halted, rescheduled, halted again. Her daughter's school tuition withdrawn. Her neighbors, fed quiet rumors, growing cold. Ninety days of a life being disassembled screw by screw while every individual action remained perfectly, provably legal, until the woman stood in her empty kitchen and signed the annex just to make the world start again.

The timestamp at the end showed the signing. She was crying, but quietly, the way you cry when you've stopped expecting help.

Voss let it play to the end. She always let it play to the end. It was the only honest thing in the entire process, and she insisted on it—for them, and for herself. If she was going to be the instrument, she would at least be a transparent one.

"That," Reyes said, "was a person."

"That was a choice," Voss said. "Hers. Made badly, at cost. The system doesn't break people, Mr. Reyes. People break themselves against it, and then the system bills them for the repairs." She tapped the decommission order. "Or they take the exit we're offering, which is genuinely generous—I want you to hear that from me and not the fine print. Nine years alone on a rock, and they're offering you a house and a pension. There are worse trades."

"There are," he agreed. "There's also a third option nobody's offering, which is where I find out what the beacon actually did, and then decide what a person ought to do about it."

Okonkwo shifted by the door. One of the younger team members, Vasquez, let her hand drift toward her stowed sidearm, and Voss caught it with a glance. No. Not today. Today they were auditors.

"Mr. Reyes." Voss folded her hands on the table. "I'm going to say this once, plainly, because you've been courteous and courtesy deserves honesty. My orders, should you refuse, are to log your death as equipment failure. I am telling you this not as a threat but as data, because you seem to be a man who values data. I have sanitized three stations in my career. I have never once enjoyed it. I would very much like for you to take the money, board my ship as a guest rather than a problem, and let us both go home having had a pleasant cup of terrible tea."

The silence stretched. Out in the corridor, one of the drones—Bort, presumably—bumped gently against the doorframe, and Reyes glanced toward the sound with something that was almost affection.

"You really would do it," he said. "Log me as a coolant leak."

"I really would file the paperwork," Voss said. "Whether anything happens after the paperwork is... rarely up to me."

He nodded slowly. Then, to her surprise, he picked up the decommission order and set it neatly in front of him, squaring its edges against the table.

"Okay," he said. "Let's talk terms. But I want it on the record that under clause fourteen of my original contract—which I reread last night, all forty pages, fascinating document—any termination of my posting requires a seventy-two-hour review period before execution. Seventy-two hours, Warden. Company-standard. It's in there. Page thirty-one, subsection C. I checked twice."

Voss felt the floor tilt, very slightly, beneath a conversation she had believed she was steering.

"That clause applies to performance terminations," she said.

"It applies to *all* terminations. The word 'performance' appears nowhere in subsection C. I looked." He smiled—it was a tired smile, an apologetic one, the smile of a man who had spent nine years with nothing to do but prepare for exactly this. "Seventy-two hours, Warden. And during a review period, per clause nine, all station systems—including the beacon—are required to maintain full operational status and full *records*. Nothing gets wiped. Nothing gets sanitized. Legally, the station has to stay exactly as interesting as it is right now."

Somewhere in the walls—Voss would have sworn it, though Okonkwo shot her a look that said *you imagined that*—somewhere in the amber-lit corridors of Terminal Odile, a ventilation fan cycled on with a sound that was almost, almost, a sigh of relief.

Reyes picked up his terrible tea and raised the cup an inch, a toast to absolutely nothing.

"So," he said. "Seventy-two hours. Can I interest anyone in a tour?"
