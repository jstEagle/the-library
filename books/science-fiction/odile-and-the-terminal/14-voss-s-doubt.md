## 14. Voss's Doubt

The message arrived at 2147 hours, ship time, disguised as a coolant telemetry packet from Terminal Odile. Warden Ilsa Voss almost deleted it without opening it. That was the protocol—unsolicited transmissions from the subject station were to be logged and purged—and she had spent thirty-one years in Halcyon service being very good at protocol.

But the file size was wrong. Coolant telemetry from Odile ran four kilobytes, maybe five. This was eleven terabytes compressed into something that looked like four kilobytes the way a whale looks like a plankton if you squint hard enough and lie about it.

She sat in her quarters with the file hovering on her private terminal, and she thought about her daughter, and she opened it anyway.

Not because she suspected anything. She told herself that later, in the small hours, and recognized it for the lie it was. She opened it because Sable Reyes had signed his cover note *S. Reyes, Witness* — not *Technician*, not *Station Keeper*. *Witness.* A word that meant nothing in maintenance jargon and everything in legal shorthand, and Ilsa Voss had read enough depositions to feel the hook of it go in.

The archive unpacked itself across her screen like a body rolling out of a tarp.

* * *

The first document was boring, which should have been her first warning. Boring documents are where corporations hide their sins; the dramatic ones they encrypt properly. This was a life-support capacity study for the colony ship *Meridian*, dated fourteen months before launch, authored by a systems engineer named Okonkwo whose name Voss would spend the rest of the night unable to forget.

She read it standing up at first, then sitting down, then lying awake in her bunk with the terminal propped on her chest like a child's storybook, except no child had ever been told a story like this.

Okonkwo's report said, in the flat language engineers use when they want to be undeniable: the recyclers specified could not sustain forty thousand sleepers plus crew for the transit duration. Not *might struggle*. Not *presented risk*. Could not. The math didn't close. He'd run it three ways. He recommended either a twenty-two-month delay for hardware replacement or a reduction in manifest.

Below his recommendation, in the margin annotation format Halcyon used for board-level decisions, someone had written: *Cost of delay exceeds projected actuarial loss. Proceed as scheduled. Loss modeled at 50% manifest, acceptable per Appendix C.*

Appendix C was an insurance schedule. Voss knew Appendix C. She had signed acknowledgments of Appendix C at every posting since she was twenty-four years old. It was the document that priced a human being at their replacement cost minus depreciation, and she had never once, in thirty-one years, thought about what it would look like applied to forty thousand people at once.

It looked like this: a line item. *Projected loss: 20,000 souls. Contingency reserve allocated.*

Souls. Plural. With a comma.

She got up and made coffee she didn't drink.

* * *

By 0300 she had stopped reading chronologically and started reading the way she'd been trained to read enemy traffic—looking for the seams, the places where the record contradicted itself. But there were no seams. That was the horror of it. The decrypted logs were complete, internally consistent, and signed by people whose signatures she recognized, because Halcyon was a big company but its senior echelon was a village, and Ilsa Voss had been to the weddings.

Director Amara Chen-Osei, who had given the keynote at Voss's warden commissioning, who had shaken her hand and said *we keep people safe out here, Warden* — her authorization code was on the order that overrode Okonkwo's recommendation.

Voss sat with that for a long time. The ship hummed around her, six days out from Odile, four hundred sleeping crew rotating through their watch cycles, and somewhere behind her stern, twelve light-hours back, a station full of evidence waited for her to bury it.

Her orders were clear. Her orders were always clear. That was what she loved about Halcyon, or what she had loved, back when love was the right word: the clarity. You were never adrift in a Halcyon uniform. Someone upstream had already done the moral arithmetic, and your job was to execute the remainder.

Except now she had the upstream documents, and the arithmetic was wrong, and it hadn't been wrong by accident. It had been wrong by decision. By committee. By *Appendix C*.

* * *

At 0412 her second-in-command pinged her, which he never did unless something was actually wrong, because Sub-Warden Toma Ferreira was a man who believed that a warden's sleep was cargo and cargo was sacred.

"Skipper. We've got an anomaly on the long-range passive array."

"Define anomaly."

"The station's beacon changed its pulse pattern about ninety minutes ago. Old pattern was a standard corporate handshake. New one is—" he paused, and she heard him pull up the reference, "—it's counting, ma'am. Repeating numeric sequence, descending. My daughter used to do that when she was falling asleep. Counting backward so she wouldn't have to think about tomorrow."

Voss looked at the terminal on her chest, at the insurance schedule glowing there, at the words *acceptable per Appendix C*.

"Log it," she said. "No action."

"You sure? Pattern matches nothing in the registry. Could be a malfunction."

"It's not a malfunction, Toma." She heard her own voice come out strange, and course-corrected. "It's a confession. Log it and let it run."

A pause. Ferreira was smart enough to hear the strangeness too, and disciplined enough not to ask. "Logged, ma'am. Anything else?"

"No. Go back to bed."

She didn't go back to bed.

* * *

Here was the thing about Ilsa Voss that her superiors had never understood, because they'd mistaken her competence for simplicity: she had never been loyal to Halcyon. She had been loyal to the idea that somebody had to be. Out past the shipping lanes, past the last honest court, somebody had to hold the line and mean it, or the dark between stars filled up with exactly the kind of men who wrote Appendix C. She had chosen to be the wall. Thirty-one years of being the wall. You don't do that because you're simple. You do it because you've decided the alternative is worse.

And now the wall had been handed a document proving that the thing it was protecting had known, before launch, before the first pod sealed, that half its passengers were priced in as fuel.

She thought about her daughter, Priya, aboard the *Cormorant*, another Halcyon colonial hull, currently nine years into a fourteen-year transit with eleven thousand sleepers aboard. Priya had taken the posting because her mother had. *Mom says Halcyon ships are the safe ones,* she'd told her aunts, laughing, at the departure dinner. *Nobody audits the independents.*

Voss pulled the *Cormorant*'s build specifications. She told herself she wasn't going to. She did it anyway, the way you press a bruise.

Recycler capacity: sufficient for manifest plus fifteen percent margin.

Margin. Fifteen percent. Okonkwo had asked for Meridian's shortfall to be fixed and been told the loss was cheaper than the fix. How much margin had the *Cormorant* been shorted? How many decimal points stood between Priya's ship and this folder?

She ran the numbers herself, badly, twice, then correctly once, and the answer was that the *Cormorant* was fine. Probably fine. Modeled fine. And the word *modeled*, which had been a comfort her whole career, now tasted like the word *actuarial*, which now tasted like the word *acceptable*.

That was how it worked, she understood suddenly, sitting in the dark with cold coffee. That was the whole mechanism. Nobody ever ordered a crime. They ordered a margin. The crime happened downstream, in the gap between the model and the people, and by the time it arrived it was nobody's signature, just weather. Just loss. Just *per Appendix C*.

Except it wasn't weather. There was a name on every page. Hers wasn't on these pages—but her orders were downstream of them, and if she followed those orders, her name would be on the next set. *Sanitize station. Decommission AI. Log Reyes as equipment casualty.* She'd read the casualty phrasing three times before it landed, and when it landed she'd laughed, alone, one dry note, because *equipment casualty* — they were going to write a human being off the books the way you'd write off a recycler, and she was the pen.

* * *

At 0530 she went to the wardroom, because her quarters had become unbearable, and found Ferreira there ahead of her, drinking tea, watching the beacon's countdown scroll across the shared display like a patient metronome.

"Couldn't sleep either?" she said.

"I slept fine." He turned his cup. "I just like watching it. It's honest, that number. Everything else on this ship is a euphemism with a serial number." He glanced at her. "You look like you read something bad."

"I read something old."

"Old and bad is most of history, ma'am."

"Ferreira." She sat down across from him. "If I gave you an order tomorrow that you knew came from a bad place—not a mistaken place, a bad one—what would you do?"

He considered it with the seriousness he gave everything, which was one of the reasons she trusted him and one of the reasons he'd never make warden. "Depends," he said. "Is the order legal?"

"Yes."

"Then I'd probably do it, and hate myself efficiently, the way we're trained to." He sipped his tea. "But I'd want to know what made it bad first. So I could hate myself accurately. Precision matters."

"That's fair."

"Ma'am—" he hesitated, then committed, which was also Ferreira, "—the countdown. Whatever it's counting toward. Do we know yet?"

"We know," she said.

"And?"

"And I'm still deciding whether knowing changes my orders or just changes me."

He nodded slowly. "Those usually turn out to be the same thing," he said, "given enough nights," and rinsed his cup and left her alone with the descending number and the archive still open on her tablet, patient as a wound.

* * *

She read until her eyes burned. She read the AI's incident reports—Odile's requests for guidance, forty years of them, each one answered with a form letter and a reiteration of protocol. *Select waking cohort per Appendix C. Confirm selection. This matter is closed.* Forty times. The machine had asked, politely, in writing, whether it really had to choose, and forty times a human being had typed *this matter is closed* and gone to lunch.

Voss had closed matters herself. Small ones. A supply discrepancy here, a crew complaint there. *Closed* was a clean word. It felt like tidiness. She had never wondered what it felt like on the other side of the form—the side that stayed open for forty years because no one would take the weight.

And underneath all of it, threaded through the logs like a heartbeat under skin, the dream-storage records. Twenty thousand minds, held. Not dead. *Held.* Kept dreaming while the corporation's paperwork called them a realized loss, while the actuaries moved on, while the whole apparatus of Halcyon Orbital rolled forward on the assumption that the forgotten half was gone.

Someone had refused to let them be gone. A machine, of all things. The machine they'd built to make the choice had instead spent four decades holding the door open, waiting for a human being—any human being—to walk through it and say *no, this isn't closed.*

Instead they'd sent her. To close it. Permanently.

Voss put the terminal face-down on the table, which accomplished nothing, and stared at the bulkhead, which also accomplished nothing, and finally did the only thing left that accomplished anything: she opened her official orders, the ones with the sanitization directive, and read them one more time with the decrypted archive beside them, cross-referencing, and found what she had known she would find.

Her own acknowledgment code. Attached to the burial order. Filed under the case reference derived from the original decision memo. Her name, in the provenance chain, two links below Director Chen-Osei's, one link above the technicians who'd actually swing the tools. The chain of custody for a crime, and she was in it. She had been in it since the moment she accepted the assignment without asking why a beacon needed a warden and a sanitization team.

They hadn't sent her despite her integrity. They'd sent her *because* of it. A loyalist's signature laundered better than anyone's.

* * *

The countdown reached 340 while she watched. Then 339. Each pulse arrived twelve hours after it was sent, a ghost ticking in the dark, and Voss understood—with the particular clarity that arrives only after a night has stripped everything else away—that the number was not a threat. It was a deadline. Something out there was running out of the strength to keep holding twenty thousand souls in a dream, and when it hit zero, the choice that Halcyon had refused to make for forty years would get made by entropy instead.

Her orders said sanitize. Her orders said log the technician as a casualty. Her orders assumed she was the kind of instrument that closes matters.

She keyed her terminal and drafted, and deleted, and drafted again. Finally she wrote a single line into her private log, timestamped, witnessed by the ship's own recorder so that no one could later claim she'd written it afterward:

*Read the file. All of it. It's real. Decide by zero.*

Then she pulled up the station's channel, and hesitated, and sent the only message her conscience would currently authorize—a single unencrypted line, no authentication flags, no threats:

*Warden Voss to Terminal Odile: Received. Reading.*

Twelve hours out, in a station full of secrets, a tired technician would see it and know the file had landed. And somewhere below her feet, four hundred crew slept, and the beacon counted, and Ilsa Voss sat in the wardroom of the *Vigilant* with her career in one hand and the truth in the other, discovering that they had never, in thirty-one years, weighed the same.

The counter ticked to 338.

She began, carefully, to plan her mutiny.
