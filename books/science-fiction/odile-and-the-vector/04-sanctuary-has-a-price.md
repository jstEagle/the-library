## 4. Sanctuary Has a Price

The fireball came down over the eastern ridge at 0300 vault time, and Yara Amberdale's first coherent thought was not *someone is dying* but *the frost-tolerant barley plots are under there.*

He stood in the observation cupola with his tea going cold in his hand and watched the smoke plume rise against the stars, feeling the peculiar double vision of a man who has spent eleven years wanting something to happen and dreading it in equal measure. The beacon hummed behind him, steady as ever, singing its little song to a fleet that had never once sung back. And now the sky had answered. The sky had answered with a burning thing.

"Right," he said to the empty room, because talking to empty rooms was one of his established competencies. "Right."

He did the arithmetic while he pulled on his field coat. A ship that size, coming down that fast, from that vector — it hadn't been aiming for the vault. It had been aiming for anywhere else and failed. Which meant either an accident, which happened even now, or a pursuit, which did not happen here, had never happened here, because nothing had ever come here at all.

He took the med kit anyway. He took the fire suppressant foam. He took the long way around through the tunnel network so he'd come up behind the wreck rather than in front of it, because eleven years of solitude had not made him stupid, only quiet.

* * *

The courier ship lay on its back in the scoured earth of the old test fields like a beetle someone had stepped on, hull split along the flank, one engine still ticking as it cooled. Small fires guttered in the barley stubble. Yara put them out first — habit, and also because if he was about to have the most consequential conversation of his life he wanted his hands busy for it — and then he crouched by the breach in the hull with the med kit open on his knee and listened.

Inside: breathing. Ragged, wet at the edges, but breathing.

"I'm not armed," he called into the dark of the hull. "Well. I'm armed with pruning shears, but I want to be honest about how much good they'd do."

Silence. Then a voice, low and scraped raw: "Identify yourself."

"Yara Amberdale. Keeper of the Meridian Seed Vault." He hesitated, then added, because it seemed suddenly important, "Population one."

A pause. "That's not possible. This is a Directorate listening post. Beacon Seven-Alpha is a classification-three surveillance asset."

Yara looked up at the sky, where his beacon's signal pulsed invisibly onward, faithful as a heartbeat. "It's a lighthouse," he said. "I've maintained it for eleven years. It says hello to the fleet every six hours and the fleet has never said anything back. If it's also a surveillance asset, then I'm afraid I've been the world's most diligent unpaid janitor for a spy."

"You're lying."

"I'm really not. Are you bleeding? You sound like you're bleeding."

More silence, and then movement inside the wreck — a scraping, a curse in a language Yara knew only from brittle archive fragments and eleven years of practicing aloud to no one, a language that had been dead for two centuries and should not have been in anyone's living mouth. Old Meridian. She had cursed at him in Old Meridian.

His hands went cold around the med kit handle.

The hatch ground open a hand's width and stopped. In the gap: one eye, dark and ferociously awake, set in a face gray with pain and soot. A woman, maybe his age, maybe younger — hard to tell when someone has recently been on fire.

"Step back," she said, in accented but functional Standard this time. "Slowly. Show me your hands."

He showed her his hands. They were, he noted with distant embarrassment, still holding the med kit, which somewhat undermined the gesture, so he set it down between them instead, on the scorched earth, and pushed it toward the hatch with two fingers.

"There's a coagulant and a bone-setter splint in there," he said. "And a thermal blanket that smells faintly of rosemary, because I store it next to the herb cultures. I'm going to step back ten paces now. Please don't die while I'm doing it. It would be very anticlimactic after the landing."

Something flickered across her face that might, in a person less obviously furious and terrified, have been the beginning of amusement. Then she dragged herself out through the breach.

She was worse than the voice had suggested. Her left leg was wrong below the knee, her flight suit was charred along one side, and she moved with the deliberate economy of someone who had catalogued every injury and decided none of them were fatal yet. She took the med kit without taking her eyes off him, and she kept a jagged shard of hull metal in her right hand the whole time, which Yara felt was fair.

"Eleven years," she said. "You've been here eleven years?"

"Yes."

"Alone."

"Except for approximately four hundred thousand seed varieties and a cryo-garden that doesn't talk back. Though I'll admit the tomatoes have presence."

The eye narrowed. "Nobody is alone for eleven years. Nobody is left somewhere for eleven years unless they're meant to be there. You're bait. Or you're a handler. Either way—" She shifted her weight off the bad leg, and even hurt half to death she managed to make the hull shard look like a serious argument. "Either way, you should know I didn't come alone, and the people following me will be less polite than I am."

"That's a remarkable sentence," Yara said, "because I watched you arrive entirely alone, on fire, upside down. Unless your colleagues are also fire, in which case I've already extinguished them and we can move on."

Her jaw tightened. But he saw it land — the small recalculation behind her eyes, the moment when a person running checks their pursuer's logic against the evidence and finds the evidence inconveniently calm. A man planted here as bait would not make jokes about tomatoes. Probably. Although, she was clearly thinking, a truly deep-cover operative might.

"The beacon," she said instead. "Say something about the beacon. Something only its keeper would know."

"Its phase modulator drifts four hundredths of a degree every cycle and I've been compensating by hand since year three because the replacement part hasn't been manufactured in sixty years," Yara said. "Its housing is original Meridian-era construction, which means it whistles in the wind from the north in a key I can only describe as mournful. And last month a family of burrowing lizards got into the coolant channel and I spent four days evicting them and felt genuinely terrible about it." He paused. "I don't know what any of that proves to you, but it's all true, and the lizard part cost me real sleep."

For a long moment she just looked at him. Then, slowly, she lowered the hull shard — not all the way, but enough — and let herself slide down against the wreck's flank until she was sitting in the dirt, leg out straight, breath hissing between her teeth.

"My name is Odile Voss," she said. "And I need to tell you that helping me is the worst decision available to you."

* * *

They got her inside through the service tunnels, which took forty minutes and cost Yara two ribs' worth of bruising and most of his dignity, since the stretcher rig was designed for seed crates and Odile weighed considerably more than a seed crate and had opinions about being handled like one. ("If you say 'fragile contents' one more time—") By the time they reached the infirmary annex — a room Yara had kept stocked and dusted for eleven years against a need he'd told himself was hypothetical — dawn was graying the observation ports.

He set the bone while she bit down on a strap of her own flight suit and refused, with visible effort, to make a sound. Afterward she lay pale and sweating on the cot while he ran the diagnostic scanner, and the silence between them had a different texture than before. Less like a standoff. More like two people standing in the same rubble, looking at it.

"You keep a fully equipped medical bay," she said finally. "In a seed vault. For one man."

"For emergencies."

"What kind of emergency were you expecting?"

"Honestly?" Yara peeled off his gloves. "I think I was expecting you. Not you specifically. Just — somebody. Eventually. Eleven years is a long time to keep a spare bed made without admitting why."

Odile turned her head and looked at him for a long time, and whatever she saw made her look away first, out the port, at the light coming up over the ruined test fields and the smoking wound of her ship.

"They'll come here," she said. "The ones chasing me. Maybe not today. Maybe not this week. But they'll come, and when they do, this place stops being hidden. That's what I bring you. That's the price of the bed."

"I know."

"Do you? Because you've been safe for eleven years. You've been *nothing* to anyone for eleven years, and I promise you that feels like peace, and I am asking you to burn it down before breakfast."

Yara sat down on the stool beside the cot. He thought about saying something brave. What came out instead was the truth, which was braver and more embarrassing.

"I've spent eleven years telling myself that being invisible was the same as being content," he said. "I cataloged seeds. I talked to plants. I convinced myself the fleet's silence was a kind of trust — that they knew I was here, keeping the light on, and simply didn't need to answer. It was a comfortable story. And then last night I heard you cursing at me in a language that everyone agrees is dead, and I realized I don't actually know what's comfortable anymore. I just know what's true, and I haven't met much of it in a decade."

Odile's expression did something complicated. Suspicion was still in it — suspicion would probably live in that face permanently, the way weather lives in certain coastlines — but underneath it, for the first time, was something almost like grief for him, which was such an unexpected direction for pity to come from that Yara nearly laughed.

"You learned fragments of Old Meridian," she said. "From forbidden archives. Alone. Why?"

"Because the records said the language never existed. There was no Meridian colony, no massacre, no tongue — official history has a hole shaped exactly like it, and everyone walks past the hole without looking down. I couldn't stop looking down." He rubbed his eyes. "I told myself it was scholarly curiosity. It wasn't. It was loneliness. Dead languages are company. Someone had to speak them."

The infirmary was quiet. Somewhere in the walls, the vault's circulatory systems breathed and ticked, the sound he had slept inside for eleven years, the sound that had always meant *you are alone and safe.* Now it meant something slightly different. Now it meant *there are two heartbeats in here, and one of them belongs to trouble.*

"If I help you," Yara said, "what happens?"

Odile pushed herself up on her elbows, wincing, and fixed him with the eye that had assessed him through a hatch gap and found him, apparently, ninety percent harmless.

"Then in a few days, when I can walk, I show you what I carried out of that ship," she said. "And you'll wish I hadn't. And then we'll figure out how to get it off this planet before the people who burned my engine find yours." She held out her hand — the uninjured one, still smudged with soot, steady despite everything. "Grudging alliance?"

Yara looked at the hand. He thought about the frost-tolerant barley, the tomatoes, the made-up spare bed, the whistle of the beacon in the north wind. He thought about the hole in history, shaped like a language, that he had peered into for eleven years from the outside.

He shook it.

"Grudging alliance," he said. "But I want it noted that you crashed on my barley."

"Noted," said Odile Voss, and closed her eyes, and slept — trusting him, or too exhausted to do otherwise, and Yara suspected the distinction mattered less than she'd want it to.

He sat by the cot for a while as the morning strengthened, watching her breathe, listening to the vault breathe around them both. Then he went and got the scanner readout she'd been too proud to ask about, and read it twice, and felt the floor tilt gently beneath everything he'd planned for the next forty days.

Because the scan showed more than fractures. It showed a subdermal implant in her forearm, military-grade, still active — and transmitting, on a tight directional burst, a repeating handshake signal that Yara's beacon, his faithful, whistling, lizard-haunted beacon, was already answering.
