## 15. Sabotage

I woke to the smell of burnt rosemary and the sound of Yara swearing in two languages.

The second language was Old Meridian, which was how I knew it was serious. He only swore in the dead tongue when something had actually died.

"Yara?" I pulled on my boots and followed the smell down the corridor, past the cryo-garden racks where the seedlings slept in their amber light, into the transmitter room at the vault's heart. He stood in front of the main console with a mug of tea in one hand and a diagnostic wand in the other, and his face had gone the color of old paper.

"Tell me it's just the tea," I said.

"The tea is fine." He set it down with exaggerated care, the way you do when your hands want to shake and you've decided they won't. "The transmitter is not."

I crossed the room in four strides. The console's status display, which yesterday had glowed its steady, boring, beautiful green—*all systems nominal, beacon active, listening for a fleet that never calls*—was now a wall of amber warnings. I read them twice because my brain kept refusing the first pass.

**PRIMARY TRANSMITTER: OFFLINE.**
**FAULT ORIGIN: BEACON SUBSYSTEM 7.**
**SUBSYSTEM 7: NO SUCH SUBSYSTEM.**

"No such subsystem," I repeated. "The machine is gaslighting us."

"It's been doing it for eleven years, apparently." Yara's laugh came out wrong, flat as a pressed flower. "Subsystem Seven. The hunter-killer array. It reached out through the beacon architecture and—" he made a small, terrible gesture, "—pinched the transmitter shut. Like a hand closing around a throat."

I stared at the screen. Somewhere in orbit above us, or maybe in high relay, or maybe—I thought of Hale's squadron, still days out but closing—somewhere a Directorate technician had typed a command, and the thing we'd discovered coiled inside our own lighthouse had obeyed.

"They knew," I said. "They didn't even have to come here. They built the trap, they left it armed, and when someone finally tried to use the door, they locked it from across the system."

"Efficient," Yara said. "You have to admire—no. No, you don't. Forget I said that."

"I already forgot."

He picked up his tea, drank it, made a face. "It *is* the tea. Rosemary's burned."

"Yara."

"I know." He put the mug down. For a moment he just looked at the console, and I watched him do the thing he does—the thing I'd first found infuriating and now find unbearable to watch, because I know what it costs him—where he takes whatever catastrophe is in front of him and folds it down small enough to fit inside a routine. Cataloging seeds. Tending lights. One crisis at a time, filed neatly, so the loneliness doesn't get in.

But this one wouldn't fold. I could see it in his jaw.

"Thirty-one days," I said quietly.

"Thirty-one days until the truce expires. Twenty-nine until the arbitration council convenes for the last session before recess. We need the vector in their hands before they gavel in." He turned to me. "And our transmitter is a very expensive piece of garden sculpture."

* * *

We spent the morning trying to argue with it.

Yara is, when you get past the seed-packet humor and the habit of apologizing to machinery, a genuinely brilliant engineer—he kept a two-hundred-year-old orbital beacon alive alone for a decade, which is the engineering equivalent of keeping a campfire burning through a hurricane with nothing but damp twigs and stubbornness. So we tried everything. He bypassed Subsystem Seven; the fault migrated. He physically disconnected the array's trunk line; the transmitter stayed dark, some deeper handshake still poisoned. He rebooted the whole stack from cold iron, and for ninety glorious seconds the status display flickered green, and I actually cheered, and then the display resolved itself back to amber with what I swear was an air of polite apology.

"It's not broken," Yara said finally, sitting back on his heels amid a drift of fiber-optic cable. "That's the horrible part. It's *working*. Exactly as designed. Someone at the Directorate designed a beacon that could kill its own voice on command, and they did it so cleanly that there's no repair. There's only obedience or replacement."

"Replacement," I said. "How long to rebuild a transmitter from scratch?"

"With what parts? This place is a seed vault, Odile. My spare parts inventory is a jar of fuses and a very confident attitude."

I laughed despite myself, and he smiled despite himself, and for a moment we were just two people sitting on the floor of a doomed room surrounded by cables, and it was almost nice. That's the trick of catastrophe nobody warns you about: it keeps handing you these little pockets of ordinary, like it's sorry.

Then the pocket closed.

"Relay stations," I said. I'd been turning it over since the word *replacement*, worrying it like a seed coat. "The fleet's comm network. Relay Seven sits on the lunar far side—it's in the chapter of the archive files I memorized before I ran. Arbitration council traffic routes through it every session. If we can't send the vector to the council..." I looked up at him. "We carry it to the mailroom."

Yara blinked. "Luna is three days' burn in anything with a decent drive. What do we have with a decent drive?"

"...The tug."

The silence that followed had texture. The tug was Yara's other secret shame—a scavenged orbital transfer vehicle parked in the vault's landing cradle, older than both of us combined, which he'd spent years keeping technically flightworthy mostly, he admitted, as a hobby. Its name, stenciled on the hull by some long-dead crew, was *Patient Otter*.

"She leaks atmosphere at a rate I would describe as 'characterful,'" Yara said. "Her life support was rated for two crew for six hours in 2087. Her radiation shielding is, and I quote her original documentation, 'adequate.'"

"She got us this far as a plan."

"She got us this far as a *joke*. Odile, the far side of Luna is behind the moon. No direct line to Earth, no beacon coverage, no help. If anything goes wrong out there, no one knows where we are. No one *can* know—we're not exactly filing a flight plan with the people hunting us."

"Yes," I said. "All true. And in twenty-nine days the council recesses, the truce dies, and the Revisionists purge every record, every witness, every memory-carrier in the fleet—and the massacre happens again, except this time it never happened the *first* time either, and there's no one left alive who remembers otherwise except me, and eventually not even me."

He was quiet. Outside the narrow window, Earth turned, blue and indifferent and full of people who would never know what had been taken from them.

"The vector," he said at last. "Can it survive the trip? You said the encryption degrades if—"

"If it's exposed to certain scan frequencies. The tug's systems are too primitive to trip it. Honestly, the tug being ancient might be the only reason the Directorate hasn't flagged it—they're looking for modern emissions, modern encryption chatter. *Patient Otter* doesn't emit anything so much as... mumble."

"So we fly a mumbling antique to the far side of the moon, carrying the only proof of a genocide, through a blockade-in-progress, to break into a military relay station." He rubbed his face with both hands. When they came down, something in his expression had settled—not calm, exactly, but decided. I was learning to recognize that look. It was the look of a man who has spent eleven years practicing invisibility and has finally, reluctantly, concluded that he is going to be extremely visible. "When do we leave?"

"Tomorrow. Dawn burn. We'll want every hour of transit margin."

"Of course we do." He stood, brushed cable dust off his knees, and offered me his hand. "Well. I always wanted to see the far side of the moon."

"You've literally never said that."

"I'm saying it now. It's called growth."

* * *

We spent the afternoon preparing, and I will record honestly that half of it was preparation and half of it was Yara saying goodbye to a building.

He walked the cryo-garden rows twice, checking seals he'd checked that morning, murmuring to the dormant seedlings the way he does—*there you are, hold on, back soon*—and I pretended not to notice because noticing would have embarrassed him and because my chest had gone tight in a way I didn't have time to examine. Eleven years he'd kept this place. Eleven years of talking to green things that couldn't answer, and now he was leaving them for a moon he might not come back from, for a truth he'd never asked to carry.

"You could stay," I said, from the corridor. "I could take the tug alone. I've flown worse."

"You have not flown worse. You flew *here* on fire."

"That was a controlled descent."

"Odile."

"Yara." I stepped into the row, into the amber light. "I mean it. Everything the Directorate wants is in me. If I go alone and I'm caught, you're just the keeper of a derelict vault. They might leave you. You could keep the gardens. Keep the beacon lit. Go back to—"

"To what?" His voice cracked on it, gentle and furious at once. "To the comfortable lie? To cataloging seeds for an institution that built a killing machine into my lighthouse? Odile, they didn't just sabotage the transmitter today. They sabotaged *me*. Every year I spent faithfully maintaining that beacon, I was maintaining a weapon aimed at people like you. I'm not staying behind to preserve that life. That life is over. What's left is this." He held up his wrist, where the vector's access band sat against his skin—we'd taken to wearing it in shifts, like a shared pulse. "So no. We go together, or the plan is stupid, and I refuse to be part of a stupid plan. I'll accept doomed. Doomed has dignity."

"Doomed has dignity," I agreed. "God, you're insufferable when you're brave."

"I've been told it's my best quality. Second best. After the tea."

* * *

Night before launch, we loaded the tug by floodlight, and it went wrong in the specific way that things involving *Patient Otter* go wrong.

The cargo bay's loading arm seized halfway through lifting the emergency supplies, and Yara disappeared under the chassis with a work lamp clenched in his teeth, issuing muffled commentary that drifted between technical diagnosis and personal grievance. I held the light. It felt ceremonial.

"—she's *seized*, she's not broken, there's a difference, Otter, we've talked about this—"

"Are you negotiating with the ship?"

"I'm establishing expectations." A clang. A pause. "Ah. There's a nest."

"A nest."

"Mice. Or the descendants of mice. Vault mice, eleventh generation, absolutely fearless." He emerged holding, cupped in two hands, a small brown mouse with an air of having owned the place longer than he had. "This is Gerald. Possibly Gerald the Fourth. They're a dynasty."

"Gerald," I said gravely, "we are commandeering your vessel."

"He'll be fine. There's a whole ecosystem in the hydroponics bay. Gerald, off you go." He deposited the mouse on a rack of winter wheat, and it regarded us both with what I choose to believe was blessing.

We finished loading under the stars through the open bay doors: water, rations, medical kit, tool roll, the decryption keys from the predecessor's confession sealed in a shielded case, and the vector itself, double-wrapped in foil and foam and, at Yara's insistence, a wool sock.

"The sock is ceremonial," he explained.

"Everything about this ship is ceremonial."

"And yet she flies." He patted the hull. "Mostly downward, but with conviction."

It was nearly midnight when we finished, and we ended up sitting in the tug's tiny cockpit, shoulder to shoulder in seats designed for smaller people in a more optimistic century, drinking the last of the non-burned tea and watching Earthrise creep up over the crater rim. Three days to Luna. Three days back, if we lived. Twenty-five days of margin after that, minus whatever the universe deducted along the way.

"Odile." Yara's voice was quiet. "When we get there. Relay Seven. You know it won't be empty. Even with the truce holding, it's a military station. There will be guards, locks, systems we can't predict."

"I know."

"And you know I'm going to say something unforgivably sentimental right now, and I'd like you to let me finish before you make fun of me."

"I would never."

"You absolutely would. But—" he turned the mug in his hands, "—eleven years I kept a light lit for people who were never coming. And then you came. Out of a dead language, out of a fire, out of everything. And I know I'm bad at saying things plainly, so I'll say it sideways: whatever happens on that rock, the last month has been the least invisible I've ever felt. Being seen, it turns out, is survivable. Nobody told me."

I looked at him—this ridiculous, careful man who apologized to machinery and named the mice—and I leaned my head against his shoulder, because some things don't need translating.

"Get some sleep," I said. "Dawn burn."

* * *

The alarm woke us at 0430, and it wasn't ours.

It was the vault's perimeter chime—the soft, apologetic tone Yara had installed years ago to announce supply drones—which meant something was descending toward the landing cradle that was not a supply drone and definitely not cleared. We were in the cockpit in under a minute, and there it was on the scope: a single contact, small, fast, Directorate signature, dropping toward the vault with the unhurried confidence of something that owns the sky.

"They're not waiting for the blockade," I breathed. "They're coming *now*. A strike team. They found the tug's heat plume from the loading floods, or they traced the sabotage handshake, or—"

"Doesn't matter." Yara was already moving, hands flying across the ancient board, waking *Patient Otter* system by system. "Otter's cold. Cold ships don't show on approach scopes until they light up. If we sit tight, they land, they sweep the vault, they find an empty transmitter room and two missing keepers and—"

"And they find the tug."

A long breath. On the scope, the contact descended through five kilometers. Four.

"Or," Yara said slowly, and I heard the idea arrive in him the way ideas arrived in him, sideways and fully formed, like a seed that had been waiting years for rain, "they find a tug that looks abandoned. Cold hull, no emissions, no crew. Because its crew launched *before* they arrived, on a trajectory that reads as debris drift, and by the time anyone thinks to check, we're already a smear of unremarkable telemetry headed for the far side of the moon."

Four kilometers. Three.

"They'll have scopes on the cradle the whole descent," I said. "The second we light the drive—"

"Then we don't light it in the cradle." His grin was wild and terrified and the best thing I'd ever seen. "Odile, how do you feel about launching a hundred-and-fifty-year-old tug out of a gravity sling with no engine burn?"

"I feel that I have no idea what that means."

"Neither do I! It's called growth!" He slammed the release for the cradle clamps. "*Patient Otter*, darling, do the thing you were never designed to do—"

The cradle's mass-driver coils, built to fling cargo pods gently toward orbital tugs, caught our hull like a hand catching a thrown apple, and the stars outside the viewport swung, and the moon swung, and the Directorate dropship swung away beneath us like a toy sinking in a bath, and *Patient Otter* fell upward into the dark, silent, cold, free—

—and then, three seconds later, the drive sputtered, coughed, and died.

The cockpit went very quiet. Ahead of us, the moon hung enormous and indifferent, three days away by engine and considerably farther by nothing.

"Gerald," Yara said faintly, "if you can hear me, we may need to discuss the fuel lines."
