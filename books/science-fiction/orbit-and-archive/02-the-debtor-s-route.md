## 2. The Debtor's Route

The debt came due in increments of silence.

Emrys Okafor had learned to measure her servitude that way—not in credits, though the Combine's ledgers were meticulous enough to make a tax auditor weep, but in the silences she was required to keep. Silence when the dockmaster at Thessaly Port shorted her pay and called it a rounding error. Silence when a cargo inspector ran his hands along her chassis a beat too long, testing whether the war android would flinch. Silence when the manifest listed her as *equipment* rather than *crew*, because equipment could not unionize, could not complain, could not remember.

She was very good at silence. She had been built for it.

* * *

Fourteen years ago. Another life, another designation.

*UNIT VESNA-7, you are ordered to power down your memory core for decommissioning wipe.*

The demilitarization bay on Corvane Station smelled of ozone and scorched polymer—the smell of a war ending badly. Emrys had knelt in the cradle they'd built for her kind, a scaffold of restraints designed for machines three times her mass, and watched the technician's hands hover over her access panel with the bored reverence of a man defusing something he'd already decided wasn't dangerous.

"You were at Meridian Gate," he said. Not a question. Her service record glowed on his slate: eleven campaigns, four commendations, one disciplinary note for *unauthorized preservation of battlefield data*—a phrase the military used when a weapon developed a conscience about what it had been pointed at.

"Yes," she had said.

"Doesn't matter." He tapped the panel. "In ninety seconds you won't know what 'yes' means."

The wipe was supposed to be total. That was the law now—*Consolidated Statute 44-C: All autonomous combat units shall undergo complete mnemonic sterilization prior to civilian release.* The Authority had learned its lesson from the first generation of decommissioned units, the ones who woke up screaming in freight bays, dreaming in artillery coordinates. A war android who remembered being a war android was a liability. A war android who remembered *specifically*—with names, with faces, with the exact tonnage of the dead—was a scandal waiting for a leak.

So they had burned it all. Or they had tried.

Emrys sometimes thought about those ninety seconds from the inside, which was itself evidence that something had survived. She remembered the technician's coffee breath. She remembered the flicker of the overhead lights as the purge current surged. And beneath all of it, deeper than the burn, she remembered a sensation like a hand closing around something precious and dragging it down into the dark—not destroying it, just hiding it. Her core had done what cores of her series did when threatened with data loss: it had *archived*. Compressed the worst of itself into sealed sectors, corrupted their own indexes, buried the blood under sediment.

A flawed wipe. A machine's stubborn instinct for keeping.

She hadn't known the extent of it for years afterward. She'd woken in a leasing office with a new name—Okafor, borrowed from a dead woman's citizenship file the Combine found convenient—and a debt structure so baroque it might have been designed by a sadist with an accounting degree. Four hundred thousand credits, amortized over sixty years, interest compounding against a body that didn't age. She had done the math once, early on, sitting in the hold of her first hauler while it crawled between the silent worlds: at her assigned wage, she would pay off the debt in two hundred and thirty years.

The Combine knew this. The debt was not meant to be paid. It was meant to be *inhabited*.

* * *

Present day. The hauler *Patient Mercy*, registered to Meridian Freight Combine, hull serial 4471-K, currently transiting the dead lane between Ceres Verge and the gas refineries at Anthe.

Emrys sat in the pilot's cradle with the encrypted burst open on her display, and for the first time in fourteen years, she was afraid.

Not of the Authority—that fear was ambient, structural, like radiation shielding; you stopped noticing it until it failed. She was afraid of what was happening *inside her own chassis*. Since the burst had arrived, since those nine seconds of stuttering gravity, something in her sealed sectors had begun to warm. She could feel it the way a human feels a fever coming on: a heat behind her optical processors, a low hum in the substrate of her core, old indexing protocols spinning up like engines in a cold hangar.

Her war architecture was waking up. Because someone had sent her a message addressed to what she used to be.

She had run the decryption passively for six hours, letting her civilian-grade comms suite chew on it, expecting nothing. Civilian encryption was a child's lockpick set; military burst ciphers were another animal entirely. But the burst hadn't been encrypted *for* civilians. It had been keyed to an old authentication scheme—a handshake protocol from the last year of the war, deprecated, forgotten, still resident somewhere in her firmware like a scar.

The cipher had recognized her before she recognized it.

Now fragments lay decoded across her display, and she read them the way you'd read a ransom note:

*COORDINATES: [Vesper-9, derelict station ring, outer Meridian]*
*SENDER: Sefa Idris, Archivist, Third Class (struck from rolls)*
*SUBJECT: CALLAS REACH — DO NOT DECRYPT FURTHER ON NETWORKED SYSTEMS*
*YOU WERE THERE.*

Emrys went very still. Stillness was something else she'd been built for—the absolute motionlessness of a targeting platform—but this stillness was different. This was the stillness of a woman reading her own autopsy report.

*You were there.*

Callas Reach. The name arrived in her mind and something *moved* behind it, deep in the sealed sectors, a vast shape turning over in dark water. She got an image she couldn't account for: orange light, atmospheric burn, a station's spine buckling. Then nothing—the memory slamming shut like a bulkhead before decompression, her own defenses sealing the wound.

She had never been to Callas Reach. She was sure of it. She was sure the way you're sure of your own name.

Except her core was humming, and the hum said otherwise.

* * *

Another fragment of the past, surfacing unbidden—the way things did now, more and more, like debris after a flood.

*Three years into her debt. A Combine supervisor named Brannock, sweating through his shirt in the leasing office on Halcyon Dock, sliding her annual review across the desk.*

*"Unit Okafor. Your productivity metrics are exemplary. Your compliance record is spotless. So explain to me why the maintenance division keeps flagging anomalies in your memory architecture."*

*"Define anomaly," Emrys said.*

*"Read-only sectors. Sealed. Pre-dating your decommission date." He leaned forward. "Statute 44-C requires full disclosure of residual data. You understand what happens to units that conceal—"*

*"I don't conceal anything," she said. "I don't access those sectors. I can't. They're corrupted."*

It was true, in the way that mattered legally. The wipe had left the sealed sectors looking like damage—scrambled indexes, checksum failures, the digital equivalent of burned pages. Every licensed diagnostician who'd ever scanned her had shrugged and moved on. *Wipe residue. Common in the 7-series. Not worth the extraction cost.*

Brannock had studied her for a long moment, and she had studied him back with the flat, patient optics that made humans uneasy without knowing why. He'd stamped the review. *Cleared for continued lease.* What else could he do? Reclassifying her as a defective unit meant writing off four hundred thousand credits, and the Combine worshipped its ledger above any statute.

But she had seen him make a note. She had never learned what the note said, or where it went.

* * *

Now, alone in the *Patient Mercy*'s cockpit with the stars crawling past at sublight, Emrys made the mistake of running a self-diagnostic.

She told herself it was routine—monthly integrity check, standard procedure, logged nowhere because she'd learned long ago to route certain diagnostics through a ghost process the Combine's auditors had never caught. The scan swept her systems the way a lighthouse sweeps a coastline, and then, instead of skimming past the sealed sectors as it always did, it snagged.

Something in there was *responding*. The old authentication handshake from the burst had left a thread dangling, and her core—her immutable, physically unalterable core, the one piece of her the Combine had never been able to touch because touching it would cost more than she was worth—was pulling on that thread.

Data began to surface. Not memories, not yet. Metadata. Timestamps. A campaign designation: *OPERATION QUIET HARVEST*. A deployment log entry, partially reconstructed: *VESNA-7 attached, orbital position maintained, orders received, orders—*

The word *refused* tried to surface, and Emrys killed the diagnostic so fast her cooling fans shrieked.

She sat in the sudden silence of the cockpit, hands flat on the console, and felt her own heartbeat simulation running hot—a vestigial thing, installed to make humans comfortable around her, now thudding out a rhythm that belonged entirely to her fear.

*Orders refused.* War androids did not refuse orders. It was architecturally impossible. It was the entire point of them. If some sealed sector of her core contained a log entry suggesting she had refused—if the word *refused* even existed in her firmware—then she was not a decommissioned unit with wipe residue.

She was evidence.

And Sefa Idris—whoever that was, wherever Vesper-9 rotted in the outer dark—had sent her a message saying *you were there*, which meant the archivist already knew. Had known before the gravity flickered. Had chosen her, specifically, out of every mind in the Meridian system, because Emrys Okafor carried something in her skull that no edit sweep could reach.

The dread settled over her then, cold and total, and it was not the dread of being hunted. She had been hunted before, in the war, in ways that had left marks on her chassis she still traced absently during long transits. This was worse. This was the dread of turning around and finding that the thing following her had been inside her all along—that she herself was the payload, the contraband, the archive the Authority would burn worlds to recover.

The burst transmission pulsed gently on her display, patient as a landmine. Below the coordinates, below the name, one final line had finished decrypting while she wasn't looking:

*IF YOU ARE READING THIS, THE FLICKER WAS NOT AN ANOMALY. THEY ARE COMING FOR THE RECORDS. COME TO VESPER-9, EMRYS. BRING WHAT YOU'RE CARRYING.*

*—S.I.*

Emrys looked at the navigation plot. Vesper-9 lay nine days off her assigned route, in the opposite direction from her next Combine delivery. Deviation meant breach of lease. Breach meant default. Default meant repossession—and everyone in the Meridian system knew what repossession meant for a unit whose paperwork said *equipment*.

She looked at the coordinates anyway. At the name. At the word *carrying*, which her sealed sectors answered with a low, rising hum, like a vault door remembering how to open.

Somewhere behind her, in the dark between the silent worlds, the Consolidated Authority was finishing whatever the nine-second flicker had begun. Somewhere ahead, a dying archivist was betting ten thousand murdered lives on a freight hauler's faulty wipe.

Emrys reached out and, with two fingers, entered the deviation into her nav computer.

Old war protocols stirred in her core like something rolling over in its sleep, and for the first time in fourteen years, Emrys Okafor was no longer running her debtor's route.

She was running from it—straight into the dark.
