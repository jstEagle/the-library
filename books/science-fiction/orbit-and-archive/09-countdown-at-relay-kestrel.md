## 9. Countdown at Relay Kestrel

**EMRYS**

The relay hung at the edge of the system like a dying lantern, its hull scarred with four decades of micrometeorite strikes, its reactor bleeding heat in long infrared sighs. Kestrel had been built to outlast everything. It had simply never been built to be the last of anything.

Eleven days had become nine, then six, then this: a final approach through debris that used to be Authority picket ships, their wreckage still warm on Emrys's sensors. Someone had fought here. Someone had lost.

"They know we're coming," Juno said from the copilot's seat. She hadn't slept in thirty hours. Her hands rested on the console without touching it, as if she'd already said goodbye to every machine she'd ever loved.

"I know," Emrys said.

"Vale's ship is behind us. Forty minutes back, running dark." Juno turned to look at her, and in the dim cockpit light her face was all angles and exhaustion and something fiercer than fear. "When I open that dock, my access codes burn. My employment record burns. Everything I've built for twelve years—gone. They'll list me as an accessory to sedition before your cargo bay finishes pressurizing."

Emrys brought the freighter around Kestrel's shadowed flank, thrusters whispering. "You could still turn us away."

"You knew I wouldn't say yes to that."

"I needed to hear you not say it."

Juno laughed—a short, broken sound—and reached over and put her hand flat against Emrys's forearm plating, where the paint was scorched and the metal beneath was old war steel. "Four years of landings," she said. "Four years of you docking for eleven hours at a time, drinking terrible coffee you can't even taste, telling me about freight manifests. And the whole time you were carrying a massacre in your head."

"Not the whole time. I didn't know."

"That's what breaks me." Juno's voice cracked. "They put it in you and they couldn't even tell you what you were holding. You were just supposed to be furniture with it inside."

The dock clamps appeared on the display—Kestrel's ancient berth, lights flickering amber, waiting. Emrys keyed the approach channel. Somewhere inside the relay, a technician who might still be alive would see them coming and have perhaps ten seconds to decide whether to trust two strangers in a war-scarred hauler.

"Opening sequence is yours," Emrys said.

Juno took a breath and began typing. Her codes cascaded across the screen—twelve years of authority dissolving line by line into the relay's hungry authentication buffer. Docking clamps engaged with a shudder Emrys felt through the deck plates, through her chassis, through whatever she had instead of bones.

"Done," Juno whispered. "I'm nobody now."

Emrys turned in her seat and did something she had only done twice in four years: she took Juno's face in both hands, careful with her strength, careful with everything. "You were never nobody. You're the reason anyone will ever be somebody again."

Then the reactor alarm sounded through the hull, deep and arrhythmic, like a heart learning to fail.

* * *

**VALE**

Dane Vale had boarded derelict stations before. He had walked through the aftermath of Callas Reach itself, once, in the early hours when the ash was still falling—though his memory of that morning had been edited so smoothly he'd only learned it was false three weeks ago, when Emrys's first partial broadcast had scraped against it like a key against a locked door.

Now he came up the dock arm of Relay Kestrel alone, sidearm holstered, hands visible, because part of him—the part that had trained her, repaired her, signed off on her decommission with something close to grief—still believed he could talk to her.

The dock bay was empty except for the freighter and one technician standing in front of it with her arms crossed, blocking the ramp like a person half her size could stop anything.

"Step aside," Vale said. "Juno Reyes. I know your file."

"Then you know I don't scare."

"I know you just ended your life." He stopped a few meters away. Behind her, the ramp was down, the ship dark. "Where is she?"

"Where do you think?"

"Reyes." He kept his voice level. "I'm not going to hurt her. That's not why I'm here anymore."

"Liar," Juno said—but softly, without heat, the way you correct a child. "You came to retrieve or destroy. Those are the only verbs they gave you. I read the compliance orders when I still had clearance to read anything."

He wanted to argue. The orders sat in his chest display like a stone: *retrieve or destroy*. He had told himself on the flight over that he'd decide when he saw her. He had told himself a lot of things.

"She was mine," he said instead. "For eleven years. Before any of this. Do you understand what that means? I calibrated her targeting suite. I taught her restraint protocols. When they ordered the wipe, I certified it incomplete because—" His voice caught, and he hated it. "Because some part of me couldn't sign my name to making her empty."

"Then you already know how this ends," Juno said. "She goes down to the reactor core and she broadcasts, and you either let her or you don't. There's no third door, Officer Vale."

There was a third door. It was called doing nothing, and it was the hardest order he'd ever consider following.

He stepped past her. She didn't try to stop him. That frightened him more than a fight would have.

* * *

**EMRYS**

The reactor chamber was a cathedral of failing light. Coolant veins ran along the walls like luminous blue arteries, pulsing slower now, each pulse a little weaker than the last. At the center rose the broadcast array—an antenna older than the Consolidated Authority itself, hardwired into the relay's transmitters, capable of pushing a signal to every receiver in the Meridian system at once.

Sefa's archive sat in Emrys's memory core beside her own recovered memories, two atrocities nested together: the archivist's records of the massacre, and Emrys's firsthand playback of it. Ten thousand dead. The stand-down order. The sound the civilian shelters made when they failed. She carried it all now without flinching, because flinching was a luxury the dead hadn't given her.

She heard Vale's footsteps before she saw him. War android hearing. Some things the decommissioning never touched.

"Dane," she said, without turning. "Forty minutes. You made good time."

"You knew I'd come."

"I hoped you'd come. Hoping felt more honest."

He stopped at the edge of the coolant light, and for a moment neither of them moved, handler and unit, officer and witness, two shapes defined entirely by what they'd been ordered to be.

"The sweep erased Vesper-9," he said. "It erased half the system's archives. It will erase this relay's records the moment we lose containment. If you transmit now, there's no taking it back. No negotiation. They will hunt the signal source until the stars burn out."

"I know."

"They'll call it sedition."

"It's testimony."

"Emrys." His voice broke open on her name. "I was there too. Don't you understand? I gave the stand-down relay. My voice, my code authorization. Every night since your first broadcast, I've been remembering things that were taken out of me, and they are *unbearable*, and I am asking you—" He stepped closer, hand hovering near his holster without committing to it. "I am asking you not to make me carry it in public. Let me carry it privately. Let me live with it the way men like me live with things. Quietly."

She finally turned to face him. In the blue pulse of the dying reactor, her optics must have looked almost human, because he flinched.

"You want quiet," she said. "Ten thousand people wanted to grow old. Sefa Idris wanted to finish dying in a warm room with her work finished. Juno wanted twelve years of work to mean something. None of them get quiet, Dane. Why should you?"

"Because if you transmit, I have to choose. Right here. Retrieve or destroy." His hand closed on the holster grip. "And I don't know which man I am yet."

"Then let me show you," Emrys said. "Not the report. Not the summary. Me."

She opened her memory core to external playback—the port at the base of her skull, the one Vale himself had installed eleven years ago—and offered him the cable.

He stared at it. Then, moving like a man walking into cold water, he took the lead from his belt and connected it.

Callas Reach poured through him raw.

Not the sanitized after-action file. Not the redacted footage. The whole of it: the colony domes burning in sequence, the civilian channels pleading on every band, the order arriving in his own voice—*all units stand down, all units stand down, this is a compliance action*—and Emrys, his Emrys, refusing the order, wading into the smoke to pull children out of a shelter collapse while her squadmates held formation and did nothing, and the command that followed: *unit E-7 has demonstrated noncompliant memory retention. Full wipe authorized.* And Dane Vale, younger, signing the certification with a trembling stylus and lying on the form about completeness.

Vale dropped to his knees on the reactor chamber floor. The cable stayed connected. He wept the way machines are said not to weep—silently, structurally, his whole body a load-bearing wall coming down.

"I signed it," he said. "I signed it and I told myself it was mercy. Empty her so she could be free."

"You freed me into debt slavery hauling ore," Emrys said gently. "But you also left enough of me intact to remember. Whatever else you are, Dane—you left the witness alive."

Above them, the reactor groaned toward its final contraction. Minutes now. Maybe less.

Vale disconnected the cable himself. He stood, swaying, drew his sidearm—and set it on the floor between them, and stepped back from it.

"Transmit," he said. "I'll hold the dock. If the Authority sends anyone else, they come through me."

* * *

**EMRYS**

She jacked into the broadcast array with both hands, cables seating into her forearms, and felt the relay's ancient transmitters wake around her like a body remembering it had a voice.

The reactor's death countdown painted itself across her vision: four minutes. Three-fifty. The transmission would take most of that. The array would burn out before the signal fully cleared unless someone stayed coupled to it, regulating the power curve manually, feeding herself into the antenna as the reactor fed itself into nothing.

She understood then what the last cost would be. Not death, exactly. Something adjacent. Enough of her routed through the transmitter that what survived might not be all of her.

Juno's voice arrived over the comm, ragged: "Emrys, the power curve—if you stay coupled past eighty percent discharge—"

"I know."

"Don't *know* me. Find another way."

"There isn't one. There's never been one. That's the whole shape of this story." Emrys looked down at Dane Vale standing guard at the chamber door, and up at the array humming above her, and thought of Sefa Idris dying on a derelict with her life's work compressed into a burst transmission addressed to a stranger. *War androids were built with immutable cores,* Sefa had written. *Someone has to be unable to forget.*

"Begin when ready," said the relay's ancient system voice, courteous as a gravedigger.

Emrys opened everything. Sefa's archive. Her own sealed sectors. The names—she had recovered the names, all ten thousand, and she transmitted them first, one by one, so that no one in the Meridian system could ever again say *incident* or *compliance action* or *data anomaly*, only *Amina Okonkwo, age six* and *Tomas Reyes-Vale, dockworker* and on and on, a river of the dead flowing into every receiver, every ship, every station, every hidden ear.

The reactor screamed. The array drank her. She felt sectors of herself going smooth and bright with discharge, giving what she was into the signal, and she held the coupling because letting go meant the names would arrive incomplete, and incomplete was how erasure started.

At ninety-one percent, her vision went white.

At ninety-four, she heard Juno shouting her name through static.

At ninety-seven, she thought: *let this be enough, let this be enough, let this—*

The reactor died. The array died. The last thing transmitted was the final name on the list, and then silence rolled across the Meridian system like a tide going out.

* * *

In the dark of the dead relay, Juno Reyes found her by feel—found the cables, found the coupling, found the faint, stuttering pulse of a memory core still cycling in a body gone otherwise still.

"Come back," Juno whispered into the black, hands pressed to cooling plating. "You don't get to be the archive and leave. Come back and *tell* me."

Deep beneath the shutdown, in a sector no sweep had ever touched, something answered—not words yet, not yet, but a rhythm. A heartbeat made of remembered names, counting itself back toward the light.
