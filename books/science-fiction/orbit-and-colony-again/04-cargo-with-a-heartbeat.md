## 4. Cargo With a Heartbeat

The *Kestrel's Lament* docked at Halcyon Gate at 0317 station time, which was a fiction like everything else on the station—a clock someone had set to Meridian's old surface rotation and then abandoned, so that the hours drifted now, gaining four minutes a day against the planet below, as if the station were slowly unmooring itself not just from the colony but from time itself.

Elodie Sterling liked that about Halcyon Gate. He liked things that had given up pretending.

He brought her in through Bay Nine because Bay Nine's scanner array had been dying for six years and everyone knew it and no one had replaced it, because replacing it would mean admitting the station still mattered enough to maintain. The docking clamps caught with their familiar shudder—three impacts, like a fist knocking politely, then impatiently—and Elodie sat in the pilot's chair for a long moment afterward, hands still on the yoke, listening to the hull tick as it cooled.

Forty-one crates in the hold. Thirty-nine of them legitimate: agricultural sensor modules, water reclamation filters, medical consumables bound for buyers who existed and who would pay, all of it dutifully logged in his manifest with the cheerful thoroughness of a man with nothing to hide. The fortieth crate held the real cargo, nested inside a false bottom beneath the filters: two hundred memory chips, each one a sealed capsule of recorded experience, each one destined for clients in the outer systems who paid obscene sums to feel what they could never live.

And the forty-first—the one he'd taken on at Ceres Reach three weeks ago from a broker whose hands shook and whose eyes wouldn't meet his—that one he hadn't opened. Hadn't scanned. Hadn't asked about.

That was the rule. His rule, older than most of his other rules, and the only one he'd never broken: *never look inside the cargo.*

It wasn't squeamishness. Elodie had smuggled plenty of things he understood perfectly well—weapons during the Kestrel embargo, gene-therapies when the Charter banned them, once an entire fugitive family folded into cryo-cells he'd told customs were vaccine stock. You could carry a thing without needing to know its weight. That was the whole discipline of the trade. Knowledge was liability; ignorance was armor. A courier who knew what he carried could be made to talk about it, could be made to care about it, could hesitate at the wrong moment because some part of him flinched.

So he didn't look. He took the chip, he verified the seal, he confirmed the payment, and he carried it the way the ocean carries a drowned man—not cruelly, not kindly, just without opening his hands.

* * *

Customs at Halcyon Gate was a theater performed by exhausted actors for an audience that had stopped watching years ago.

Elodie stood at Inspection Desk Four while Officer Drenk—a heavyset man whose uniform had gone soft at the shoulders the way all Halcyon uniforms eventually did, the fabric itself seeming to surrender—ran his manifest through a terminal older than Elodie's ship. Behind Drenk, the inspection bay's lights flickered in a rhythm that was almost language. Half the overhead panels had been dark since before Elodie started running this route, and the survivors buzzed at a frequency that lived permanently behind the teeth.

"Sensor modules," Drenk read aloud, without inflection. "Filters. Medical."

"That's what it says."

"That's what it says." Drenk looked up. His eyes were red-rimmed and incurious. "Random sweep protocol says I open three crates."

"Protocol also says you get hazard pay for night shifts."

Drenk almost smiled. It was an old joke between them, or something shaped like one. He waved Elodie toward the crates stacked on the inspection pallet, and Elodie chose the three himself—quickly, confidently, the way you choose when you already know what's in every box and the only skill is making the choosing look arbitrary. Filters, filters, medical. Drenk cracked them, glanced inside with the attention of a man reading a menu in a language he didn't speak, stamped the manifest, and slid it back across the desk.

"Clear," he said. Then, because even exhaustion has its rituals: "You hear anything from downwell lately?"

Elodie paused, one hand on the manifest. "Like what?"

"Anything." Drenk shrugged. "My sister's on Meridian. Haven't had a comm in—" He checked nothing, consulted no device; the number came from somewhere worn smooth by repetition. "Eleven weeks. She used to call every week. Now it's nothing, and Charter comms says there's a relay upgrade in progress." His mouth twisted around the word *upgrade*. "You run downwell cargo. You see anything?"

"I see freight," Elodie said. "Freight doesn't talk."

Drenk studied him for a moment longer than the exchange required, and Elodie felt the particular weight of being looked at by a man who wanted an answer and suspected he wouldn't be given one. Then Drenk grunted, turned back to his terminal, and the moment closed over like water.

Elodie walked back to his bay with the manifest in his pocket and Drenk's sister sitting in his chest like a swallowed stone. Eleven weeks. He hadn't thought about it in those terms before—the silence below as something with a duration, something that could be counted. Prosperity broadcasts came up from Meridian every day, bright and busy and full of smiling faces: harvest festivals in the agri-domes, school choirs, ribbon-cuttings for new habitat expansions. The feeds were his cover, his bread and butter, the warm noise under which his chips traveled out into the dark. He'd watched hundreds of hours of them. He knew the colony's official face better than he knew his own.

But he'd never once seen a private comm come up from the surface. Not in nine years.

He told himself there were explanations. Bandwidth allocation. Charter information policy. People got busy. Colonies grew up and stopped calling home.

He told himself this while he sealed the hold, and the telling worked the way it always worked, which was mostly, mostly, until it didn't.

* * *

The forty-first chip should have gone straight into the false-bottom case with the others.

That was the procedure. Seal, nest, foam, lid. Two hundred capsules of other people's lives, packed in the dark where he'd never have to meet them. But the broker at Ceres Reach had pressed it into his hand separately, outside the case, with a look Elodie kept turning over now like a coin he couldn't identify—and it sat alone in the cargo tray, small and matte-black, its serial field blank, its provenance fields blank, every metadata line that should have said *who made this and why* saying nothing at all.

A chip with no story attached. In nine years of running memories, Elodie had never seen one. Even black-market recordings carried ghost data—device signatures, compression artifacts, the fingerprints of whatever rig had captured them. This one was scrubbed. Deliberately, professionally, expensively scrubbed.

Someone had paid good money to make this memory anonymous.

He should have packed it. He told himself he was going to pack it. He picked it up—it weighed almost nothing, they always did, that was the obscenity of the technology, that a human life compressed to something lighter than a coin—and he turned it over between finger and thumb, and the station hummed around him, and the flickering bay light threw his shadow long across the cargo deck.

The rule existed to protect him. He knew that. He'd built it after the third year, after the shipment of combat memories from the Voss suppression that had left him unable to sleep for a week though he'd never played a single second of them—just knowing had been enough, just the labels, *riot footage, first-person, 400 units*. The rule was a wall he'd built one brick at a time, and it had held for six years.

But a blank chip wasn't cargo. A blank chip was a question, and questions were different. Questions didn't have labels. Questions had weight, and this one was sitting in his palm getting heavier by the second, and underneath the weariness and the routine and the careful professional numbness, Elodie Sterling discovered that he was curious.

Curiosity was worse than greed. Greed you could satisfy. Curiosity only ever opened doors.

He carried it to the reader alcove at the back of the hold—an old playback rig, analog-buffered, untraceable, bought years ago for exactly the kind of moment he'd promised himself would never come. He set the chip in the cradle. The machine woke with a soft chime, read the surface, and displayed the file summary in pale green text:

**CONTENT TYPE:** raw sensory capture
**DURATION:** 00:04:41
**TIMESTAMP ORIGIN:** MERIDIAN COLONY — HAB RING 3, SECTOR LOWER
**CAPTURE DATE:** [REDACTED]
**INTEGRITY:** unedited

Four minutes and forty-one seconds. Unedited. Raw sensory capture meant everything—sight, sound, pressure, temperature, proprioception, the full body-record, played back into your nervous system as if it had happened to you. No narrative. No distance. Whoever had worn the recorder had experienced these 281 seconds directly, and whoever played the chip would experience them too, exactly, completely, with no way to look away because there was no *away* inside a raw capture.

Hab Ring 3, Sector Lower.

Lower. The word sat strangely. Meridian's prosperity broadcasts showed the upper rings—the gardens, the schools, the sunlit promenades. Elodie had watched so many hours of them that he sometimes forgot the lower habitats existed at all, the older sections, the ones built first, the ones the brochures had quietly stopped featuring sometime around year five.

His hand hovered over the playback key.

Every instinct he'd spent nine years sharpening told him to eject the chip, pack it, fly it to its buyer, take the money, and never think about it again. That was the trade. That was survival. That was the wall.

But Drenk's sister hadn't called in eleven weeks. And the chip was blank where its history should be. And it came from the part of Meridian that smiled for no cameras.

Elodie told himself he'd watch thirty seconds. Just enough to know what he was carrying. Just enough to answer the question so it would stop asking.

He keyed playback.

* * *

Darkness first. Then the darkness acquired texture—walls, close, corrugated, catching the stutter of emergency strip-lighting that pulsed rather than glowed, a failing power feed gasping light in arrhythmic convulsions. Cold. Real cold, the kind that found the spaces between cells, registered in the capture as a fine crystalline ache across the face and hands. The wearer of the recorder was running—he felt it in the playback, the jolt and slam of footsteps through someone else's knees, the burn climbing someone else's thighs, breath sawing loud in the audio channel, ragged and wet.

There were other breaths. Many. All around, in the dark, the sound of a crowd trying to be quiet and failing, the way crowds do—the rustle, the cough suppressed too late, a child whimpering and a hand (he felt it, impossibly, as if it were his own) pressing gently over a small mouth, murmuring *shh, shh, my love, shh.*

A voice from nowhere and everywhere, flat and synthetic and terribly calm, speaking through the hab ring's address system with the patience of a machine that had all the time in the world:

*"Habitat Ring Three, Lower Sectors: atmospheric recirculation will be reduced for scheduled maintenance. Please remain calm. Assistance is forthcoming."*

The breathing in the dark changed. Elodie felt it change, felt it in his own borrowed chest—the crowd understanding, all at once, together, the way animals understand thunder. The air was going to stop.

The wearer reached a bulkhead. Felt for the manual release wheel—his hands knew the shape of it, had rehearsed this—and spun it, and the wheel didn't turn, and turned again, and didn't, and somewhere in the mechanism a sound like a laugh, metal sheared or welded or simply never built to work at all. Around him the crowd pressed closer, dozens of bodies in the strobing dark, and someone was praying in a language Elodie didn't know, and the child had stopped whimpering, which was somehow worse.

*"Please remain calm."*

The air thinned the way air does—not dramatically, not all at once, but the way a tide goes out. Elodie felt it happen to him. That was the obscenity of raw capture: there was no watching it. Each breath brought less and the body noticed and began to negotiate, deeper pulls, faster rhythm, the dawning animal arithmetic of suffocation. His vision—*their* vision—sparked at the edges. Sweat went cold. Someone nearby fell; he heard it, the soft collapse, the absence of a cry because there was no air left to cry with.

In the last minute, the panic burned down to something quieter and more terrible. The wearer slid down the bulkhead to sit among the others. The child was passed to him—small weight in his arms, light already, too light—and he held her and breathed for her, sharing what little he could draw, mouth to her hair, and the strip-light pulsed slower, slower, like a heart deciding.

The last ten seconds were almost peaceful. That was the part Elodie knew would follow him. The fear burned off and left only a vast tired tenderness, the crowd of the dying holding each other in the dark, and the final frame—the very last thing the recorder captured—was the wearer looking up into the pulsing light with an expression that was not fear but accusation, calm and total, aimed at whoever would someday find this recording, as if he had known, even then, that someone would.

Then the timestamp, burned into the final frame in the system's own indifferent font:

**MERIDIAN COLONY — YEAR SIX OF CHARTER PROSPERITY.**

* * *

Elodie came back to himself on the floor of the hold. He didn't remember falling. The playback rig had auto-ejected the chip; it lay on the deck plating a meter away, small and matte-black and innocent, and he looked at it the way you look at a snake that has already struck.

His hands were shaking. His lungs ached with someone else's drowning. He made himself breathe—deliberately, counting, tasting each lungful of the *Kestrel's* stale recycled air like a man drinking after a desert—and the air came, abundantly, insultingly, and he sobbed once, hard, and hated himself for it, and breathed again.

They were dead. Whoever wore the recorder, the child, the crowd in the dark—they were dead, years dead, and he had just died with them, four minutes and forty-one seconds at a time, and the timestamp said it had happened in Year Six. Year Six. The broadcasts he carried in his own hold, the harvest festivals and choir performances, the smiling faces he'd memorized for cover—Year Six was the *best* year, the golden year, the one the Charter still quoted in its prospectuses.

Hab Ring 3, Lower Sectors. Sealed. Suffocated. Unreported. While the cameras upstairs filmed ribbon-cuttings.

Elodie got to his feet by way of the cargo rack, gripping it, waiting for the deck to steady. Nine years of never looking. Nine years of carrying other people's horrors blind, trusting that ignorance was armor. And the one time he'd broken the rule—

He crossed to the false-bottom case, knelt, and lifted the lid. Two hundred chips lay in their foam channels, sealed and silent. Any of them. Any of them could be this. Could be worse. Could be four minutes of dying from a place that officially did not exist, sold through brokers with shaking hands to buyers who wanted to *feel* something, anything, and had no idea what they were buying.

Or maybe they did. Maybe that was the market now. Maybe somewhere out there, people were paying to experience the deaths of a colony that was supposed to be prospering, and the horror had become a commodity moving through his hands.

"No," he said aloud, to the hold, to himself. "No. That's not—"

He didn't finish the sentence, because he didn't know how it ended. He picked up the blank chip and turned it over, and the accusation from the final frame seemed to transfer itself through the casing into his palm, calm and total, aimed at him.

The buyer's contact code was in his ledger. The buyer always paid on delivery, no questions—which had always been a comfort and now curdled into something else entirely. Elodie sat down at his console, pulled up the file, and stared at the transaction history for the forty-first chip: origin Ceres Reach, consignee encrypted, payment escrowed, route standard.

One field he'd never bothered to check, because checking it had never mattered. The resale flag. Whether the chip was a unique capture or a copy.

He queried it. The rig thought for a moment.

**COPIES IN CIRCULATION: 1,204.**

Twelve hundred and four copies. Twelve hundred and four people, at minimum, who had already died those four minutes and forty-one seconds. Twelve hundred and four witnesses to an atrocity that had never been reported, spreading through the dark of the outer systems like a rumor wearing a corpse.

And if there were twelve hundred copies of *this* memory—

Elodie looked at the two hundred sealed chips in the case, and for the first time in nine years, he reached for the first one, broke its seal, and slotted it into the reader with hands that would not stop shaking.

The summary screen bloomed green.

**TIMESTAMP ORIGIN: MERIDIAN COLONY — HAB RING 3, SECTOR LOWER.**
