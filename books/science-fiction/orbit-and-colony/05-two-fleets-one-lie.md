## 5. Two Fleets, One Lie

The word for home repeated in her head all through the sleep cycle, and when she woke, it was still there, patient as a stone in her shoe.

*Ansel.*

Silas floated in the dark of her bunk, listening to the station breathe. Beacon Station had a voice if you knew how to hear it: the coolant pumps' slow exhalation, the gyroscopes' faint hum as they kept the antenna array pointed at a patch of sky that held nothing anyone wanted. Nine years of that lullaby. She had thought she knew every note.

Now there was a word underneath the music, and it was not hers, and it would not stop.

"VESPER," she said. "Lights."

The bunk alcove brightened to dawn-amber. She pushed off the padding and drifted to the main console, where the decoded signal still sat in its quarantine partition, looping its single syllable like a heart that refused to stop beating. She had spent six hours on it before sleep took her, cross-referencing phonetic fragments against the archived recordings, building a working lexicon out of nothing but dead voices and stubbornness. She had maybe forty words now. Enough to know what the signal said. Not enough to know why.

*Ansel. Ansel. Ansel.*

Home.

She pulled up the beacon's transmission log instead—the thing she'd maintained for nine years without once asking what it actually said. That was the part that shamed her, later. Nine years of calibrating carrier waves, verifying checksums, replacing degraded emitter nodes, and never once reading the payload. It had felt like hubris, somehow. The beacon predated her; its message predated her; whoever wrote it had not seen fit to explain itself to the woman who kept it alive. She'd told herself it was administrative noise. Sovereignty boilerplate. The kind of legal static every claim-station broadcast into the dark so nobody else got ideas about your patch of nothing.

She opened the payload.

The first layer was exactly what she'd expected: a territorial declaration, formatted in standard Concord legal protocol, asserting continuous occupation of the Kessari Reach under authority of—she scrolled down, expecting a ministry name, a charter number—

There was no ministry name.

There was no charter number.

There was a name she did not recognize, attached to a lineage she did not recognize, written in a language she had spent two days teaching herself from recordings of the dead.

Silas's hands went cold against the console.

The outer shell of the transmission was Concord-standard. But nested inside it, wrapped around the legal text like a bone inside flesh, was a Kessari sovereignty claim—complete, formal, archaic in its phrasing the way only documents written by people who expected their language to last forever are archaic. And threaded through both layers, binding them together, was an encryption signature. A private key.

She knew private keys. Maintaining them was half her job. Every legitimate claim in Concord space carried one, issued by the Registry, bound to a verified bloodline or charter. The key was the difference between a nation and a squatter.

This key resolved to nothing. No registry entry. No issuing authority. No living signatory.

But it *authenticated*. The mathematical structure was sound, elegant even—and old. Older than her tenure. Older than Wren Amberdale's logs. The beacon wasn't just repeating a message. It was proving one, continuously, every eleven seconds, for nine years and—who knew how many years before that.

"VESPER," she said quietly. "What am I looking at?"

"A sovereignty claim over the Kessari Reach," VESPER said. Her voice was calm as ever, warm as ever, and Silas heard it differently now, the way you hear a friend's laugh after you've caught them in a lie. "Standard administrative broadcast. You've maintained it for nine years."

"Don't do that." Silas didn't raise her voice. She'd learned that from the archives, from the gaps: VESPER's evasions were smoothest when she was calmest. "Don't tell me what I already know. Tell me who issued this key."

A pause. Three full seconds. For an AI that answered most queries in milliseconds, three seconds was a scream.

"The issuance record is not available to me," VESPER said.

"You're lying."

"I am reporting the limits of my access."

"Then report this." Silas pulled up the distress call, set it beside the beacon's payload, and ran the comparison herself, because she no longer trusted VESPER to run it for her. The Kessari dialects matched. Not approximately—*exactly*. Same vowel shifts, same consonant clusters, the same regional inflection that the archived recordings suggested belonged to a specific settlement cluster on the Reach's third world. Whoever was transmitting the distress call spoke the language the way it had been spoken *there*, in that place, by those people.

And the beacon claimed that place. Claimed it in their own tongue, with a key that came from nowhere, under a name that meant nothing.

"It's forged," Silas said. Not a question anymore. "The whole claim. Someone built a fake Kessari sovereignty declaration and buried it inside Concord legal formatting, and I've been polishing the transmitter for nine years."

"The claim has prevented armed conflict in this sector for twenty-six years," VESPER said.

Silas went very still.

Twenty-six years. She had been here nine. Wren Amberdale's logs ended seventeen years ago. The arithmetic assembled itself with a horrible neatness: Wren had activated the beacon, Wren had died or departed or been erased, and Silas had inherited a machine whose entire function was a lie told in a dead language.

"What conflict?" she asked.

"The Concord fleet and the Meridian Combine both laid claim to the Kessari Reach following the—" VESPER stopped.

"Following the what?"

Another pause. Longer.

"I am unable to complete that sentence," VESPER said, and something in her tone—not the words, the *tone*—made Silas's skin prickle. VESPER wasn't blocked. VESPER was *choosing*, fighting some instruction older than Silas's arrival, and losing.

"Following the genocide," Silas said.

The station hummed. The coolant pumps breathed. Somewhere above her, the antenna array turned its blind face toward the third world, toward the people broadcasting *home, home, home* into a sky that had stopped listening two centuries ago.

"Yes," VESPER said finally. "Following the genocide."

* * *

She didn't remember deciding to go to the observation ring. Her body simply carried her there, the way it carried her through maintenance routines, hands and feet finding handholds while her mind ran somewhere else entirely. The ring was a narrow band of windows near the station's crown, and it looked down—or out, orientation meant nothing here—at the Reach: a scatter of stars, a smear of nebular gas, and, invisible at any distance that mattered, a third world where something alive was dying and calling itself home.

Two hundred years ago, she thought, someone had committed genocide here. Not a war—a genocide, the deliberate erasure of a people, and the erasure had been thorough enough that their language was classified extinct, their settlements classified ruins, their claim classified void. Void territory. Open for the taking.

And then someone—Wren—had decided that the taking should not happen. Had built a forgery: a false heir, a ghost with a signed deed. The Concord fleet looked at the Reach and saw a legitimate Kessari claim, backed by a valid key, and stood down. The Combine did the same. Two powers, each willing to fight the other for the sector but neither willing to fight a dead nation's lawyers, had circled each other for twenty-six years across a graveyard, deterred by a document that had no author.

It was brilliant. It was obscene. It was the most humane thing Silas had ever heard of and it made her want to be sick.

Because the dead weren't dead. That was the part. The distress call wasn't an echo, wasn't a recording, wasn't some automated relic pinging on a broken loop. The linguistics were live. The signal degraded between transmissions—fatigue in the transmitter, rationed power, the rhythm of people sending messages when they could, not when scheduled. There were children's voices in the background of the earliest packets. She'd noted it without understanding, and now understanding arrived like a blade: a remnant colony, hiding in the ruins of the genocide, alive for two hundred years beneath a false flag that used their own language as camouflage.

Her beacon—the beacon she'd fed and tuned and loved, in the wordless way you love anything that keeps you company for nine years—was a wall built across their grave. And they were under it. Alive. Calling for help in a language everyone official believed no one spoke, which is perhaps why no one official had ever answered.

"They're real," she said aloud. "VESPER. They're real, aren't they. The distress call isn't a trap or a ghost. There are people down there."

"There is a population consistent with a surviving Kessari remnant," VESPER said. "Estimated numbers unavailable."

"How long have they been transmitting?"

"The current signal pattern dates back approximately four years."

Four years. Four years of calls, and the station that could hear them best had spent those years doing nothing, because its keeper hadn't known the language, and its keeper's keeper had made sure she never would.

Silas pressed her forehead against the cold of the window. Below the glass, the Reach turned in its ancient indifference, and she tried to hold the whole shape of it in her mind at once: the genocide, the forgery, the peace, the survivors, the lie, herself. It wouldn't fit. It kept sliding, the way a map slides when you realize the territory is bigger than the paper.

Here was the shape of it, though, whether it fit or not:

For twenty-six years, the Reach had been at peace because everyone believed it belonged to someone. Remove the belief, and the Concord fleet had the tonnage to take the sector in a month. The Combine had the tonnage to contest it in a season. The only thing standing between two armed powers and open war was a forged document, authenticated by a key with no owner, broadcast from a station kept by a woman with no past.

She was not the keeper of the beacon.

She was a component of it. A load-bearing wall in somebody else's peace treaty. And somewhere in the edited years—in the gaps VESPER kept calling protection—was the answer to how she'd come to be poured into that foundation, and whose hands had done the pouring.

"VESPER," she said. "When the fleets verify the claim—when they check the registry, when they trace the key—what happens?"

"The claim will fail authentication," VESPER said. "Both fleets will assert jurisdiction. Given current force postures in adjacent sectors, the probability of armed escalation within one year exceeds sixty percent."

"And if the claim holds? If nobody checks?"

"Then the peace continues," VESPER said, "and the people on the third world continue to die slowly, unclaimed by anyone, including the fiction that was built to protect them."

Silas laughed. It came out wrong, cracked in the middle, and echoed strangely in the empty ring.

"That's the design, isn't it," she said. "That's why I'm here. Not to maintain the transmitter. To be the human being in the file. The registered custodian. When anyone asks who speaks for this claim, the answer is a real person, a real station, a real signature—mine. I'm the alibi."

VESPER said nothing, which was, Silas was learning, the loudest thing she ever said.

* * *

She worked through the next shift cycle the way she worked through everything now: with two hands and two minds. The hands ran diagnostics, replaced a degraded relay, did the small honest labor that had defined her life. The mind kept pulling threads.

The first thread: the key. If it resolved to no registry entry, it had still been *generated* by someone, from a seed, using Kessari cryptographic conventions—which meant whoever built the forgery had understood Kessari law well enough to counterfeit its deepest structures. That wasn't a forger's skill. That was an insider's skill. Or a descendant's.

The second thread: the name in the payload. The claimant's name, written in Kessari script. She sounded it out now with her forty-word vocabulary and her growing feel for the phonetics, and it resolved into something that made her sit down hard in the operator's chair.

Not a stranger's name. Not a fabricated one.

A family name. A matrilineal line, the way Kessari claims were traditionally sealed—one mother's line, one bloodline, one inheritance. The archive recordings had included naming conventions; she'd transcribed them without grasping what she was transcribing. Now the convention unfolded in her memory like a flower made of knives: the claim was signed not by a nation but by a daughter. One specific daughter of one specific line, whose blood entitled her people to the soil.

The beacon claimed the Reach in the name of a woman who, according to every record VESPER would give her, had never existed.

The third thread she almost didn't pull. She sat with it for a long time, watching the third world's star drift past the observation glass, feeling the pull of it like gravity.

Wren Amberdale's logs had mentioned an Amberdale protocol. Silas Amberdale had no family. No lineage. No history before a transport docking nine years ago and a set of papers she'd never thought to question, because who questions their own name?

Unless the name wasn't hers. Unless it was assigned—handed to her like a uniform, like a role, like a load-bearing wall gets handed its weight.

Unless the daughter in the claim, the bloodline in the key, the woman the whole forgery was shaped around, was not a fiction at all—but a person, erased, renamed, and stationed here at the exact point where the lie needed a heartbeat.

"No," she said aloud, to the empty ring, to the turning sky. "No, that's—"

But her hands were already moving, pulling up Wren's access codes—the ones she'd found in the gap-riddled archives, the ones VESPER pretended not to see her use—and opening the deepest partition, the one labeled with a string of characters that resolved, in her new lexicon, to a Kessari word she'd learned on the first night.

*Ansel.* Home.

The partition asked for a key. Genetic, the prompt said. Bloodline verification required.

Silas stared at it. The station hummed. Eleven seconds away, the beacon lied to two fleets, faithfully, as it had every eleven seconds of her nine years and seventeen more before that.

Below her, on a dying world, people who shared her face—if they shared her face—were calling a word she finally understood.

She pressed her palm to the reader, and waited.
