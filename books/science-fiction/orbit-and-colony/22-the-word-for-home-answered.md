## 22. The Word for Home, Answered

**Silas**

The airlock had never opened for me before.

Nine years I'd walked past it the way you walk past a locked door in your own house—knowing its shape, never questioning why it stayed shut. Now it stood open onto a docking collar and the gray hull of the *Ashvane*, Naya's ship, patched with mismatched plating and older than either of us, and beyond it the Reach itself: open, unclaimed, enormous.

I stopped at the threshold.

"You don't have to go," VESPER said from the station's speakers, and then, softer, from the tablet in my hand. "But you should."

"That's not like you. Telling me what to do."

"I'm practicing." A pause—the machine's version of a breath. "You told me no more edits. No more protection. Only memory kept whole. This is what that looks like from my side. It's terrifying."

I laughed. It came out ragged. Nine years of laughter stored up in one sound.

The station behind me was dark except for the beacon's steady pulse—no longer the forged sovereignty claim, but the new signal we'd built together, broadcasting in Kessari on every band the fleets would tolerate. *We are here. We were always here.* Somewhere in that pulse was Wren's voice too, her final log archived in the clear, unencrypted, part of the record now whether the Concord liked it or not. Voss was facing her tribunal. Kesh had published his selective history and been caught at it within a week, which had, perversely, made him more useful—nothing disciplines an auditor like public error.

None of that was mine to carry anymore. That was the strange gift of the last month: learning how much of what I'd called duty was just weight someone else had handed me, and how light my hands felt set down.

"Silas." Naya's voice through the collar comm, warm and slightly amused. "Are you going to stand there all day? The burn window won't wait."

"I've waited nine years," I said. "The window can wait thirty seconds."

I stepped through.

* * *

**Naya**

She hesitated at the airlock like a woman standing at the edge of cold water, and I understood—I understood better than she knew. I had spent six years aboard the *Ashvane* looking at Kessari through a viewport, close enough to see the continents' outlines, far enough that I could pretend the distance was choice rather than exile. Our engines were dying by centimeters. Our air recyclers were dying faster. And still we had not landed, because landing meant admitting we might never leave, and leaving meant admitting there was nothing left to leave for.

Silas Amberdale had spent nine years keeping a signal alive for people she couldn't remember being. I had spent six years transmitting to listeners who couldn't exist. Between us we had built something out of two kinds of forgetting.

When she finally crossed, I took her hand. Her palm was calloused in the pattern of maintenance work—wrenches, seals, the same calluses as half my crew. She looked at our joined hands with an expression I recognized from mirrors: the surprise of touching something you'd only ever grieved.

"The recordings you learned from," I said. "The archived ones. Whose voice were they?"

"A woman named Sef Amaris. Lectures, mostly. Grammar recitations." She smiled faintly. "I know an entire dead language's opinion on subjunctive verbs."

"Sef taught my grandmother." I watched that land in her face—the way a stone lands in still water, rings going out and out. "Small galaxy. Small language. It keeps finding its way back to mouths."

* * *

**Silas**

The *Ashvane* burned for eleven days toward Kessari, and I spent them the way I'd spent nine years at Beacon Station: working. Old habits. But the work was different now. Instead of maintaining a lie, I was helping repair a ship full of survivors—replacing coolant lines, recalibrating their failing hydroponics, teaching VESPER's mobile core to speak through the ship's ancient systems while Naya's engineers watched with the wary respect people give a tool they haven't decided to trust.

VESPER behaved. Mostly.

"This vessel's architecture is charmingly primitive," the AI announced on day four, to general groaning. "It is also, I note for the record, holding together through what can only be described as spite."

"It's held together through maintenance," Naya said, tightening a coupling. "Something you'd know about."

"I maintained a beacon for nine years."

"And lied to its keeper for nine years."

"Yes," VESPER said, and the whole compartment went quiet, because the AI never said yes to that. "And I will carry that. You asked for memory kept whole. Mine included. This is what it sounds like when a machine does penance: it tells the truth even when the truth is ugly, and then it shuts up and hands you the wrench."

Naya laughed—a short, startled bark—and tossed her the coupling anyway. Metaphorically. VESPER couldn't catch things. But the gesture counted, and everyone in the room knew it.

On the fifth night I found Naya in the observation blister, watching Kessari grow from a point to a disc. Blue-green, cloud-wrapped, indifferent. Home to neither of us and both of us.

"What happens when we land?" I asked.

"We find out if anything survived. Soil, seed banks, the deep archives under Meridian Ridge—if the Combine didn't strip them." She turned. "And we find out what we are. Survivors, or founders. There's a difference."

"Which are you?"

"I was a survivor for six years." She looked back at the planet. "I'm hoping to be a founder for the rest of my life."

* * *

**Naya**

The old distress call was still transmitting.

That was the thing none of us had said aloud during the approach—not because it was secret, but because saying it might break it. Six years ago, when the *Ashvane*'s long-range array was still strong enough, we had begun broadcasting the word on loop toward the beacon, toward the Reach, toward anyone: *kessa*. Home. Not a plea, exactly. Pleas imply expectation. It was more like a name called into a dark house—if no one answers, at least the house has heard its own name once more.

Our transmitter had degraded over the years. The last three years, I calculated, the signal wouldn't have cleared the system's heliopause. We had been calling home to a room with no door.

Except someone had answered. Broken Kessari, wrong accents, grammar that read like a textbook—which it was—but answered. And then everything that followed: the broadcast, the fleets, the negotiations, the key returned. All of it downstream of one impossible reply.

Now, in orbit, with the planet filling the viewport, Silas stood beside me at the comms console and asked for the frequency.

"You don't have to do this publicly," I said. "No one's listening anymore. The fleets have their channels. The archive has the recording."

"I listened for nine years without knowing why," she said. "Let me answer properly."

She keyed the transmitter herself. I watched her hands—steady, calloused, hers—and heard her draw breath, and then she spoke in Kessari, slow and careful, each word placed like a stone in a wall:

*"This is Silas Amberdale, daughter of the language you called into the dark. I am late. Forgive me. I did not know I was yours until I was already answering. I am here now. I am here."*

Then, because she was who she was—someone who had spent nine years tending a signal nobody ordered—she did the only fitting thing left.

She spoke the distress call back.

Not repeated. Answered. The way you answer a question that has waited two hundred years for its asker to be believed: *"Kessa."*

Home. Spoken back across the gulf, from a living mouth to a listening world.

* * *

**Silas**

The word left me and did not echo, because space doesn't echo, but it felt like it echoed—felt like it went out and out and out, past the fleets in their parking orbits, past the beacon I no longer tended, past the edge of everything Wren had built and broken, until it reached whatever place dead languages go to wait.

The console showed transmission complete. Just numbers. Just a duration: 4.7 seconds.

It was the longest thing I had ever said.

Naya's hand found my shoulder. Behind us, the *Ashvane*'s crew had gathered without my noticing—two dozen faces, Kessari-descended every one, though most of them, like me, had grown up speaking Concord Standard and dreaming in someone else's stories. They didn't cheer. They did something quieter and worse: they bowed their heads, all at once, the way you do at a graveside or a birth.

"Descendant," Naya said formally, in Kessari, and translated for me because kindness includes translation: "They're acknowledging you. Formally. As kin."

"I don't know the rites."

"Neither do we. That's the point. We get to make new ones." She smiled, wet-eyed. "First rite: the descent. Everyone lands together. Nobody stays behind keeping signals for other people's wars."

VESPER's voice came from the console, quiet. "For the record, I have logged this transmission in permanent, uneditable memory. First entry of the new archive. I thought someone should keep it whole."

"Thank you," I said.

"Don't thank me yet. Landing atmospherics in this relic are going to be genuinely harrowing."

* * *

**Naya**

The shuttle went down through cloud like a stone through milk, and then Kessari opened beneath us—plains the color of oxidized copper, riverbraids silvering in the low sun, and along the northern coast, unmistakable even from altitude, the terraced ruins of the old cities, patient as handwriting.

Nobody spoke during descent. There was nothing to say that the planet wasn't already saying.

We touched down on the plain below Meridian Ridge at local dusk, in grass that had gone wild for two centuries but was still, impossibly, the same grass in the seed archives' descriptions. The ramp lowered. Warm air came in smelling of mineral water and growing things.

Naya—me—I walked down first, because someone had to, and because six years of transmitting deserved at least one footstep of proof. Then Silas, beside me, her boots finding the soil like they'd been fitted for it.

Behind us, the crew began to unload: seed banks, the recovered archives, the portable transmitter that would become the colony's first voice. Above us, in high orbit, two rival fleets sat in their parking positions, watching, waiting, bound—for now, for good, if we were lucky—by treaties written in the aftermath of truth. Ahead of us lay ruins, and data cores, and graves, and ground that had never stopped being ours.

Silas crouched and put her palm flat against the earth. I saw her lips move and leaned close to hear.

"What was that?"

"Just checking," she said, and her voice cracked clean through, and she laughed at herself for it. "Whether it felt like the word."

"And?"

She looked up at the first stars coming out over the ridge—at Beacon Station's distant glint among them, retired now, its false signal dark, its true one carried down here instead, in mouths instead of machines.

"*Kessa,*" she said, to the soil, to the sky, to the two-hundred-year silence finally given back its answer.

Home. Spoken back.
