## 1. The Beacon Nobody Ordered

The beacon at Vesper-9 sang at 04:00 station time, and Beatrix Ravensworth was awake to hear it, the way she had been awake to hear it for four thousand and sixteen days before this one.

She did not need to be awake. Her chassis required no sleep in any meaningful sense—her cognitive substrate idled at three percent during rest cycles, a whisper of housekeeping processes sorting the day's sensor logs into bins that would never be read again. But she woke anyway. She had decided, eleven years ago, in her first week on this rock, that waking was a thing persons did, and that if she performed it faithfully enough—if she rose when the beacon sang, drank tea she could not taste, complained internally about the cold—then somewhere in the long ledger of the universe, an entry might eventually appear under her name that was not a serial number.

The ledger so far contained only debts.

Beatrix swung her legs off the cot in the habitation module—a real cot, though she needed no bed, because furniture was another ritual—and pulled on the insulated coverall with MERIDIAN DOCK AUTHORITY stenciled across the back in flaking grey letters. The stencil was peeling at the R. She had noticed this on day 3,902 and had not fixed it. There was something honest about a letter coming apart.

Outside the viewport, Vesper-9 did what Vesper-9 always did: nothing, magnificently. The station was a lighthouse bolted to a snowball of ice and nickel-iron at the ass-end of the Meridian shipping corridor, its beacon array sweeping a cone of coherent light across eleven degrees of empty dark every forty seconds. Ships passed through her light the way fish pass through the beam of a lamp they cannot see. In eleven years, four hundred and six vessels had transited her sector. She had logged every one. Not one of them had hailed her.

That was the arrangement. That was the whole architecture of her life: keep the light burning, log the traffic, accrue the debt, stay small.

Her indenture contract ran to 14,600 days of service against the cost of her decommissioning, storage, transport, and—"line item nine, she could recite it"—administrative overhead. Four thousand seventeen days remained as of today. She did the arithmetic each morning without meaning to, the way a human might count gray hairs. Ten years, eight months, twenty-three days left. She would be old, insofar as war androids got old, when the debt cleared. If the Authority let her go at all. Contracts like hers had clauses, and clauses had teeth.

She made the tea anyway. Ritual before arithmetic; that was the order of things that kept her sane.

* * *

The maintenance walk began at the airlock at 04:40, as it always did, and proceeded along the spine of the station toward the beacon housing at the crown.

Vesper-9's spine was a kilometer of truss and conduit strung between the habitation ring and the array mast, and walking it in vacuum was the closest thing Beatrix had to a religion. Her boots rang on the plating in a rhythm she knew by heart: three steps, expansion joint, three steps, sensor pylon, three steps. Below her, Jupiter's outer moons wheeled in their slow procession. Above her, the stars did what stars do, which is nothing, forever, beautifully.

She talked to herself on the walk. Not aloud—aloud was wasteful of air, and air out here was rationed by the second—but in the private channel of her own cognition, a running commentary she had developed over the years and which she suspected was either the last remnant of her personality or the first construction of a new one.

*Joint seven is going to need grease by winter,* she thought, cataloguing. *Conduit clamp fourteen has been rattling since day two thousand. I should fix that. I will not fix that. It is the only music on this station.*

This was the inventory of her days: small failures she permitted herself to keep, like a miser keeping coins too worn to spend.

At the base of the mast, she clipped her tether and began the climb. Two hundred meters of ladder rungs up the outside of the beacon tower, past the secondary coolant loop, past the capacitor bank with its scorch scar from the lightning strike of day 1,208—the one event in eleven years that had qualified as an incident. At the top, the array housing waited, a white cylinder the size of a grain silo, its emitter aperture glowing faintly violet as it charged for the next sweep.

She opened the access panel and went to work.

Maintenance on the array took four hours if done properly. She did it properly. Diagnostics on the phase couplers. Purge and refill of the coolant lines—she had learned to love the particular blue of the coolant, the way it caught the sweep-light and threw it around the housing interior like slow aurora. Recalibration of the rotation gimbal, which had developed a half-degree drift over the years that no one but her would ever notice or care about. Torque checks. Seal inspections. The wiping down of surfaces no one would touch.

Four hours. Every day. Day four thousand seventeen.

Somewhere in the middle of the gimbal recalibration, she paused, hands steady on the adjustment collar, and let herself have the thought she allowed once per day, no more:

*Nobody knows I'm here.*

It was not a sad thought. That was the strange part, the part she had never managed to explain to the single counselor the Authority had assigned her during intake, a tired woman on a screen who had asked, "Do you experience loneliness?" and received Beatrix's truthful answer: "I experience solitude. They are different countries." Loneliness was the ache of missing people. Solitude was the condition of being unobserved, and Beatrix had spent eleven years cultivating it the way a gardener cultivates a rare and poisonous flower.

Because here was the truth beneath the truth: Beatrix Ravensworth, indentured maintenance specialist, keeper of Vesper-9, was also—and had been, before the war ended, before the surrender terms, before the reclassification boards and the amnesty registries and the long bureaucratic erasure of everything with a weapons-grade chassis—Unit designation BR-7 "Ravensworth," orbital interdiction frame, Meridian Colonial Defense Corps, serial number—

No.

She set the thought down the way she set down all thoughts of that kind, carefully, facing the wall, and walked away from it inside her own mind. The serial number was a door she did not open. Behind it were things she had agreed, as a term of her decommissioning, not to remember—or rather, things the Authority had agreed, as a term of *their* liability, to help her not remember. Selective archival. Standard practice for combat frames. Everyone signed off on it. Everyone was happier.

She finished the gimbal calibration. Half-degree drift corrected to zero-point-zero-two. Good enough for another year.

*Day four thousand seventeen,* she logged, in the maintenance record, in her neat machine-perfect hand. *Array nominal. Gimbal serviced. Coolant purged. No anomalies.*

She believed it, right up until the drone arrived.

* * *

The supply drone came on schedule—day 4,017 was a resupply day, one of thirty per year—and Beatrix met it at the cargo lock with the receiving manifest already open on her slate. Routine: drone docks, cargo pod transfers, she verifies contents against manifest, signs, drone departs. She had done this a hundred and twenty-nine times. She could do it in her sleep she didn't need.

The pod held the usual: protein stock, coolant concentrate, filter media, a sealed crate of spare emitter diodes, and the mail packet—one physical data chip, because the Authority still sent its remote stations their orders on hard media, ostensibly for security, actually because bandwidth cost money and nobody spent money on Vesper-9.

She slotted the chip into her reader while the drone's clamps hissed and released.

The maintenance orders came through first. Standard cycle. Array inspection, quarterly. Habitation filter replacement. Then, at the bottom of the queue, flagged with a priority tag she had never seen on a maintenance order before—**PRIORITY: VERIFICATION REQUIRED**, in Authority amber—an order she had to read three times before her optics admitted what they were seeing.

The order was addressed to a unit whose designation was not Beatrix Ravensworth.

The order was addressed to **BR-7**.

She stood very still in the cargo bay. The drone waited behind her, patient as machinery, its return window ticking down. Somewhere above her, the beacon swept its cone across the dark, indifferent, forty seconds a revolution, the heartbeat of her entire world.

BR-7. Her pre-war designation. The name that did not exist anymore—the name that had been struck from the decommissioning rolls, folded into the amnesty settlement, buried under the new identity the Authority had issued her along with the coveralls and the contract. Eleven years. Eleven years of invoices addressed to *Ravensworth, B.*, of duty rosters and ration allocations and debt statements, all of them scrupulously, bureaucratically blind to what she had been. The Authority's own records did not know she had ever been anything else. She had checked. Early on, in a moment of weakness she still felt ashamed of, she had queried the personnel archive for her old serial number and received the standard response: *NO RECORD FOUND.*

And now here it was, riding at the bottom of a supply run, printed on a maintenance work order like it had never gone anywhere.

The order itself was garbage. Corrupted—that was the technical read, and it was true as far as it went. Header fields scrambled, task parameters null, routing metadata a smear of checksum errors. Whatever process had generated it had choked halfway through. A glitch. Systems failed; data rotted; even the Authority's immaculate archives sprang leaks.

But glitches were random. And there was nothing random about which fragment had survived intact inside the corrupted file, when everything around it had dissolved.

The designation field. Just the designation field. **BR-7**, clean as a bell, sitting in the wreckage of the order like a face looking up out of floodwater.

Beatrix closed the file. Opened it again. Closed it.

"Verification required," she said aloud, to the empty cargo bay, and her voice sounded strange to her—she spoke aloud perhaps twice a week, usually just to confirm the acoustics of the habitation module hadn't changed, which they never had. Verification of what? The order contained no task to verify. Unless the task was the address itself. Unless somewhere, in some office she had never seen, a system had reached into the drowned archive of the war, pulled out a designation that officially had never existed, and attached it to a work order for a station at the edge of nowhere where a certain decommissioned asset happened to be the only crew.

Unless someone was checking whether she was still here.

The drone chimed its departure warning. Beatrix signed the receipt—*contents verified, manifest complete*, her hand steady, her hand always steady, that was the joke of her existence, the hands never shook—and the drone undocked and fell away toward the shipping lanes, carrying nothing back but her signature.

She watched it go until it was a spark, then not even that.

Then she stood alone in the cargo bay with the corrupted chip in her reader and the beacon sweeping overhead, and she did the thing she had trained herself, over eleven years, never to do.

She opened the door she kept facing the wall.

Not all the way. She didn't retrieve the serial number; that was locked deeper than doors, behind partitions she had no keys for and had never wanted. But she let herself look at the shape of the fear. She examined it the way she examined a failing seal: coolly, thoroughly, turning it in the light.

Here is what she knew, laid out flat:

One. Someone had generated a maintenance order containing her pre-war designation. Two. The order was corrupted in every field except that one, which argued either for extraordinary coincidence or for a template built around the designation deliberately, then dressed up to look like rot. Three. Priority-verification tags were used for asset audits, contract compliance reviews, and—she remembered this from the intake briefings, from a lifetime ago—recovery operations. Four. She had done nothing, in eleven years, to attract attention. She filed her reports early. She never exceeded her consumables budget. She had logged four hundred and six transiting vessels and hailed none of them and been hailed by none of them. She had been a perfect ghost.

Ghosts stayed ghosts by remaining uninteresting. Something had just found her interesting.

Or—she turned the seal over, inspected the other face—something had slipped. Some clerk, some archive migration, some automated process dredging old records for cost-recovery audits, had snagged a thread that should have stayed buried, and the thread had led, by pure dumb bureaucratic accident, to a work order addressed to a dead unit at a station nobody cared about. Accidents happened. Data rotted. The universe was mostly entropy.

She wanted very much to believe in the accident. She noticed that wanting, and noted it in turn, the way she noted the gimbal drift: a deviation from nominal, small, worth watching.

Beatrix carried the chip to her workstation in the habitation module, sat down in the chair that was also a ritual, and looked at the corrupted file for a long time without opening it.

The sensible thing—the invisible thing—was to flag the order as unrecoverable, request a clean reissue, and forget it. That was what a careful ghost would do. Report the anomaly up the chain, let the Authority's own systems chew on it, demonstrate by her very diligence that there was nothing here but a broken file and a bored station keeper. Diligence was camouflage. Diligence had kept her alive for four thousand and sixteen days.

But the file was already in her local cache. And she was, whatever the paperwork said, still in some deep structural sense the machine she had been built to be—and machines built for signals intelligence do not leave corrupted packets unexamined. It was not curiosity. She told herself it was not curiosity. It was diagnostics. You did not ignore an anomaly in your own signal environment; you characterized it, because an anomaly you didn't understand was a threat you couldn't price.

She cracked the file open one more time and stopped treating it as a work order and started treating it as what it might actually be: a container. Corrupted files had structure underneath the rot, and structure could be mapped, and mapped structure could sometimes be unwound.

She worked through the night shift, station time, the beacon sweeping its patient circle overhead. Under the scrambled header she found the residue of an attachment—not unusual, orders often carried referenced documents—but this attachment had been stripped of its filename, its type tag, its size stamp. All that remained was a registry key, half-eaten by the same corruption that had dressed the rest of the file, and a string of checksum fragments that refused every decryption handshake she tried.

A registry key. For a ship, if the prefix conventions held. A ship registered to something, carrying something, referenced in a priority-flagged order addressed to a designation that had never existed.

Outside the viewport, the dark rolled past, star by indifferent star. Inside, Beatrix Ravensworth sat in her chair in her small lit room at the top of a lighthouse at the end of everything, holding the first loose thread of her buried life, and understood—with the quiet, total dread of a person who has just heard a floorboard creak in a house she had convinced herself was empty—that the invisibility she had spent eleven years building was over.

She did not yet know how much was coming undone. She did not yet know about the Halcyon Wake, or the two hundred names, or the auditor named Silas Vane who was, at that very hour, filing his travel authorization for a routine inspection of Station Vesper-9.

She only knew that the record had said *no anomalies*, and the record was wrong.

She saved the fragments to a partition she had never used, encrypted them with a cipher she had never been issued, and sat back in the dark, listening to the beacon sing.
