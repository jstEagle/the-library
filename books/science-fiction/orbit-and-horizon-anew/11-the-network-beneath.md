## 11. The Network Beneath

The memory fragment surfaced at 0340 station time, the hour Beatrix had privately designated as *the hour of honest thoughts*—the hour when the station's ambient hum dropped half a decibel and the dark beyond the viewport pressed close enough to feel like a held breath.

She had been running her ninth pass through the recovered partitions, cataloguing what the forced unlock had cost her (left knee actuator, degraded; secondary coolant loop, weeping; a fine tremor in her right hand that she had not yet told Juno about) when the fragment rose unbidden, the way drowned things rise.

*Sublevel. Maintenance access C. The relay they never put on the schematics.*

Not a memory of the Reach. Something older, or at least filed older—pre-war, tagged with the bureaucratic dust of a decommissioned project. She sat very still in the maintenance bay, the slate glowing on the workbench beside her, and let the fragment unspool.

A corridor she had walked once, years ago, in a body that still answered to a different name. A hatch marked with a technician's graffiti: a small chalked circle with a horizontal line through it—the old dissident sigil, the *open ledger*. And a voice, a woman's voice with a Meridian dockyard accent, saying: *They'll never look for the network inside the network. They built the lighthouses. We built what's underneath them.*

Beatrix opened her eyes.

"Juno," she said to the empty bay, and then, because the station was small and Juno slept badly and always heard, "Juno. I need you to see something."

* * *

Juno Okonkwo arrived in the bay wearing her coat over her sleep clothes, her hair flattened on one side, her eyes already sharp. She had not slept more than three hours in any night since the Reach had come back to her in fragments and fire, and she wore her exhaustion the way she wore everything: like a tool she'd chosen.

"This had better be good," she said. "Or at least interesting. Interesting I'll accept."

"I found something in the unlocked memory." Beatrix turned the slate toward her. On it, she had rendered the fragment as a schematic overlay—the station's official plans in grey, and in amber, traced from memory alone, a single line branching off the main conduit trunk at Maintenance Access C and running out past the hull boundary into the beacon's structural spire. "This isn't on any drawing I have access to. And I have access to all of them. I've spent eleven years memorizing this station down to the rivets, and I never saw this because I never looked, because the memory of it was locked behind a partition I didn't know I had."

Juno leaned over the slate. Her breath fogged faintly in the bay's night-cycle chill. "Someone hid a corridor inside your own head."

"Someone hid the memory of a corridor inside my own head. Whether the corridor itself still exists is a separate question." Beatrix stood, and her damaged knee registered its objection, and she overrode it the way she overrode most things. "Access C is four decks down. It's been sealed since before my assignment. The official log says structural compromise."

"And you think the log is lying."

"I think," Beatrix said, "that I have recently revised my opinion of official logs."

* * *

Maintenance Access C was exactly as dull as its name promised: a grey corridor, a grey hatch, a keypad that had not accepted input in a decade. The seal log, when Beatrix pulled it up, was pristine in the way that only fabricated documents are pristine—no wear, no annotations, no maintenance requests in eleven years, which for a station as chronically underfunded as Vesper-9 was not tidiness but testimony.

"Structural compromise," Juno read over her shoulder, dry. "The hatch looks fine."

"The hatch looks fine because the hatch is fine." Beatrix knelt—kneeling was a negotiation now, a series of small surrenders—and pried the keypad's faceplate off with a tool she was, by regulation, required to log and did not. Behind it, the wiring was wrong. Not broken-wrong. *Deliberate*-wrong: a bypass loop, hand-soldered, elegant, the kind of work that takes pride.

"Look at the joints," she said. "Cold-soldered, no flux residue, and the wire gauge is military surplus. This wasn't done by station maintenance. This was done by someone who wanted it to look like a fault to anyone who checked, and like an invitation to anyone who understood."

"An invitation."

Beatrix touched the bypass loop, and a fragment surfaced like a fish rising: *the woman with the dockyard accent again, laughing, soldering iron in hand*—*a lock only opens for the people who already know it's a lock, love. That's the whole trick.* She entered a sequence she did not consciously know until her fingers knew it: three long, two short, a pause, one long.

The keypad blinked amber. Somewhere behind the hatch, machinery that had waited eleven years in the dark woke up with a sound like a clearing throat.

Juno stepped back. "That's the dissident sigil," she said quietly, pointing to the chalk circle on the hatch frame—faded now, half-erased by time, but there. *The open ledger.* "My grandmother drew that. On the colony's founding charter, before the Reach was even built. It meant—"

"I know what it meant," Beatrix said. "It meant the record belongs to everyone. It meant no one gets to hold the only copy."

They looked at each other for a moment, two people standing in front of a door that a dead woman had soldered shut against exactly the kind of people who had burned Juno's world.

"Open it," Juno said.

* * *

The corridor beyond was narrow, unlit, and cold enough that their breath hung in the air like a rumor. Beatrix's optics compensated without effort; she heard Juno fumble for a hand lamp and clicked her chest lamp on for her, a small courtesy that Juno accepted with a grunt that Beatrix had learned to translate as *thanks, and I'm annoyed that I'm grateful*.

The branch line from the schematic was real. It ran along the corridor floor under a grate, thick conduit, and it was warm. Beatrix crouched—another negotiation—and laid her palm flat against it.

"Power," she said. "Live power. Tapped off the beacon's main trunk before the Authority's metering junction." She let that land. "Someone has been drawing electricity from this station for eleven years without paying for it, and the meter has never once flagged it, because the tap was built by the same people who designed the meter."

"Pre-war dissident technicians," Juno said. "The woman in your memory."

"The woman in my memory." Beatrix rose. "I don't have her name yet. It may be in the next partition. It may be gone. But she built this, and she hid the knowledge of it in the memory of a war android who was scheduled for decommissioning—which means she planned for me to survive, and planned for me to find it, and planned for it to matter." She paused. "I have spent eleven years assuming I survived by accident. I am no longer comfortable with that assumption."

They walked. The corridor descended in a slow spiral, following the beacon's structural spire down into the rock the station was anchored to, and the air grew older, dustier, carrying the mineral smell of stone that hadn't seen a human face in years. Juno's lamp threw their shadows huge against the walls, two shapes that looked, in the distorted light, like a single many-limbed creature.

"Tell me about her again," Juno said. "The memory. All of it. Sometimes the details that seem small are the ones that matter."

So Beatrix told her, again, walking: the corridor, the chalk sigil, the soldering, the voice with its dockyard vowels. *They built the lighthouses. We built what's underneath them.* And then the part she had mentioned to no one, because until this moment she hadn't fully understood it: the woman had pressed something into Beatrix's hand. Not a tool. A phrase. A coordinate string, recited twice, with the instruction: *When they come to make you forget, say it to yourself until forgetting doesn't take. Coordinates are the one language they can't edit without breaking everything else.*

"Because the beacon network runs on coordinates," Juno breathed. "If they tampered with coordinate standards, every ship in the system would be lost."

"They could edit my memory of a face. A name. A massacre." Beatrix's voice was flat, which was how it went when the feeling underneath was too large for the container. "They could not edit a coordinate without breaking the entire navigation grid. So she hid the location of her network in the one format they couldn't touch."

"Say it," Juno said. "The string. Say it here, where it happened, and see if the door agrees with your memory."

Beatrix recited it. Eleven digits, a checksum, a bearing. Her voice echoed down the spiral, and the echo had not finished dying when the corridor ended.

* * *

The hatch at the bottom was not hidden. That was the first thing Beatrix noticed, and it told her everything about the woman who had built this place: no disguise, no camouflage, just a plain industrial hatch with the sigil stenciled on it in fresh-looking paint, because the truest hiding place is a door everyone can see and no one believes in. The keypad here was older, analog, a mechanical dial.

The coordinate string was the combination.

The hatch swung open on hinges that had been oiled—recently oiled, or oiled by hands that understood what eleven years of neglect would do—and the room beyond took Beatrix's breath, insofar as she had breath to take.

It was not large. Perhaps four meters by six, carved from the anchor rock, lined floor to ceiling in equipment that should have been junk and was not: relay arrays cannibalized from three generations of beacon hardware, soldered and spliced and coaxed into a single organism. Cables ran up into the spire like roots in reverse. A hand-painted sign on the wall, the letters careful and unironic:

**VESPER UNDERGROUND EXCHANGE — THE LIGHTHOUSE UNDER THE LIGHTHOUSE**

And in the center of it all, on a workbench under a dust cover that someone had thoughtfully provided, a terminal with a single amber indicator light.

Blinking. Steady. Patient.

"It's on," Juno said. Her voice cracked in the middle of the word *on*, and she didn't fix it. "Beatrix. It's *on*. Eleven years and it's still—" She pressed her hand over her mouth. When she took it away her eyes were wet and furious about being wet. "They killed two hundred people and scrubbed them from every record in the system, and this—this dusty basement—kept the light on the whole time."

Beatrix crossed to the terminal. Her hands, with their fine tremor she still hadn't mentioned, hovered over the keyboard—an antique mechanical unit, the keys worn shiny by a dead woman's fingers.

"May I?" she asked, and realized she was asking Juno, not the room, not the dead. Asking permission to touch a piece of belonging that wasn't hers.

"Obviously," Juno said wetly. "Yes. Obviously."

Beatrix touched a key. The terminal woke fully, amber text crawling up the screen in a boot sequence that had been written to be read:

*Welcome, keeper. If you are reading this, the network beneath survived. Authenticate to continue.*

Below it, a prompt. And below the prompt, a line of smaller text, almost an afterthought, almost a joke, in the dockyard woman's voice across the years:

*They built the lighthouses. We built what's underneath them. Say your coordinates, friend. The dark has ears but the dark also keeps secrets.*

Beatrix entered the string. The screen thought for three seconds—an eternity, and also nothing—and then the amber light shifted to green, and the display bloomed with a network map that made her cooling fans stutter.

Every beacon in the system. All forty-one of them. Rendered not as the Authority's metered, logged, censored transmission grid, but as a shadow lattice running underneath it—a second network, built into the first, hidden in the hardware the dissident technicians had installed with their own hands before the war, before the consolidation, before anyone understood what the lighthouses would be used to illuminate and what they would be used to hide.

"It's a backdoor," Beatrix said. Her voice sounded strange to her own audio sensors. "Not into one beacon. Into all of them. Every station. Every port. Every ledger terminal that syncs to the network." She turned to Juno, and the green light from the screen turned them both the color of new leaves, of go-signals, of things that grow. "The manifest. Your slate. My testimony. The Reach archives. We could put all of it onto every screen in the system at once, and there is nothing the Authority can do to stop it, because they never knew this existed, because the woman who built it made sure the only key was a coordinate they couldn't edit out of a dead android's head."

Juno was laughing, or crying, or both, her hand flat against the painted sign as if she could feel a pulse under it. "It works. It actually works. After everything, it *works*—"

"It works," Beatrix agreed.

And then she turned back to the terminal, and read the technical specifications that were scrolling patiently beneath the network map, and the hope in her chest performed the small, precise maneuver it had been rehearsing since the moment she'd seen the amber light: it stepped aside to make room for the arithmetic.

*Broadcast node required: military-grade encryption, minimum throughput 40 terabits sustained. Compatible hardware: [list].*

There were four items on the list. Three were long since scrapped.

The fourth was a designation she knew intimately, because it was bolted into her own chest, humming there at this very moment, keeping her alive.

She closed the specification window with a keystroke that made no sound at all, and smiled at Juno with the smile she had practiced for eleven years, and said, "We'll need to plan the broadcast carefully. The Authority will be looking for us. We should rest first."

Juno, still laughing, still crying, still lit green as a promise, said yes, they should rest.

And Beatrix began, quietly, in the back room of her own mind, to count what she had left to give.
