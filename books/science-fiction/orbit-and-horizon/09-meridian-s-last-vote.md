## 9. Meridian's Last Vote

Tomas Reyes had given the same speech three times in his life, and he had hated it every time.

The first was at his daughter Marisol's wedding, when he'd stood up with a glass of plum wine and told the room that love was like gravity—invisible, patient, and responsible for everything worth noticing. The second was at her funeral, four years later, when he'd stood up with nothing at all and discovered that silence carried further than any speech.

The third was tonight.

He stood in the assembly hall of Meridian Colony—the one building on the whole plateau they still heated properly, because cold made people argue and Tomas no longer had the stamina for arguments—and looked out at two hundred faces. Two hundred and six, if you counted the children asleep on coats in the back rows, which he did. He counted everyone. It was the only arithmetic left to him that mattered.

Two hundred and six souls on a colony built for forty thousand. A city that had once needed three tram lines now fit inside one hall, and even then the seats weren't full, because half the chairs had been sold for scrap and the other half were broken.

"You're wondering why I called you out in this weather," he said.

Outside, the wind coming off the ice fields was doing its best impression of a dying animal. Inside, the old fusion heater ticked and clanked like it was arguing with itself, which was more conversation than some of them had had all week.

"First," Tomas said, "I want to thank the kitchen crew. Whatever that stew was, it was hot, and nobody has died from it yet, which by our current standards counts as a triumph."

A ripple of laughter. Good. He'd need it. Laughter was a door, and you could walk through a door carrying heavy things if people opened it for you smiling.

"Second," he said, "I have a confession to make."

The laughter stopped. He had expected that too. He had rehearsed this moment so many times in the dark of his quarters that he knew its shape the way a blind man knows his own hallway—every creak, every turn, every place where the floor dropped away.

He gripped the edge of the podium. His hands had been shipwright's hands once, back when Meridian built things instead of selling pieces of itself off. Now they were just old hands, spotted and trembling, good mainly for holding things up.

"Forty-one of our neighbors are not dead," he said. "And they are not gone."

* * *

He had decided, in the end, to tell it as a story, because Meridian people trusted stories more than documents. Documents could be forged. Stories had to be lived with.

"Nine years ago," he began, "when the war scare started—you remember. Everyone remembers. The Concordance broadcasts, the troop movements two systems over, the man on the news saying *civilian infrastructure should prepare for contingency relocation.*"

He let them remember. He watched it move through the crowd like weather: the widow Okafor sitting very straight; young Devi Nair, who'd been a child then and was now the closest thing the colony had to an engineer; old Havel, who never sat down during announcements, standing at the back with his arms crossed as if daring the past to try something.

"The Concordance came to me privately. Not the fleet—a contractor. A polite woman with a briefcase full of contracts, offering what she called a *continuity service.*" He smiled, and it hurt. "She said: *In the event of catastrophe, your people can be preserved. Their memories, their selves, stored safely until reconstruction. Better than dying, Elder Reyes. Better than losing everything.*"

"And I said, how much."

Silence. Somewhere in the back, a sleeping child murmured and was hushed.

"She said the service would be funded by a partnership arrangement. That the colony's contribution would be—" He stopped. Started again. There was no way to say it gently, so he said it plainly, the way you pull a splinter. "Our memories. Not all of them. Selected archives. Childhoods, mostly. First loves. The things that make a person feel continuous. They called them *redundant emotional holdings.*"

"They paid us," he went on, before anyone could speak, "in evacuation guarantees, medical supplies, and a promise that after the war—if there was a war—every backup would be returned to its owner, restored, whole. Forty-one volunteers signed. Good people. People who wanted their grandchildren to have medicine more than they wanted their own memories of being nine years old."

He looked down at his notes, though he didn't need them. He had written them anyway, because his doctor said writing things down helped him hold on, and lately holding on was a skill he practiced daily, like a musician running scales.

"What I did not tell you," he said, "what I signed alone, in my office, at night, because I was ashamed before the ink was even dry—was the second clause. Page eleven. The clause that said the Concordance retained custody of the backups *in perpetuity, at their discretion,* and that the colony waived all claims."

He lifted his head.

"I sold pieces of forty-one people to buy time for two hundred. And when the war never came, and we asked for them back, the Concordance stopped answering our messages. And then, last month, they erased us from the charts entirely. As if we had never been here. As if the contract had never existed. As if *I* had never existed."

He stepped back from the podium. His knees were bad and his voice was going, but he made himself stand straight, because these people deserved a leader who stood up while confessing, not one who slumped like the confession was about him being tired.

"I am sorry," he said. "I have been sorry for nine years. Sorry is a small word for it. But it is the word I have, and I am giving it to you, all of it, tonight, because I will not let this colony be erased twice—once by the Concordance and once by my cowardice."

* * *

The silence afterward lasted long enough that Tomas heard the heater cycle down, tick, and start again.

It was Havel, of course, who broke it. Havel had been the colony's chief mechanic for thirty years and had never once accepted that anything was beyond repair, including meetings.

"So they have them," Havel said. "The backups. The forty-one."

"Yes."

"On that relay. The thing out past the shipping lanes that they told us was a weather station."

Tomas closed his eyes briefly. "Yes. That was part of it too. The volunteers were told their memories would be stored *locally, for safety.* Local meant the relay. I signed that page too." He opened his eyes. "I signed everything, Havel. That was my job. That was my sin."

Devi Nair raised her hand like they were in school, then seemed embarrassed and lowered it halfway. "Elder Reyes—sorry—Tomas. If the memories are stored on the relay... and the relay needs power..." She trailed off, but her face finished the sentence for her, and Tomas watched the horror travel row by row through the hall like a lamp being lit in each window.

"We don't know that," he said. "We don't know anything about how it works. That's the truth. What we know is that forty-one of ours are held somewhere we can't reach, by people who deleted our existence from the official record, and that I helped put them there."

"Why tell us now?" It was Widow Okafor, whose husband Mateo had been volunteer number nineteen. Her voice was not angry. That was somehow worse. "Why tonight?"

"Because a ship came," Tomas said. "Three days ago. A salvage freighter, the *Vesper's Lantern.* They've been asking questions. About the contracts. About the relay. And yesterday their navigator sent a message asking to meet with me privately, because they found something in the hold of their own ship that shouldn't exist, and they think it connects to us."

Murmuring now, real murmuring, the sound of a community waking up.

"And because," Tomas continued, over it, "I am dying. Dr. Adeyemi has been kind about it, but I read my own charts, and my charts say months, maybe weeks. And I refuse—I *refuse*—to leave this world with page eleven still folded in my pocket. You deserved the truth while I was alive to answer for it. So here it is. Here I am. Do with me what you will."

* * *

What they did with him, at first, was yell.

Not all of them. But enough. A man named Corrigan whose sister had been volunteer number seven stood up and shouted until his voice cracked, and Widow Okafor did not shout but asked questions in a flat, terrible voice—*did they suffer, do they know, are they aware in there*—and each question landed on Tomas like a stone placed carefully on a cairn. He answered everything he could and admitted everything he couldn't, and he did not defend himself, because there was no defense, only context, and context was what guilty men hid behind.

It went on for two hours. Then Devi Nair stood up and said, quietly, "Sit down, Corrigan. Sit down, everyone. Yelling at him doesn't get Mateo Okafor back."

And Corrigan sat down, because Devi had that kind of voice, the kind that sounded like reason even when it was furious.

It was old Havel, arms finally uncrossed, who asked the question that changed the shape of the night.

"Tomas. This freighter crew. The ones asking questions. Are they going to do something about it? Or are they just curious?"

"I don't know," Tomas said honestly. "Their captain strikes me as a woman who does not abandon cargo. And their navigator—" he paused, choosing the words, "—their navigator writes in his log about things other people ignore. Streets. Empty rooms. Names. He checked on our forty-one cradles in his hold like they were sleeping passengers. I think he is either the best man we could hope for or a fool, and in my experience those categories overlap considerably."

A few people laughed, wetly, the way people laugh when crying is close.

"So here is what I propose," Tomas said. "Not a decree. I'm done with decrees. I've seen what a man can do alone with a pen and good intentions. I propose we vote. All of us. Tonight. On whether we give the *Lantern* everything—the original contracts, the correspondence, the names of all forty-one volunteers, my testimony recorded and sealed. Whether we help them carry this to whatever end they're carrying it toward."

"And if the Concordance comes back?" someone asked. "If helping them paints a target on what's left of us?"

Tomas looked around the hall. At the patched walls. At the children asleep under coats. At the two hundred and six.

"My friends," he said, "we are two hundred people on a colony the Concordance has already deleted from every chart in human space. We are not hiding from a target. We *are* the target. The only question left is whether we stand still for it or whether we spend what we have left buying the truth a way out into the light."

He let that sit. Then, because warm words were cheap and he had spent nine years paying interest on cheap words, he added:

"I'll vote first. I vote yes. I'd vote yes if it killed me, and for all I know it will, since apparently stress is bad for the heart and I intend to be extremely stressed."

That got the laugh he wanted—the real one, the one that loosened shoulders—and Devi was already moving through the aisles with a slate, taking the count the old way, name by name, because Meridian didn't trust anonymous voting anymore, hadn't since the contractors taught them what anonymity was worth.

* * *

The vote took forty minutes, because everyone insisted on speaking, and Tomas let them, because that was the point. This was the last decision the colony would likely ever make together, and he would not rush it the way he had rushed the signing, alone, at night, ashamed.

One hundred and ninety-eight voted yes.

Six voted no—all of them parents of small children, and Tomas honored them most of all, because their no was an act of love and he told them so, publicly, and one of them, a young woman named Petra with a baby on her hip, cried and said she wished she could be brave, and Tomas said, "You are brave. Bravery isn't voting yes. Bravery is voting your conscience in a room full of people hoping you won't."

Two abstained. One was drunk. The other was Havel, who said, "I abstain because I don't vote on repairs. I just start fixing. Get me a channel to that freighter."

So it was done. By dawn, the *Vesper's Lantern* would have everything: the contracts, the names, the confession, the whole rotten beautiful history of what Meridian had traded to survive a war that never came.

Tomas stood at the hall's great window afterward, alone except for Devi, watching the stars come out over the ice fields. His chest ached in the way it always ached now, deep and slow, a tide going out.

"You did the right thing," Devi said.

"I did a thing," Tomas said. "Whether it was right depends on what happens next. That's the trouble with right things. They're only right in retrospect, and retrospect is a luxury I'm short on."

Below the horizon, far out past the shipping lanes where no ships came anymore, something glinted—or perhaps he imagined it. A light where no light should be. Moving. Descending.

Devi saw it too. He knew because she went very still.

"Tomas," she said. "Is the *Lantern* supposed to be arriving tonight?"

"No," he said slowly. "They're still two days out."

They watched together as the light grew, steady and deliberate, falling toward Meridian out of the dark like a verdict.

"That's not a freighter," Devi whispered.

"No," said Tomas Reyes, and felt his old heart hammering against its failing walls, furious to be alive, furious to see this. "That's a warship. Somebody knew we voted."

He turned from the window and reached for the comm panel, and behind him, in the hall, two hundred and six people were beginning to wake.
