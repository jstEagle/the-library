## 21. Horizon

**[Recovered log fragment — Felix Zhou, personal. Timestamp corrupted. Playback begins mid-entry.]**

—don't know how long I've been sitting here. The galley smells like burnt tea again, which means Sef has been trying to make tea, which means I should probably go rescue the kettle before it files a grievance. But I wanted to write this down first, while it's still true.

I remembered the kettle this morning.

Not everything. Not even close. But I walked into the galley and I saw the scorch mark on the bulkhead above the burner—the one shaped a little like a hand, a little like a bird—and I knew, without being told, that it was mine. That I'd made it. That I'd laughed about it and never fixed it because fixing it felt like admitting I was the kind of person who burned tea, and apparently I would rather live with a permanent scorch mark than a permanent character flaw.

Sef says that's very consistent of me.

Sef says a lot of things. She's been reading me my own life for—weeks? I genuinely don't know. Time in dead space is less a measurement and more a suggestion. But every morning she sits across from me with my log open on her slate and she reads, and sometimes I remember along with her, and sometimes I just listen, the way you'd listen to a story about a stranger who happens to have your hands.

The stranger seems like a decent man. I hope I grow into him.

* * *

**[Fragment continues. Some passages degraded.]**

She offered to give it all back.

I want that on the record, in case anyone ever wonders—Sef never held anything back from me. Not the choice. She sat me down in the hold, where the empty cradles still sit in their rows like forty-one little beds after the children have grown and gone, and she said, "I can give you all of it. Every fragment that's left in me. I checked with Amara. It would work."

And then she said the other part. The part that costs.

Every piece she returns, she loses. That's the physics of it—memory doesn't transfer, it *trades*. It's not a copy machine, it's a scale. Amara explained it in her careful, horrified doctor's voice, the voice she uses when the universe has disappointed her again. Sef just explained it like a fourteen-year-old who has already decided: "So how much do you want?"

Not *how much can you give*. How much do I *want*.

I sat there for a long time looking at those empty cradles. And I thought about the man in the log—the one who logged empty streets and counted sleepers and kept a private record of a colony everyone else had agreed to forget. He was a man made almost entirely of what he remembered. Grief, mostly. Grief and duty and a stubborn, stubborn refusal to let go.

And I thought: I like the scorch mark.

* * *

**[Fragment. Handwriting analog: voice-only entry. Speaker sounds tired. Also, faintly, amused.]**

I took back some things. I want to be honest about which ones, because I think the choosing matters more than the having.

I took back Ilsa.

All of her. The contract she signed, the crew she saved with it, the years of guilt she carried like ballast, the last thing she said to me on the medbay cot—*"Amnesty is just a quieter erasure, Felix. Don't let them erase you politely."* I took back the way her hands shook and didn't shake at the same time, and the way she called me "Navigator" when she was angry and "Felix" when she was scared, and how I could always tell the difference.

That one hurt going in. It hurt like swallowing something too big. I sat in the corridor outside the medbay for an hour afterward, holding all of her, and I cried the way I imagine I cried the first time—except the first time I had the memory of crying about it and this time I just had the crying. Cleaner, somehow. Worse, somehow. Both.

But she's mine now. All the Ilsa there is. I checked with Sef afterward—she still has her own fragments of Ilsa, the ones she gathered herself, the ones from the reading and the rescue and the dying—and we compared notes like two people who'd both loved the same stubborn woman from different angles. We agreed: she'd have hated all this sentiment and demanded we get back to work.

So we got back to work.

I took back Meridian. The streets, the vote, Tomas's confession. I took back the smell of the hydroponics bays and the sound of two hundred people choosing to face erasure with their eyes open. I took back the forty-one names. All forty-one. I recited them to Sef last night like a litany and she checked them against the log and I got every one. I cried about that too. Apparently that's also consistent.

I did *not* take back the flight through dead space.

Sef was offended. Genuinely offended, arms crossed, the whole performance. "You flew through *other people's memories*," she said. "You steered a freighter through forty-one lifetimes. That was the most beautiful thing that's ever happened on this ship and you don't want it back?"

"It's not that I don't want it," I told her. "It's that I want to hear *your* version."

Because here's the thing I've figured out, sitting in this galley with my scorch mark and my partial self: memory isn't a possession. Ilsa thought it was—I took that back too, the way she hoarded her guilt like a miser. The Concordance thought it was, something you could own and sell and spend as fuel. Even the colony thought it was, something you could bank against disaster.

But it isn't. It's a *story*. And a story changes depending on who tells it. The version of dead space that lives in Sef is not the version that lived in me. Hers has a fourteen-year-old in it, terrified and magnificent, holding a whole ship together with borrowed lives. Mine had a navigator white-knuckling a yoke, trusting a child with everything. Same flight. Different stories. And I'd rather have hers—new, told in her voice, with her terrible dramatic pauses—than recover my own like picking up a coat I'd already worn out.

Amara says that's not how the physics works. Amara says a returned fragment is a returned fragment, identical or nothing.

Amara also biologically aged backward sixty years and spent nine years being lied to by her own government, so I feel comfortable saying her relationship to how things are *supposed* to work is complicated.

* * *

**[Fragment. Background noise: engine hum, higher than standard. Course corrections, frequent. Someone humming off-key—identified by context as Sef.]**

We left dead space on a Tuesday.

I know that because Sef told me it was a Tuesday, and I've decided to believe her, because a man rebuilding himself out of a log doesn't get to be picky about his sources. She announced it with great ceremony—"We are leaving dead space, on a Tuesday, at approximately whenever"—and then the Lantern crossed the boundary and every instrument on the bridge woke up at once like a roomful of people who'd all been holding their breath.

The nav display filled with stars. Real ones, charted ones, stars with *names*, and I sat there and read them aloud just because I could. Cygnus reach. The Meridian approaches—no. Not those. The charts still don't have Meridian. But the Concordance doesn't own the charts anymore either; the broadcast saw to that. The charts are just charts now, honest about their ignorance, and there's a strange comfort in that. A map that admits what it doesn't know is a map you can trust.

We are not going back to settled space. Not yet. The fleet scattered when the relay died and the truth went out on every open channel, but scattered isn't gone, and Sef has had enough of being hunted for one lifetime, and frankly so have I, or so I'm told I had. Tomas is in a hospital bed on Meridian's surface watching the colonies rise up on the news feeds, and he sent us one message before we burned out of the system. Four words.

*"Go be found later."*

So that's the plan. Go. Be found later.

Amara plotted us a course toward an uncharted star—a dim orange thing at the edge of the survey data, no colonies, no stations, no history at all. "It's probably nothing," she said, in the tone of a woman who once found a region where time runs backward and has therefore lost the ability to call anything *nothing*. "But it has a planetary body in the habitable band, and it's far from everything, and—" she hesitated, and I recognized the hesitation, because I'd taken back enough of my log to know it was mine first, "—and I think we've earned somewhere with no history. Somewhere we could make our own."

Sef voted yes by immediately renaming the star. It's called Sef's Star now. There was no committee. There was no discussion. There was a fourteen-year-old with a slate and a tyrant's instinct for nomenclature, and now it's Sef's Star, and Amara and I have both privately agreed not to fight it.

I didn't take back the memory of naming things. But I'm told I was good at it.

* * *

**[Fragment. Voice steady. A long pause near the beginning, as though the speaker is choosing words carefully.]**

Sef asked me last night, at dinner—reconstituted protein and actual, non-burned tea, because I have taken custody of the kettle and instituted reforms—she asked me if I regretted it. Leaving gaps. Walking around inside my own life with rooms I can't enter.

I told her the truth, which is that I don't know yet, and that I think not knowing is the point.

Here's what I know. I know who I loved. I know what I did. I know the forty-one names and the one broadcast and the price I paid for it, because everyone has made sure I know that last part—Amara checks my neural scans weekly with the grim thoroughness of a woman atoning, and Sef has appointed herself the official witness of my ongoing existence, and between the two of them I am the most documented hollow man in human space.

But I don't know, yet, who I'm going to be. And there is a version of me—the one in the log, the one who kept everything, who logged every empty street because letting go felt like betrayal—who would have taken back every last fragment. Who would have filled every room. Who would have stood in a house made entirely of the past and called it home.

I don't want to live in a house made entirely of the past.

I want a house with a scorch mark over the kettle and a window I haven't looked out of yet. I want some rooms empty on purpose. I want to meet Sef's Star for the first time, actually *first*, with no earlier me whispering how I'm supposed to feel about it. I want the stranger in the log to stay a stranger in the ways that don't matter, so that the man standing here can be new in the ways that do.

Sef thought about that for a while. Then she said, "Okay. But if you forget my birthday I'm never letting you forget it."

"I don't know when your birthday is," I said. "I may never have known."

"Then we'll pick one," she said, like it was the most obvious thing in the world. Like a birthday is a story too, and stories can be told by whoever's holding the slate.

We picked one. It's the day we left dead space. Tuesdays are apparently load-bearing now.

* * *

**[Final fragment of this entry. Signal quality good. Speaker's voice is quiet, but clear.]**

One more thing, and then I'm going to go stand on the bridge and look at a star that has no name except the one a teenager gave it.

The log ends soon. I can feel it—not because the archive is failing, but because I'm running out of things to recover and starting to accumulate things to record, and a log is for the second thing more than the first. That's the lesson I'd write in the margins if I could find the man who kept this log and hand him a pen: you don't keep a record to hold onto the past. You keep it so someone can start.

Sef says someone will read this someday. She says it with the absolute conviction of a girl who carried forty-one lives in her head and gave pieces of herself away to bring a man back, and who has therefore earned the right to be believed about anything. Someone will read this, she says, and they'll know Felix Zhou existed. Not because a relay screamed his memories across human space—though one did, and it worked, and the colonies are rising, and Meridian will not be forgotten, not ever again—but because of this. The small record. The honest one.

I hope they like him. The stranger in the log. I hope he reads like someone worth remembering.

And I hope—this is the selfish part, I'm allowing myself one selfish part—I hope whoever finds this understands why some of the entries stop mid-thought, why some rooms are empty, why the man in the later entries laughs more and knows less. I hope they understand that the gaps are not damage.

The gaps are the door.

The Lantern is making good time. Sef is humming in the pilot's seat, off-key, a song I don't recognize yet—which is a strange sentence to write, and a wonderful one, because it means there are still songs I don't know. Amara is in the lab growing something she won't tell us about, which historically means either a miracle or a small explosion, and honestly we could use either. And ahead of us, getting brighter by the hour, is a dim orange star with no history at all.

Sef's Star.

We're going to go make some.
