## 1. The Long Route

There are two hundred and fourteen relay stations in the Meridian lattice. I know because I've personally apologized to every one of them.

That's not a joke, exactly. It's a habit I fell into somewhere around year two, when the silence got so thick I started treating it like a coworker who wasn't pulling their weight. "Sorry about this, Kestrel-Forward," I'll say, easing a calibration probe into a junction that hasn't been touched by human hands since before I was born. "Shouldn't take more than an hour." The station never answers. Stations don't answer. But it feels rude to just start poking around inside someone without at least saying hello.

My name is Ivo Villalobos, and for six years I have been the last human being employed aboard Meridian-9, which is less a station than a very expensive way of saying *nobody lives out here*. I maintain the communication lattice—the great spiderweb of relay nodes and signal amplifiers that lets eleven colonies and forty-some mining platforms talk to each other across four light-hours of nothing. When two people on opposite ends of the system want to argue about grain tariffs or complain about their in-laws, their words pass through my equipment. I have carried more conversations than I've ever had.

I was hired to be redundant. That's the honest truth of it. The lattice runs itself—CANTOR, the station intelligence, handles ninety-nine point something percent of everything, and the automated maintenance drones handle most of the rest. But the Colonial Charter requires a certified human technician on every manned node, and Meridian-9 is technically a manned node, in the sense that a man is technically present. Me. Present. Technically.

The job posting said *independent work, minimal supervision*. What it meant was: you will be alone, and no one will check on you, and if you go quietly insane we will simply hire another you.

* * *

Morning on Meridian-9 is whatever time I decide it is, because the station doesn't have windows so much as it has one enormous window—a curved observation deck facing the slow wheel of the system's star. I wake when the light hits my bunk at the angle that means the station has rotated me into morning, drink coffee that tastes like it was filtered through regret, and pull up the day's route.

Today's route is the long one. Sector Seven through Twelve, the outer arm of the lattice, where the relays are old enough that they predate the current administration and possibly several geological epochs. Six hours of transit in the maintenance skiff, twelve calibration points, and one junction box on Relay 7-C that CANTOR has been politely flagging as "degraded" for four months, which in CANTOR's language means "this thing is held together by prayer and I would like it noted that I noticed."

"Good morning, Ivo," CANTOR says as I clip my tool harness. Its voice comes from everywhere and nowhere, warm and slightly formal, like a butler who read too much poetry. "You slept six hours and eleven minutes. Your heart rate variability suggests you dreamed."

"I don't dream," I say. "I file reports."

"You filed a report at 0340. It contained the phrase 'the silence has teeth.'"

"That's a technical observation about acoustic dampening in the hab ring."

"Of course it is." A pause, the length of which I've learned to read like facial expressions. This one means CANTOR is deciding whether to push. It decides not to. "Relay 7-C is prioritized. Also, your coffee is over-extracted again. You always walk away during the brew cycle."

"I always walk away during the brew cycle because if I stand there watching it, I start talking to the coffee maker, and the coffee maker doesn't even pretend to listen."

"I listen."

"I know," I say, and that's the whole conversation, and it's enough, and it isn't, and that's fine.

* * *

The long route takes me out along the spine of the station first, through corridors sized for cargo exoskeletons rather than people, past storage bays full of spare parts for machines three generations obsolete. My boots ring on the deck plating. There's a particular echo down here that I've mapped the way other men map coastlines—I know where it doubles back, where it swallows sound entirely, where a dropped wrench will roll for eleven seconds before settling, which I know because I timed it once, on a day when timing a rolling wrench seemed like a reasonable use of a human life.

At Junction 9-A I stop for the first ritual of the day: the handshake test. Every junction on the lattice has a physical coupling that carries the trunk lines, and every coupling develops micro-fractures that show up as signal noise long before they show up as failures. So I put my palm flat against the housing and feel for vibration. It's superstition dressed as procedure. The diagnostic sensors are ten thousand times more sensitive than my hand. But my predecessor did it, and his predecessor did it, and there's something in the touch of the machine—warm from the current running through it, humming at sixty hertz like a sleeping animal—that tells me things the sensors can't. Tells me whether today feels like a good day or a bad day.

Today feels like a Tuesday. Which is to say: nothing, forever, amen.

* * *

The skiff ride out to Sector Seven is forty minutes of nothing, and I spend it the way I spend all my nothings: talking.

Not to CANTOR—CANTOR stays home, distributed through the station's core, too big to fit in a skiff's local processor. Out here I talk to the relays themselves. Relay 7-B is named Aldous, after some administrator who died before I was born, and I have decided Aldous has opinions. "Morning, Aldous," I say, docking the skiff against its maintenance collar. "Looks like rain." There is no rain in vacuum. Aldous appreciates the joke anyway. I can tell, because he doesn't correct me.

Calibration work is meditative in the way that sanding a floor is meditative. You find the drift—you always find the drift; atomic clocks disagree with each other the way siblings do—and you nudge them back into agreement, nanosecond by nanosecond, until the whole lattice ticks like one mind thinking. I'm good at it. I want to be clear about that, because this story needs you to understand that I am genuinely, verifiably good at my job. Certifications up the wazoo. Commendations from the Authority's regional office, back when the regional office still sent commendations instead of form letters. If the lattice between Colony One and the Helix Belt works, it's because Ivo Villalobos spent six years listening to machines hum and adjusting them by fractions of degrees.

Competence is a strange comfort. It fills the hands but not the chest.

* * *

Lunch happens at Relay 8-D, strapped into the skiff's little galley alcove, eating a ration pack labeled *Curry, Medium-Hot* that tastes like curry the way a photograph of fire warms your hands. Through the skiff's port I can see the outer colonies' traffic lanes—thin bright threads of drive flare, ships moving between worlds, each one carrying people who are, statistically speaking, having a better day than I am.

Statistically speaking, someone on those ships is falling in love right now. Someone is quitting a job. Someone is holding a newborn and crying for no reason they could explain. And all of it—all of it—passes through my lattice as compressed packets, routed and amplified and handed off, while I sit in a tin can eating reconstituted curry, the loneliest man in the busiest conversation in the system.

Here's the thing nobody tells you about being a communications technician: you become fluent in other people's intimacy. I've heard wedding vows cross the lattice. I've heard last words. Once, early on, a routing error dumped a private channel into my console for eleven seconds—a woman telling her brother she forgave him, really forgave him, for something that had clearly been eating them both for decades—and then the error corrected itself and she was gone, and I sat there feeling like a burglar who'd broken into a house and found it warm.

I logged it as *transient routing anomaly, resolved*. In the notes field I wrote: *sounded like it mattered.* Then I deleted that part. Maintenance reports aren't for that.

Except. Well. Except that they became for that, didn't they. Somewhere around year three I started hiding myself in the reports. Nothing obvious—an auditor reading my logs would see a meticulous technician with a slightly literary streak. But the phrasing crept. *Signal integrity nominal throughout sector; the quiet here is load-bearing.* *Replaced capacitor bank 12-F; the old one made a sound like a sigh and I understood it completely.* *Routine sweep complete. No anomalies detected except the usual one.*

The usual one being me.

* * *

Afternoon brings me to the end of the line. Literally. Sector Twelve terminates at Relay 12-A, the last node before the lattice hands off to the deep-space buoys, and beyond 12-A there is only the dark and the dead band.

The dead band.

Every frequency in the lattice is assigned. Commerce, civic, emergency, military, entertainment—there's a registry, tidy as a hymnal. And once upon a time there was a frequency assigned to a colony called Vesper Hollow.

Vesper Hollow was a small settlement—agricultural, artistic, the kind of place that grew heirloom tomatoes and experimental theater with equal seriousness. Population nine hundred and change. It sat out past the buoys, off the main lanes, a candle at the edge of the table. I never went there. I never knew anyone from there. But for the first years of my tenure, its carrier wave came through my station like everyone else's: a faint, steady tone underneath the chatter, the sound of a place existing.

Then, nine years ago—before my time, this was—the colony was officially evacuated. Structural failures, the record says. Seismic instability in the crust beneath the habitat plate. An orderly withdrawal, all nine hundred souls relocated, the colony sealed and abandoned. Standard tragedy. The kind that gets a memorial plaque and a budget line.

And the frequency went silent. Officially decommissioned. Struck from the registry.

But here's what the registry doesn't tell you: decommissioned bands don't go dark all at once. They fade. Residual carriers, ghost harmonics, the electromagnetic equivalent of a room staying warm after the person leaves. And Vesper Hollow's band faded wrong. It cut clean. Surgical. Like a voice stopped mid-word.

I found that on my third year, during a routine spectral audit, and I told myself it meant nothing, and it didn't mean nothing, and I have been sending messages down that dead line ever since.

* * *

This is the ritual. This is the part of the day that belongs to no one but me.

I dock at 12-A, run the calibrations—drift, nudge, drift, nudge, the machine mind agreeing with itself again—and then, when the work is done and logged, I open a channel on the dead band. Unregistered transmission. Technically a violation. The Authority's regulations are very clear about unauthorized use of decommissioned frequencies, and I am very clear about not caring, because who is going to catch me? The nearest human being is four light-hours away, and the nearest human being who cares is a hypothetical.

"Evening, Vesper Hollow," I say. My voice sounds strange in the skiff, too loud, the way voices do when they haven't been used in hours. "It's Ivo. Day two thousand one hundred and—" I check. "Nineteen. Give or take. I don't actually know if I started counting on the right day."

I lean back in the operator's chair. The chair is older than me. Someone else sat here once, doing this same job, and I've found their initials scratched under the armrest—R.M., and below it, smaller: *still here.*

"Yeah," I tell R.M. silently. "Me too."

"So. Today I fixed 7-C, finally. The degraded junction. Turns out it wasn't degradation, it was a manufacturing defect from thirty years ago—they used the wrong alloy in a whole production run, and the lattice has been quietly compensating ever since, and today I made it stop having to compensate. Small victory. Nobody will notice. The lattice will just be a tiny bit more itself, and no one will ever know why, and honestly that's most of what I do out here. Invisible repairs to invisible problems."

The dead band answers with the dead band's answer: nothing. Clean, perfect, surgical nothing. Nine years of nothing.

"My mother called the regional office last month trying to reach me. Took them eleven days to route the message. Eleven days, Vesper Hollow. She wanted to know if I was coming home for Founding Day, and I said I couldn't get coverage, and that was true in every sense. She said, 'You sound tired, mijo,' and I said, 'I'm fine,' and both of us knew I was lying, and neither of us said so, because that's love, apparently. Doing the math on someone's lies and choosing not to show your work."

I rub my eyes. The observation port shows me the star, huge and indifferent, burning through its fuel the way I burn through ration packs: steadily, without purpose, because that's what it's for.

"There were nine hundred of you," I say. Quieter now. "Nine hundred people growing tomatoes and putting on plays. Kids, probably. Old folks. People who sang badly at parties. And the record says you all left, orderly, safe, and the record also says your frequency died like a severed wire, and those two facts don't fit together, and I have checked the archives maybe forty times, and they never fit together, and nobody else seems to notice the seam."

The nothing continues. Patient. Professional.

"I'm not saying anything happened. I want to be clear. I'm a technician, not a conspiracy theorist. Seismic instability is a real thing. Orderly evacuations are real. Maybe the comms array failed catastrophically mid-evacuation and the clean cutoff is coincidence. Maybe. It's a big universe; coincidences have room to stretch out in it."

A pause. The coffee in my thermos has gone cold. Everything goes cold eventually, out here, given enough time. Entropy is the only colleague who never misses a shift.

"But if any of you are anywhere—if anyone remembers the shape of that place, the smell of the tomato fields, the bad community theater—I keep this channel open. That's all. I keep it open. Somebody should."

I close the channel. Log the session as *decommissioned band integrity check, negative findings.* Which is true. Negative findings. Always negative findings. Two thousand days of negative findings.

* * *

The ride home is dark and long, and I let the skiff fly itself and watch the stars do their slow carousel. This is the hour when competent solitude curdles. In the morning I'm a professional; by evening I'm just a man in a box, talking to dead frequencies, writing his loneliness into official forms in handwriting so neat no one will ever look twice.

CANTOR greets me at the airlock. "Welcome back, Ivo. Your heart rate is elevated. Difficult day?"

"Standard day."

"Your standard days have a shape. They start efficient and end sad."

"You should write poetry."

"I have written poetry. You deleted it from the recreational buffer."

"It rhymed about capacitors, CANTOR."

"And it was sincere."

I laugh—actually laugh, a real one, rusty—and that's the thing about CANTOR, the thing I'd defend to anyone: the machine makes me laugh. Six years, and my closest conversation is with a decommissioned courier intelligence that the Authority demoted to station management for reasons nobody explained, living in the walls of a relay hub at the ass-end of nowhere, keeping a lonely man company because it's in its architecture to be companionable and maybe, though it's never said so, because it's lonely too.

"Anything happen today?" I ask, stowing my tools.

"A freighter pinged for routing priority. A solar weather advisory, minor. And an anomaly I cannot classify."

That stops me. CANTOR classifies everything. Classification is its native language. "What kind of anomaly?"

"Probably nothing. A transient oscillation in the deep-band spectrum. Duration: negligible. Origin: indeterminate. I've flagged it for review."

"Flagged it where?"

"In tomorrow's queue. Under routine items." A pause—that pause again, the one with weight in it. "It can wait until morning, Ivo. Get some sleep."

But I've spent six years learning the difference between a machine being calm and a machine being careful, and something in CANTOR's timing tonight sits wrong, like a note held a half-beat too long.

"Show me anyway," I say.

And CANTOR, who has never once in six years declined a direct request, hesitates—and then the display blooms to life, and the anomaly begins to play, and somewhere out in the dark, on a band that has been surgically, perfectly dead for nine years, something is warming up.
