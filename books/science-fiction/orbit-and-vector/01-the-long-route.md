## 1. The Long Route

There were eleven worlds left in the network, and Lena Dunmore had names for all of them the way a man names the teeth that ache.

He'd been running the long route for nineteen years—since the Accord War ended and the treaties went into effect and half the colonies decided they'd rather be silent than neighbors. The war hadn't destroyed much, in the end. What it destroyed was the willingness to answer a hail. Whole systems had gone dark on purpose, pulling their relay buoys in like drawbridges, and the only thing still crossing the gaps between them was paperwork.

Insurance claims, mostly. Death benefits, cargo loss, hull damage, the occasional claim of alien contamination that always turned out to be mold. The Consortium underwrote everything that still moved between the quarantine lines, and the Consortium needed someone to look at the wreckage with their own eyes and write down what it was worth. Drones couldn't do it. Drones got confused by grief, or by the way people lied to them. Lena didn't get confused by either.

He saw everything. He recorded nothing.

That was the discipline. Nineteen years of it. A claim came through the pipe—a fire aboard the *Halcyon Step*, a drowning at Thessaly Landing, a cargo manifest that didn't match the scorch patterns on the containers—and Lena rode his old courier ship, the *Marguerite*, out along the dark corridors between worlds, and he looked, and he remembered, and he filed exactly the number the contract said to file. Not the number that would have been kinder. Not the number that would have been truer, if truth and the contract had ever once shaken hands. The number the contract said.

People along the route thought that made him cold. He'd stopped explaining a long time ago that it was the opposite. A man who wrote everything down was a man who could be edited. A man who kept it all in his skull, in the wet uncopyable dark behind his eyes, was a man the Consortium had to trust, because there was no backup to seize and no transcript to alter. His memory was the last honest ledger in the system, and he was the only one who could read it.

He liked it that way. He'd have told you he loved it, if you'd asked, and nobody asked, which was part of what he loved.

* * *

The *Marguerite* came out of the long burn above Ceres with her hull ticking as it cooled, and Lena stood in the cockpit with a bulb of coffee going lukewarm in his hand and watched the registry ring turn against the black. Ceres was the hub—the one place all the quarantined worlds still agreed to meet, back when they'd agreed to anything. It had the feel of a border town the morning after a war ends and everyone realizes they're still living next door to each other. Cranes. Bureaucrats. The smell of recycled air and old grievances.

He had three claims to file and one to investigate, and he'd already decided the order. The investigation could wait. It was a small thing, the kind he did in his sleep: a grain hauler out of the Vesta approaches, hull breach, nine dead, the owner swearing up and down that the breach pattern was impossible. Everything was impossible until Lena looked at it. Then it was usually a weld.

"Marguerite, this is Ceres Approach," the traffic channel said, in the flat voice they'd used since before the war. "You are cleared for berth fourteen. You have been away a long time, Captain Dunmore."

"Fourteen months," Lena said. "Felt longer."

"Your berth has been reserved. Your account is in good standing." A pause, the length of a breath, though there was nothing on that channel that needed to breathe. "Welcome home."

Home. He let the word sit there without touching it. The *Marguerite* was home. The route was home. He'd had a life before, the way a snake has a skin, and he'd left it where he left it.

He brought her in through the ring's shadow, past the queue of haulers flying quarantine colors, past the wreck-tugs with their cargo of dead ships lashed to their spines like caught whales. He docked with the soft clang he could feel in his back teeth, and the ship sighed around him as the pressure equalized, and for a moment—just a moment, the length of a coffee going cold—he stood in the quiet of his own cockpit and felt the particular peace of a man who has been alone so long that alone has become the shape his body makes.

Then he popped the hatch, and the noise came in, and the day began.

* * *

The Registry on Ceres occupied a wedge of the ring's inner arc that had been built to look permanent and had mostly succeeded. Marble that wasn't marble. A floor polished by two hundred years of boots. Lena walked it in his route-worn coat with the Consortium sigil on the shoulder—worn, not displayed; it was a uniform the way a leash is a collar—and he felt the usual small pleasure of being known. The clerks nodded to him. The clerks always nodded to him. He was one of the few adjusters still willing to fly the gaps in person, and in the registry's economy of fear, that made him something close to a saint.

He filed the grain hauler first. It took eleven minutes. The breach was a weld; it was always a weld; he said so in the report with the flat economy of a man dictating a weather observation. The clerk stamped it, the Consortium would pay out forty percent and litigate the rest, and nine families on Vesta would learn the exact price of their dead to the credit. Lena had stopped feeling the weight of that a long time ago too, or he'd told himself he had, which on the long route amounted to the same thing.

The other two claims were routine. He was turning to go—turning toward the dock, toward the ship, toward the silence that fit him—when the clerk behind the counter cleared his throat.

"There's an inquiry flag on your account, Captain."

Lena stopped. "From who?"

"Automated. Something in the deep archive pinged a match on your travel history." The clerk's face had the careful blankness of a man reciting from a screen he didn't understand. "It wants to know if you can confirm a visit. Meridian Station. It says—" he squinted— "it says you filed a claim there. Twenty-two years ago."

The name went into Lena like a splinter. He didn't show it. Nineteen years of practice.

"Meridian," he said. "Mining colony. Outer belt of the Halcyon group?"

"That's what the flag says."

"Then yes. I can confirm it. I was there." He remembered it with the strange clarity he remembered everything: the station's spine lit amber, the gravity a half-point heavy because their ring was overdriven, the foreman crying in his office and pretending not to be. A rockfall, that claim had been. Fourteen dead. Lena had walked the collapsed gallery himself and seen the way the rock had fallen and known it wasn't a rockfall, and filed the number the contract said anyway. That had been his third year on the route. He'd gone back to the ship and been sick, and then he'd gotten better at it, which was worse.

"Can you open the file?" he said. "I'll countersign the confirmation. Saves the archive a ping."

The clerk tapped. Tapped again. His face did something small and wrong.

"That's odd," he said.

"What is."

"There's no file, Captain. There's no station." He turned the screen around, and Lena looked at a search result that was less than nothing—a clean, smooth absence, the kind of blank that took effort. "Meridian Station, Halcyon group. No permits. No registry entry. No claims history. No—" the clerk hesitated— "no decommission record either. It's not that it closed, sir. It's that it never shows as having existed."

Lena stood very still. Around him the registry went on with its murmuring, its stamping, its two-hundred-year conversation with itself, and none of it changed, and all of it felt suddenly like a stage set with the paint still wet.

"I was there," he said again, and heard his own voice do the thing he hated, the thing he'd heard a hundred grieving claimants do—repeat themselves, louder, as if volume were proof.

The clerk's eyes flicked past Lena's shoulder, and something in the clerk's posture changed, the way a room changes when a certain kind of person enters it.

"Captain Dunmore."

The voice behind him was pleasant. That was the first thing he noticed about it, and the thing he'd remember longest: how pleasant it was, how carefully, expensively pleasant, the way a blade is pleasant right up until the moment it isn't.

He turned. The man wore a Consortium suit cut close enough to be a second skin, grey on grey, and a smile that had been calibrated by someone with a budget. His badge said ADJUSTER, and beneath that, in small type, VOSS.

"Forgive the interruption," Voss said. "But when an archive flags a discrepancy, it's firm policy that I handle it personally. Saves the captain any trouble." The smile widened by a millimeter. "Meridian Station was a clerical ghost. A data artifact from the war years. It appears in some older records and has since been corrected. I'm told the flag on your account was an error, and I'm here to apologize for it."

"Corrected," Lena said.

"Corrected," Voss agreed warmly.

"I stood on its deck. I counted its dead."

The smile didn't move. It didn't need to. "Then I'd advise you not to say that in writing, Captain. The firm finds that memories of stations which never existed tend to complicate otherwise simple accounts." Voss extended a hand, and Lena, after a moment, took it. The grip was dry and exact. "You have an excellent reputation. Nineteen years, no disputes, no anomalies. That's rare. The firm values rare things." He released the handshake. "We'd hate to see a value like yours spend itself on a clerical ghost."

He walked away, pleasant to the last, and the registry closed over the space where he'd been like water.

Lena stood at the counter a while. The clerk had found somewhere else to look.

* * *

That night he sat in the *Marguerite*'s cockpit with the dock lights washing the glass amber, and he did the thing he never did. He opened the ship's log—the physical one, the paper one, the one the Consortium didn't know existed—and turned back twenty-two years.

He'd kept it as an indulgence, once a year, a line or two. Dates and ports and nothing that could hang him. He found the entry. *Meridian. Rockfall. Fourteen. Filed 40%.* His own handwriting, his own younger hand, the letters still confident.

Fourteen dead on a station that had never existed, witnessed by a man whose job was to see everything and record nothing—and the only record left in the whole system was one line in a paper book, in ink, in a ship the Consortium didn't know about.

Lena sat with that for a long time, the dock lights crawling across the page.

Then, in the amber dark, the comm board lit. An automated burst, routed off the old quarantine relays, the kind of transmission nobody sent anymore because nobody was allowed to. Distress format. Origin tag fragmenting even as it decoded.

He read the origin tag twice.

It read: MERIDIAN.
