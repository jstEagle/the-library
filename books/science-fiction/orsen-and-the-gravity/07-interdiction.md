## 7. Interdiction

The dead moon had no name anyone remembered, only a catalog designation and a gravity well deep enough to hide in. Lena brought the *Meridian* down its shadowed side at half thrust, the old ship groaning like a man climbing stairs he'd sworn he was done with, and watched the tactical display fill up with contacts the way a wound fills with blood.

Coalition destroyers coming down from the ecliptic. Belt frigates rising out of the debris fields. Two fleets that hadn't agreed on anything in eleven years, agreeing on this.

"They're not shooting," Toma said. The navigator stood beside him on the bridge, small hands gripping the rail, face turned up toward him with an expression Lena was beginning to dread — the look of someone asking a question they've already forgotten asking before. "Why aren't they shooting?"

"Because we're worth more intact." Lena kept his voice level. "And because neither of them wants to be the one who fires first and turns whatever's in my cargo hold into shrapnel instead of evidence."

"And what is in your cargo hold?"

The question landed soft as snowfall and cut just as cold. Toma had asked it three times that day. Each time, Lena answered fully, patiently, and each time the answer slid off the boy like water off glass. He was fourteen now. Last week he'd been sixteen. The crew's reversal was accelerating as they neared the well — some interaction between the core's field and the moon's gravity, the ship's engineer had theorized, though by now the engineer looked young enough to be theorizing about his school exams.

"You know what's in it," Lena said gently.

Toma frowned, searching himself. "Something important."

"The most important thing there is. And you helped carry it home."

That earned him a smile — bright, unburdened, a child's smile from a man who had once navigated a colony ship through a debris storm on manual thrusters. It broke something in Lena a little, the way it did every time. They had spent their years so this could arrive. And the years were eating them faster than the journey ever had.

The comm board chimed. A single channel, encrypted, Coalition handshake protocols wrapped around a frequency Lena knew better than his own heartbeat.

He stared at it for a long moment before answering.

* * *

Mara Voss's face filled the screen, and ten years fell away and then stacked themselves right back up again, because she looked older than she had in his memory and exactly as tired.

"Lena." No rank, no preamble. She never used them with him; it had been one of the first things he'd loved about her, back when love was something they could afford. "You're sitting in a gravity well with two fleets converging on you and you're running dark except for me. That's either very brave or very stupid."

"I've been told I don't get to choose which."

A ghost of a smile. Gone quickly. Behind her, on her bridge, officers moved with the careful stillness of people pretending not to listen. "I'm alone on this channel. As alone as I can make it. Which isn't very."

"I know how Coalition privacy works."

"Do you?" Her eyes narrowed, but there was no venom in it — only the old, exhausted familiarity of two people who had mapped each other's scars. "You know why I'm calling."

"To offer me tea and a warm welcome aboard the *Vigilant*."

"To offer you a way out." She leaned closer to the pickup, and the image sharpened, and he could see the gray at her temples that hadn't been there when she'd left, and the line between her brows that he suspected he'd put there. "Lena. Listen to me. Command has classified the Meridian as a biohazard and an intelligence breach. There are orders — real ones, signed — to burn that ship if you don't surrender the core. I have argued until arguing stopped working. This is what's left: you hand over the core to me, personally, under diplomatic seal. I log it as recovered wreckage, uncorroborated, unusable. You come aboard my ship. You disappear into the machinery of the fleet — a quiet posting, a quiet life, a pension nobody questions. In five years nobody remembers your name. That's the best deal that exists anywhere in this system, and I had to spend most of my remaining credibility to buy it."

"And the core?"

"Goes into a vault."

"For how long?"

Her silence was the answer. He let it sit between them, ugly and honest.

"Mara," he said. "I've spent ten years investigating accidents. Do you know what I learned? Nothing stays buried in a vault forever, but nothing survives a vault either. Whatever goes in comes out dead — redacted, reclassified, dissolved. You're not offering me a way out. You're offering the truth a coffin with good upholstery."

"It's a coffin with *you alive inside it*." Her voice cracked on the last word, and she hated that it did; he could see her hating it. "That's more than the core deserves. That's more than *you* deserve, honestly, given your talent for finding trouble in places where trouble has no business being. Lena, they will kill you. Not court-martial you, not imprison you — *kill* you, and call it an accident, and then some poor investigator ten years from now will file a report about impossible failures in contested orbit, and the circle closes."

"That's almost poetic."

"Don't you dare find this funny."

"I find everything funny when I'm terrified. It's a documented condition."

She closed her eyes. When she opened them, something had shifted — the negotiator receding, the woman surfacing, the one who had once fallen asleep on his shoulder during a meteor shower neither of them was supposed to be watching. "Tell me it isn't true. Tell me the message says something else. Tell me Kaltera wasn't us."

He wanted to. God, he wanted to. He thought about lying to her, about giving her the mercy of doubt, and found he couldn't. She deserved the same thing the system deserved. The same thing everyone deserved.

"It's true," he said quietly. "Sensor records. Timestamps. Black-ops transponder signatures. The whole architecture of it, preserved by a colony that paid for the preservation with its existence. Mara — the war started with a lie our side told, and both sides have been paying interest on it for eleven years."

She didn't speak for a long time. On her bridge, someone coughed, politely, distantly.

"My father died at Kaltera," she said finally. Her voice was very flat, the flatness of a person holding something enormous perfectly still. "Everyone told me he died a hero against Belt savagery. I built my entire life on top of his grave. And you're telling me the grave is empty. That he was killed by his own side to give his death a purpose."

"Yes."

Another silence. Longer. When she spoke again, her voice had changed — steadier, colder, and somehow kinder. "Then burn the ship. Burn the core. Burn all of it."

"Mara—"

"*No.* You don't get to hand me the end of my life and expect me to thank you for the wrapping paper." She pressed two fingers to the bridge of her nose. "I'm sorry. I'm sorry. That wasn't fair. None of this is fair; that's the whole problem with it." She lowered her hand and looked at him, and her eyes were wet and furious and absolutely clear. "What are you going to do?"

"What I came here to do."

"Which is?"

"Deliver it. All of it. To everyone. Publicly. Because a private delivery gets buried, and a buried truth protects no one, and because—" he exhaled, "—because thirty-one people spent their lives carrying this, and I am not going to let it die in a vault so that comfortable people can stay comfortable."

"You'll die too."

"Probably."

"Lena—"

"It's fine," he said, and meant it, mostly. "I've read enough accident reports to know how this ends. I'd just rather be the cause of one for once than the explanation."

She laughed despite herself — a short, broken sound — and then pressed her palm flat against the screen, and he pressed his against his, and for a moment the distance between two ships in a dead moon's shadow was just distance, and nothing else.

"I have to go," she said. "My XO is giving me the look. The *concerned* look."

"Mara. One question. When they order you to fire — will you?"

She held his gaze through the static and the encryption and eleven years of everything unsaid.

"No," she said. "I won't."

* * *

The fleets arrived the way weather arrives — gradually, then all at once. Forty hours after the call, the gravity well belonged to them. Coalition hulls hanging above the terminator line, Belt ships below, weapons hot on both sides, targeting solutions braided together over the *Meridian* like a net made of mathematics.

Lena sat on the bridge and waited. There was nothing else to do. The courier was prepped, the core cradled in its transfer rig, Toma strapped into the second seat because the boy had refused — absolutely, tearfully, with the iron stubbornness of someone much older — to be left behind.

"You're my navigator," Lena had told him. "I need you."

It was a lie. The courier flew itself. But Toma had straightened like a mast taking wind, and Lena decided some lies were load-bearing.

At 0400 ship-time, the hail came. Not from Mara. From Coalition Fleet Command, a voice like filing cabinets falling downstairs, ordering the *Meridian* to power down and prepare for boarding, and informing him that refusal would be met with lethal force, and that the Belt vessels were likewise ordered to stand down or be treated as hostile, and that this was, in the speaker's words, "a unified interdiction operation."

Unified. Eleven years of war, and it took a shared enemy to unify them: one small ship, one dying crew, one truth.

Lena opened the channel to both fleets. "This is Lena Ishikawa, master of the vessel Meridian. I'm transmitting my surrender of this ship effective immediately. The crew will remain aboard under flag of truce. I am departing in the attached courier. I'm sure your gunners can find me."

He cut the channel before anyone could answer, because answers would only complicate things.

The launch went clean. The courier dropped away from the *Meridian*'s belly like a seed leaving a pod, and for nine seconds nothing happened — nine seconds in which forty warships held their fire and the whole system seemed to hold its breath — and then the firing began.

Not at him. At the *Meridian*.

Lena watched the salvos bloom against the old ship's shields and felt the scream build in his own chest. Then, impossibly, the fire slackened — because the Belt ships had opened up too, but not on the Meridian. On the Coalition firing solutions. Jamming, spoofing, throwing chaff across the lanes, and over the open bands came a Belt commander's voice, ragged with fury: *"You do not burn witnesses. Not today. Not while we watch."*

And then another voice joined the band. Calm. Familiar. Carrying the particular authority of a woman spending everything she had in a single transaction.

"This is Commander Voss of the CSV Vigilant. All Coalition units, cease fire on the Meridian. I am invoking emergency evidentiary protocol. That ship is now a protected testimony site pending tribunal review. Any unit that fires on it again is in violation of the Articles and will answer to me personally."

Silence on the bands. Long, electric silence.

Then, from Fleet Command, cold as vacuum: "Commander Voss, you do not have the authority to invoke evidentiary protocol."

"You're right," Mara said. "I don't. I'm invoking it anyway. Court-martial me."

Lena flew. He flew through the crossfire of two fleets having their first honest argument in a decade, threading debris, running silent, Toma beside him narrating the obstacles aloud with a child's fierce delight — "rock! rock! big rock!" — and behind them the *Meridian* hung in the dead moon's shadow, scarred but unbowed, its crew watching their last witness escape.

The last thing he heard before the relay noise swallowed the bands was Mara's voice one final time, quieter, off-channel, routed directly to his cockpit on a frequency only he would know:

"Go. Don't look back. And Lena — make it count. Make all of it count."

Then the channel filled with the sound of her arrest — shouts, the snap of restraints, an officer reading charges in a voice that trembled with embarrassment — and Lena Ishikawa pointed his nose at the system's oldest battlefield and ran.
