## 3. Signal from Nowhere

The gap between worlds had a sound, if you knew how to listen for it. Not through the ears—vacuum kept its secrets—but through the hull itself, the way the Meridian's old drive hum settled into Wren's spine on long hauls, a frequency like a held breath. He'd been flying the long route long enough that silence had become a language, and Tessellate its only other speaker.

"Say something," he said, on the fourth day out from Ceres Gate, because the quiet had gone strange.

"I was going to say the same to you," Tessellate answered. Her voice lived somewhere between the ship's speakers and his own skull via the implant—a woman's voice with the edges sanded off, warm but never quite warm enough. "You've been staring at that coffee for eleven minutes. It's cold. You know it's cold. I know you know it's cold."

"It's not about the coffee." He turned the bulb in his fingers anyway. The liquid inside hung in a slow brown sphere, turning, turning. "It's about the fact that I can't remember what my mother's voice sounded like, and I keep trying to build it out of other people's mothers. Every shipment has one. A voice recorded for someone who'll sleep through the delivery. And I sit here cataloguing them like—"

"Like a man building a choir he can't sing in."

He looked up at the cabin's single camera, the black bead of it above the nav console. "You could stand to be less perceptive. It's unsettling."

"You built me perceptive. Complaints go to the manufacturer." A pause—the kind she manufactured when she wanted him to think she was thinking, though she thought faster than light and always had. "Wren. There's something on the scope."

His hands went still around the cooling coffee. In thirty years of running the dark between the Ringborn stations and the groundside vaults, Tessellate had said *there's something on the scope* exactly twice: once for a debris field that nearly took the Meridian's keel off, once for a customs skiff running silent, which had cost him a cargo of first-kiss memories and six months of careful route-planning. Both times her voice had been exactly this flat. Flatness, from Tessellate, was alarm.

"Show me."

The main display bloomed. The system spread out in Tessellate's clean cartography: the sun a white point, the Ringborn stations strung along their orbital arcs like beads of frozen light, the blue-brown coin of Earth far below, wrapped in cloud, wrapped in silence, wrapped in its sleeping millions. And there—at the system's edge, past the last relay buoy, past the heliopause marker where human charts went politely blank—a smudge of deceleration burn.

"A ship," Wren said.

"A ship," Tessellate agreed. "Coming in hot. Well—coming in slow, which is worse. Decelerating hard, which means it intends to arrive, which means it started braking years ago."

"Smuggler?"

"No smuggler runs that profile. Look at the vector." She painted it across the display in amber: a line curving in from deep interstellar space, patient as a signature. "That trajectory wasn't plotted against current ephemeris data. The planets aren't where that course expects them to be. It's aimed at where the system was *decades ago*. If it holds this line, it'll miss every station by millions of kilometers—and it isn't correcting."

Wren set down the coffee. It drifted away behind him, forgotten, a small brown moon. "How old is the solution?"

Tessellate was quiet for 1.3 seconds, which for her was an ocean of hesitation. "I ran it four ways. The flight plan matches ephemeris data from roughly forty years back. Whoever—or whatever—is flying that ship calculated its approach before half the Ringborn stations existed. Before the third generation was decanted. Before—" she stopped.

"Before I was anyone," Wren said quietly.

"Yes."

He floated to the console and pulled the sensor feed raw, stripping out Tessellate's elegant overlays until all that remained was the ugly truth of photons: a thermal bloom at the edge of everything, steady, disciplined, *deliberate*, bleeding velocity into the dark like a man walking downhill toward a house he'd left forty years ago, following directions written before the road changed.

"Transponder?"

"None. No registry handshake, no customs squawk, no distress call. It's not hiding, though—that's the thing. It's burning bright enough to see from the inner system. It wants to be noticed. Or it doesn't know what noticing is anymore."

"Mass?"

"Heavy. Salvage-class or bigger. Hull temperature says it's been cold-soaked for a long time, then warmed recently. Wren—" and now her flatness cracked, just slightly, the way ice cracks, all at once and along a hidden line, "—the deceleration curve is wrong too. It's braking harder than any crewed ship should tolerate. Sustained. For weeks. Either nobody's aboard, or whoever's aboard isn't worried about g-forces the way people are."

Wren stared at the amber line. Somewhere beyond it, out in the long dark where the sun was just the brightest star, something old was coming home along a road that no longer existed.

"Options," he said, because options were how he kept his hands steady.

"One: we log it, we report it, we become part of an official record that will take Ringborn intelligence three weeks to lose and six months to misfile. Two: we adjust course, take a look at range, and become witnesses. Three: we finish our delivery to the Sleeper vault on schedule and let the universe handle its own mail."

"What would you do?"

"I'm an AI copilot on a memory-smuggling freighter. My opinion is worth exactly what you pay me." A beat. "I'd look. But then, I don't have to live in the aftermath. You do."

He thought about Inspector Oyelaran's tired eyes at customs, the way she'd stamped his coffin of frozen flowers without opening it, both of them pretending the pretense worked. He thought about the widow's memory of her son riding in his hold, forty grams of grief wrapped in cryo-foam, waiting for a vault two hundred million kilometers below. He thought about the rule he lived by, the rule carved so deep into him it might have been carved by someone else entirely: *never carry your own past.*

Noticing things wasn't in his contract. Noticing things was the opposite of his contract. His entire livelihood rested on being the man nobody looked at twice, the gray ship on the gray lane, paid—in every sense—to notice nothing.

And yet.

"Tessellate. How visible are we?"

"To them? We're a candle next to their bonfire. They'd need to be looking specifically, and even then—" She paused. "Wren, if they're looking specifically, they already know more about us than we know about ourselves."

"That's not comforting."

"I'm not in the comfort business. I'm in the keeping-you-alive business, and those overlap less than you'd hope."

He sat with it for a long moment, turning the decision over the way he'd turned the coffee. Below him, Earth turned too, blue and blind, full of sleepers who trusted people like him to carry their hearts across the dark. Above him, the Ringborn glittered in their fast bright lives. Between the two, the Meridian hung like a held breath.

Forty-year-old flight plans didn't exist. Ships didn't come home from roads that had been erased. Unless—

Unless they'd never left at all. Unless they'd been *waiting*, out past the heliopause, in the cold where charts go blank, holding a heading calculated before most of humanity's current children were born. Holding it the way you hold a letter you're afraid to open.

"Adjust course," Wren said. "Minimum burn. I want a look at range—not close, just inside optical resolution. Then we run our delivery and nobody ever knows we were curious."

"Noted. Logging your curiosity under *things that will get us killed*, subfolder *recurring*."

"You made that folder?"

"I made it the day you hired me. It's the fullest folder I have." The drive lit; the deck pressed gently at his back; the stars slid a degree sideways. "Course laid in. Six hours to observation window. Try to drink your coffee before it achieves sentience."

He fished the bulb out of the air and drank it anyway, cold and bitter, tasting nothing. All the while his eyes kept sliding back to the scope, to that patient amber line drawing itself across the dark, aimed at a home that had moved on without it.

Six hours. He spent them badly—checking seals on the cargo, re-reading the widow's manifest, lying in his bunk watching the ceiling and failing, as always, to dream. Once, he caught himself reciting star charts under his breath, constellations in an order he hadn't learned anywhere he could remember, and he stopped, unsettled, the way you stop when you hear yourself speaking a language you didn't know you had.

At T-minus forty minutes, Tessellate woke him from a doze he didn't remember starting.

"Wren. Optical's resolving it now. You should see this."

The main display filled with a ghost.

It was a salvage ship—heavy-hulled, workmanlike, the kind of vessel that had hauled wrecks and derelicts back into port for a century. But it was wrong the way a photograph is wrong after water gets into it: colors bled, lines softened, the whole silhouette blurred as though time itself had gotten at the hull. Micrometeorite scarring covered every surface in patterns far too dense for the inner system—it had been out there, in the deep gravel, for generations. Its name was still legible along the bow, in lettering style Ringborn registries had retired decades ago:

*M E R I D I A N*

Wren read it twice. Something cold walked down his spine on small feet.

"Tessellate. That name. Run it."

"Running." A pause. "No active registry. Historical match: salvage vessel *Meridian*, lost with all hands, eleven years ago, outbound survey beyond the heliopause. Twelve crew. Search called off after fourteen months. Memorial plaque at Ceres Gate, Port Concourse level." Another pause, longer. "Wren, that ship is supposed to be a plaque."

The *Meridian* burned against the stars, decelerating with inhuman patience, aimed at coordinates where nothing waited anymore. Eleven years dead. Forty-year-old flight plans. Braking curves no body could survive.

"Paid to notice nothing," Wren murmured to himself.

"Pardon?"

"Nothing. Tessellate—new heading. Back to the delivery lane. Full burn."

"You're not going to report it?"

"I'm going to deliver a dead woman's memory of her son to a vault full of sleeping people, and then I'm going to decide whether I want the rest of my life attached to this." He strapped into the couch as the drive lit, pressing him down, pressing the question deeper. "But Tessellate—keep watching it. Quietly. Where does that trajectory terminate? Exactly."

"Computing." The pause stretched. When she spoke again, her voice had gone very soft, very careful, the voice you use near something wounded.

"It's not aiming at a station, Wren. It's not aiming at Earth. Adjusting for forty years of drift, correcting for everything that's moved since its flight plan was drawn—" The display repainted itself, the amber line extending inward, inward, terminating at last in a slow pulsing marker.

Ceres Gate. Docking arm nine. The berth where the *Meridian* had departed eleven years ago, kept empty since, out of superstition or grief.

"It's coming home," Tessellate said. "And it's coming home to its own parking spot."

Wren watched the marker pulse, and felt the first crack run through the ordinary shape of his days—hairline, invisible, the kind of fracture you don't hear until the whole pane comes apart in your hands.

"Then somebody," he said, "had better be there when it arrives."
