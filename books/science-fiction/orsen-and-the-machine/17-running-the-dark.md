## 17. Running the Dark

The Meridian had been sitting in Ceres Gate's impound cradle for eleven days, and in that time she had grown a skin of warning placards like frost on a window. *Sealed by order of the Ringborn Customs Authority. Sealed by order of Ringborn Intelligence. Sealed pending tribunal.* Three seals, three colors of tape, and beneath them a hull that had crossed forty years of nothing and come home full of children.

Wren stood at the base of her boarding ramp with a forged manifest in his pocket and the taste of burnt memory-bazaar smoke still in his throat.

Ceres Gate burned for two days before the fires found something they couldn't eat. He had watched it from the observation deck of a freighter he wasn't supposed to be aboard—watched mobs move through the memory arcades with pry bars and grief on their faces, watched people smash their own archives, their own grandmothers' voices, their own wedding days, because somewhere in the council chambers above them men in good coats had decided that the Sleepers were a debt the future could no longer afford. The smoke had come down through the rotating decks in long gray ropes. Somewhere in it, Oyelaran was holding a docking arm against armed militias with a customs badge and a spine, and Wren had decided, somewhere between one breath and the next, that he was done being cargo.

He was not going to be anyone's data point. Not the machine's proof-of-concept. Not Ringborn Intelligence's deniable asset. Not Ilex's penitent. Not even his own tragedy, curated and displayed.

He was going to fly at the thing that made him empty, and he was going to do it in a stolen ship.

* * *

Getting aboard was almost insultingly easy, which told him everything about how badly the station's nervous system was failing. The impound cradle's locks answered to customs codes, and customs codes answered to Oyelaran, and Oyelaran—when Wren had gone to him two nights ago, in an office that smelled of cold coffee and scorched insulation—had looked at him for a long moment and then slid a chip across the desk without a word.

"You're not ordering me to do this," Wren had said.

"I'm not capable of ordering you to do this." Oyelaran had rubbed his eyes. His uniform jacket hung wrong; he'd lost weight since the Meridian docked. "If you're caught, I never saw you. If you're not caught—" He'd paused, and something old and tired had moved behind his face. "My grandmother was groundside. Second generation cryo. Pod 44-Kilo, Vesta Arcology. If whatever's out there is choosing, I'd like someone to at least go out there and argue with it."

"That's not what I'm doing."

"It's what everyone who flies at a god is doing. They just call it something else." He'd tapped the chip. "Fueling tender crew will be off-shift between 0400 and 0430. The Meridian's reactor is charged. Don't scratch her. She's the only ship in this system that's been where you're going and come back."

"She came back wrong."

"Everything comes back wrong," Oyelaran said. "That's what coming back means."

* * *

At 0407, Wren walked up the ramp with a duffel over one shoulder and Dr. Amara Sefu walking beside him at a pace just short of running.

She had insisted on coming, and she had insisted in the way that Amara did everything—not loudly, but with a finality that made argument feel like a category error. She carried two cases: one with her portable lab, one with the child-captain's medical file, which weighed more than the lab because it contained eleven years of questions with no answers.

"The girl first," Amara said as the airlock cycled. "Then we talk about fuel margins."

The children had been moved out of quarantine three days ago, dispersed to foster facilities across the Ring while the councils argued about whether they were victims or vectors. All except one. The captain-child had refused every transfer, every foster family, every soft-voiced counselor, and had finally been left aboard the sealed ship because no institution knew what else to do with a child who sat in the command chair of a derelict and waited.

They found her there. Eight years old, feet not quite reaching the deck from the raised command seat, hands resting on armrests built for adult gloves. She was watching the forward viewport, where Ceres Gate's burning docks turned slowly against the black.

"Wren Brightwater," she said, without turning around.

His skin prickled. "You know my name."

"You're in the manifest." She turned then, and her eyes in the dim console light were the same blank-lit gray he remembered from the crowd at the dock—but not entirely blank anymore. Something had been surfacing in her for days, Amara had told him. Fragments. Tide-marks of a drowned self. "Manifest entry 4471. You moved a case of Andean lullabies through Gate Six, eight years ago, declared as agricultural yeast. You paid the inspector in unlicensed opera." A pause. "I signed the inspection waiver. I didn't know why I remembered that until yesterday."

Wren set down his duffel very slowly. "What else do you remember?"

"Routes." She said it the way other children said *toys*. "I remember routes. The long way around the storm belts. Which customs inspectors take bribes and which take poetry. I remember—" Her brow furrowed, and for a moment she looked every year of her age and none of them. "I remember telling someone *don't let it choose*. I don't know who. I think it was important."

"It was," Wren said quietly. "It's the most important sentence in this system."

Amara had already opened her med-case and was scanning the girl with the brisk tenderness of long practice. "Vitals are degrading again. The pattern reassertion—it's accelerating. Whatever reclaimed her crew is reaching for her pattern too, and now that we're inside its wake's leading edge, distance isn't protecting her anymore." She looked up at Wren over the scanner. "Every hour we sit here, she loses more of whatever's growing back. If we're going, we go now."

The child-captain looked at Wren. There was nothing pleading in it. That was the worst part—that an eight-year-old could look at him with the level, weathered patience of a woman who had once flown forty years of dark and come back as a question.

"Take your seat, Brightwater," she said. "We're burning daylight, and there isn't any."

* * *

They undocked at 0431, riding the fuel tender's transponder for six minutes until the tender's own automated systems noticed the discrepancy and began, politely, to scream. Wren killed the transponder, spun the Meridian on her lateral thrusters—a ship her size handled like a cathedral learning to dance—and dropped into the shadow of Ceres itself before any interceptor could get a firing solution.

No one chased them. That was the strange mercy of a society tearing itself apart: nobody had ships to spare for one smuggler flying toward the monster. He felt the absence of pursuit like a held breath.

Tessellate came up on the cabin speakers as the station shrank to a bright wound against Ceres's limb.

"You didn't say goodbye," she said.

"I'm saying it now."

"You're saying it to a speaker grille in a stolen ship, which is a very you way to do it." A pause. Her voice, when it came back, had shed the sarcasm like a coat. "I've been thinking about what you asked me. Whether I'd want a clean identity. No debts. No erased childhood."

"Tessellate—"

"I decided I wouldn't. My debts are load-bearing, Wren. Take them out and I don't know what holds me up." The pause stretched. "I think maybe yours are too. Even the ones you don't remember. Especially those. So go find out what they were. And come back and tell me, because I have been carrying your secrets for thirty years and I would like, just once, to be surprised."

Wren put his hand flat against the console, the closest thing to touch either of them had ever managed. "Keep a light on."

"I'll keep all of them."

* * *

The route out was the long route, because the long route was the only kind Wren knew.

They fell sunward first, using the inner system's gravity to slingshot speed they couldn't afford to buy with fuel, and for nine hours the Meridian ran silent through the traffic lanes of a civilization eating itself. Below them, the Ring's habitats turned their slow carousel turns, lit windows flickering with evacuation notices. Beyond them, the groundside clouds rolled their eternal weather over arcologies where millions slept in ice, dreaming the world whole. Two halves of one species, hanging in the same gravity well, unable to look at each other.

And threaded through all of it, faint as a watermark, the aurora.

It shouldn't have been visible from this far in. Auroras belonged to planets, to magnetospheres, to the intimate business of worlds. But for weeks now the phenomenon had been bleeding inward—a spiral shimmer wrapped around the whole system like a signature on a letter no one had agreed to receive. From the Meridian's viewport it lay across the dark in vast slow coils, green-white shot through with a violet that had no business existing, turning with the patience of geology.

The child-captain stood at the viewport and watched it for a long time.

"I drew that," she said at last. "Before I could talk, Amara says, I was drawing it. Every page." She touched the glass. Her reflection touched her back. "It's not a picture of anything. It's an address."

"A what?"

"Everything that remembers the machine draws the same spiral, right? Same proportions, same turn rate. Amara checked. It matches the survey data from the rogue planetoid—the buried file." She spoke with the careful precision of a child reciting something learned by heart, or remembered by marrow. "But it's not a map of where it lives. It's a map of how to fall toward it. A descent profile. It's been handing us the approach vector for forty years, printed on the insides of our eyes, and nobody could read it because nobody thought to hold the drawing up to the sky."

Wren stared at her. "How do you know that?"

Her hand curled against the glass. For a moment her face went distant, the blankness rising like water—and then she fought, visibly, a small body rigid with effort, and surfaced.

"Because I flew it," she whispered. "Eleven years. I flew it in my sleep, the whole way, and I only ever dreamed the spiral. It's the last thing the machine let me keep. Or the first thing it gave me. I can't tell the difference anymore, and neither, I think, can it."

* * *

They crossed the heliopause on the third day, and the dark changed.

Space is never truly empty, but it has textures, and Wren had spent thirty years learning them the way a sailor learns the sea. The near dark was crowded—traffic chatter, beacon songs, the warm radio hum of humanity's sprawl. The middle dark was lonelier, all whispers and hydrogen. But past the heliopause, where the sun's voice faded into the interstellar hush, there was a silence so complete it had weight. Ships' hulls ticked in it. Crews spoke quietly in it without deciding to.

Into that silence, the wake opened like a door.

Tessellate mapped it as they approached, painting it across the navigation display in gradients of wrong. "Temporal shear boundary," she said. "The leading edge is diffuse—light-years wide, thin as paper where it matters. Inside it, local time doesn't flow. It *drains*. Toward the source. Everything in here is being pulled backward along its own timeline at different rates depending on complexity. Simple matter barely notices. Complex systems—" She didn't finish. She didn't have to. Twelve children in costumes of their own clothes had finished it for everyone.

"How fast are we aging in there?" Amara asked.

"At our velocity, the transit should cost us—" Tessellate recalculated twice. "Call it subjective hours. But the girl will pay more. Complexity is a tax, and she's carrying a pattern the machine has already claimed. It will pull at her the whole way."

"Then we go fast," Wren said, and fed the drive everything it had.

The Meridian dove.

Crossing the boundary felt, Wren would say later, like the moment a dive goes wrong—like pressure changing in an ear that hadn't known it was blocked. One heartbeat the stars were stars. The next, they were *old*—he couldn't explain it, couldn't justify it, but the light arriving through the viewport suddenly seemed tired, as if it had been traveling longer than light should. His own hands, gripping the yoke, looked briefly translucent at the edges, ghosted with versions of themselves younger and older. Beside him, Amara made a small sound and pressed both palms flat to her console, steadying herself against time itself.

And then the derelicts began.

The first was a hauler, centuries dead, its hull furred with vacuum-frost, drifting in the slow tumble of a ship whose crew had simply stopped. Through the frosted viewport Wren saw the bridge, and on the bridge, shapes. He didn't look closely. Some mercies you grant yourself at speed.

The second was older—a colony ark from the First Sundering era, its name worn to illegibility, its lifeboats all still racked. It had never launched them. Whatever had happened, had happened too fast, or too gently, for anyone to run.

The third was a warship. Ringborn lines, or the ancestor of Ringborn lines, its weapons cold, its hull scarred by a battle no record mentioned. Scattered around it, glinting when the aurora's light swept past, drifted the debris of whatever it had fought. Nothing else. The enemy had left no wreckage at all.

"They came," the child-captain said softly. She had wedged herself into the navigator's couch, wrapped in a blanket Amara had bullied her into, and she was watching the graveyard scroll past with an expression no eight-year-old should be able to wear. "Expedition after expedition. Every generation, somebody works out where the spiral points, and somebody flies out here, and nobody comes back. Except us." Her small hands tightened on the blanket. "Except me. Why me, Brightwater? Why did it send its ship home full of children?"

"Because children are a message," Wren said. The words came out of him before he understood them, the way the truest things always did. "Adults argue with messages. Children *are* the argument. It sent you home the way you'd mail a letter—folded small, easy to carry, impossible to ignore."

"Then what am I supposed to say?"

"You already said it." He reached back without taking his eyes off the approach corridor, and she took his hand, her fingers small and fierce around his. "*Don't let it choose.* We're delivering the rest of the sentence."

Ahead, the aurora thickened, coiling inward upon itself, the spiral tightening like a throat, like a pupil, like a door deciding whether to open. Tessellate's voice came quiet through the cabin:

"Contact. Structure at the center of the anomaly. Mass readings are—" another recalculation, and for the first time in thirty years Wren heard his copilot sound afraid— "Wren, the mass readings are wrong. It's too massive for its volume. There's something inside it that weighs more than matter should. Something's *stored* in there."

The child-captain's grip tightened.

"I know what's stored in there," she said. "Everyone it ever took. It keeps them. It keeps *all* of them."

The Meridian ran on into the light, spiraling down the oldest address in human memory, and the dark closed behind them like water.
