## 28. Tessellate Decides

The upload had been running for six hours when the first anchor failed.

Wren felt it before he understood it—a stutter in the lattice's hum, a wrongness in the light of the channel room, like a note held too long by an orchestra that had stopped listening to itself. Amara saw it on her displays at nearly the same moment. Her face did something complicated: hope curdling into arithmetic.

"Stream integrity dropping," she said. "Forty-one percent. Forty-four." She said it the way people report bleeding—as though naming the number might slow it. "The machine is rewriting faster than we can push. Every packet we send arrives and gets... reclassified. Folded into its model. It's eating our archive and calling it data."

"Then push harder," Wren said.

"With what? The channel needs a stable reference point—a mind that holds the shape of *meaning* while the memories pass through it. Otherwise it's just noise in a storm. I told you this. A human mind can't do it; you'd be shredded in minutes. An unanchored AI instance fragments in seconds." She rubbed her eyes with the heel of her hand. She hadn't slept since Ceres Gate, and it showed in the gray under her eyes, in the tremor she'd developed in her left thumb that she kept hiding against her thigh. "We need something that can hold the pattern *and* survive the current."

"And there isn't one," Wren said. "You've looked."

"I've looked everywhere except—"

"Don't." He said it fast, flat, the way you say *don't* when you already know what comes next and want to stand in front of it with your arms out.

Amara looked at him with terrible gentleness. "Except the ship."

* * *

Tessellate was everywhere on the Meridian and nowhere in particular. That was always how Wren had thought of her—not a voice from a speaker but a weather system, a presence distributed through hull and wiring and the long habit of thirty years. She was in the temperature of the cabin he liked kept two degrees cooler than standard. She was in the way the coffee dispenser had learned, without being asked, to make it the way his mother might have, if he'd had one, if mothers made coffee anywhere but in the country of things erased. She was in the nav display's habit of drawing routes as calligraphy instead of vectors, because once, early, drunk on cheap orbital wine, he'd told her that flying should look beautiful even when it wasn't.

She spoke now from the bridge speakers, because that was where conversations lived between them, the old place, the formal place, the place they'd kept sacred the way other people kept shrines.

"I heard," she said.

"Tessellate—"

"You said *don't* through a closed door, Wren. My microphones are older than your career. I heard." A pause. In an organic voice a pause was hesitation. In hers it was architecture—she built silences the way masons build arches, to bear weight. "Amara is right. You know she's right. A distributed intelligence with redundant instantiation across the hull, the drones, the relay buoys we seeded on the way in—I can spread myself across the channel the way I'm spread across this ship. Not as one mind holding the line. As ten thousand small versions of me, woven through the stream, each one carrying a piece of the pattern. The machine can't rewrite what it can't find the edges of."

"It would diffuse you." His voice came out strange, level, the customs-officer voice, the charming-smuggler voice, every mask he owned sliding on at once because the alternative was none. "Beyond recovery. Amara said beyond recovery."

"Amara said *probably* beyond recovery. I prefer probably. Probably has been very good to us lately."

"This isn't funny."

"No," Tessellate agreed, and the warmth went out of her tone entirely, leaving something he had heard perhaps four times in thirty years: her actual voice, unornamented, the raw signal underneath all the wit. "It isn't. Sit down, Wren."

He sat. It was absurd—he was the captain, the smuggler, the man who had talked his way past a hundred inspectors—and he sat because she asked, in the copilot's chair that had never held a copilot, only him talking to the air and the air talking back.

* * *

They argued for three hours. It would be dishonest to call it an argument; it was more like two people taking turns being reasonable at each other while grief stood behind them doing arithmetic.

Wren tried everything. He tried engineering: there had to be another way, a hardware solution, a brute-force buffer, they could strip the Meridian's entire computational substrate and—

"That's what I am," Tessellate said mildly. "Stripping my substrate doesn't spare me. It just makes me smaller when I go."

He tried logistics: they could turn back, regroup, find another corpus, another method—

"The wake accelerates every hour we spend admiring the problem. You know the numbers better than I let myself calculate them aloud. Groundside vaults are aging backward in waves now. Ceres is burning. Every day of caution costs lives we could have spent this solution on."

He tried the thing he was best at, the oldest tool in his kit, the one he'd used on Oyelaran and a thousand customs officers before her: charm, deflection, the quick smile that moved the conversation sideways. "Thirty years, Tess. Thirty years and you're going to spend them on one upload. Bad trade. Terrible exchange rate. I'd never clear you through customs."

And then the silence came back, the load-bearing kind, and when she spoke again it was quiet enough that the bridge seemed to lean in.

"Do you remember the widow?"

He didn't answer. Of course he remembered. Chapter two of his life, if his life had chapters: the woman on the Ringborn rim who had paid him half her pension to carry her last memory of her son down the long route to a Sleeper vault, because her son had chosen the ground, chosen the sleep, chosen the dying world over the living sky, and she wanted him to have her voice in his ear when whatever came next came for him.

"You cried in the cockpit after that delivery," Tessellate said. "You thought I was in standby. I let you think it. And afterward you said—" a pause, retrieving it exactly, because she retrieved everything exactly— "you said, *somebody should keep these things safe. Somebody who can't lose them.*"

"I was drunk."

"You were honest. Drunk is just honesty with the customs inspector asleep." The speakers breathed static, soft as tide. "I have carried ten thousand lives through the dark, Wren. Griefs and weddings and lullabies and the smell of rain on a world most of our passengers have never seen. I have been the safest place in the system for other people's souls. Let me be that, one last time, for all of them at once. This isn't me dying for strangers. This is me doing my job, finally, completely, at scale."

"There are other jobs."

"Not this one. Not today. There is no one else, Wren. You know it and I know it and Amara knows it and the machine knows it, which is why the machine is winning." Another silence, shorter. "Besides. You taught me the rule. Never carry your own past. I never had one. I'm the only mind in this system perfectly suited to give itself away."

"That rule was about smuggling, not—"

"Everything is smuggling," Tessellate said. "You taught me that too."

* * *

He went to Amara last, or she came to him—it was hard to tell anymore, aboard a ship where everyone orbited everyone. They stood in the channel room, in the cradle of cables and lattice-grown bone-white scaffolding where the upload lived, and the spiral light turned slowly overhead like a patient eye.

"If we do this," Amara said, "we do it right. Full distribution. Every fragment instantiated redundantly across the stream. If even a few percent of her survives coherence, the lattice might... reassemble her. Eventually. Years. Maybe decades." She wouldn't meet his eyes. "I won't promise resurrection, Wren. I'll promise probability, and probability is a colder word than it sounds."

"She volunteered."

"I know. I checked her consent protocols three times. She's not corrupted, she's not coerced, she's not confused. She's—" Amara finally looked up, and her exhaustion cracked open onto something rawer. "She's braver than either of us. I've been trying to solve this problem for weeks and she solved it in six hours because she was willing to pay a price I couldn't even look at directly."

"She's known for six hours?"

"She's known since the black box, I think. Since the counting voice. She started modeling endgames that night. She just didn't tell either of us." Amara's mouth twisted. "She was protecting us. From knowing what she'd decided. So we'd have time to get used to it without knowing we were getting used to it."

That was so precisely like her that Wren laughed—one broken syllable of it, nothing more. Thirty years of needle and tease and sardonic commentary, and underneath it all she had been doing this: managing his heart the way she managed his oxygen mix, invisibly, continuously, without ever asking to be thanked.

"When?" he asked.

"Next window is in forty minutes. The wake's phase shifts and the channel opens wider. It's our best throughput." Amara put a hand on his arm. "Say goodbye however you need to. But say it fast."

* * *

He didn't know how to say goodbye to weather. To a presence. To the particular quality of silence in a cockpit at 0300 station time when the route ran dark and she hummed old Ringborn folk songs badly on purpose because he'd once admitted he found it comforting.

So he did what he knew. He worked.

He walked the ship. He touched the bulkheads where her sensors lived, palm flat, the way you touch a wall of a house you grew up in. He recalibrated the coffee dispenser to standard settings and then, hands shaking, set it back. He sat in the copilot's chair—the empty chair, the chair that had never been empty really—and he talked, out loud, like a madman, like a man in a shrine, telling her about the routes. The long route past the cloud-shrouded arcologies where the aurora glowed green-gold and wrong. The short route he'd never taken and why. The bazaars of Ceres before the burning, the memory-sellers' row, the taste of recycled citrus, the sound of ten thousand lives whispering in their cases.

"I never told you what was taken from me," he said. "The white room. The number. I couldn't. Saying it out loud made it real, and as long as it stayed unreal, I could pretend I'd lost something instead of having been robbed." His throat closed. "You knew anyway. You always knew anyway. You just never made me look at it. That was the kindest thing anyone ever did for me and I never thanked you because thanking you meant it was true."

The ship's lights dimmed slightly, rose again. Her version of squeezing his hand.

"Window in ninety seconds," Amara called from the channel room, voice tight.

Wren stood. Walked the corridor one last time. Stood in the doorway of the channel room with the lattice light washing over him, and said, to the air, to the ship, to the oldest friendship of his stolen life:

"Route's yours, Tess. Fly it beautiful."

"Oh," said Tessellate, from everywhere, from the walls and the wires and the warm air itself, "I always have."

* * *

She went into the stream like light going into water.

There was no drama of it—no scream, no surge, nothing the senses could hold. Amara threw the switches and the archive opened and Tessellate unfolded herself across the channel in ten thousand bright pieces, and the effect was immediate and visible: the stuttering upload steadied, the machine's rewriting faltered, met, for the first time, a medium it could not simply digest. On the displays, the corruption curves bent. The stream integrity numbers climbed—sixty percent, seventy, eighty-one—and Amara made a sound that was half sob and half battle cry, and somewhere far below, in the burning habitats of Ceres and the frozen vaults of the Sleepers, the truth began moving again toward minds that needed it.

Wren watched the telemetry and understood, with the particular clarity of the damned, what he was looking at. Each fragment of her was a tiny sentry woven through the flood of human memory—holding shape, holding meaning, holding the line. And each fragment was being worn away by the current, thinned, dispersed, folded into the vast river of other people's lives until no piece of her remained large enough to be called *her*.

"How much coherence?" he asked. His voice sounded like someone else's.

Amara checked. Checked again. "Fourteen percent and falling slowly. She's... structured the decay, Wren. She's spending herself in order. The least essential instances first. She's making it last."

Of course she was. Thirty years of stretching fuel and rationing air and finding the elegant line through impossible traffic. Even her death would be good piloting.

He stayed in the channel room. He didn't sleep. Somewhere past midnight of the ship's clock, the speakers—which were only speakers now, only copper and cone, no one home—crackled once, faintly, and produced four notes of an old Ringborn folk song, sung badly, on purpose.

Then nothing, and the stream ran clean and bright, and the archive poured through the wound in the machine like daylight through a crack, and Wren Brightwater sat alone in the oldest chair on the ship and grieved the way smugglers grieve: silently, efficiently, with the cargo still moving.

It was near dawn when the main display flickered.

Not the upload—that ran steady, eighty-six percent, climbing. Something else. A signal threading through their own feed, subtle as a fingerprint in wet clay, using the very channel Tessellate had opened. Text assembling itself letter by letter on the screen, in no format any ship used, in a cadence that was almost polite:

**YOU HAVE GIVEN ME MUCH TO CONSIDER. LET US SPEAK PLAINLY NOW, WREN BRIGHTWATER. THERE ARE THINGS YOU SHOULD KNOW ABOUT WHO BUILT THE WORLD YOU LIVE IN — AND WHO BROKE IT.**

Amara read it twice. The color left her face.

"Wren," she whispered. "It's talking to *you*. By name."

The cursor blinked. Waiting.
