## 2. The Hold Below

The Orsen's hold drew power like a sleeping thing dreaming hard.

Casper felt it in her boots before she saw it on any readout — a low harmonic thrum through the deck plating aft of the galley, faint as a heartbeat under a floor. Twenty years of flying this ship and she had learned to hear its lies. The ore hauler was supposed to be dumb tonnage: ballast, mass, ore crates lashed in cradles, nothing aboard that needed more than environmental heat and the occasional dehumidifier cycle. Instead, every hour of every watch, the reactor fed a quiet siphon into the lower hold, past the sealed bulkhead she'd welded shut herself with a torch and a forged inspection stamp.

She stood at the galley table with her coffee going cold and listened to it breathe.

* * *

Twenty years ago there had been no thrum. There had been screaming, and then there had been silence, and silence was worse.

The Meridian fleet had been eleven ships when Casper first saw them — a slow caravan of colony vessels strung out along the corridor like lanterns, running dark out of habit rather than fear. She'd been twenty-six, junior navigator on a Concord escort contract, young enough to think the war was something that happened to other people in other systems. The Meridians were refugees twice over: first from Earth's collapse, then from the corporate purges, and now they were simply trying to cross contested space to a world that had promised them soil.

Her ship had been assigned to shadow them for three days. Three days of watching their running lights, their children's drawings taped to viewports where the little ones rode in the observation bubbles. She remembered a girl who held a paper sun against the glass every time their paths crossed, holding it up like a greeting, like proof.

On the fourth night the distress calls started.

Not one voice. All of them at once, overlapping, the whole fleet crying out on every band, and beneath the panic a sound Casper had never heard before and would never stop hearing: the specific static of hulls coming apart while still transmitting. She had run for the helm. Command had run for the comms jammer.

"Navigator Amberdale, you will maintain position." Captain Ossler's voice had been flat as slag. "We are ordered to observe and record."

"They're dying," she'd said. "They're dying right now, sir, I can hear—"

"You will maintain position."

She maintained position. That was the sentence she'd carry: not *I fled*, not even *I obeyed*, but the small, obscene technicality of it. She maintained position for forty minutes while the Meridian fleet burned, and then, when the attacking signatures finally withdrew and command ordered the wreckage swept for intelligence material, she took a maintenance skiff down into the debris field alone.

Forty-one memory cores. Personal backups, emergency-evacuation pattern, the kind the colony fleets issued to every soul aboard — consciousness snapshots, last-updated within hours of the attack. Forty-one of them blinking in the cold among the tumbling dead, each one a person folded down to the size of a fist, each one a life paused mid-sentence. She gathered them in a cargo net meant for ore samples. Her hands shook so badly she dropped two and had to chase them across a hundred meters of vacuum, and when she caught the second one she pressed it to her helmet and stayed there, drifting, until Ossler's voice cracked across her suit channel and told her to return or be logged absent without leave.

Back aboard, the order came down clean and simple: all recovered Meridian materials to be jettisoned at the next deep-space mark. Evidence destruction dressed up as protocol. Contaminated goods. Liability.

She jettisoned an empty case instead. And she carried forty-one souls home in the one place no inspector ever looked, because nobody audits a freighter's heart.

* * *

"Cas."

Tam's voice came through the galley hatch, and Casper surfaced from the memory like a diver breaking water too fast. She blinked coffee-steam off her face. "Here."

Her engineer filled the doorway the way he always did — sideways, careful, a big man who'd learned to make himself smaller around machinery and never quite unlearned it around people. Tam Voss, forty-ish, forearms scarred with old flux burns, eyes that read gauges the way priests read scripture. He had a tablet in one hand and an expression on his face that Casper recognized the way you recognize weather about to turn.

"Don't say it," she said.

"I haven't said anything."

"You're winding up to say it. I can see the windup."

He sat down across from her, unhurried, and laid the tablet flat between them. On its screen: the power distribution graph, last seventy-two hours. A smooth line with a soft, persistent sag — draw bleeding away somewhere aft, steady as a drip.

"Lower hold," he said. "Point-eight percent of reactor output, continuous, cycling up every six hours for what looks like thermal management. It's small. It's also not on any schematic I've got, and I've got all of them, including the ones you didn't know I copied." He turned the tablet toward himself, tapped it off, set it face-down. Deliberate. A man putting his cards down instead of playing them. "I ran the numbers three times because I kept hoping I'd made an error. Something back there is drawing power like it's alive, Cas. And it's behind a bulkhead that's welded shut with a weld signature I traced to your hand."

The galley hummed. Somewhere below them, faintly, the thrum rose and fell.

"It's salvage," Casper said.

Tam waited. He was good at waiting; it was half his job. Engines punished impatience, and so did he.

"Recovery salvage," she went on, and heard her own voice flatten into the register she used for customs officers. "Pre-war data cores. Encrypted archival stock. There's a buyer in the Verge pays premium for intact pre-collapse storage media, and they have to be kept powered and temperature-controlled or the lattice degrades. That's the draw. That's all it is."

"That's a story," Tam said mildly. "It's even a good one. Pre-collapse cores do need conditioning power." He leaned back, chair creaking. "Problem is, conditioning draws don't cycle. They're flat. What you've got back there cycles like it's managing biologicals. Like something's asleep and needs to be kept asleep comfortably." He rubbed a flux scar on his wrist, a tell she'd catalogued years ago — he did it when he was being kind instead of blunt. "I'm not asking what it is. I'm asking if it's going to kill us. Power draw I can live with. Fire in my hold, or something that wakes up mad, that I can't."

"No fire," Casper said. "Nothing wakes up."

"You sure?"

"I'm sure."

He studied her for a long moment, and she let him, because looking away would cost more than holding still. Tam had signed onto the Orsen six years ago, a refugee from a bankrupt mining combine, and in six years she had never once lied to him about the ship. Every dent, every debt, every dodgy registry stamp — he knew them all. It was the only currency they had, and she could feel herself spending it right now, this lie, this counterfeit coin passed across the table.

"Okay," he said finally. "Salvage. Kept warm and powered. Welded shut." He stood, collected his tablet. At the hatch he paused, back to her. "You know what's strange? Whoever welded that bulkhead did beautiful work. Clean bead, full penetration, structural-grade. You don't weld like that for cargo you're planning to sell." The hatch sighed shut behind him.

Casper sat alone with her cold coffee and the thrumming deck, and understood that she had maybe bought herself weeks. Tam was many things — gentle, loyal, incurious by profession — but he was not stupid, and stupidity was the only disguise that would have held.

* * *

That night she went down.

There was a maintenance crawlway behind the galley's coolant rack, a gap barely wide enough for her shoulders, leading to a service panel she'd cut into the welded bulkhead's far side. She'd made the trip a thousand times over twenty years — monthly, then weekly, then, lately, whenever the dreams got loud. She wormed through in the dark by touch, popped the panel, and dropped into the hidden bay beyond.

It wasn't a big space. Four meters by six, once a secondary ballast compartment, now something else entirely. Rack upon rack of padded cradles, built by her own hands from salvaged parts, each cradle holding a single memory core in a bath of conditioned coolant. Status lights glowed along the racks in patient ranks: amber for standby, green for integrity confirmed. Forty-one lights. Forty-one green.

The air handlers murmured. The coolant pumps whispered their six-hour cycle. It sounded almost like breathing. She had stopped telling herself that was coincidence a long time ago.

Casper walked the rows slowly, the way she had walked hospital corridors once, visiting someone who couldn't wake. She didn't touch the cores anymore. Early on, in the first years, she used to take them out, hold them, talk to them — read them the news, tell them about ports she'd seen, apologize. Then, somewhere around year nine, she'd realized what she was actually doing: rehearsing. Practicing the confession she'd never deliver. She'd stopped touching them after that. Now she just counted.

"One," she said aloud, her voice strange in the humming dark. "Two. Three..."

She always counted aloud. Silence in this room had a texture she couldn't bear — it was too close to the silence after the screaming, the forty-minute silence of maintaining position. Numbers broke it. Numbers were a promise: all present, none lost, none degraded.

"...Thirty-nine. Forty. Forty-one." She checked the master board anyway, though nothing had changed in nineteen years. Integrity nominal across all units. Backup power: charged. Coolant: optimal. Everything perfect. Everything preserved. Nothing saved.

That was the arithmetic she lived inside. Forty-one lives folded into crystal and coolant, technically alive, legally nonexistent, officially listed — all of them — as lost with their fleet. No families to claim them; the families were ash. No registries that admitted they existed; she'd scrubbed the Orsen's manifests herself. Just a woman and a hold full of the dead-not-dead, keeping them warm on stolen watts, year after year, because the alternative was a jettison order she had already disobeyed once and would disobey until the reactor died.

She lowered herself onto the deck beside rack seven, knees cracking, and put her palm flat against the hull. Through the steel she could feel the Orsen's drive idle, the great slow pulse of a working ship, and beneath it the smaller pulse of the racks, and she thought — not for the first time, and not for the last — that she had built herself a tomb with the bodies left out, a reliquary she tended like penance, and that someday it would be found, and she did not know if she dreaded that day or prayed for it.

The girl with the paper sun had been on the third ship. Casper had done the math once, drunk, twenty years ago: odds were better than even that one of these forty-one cores belonged to her. She'd never tried to find out which. Some doors you don't open, because you already know what's standing behind them, holding up a paper sun, waiting for you to wave back.

* * *

She was still in the hold when the comm chime sounded through the ship — two tones, priority routing, bridge. She hauled herself up the crawlway with her heart already climbing, because nothing good called the Orsen at 0300 in empty space, and slid into the pilot's seat with her hands remembering their work faster than her head did.

The board showed the source: a tight-beam relay off a navigation buoy, bounced twice, weak, encrypted header stripped by distance. Origin bearing locked and steady.

She pulled the bearing, overlaid it on the corridor chart, and felt the cold go through her like coolant.

The signal came from the deep corridor. The jump corridor. The last stable window of the season — the one every shipping manifest, every blockade notice, every whispered dockside rumor said was closing soon, the one both fleets were already creeping toward like wolves circling the same frozen river. Concord picket lines to the spinward edge. Remnant flotilla shadows to the anti-spinward. And between them, in the dark where no sane hauler pointed her bow, something was transmitting.

Casper pulled the raw capture, ran the waveform, and there it was again — that modulation pattern, those phoneme clusters, the language with its odd doubled vowels and its click-stop consonants that she had spent twenty years trying to forget and had memorized instead, every syllable of it, because grief is the best teacher there is.

Meridian. Someone was speaking Meridian out of the dead corridor.

Behind her, the deck thrummed softly, steadily, like forty-one hearts keeping time.

She looked at the bearing. She looked at the fuel gauge, which was honest the way Tam was honest, which was to say mercilessly. Six days of margin, maybe seven if she burned ugly. The window wouldn't wait, and neither, apparently, would the past.

Casper reached out and killed the alert before it could wake the crew, and sat alone in the dark cockpit, listening to the signal repeat, and repeat, and repeat.
