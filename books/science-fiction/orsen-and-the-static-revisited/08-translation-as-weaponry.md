## 8. Translation as Weaponry

The message from Vey arrived at 0400 ship-time, which told Casper everything she needed to know about how the Concord handled patience.

*Full translation within seventy-two hours. Encrypted channel, the one you know. Format per attached protocol. Failure to comply will be noted in your file.*

Casper read it twice in the dark of her cabin, the words glowing against her retinas, and thought about files. Files were what they kept on people they intended to use up. She'd been in one before — twenty years ago, a younger navigator with a clean record and a clean conscience, both of which had been spent by dawn of a single terrible day.

She got up, made coffee she didn't want, and sat down at her workbench to build a lie.

It was strange, how calm the work felt. She had expected her hands to shake. Instead they moved with the same sure economy they used for plotting a burn through debris fields, because that was what this was: navigation. There was a corridor between two fleets, and the corridor was made of words, and she was going to thread the Orsen through it without getting anyone killed.

The original transmission filled her secondary screen. She had decrypted another eleven percent of it overnight, working from the linguistic keys she'd reconstructed over two decades of quiet, guilty study — a language kept alive in her head by forty-one sleepers who could no longer speak it themselves. Meridian Standard, the trade-tongue of the colony fleet, built on Old Terran roots but bent by three generations of isolation into something softer, rounder, sung more than spoken. She could still hear Sefa's voice teaching it to her across a cargo bay table, laughing at Casper's flat Concord accent. *You say it like you're filing a report. It's not a report. It's a promise.*

The transmission was not a promise. The transmission was an indictment, and it was accurate, and that was the problem.

*They came under our own colors,* the text said, in the fragment she'd decoded before Vey's message arrived. *Concord hulls. Concord weapons. We counted the squadron markings. We were not attacked by strangers. We were murdered by our protectors.*

Every word true. Every word a match to the dry fuel soaked through both fleets. If this reached the Remnant flotilla intact — if the Concord's analysts confirmed it, or worse, if the Remnants' own linguists cracked it independently — the corridor would run red inside a week. Two hundred ships' worth of grief and grievance, all of it converging on the only stable jump window of the season, the window that stood between Casper and home.

So she did what navigators do when the direct route is mined.

She began to falsify.

* * *

The work took her most of two days, and it was the hardest translation she had ever done, because the difficulty wasn't linguistic. It was architectural. A bad lie collapses under its first inspection; a good one is load-bearing all the way down. She needed a fabrication that would survive Concord analysts, Remnant linguists, and the one person she couldn't model at all — whoever had written the original signal, someone alive, someone who remembered the night as clearly as she did.

She started with the truth and bent it, the way you bend a bulkhead just past tolerance without cracking it.

The massacre had been carried out by ships flying Concord markings. True. But there had been a third party in the system that night — she remembered it from the traffic logs she'd memorized during the court of inquiry, logs since sealed. The Kestrel Line. A pirate syndicate, defunct now for fifteen years, wiped out in a joint Concord-Remnant operation that both sides still cited as their last act of cooperation. The Kestrels had been raiders of Meridian's old shipping lanes. They had the motive, the history, the regional presence. And crucially, they were dead. Dead men made perfect scapegoats; they never contradicted you.

Her version went like this: the attacking squadron had been Kestrel ships operating under stolen Concord transponder codes — a documented Kestrel tactic, attested in no fewer than four historical incidents she could cite from open records. The real Concord squadron whose codes were stolen had been lured away on a false distress vector, which explained why no legitimate relief ever arrived. The signal's author, seeing Concord markings and hearing Concord-coded chatter, had drawn the only conclusion available to them. An understandable error. A tragic one. But an error.

She wove the misdirection into the translation itself, not by changing words — any competent analyst would compare against raw intercepts eventually — but by emphasis and framing. Where the original said *their markings*, her annotation read *the markings displayed*, with a translator's note flagging the well-documented practice of transponder spoofing among Kestrel remnants. Where the original named the squadron by its call-sign prefix, she rendered the prefix phonetically ambiguous and footnoted two possible readings, one Concord, one Kestrel, weighting the commentary toward the latter. She wrote eleven pages of scholarly apparatus around a core of maybe forty translated lines, and the apparatus was the weapon. Analysts trust footnotes. Analysts trust a translator who seems to be arguing with herself, reluctantly arriving at conclusions. Certainty reads as forgery; doubt reads as honesty.

She built herself a translator's persona while she worked — a cautious, meticulous professional haunted by ambiguity, someone who flagged every uncertainty in italics. It was almost fun, in the way that disarming a bomb is almost fun. She gave this fictional translator a habit of second-guessing, a tendency to defer to established record, a small vanity about her own rigor. Then she let that persona deliver the lie in the voice of a woman telling the truth about everything except the one thing that mattered.

By the second evening she had a draft, and she hated it, and it was good.

* * *

Tam found her at the workbench at 2200, carrying two mugs and wearing the expression he wore when he'd decided not to ask something.

"Power draw on your console's been triple baseline for two days," he said, setting a mug down. "Whatever you're running, it's cooking the local bus. I rerouted you off the spare inverter so you don't trip a breaker mid-burn."

"Thank you."

He looked at the screens. She didn't angle them away. That was part of it too — letting him see shapes without meanings, the apparatus pages scrolling, the linguistic tables. A curious man shown a locked door will pick it; a curious man handed a key to the wrong door will stop looking.

"Meridian Standard," Tam said. "That's the dead colony language."

"It is."

"You speak it?"

"Badly." She sipped the coffee. It had gone bitter. "I'm translating a distress signal for a client. Salvage arbitration. Long story."

Tam nodded slowly, the way he did when he was filing something rather than accepting it. "The distress signal from the contested corridor. The one you told us was background chatter."

"The same." She met his eyes and held them, steady as a bearing. Lying to Tam cost more than lying to Vey, and she noted the cost the way she noted fuel burn — a debit against a finite account. "There are parties interested in what it says. Interested enough to pay. Interested enough to make trouble if it goes wrong. The less you know, the less trouble can find you."

"That's the second time you've used that line."

"It keeps being true."

He stood there a moment longer, mug steaming, and she watched him decide — visibly, the way his shoulders set — to take the key to the wrong door. For now. "Triple draw again tonight and I'm cutting you to battery," he said. "Lie or no lie."

"Fair."

He left. She looked at the draft translation glowing on her screen and felt the account debit settle somewhere behind her sternum, next to the older debts. One more person aboard the Orsen she was keeping in the dark to keep alive. The list had started at forty-one. It was growing again, and she didn't have a hold big enough for what that meant.

* * *

Vey's reply came back in nine hours, which meant the Concord had analysts awake and waiting, which meant the stakes were exactly as high as Casper had priced them.

She transmitted the package at 0330 — full translation, apparatus, source annotations, the works — and then did something she had planned carefully: she included a cover note in her cautious-translator persona, flagging two residual uncertainties and recommending independent corroboration of the transponder-spoofing hypothesis against Kestrel operational archives. It was a risk. Handing them a thread to pull could unravel her. But an analyst with nothing left to check starts generating his own checks, and Casper wanted their skepticism aimed at the Kestrel question, not at her.

Then she waited, and waiting was worse than building.

She passed the hours doing what she always did when the fear got loud: she went down to the hold. Not to the cores themselves — she didn't touch them anymore, hadn't in years, some superstition about disturbing the sleepers — but to the observation hatch above the bay, where she could look down at the rack of forty-one dark cylinders and feel the weight of why.

"They're going to ask me to help them bury it," she said quietly, to nobody, to all of them. "And I'm going to help. Because if the truth comes out sideways, in the middle of two fleets with loaded guns, it won't free anyone. It'll just give them permission."

The cores said nothing. They never did. Twenty years of silence, and she had learned to hear it as a kind of answer: *do what you have to, and don't forget.* She hadn't forgotten. Forgetting was the one thing she'd never been able to do, though God knew she'd tried, in the early years, with drink and distance and the anesthetic of routine.

Below her, in the dark, forty-one people waited to be remembered properly. She was buying time for that remembrance with a lie, and she told herself — as she would tell herself many times in the coming days — that the ledger balanced.

It didn't. She knew it didn't. But it bought six days, and six days was a jump window, and a jump window was lives.

* * *

Vey came in person.

That was the part Casper hadn't predicted. Forty-one hours after transmission, the Orsen's proximity alarm sang once and a Concord cutter slid alongside, sleek as a knife, and Vey boarded through the docking collar with two escorts who stayed aboard the cutter and a smile that stayed with her.

"Navigator Amberdale." She looked around the Orsen's cramped command module with the frank appraisal of someone pricing a used ship. "Your translation arrived. I read it twice."

"And?"

"And it's very good work." Vey settled into the co-pilot's chair uninvited, crossing her legs, producing a slate she didn't look at. "Thorough. Cautious. The kind of analysis that makes a reviewer feel smart for agreeing with it." Her eyes came up. "Almost too good. You understand my problem."

"I imagine you have several."

"One," Vey said, holding up a finger. "A freight navigator who happens to speak an extinct colonial language fluently enough to produce eleven pages of philological apparatus. Two: a translation that resolves, neatly, in the direction of de-escalation, delivered precisely when my superiors need de-escalation. Three—" the third finger, and here the smile thinned into something honest, which was somehow worse— "the original signal names Concord squadron markings. Your translation explains them away. In my experience, explanations that arrive exactly when needed deserve a closer look than explanations that arrive inconveniently."

Casper held the gaze. Her pulse was a metronome; she kept it below the audible range. "Officer Vey, I learned Meridian Standard hauling contract freight through the old colony lanes, off crews who still spoke it at cards. As for direction — I translate what's there. If the text exonerates the Concord, that's the text's doing, not mine. Unless you'd prefer I find guilt instead? I can probably manage it. Texts are flexible, given enough apparatus."

Something flickered in Vey's face — respect, or its counterfeit. "That," she said, "is exactly the sort of thing a fabricator would say."

"And also exactly the sort of thing an honest translator would say. Which is why you're not paying me for honesty. You're paying me for a document that keeps two fleets from shooting each other across your jump corridor. Does the document do that?"

Vey was quiet for a long moment. Outside the viewport, the cutter's running lights pulsed green, patient.

"Yes," she said finally. "It does. Preliminary review accepts it. The Kestrel hypothesis is elegant — the syndicate's spoofing history is well-documented, and dead men complicate retaliation nicely." She stood, smoothing her jacket. "My superiors will transmit a moderated version to the Remnant council within the day, framed as preliminary findings pending verification. Verification takes weeks. Weeks take us past the window."

"And after the window?"

"After the window," Vey said, pausing at the airlock, "everyone re-examines everything, including you. Enjoy the corridor while it lasts, Navigator." She stepped through, then turned, one hand on the collar. "One more thing. Professional courtesy. The Remnants have revived someone. A witness. Backup revival — old tech, but it took. She's fragmentary, traumatized, and she remembers the attack night. If her memory sharpens before the window closes, your careful document may find itself competing with a living testimony." The smile returned, thin as foil. "I do hope your translation holds."

The airlock cycled. The cutter undocked. Casper stood alone in the command module, listening to the station's ambient hum, feeling the shape of the deadline close around her throat like a collar fitted to measure.

A witness. Alive. Someone who had been there, whose memories were waking up out of the same darkness that held her forty-one sleepers — and whose memories would not bend, would not footnote themselves, would not accept a cautious translator's elegant apparatus. Memory didn't argue. Memory simply remembered, and it remembered the truth: Concord hulls, Concord weapons, and a rescue ship that ran.

She walked to the viewport. Out past Brink Station's spinning ring, far off, the contested corridor hung between the two fleets like a fuse burning at both ends. Somewhere out there, a woman was dreaming her way back into the worst night of a dead colony, and every detail she recovered was a degree of heat.

Casper had bought calm. She could feel it already starting to cost.

She keyed the intercom. "Tam. Spin up the drive. We're leaving orbit within the hour — I want us through the corridor ahead of whatever's coming."

"Ahead of what's coming?" Tam's voice crackled back.

She watched the distant fleets glitter, cold and patient, and gave the honest answer to the empty room before she gave the safe one to her crew.

"Everything."
