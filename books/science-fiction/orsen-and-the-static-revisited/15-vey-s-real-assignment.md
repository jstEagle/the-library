## 15. Vey's Real Assignment

The encrypted channel opened at 0300 station time, which told Ilsa Vey everything she needed to know before a single word was spoken. Nobody scheduled a routine debrief at 0300. Routine debriefs happened at 1400, in well-lit rooms, with coffee and a junior officer taking minutes. What happened at 0300 was the other kind of conversation — the kind that left no minutes, took no notes, and existed officially nowhere at all.

She sat alone in her quarters aboard the *Concordant Vigil*, the blockade's flagship, with the lights dimmed to quarter power because bright light made her eyes ache after fourteen hours of staring at signal intercepts, and because she had learned long ago that darkness made people honest on comms in ways they never managed face to face. The screen before her resolved into the grey, featureless mask of a voice-scrubbed feed: no face, no insignia, just a flattened harmonic that Concord intelligence used for exactly this tier of conversation.

"Officer Vey. Status."

"The translation is complete." She kept her voice level, professional, the voice of a woman delivering a report rather than a confession. "Delivered by the freighter navigator, as contracted. Full linguistic reconstruction of the Meridian-band transmission. I've verified it against our own fragmentary archives. It's good work. Better than good."

"And its conclusions?"

Here it was. The pivot point. The moment where the night became what it was.

Vey had rehearsed this. Not the words — the words were simple, a sentence and a half — but the act of saying them into an official channel, knowing that saying them made her either complicit or disposable, and that those categories were not mutually exclusive.

"The transmission names the perpetrator of the Meridian massacre," she said. "It identifies the attacking vessels by hull configuration, drive signature, and squadron call-signs. The navigator's translation attributes the attack to the Kestrel Combine — a defunct pirate syndicate, dissolved eighty years ago. Her evidence is internally consistent. It is also," she paused, and let the pause do its work, "fabricated."

The silence on the channel lasted four seconds. In Vey's experience, four seconds of silence from a scrubbed superior was practically a shout.

"You're certain."

"I'm certain the translation is false. I'm less certain about why." She leaned back in her chair, and the old acceleration couch creaked under her, a small human sound in a conversation designed to contain none. "The original transmission doesn't name the Kestrel Combine. It names a Concord squadron. Call-sign designators match Third Interdiction Wing, detached operations, twenty-two years ago. Hull configurations match Concord corvettes of that vintage. Drive signatures match Concord military drives of that vintage. I've cross-referenced everything twice. There is no ambiguity in the source material. The ambiguity exists only in the translation we purchased."

Another silence. Longer this time. Somewhere behind the scrubbed voice, she imagined a room she'd never see — a room with better chairs and worse air, filled with people whose careers depended on the contents of this call going a particular direction.

"Your assessment of the navigator's motive?"

"That's the interesting question, sir." She used the honorific deliberately, a small offering, a reminder that she understood hierarchy even while she was holding a knife of information. "Casper Amberdale is not a Concord asset. She's not Remnant either. Her service record is clean, her cargo manifests are boring, and her financial history shows a woman who has spent two decades hauling ore and paying her crew on time. People like that don't fabricate translations to protect Concord squadrons out of loyalty. They do it for one of three reasons: money, fear, or guilt."

"And your read?"

"Guilt." Vey said it flatly, because there was no softer way to say it. "I've spent six days aboard Brink Station with that woman. I've watched her drink exactly one glass of wine per evening and check her ship's power draw three times a night. I've watched her flinch at the word 'Meridian' when she thinks no one is listening. Whatever she's carrying, she's been carrying it a long time, and she has decided that the shape of her penance is a lie that keeps two fleets from shooting each other across the only jump corridor either of them can still use."

"So she's protecting us."

"She's protecting everyone," Vey said. "Which is not the same thing, and which is precisely the problem."

* * *

She had known, of course. That was the thing she could never say aloud, the thing that sat in her chest like a swallowed stone: she had known since her second year in the service, when she'd pulled a file she wasn't cleared for and read it anyway, because curiosity was the one sin the Academy never managed to train out of her.

Third Interdiction Wing, detached ops rotation, cycle 2247. Officially: anti-piracy patrol along the outer Meridian approach vectors. Unofficially — in the addendum that someone had filed under a mislabeled classification code, buried in a logistics subdirectory where no historian would ever think to look — unofficially, the wing had been running interdiction exercises against civilian traffic without authorization, escalating over eleven weeks from signal interference to physical blockade to, finally, on the night of the massacre, live fire.

The file didn't say who gave the order to fire. That was the elegant part, the part that told Vey the cover-up had been designed by professionals. The file said only that the wing's engagement protocols had been "amended locally," a phrase so bland it was almost beautiful, and that the subsequent loss of the Meridian colony fleet had been attributed to "unidentified hostile action consistent with piracy." Forty-one survivors' backups had been logged as evidence. Then the evidence lockup had suffered a catastrophic environmental failure — pressure seal breach, total core degradation — and the forty-one backups had been written off as destroyed.

Except they hadn't been destroyed. Someone had stolen them first. And now Vey knew who, because the woman sitting across a card table from her at Brink Station, translating a dead language with hands that trembled slightly on the third cup of tea, was the same woman whose name appeared in the lockup's access logs on the night of the "failure."

Casper Amberdale hadn't just survived the massacre. She'd robbed the Concord of its evidence, smuggled forty-one dead souls across contested space for twenty years, and then — when the past finally came calling in the form of a distress signal in an extinct language — she'd built a lie elaborate enough to keep the truth buried and two fleets alive.

And Vey's superiors knew all of it. Had known, in whatever way institutions know things — partially, deniably, through channels that left no fingerprints. Which was why the call had come at 0300.

* * *

"Officer Vey." The scrubbed voice returned, and something in its cadence had shifted — a fraction more careful, a fraction more measured. "Let me be clear about your assignment parameters."

"I assumed we were arriving at them."

"Your contract with the navigator was to obtain a translation of the Meridian transmission. You have done so. The translation you have obtained attributes the massacre to a defunct third party. This translation is now in Concord hands, and its conclusions are... operationally convenient."

"Operationally convenient," Vey repeated. "That's a remarkable phrase for a war crime."

The silence that followed had a different texture than the earlier ones. Colder. She'd crossed a line, and she noted, with the detached professionalism that was both her greatest asset and her deepest character flaw, that she did not particularly care.

"Officer. Your task is to ensure that translation becomes the authoritative record. Both fleets will demand verification. The Remnants especially — they have revived a witness, and witnesses are unpredictable. When verification requests arrive, you will corroborate the translation. You will produce supporting archival material as required. You will, if necessary, testify. The Kestrel Combine attribution must hold."

"And if the witness contradicts it?"

"Then the witness's memory integrity will be called into question. Backup revival is an imprecise science. Degradation, confabulation, implanted suggestion — these are documented phenomena. A single fragmented witness cannot outweigh a full linguistic reconstruction supported by archival corroboration."

"A single fragmented witness," Vey said slowly, "who was actually there."

"A single fragmented witness," the voice repeated, with the patience of a man explaining arithmetic to a child, "whose testimony would ignite a fleet engagement in the last stable corridor between Concord space and the Remnant holdings. Do you have any conception of the casualty projections for a corridor war? Neither fleet can retreat. Neither fleet can reinforce fast enough. The projections run to sixty thousand dead in the opening month alone. Sixty thousand, Officer Vey, weighed against the reputational discomfort of one squadron that no longer formally exists, commanded by officers who are themselves mostly dead."

"There it is," Vey said quietly. "The arithmetic."

"The arithmetic is my job. Making the arithmetic true is yours."

* * *

She terminated the call and sat in the dark for a long time.

This was the part no recruitment holovid ever showed: the moment when you understood, fully and without remainder, what the institution actually was. Not the flag, not the charter, not the solemn oaths about protection and order. The institution was a machine for converting uncomfortable truths into manageable ones, and every officer inside it was either a gear or a grievance, and grievances got ground down or spat out, and gears got to keep their pensions.

Twenty-two years ago, a rogue squadron had murdered a colony fleet, and the Concord had written it off as piracy, and the evidence had conveniently destroyed itself, and the whole rotten structure had held for two decades because nobody with standing had ever pulled the thread. Now a freighter navigator was pulling it, not out of ambition but out of grief, and the institution's response was not reckoning but containment. Contain the translation. Discredit the witness. Bury the squadron. Keep the peace — the word *peace*, deployed in defense of a massacre, doing more violence to the language than the massacre had done to the colonists.

And yet.

Vey rose, crossed to her viewport, and looked out at the blockade line. Beyond it, visible only as a scatter of drive-flares at extreme range, the Remnant flotilla held its own formation, and between the two fleets hung the empty stretch of black where the jump window would open in a matter of days. Sixty thousand projected dead. She turned the number over in her mind and found that she could not make it weigh what her superiors wanted it to weigh. Numbers were supposed to be neutral. But some numbers were load-bearing lies, and some were just bodies waiting to happen, and the difference between them was exactly the kind of distinction the institution had trained her never to make.

Because here was the thing she had not said on the call, the thing she would carry into whatever came next:

She suspected Casper Amberdale's translation was false. She was certain of it. She had watched the woman build it, had seen the micro-hesitations, the too-smooth phrasing, the way Amberdale's eyes went distant when she described the Kestrel Combine's alleged attack patterns — reciting, not remembering. And none of it mattered, because the fabrication was useful. Useful to the fleets, who would not burn the corridor. Useful to the Concord, whose rot would stay sealed another decade. Useful, perhaps, even to the dead, who were beyond caring what story explained their deaths.

A lie that saved sixty thousand lives. A truth that might start a war. Vey had joined the service believing the choice would never be hers to make, and now it was, and the institution had made it for her, and she despised them for the ease with which they'd made it.

But she was a gear. Gears turned.

* * *

At 0415, she composed her operational directive and transmitted it down the chain: verification protocols for the Amberdale translation, archival support packages, briefing schedule for the diplomatic liaison teams. Clean, competent, damning work. Then she opened a private folder — unencrypted, stored locally, the digital equivalent of a letter pinned inside a coat — and began, for reasons she did not fully examine, to draft a memo.

It was not a confession. Confessions were for courts, and there would be no court; she understood that now with perfect clarity. It was a record. Dates, file references, the mislabeled classification code, the phrase *engagement protocols amended locally*. Everything she'd pulled from that logistics subdirectory nine years ago, laid out in dry bureaucratic prose, addressed to no one, dated today. If the corridor burned anyway — if the lie collapsed, if Sefa Kell's memories proved louder than Vey's paperwork — then somewhere, somehow, the real record needed to exist outside the institution's hands.

She saved it, closed the folder, and told herself the exercise was sentimental. Intelligence officers were not permitted sentiment. Sentiment was how navigators ended up stealing evidence lockups and spending twenty years hauling ghosts around contested space.

Her console chimed. An intercept priority flag — automated, routine, one of hundreds processed nightly. She almost dismissed it. Then she saw the origin vector, and her hand stopped above the key.

The Remnant flotilla had altered course. Not toward the corridor. Toward a search pattern — a sweep grid centered on a shipping lane, running registry matches against commercial drive signatures. They were hunting a specific ship.

Vey pulled the parameter set, ran it against the merchant registries, and felt the cold move up her spine as the match resolved. One vessel. One aging ore hauler with a twenty-year service history and a captain who translated extinct languages for spare credits.

They weren't hunting the truth. They were hunting Casper Amberdale — and they were closing on her position faster than any lie Vey could build would travel.

She reached for the comm channel, then stopped, her finger hovering. Warning the navigator meant betraying the institution. Staying silent meant feeding a witness to the people who wanted her silenced — or worse, watching the Remnants take her, crack her open, and learn everything, lie and truth alike, with no one left to control the story.

The window was closing. Not the jump window. The other one — the narrow, human window in which events could still be shaped by intention rather than momentum.

Vey keyed the channel.
