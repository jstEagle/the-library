## 21. Forty-One Names Read Aloud

The hall had been a grain exchange once, back when Brink Station still pretended to be something other than a crossroads for armed men. Now its vaulted ceiling held two fleets' worth of grief instead of futures in wheat. Sefa Kell stood at the edge of the dais and counted the exits the way she had counted them every day since her revival — four, plus the maintenance crawl beneath the eastern gallery — and then made herself stop counting.

*You are not on the Meridian,* she told herself. *You are not burning. You are here, and it is cold, and the cold is only air.*

Weeks had passed since the window closed. Weeks of depositions, of analysts picking through forty-two cores of evidence like crows over a battlefield, of officers who had spent their careers preparing to kill each other suddenly forced to sit in the same rooms and read the same command logs. The jamming order was there in Casper's own backup, timestamped and signed — *Comms blackout, Meridian approach, by authority of Fleet Command, Concord Third Squadron* — and there was no un-reading it. There was no un-hearing it either, when Sefa had stood before the Remnant council and testified that vengeance against one navigator would not resurrect anyone.

She had said it because it was true. She had said it while wanting, with her whole reconstituted body, to be wrong.

Now the hall filled around her: Concord uniforms in slate gray, Remnant crews in their patched civilian blacks, stationers in whatever they owned, all of them seated together in rows that no one had assigned and no one dared rearrange. Somewhere near the back a child coughed, and Sefa's chest seized so hard she had to grip the rail. Children's voices. She remembered children's voices, still aboard when the rescue ship jumped. She remembered which ones.

The lights dimmed. A single beam found the podium, and beside it, a second figure.

Casper Amberdale looked smaller than Sefa remembered from the parley. The navigator had always seemed built out of tension, coiled wire and held breath, a woman perpetually braced against an impact twenty years overdue. Tonight the impact had landed and she was still standing, which was its own kind of astonishing. Her hair was grayer than the station records claimed. Her hands, folded at the podium, were steady — or steady enough that only someone watching for tremors would see them.

Sefa watched for tremors. She had earned that.

A station official read a short statement about truth and record and the restoration of history, words polished smooth by committee, and then he stepped aside, and the hall went quiet in the particular way large crowds go quiet — not silent, but holding itself, a thousand breaths synchronized into one long inhale.

Casper leaned to the microphone.

"The Meridian colony fleet carried eleven thousand four hundred and six people," she said. Her voice carried without amplification needing much help; it was a voice used to being heard over engine noise, over static, over the sound of her own conscience. "When the Third Squadron opened fire, most of those names died twice. Once in the flesh. Once in the record, where they were filed as casualties of an unnamed pirate action, and then as a navigational error, and then as nothing at all."

She lifted a thin data slate. Sefa knew what was on it. She had helped build it — forty-one names recovered from the cores Casper Amberdale had stolen out of a war-crimes lockup and hidden in a freighter's hold for two decades, names cross-checked against evacuation rosters, against backup manifests, against the memories Sefa herself had contributed. Forty-one people whose consciousnesses survived the massacre in stasis, whom Casper could not save and could not abandon and could not explain.

"Forty-one of them did not die," Casper continued. "They waited. In my hold. In the dark. For twenty years, while I hauled ore and told myself I was keeping them safe." A pause. The tremor showed now, in her voice if not her hands. "I am not asking forgiveness. I am asking the record to hold them properly. Their names follow. They will be entered into both fleets' official archives, jointly, permanently, and no future government will be permitted to file them under anything but their own names."

She began to read.

"Adeyemi, Folake. Pilot, second class. Meridian transport *Lantern's End*."

The name fell into the hall like a stone into deep water, and the ripples went outward through the crowd. Sefa saw a Concord captain close his eyes. She saw a Remnant engineer mouth the name back to herself, learning it.

"Calloway, Iris. Hydroponics director. Fleet ship *Second Morning*."

Sefa had known Iris Calloway. Not well — Calloway had been old-guard fleet, one of the founding generation, the kind of woman who measured soil acidity by taste and trusted no instrument over her tongue. Sefa had been nine years old when the fleet launched. Calloway had given her a tomato grown in recycled water and told her it was the most expensive fruit in human history, and Sefa had believed her, and it had been delicious, and now the name was being read aloud by the woman who had fled past the burning fleet without answering hails.

It should have been unbearable. It was unbearable. And yet Sefa found, standing in the cold air of a converted grain exchange, that the unbearable thing was also the first thing in her second life that felt *finished*. Grief with a shape. Mourning with an address.

"Daramola, Teju. Age six. Civilian."

The child's name landed hardest. Six years old. Backed up during the evacuation scramble, core recovered from debris field sector seven by a navigator who was supposed to jettison everything and didn't. Sefa heard someone weeping openly in the third row, a man in Remnant black, and no one asked him to stop, and somewhere in the Concord section a hand came to rest on his shoulder — a stranger's hand, an enemy's hand, a hand.

Casper read on. She read for forty minutes without stopping, and Sefa stood beside her the entire time, because this had been the agreement: Casper would speak the names, and Sefa would stand witness, so that no one in either fleet could say the dead were mourned by only one side. When Casper's voice frayed, Sefa took the slate and read the next ten herself, and when her own voice cracked on a name — *Okonkwo-Bell, Amara, age eleven* — Casper took it back without comment, the way crew hand off watches, seamless, wordless, each covering the other's exhaustion.

That was the thing Sefa had not expected to find in Casper Amberdale: not absolution, not even remorse exactly, but *watch-standing*. Twenty years of it. A woman who had kept a lonely, illegal vigil over forty-one sleepers the way a helmsman keeps a lonely watch over a dark sea, and who had come tonight to hand that watch over to everyone.

When the last name was read — *"Voss, Emeric. Fleet archivist. Keeper of the Meridian language."* — Casper set down the slate and stepped back from the podium, and Sefa moved forward into the light alone.

"I was revived eight weeks ago," she said. "From a stolen core, by people who wanted a weapon. What they got instead was a witness, and what the witness wants you to understand is this: the Meridian fleet was not a tragedy. Tragedies are weather. The Meridian fleet was a decision, made by people with names and ranks, and it was covered up by more decisions made by more people with names and ranks, and every one of those decisions is now in the public record, and none of us will be permitted to look away from it again."

She let that sit. Then, softer:

"But neither is this true: that we are only what was done to us. Eleven thousand people built a civilization between the stars. They wrote songs in a language that existed nowhere else. They grew tomatoes in recycled water and told children they were priceless, and the children believed them, and the children were right." Her throat tightened. "Iris Calloway's tomatoes are gone. Her language survives in one archive core and one revived witness and one navigator who learned enough of it to broadcast a parley across a war line. That is what remains. It is small. It is also, I have decided, worth defending."

She looked at Casper. Casper looked back, and for a moment the hall fell away — the fleets, the cameras, the officials — and it was just the two of them standing in the wreckage of a shared night twenty years old, the only two people alive who remembered Meridian from inside it, one from the bridge of the fleeing ship and one from the burning deck of the *Second Morning*, bound together by the oldest and strangest kinship there is: the kinship of survivors who cannot explain themselves to anyone else.

Then the moment passed, as moments do, and the hall rose — not cheering, nothing so simple — but rising, a thousand people standing in silence for eleven thousand dead, and Sefa thought: *this is what peace sounds like before it learns to speak.*

* * *

Afterward, in the corridor outside the hall, the crowds thinned and the light turned amber and honest. Tam Okafor was waiting by the viewport with two cups of station tea that smelled like it had been brewed in an engine housing, which, knowing Brink Station, it probably had.

He handed one to Sefa. "For the shaking," he said. "It helps. Or it gives the shaking something else to do."

She drank. It was terrible. She drank more of it.

"You read Amara's name," Tam said. "You knew her?"

"She was in my class. Meridian school, cluster three." Sefa watched the traffic beyond the glass — ore haulers, tugs, a Concord patrol running lights-off, everyone behaving, everyone watching everyone behave. "Eleven years old. She wanted to be a comet hunter. She'd mapped seventeen fake comets and named them after her brothers."

Tam nodded slowly. He was a big man with careful hands, an engineer to the bone, the kind of person who fixed things before he judged them. Sefa had decided, somewhere in the last weeks, that she liked him very much.

"You know what I keep thinking about?" he said. "Core zero. Her backup. She recorded it the night of the massacre — walked into a memory booth with her ship burning behind her and her command jamming her comms, and she saved herself. Not to live in. To testify." He shook his head. "Twenty years carrying evidence against your own navy in your own head. I've hauled toxic cargo with less containment protocol than that."

"And now the containment is open," Sefa said.

"Now the containment is open." He smiled, tired. "Hold's empty for the first time since I signed aboard. She keeps going down there and just standing in it. Like she doesn't trust the quiet."

"Does she?"

"No," Tam said. "But she's learning to."

Through the viewport, moored two berths down, the *Orsen* sat floodlit for the ceremony — an ugly, honest freighter with a scarred hull and a hold that had carried the dead home. Sefa looked at it a long time.

"Take me aboard sometime," she said. "I want to see where they slept."

"They'd have liked that," Tam said. "Forty-one new crew. Worst passengers in history, never once complained about the food."

Despite everything — despite the burned fleet and the jammed comms and the six-year-old named Teju Daramola — Sefa laughed. It came out cracked and surprised, half sob, and Tam pretended not to notice, which was its own kindness.

* * *

The reception afterward was held in the station's botanical deck, because the organizers had decided that after a day of death the living should stand among growing things. Sefa drifted along the perimeter with her terrible tea, declining conversations gently, until she found Casper at the far end, alone beneath a trellis of engineered wisteria, looking at nothing.

"You did well," Sefa said.

"I read names off a slate."

"You gave them back their names. There's a difference, and it's the size of the corridor we nearly burned." Sefa stopped beside her. Up close, Casper looked wrung out, translucent with exhaustion, and also — for the first time since Sefa had known her — unburdened. Lighter by exactly the weight of forty-one souls. "What happens to you now?"

"Joint escort contract. Both fleets signed it yesterday. Someone has to run supplies through the corridor, and apparently no one trusts anyone else's ships, so they're hiring mine." Casper's mouth twitched. "An orphan freighter guarded by two navies. My engineer says it's the safest job in the system. He also says it's the strangest, and he's right twice."

"Where does it go first?"

"Relief run to the outer settlements. Then back. Then out again." Casper turned, and her eyes were clear in a way Sefa suspected cost her something. "There's room aboard. If you wanted it. Not as cargo. As crew. We're short a linguist, and I'm told the Meridian language has exactly one living speaker, and I'd—" her voice caught, steadied— "I'd like to hear it spoken on my ship. Properly. Not just in distress calls."

Sefa looked at her for a long moment. The woman who had fled. The woman who had stayed. The woman who had carried the dead for twenty years and then handed them to the light in front of two armies. There was no version of forgiveness that arrived clean; Sefa knew that now. But there was this — a berth, a chair, a place at a table where the only other person who remembered could sit across from her and remember too.

"I'll want my own cabin," Sefa said. "And I'll want to rename one of the fake comets after Amara Okonkwo-Bell, officially, in the fleet registry."

Casper blinked. "You can do that?"

"I'm the fleet's sole Meridian authority. I can do anything I want." 

Something loosened in Casper's face — not quite a smile, but the ground a smile gets built on. "Deal."

Across the deck, Tam raised his cup to them, and beyond the glass the *Orsen* rode at her moorings with an empty hold and a full future, and Sefa Kell — witness, archivist, survivor, about to become crew — finished her terrible tea and thought that mourning, given enough time and enough company, eventually learned a new word.

The word was *us*.
