## 9. Broadcast

The vault's transmitter had not spoken in eleven years.

Coral knew this the way she knew the weight of every door handle in the place — by touch, by habit, by the small ceremonies of maintenance she had performed alone through a decade of silence. She had oiled the transmitter's housing every solstice. She had replaced its coolant lines twice, though no order had ever come down from any authority asking her to. She had done it the way she read letters to the dormant stacks: because a thing tended is a thing ready, and some stubborn animal part of her had always believed that someday the vault would need to speak.

She had just never imagined it would be her voice it borrowed, or that the words would be a weapon.

The core sat on the transmission deck now, cradled in a field harness she'd improvised from seed-cart magnets and prayer. It hummed — not with sound but with absence, a hush in the air that made her teeth ache and the children in the corner go quiet in a way that was worse than crying. The Meridian's crew — her crew, Ede's crew, eleven adults folded down into small frightened shapes — huddled beneath the old catalog displays. Captain Vess, eight years old and grave as a judge, held the hand of a girl who had once been her navigator. The others pressed together like seed pods before the split.

And Ede.

Ede stood apart, the way he always did now, near the window of frosted glass that looked down into the archive levels. He was drawing again — crayon stubs on the back of a supply manifest, the antenna array, always the antenna array, its lines grown frantic and overworked until the paper nearly tore. He was seven years old today. Yesterday he had been nine. She could measure his forgetting now in the shrinking of his handwriting, in the way he'd stopped calling her *Coral* and started calling her *the seed lady*, and this morning, worst of all, had looked at her with the polite blankness of a child meeting a stranger.

"Is it going to hurt?" he asked, without looking up. He meant the transmitter. He had heard her and the core talking to each other in whatever language the deep dark had taught them both.

"No," Coral said. It was the second lie she had ever told him. The first had been to a patrol boat, and it had saved his life. She was beginning to understand that lying was just love wearing work clothes.

* * *

The fleets arrived the way weather arrives — first the pressure, then the light, then the ruin.

She saw them on the old survey scope: two formations hanging above the terminator like iron fruit, the Concord's white-lattice hulls and the Meridian Compact's black-sheathed wedges, close enough now that their weapons lit the upper atmosphere in slow silent flickers. Between them, the vault. Between them, her.

The hail came on both channels at once, and she let them speak over each other for a full minute before she answered either. The Concord's commodore — a woman named Ilsa Reyen whose voice Coral knew from a decade of supply manifests and requisition orders, a voice that had once thanked her, warmly, by name, for her service — spoke of sanctuary, of protection, of the sacred trust of the vault. The Compact's admiral spoke of justice, of the truth finally coming home, of how the recording belonged to the victims. Both demanded the core. Both used the word *surrender*.

Coral sat in the transmitter chair with her hands flat on the console and listened to the war ask her, politely, twice, to give it the thing that would let it win.

"Commander Thornbury." Reyen again, alone on the channel now; the Compact had gone to encrypted silence. "You are a custodian of the oldest living archive in human space. You know what that trust means. Hand over the device and the vault stands. Refuse, and I cannot answer for what my gunners do when they're frightened. You have children aboard. Think about the children."

"I am thinking about the children," Coral said. "That's the whole of what I've been doing."

She thought of the vault stacks below — ten thousand years of wheat and rice and sorghum, the patient green machinery of every harvest that had ever fed a human mouth. She thought of the strike team she'd found down there three days ago, Concord-uniformed, cataloguing blight strains with the tender efficiency of people building a plague. She thought of how Reyen's voice had gone flat when Coral had asked her about it, and how *sacred trust* sounded when the mouth saying it was full of knives.

"Commander. Last time."

"No," Coral said, and it was the truest word she'd spoken in ten years.

* * *

The transmitter's uplink had been built for one purpose: to send germination protocols, genetic manifests, and planting calendars to colony agronomy stations. It had never been built to reach a war. But the Static War had not destroyed the old network — it had only frightened it into silence, and silence, Coral had learned across a decade of empty routes, is just a door that no one has knocked on.

She routed the core through the archive's own handshake protocols. Every colony station in the network had once checked in with the vault on a schedule; the schedules still lived in their systems like sleeping hearts, waiting for a beat. She gave them a beat.

The core fed her the recording the way a wound feeds a fever. She had lived inside it once already, during the activation in the sealed dark — had stood on the observation deck of a Concord ship eleven years gone and watched the first Static lance fire from a Concord hull at an unarmed Compact relay, had heard the orders, the calm bureaucratic cadence of them, the word *deniable* used twice. She knew every second of it now. She let it play anyway, unedited, uncut, her own hands steady on the controls while her chest came apart quietly behind her ribs.

"This is the Earth Seed Vault," she said, when the recording finished, and found her voice did not shake, which surprised her, because everything else in her was shaking. "This is the origin of the Static War. It was not fired by the Compact. It was not fired in self-defense. It was fired by the Concord, and both sides have known, and both sides have spent eleven years feeding you the lie. I am Coral Thornbury, custodian of this vault, and I am done carrying secrets for people who plant wars and call the harvest defense. The truth belongs to no flag. Take it. All of you. Take all of it."

She sent it to every address in the old network. Every colony. Every station. Every ship registry she could reach. Then, because the war had taught everyone to distrust a single source, she sent it again, and again, on a loop that would outlive her.

* * *

The first shot came ninety seconds later.

It did not come from the Compact. That was the thing she would remember afterward, in whatever life she had left: the first shell that struck the vault's outer shield came from a Concord gunboat, and it came before Reyen had finished her final warning, and it came while the recording was still playing on the open channel — as if the truth, heard plainly, could not be tolerated even for the length of its own telling.

The shield held. The shield had been built to hold against meteor storms and the long slow anger of space. It would not hold against two fleets.

Coral felt the deck shudder and did not flinch. Below her, the children screamed, and then — this was the part that undid her — they stopped screaming, and Captain Vess, eight years old, began moving them into the deep archive levels with crisp hand signals, her small voice steady, her small body remembering a decade of drills her shrinking mind had already lost. Children leading children to shelter. The war had made even this.

On the survey scope, the sky above the vault changed. The Compact fleet — which had come to *rescue the truth* and found the truth refusing to belong to anyone — opened fire on the Concord line. Not in defense of the vault. In fury at being made a fool in front of every colony in human space, at the recording playing on their own bridges, their own mess halls, their own crew decks, sailors hearing for the first time the name of the ship that had fired first.

The fleets turned on each other above her head, and the vault's shield became the floor of a battle it was never built to be.

* * *

And then the colonies answered.

It began as static on the open channel — Coral almost missed it, the way you almost miss a seed splitting in dark soil. Then a voice: Halcyon Station, refusing Concord docking rights. Then Meridian's Reach, the Compact's own jewel, broadcasting the recording on its public bands and declaring its ports closed to both fleets. Then another, and another, the old network waking station by station like a field coming up green after rain, voices she had ferried seeds to for ten years, voices that had gone silent in the war's first winter, all of them saying the same word in a hundred accents: *no*.

No to the Concord. No to the Compact. No to the war, to the lie, to the appetite that had worn both flags as clothing.

Coral stood in the transmitter room with the deck bucking under her and listened to ten years of silence end, and she wept without noise, the way she had learned to, and she did not stop working.

* * *

The shield failed at dawn — vault dawn, the soft programmed brightening that had woken her for a decade, absurd and gentle against the fire falling from orbit. Consoles died in ranks around her. The lights went amber, then red, then the emergency strips only.

She should have run. The shelter levels would hold; Vess had the children there; there was a maintenance tunnel she'd mapped years ago out of an old habit of distrust. She had time. She had, perhaps, just enough time.

She stayed, because someone had to keep the broadcast alive, and because the transmitter was the last thing in the vault still speaking, and because she was, whatever else she had become, the custodian.

The door opened behind her and she turned with a fire extinguisher raised like a sword, and it was Ede.

He had come up from the shelter alone, seven years old in socks, crayon still staining the edge of his palm, and he looked at her with an expression she could not read — and then she could, because she had seen it before, years ago, on a grown man's face in a docking bay on Meridian's Reach, the last time she'd seen him before the Meridian went dark. It was the look of someone who has remembered something important in the middle of the night and crossed a cold room to say it.

"You were going to leave without saying goodbye," he said. "You always do that."

"I always—" Her voice failed. "*Always?*"

He didn't answer. Maybe he didn't know. Maybe some floor of him, deeper than the forgetting, knew only the shape of her and not the story. He crossed the shaking room, took her hand — her hand, with the burn scar and the seed-calluses, the hand that had fed colonies — and held it the way children hold things they have decided to keep.

Above them, the sky burned. Around them, the network rose. On the channel, the recording played again, and again, and would play until the vault's last power cell went dark, which would not be today, because Coral Thornbury had tended this place for ten years and she had oiled everything.

The vault shuddered. Dust fell from the ceiling in slow gray curtains, settling on the console, on their joined hands, like the first snow over a planted field.

"Tell me a story," Ede said, "while it's loud."

So she did. She told him about a woman who had stayed behind to keep the seeds safe, and about a ship that came home carrying children, and about how the woman had learned that the only things worth keeping are the things you give away. She did not know if any of it would survive in him. She did not know how much of her would survive in herself — the core was still humming, still drinking, and she could feel, at the edges of her mind, her own memories going soft as overripe fruit.

But his hand was in hers. The truth was in the sky. And somewhere below them, in the dark of the deep archive, ten thousand years of green things waited, patient as morning, for the war to end and the planting to begin.

The vault doors, both sets, both fleets', began to open at once.
