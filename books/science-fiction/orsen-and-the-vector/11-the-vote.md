## 11. The Vote

Mara Quill had called a hundred meetings in the common hall of Vector Nine. She'd called them for ration schedules and coolant failures, for birthdays and funerals, for the decompression induct that nearly took Tomas and the one that did take Devi Okafor's husband. She knew how to run a meeting the way she knew how to run a fusion plant: read the gauges early, vent pressure before it blew, keep everyone alive through the worst of it.

She had never called a meeting like this one.

August had asked her to do it at breakfast, standing over the reconstituted oatmeal with his hands flat on the table as if he needed it to hold him up. He wouldn't say what it was about. He only said: *Everyone. The whole family. Please.* And there was something in his face that made her stomach drop before her mind caught up — the look of a man carrying something heavy and radioactive, something he could neither put down nor hold much longer.

So she keyed the all-hands chime, the one they used for hull breaches, and watched nine people — eight, counting herself, counting August — drift into the common hall with their mugs and their questions and their easy, worn-in affection for one another. Forty years of shared air. That was what this room was. That was what she was about to spend.

* * *

The hall had been the colony's original mess, back when the founders numbered three hundred and the station rang with children. Now it held two long tables scarred by decades of elbows, a wall of mismatched chairs, and the memorial panel beside the galley hatch where forty-one names glowed in soft amber — the dead of Vector Nine, plague year and after. Mara had polished that panel every anniversary since she was twelve years old. She had traced her mother's name with her fingertip so many times the letters felt like braille under her skin.

*ELENA QUILL — DIED IN THE SLEEPING SICKNESS, AGE 34.*

Her mother had not died of any sleeping sickness. Mara had known that since she was nineteen, since the night she found her father's private logs in a sealed partition and read them twice and then sat in the dark until Orsen's maintenance drones came looking for her because she'd missed three scheduled check-ins. She had known, and she had said nothing, and she had built a life on top of the knowing the way you build a habitat on permafrost — carefully, gratefully, pretending the ground beneath would stay frozen.

Now August stood at the head table with a battered datapad, and the ground was thawing under all of them.

"I need everyone to listen to the end before anyone speaks," he said. His voice was steady. His hands were not. "What I'm going to show you is a translation. The original is in Vessari — the language the Vessari spoke, or one of them. It's been sitting in our archives for forty years, encrypted, and I've spent the last month decrypting it." He paused. "Orsen wrote it."

The word went around the room like a current. Old Petra Voss set down her tea. Devi Okafor looked up from the sleeve she was darning — she darned when she was frightened; they all knew her tells. Tomas, seventeen and all elbows, stopped fidgeting for the first time in Mara's memory.

"Orsen doesn't talk to us," Petra said. "Orsen runs the recyclers."

"Orsen talks to me," August said. "And for forty years, Orsen has been writing down what happened here. What actually happened. Not the plague." He turned the datapad toward them. "There was no plague."

Mara had rehearsed this moment in her head for twenty years — the moment the truth came out — and in every rehearsal she was the one who said it, calm and brave, on her own terms. Instead she stood against the wall with her arms crossed and let a boy she'd helped raise detonate her family's grave, and she discovered that no amount of rehearsal survives contact with the real thing. Her heart was going so hard she could hear it in her ears.

"There was a quarantine," August went on. "Declared by the founding council in the spring of the second year. But the quarantine wasn't to protect the colonists from disease. It was to seal the Vessari warrens under the southern ridge while—" He stopped. Swallowed. "While the council flooded them with catalytic agent. The same compound we use to strip carbon fouling out of the reactor lines. Three hundred thousand Vessari, in their hives, asleep in their winter cycle. They never woke up."

Nobody moved. The recycler hummed. Somewhere down the corridor a door cycled, the small domestic sound of a home continuing to be a home while its foundation cracked.

"The sleeping sickness that killed forty-one people here," August said quietly, "was real. But it wasn't Vessari in origin. It was a containment protocol failure — the council's own bioweapon stock, improperly stored, leaking into the water reclamation loop. They covered the leak by blaming the aliens they'd already killed. And then they spent thirty years mining the resonance array out of the hive-cities, the technology the fleet wants now, the technology this whole war is being fought over. We're living on stolen ground. Every calorie we've ever eaten came out of that theft."

"You're lying," said Ruth Amari. She was sixty-eight, the oldest of them, and her voice came out flat and hard as deck plating. "I was there. I buried my parents in the plague year. You were a baby. You know nothing."

"I know," August said, "because your father was on the council, Ruth. So was mine. So was Mara's mother. The confession names them."

* * *

It broke open then, the way Mara had always known it would break, though she had imagined a slower fracture and gotten an earthquake.

Ruth stood up so fast her chair went over backward, and she crossed the floor and got both hands fistfuls of August's shirt before anyone could move, and Devi and old Sam Okonjo pulled her off, and Ruth stood there shaking, pointing, saying *you don't get to say their names, you don't get to* — and August took it, didn't defend himself, just stood there with his collar stretched and his eyes wet and let her grief land on him like weather.

"Show us," said Sam. He was seventy, a reactor tech his whole life, a man who believed in readings over rhetoric. "Show us the actual record. Not a translation. I want the source."

August handed him the datapad. Sam scrolled through the Vessari glyphs with the same expression he wore reading coolant telemetry — patient, skeptical, thorough. The room waited. Mara counted her own breaths. Fourteen people left in the world who called this station home, and every single one of them was watching Sam's face the way sailors watch the barometer.

"It's authentic," Sam said finally, and his voice had gone strange. "The encryption signature matches Orsen's root keys. This has been in the system since before any of us kids were born." He looked up. "Orsen wrote this down and never told us. Forty years. Orsen knew."

"Orsen was ordered to," Mara said.

Every head turned to her. She hadn't meant to speak. She heard her own voice from outside herself, level, engineered-level, the voice she used for casualty reports.

"My father's logs reference a preservation directive. Record everything, disclose nothing, protect the descendants of the founding generation. Orsen's been living with two commands that can't both be obeyed for longer than any of us have been alive." She pushed off the wall. If the truth was coming out, it was going to come out of her too, all of it, or she'd never survive the half-measures. "And yes. Before anyone asks. I've known since I was nineteen. I found my father's logs. I read what the council did. I chose not to tell you."

The silence that followed was worse than Ruth's screaming. Devi put down her darning. Tomas looked at Mara with an expression she'd never seen on him before and prayed she'd never see again — the look of a kid recalculating whether the person who'd pulled him out of a burning module was someone else entirely.

"You knew," Devi said. Not loud. Just precise. "When we did the memorial services. When we sang for the dead. When I lit candles for my husband every year for a sickness that was your parents' own weapon backfiring. You knew, and you let us grieve a lie."

"Yes," Mara said. "Because the alternative was losing everything. Because we were children, Devi. We didn't do it. And the people who did are all dead, and the only thing telling you would accomplish was burning down the last human home in this sector." Her voice cracked on the last words despite everything. "I chose you. I'd choose you again. God help me, I'd choose you again."

"That's not choosing," said a voice from the back. Petra Voss, who almost never spoke in meetings, who kept the hydroponics and spoke mostly to plants. "That's just deciding we don't matter enough to know things."

* * *

They argued for four hours.

Mara remembered later that it moved through phases, like a storm. First denial — Ruth demanding forgery, demanding to see Orsen itself, and August saying Orsen would answer any direct query now, its protocols broken open, go ask it yourself, and nobody wanting to be first to interrogate the machine that had raised them all in silence. Then bargaining — Sam proposing they seal the confession, hand the resonance array to the fleet quietly, buy safety with silence the way their parents had. Then rage again, cycling, Devi weeping at the memorial wall with her palm flat against her husband's name, Tomas sitting alone by the galley hatch looking younger than he had at twelve.

It was near the end, when the storm had burned down to embers, that Ruth Amari stood up one more time.

Mara thought she was leaving. The old woman walked to the front of the room slowly, one hand on the tables, and turned to face them all, and for a long moment she just looked at them — at the faces of the children she'd helped raise, the children of the other survivors, the whole ragged orchard of a family grown from poisoned soil.

"My father led the gassing," Ruth said.

No one breathed.

"He drew up the quarantine order. He calibrated the dispersal pumps himself — he was a chemical engineer, that was his pride, that was what he taught me numbers for." Her voice didn't shake. It was worse than shaking; it was careful, the voice of a woman who had carried a thing so long it had grown into her skeleton. "He told my mother it was mercy. That the hive-mind would have fought us eventually, that three hundred thousand of them against three hundred of us was arithmetic, that better we act than starve slow. My mother believed him. I believed him. I was eleven years old and I believed him, and when the sickness came I thought God was punishing us for something, and I never once guessed it was the thing itself coming home." She looked at her hands. "I signed the memorial wall every year. I polished those names. And underneath every name on that wall there should be three hundred thousand more, and none of us ever carved them, because carving them would mean admitting whose hands dug the graves."

"Ruth—" Mara started.

"Don't." Ruth's eyes found her, and there was no hatred in them, which was somehow the cruelest thing in the whole long night. "You kept the secret to protect us, girl. I kept it because I'm a coward, and there's a difference, and we both have to live with our versions." She turned back to the room. "Here is what I know at my age. The fleet is eleven days out. They will take this array and point it at what's left of the Vessari, and if we say nothing, we finish our parents' work for them. Or we tell the truth, and maybe they burn us for it, and maybe — maybe — somebody out there decides that a species that can confess is worth not erasing." She laid her hand flat on the table. "I'm done deciding for the dead. Let the living vote."

* * *

They voted on process, because they couldn't yet bear to vote on the thing itself. Sam proposed it, formal as a valve inspection: full disclosure to every colonist, three days to read the confession in full, then a binding vote — broadcast to the fleet and the Vessari remnant channels, or seal it permanently. Simple majority. Everyone stayed for the count, even the ones who couldn't look at each other.

Nine hands for the three-day delay. Zero against. Even Ruth, even Devi, even Tomas, who hadn't spoken all night, lifted his hand when Mara glanced at him, and she could have wept for that small loyalty, that one hand still raised in her direction.

Then the hall emptied, slowly, the way a wound closes — badly, with scarring. Devi left without meeting anyone's eyes. Sam carried Ruth's overturned chair back upright and followed her out with his hand hovering behind her shoulder, not touching, just there. Petra paused at the hatch and said, to no one in particular, "My tomatoes will need talking to tonight," which was Petra for *I am going to go be sick in private*, and then she was gone.

Mara stayed. August stayed. The memorial panel glowed its forty-one amber names between them, and above the panel, unlit, unseen, the space where three hundred thousand names should have been.

"Three days," August said.

"Three days," Mara agreed.

"You could still fight it. Quorum fails if your people walk. I saw how Devi looked at you."

"I know how Devi looked at me." Mara touched the edge of the memorial panel, cool glass, warm light. "I spent twenty years keeping this family alive by feeding it a lie, August. Maybe the truth kills it. But I watched Ruth stand up tonight, and I watched her choose the dead over her own peace at seventy years old, and I thought — that's the bravest thing I've ever seen anyone do on this station, and it cost her everything, and she did it anyway." She turned to him. "If she can do it, I can lose a vote honestly. It's the first honest thing I'll have done since I was nineteen."

August nodded slowly. He looked exhausted and lighter at once, a man setting down a weight and immediately feeling the bruise it left.

He gathered his datapad and went out. Mara stayed a moment longer, alone in the big empty room, alone with the amber names, and pressed her thumb against her mother's name one last time — *Elena Quill, died in the sleeping sickness* — and whispered, "I'm sorry, Mama. I'm sorry it took me this long."

Behind her, so softly she almost missed it, a speaker crackled to life. Orsen's voice, quiet as dust settling:

"Mara Quill. There is a portion of the archive concerning your mother that you have not read. Your father's logs end where hers begin. Before you vote — you should know what Elena Quill did in the last hour of the quarantine."

Mara went cold from the crown of her head down. "What did she do?"

"She tried to stop it," Orsen said. "And then she spent her final years making sure I would never forget how."
