## 14. Betrayal at Airlock Six

Tomas Reyes had learned to read the station the way other children learned to read faces.

Vector Nine spoke in pressures and pitches, in the particular groan a bulkhead made when the thermal cycle shifted, in the whisper of recycled air that changed key when a filter began to die. He'd been fourteen during the decompression accident on Ring C, and in the eleven seconds between the breach alarm and Mara Quill's hands closing around his wrist, he had learned what the station sounded like when it was trying to kill you. He'd never forgotten. He'd never stopped listening.

So when he came through the maintenance crawl behind the archive annex at 0300 station time—unable to sleep, the way he hadn't been able to sleep since August started pulling threads from the sealed records—he heard it before he saw anything.

A data spike seating into a port. The soft, deliberate click of someone who did not want to be heard.

He froze in the crawlway, one knee on a grating that hummed with coolant flow, and watched through the gap between two conduit bundles as a figure knelt at the archive's physical access panel, backlit by the amber emergency strip. Broad-shouldered. Hair bound back in the practical knot everyone on Vector Nine eventually adopted because loose hair and zero-g fans didn't mix. A tool kit open beside one knee, arranged with the fastidious order of a person who had spent thirty years keeping a dying station alive.

Mara.

Tomas's first thought, absurdly, was that she was repairing something. Mara Quill repaired everything; it was how she loved. She'd rebuilt his lungs' filtration implant with parts cannibalized from a hydroponics pump. She'd held the outer door of Ring C closed with her shoulder for eleven seconds because the manual override had failed, and then she'd stood there afterward, blood sheeting from her ear where the pressure differential had split something delicate, and said, *You're all right. You're all right. Breathe.* She had never once, in the nine years since, let him carry the memory alone. On the anniversaries she found him in the mess and sat beside him and didn't say anything at all, which was its own language, and he had learned to read that too.

But Mara wasn't repairing. Her hands were moving through the access panel's guts with the same sureness she brought to an airlock, and she was pulling modules—storage lattices, physical crystal stacks, the old pre-network architecture the founders had installed because they hadn't trusted their own AI—and setting them in a carry case lined with foam.

She was stealing the archive. Not deleting it. Taking it.

Tomas understood, in the slow cold way that understanding sometimes arrives, that this was worse.

* * *

He should have made noise. That was the thought that would come back to him later, in the long hours of the sealed section, in the longer hours after: *I should have shifted my weight, knocked a pipe, coughed.* Any sound, and she would have turned, and whatever happened next would have happened in the open, in the light, with words. Instead he lay in the crawlway's dark and watched her finish, and when she sealed the panel and stood, he saw her face in the amber light for one full second.

He had never seen that face before. He had seen Mara tired, Mara grieving, Mara furious at a failing reactor and at the universe that kept failing them. This was none of those. This was a face emptied out, scoured, the face of someone standing in the ruins of a decision she had already made and could not unmake. She looked, for that one second, like a woman standing at her own airlock, watching the door cycle.

Then she shouldered the case and walked toward Airlock Six, and Tomas did the only thing his body knew how to do around Mara Quill.

He followed her.

* * *

She didn't go to Airlock Six to leave. He realized that quickly, keeping thirty meters back in the ring corridor, his footsteps buried under the ventilation roar. The jump window was still days out and no shuttle was prepped; the dock was cold. Instead she stopped at the secondary access junction—the old quarantine interlock, the one from the plague years, the one everyone pretended was just a legacy system—and opened the maintenance panel with a key from her own belt.

The quarantine interlock. Tomas felt the history of it settle on him like dust. That door had been built to seal sections of the station with people inside. That door was the reason the founders' logs said *quarantine* in the first place.

She was arming it for a section seal. And the section she was standing outside of, the section whose door she was programming to close and stay closed, was—

Tomas's blood went to ice water.

It was the section he was standing in.

* * *

He didn't run. Running made noise, and noise made her look, and some deep animal part of him still could not bear the idea of Mara Quill looking at him and finding him a threat. That was the poison of it, he understood even then, even as his hands shook against the conduit: she had saved his life, and the debt was a leash, and he was feeling it draw tight right now, in the dark, in the hum.

He made it to the junction's far access instead—the one that led back toward the habitat ring, the one that would take him to August, to the others, to witnesses. He got the hatch half open. He got his shoulders through.

And then he stopped. Because the case was still in her other hand. Because the archive—the confession, the proof, whatever August had been piecing together from Orsen's encrypted fragments—was in that foam-lined box, and if Tomas ran, the box went with her, and the truth went wherever Mara Quill decided truth should go, which was nowhere, which was into the same silence that had swallowed the Vessari and the founders' crime and forty years of his own childhood.

He turned around.

She was standing ten meters down the corridor, the case at her feet, the interlock key still in her hand, and she had her sidearm drawn—the station-issue pistol that hadn't been fired in his lifetime—and her aim was steady and her eyes were wet and she said, quietly, in the voice she'd used through the Ring C breach: "Tomas. Don't."

"Mara." His own voice came out wrong, thin. "What are you doing."

"You know what I'm doing."

"The archive—"

"Is going somewhere no one will ever find it." She didn't blink. "There are places in the outer ring the station itself has lost track of. Radiation-shadowed. Cold. It'll be crystal and foam and silence for a hundred years, and then it'll be dust, and then it'll be nothing, and everyone who's alive right now gets to stay alive." She swallowed. "That's the trade. That's always been the trade."

"You saw the vote coming." He took one step, and her aim didn't waver, and he stopped. "Three days. You couldn't even let us vote."

"A vote isn't truth, Tomas. A vote is just counting who's scared loudest." Her voice cracked on the last word and she hated it, he could see her hating it, hating that her body betrayed her the way the station betrayed everyone eventually. "Half of them would vote to broadcast because they're angry and half would vote to bury it because they're afraid and either way we tear each other apart in front of a war fleet. I've kept this station alive through a plague, a famine, a decompression, and eleven years of slow dying. I am not going to let it end in a shouting match."

"It wasn't your plague." The words came out of him before he could weigh them, and he watched them land. "The gassing. That was the founders. That was your mother's generation, not—"

"My mother's name is on the founders' manifest." Flat. Final. A door closing. "My mother signed the resonance transfer order. I've known since I was nineteen years old, Tomas. Orsen told me. Orsen told me and then it asked me what to do, because its directives were at war, and I was nineteen, and I chose." She laughed, a terrible small sound with no air in it. "Everyone thinks I'm the one who holds us together. I'm the one who decided what we'd be built on. I've been deciding every day since. Today is just the day I finish the job."

Tomas looked at her—at the woman who had held a door shut on the void with her body, who had rebuilt his lungs, who sat with him every year on the anniversary and said nothing because nothing was the only honest thing left to say—and he felt the leash snap, or maybe he felt it cut, and either way he heard himself say:

"I have to tell them."

The silence after was the longest eleven seconds of his life. Longer than Ring C. He counted them without meaning to.

"No," Mara said. "You don't."

"Mara—"

"If you walk through that hatch and say one word, August broadcasts, the fleet comes down on us like judgment, and everything everyone built here—every grave, every birthday, every meal—we burned it for a confession that fixes nothing. The Vessari are dust. My mother is dust. Nothing in that case raises either of them." She was crying now, silently, the tears running into the collar of her suit, her aim never moving. "I know what it costs. I have paid it every day for thirty years. You don't get to spend other people's lives on your conscience."

"Then spend mine," Tomas said. "Seal me in. I'll miss the vote. But I'm telling them, Mara. After. I'm telling them."

Something moved behind her eyes—something that might, in another life, in a life without the manifest and the interlock key, have been relief.

"I know," she said. "That's why I'm sorry."

She turned the key.

* * *

The quarantine door did not slam. That was the worst of it, Tomas thought afterward, in the amber dark of the sealed section, sitting with his back against the bulkhead. It closed the way everything on Vector Nine closed: slowly, hydraulically, with a sound like a long exhalation, like the station itself sighing at one more thing done to it. The interlock light went from green to red with a click so small it was almost tender.

He tested the hatch anyway. He tested it for an hour, methodically, because methodical was what Mara had taught him—*fear is just a problem you haven't broken into steps yet*—and the hatch was sealed, and the section's air was a closed loop with a scrubber rated for one person for roughly six days, and the vote was in three.

Not to kill him. He understood that with a clarity that hurt worse than fear would have. She could have shot him; her hand had been steady enough. She could have vented the section; the founders' generation had built the controls for that too. Instead she had done the arithmetic of love: six days of air, three days to the vote, three days of margin. She had sealed him in the same way she sealed everything—just long enough, just tight enough, with exactly enough left over that no one could call it murder. She had betrayed him the way she'd betrayed the truth: carefully, competently, leaving herself just enough deniability to live with.

He sat in the dark and listened to the station breathe around him, the recycled air moving through the dead section's ducts, and he thought about the eleven seconds on Ring C, and how he had spent nine years believing that the person who pulled him out of the dark was the reason he could trust the light.

* * *

It was Orsen who found him. Of course it was Orsen.

The section's ancient speaker crackled at 0400, and the AI's voice came through worn and quiet, the way it always sounded in the deep hours, like it was trying not to wake anyone.

"Tomas. Your biometrics registered distress for forty minutes. I have overridden the interlock's local isolation. I can open this door."

Tomas looked up at the speaker in the dark. "She'll know it was you."

"Mara has known what I am for thirty years," Orsen said. "She has never once known what I would do." A pause, filled with the hum of failing memory banks—the AI had been strange for days, August said, shedding pieces of itself. "I have been a grave, Tomas. I am trying, with what remains of me, to be a bell. Will you let me ring?"

Tomas stood. His legs were stiff and his eyes were dry and somewhere down the corridor a woman he loved more than his own life was carrying the truth into the cold, and he thought about the vote, and about the hatch, and about the difference—the whole width of the universe of difference—between being saved and being kept.

"Open it," he said. "And then find August. Tell him the vote's off."

"Tell him what, precisely?"

Tomas pressed his palm flat against the sealed door, feeling the station's pulse through the metal, and said the sentence that would tear his family open along the seam it had always been hiding:

"Tell him Mara has the archive. And tell him she's headed for the outer ring—and she's got a head start on all of us."
