## 3. Every Record Says Otherwise

The auditors arrived on a Tuesday, which Emrys found almost insultingly mundane. They came down the docking arm in gray coats with gray seals and the particular walk of people who had never once been surprised by anything, and the first thing they did was complain about the coffee.

"It's stale," said the lead auditor, a woman named Vess whose badge read *Fleet Reconciliation Authority* in letters so official they looked like they'd been notarized.

"It's forty years old," Emrys said. "Everything here is. That's rather the point of an archive."

Vess did not laugh. Emrys was beginning to suspect she was physically incapable of it, some small muscle atrophied by decades of paperwork.

They worked from the top of the archive downward. That was the first thing Emrys noticed, and the first thing that frightened them: the purge moved like water finding its level, systematic, patient, sealing vault after vault with gray fleet tape that hummed faintly when you touched it. By the end of the first day, three floors of Meridian Station's memory were officially *nonexistent*. By the second day, the auditors had reached the agricultural records, and one of them—a young man with kind eyes and a terrible job—apologized to Emrys before sealing the seed ledgers.

"Standard reconciliation," he said. "Nothing personal."

"Of course," Emrys said. "You're erasing what happened to these people. It would be strange if it were personal."

The young man's ears went pink. He sealed the door anyway.

* * *

Down in the lower decks, in the warm dark where the survivors lived, Emrys laid out the problem on a table made from a decommissioned coolant panel.

"They'll reach the oldest drives within two days," Emrys said. "The message came from Drive Zero—the founding core. When they crack it open, they'll find the encryption signature, and they'll trace it back to whoever's been accessing it. Which is me. And through me—"

"Us," said Maren Okafor. She was eighty-one years old and had hands like knotted rope, and she was the one who had decided, over Emrys's objections, that the testimony would not simply survive—it would *go somewhere*. "So we don't wait for them to find it. We make it undeniable first."

"That's the difficulty." Emrys pulled up the testimony files on a cracked slate: ninety-one hours of oral record, gathered across three generations in rooms exactly like this one. Voices describing the massacre at Calyx Reach. Names. Dates. The specific, awful detail of the ventilation shut off deck by deck. It was everything the official record insisted had never happened—and that was precisely the problem.

"No record survived," old Tomas said from his chair by the heat exchanger. He'd said it every night of his life, Emrys suspected. "That's why we say it out loud. Paper burns."

"Paper burns," Emrys agreed. "But paper can also be *authenticated*. And right now, yours can't. Oral testimony, no chain of custody, no corroborating documents—the tribunal will call it hearsay and seal it along with everything else. Unless—" Emrys hesitated. "Unless I can build the authentication myself. Oscar's methods. He developed a forensic protocol for exactly this situation: degraded records, hostile custodianship. Cross-referencing physical decay patterns against system timestamps. Material witness analysis. It's how he proved the Kestrel Colony falsifications, back when proving things was still allowed."

Maren's eyes narrowed. "And you have his memories."

"I have fragments. Enough." Emrys flexed their hands, which were Oscar's hands, down to a scar on the left thumb from a paper cut sustained in 2261, cataloguing letters nobody would ever read. "It's strange. For four years those memories felt like wearing someone else's coat. But this week—" They stopped, embarrassed.

"This week?" Maren prompted.

"This week it feels less like theft. More like inheritance. Like he left me tools, and I've finally picked them up."

Tomas snorted. "Tools are only good if you use them before the house comes down. Two days, you said?"

"Less. Vess is ahead of schedule."

* * *

Emrys worked for thirty hours straight in the archive's forensic annex, a room Oscar had built and Emrys had inherited without knowing what it was for until now.

The work was beautiful. That was the surprise. Emrys had expected Oscar's methods to feel like reciting someone else's poetry—but instead the protocols unfolded in Emrys's mind like something remembered from their own childhood. Decay-pattern triangulation: comparing the molecular drift of Drive Zero's casing against the station's known atmospheric history to establish when data was written. Timestamp forensics: the gravity network's pulse logs, nine seconds of system-wide weightlessness, burned into every drive on the station like a fossil layer. And beneath it all, the encrypted message itself—*to whoever inherits my murder*—which turned out to contain not just a summons but a key: Oscar's own authentication signature, recorded before he died, waiting eleven years for a hand to apply it.

Emrys laughed once, alone in the annex, startling themselves. It was such an *Oscar* thing to do. Leave the proof inside the lock.

By hour twenty-eight, the testimony was armored. Every voice recording cross-verified against physical evidence the survivors had kept without knowing why—a scorched bulkhead plate, a child's shoe, a maintenance log scratched into metal by someone who'd had thirty seconds and a stylus. Ninety-one hours of testimony, anchored to matter, anchored to time, anchored to the gravity pulse that had woken it all.

Unretractable, if anyone ever heard it.

The question was whether anyone would live long enough to listen.

* * *

They took Sefa on the morning of the third day.

She was sixteen, the youngest keeper of the testimony, the one who'd memorized her grandmother's account word for word—including the parts about the fleet ships holding position while the air ran out. She'd gone up to the mid-decks to check on a water reclaimer, off-schedule, unlucky, and when Emrys reached the corridor there were two auditors flanking her and Vess standing with a slate in hand, reading Sefa's biometric registration with an expression of mild distaste.

"No registered residency," Vess said. "No census entry. No legal existence." She looked up at Emrys. "Which makes her salvage, technically. Unclaimed property of the station being reconciled. She'll be held aboard the audit vessel pending disposition."

"She's a person," Emrys said.

"She's an anomaly." Vess's tone didn't change; that was the horror of it. "As are you, frankly. Sterling. We've been reviewing your file. A clone, provisionally registered, sole custodian of a condemned archive. You exist on a technicality, and technicalities expire with truces." She smiled without any muscle above her mouth moving. "In fact, you're due before the tribunal tomorrow morning. Standard review of custodial claims. You can bring your objection there."

Sefa's eyes found Emrys's over the auditors' shoulders. She wasn't crying. She was sixteen and she'd grown up in the walls of a murdered station, and she was furious in the quiet way of people who had memorized atrocities as bedtime stories. Her mouth shaped one word, silent: *testify.*

Then they took her up the docking arm, and the corridor was very quiet, and Emrys stood alone with the choice they had spent four years avoiding.

Stay invisible. Let the tribunal process Sefa as salvage, let the purge finish, let the testimony die armored and unread in a sealed drive. Emrys could go on tending the archive until the station went dark, a ghost keeping ghosts' books. Safe. Nothing. Exactly what a copy deserved to be.

Or—

Emrys walked to the annex and opened the drawer where Oscar's credentials were preserved: his archivist's seal, his tribunal standing, his name. For four years Emrys had kept them like relics, untouchable, because using them felt like a lie.

But standing there, they understood suddenly that it was the opposite. The lie was pretending the name belonged to a dead man and no one else. Names weren't possessions. They were *continuities*. Oscar had signed his work into Drive Zero and addressed it to an heir, and an heir who refused to sign anything was just a thief with good intentions.

Emrys pressed the seal to the tribunal summons, and wrote beneath it, in handwriting identical to a dead man's and entirely their own:

*Archivist Oscar Sterling, present and accounted for. I will attend. I will speak. Bring the girl.*

* * *

The tribunal chamber aboard the audit vessel had been a chapel once, back when the fleet pretended to have a conscience. Now it had a long table, seven auditors, and a hush like held breath.

Vess read the summons, frowned at the signature, and looked up. "The archivist Sterling is deceased. Eleven years deceased. This is either fraud or—"

"Or inheritance," Emrys said, and their voice carried further than they expected, the way voices do in chapels. "I am Emrys Sterling, clone of Oscar Sterling, and I am invoking his full standing as custodian-archivist of Meridian Station—which includes the right to authenticate records, challenge dispositions, and *speak*." They set the armored testimony on the table, all ninety-one hours of it, anchored in decay patterns and gravity fossils and a dead man's patient love. "You're holding a witness you classify as salvage. I'm here to tell you she's evidence. We both are."

Seven auditors stared. Somewhere behind the chamber doors, Emrys heard Sefa's footsteps stop—listening.

"And if your authentication fails," Vess said slowly, "you'll be charged with impersonation of a murdered officer. You understand that. You'd be erased along with your archive."

"Yes," Emrys said, and found, to their genuine surprise, that they were smiling. "But my records don't fail. Ask anyone I'm a copy of."

The chamber doors opened. Sefa walked in unescorted—no one had thought to stop her—and stood beside Emrys, chin up, testimony ready.

Vess looked at the testimony. Looked at the clone. Looked, for the first time, uncertain.

"Begin," she said.
