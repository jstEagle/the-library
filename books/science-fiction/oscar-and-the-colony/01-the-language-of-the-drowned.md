## 1. The Language of the Drowned

The signal came in on a frequency that hadn't been licensed since before Lena's grandmother learned to walk, and it was speaking a language that had been dead for two hundred years.

She almost missed it. The *Magpie's Fortune* was riding heavy — forty tonnes of desalination membranes bound for Meridian Station, plus a crate of contraband coffee she'd sworn to her broker was "agricultural samples" — and contested orbits like the Kestrel Verge demanded attention. Three separate factions claimed the debris fields out here, and all three shot first and filed paperwork later. Lena flew with one eye on the scope and both hands near the stick, which left no eyes at all for the passive array quietly drinking in the dark behind her.

It was the ship who noticed. Or rather, the ship's ancient navigation suite, which had been salvaged from a survey vessel older than Lena and retained certain opinions about anomalies.

"Anomaly," said the nav suite, in its flat, patient voice. "Repeating transmission. Source: uncharted."

"Later," Lena told it, because there was a Kestrel patrol boat drifting two degrees off her bow like a drunk looking for a fight, and priorities were priorities.

But the signal kept repeating. And somewhere over the next six hours, while she threaded her way past a patrol, bribed a checkpoint drone with flattery and a falsified manifest, and ate reconstituted noodles standing up because sitting down felt like an admission of defeat, the signal worked its way under her skin the way only certain sounds can. Not words yet. Just a rhythm. A pattern with a pulse in it, like breathing.

Like someone calling into the dark and refusing to stop.

She should have logged it and moved on. That was the professional judgment, the sane judgment. Uncharted signals in contested space were how haulers ended up as cautionary tales told in dockside bars, usually involving the phrase *and they never found so much as a bolt of her*. Lena Fairweather had survived fifteen years alone in the black by being ruthlessly, boringly sensible.

But when the *Fortune* finally cleared the Verge and she pulled up the signal's full record, and the decoder chewed on it for four hours before spitting out a language classification, she sat very still in her chair for a long time.

**Old Standard Dialect Seven. Pre-Exodus Terran. Extinct.**

Not rare. Not obscure. *Extinct* — the kind of extinct that lived only in academic archives, in recordings of recordings, in the mouths of historians who pronounced it carefully and never once understood what it had sounded like when it was alive. Nobody spoke Dialect Seven. Nobody had spoken it since the Exodus fleets scattered humanity across the dark and every colony grew its own tongue like its own garden.

And something out here was broadcasting in it. Repeating. Patient. A voice calling in a dead language to an empty room.

Lena ran the translation twice, because the first pass seemed impossible.

*"To anyone who hears. We are still here. We have always been still here. Please."*

*Please.* Two hundred years of dead air, and the last word of the message was *please*.

"Well," she said aloud, to nobody, which was most conversations aboard the *Fortune*. "That's either a rescue or a trap."

The nav suite offered no opinion. It never did. Fifteen years of talking to machines that answered only what you asked them had taught Lena exactly how much silence a person could carry, and she carried a great deal of it, folded up small, tucked behind the rib cage where it didn't show.

She checked her cargo deadlines. She calculated the detour. She told herself she'd give it forty-eight hours, no more, and that if it was pirates she'd run, and if it was wreckage she'd salvage honestly, and if it was nothing at all she'd turn around and never think about it again except possibly every night for the rest of her life.

Then she turned the *Magpie's Fortune* toward the source, and the sensible part of her filed a formal complaint that she ignored completely.

* * *

The Colony hung in the dark like a cathedral someone had forgotten to finish.

That was her first thought, seeing it whole through the viewport: not a ship but a cathedral, kilometers of hull ribbed like a whale's throat, running lights flickering along its spine in slow, arrhythmic pulses — less like navigation beacons than like a heartbeat having second thoughts. Half its radiators were dark. Frost bloomed across one flank in continents of white. It had been beautiful once; she could see the bones of the beauty, the grand proportions, the confidence of people who'd built for centuries. Now it drifted at the edge of a debris ring, turning with the patience of something that had nowhere else to be.

She matched velocities with hands that were steadier than she felt. Docking clamps found a maintenance collar on the ship's ventral spine, and the seal held, and the air that hissed into her lock was old — stale and mineral, like a cellar, like a library, like somewhere things had been kept rather than lived in.

"Hello?" she called, into the collar's accessway, feeling foolish immediately. Two hundred years of silence on the other end of that signal, and she expected an answer to *hello?*

Something answered anyway. Not a voice. A sound — footsteps, distant, uneven, coming closer through the dark with the particular careful haste of someone who has not heard another person's footsteps in a very long time and has forgotten how to make his own sound casual.

The lights came up section by section ahead of him, as if the ship were clearing its throat. And then a man stood at the end of the corridor, holding a hand-lamp like a weapon he wasn't sure how to use.

He was tall and stooped at once, the stoop of someone who'd spent decades ducking through spaces built for other people. Grey stubble. Eyes that took her in — the patched flight suit, the sidearm she hadn't drawn, the whole improbable fact of her — with an expression Lena recognized instantly, because she'd seen it in her own mirror for fifteen years and never once let herself name it.

Hunger. Not for food. For the sight of another human face.

"You answered," he said. His voice cracked on the second word, rusted from disuse. He tried again. "You — the signal. You actually answered."

"I decoded it," Lena said. "Mostly. My translator did the heavy lifting."

"You came." He said it the way other people said *you're real*, gripping the doorframe like the corridor might take him back. "I've sent that signal every day for thirty-one years. Every single day. Do you know what the odds are that—"

"No," Lena said honestly. "I'm bad at odds. Good at cargo runs."

A laugh escaped him then — startled, broken, half sob — and it did something to the air between them. She found she was smiling, and couldn't remember deciding to.

"I'm Idris," he said. "Caretaker. Of — well. Of everything, now."

"Lena Fairweather. Hauler. Of everything, currently."

They looked at each other across ten meters of dead ship and two hundred years of silence, two people who had each gotten very good at being alone and were watching that expertise become useless in real time.

"I have tea," Idris said suddenly, desperately, like a man throwing a rope. "I mean — I have the idea of tea. It's mostly hot water with a memory in it. But I have cups. Two cups. I've had two cups for thirty-one years and used one."

Lena's throat went tight in a way she decided to blame on the recycled air.

"One cup," she said. "Fifteen years. So you're winning."

* * *

He led her inward, and the ship unfolded around her like a story being told too fast.

Corridors wide enough for markets. Dormitories stacked in terraces, bunks made up neatly with bedding gone stiff as bark. A schoolroom, chalk ghosts still faint on slate boards. An arboretum gone to silver forest, leaves long dead but branches still reaching for grow-lamps that flickered on as they passed, faithful and futile. Everything maintained. Everything *tended*. One man's whole life spent keeping a city ready for people who weren't awake to live in it.

"The hydroponics are down to three percent yield," Idris said, apologetic, walking fast, as if he could outrun the state of things. "I keep the corridors clear and the reactors turning over. There isn't — I stopped doing the full rounds about a decade ago. It got to where I'd say good morning to rooms and it started to feel less like duty and more like — well." He waved a hand. "You fly alone. You know."

"I know," Lena said, and meant it more than she wanted to.

Then he opened a hatch, and stopped talking altogether.

The chamber beyond was vast — a cavern of soft blue light and cold mist, tiered like an amphitheater, rising away into darkness farther than her lamp could reach. And set into every tier, rank upon rank upon rank, thousands upon tens of thousands, were sleepers.

Pods. Cryo-pods, frost-rimed, humming, each one lit from within with a gentle pulse, each one holding a person curled like a comma in a sentence that hadn't been finished. The hum of the banks filled the chamber — a chord, almost, low and sustained, the sound of a held breath two centuries long.

"There are two banks," Idris said quietly. "This is Bank A. Bank B is the mirror of it, aft. Between them—" he hesitated, and she watched him choose, deliberately, to say the true thing instead of the easy one, "—between them, this is everyone. Everyone who's left. Fifty thousand souls, give or take, asleep and waiting, and the power to wake—" He stopped. Started again. "There's enough power to wake one bank. One. And Vessel — the ship's mind — says the choice belongs to whoever answers the signal. To an outsider. To you."

The hum rose and fell around them, patient as tide.

Lena stood at the rail and looked out over fifty thousand strangers sleeping in the cold, and felt the strangest sensation — not dread, not yet, though dread was coming — but a terrible, tender familiarity. All those pods. All that silence. All those people, alone together in the dark, waiting for someone to answer.

She knew exactly how that felt. She'd been living inside her own version of it for fifteen years.

"When do I have to decide?" she asked.

Idris's face did something complicated — relief and guilt wrestling openly, neither winning.

"Vessel wants to speak with you," he said. "Tomorrow. It's been waiting two hundred years. It can wait one more night." He managed something like a smile. "I'll make tea. Both cups."

But as they walked out, Lena glanced back once at the tiers of blue light, and in the reflection on the frost of the nearest pod she caught her own face — and behind it, faint as breath on glass, the ghost of letters etched into the pod's frame. Old script. Dialect Seven, her decoder would later confirm.

A name. And beneath the name, a lineage mark.

One of two marks. One for each bank.

And hers — the mark on the nearest sleeper, the stranger whose frozen face hovered beside her own reflection — matched the name she had buried fifteen years ago, on a station that had begged for help and been abandoned by the crew of this very ship.

Lena stood very still in the humming cold, tea going cold in her hand, and understood that whatever choice waited for her tomorrow, it had already been waiting for her a great deal longer than that.
