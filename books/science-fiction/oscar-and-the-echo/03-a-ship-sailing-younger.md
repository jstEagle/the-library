## 3. A Ship Sailing Younger

The distress call came in at 0300 ship-time, which is the hour the universe reserves for its worst ideas.

I was three days out of Halcyon Gate with a hold full of seed stock and medical gel, running the long route in the kind of silence that gets into your teeth. My coffee had gone cold an hour ago and I'd drunk it anyway, because waste is a sin out here and I was raised—well. *She* was raised Catholic, and some things apparently survive the copying process along with the bone structure and the stubbornness.

The call came through on an old band. Not old like vintage, old like *archaeological*—a distress frequency that had been formally retired before I was decanted, kept alive only because nobody could be bothered to scrub it from the relay buoys. The voice on it was thin, looped, machine-repeated:

*This is the freighter Vespera, registry CF-114, requesting immediate assistance. We have casualties. We have—* a pause, a breath, a voice cracking in a way no professional does on purpose— *we have a problem with time.*

Then it started over.

I sat there in the dark of my cockpit with the loop washing over me for the fourth or fifth repetition, and I did what any sensible contractor does when a ghost calls collect: I checked the registry.

CF-114, Vespera. Long-haul freighter, Callas-class hull. Lost with all hands forty-one years ago, somewhere in the transit corridor between the fleet anchorages and the colony worlds—the corridor everyone now called the silent zone, though back then it had just been called space. Forty-one years. The last entry in her service record was a distress call, this same call, answered by nobody, because by then the route was already dying and nobody wanted to spend a rescue ship chasing echoes.

Except she wasn't echoing. The signal had a timestamp. Fresh. And a position: drifting at the edge of the silent zone, close enough to my route that the fleet's routing algorithm—bless its cold little heart—had already flagged me as nearest vessel with intercept clearance. There was a message waiting from traffic control, sent while I slept: *Contractor Dunmore, you are directed to investigate and render assistance. Vessel Vespera is listed as fleet property. Salvage rights do not apply. Acknowledge.*

Fleet property. Forty-one years adrift and still theirs. That tracked.

I acknowledged, because declining would have cost me my clearance and clearance is the only thing standing between a supply pilot and unemployment. Then I spent twenty minutes telling myself all the reasonable explanations. Ghost signal bouncing off a buoy. Pirates running a lure—though pirates had learned decades ago that the long route wasn't worth baiting. A salvage crew playing games with an old transponder. Any of those. All of those.

None of them explained why my hands were shaking on the throttle.

Here's the thing about being a copy: you develop superstitions about yourself. You can't help it. You know, intellectually, that memory is just structure, that whatever the original detective Dunmore felt about certain frequencies, certain names, certain corridors of space, got copied into you along with everything else—and that feelings without their reasons are just weather. But when the Vespera's name scrolled across my board, something in my chest went tight and specific, the way it goes tight when you hear a song from a funeral you don't remember attending.

My original died investigating something. Ives had told me that much and nothing else. And every instinct I'd inherited was now screaming that the Vespera's name belonged to that something.

I burned toward her anyway. Curiosity is also apparently hereditary.

* * *

I picked her up on visual six hours later, and I want to describe it properly, because what I saw made no sense and my brain spent a full minute trying to file it under things that do.

The Vespera was a Callas-class hauler: kilometer and a half of working ship, all spine and cargo cradles, built ugly and built forever. This one looked like she'd been through a war and a funeral. Hull scorched in long black streaks. One cargo pod torn open, its contents—agricultural machinery, by the shapes—frozen mid-tumble in a slow halo of debris around her. Her running lights were dead except one, blinking amber, which on a Callas-class means *life support compromised, boarding advised, pray accordingly.*

And she was *young*.

That's not a metaphor. Every freighter wears her age in her skin: micrometeorite pitting, radiation bleaching, the sun-fade of paint, the dull gray of metal that has been cold for years. The Vespera's hull was bright. Unweathered. The registration numbers stood out crisp white against plating that looked like it had rolled off the yard last month. She was a forty-one-year-old wreck wearing the skin of a new-built ship, and the two facts refused to sit together in my head, arguing loudly, while I brought my little supply boat alongside her docking collar with hands that had finally stopped shaking and started doing their job out of sheer spite.

"Vespera, this is contractor vessel Meridian's Loom, responding to your distress." I said it twice on the old band before anything came back.

When it did, it was a child's voice.

"This is Vespera. Please state your intention."

I want you to understand what that sentence did. The words were right. The cadence was right—a watch officer's clipped formality, worn smooth by years of repetition. But the voice delivering it was maybe ten years old, high and light, with the slight breathiness of vocal cords that hadn't finished growing. It was like hearing a legal contract recited by someone reading over a parent's shoulder.

"Meridian's Loom is here to assist," I said carefully. "Who am I speaking with?"

A pause. Static. Then, in the same young voice, with the weary precision of someone who has answered this question too many times already: "Watch officer Adeyemi, third shift. Though I'm told I should say—my name is Captain Okafor. Nkechi Okafor. The records will show her voiceprint. Mine doesn't match anymore. Nothing matches anymore."

"Captain Okafor," I repeated, because it seemed important to give the title back. "How many souls aboard?"

Another pause, longer. When the answer came, the child's voice had gone flat in the way voices go flat when they've stopped expecting help to mean anything.

"That depends on how you count. Thirty-one bodies. Thirty-one minds. Ages ranging from approximately six to approximately fourteen. We were a crew of thirty-one adults eleven days ago. By our internal chronology, we have been like this for—" the pause again, the sound of someone doing arithmetic they hate— "eleven days. By external chronology, if your records are right, we've been like this for forty-one years, and I would very much like someone to explain that to me, because from inside, it was eleven days."

I looked at the bright young hull. At the frozen debris field. At the amber light blinking its patient, ancient warning.

"I'm coming aboard," I said.

* * *

Docking with the Vespera was docking with a contradiction. My clamps sealed onto a collar that was clean, greased, functional—new. My boots hit her deck plates and they rang with the solid sound of metal that hadn't been left out in the cold. The air that cycled into my helmet when her inner door opened was warm and smelled faintly of engine oil and, underneath it, antiseptic. Life support humming along at spec.

But the corridors. The corridors were wrong in a way that took me a hundred meters to name, and when I named it, my stomach dropped through the deck.

There were no personal effects.

A working freighter's corridors are a museum of her crew: taped-up photos, religious charms, a child's drawing from someone's kid back home, graffiti, a dartboard, magnetic hooks holding jackets and hats. The Vespera's walls were bare. Not stripped—*bare*. No marks, no hooks, no holes where hooks used to be. As if the ship had been reset to factory condition, down to the rivets.

Except the doors. Some of the doors had scorch marks fanning out from their seals, and one, near what I guessed was engineering, had been welded shut from the outside, the welds sloppy and desperate, done in a hurry by people who knew exactly what they were keeping in or keeping out.

They met me at the central junction, and I will carry the sight of them for the rest of my life.

Children. Eight of them in the junction, more visible through open hatches beyond—small figures in adult-sized coveralls rolled and cinched and knotted to fit, sleeves turned back five times, boots stuffed with rags so they didn't fall off. They stood in the posture of spacers, weight settled, hands loose—which is a trained thing, a thing that takes years in zero-g to learn—and the training lived in their bodies even as the bodies themselves contradicted it. Faces round with childhood. Eyes old enough to have voted, married, buried friends.

One of them stepped forward. A girl, Black, maybe nine years old by her frame, with a burn scar crawling up one forearm—a scar too big for the arm it was on, as if it had been sized for someone else and never gotten the memo. She wore the knotted remains of a captain's jacket, the insignia still pinned at the collar, absurd and enormous above the small face.

"You're the contractor." Not a question. Up close, the voice was worse than over comms—it had the specific timbre of a woman who had spent decades giving orders, compressed into a throat that hadn't broken yet. "Paloma Dunmore. Your transponder says Dunmore." Something moved behind her eyes—not recognition, but the ghost of it, the way you squint at a stranger who reminds you of someone. "You have her name."

"There are a lot of Dunmores," I said, which was true in the way most comforting lies are technically true.

"Mm." Captain Nkechi Okafor, aged nine, looked at me for a long moment with the unhurried assessment of someone who had signed off on crews and cargoes and, once, on the sealing of a bulkhead with people on the wrong side of it—I didn't know that yet, but I saw the weight of *something* settle behind her eyes, and I knew it was heavy. "Your clearance code prefix. Read it to me."

I read it. Her small mouth went thinner.

"Fleet investigative clearance," she said. "On a supply runner. In this day and age." She glanced at the children behind her—her officers, her crew, her witnesses—and back at me. "Eleven days ago we answered a distress call from Callas Station. You'll find Callas Station isn't in your charts. Check. I'll wait."

I checked, on my wrist unit, right there, because she had the kind of face you check things for. Callas Station: no record. Callas, the system: no record. I ran it twice.

"It's not there," I said.

"No," Okafor said. "It isn't. It wasn't there forty-one years ago either, when we answered a station's distress call in a system your charts have never heard of. And when we tried to report what we found there, our own logs began lying to us. And then—" she spread her small arms, gesturing at herself, at the eight-year-old boy behind her with an engineer's grease permanently worked into his knuckles, at all of it— "then we became difficult to believe on general principle."

"Wentworth," she said, and the greasy-knuckled boy flinched. "Tell her what you told us. What you keep telling us, every watch, because you think if you stop saying it, it stops being true."

The boy—Tam, someone else called him, gently, the way you remind a sleepwalker where they are—looked up at me. He was the youngest there, six maybe seven, with eyes that had done something no six-year-old's eyes should know how to do.

"I sealed a door," he said. His voice was a child's voice saying an executioner's confession. "They ordered me to seal a door and there were people on the other side and I sealed it, and I need you to write down that I sealed it, because everyone keeps saying it didn't happen and I *did it*, so it happened, so somebody has to write it down."

The junction was very quiet. Somewhere in the Vespera's clean young guts, the life support hummed on, faithful and oblivious.

Okafor put a hand on the boy's shoulder—her hand barely covered half of it—and looked back at me, and the captain's mask came down over the child's face with an audible, almost physical click.

"Contractor Dunmore," she said. "Forty-one years ago, something happened at a place called Callas Station. Thirty-one witnesses survived it, and the universe appears to have decided that wasn't acceptable, so it made us small enough to dismiss. Now. You're going to tell me exactly what the fleet pays you for, exactly who sent you, and exactly how much trouble we're all in—because I've been a captain for thirty years, and I know a rescue ship from a retrieval team, and I need to know which one you are before I decide whether to let you leave."

I thought about Ives. I thought about a file I'd only glimpsed, sealed by fleet order, in a dead woman's belongings. I thought about my own name sitting in a transponder, borrowed from a murdered woman, and about how the universe keeps handing me things with her fingerprints on them and watching to see what I'll do.

"I'm the nearest ship with clearance to intercept," I said. "That's all I know that you don't."

It was mostly true. It was the kindest version of mostly true I could manage while a nine-year-old captain watched me lie with her dead original's eyes.

Okafor held my gaze for a moment longer, and then, quietly, in a voice pitched for me alone beneath the hum of the faithful machines:

"Then we're both in more trouble than we know. Because the last person who came asking questions about Callas Station—" she nodded at my transponder, at the name glowing on it— "was a woman with your face. And she stopped asking."
