## 4. The Girl Who Was the Captain

Docking with a ghost is a particular kind of humiliation. You spend the whole approach telling yourself it's routine—magnetic collar, pressure seal, standard boarding protocol—and then the other ship's hull fills your viewport and you remember that routine is just a word we use for things we've stopped being afraid of.

The *Vespera* was a Meridian-class long-hauler, or had been, forty years ago. I knew the class from museum holos and one very dull certification module. The real thing looked like a cathedral that had lost an argument with the sea: buttressed drive spines, hull plating patched in three generations of alloys, the faded ghost of a fleet registry number that someone had half-sanded and then, apparently, lost the will to finish. She was dark except for the docking beacon, pulsing that patient amber distress rhythm like a heartbeat someone had set to *stall*.

I ran the numbers again while the collar caught. Crew manifest, recovered from the transponder archive: nine aboard at last registered departure. Life support readings suggested nine aboard now. The distress call was forty-one years old. The people inside it were, by every scan I could throw at the hull, between six and eleven years old.

Nine children, alone on a forty-year-old freighter, broadcasting a mayday from before most of their parents were born.

There's a flavor of wrong that sits on the back of the tongue. I cycled my suit's air, told myself it was recycled CO₂, and popped the inner hatch.

* * *

The first thing I noticed was the smell, which was wrong in a way I couldn't immediately name. Ships have a smell—ozone and lubricant and recycled air with a faint undertone of human endurance. The *Vespera* had all of that, but under it was something sweeter, almost botanical, like a greenhouse running on the wrong season. Later I would learn the hydroponics bay had been running unattended for four decades, breeding things no botanist had ever catalogued. At the time it just made my skin crawl, the way a familiar room does when one object has been moved two centimeters.

The second thing I noticed was the corridor lights. They were set low—night-cycle low—but they weren't flickering or failing. They were *dimmed on purpose*, the way you'd dim them for a sleeping child.

"Hello?" I said, and my voice did that thing voices do on dead ships, arriving too loud and too flat, like a stranger's.

"Corridor B," said a voice from the dark ahead of me. "Follow the guide lights. Mind the step at the junction—the plate's been loose since before you were born."

It was a child's voice. High, clear, unhurried. And it had the specific cadence of someone giving orders, which is a cadence you can't fake and can't mistake. I've heard it from fleet admirals and from dock bosses and once, memorably, from a nun herding orphans through a station fire. Authority has a shape, and it doesn't care what throat it comes out of.

I followed the guide lights to a junction where a step plate was indeed loose, and past it, into a common room that had been converted—evidently decades ago—into something between a mess hall and a classroom. Small tables. A wall of hand-drawn star charts, the ink faded to sepia. And children.

Three of them, at least, in the room. A boy of maybe eight sitting cross-legged on a bunk with a maintenance manual open on his knees, reading it the way adults read scripture—slowly, and with the expression of someone who already disagrees with the author. A smaller girl asleep in a nest of blankets, thumb near her mouth, and beside her a data slate still glowing with what looked like orbital mechanics homework. And standing at the room's center, hands clasped behind her back in a posture I had only ever seen in fleet command portraits, a girl of perhaps nine or ten, with close-cropped hair growing in unevenly, as if it had recently been shaved and was coming back on its own schedule and no one else's.

She looked me up and down with an expression I can only describe as *audit*.

"You're not fleet," she said.

"Paloma Dunmore. Independent contract, supply and salvage. I answered your beacon."

"Our beacon is forty-one years old." She said it without accusation, just as a fact to be placed on the table between us. "You're either very slow or very lost."

"Both, some days." I pulled my helmet off, because standing sealed in a room full of children while they breathed open air felt like an insult. "And you are?"

The boy on the bunk snorted. The sleeping girl didn't stir. And the small figure at the center of the room straightened another degree, which I hadn't thought possible.

"Captain Adaeze Okafor," she said. "Commanding officer, *CSLV Vespera*."

I want to be honest about what happened in my body in that moment, because I've had time to think about it since, and honesty is the only thing I have that's actually mine. Every hair on my arms stood up. Not fear, exactly. Recognition's uglier cousin. Because I knew that name. Every long-haul pilot knew that name. Adaeze Okafor, the woman who'd held the Meridian route open through the worst years of the severance, who'd run medical cargo through the silent zone when the colonies went dark, who'd retired—no. Who'd *vanished*. Who'd gone out on the *Vespera* forty-one years ago and never come back, and whose name was on a memorial wall on Callas Station, and whose file I had—

Whose file I had, in a box, in my cabin, sealed by fleet order, in the handwriting of a dead woman whose face was my face.

"Captain," I said carefully, "how old are you?"

"Sixty-three." She said it the way you'd say your blood type. Then, because she was evidently a captain who anticipated her crew's confusion: "The body is nine. The discrepancy is under review."

"Under review by whom?"

"By me." A pause. "It's been a long review."

The boy on the bunk—Tam, I'd learn, though I didn't know it yet—looked up from his manual and said, "She's been reviewing for four days. She keeps writing things down and crossing them out." He said it with the flatness of a child reporting the weather, and the weariness of a man reporting a marriage.

"Tam," the captain said, and it was all she needed to say. He went back to his manual, but I noticed he'd gone very still, the way people do when a subject has edges.

* * *

She gave me the tour herself. Of course she did. A captain doesn't delegate first contact, and I suspect that after four days of being nine years old, she'd have walked through a hull breach for the excuse to stand at full height in front of someone new.

The *Vespera* was, in most ways, a miracle of stubborn maintenance. The children—no. I have to keep correcting myself, and I want the reader to feel that correction grind, because I felt it grind for the whole time I was aboard. The *crew* had kept her alive. The engines had been hand-tuned past any manufacturer's tolerance. The water recyclers had been rebuilt with parts machined from scrap, and the machining was good—better than good, the kind of work you do when you have forty years and nothing but time. The hydroponics bay was the source of the sweet wrong smell: three decades of untended cultivation, a jungle of engineered greens gone feral, tended now by a girl of seven with a sixty-year-old's patience and a pair of pruning shears too big for her hands.

"The reversal wasn't uniform," Okafor said as we walked. She had to half-jog to keep pace with my stride, and I slowed down, and she noticed me slowing down, and her jaw tightened at the courtesy. "The field—whatever it was, we still don't have the physics—caught us all in transit. Sleep rotation. I was in my bunk. I woke up and my hands didn't reach the sides of the bunk anymore." She held one up, a small brown hand with a child's soft knuckles and, on the inner wrist, a faded tattoo of a ship's wheel. "The minds came through intact. Mostly. The bodies went back to—" she gestured at herself, at the sleeping girl, at the boy hunched over his manual, "—this. The age we were when we first shipped out. As if the universe looked at us and said, *ah, no, you haven't earned these years, give them back*."

"Do you know what did it?"

"No." We stopped at a viewport. Outside, the silent zone's stars hung in their indifferent rows. "That's the part that should frighten you, Dunmore. It wasn't an accident. Accidents are sloppy. This was—" she searched for the word, and her child's face made the searching look almost comically adult, "—*curated*. The ship's chronometers are consistent. Our own memories are consistent. Everything else—the logs, the nav records, the mission file—" she tapped the bulkhead, "—has been rewritten. Not deleted. *Rewritten*. Do you understand the difference?"

I thought of a box in my cabin. A file with pages that had been photocopied so many times the edges had gone soft, with sections that read like they'd been written by a committee whose only passion was tedium. Records not deleted but overwritten with bland falsehoods.

"I think I might," I said.

She looked at me for a long moment. Nine years old, sixty-three years deep, and behind her eyes something moved that made me think of deep water—something that had been down there a long time and knew the pressure.

"Then you'll want to see the bridge," she said.

* * *

The bridge of the *Vespera* was a shrine. I don't mean that decoratively. Someone had kept it pristine—dust-free, consoles wrapped, the command chair positioned with its back to a viewport full of stars, the way fleet tradition insists even though every ergonomics study says it's wrong. On the main display, frozen, was a star chart with a single system highlighted.

Callas Station.

"Tell me what you know about Callas," Okafor said.

"It's a fleet logistics hub on the outer Meridian corridor. Decommissioned." The words came out smooth, and they were lies, or at least they were the shape of lies—I knew them the way you know a slogan, without knowing where you'd absorbed it. "There was a station fire. Some years back. Casualties. It's in the public record."

"Say the casualty figure."

I opened my mouth and found nothing there. That's the thing about institutional lies done well: they don't give you a false number to disbelieve. They give you a smooth place where the number should be, polished to a shine, so your mind slides off it every time. "I don't— I don't actually know the figure."

"Neither does anyone," Okafor said. "That's the point. I was there, Dunmore. Not at the fire. At the *station*. Six weeks before. We docked at Callas for resupply, and I saw things in those corridors that the public record has been sanded down to a single word: *incident*. And when we went back—when we answered the distress call that brought us here, the call that was already forty years old when we answered it, which should have been the first thing that told us the universe had gone wrong—we came out the other side of whatever that field was, and the first thing I did was pull our own logs of Callas." She turned to the console. Her fingers, small and precise, moved across it with the muscle memory of decades. "They're gone. Not corrupted. *Replaced*. There's a log of our Callas docking in there, and it's a lovely log, very complete, and it never happened. We docked, we took on water and protein stock, we departed. Eleven hours. No incidents."

"And the truth?"

"The truth is eleven hours of the worst thing I have ever witnessed, and I have been a ship's captain through a hull breach and a mutiny and one genuinely bad wedding." She pulled up a file. It was small. A fragment—audio, mostly corrupted, a few seconds of video with the color bleeding out of it. "This survived. Whatever rewrote the logs missed it because it wasn't in the ship's system. It was in my personal recorder, which I wore on a chain, which is now—" she touched her collarbone, where a chain mark showed faintly on the child's skin, "—too big for me. I've kept it off the network. In my head, mostly. I've had four days to memorize every frame."

"Captain." I sat down in the second chair, because my legs had opinions. "Why did you answer a forty-year-old distress call?"

"Because it was *our* distress call." She said it simply. "The *Vespera*'s own beacon, our own voices, timestamped four decades back. And when a ship hears itself calling for help from a part of its own history it can't account for, there's only one correct response." Her chin came up. "We went. And I'd go again. That's not bravery, before you write it up that way. That's just the job."

The fragment played. I won't reproduce it here—there are things that belong to the dead first, and the record second—but I will say that in eleven seconds of corrupted audio, I heard a voice shouting an order, and the order was not *evacuate*, and it was not *stand by*, and it was the kind of order that a person gives when they have decided that the people in a room are a problem to be solved rather than lives to be saved. And I heard, underneath it, a name.

The name was Voss.

I didn't react. I'd like to claim that as discipline, but it was really just shock doing its work, freezing everything. Because I knew that name too. Not from the news, not from fleet announcements. From a dead woman's handwriting, in a sealed file, in a box in my cabin, where a murdered detective had underlined it three times and written, in the margin, in ink gone brown: *he signs the orders no one is meant to read*.

"You know him," Okafor said. It wasn't a question. Nine years old, sixty-three years deep, and reading my face like a manifest.

"I know *of* him."

"Everyone knows of him now. Admiral Rehn Voss. Architect of the truce. The man who ended the war." She smiled, and it was a terrible thing to see on a child's face, because it was an old smile, a tired smile, the smile of someone who has sat across a table from a man and watched him lie beautifully. "Do you know what I remember most about Rehn Voss, Dunmore? Not the order. Orders are noise. I remember that he *apologized* to me afterward. Shook my hand on the dock, looked me in the eye, and said, *I'm sorry you had to see that, Captain*. As if I'd walked in on him changing. As if the shame was mine, for witnessing." She looked down at her small hands. "And then the years went by, and the record went smooth, and I started to wonder if I'd imagined it. Forty years of wondering if you imagined the worst day of your life. That's what they do. That's the real weapon. Not the massacre. The *doubt*."

The sleeping girl stirred in the common room behind us; through the open hatch I could see Tam had closed his manual and was watching us with the stillness of a boy who had heard this conversation before and would hear it again, and who carried something of his own about a door—I didn't know that yet either. I knew only that the ship around us felt less like a vessel and more like a held breath.

"Captain," I said, "why did you really hail me? You've had four days. You could have stayed dark. A ship full of children in the silent zone is invisible if you want to be."

"Because we're not children." She came around the console and stood in front of me, and even at a meter and a half she managed to look down at me, which I still can't explain and have stopped trying to. "We're witnesses. Nine of the only witnesses there are. And whatever did this to us was meant to do something else—it was meant to make us *unreliable*. Who believes a child who says she watched a massacre? Who believes a child who says she's sixty-three? They didn't just take our years, Dunmore. They took our credibility. It's the same trick as the rewritten logs. Make the truth look like a madwoman's story." Her voice didn't rise. It got quieter, which was worse. "So I need someone who deals in evidence. Someone who knows what an erasure signature looks like. Someone who, when I tell them what happened at Callas Station, will not tell me I imagined it."

The ship hummed around us. Somewhere in the feral hydroponics jungle, water dripped with the patience of geological time.

"Captain Okafor," I said, "I have to tell you something, and I need you to hear all of it before you decide what I'm worth." My mouth was dry. "I'm not who you think I am. My name is Paloma Dunmore, and I'm a copy—a clone, if you like the ugly word—of a fleet detective named Mirabel Dunmore, who was murdered eleven years ago while investigating a file she wasn't cleared to open. The Callas Station massacre. Her file is sitting in my cabin right now. It's sealed by fleet order. It names the man who signed the orders no one was meant to read. And I've spent every day since I found it trying to decide whether carrying a dead woman's case is an inheritance or a haunting."

I expected—something. Pity, maybe. The look people give copies, the one that's trying very hard not to check whether you're real.

Instead, Adaeze Okafor, sixty-three years old and nine years tall, reached out and took my hand in both of hers, small and warm and steady, the way you'd take the hand of a crew member standing too close to an airlock.

"Mirabel Dunmore interviewed me once," she said. "Eleven years ago. Two weeks before she died. She came to my berth at Callas with a warrant she didn't have and questions she wasn't supposed to ask, and I lied to her, because I was afraid, because the record had already gone smooth and I thought I was the only one who remembered." Her eyes were bright and old. "She told me: *One day someone will come back for this, and when they do, I hope they look like someone who won't be lied to twice*."

The ship's lights dimmed a fraction—the night cycle, or the ship itself holding its breath.

"So," said the girl who was the captain. "Shall we open the file?"
