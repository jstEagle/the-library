## 11. Running the Silence

The thing nobody tells you about being eight years old again is that your bladder is also eight years old. I'd spent thirty-one years as a grown man who could hold it through a double shift in the drive bay, and now here I was, four days into the transit through the silent zone, waking up for the third time that night because my body had the storage capacity of a teacup.

I padded down the Vespera's corridor in socks, past the galley where somebody had left the light on—Okafor, probably, who hadn't slept properly since we burned out of fleet custody—and into the head, where I stood at a urinal mounted for adults and had to lean forward like a kid at a drinking fountain to reach it. There's dignity, and then there's physics, and physics was winning.

That's when the dream came back.

It does that sometimes—not during sleep but right after, when you're standing somewhere quiet with your guard down, and your brain decides that's the perfect moment to hand you a door. Not a metaphorical door. An actual door. Grey composite, waist-high window, stenciled number: CS-7-114. And behind it, screaming that had already stopped being screaming by the time anyone reached it, because air doesn't carry sound long once it's gone.

I gripped the sink and waited for it to pass. It didn't pass so much as recede, the way tide goes out, leaving things behind on the sand. This time what it left was geometry. The corridor outside the door. The junction forty meters aft. The secondary ladderwell. The way the deck plates hummed underfoot because life support was still running then, still running everywhere except behind the doors they'd sealed.

"Tam?" Okafor's voice from the corridor. Small voice, captain's cadence. "You're awake."

"Yeah." My voice cracked on it, which at least was age-appropriate. "Just—bathroom stuff."

"Liar," she said, without heat. She appeared in the doorway of the head, and even now, four months into this, seeing her hit me wrong every time. Captain Amara Okafor had been sixty-two years old and built like a cargo crane. The girl standing in front of me was maybe nine, with her grey-streaked hair gone dark again and her forearms thin as pipe cleaners. But she looked at me the same way. That was the part that never stopped being strange—that the reversal had taken everything except the person inside.

"Dream again," I said.

"The station?"

"The station."

She nodded like I'd reported a coolant pressure reading. "Same section?"

"Seven. Cargo ring." I wiped my face with a sleeve that was too long. "Captain, why do you keep asking me about the layout? We're not going back there."

She looked at me for a long moment, and I watched her decide something. "Get some water," she said. "We'll talk at shift change."

Which is captain for *yes, we are absolutely going back there.*

* * *

The silent zone earned its name honestly. Between the fleet's patrol volume and the colony worlds there's a stretch of maybe eleven light-days where nobody lives, nobody trades, and nobody talks. No relay buoys, no beacon traffic, nothing. The colonies went dark years ago—the whole reason our route existed was hauling supplies toward people who'd stopped answering—and the fleet, for its own reasons, preferred the emptiness stay empty. Flying through it felt like walking through a house where everyone had died mid-sentence.

Paloma Dunmore ran the transit tight. She'd explained it to us in the galley before the burn: the corvette *Halcyon* had been shadowing us since we cleared the last buoy, hanging back at the edge of sensor range, flying assistance colors—which meant its transponder read RESCUE AND AID, which meant if it caught us, legally, it was coming to help. You can't refuse help. That's the beauty of it, from the fleet's side. They don't need weapons when they've got paperwork.

"They'll close the gap at the zone's far edge," Paloma said, marking the display with two fingers. "There's nowhere to run inside the silence—no traffic to hide among, no gravity wells worth slingshotting. So we don't run inside it. We run *out* of it, hard, and we make Meridian before they can make us stop."

"And if they catch us first?" asked Reyes, our comms officer, currently occupying a body of about ten years old and clearly hating every second of it.

"Then I talk to them," Paloma said. "A lot. Slowly. With paperwork of my own." She had a stack of it, actually—clearance codes inherited from her original, salvage claims, medical quarantine declarations. Watching her work, I kept forgetting she wasn't a real detective, and then remembering she was more than one, and then feeling weird about both.

I didn't say anything in that meeting. I'd been keeping my mouth shut mostly, doing my job in the drive bay—my hands remembered everything, thank God, even if they were small enough now that I needed riser blocks to reach half the manifolds. But after, in the corridor, Okafor fell into step beside me, and her shoulder came up to my elbow, and neither of those facts made her any less the captain.

"You dreamed the layout," she said. "Tell me."

"Why?"

"Because Callas Station is still up there." She pointed vaguely spinward, at nothing. "Decaying orbit around a dead gas giant, per the old charts. And whatever proof exists that the fleet missed—the physical black box, the parts that can't be overwritten—it's aboard that wreck. Paloma thinks so. I think so. And you—" she tapped my chest with one small finger, "—you are the only living map of its interior."

"I was a junior engineer," I said. "I knew cargo ring seven because that's where I worked. I don't know the whole station."

"You know the whole station better than anyone alive, Tam. Because everyone else who knew it is either dead or standing on this ship." Her voice was gentle, which somehow made it worse. "I'm not asking tonight. I'm telling you so it doesn't ambush you later."

It ambushed me anyway, that night, and the night after. The dreams got longer. More detailed. In one of them I walked the entire length of cargo ring seven with my eyes open, counting doors, and woke up with the count in my head like a phone number: fourteen sealed, three breached, one—the one at the ring's heart, the central hub where the atmospheric controls lived—one that had been locked from the *outside*, with a manual override, and I remembered whose hand had been on it.

Not mine. Mine had been shaking too hard to turn the wheel. I'd been ordered to seal door 114 and I'd done it, and the order had come down a channel I couldn't name, from a voice I'd spent thirty years trying not to remember, and the reversal had handed all of it back to me like a returned package, postage due.

* * *

Day six, the corvette closed to visual range.

We saw it on the scope first—a bright point holding steady off our port quarter, patient as a debt collector. Then Paloma brought it up on the main display and there it was: the *Halcyon*, fleet-grey, rescue stripes along its flanks, running lights blinking the friendly pattern. Somebody aboard was broadcasting on the general band, a warm recorded voice offering *assistance to vessels in distress, repeat, assistance to vessels in distress.*

"We're not in distress," said Reyes.

"That's the trick," Paloma said. "They define distress. Engine trouble counts. Medical emergency counts. A ship full of children with adult minds—" she glanced around the galley, at the collection of small bodies wearing crew jumpsuits cinched at the waist with extra turns of belt, "—counts as a medical emergency, I'd wager. They board to 'assist,' and the assist includes securing all data cores for 'safekeeping.'"

"So we outrun them," said Devi from navigation.

"In the silence, nobody outruns anybody. Same burn, same mass, same physics." Paloma killed the display. "We just have to be stiller than they expect, and faster exactly once."

That night I sat my watch alone in the drive bay, which I requested whenever I could, because the drive bay didn't have windows and didn't have other people and the hum of the coils drowned out the memories better than silence did. Except that night the hum sounded like the station's atmosphere processors, and I caught myself checking gauges that weren't there, reaching for a diagnostic panel that had been thirty years and one de-aging ago, and I understood that the dreams weren't waiting for sleep anymore. The station had moved in. It was subletting space in my skull.

So I did the only useful thing I could think of. I got a grease pencil and started drawing on the back of a maintenance panel cover. Cargo ring seven. All the doors. The junctions. The ladderwells. The hub. I drew until my hand cramped, and when I stepped back, the whole ring looked back at me, and it was accurate—I knew it was accurate the way you know your own heartbeat—and I hated how well I knew it.

Okafor found me there at shift change. She looked at the drawing for a long time.

"You said you weren't going back," I said.

"I said I wasn't asking tonight." She picked up the grease pencil and added a mark herself—a ventilation duct running off the hub, narrow, the kind you crawl through. "I was on the command deck when it happened. I saw the atmospheric readings drop sector by sector. I heard the orders given. I've spent thirty years knowing *that* the station was killed. You know *where*. Together we know the body." She set the pencil down. "The black box on a station like Callas sits in the administrative spine, above the hub, behind a bulkhead rated against hull breach. If the purge teams missed it, it's because getting to it means going through cargo seven."

"Through the doors."

"Through the doors."

I looked at my hands. Eight-year-old hands, grease-smudged, steady enough now. "Captain, I sealed one of those doors."

"I know."

"There were people behind it."

"I know, Tam." She put her hand over mine, and her hand was smaller than mine, and it still felt like being anchored. "And there's a version of this where you stay aboard the Vespera when we dock with the wreck, and nobody thinks less of you. But I've watched you draw that ring from memory twice now, and I think some part of you needs to walk it awake. Needs to be the one who opens instead of seals."

I wanted to tell her she was wrong. I couldn't find the words to do it, because she wasn't.

* * *

Day eight, the *Halcyon* made its move—or rather, made its courtesy call.

"Halcyon to unidentified freighter," crackled the comm, in a voice so reasonable it had to be trained. "We detect anomalies in your life support telemetry. We are standing by to render assistance. Please confirm status."

Paloma took the board herself. "Vespera to Halcyon. Our telemetry is fine. We're a commercial vessel running a scheduled route. Appreciate the concern."

"Your transponder registry shows irregularities, Vespera. We'd like to verify your documentation in person. Boarding party of four, fully equipped for search-and-rescue. We can match velocities within the hour."

"Stand by," said Paloma, and muted the channel, and turned to the rest of us gathered in the galley, and for a moment nobody spoke. Outside the viewports there was nothing but starfield and the slow wheel of the galaxy, and somewhere in it a war-shaped hole waiting to refill itself.

"They'll be alongside in fifty minutes," Paloma said. "Which gives us forty-nine minutes to be somewhere else."

Devi blinked. "The burn you said we couldn't make?"

"The burn I said we couldn't sustain. Different word." She pulled up the nav plot, and there it was: the far edge of the silent zone, and beyond it the faint, unlit smudge of the Meridian system. "The Halcyon assumes we'll behave like a freighter—efficient burns, fuel discipline. So we do the opposite. Full military thrust, everything the drive has, straight down the throat of the zone's edge and out. They'll chase. They're faster. But they have to decelerate to board, and boarding a ship that won't stop takes time we will have already spent."

"And if the drive overheats?" I asked. Professional curiosity. Also terror.

"Then you fix it," Paloma said, looking straight at me, "because you're the best engineer on this ship, and I include the ship's original designer in that assessment."

I looked at Okafor. Okafor looked at me and gave the smallest nod, the kind she used to give across a command deck, the kind that meant *your station, engineer.*

"Match their hail," I said. "Tell them we're experiencing catastrophic drive instability and we're attempting an emergency burn to clear the zone before core breach. Make it good and panicked."

Paloma smiled slowly. "Tam."

"Somebody has to lie to the fleet," I said. "Might as well be the guy who's already dead inside."

The lie went out over the general band, breathless and garbled, and the *Halcyon*'s helpful voice sharpened by several degrees—*maintain position, do not attempt maneuver, we are here to help*—and Paloma answered by lighting the drive.

The Vespera lurched like a kicked dog. Everything not bolted down went aft, including most of the crew, including me; I rode the acceleration couch in the drive bay and watched the thermal readouts climb into territory I'd only ever seen in accident reports, and I talked to the engine the whole time, out loud, the way engineers do, promising it rest and coolant and forgiveness if it would just hold together for eleven more minutes.

Nine minutes in, coil housing three started singing—a high whine that meant the containment field was breathing wrong. Ten minutes in, the whine became a scream. At eleven minutes and change, the stars outside stretched and steadied, the zone edge scrolled beneath us like a border crossing, and the plot bloomed with new contacts: relay buoys, faint and long-dormant, waking up as we passed. And beyond them, on the long-range scope, the Meridian system. Dark. Silent. Waiting.

Behind us, the *Halcyon* burned to catch up, gaining, always gaining—but gaining on distance now instead of on us.

I shut the drive down to cruise, patted the housing like a horse, and sat on the deck plates shaking for a while. Not from fear. Or not only. From the strange, vertiginous feeling of having been believed—of having told the truth about the worst thing I ever did, disguised as a lie about an engine, and having the universe accept it.

Okafor came down an hour later with tea. Real tea, hoarded from her personal stores, the kind of thing a captain saves for occasions.

"For the record," she said, handing me the cup, "that was a good lie."

"It was a true lie," I said. "The drive nearly did breach. And I did seal the door. And I am going back to open it."

She sat down next to me on the deck, small shoulders against small shoulders, and we drank our tea while the Vespera ran quiet toward Meridian.

That night, for the first time since the reversal, I slept without dreaming of the station. Instead I dreamed of the drawing on the maintenance panel—just the drawing, clean lines, no screams behind any of the doors—and in the dream someone had added a new mark in fresh grease pencil, at door 114, a single word in a child's careful handwriting:

*OPEN.*
