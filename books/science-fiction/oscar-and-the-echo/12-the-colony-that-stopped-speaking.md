## 12. The Colony That Stopped Speaking

Meridian was supposed to be a tomb.

Every chart in the fleet's database said so. Every briefing Ives had ever given me said so. *Colony world Meridian: contact lost eleven years ago. Cause unknown. Do not divert resources.* The fleet had a gift for that phrasing—*do not divert resources*—which was bureaucratic shorthand for "there is nothing there and we would prefer you didn't look."

The Vespera came out of the silence zone with her drives singing and her crew of forty-some children strapped into chairs built for adults, and there she was on the forward display: a blue-green marble wearing a thin scarf of cloud, sitting in the goldilocks light of a patient yellow star. Oceans. Continents. A weather system spinning lazily off the southern coast of the largest landmass like a cat deciding whether to knock something off a table.

"Looks alive," Tam said from the copilot's seat. He was nine years old now, or had been made to be, though his hands moved across the console with an engineer's certainty that no nine-year-old should possess. "I mean. It looks *inhabited*. Look at the night side."

I looked. The night side was dark. Completely, perfectly dark. No city lights, no orbital infrastructure glinting, nothing but starlight on water.

"That's my point," he said.

"A planet with no lights," Okafor said behind us. She stood in the cockpit doorway, and even at eight years old in a captain's uniform rolled up six times at the cuffs, she managed to make it look like command rather than costume. "Eleven years ago this world had four million people on it. Cities. Spaceports. A fusion grid you could see from high orbit."

"And now?" I asked.

"Now it's playing dead," she said. "And doing it very well."

There's a particular feeling you get when a thing you've been told is impossible turns out to be merely inconvenient. It's not quite relief. Relief implies the danger is over. It's more like the moment in a long fall when you realize the ground is farther away than you thought—you get your breath back, and then you notice how much farther there is to go, and what else might be waiting down there.

I felt that. Because Meridian wasn't dead. Meridian was hiding. And things only hide from something they're afraid of.

* * *

We took the Vespera down on the night side, into the dark, running cold except for what we needed, because if there was anyone left down there, I didn't want our arrival to look like a fleet approach vector. Which meant a descent that lasted the better part of two hours, riding the atmosphere's friction in near-total silence while forty adults in children's bodies sat in the dark and waited.

You'd think that would be unbearable. Mostly it was quiet. There's a kind of patience that people learn when they've already survived the worst thing that will ever happen to them, and the Vespera's crew had it in abundance. They'd been old when the Callas Station massacre happened. They'd been made young by a technology nobody was supposed to have, as a way of scrambling them into uselessness as witnesses. And they'd spent however many decades it was falling home through the black with those memories intact, waiting for someone to ask them what they knew.

Nobody had asked. Until me. Until my original, who died for asking.

The clouds broke at about four thousand meters and I saw the first sign of life: not lights, but absence. A river valley where the terrain was too smooth, too regular. Terracing. Old farmland, fallowed. Then a road—a real road, graded and cambered, running along the valley floor toward a town whose buildings were all still standing and all of them shuttered, windows blanked with panels, roofs painted matte black to kill any reflection.

"They didn't abandon," Tam murmured, reading the same terrain I was. "They *closed*. Look at the roads—maintenance grade. Someone's been grading these within the last few years. The town isn't dead, it's sleeping."

"Sleeping through a war that hasn't started yet," Okafor said.

I brought us down in a dry lakebed thirty kilometers out, because I'm cautious, and because my original's file had taught me that the safest distance from anything is usually farther than you think. The dust settled around the hull in a slow brown curtain, and for a while we just sat there, listening to the ship tick as her skin cooled, looking out at a world that refused to admit it existed.

It was Tam who spotted them. "Paloma." His voice had gone very careful, the way it did when the engineer in him and the child in him were fighting for control of his mouth. "Ten o'clock. Ridge line."

I looked. At first: nothing. Rock and scrub and the long shadows of late afternoon. Then the nothing moved, and resolved into shapes. People. Maybe two dozen of them, spread along the ridge in a skirmish line, and every one of them holding a weapon trained on our ship.

They were too far away to make out faces. But I could make out silhouettes, and the silhouettes told a story: coats, hoods, rifles braced with the economy of people who'd done this before. Not soldiers exactly. Something older than soldiers. Farmers with guns, which history will tell you are frequently more dangerous than soldiers, because farmers have something to lose and soldiers only have orders.

"Well," Okafor said. "Either they've come to welcome us—"

"—or shoot us," Tam finished.

"There's a third option," I said, and reached for the airlock controls. "They wait to see what we do. So let's give them something worth watching."

* * *

I went out alone. Okafor argued, of course—she was the captain, she said, and captains don't send other people down their ladders first—but I pointed out, with what I felt was admirable restraint, that a woman walking toward a firing line unarmed was a message, whereas a child in a captain's coat doing the same thing was a hostage situation waiting to happen. She didn't like it. She gave the order anyway, which is the difference between a captain and a person who likes giving orders.

The ground between the lakebed and the ridge was scrub grass and rust-red dirt, and it crunched under my boots in a way that seemed enormously loud. Two hundred meters. Three hundred. The rifles tracked me the whole way, and I kept my hands visible and my pace even, and I thought, absurdly, about my original. About the woman whose face I wear and whose instincts I keep discovering in myself like furniture in a house I inherited. She'd have walked this same walk. Probably walked worse ones. Died at the end of one, in fact, somewhere in the fleet's bright corridors, for asking questions about a station full of dead people.

I wasn't going to die on a hillside on a world that officially didn't exist. That seemed like a bad place to leave a body. No one would ever find it, and worse, no one would ever be able to prove it hadn't happened.

At fifty meters they let me stop, and a figure came down the slope to meet me. Older woman, silver hair cut short, deep lines around eyes that had done a lot of squinting at horizons. She carried her rifle slung, which I chose to read as a good sign, and stopped just outside arm's reach, which I chose to read as wisdom.

"You're not fleet," she said. Her voice was flat, worn smooth like river stone.

"No."

"The ship's transponder says *Vespera*. That name's been in our ledger for sixty years." She said *ledger* the way some people say *bible*. "Long-haul freighter. Lost with all hands answering a distress call out past the shipping lanes. We ring a bell for her every year."

"We answered the call," I said. "Late."

Something moved behind her eyes—not softening exactly, more like a door opening onto a room where someone had been keeping a fire lit for a very long time. "Late," she repeated. "Yes. We know about late." She studied my face, and I watched her decide something. "You have her accent. Fleet core worlds. But you came out of the silence zone flying a ghost ship, alone, cold, and you walked toward twenty-two rifles without flinching. That's not fleet behavior. Fleet sends shuttles and forms."

"I'm not fleet," I said again. "I'm the nearest ship with clearance, which is a different thing entirely. And I've got forty witnesses aboard who've been waiting sixty years to testify."

The wind moved the grass. Somewhere up the ridge, a rifle shifted against a shoulder.

"Witnesses to what?" she asked, and her voice had changed. It was quieter. It was the voice of a person standing at the edge of a hole they'd spent a decade pretending wasn't there.

"To Callas Station," I said.

She closed her eyes. When she opened them, they were wet, and furious, and relieved, all three at once, which is a combination I hope never to see on my own face and suspect I already have.

"My name is Sefa Anand," she said. "I was born here, and I'll die here, and for eleven years this whole world has held its breath so that the people we sheltered could go on breathing. You'd better come inside, detective. There are names you need to hear."

"I didn't say I was a detective."

"You didn't have to." She turned and started up the slope. "It's in how you stand. Like you're taking notes on everything, including me."

* * *

They took us in after dark, in ones and twos, through a tunnel entrance hidden under a grain elevator at the edge of the shuttered town—the kind of entrance you could drive past ten thousand times and never see, because it wasn't meant to be seen. Below ground, Meridian opened up like a lung. Tunnels widened into halls, halls into chambers, and the chambers were full of people. Hundreds of them. Thousands, maybe—I lost count in the second hall. Lights powered low and amber. Gardens under grow-lamps, rows and rows of green in the underground dark. Schools, actual schools, children—real children, born in the dark, who stared at the Vespera's de-aged crew with the frank fascination of kids meeting kids, unaware that the newcomers carried sixty years of memory behind their small faces.

"They went dark the year after Callas," Sefa told me, walking me through it. "Word reached us that the fleet was coming to ask questions about the survivors we'd taken in. Not investigate—*ask questions*, the way a man asks questions of a witness before the trial he's already decided. So we made a choice. Every settlement sealed itself. Grids shut down. Broadcasts stopped. Ships in our yards were broken up for scrap and buried. Four million people agreed to disappear, because the alternative was to be found."

"All of them? Everyone agreed?"

She laughed, and there was no humor in it at all. "Some didn't. Some wanted to fight. Some wanted to talk. There were... difficulties, in the early years. But you'd be amazed what people can agree to when the story is 'the fleet killed three hundred workers at a station and called it an accident, and we are next.' Fear is a wonderful organizer. Terrible houseguest, but a wonderful organizer."

"And the survivors? The Callas survivors you sheltered?"

"In the deep halls. Come."

She led me down, and down, past the gardens and the schools, into older tunnels cut rougher, and there the character of the place changed. Quieter. Slower. People moved through these corridors the way people move through a hospital or a memorial—carefully, softly, as though the walls themselves were grieving.

They brought me into a chamber that had been a cistern once, and now was something else. Along its curved stone walls, shelf after shelf after shelf, were books. Actual paper books, hand-bound, thousands of them, their spines faded and their corners soft with handling. And in the center of the chamber, on a plain wooden table under a single lamp, lay one book open, and beside it a pen, and beside the pen a small brass bell.

Sefa lifted the open book and turned it toward me.

Names. Columns of names, handwritten, in dozens of different hands—some sure, some shaky, some in ink gone brown with age. Each name had a date. Each date had a place. *Callas Station, Ring C. Callas Station, Life Support Control. Callas Station, Docking Bay Two.* Three hundred and twelve names, and beneath them, in fresher ink, other names—survivors, recorded as they arrived, and beside some of those, dates of deaths since, natural ones, peaceful ones, the gentle mortality that the three hundred and twelve had been denied.

"This is what the fleet erased," Sefa said. "Not just the event. The *people*. We wrote them back. One at a time. By hand. Because a thing written by hand can't be overwritten by a records office." She set the book down gently, the way you set down something that has been entrusted to you. "Sixty years, detective. Every survivor who could remember a name, we wrote it. Every relative who came to us, we asked. This book is the massacre. Not the fleet's version. The true one."

I touched the page. My hand was shaking, and I let it shake, because it seemed important not to pretend otherwise. Somewhere in those columns were names my original had chased. Names she'd died within arm's reach of proving. I was standing in a hole in the ground on a world that had erased itself, looking at the only complete record of an atrocity the most powerful institution in human space had spent sixty years burying—and it was written in pencil margins and brown ink by farmers in the dark.

"It's beautiful," I said, and meant it, and heard how strange that sounded.

"It's not enough," Sefa said. "That's what you came to tell me, isn't it? I can see it in your face. A book proves grief. It doesn't prove guilt. The fleet will call it forgery. They'll call us liars, or mad, or both. They've had sixty years of practice calling us nothing at all." She looked up at me, and behind her the shelves stretched away into the lamplight, thousands of books, a civilization's stubborn handwriting. "What do you have that they can't erase?"

And I thought of the Vespera sitting in the dry lakebed under the stars, carrying her crew of small witnesses with their intact minds. I thought of the logs with their overwritten falsehoods, the erasure signature matching my original's file stroke for stroke. I thought of Admiral Rehn Voss's smile, and the forty days ticking down on a truce that was really just a pause between massacres.

"I have a ship," I said slowly, "that remembers everything. But her memories live in cores, and cores can be called forged too, unless—" I stopped. Because I'd known this since the silence zone; I'd just been refusing to say it out loud. "Unless we authenticate them against physical evidence. Against the station itself. Against the black box Voss's purge missed."

Sefa frowned. "Callas Station still exists?"

"Decaying orbit," Tam said from the doorway behind us—he'd followed me down, of course he had, the kid was constitutionally incapable of letting me out of his sight. His face had gone pale in the lamplight. "I know where the box is. I've always known. I sealed the compartment myself." He swallowed. "I remember the door, Paloma. I remember everything about the door."

Sefa looked between us, reading the shape of the thing before either of us said it aloud, the way people do when they've lived long enough with a truth to recognize its silhouette.

"Then," she said quietly, "you're going back to the place where they died."

Above us, four million people slept in the dark, holding their breath. On the truce clock, thirty-eight days remained. And in a decaying orbit somewhere above a world that had erased itself to survive, a dead station circled, keeping the last secret of the last witness—the box, and whatever was inside it, waiting in the cold for someone brave enough, or foolish enough, to come and open the door.
