## 13. The Survivors' Ledger

The elders of Meridian received us in a room with no windows and too many chairs.

That was the first thing I noticed—the chairs. Rows of them, wooden, hand-planed, arranged facing a long table like an audience waiting for a performance that had been postponed for eleven years. Somebody had dusted them every week anyway. Somebody had believed, all this time, that eventually someone would come to sit in them.

I counted forty-one before Okafor tugged my sleeve.

"Paloma." She said my name the way she said everything now—carefully, a child's voice handling adult cargo. "They're waiting for you."

"I know. I'm stalling."

"You're counting chairs."

"Also stalling."

She almost smiled. It was strange, watching her face learn expressions again—a sixty-year-old captain's humor trying to fit inside cheeks that had never held it. The reversal hadn't just taken her body back to eight years old; it was slowly taking her mannerisms with it, sanding her down toward the child she'd once been. Some days that terrified me more than Voss did.

The eldest of the Meridian council was a woman named Sefa Anselm, who looked at me the way you look at weather coming in off a ridge—assessing damage potential. She was small, silver-haired, with hands that had clearly done decades of actual work, and when she spoke, the whole room leaned in, because apparently that's what happens on a world that has spent a decade pretending not to exist.

"Dunmore," she said. "That name means something here."

"It means something everywhere," I said. "Usually followed by paperwork."

"No." Her eyes didn't move from mine. "Detective Dunmore. The one who ran the route. The one who came asking about Callas Station, twenty-two years ago, three weeks before they killed her."

The room went very quiet. Somewhere behind me, Tam made a sound like he'd been struck.

I had known, in the abstract, that my original had died investigating this. I had read her file, or what remained of it. But there is a difference between knowing a thing and hearing it spoken aloud by strangers in a room full of empty chairs, and the difference is roughly the width of a grave.

"She came here?" I managed.

"She came here," Sefa said. "She sat where you're sitting. She asked us for names. We gave her some of them. Then her ship went dark between here and the fleet relay, and the fleet told us she'd been lost to navigational error, and we understood exactly what kind of error that was." She folded her hands. "So we stopped speaking. All of us. Whole planet, dark. Because the last person who spoke got erased, and the people she spoke for were already erased, and we decided that silence was the only language left that couldn't be forged."

* * *

The ledger lived in a vault beneath the council hall, and they brought it up the way you'd bring up a relic—which is to say, four of them carried it, and everyone else stood.

It wasn't a data core. It wasn't even a proper book, really. It was a bound stack of ship's salvage paper, the cheap fibrous stock freighters use for cargo manifests, sewn together with wire and covered in something that might have started life as a tarp. On its cover, in ink gone brown, someone had written: **FOR THE ONES THE FLEET SAID WERE NEVER THERE.**

Sefa opened it on the table under a work lamp, and I understood then why they'd built this room with no windows. You don't want daylight touching a thing like this. Daylight makes everything look ordinary.

"There are two thousand one hundred and six names," she said. "Written by hand. Verified twice each against survivors' testimony, station manifests recovered from debris, and personal effects. We started the night we took in the first boats from Callas. We have not stopped adding since—not because anyone new has died, but because we keep remembering people the first lists missed."

Two thousand one hundred and six. The fleet's official record of Callas Station listed a crew complement of zero, because officially there was no Callas Station. It appeared in no charts, drew no supply contracts, employed no one. A labor station processing ore for the colonial shipyards, staffed entirely by colonists the fleet had quietly conscripted—and when they rose up over conditions, when they locked themselves in the command ring and refused to load another shipment, someone with enough rank to make the decision had simply cut the air.

I knew that part already. What I didn't know, what no file could have given me, was the handwriting.

Because that's what a ledger is, when you get down to it. Not information. Handwriting. Someone sat with a pen and wrote *Oyelaran, Bisi — hydroponics — survived by a daughter, location unknown* and then sat with the fact of it until their hand stopped shaking enough to write the next line. Two thousand times. You can't overwrite that with a bland falsehood. You can't issue a correction notice to ink.

"May I?" I asked.

Sefa turned the book toward me, and I read.

I read for a long time. Long enough that Okafor came and stood beside me, and Tam came too, and none of us said anything, because there was nothing to say that the book wasn't already saying better.

Some entries were just names and stations. Others had notes in the margins—little human marginalia that broke my heart in ways the main text somehow didn't. *Had a laugh like a door slamming.* *Taught my mother to weld.* *Was going home in March.* One entry, near the middle, had a pressed flower taped beside it, flattened to a brown ghost of itself, and I had to put my hand flat on the table for a moment and breathe.

"The fleet erased the station," Sefa said, watching me read. "We are putting it back. One name at a time. That is the whole of our resistance, Detective. That is everything Meridian has done for eleven years. We kept a list."

"It's beautiful," I said. And then, because I owed her more than beauty: "And it proves nothing."

Her jaw tightened, but she nodded. She'd known. Of course she'd known. You don't spend eleven years fighting an institution without learning exactly how strong your weapons are.

"Handwriting can be faked," I went on. "Paper can be aged. Testimony can be coached. If I walk into a tribunal with this book and nothing else, Voss's lawyers will have it discredited before lunch, and then the fleet will know precisely where the last copy of the truth lives, and Meridian goes dark permanently—or worse, stops being dark." I closed the ledger gently, like shutting a casket. "What we need is corroboration from a source the fleet itself authenticated. Something with fleet-grade encryption, fleet-certified timestamps, chain of custody. Something they can't call a forgery because they built the standard it would be judged against."

"The ship," Okafor said quietly.

I turned. She was standing very straight, small hands clasped behind her back in a posture I'd seen in her logs—a captain's parade rest, absurd and magnificent on an eight-year-old frame.

"The Vespera answered our distress call," she said. "Fleet-registered, fleet-maintained, fleet-certified recorder cores. Whatever happened to us happened *to* a fleet ship, logged by fleet instruments, on fleet time. They can erase a colony's ledger. They can't erase their own ship's memory without admitting the ship exists." Her voice cracked slightly on the last words, the child's throat failing the old man's anger. "Everything we saw. Everything we heard. It's still in those cores. The reversal preserved it—that's the whole horror of what they did to us. They wanted to scramble the witnesses, and instead they made witnesses who can't die of old age before testifying."

"And the cores need authentication," Sefa said. "Sealed, witnessed, certified."

"By an authority both sides will accept," I said. "Which is the problem. Any fleet authority is compromised. Any colonial authority, Voss will claim is partisan."

The room was silent again. Forty-one empty chairs and one impossible problem.

Then Tam spoke, from the back, in a voice so small I almost missed it.

"What about the box?"

Everyone turned. He flinched—I'd learned that flinch, the way sound landing on him made him want to be smaller—but he didn't stop.

"At Callas Station. There was a black box. Flight recorder, structural recorder, life-support telemetry—all of it, one unit, in the command ring. Fleet-standard. Fleet-sealed." His hands were shaking. Okafor moved toward him, slow, the way you move toward a spooked animal, but he kept going. "When they cut the air, the box kept recording. It records until the power dies, and the station had backup cells rated for—rated for weeks. It recorded everything. I know because sealing the command ring was my job, and the box was in the room I sealed, and I have spent—" his breath hitched, "—eleven years and one bad childhood hearing that box in my sleep."

He looked up at me, and his eyes were old in a way no child's eyes should ever be.

"If it's still there—if the station is still there—it's the source. Fleet-built. Fleet-sealed. Physical. You can't call it a forgery if you dig it out of the wreck yourself."

* * *

Later, in the guest quarters Meridian offered us—real beds, real gravity, the shocking luxury of a planet that stayed where you left it—I sat with the ledger open on my knees and did arithmetic.

Callas Station had been abandoned in its decaying orbit for twenty-two years. Orbital decay around a gas giant's outer moons was slow; the station could plausibly still be there, a dead hulk tumbling through the dark, its backup cells long dead but its memory intact. Recording everything. Waiting.

Waiting for someone to go and get it.

"You're doing the face," Okafor said. She was sitting cross-legged on the floor, ostensibly reviewing the Vespera's remaining fuel margins, actually watching me the way captains watch officers they've already made decisions about.

"What face?"

"The face where you've decided something terrible and are hoping someone will talk you out of it, while having already arranged matters so no one can."

"I haven't decided anything."

"Paloma." She closed her tablet. "I commanded ships for thirty years. I know a pre-briefing when I see one, even when the officer giving it doesn't know she's giving it yet." She climbed onto the chair across from me—it was a climb; the chair was adult-sized and she was not—and folded her hands on the table, and for a moment the dissonance of her was so complete it hurt. Eight years old. Captain's eyes. "Tell me about the station."

So I told her. The orbit, the decay math, the backup cells, the box. And she listened the way she listened to everything, completely, and when I finished she was quiet for a moment.

"My original—" I started.

"Your original came here and died for these names," Okafor said. "You came here and found them. I don't think that's coincidence, and I don't think it's inheritance either. I think it's the case choosing its detective. It chose her, and when the fleet killed her, it waited." She tapped the ledger's cover, once, softly. "It waited for someone who wouldn't stop."

"That's not how cases work."

"No," she agreed. "It's how people work. Cases are just the shape the people leave behind."

Through the wall, faintly, I heard Tam in the next room, talking in his sleep. Not screaming anymore—Meridian's doctors had given him something for that—but murmuring, running through the station's corridors the way he did every night, past the door he'd sealed, past the box that had listened to everything.

One name at a time, the elders had written their grief into paper. One dream at a time, Tam was writing the station's layout into something we could navigate by.

And somewhere out in the dark, a dead station tumbled through its slow fall, holding two thousand voices in a metal box, waiting for us to come and make them speak.

I closed the ledger and looked at Okafor.

"How do you feel about a salvage operation?" I said. "In a decaying orbit. Inside a massacre site. With a fleet corvette presumably still looking for us?"

She smiled, and it was the saddest, bravest thing I'd seen in two lifetimes.

"Captain Okafor," she said, "has always wanted to go back for the black box."
