## 5. The Garden That Waits

The station hung in Verity's shadow like a seed pod split open, its solar petals half-deployed, its hull furred with the first stubborn lichen Emrys had ever seeded. *Meridian*, the registries still called it. He had called it home for six years, back when the war was a rumor on the far side of the sun and his whole world was humidity gauges and germination trays and the slow, holy patience of things that grow.

"It's dead," Vess said, her voice flat in the tug's cramped cabin. "Your beacon's dark. There's no heat signature. If we dock there, we're docking in a tomb."

"It's sleeping." Emrys brought the tug around, letting Verity's crescent fill the viewport — the half-terraformed moon, its southern hemisphere gone green-brown and breathing, its north still raw regolith and salt plains. "I put it into deep maintenance myself, three years ago, when the Coalition requisitioned all terraformer personnel. The station's not dead. It's dormant. There's a difference."

"There's always a difference," Vess said, "right up until there isn't."

He glanced at her. In the three days since she'd burned her career to let him slip the interception net, he had learned to read her silences the way he read soil — by texture, by what resisted and what gave. She was afraid. She was also, he realized, not going to say so.

"Vess. The vaults are shielded under forty meters of regolith and a lattice of basalt. No fleet sensor short of a full geological survey finds them. And the message—" he touched his own sternum, where the encoded weight of it sat behind the bone like a second heartbeat, "—the message needs somewhere quiet for me to sleep and dream and listen to what my own skeleton is trying to say. Your fighter can't give me that. This can."

She didn't answer. She ran the approach vector twice, found it clean, and brought them down through Verity's thin new atmosphere with the economy of a woman who had decided that if she was going to die, she'd die well.

* * *

The docking arm accepted them with a groan of cold metal remembering its purpose. Inside, the station smelled the way Emrys remembered — wet mineral and green chlorophyll and the faint ozone bite of the hydroponics loop. Dormant, yes. But alive. Somewhere in the dark, pumps were still turning over on trickle power, keeping the root zones from freezing, keeping the whole buried organism of the place breathing at the slowest possible rate.

He walked the corridors with his hand on the wall, and it was like walking through a memory that had kept growing without him. Vess followed, sidearm drawn out of habit, though there was nothing to shoot but shadows.

"Emrys." Her voice came strange. "What is this place?"

He keyed open the main vault hatch, and the lights rose.

Sixty meters long, thirty wide, tiered like an amphitheater — and every tier filled with vaults. Not the cold cryo-racks of the fleet, but living architecture: sealed glass columns of soil, each one threaded with dormant mycelium, each one holding seeds by the tens of thousands. Wheat bred for low gravity. Rice that drank brine. Trees whose wood was engineered to hold oxygen like a lung. And at the far end, the deep vaults — the ones for the colonists' children, the ones that would not be opened for fifty years, a hundred, until the people waking from the sleep-ships were ready to put their hands in dirt that had been waiting for them since before they were born.

"The Garden That Waits," Emrys said. "That's what the crew called it. Every vault is addressed. Not to a person — to a generation. *To whoever wakes after us.*" He laughed, and it came out cracked. "I used to do the inventory checks alone. I'd read the labels out loud just to hear how they sounded. *To whoever wakes after us.* Like a letter you write to someone you'll never meet."

Vess had gone still in the doorway. She was staring at the vaults the way she'd stared at the transmission signature three days ago — as if something in her had been recognized.

"My brother," she said. "Tomas. He was a signals analyst before he was a pilot. He found fragments of Earth's last transmissions in the debris fields — the real ones, not the fleet's sanitized archive. He said Earth had sent one final message before the ark fleet burned. One message, looped, addressed to no one and everyone." Her jaw tightened. "Command told him the fragments were corrupted. Then they told him to stop looking. Then he died chasing a debris-field rumor, and they gave him a posthumous commendation and sealed his file."

"And now the message is in my bones."

"And now the message is in your bones." She holstered the sidearm. "Show me how the vaults are catalogued. If we're going to hide here, I want to know every exit, every blind spot, and how much of this garden survives if the station burns."

The question landed like a stone in still water. Emrys looked at the tiers of sleeping seeds and understood what she was really asking — not *how do we defend this place*, but *what are we willing to lose*. He had already lost one garden. Meridian's sister station, Thessaly, had been in the first orbital strike of the war, six years of soil culture and a hundred thousand sealed futures turned to expanding vapor in under a minute. He had watched it from this very viewport.

"Come on," he said. "I'll show you the roots."

* * *

They worked through the station's night cycle, and the work did what work always did — it made the fear into something with edges, something that could be held. Emrys showed her the basalt lattice, the geothermal tap, the vaults' independent power and atmosphere. Vess stripped the tug's transponder and wired its decoy beacons into the station's outer hull, so that any sensor sweep would see a derelict, not a refuge. They ate rehydrated greens from the emergency stores — actual food, grown from actual soil, and Vess closed her eyes at the first bite like a woman receiving communion.

"It tastes like before," she said.

"Everything here tastes like before."

In the deep vault, Emrys lay down on a cot between two seed columns and let himself sleep, because the message demanded it. He dreamed the way he always dreamed now: of Earth, of a blue horizon he had never seen, of a voice that was not a voice — a structure, a pattern, meaning woven into him at the frequency of bone. And in the dream the message said what it always said, the thing he had decoded in fragments over three sleepless nights aboard the tug:

*The ark fleet was not destroyed by the Coalition. The launch was real. the strike was staged. Earth's own command burned its own children to give the war its founding wound. Proof follows. Proof is the carrier. Proof is you.*

He woke with salt on his face and Vess sitting beside him, watching, her hand hovering near his shoulder without landing.

"You said it out loud," she told him quietly. "In your sleep. The whole fragment. I recorded it." Her voice shook on the next words. "Emrys, my brother died for this. He *died* for this, and I spent six years shooting at the wrong enemy."

"You weren't shooting at the wrong enemy," Emrys said. "You were shooting at the people who were also lied to. That's the whole architecture of the thing. That's how it holds itself up."

She looked at him for a long moment. Then she said, "The seed vaults. The ones for the children. If the truth never lands — if we die out here and the message dies in your bones — those kids wake up into the war's hundredth year and plant gardens in soil that's soaked in a lie." She stood. "I understand now what you're carrying. It's not a transmission. It's an inheritance."

* * *

The Coalition hunters came at the turn of the cycle, and they came quiet.

Emrys felt it before the alarms spoke — a pressure change in the docking ring, the faint wrongness of a hull matching their hull. Then the station's ancient sensors woke and screamed, and Vess was already moving, already keying the tug's decoy suite, already shouting the numbers he'd need.

"Three contacts, low orbit, dropping fast. Interdiction pattern — they know someone's here. The decoys will buy us minutes, not hours."

"Vaults are shielded," Emrys said, pulling the message-fragments from the vault's analysis deck, sealing them into a case the size of a book. "The station can burn and the seeds survive. We survive. We go out through the geothermal conduit—"

"Emrys." Vess caught his arm. Through the viewport above the vault tiers, the first strike was already falling — a lance of white against Verity's night side, and then the station's outer ring lit up like a struck bell. "They're not here for us. Look at their pattern. They're not trying to capture. They're sterilizing."

The second strike took the hydroponics ring. Emrys felt it through the floor, a deep structural moan, and somewhere above them six years of green went to fire. He thought of Thessaly. He thought of the crew, scattered to fleet requisitions, of the labels in the vaults — *to whoever wakes after us* — and of the voice in his bones that had crossed interstellar space to reach him and could not be allowed to die in a burning garden.

The third strike hit the reactor shielding. The lights died. In the red emergency glow, Vess shoved the data case into his hands and pointed down the conduit hatch.

"Go. I'm right behind you."

They ran through the roots of the world — down through basalt and regolith, through the geothermal conduit that would carry them to a maintenance lock two kilometers east, under the burning station, under the hunters' guns. Behind them, Meridian died in stages: the docking arm first, then the solar petals, then the long bright roar of atmosphere venting from the hydroponics ring into Verity's thin sky.

At the conduit's first junction, Emrys stopped. He could feel the heat of the fire through forty meters of stone, and he could hear — or imagined he heard — the vaults above him, sealed and shielded and patient, holding their hundred thousand futures in the dark.

"They'll survive," Vess said beside him, breath ragged. "The vaults. You built them to survive."

"Yes." His voice came out steady, and he was surprised by it. "They'll survive. That was the whole point of them." He looked at her, at the firelight bleeding red through the stone behind them, and the grief in him turned over and became something with a direction. "They burned one garden to stop a message. They'll burn a hundred. But the message isn't in the garden, Vess. It's in me. And there's a place where it has to land — a place with an audience too big to silence. Halcyon. The seed-vault station. The sleeping colonists."

Vess went very still. "That's the most guarded orbit in the system. Both fleets are converging on it."

"Then we'd better be faster than both of them."

They came out of the maintenance lock into Verity's grey dawn, the burning station a smudge of smoke against the stars, and Emrys Okafor stood on the soil he had made and made a vow to it: *I lost Thessaly. I will not lose this.* Behind them, the hunters' ships rose from the ruin, already broadcasting — and the broadcast cut through every open channel, a Coalition voice with command authority behind it:

*"All vessels in the Kepler-442 system be advised: Halcyon station is now under interdiction. No traffic will be permitted to approach. Repeat: no traffic will be permitted—"*

Emrys looked at Vess. Vess looked at the sky.

"They're sealing the cradle," she said. "Both fleets will be there within two days."

"Then we have two days," Emrys said, "and a stolen tug, and the truth in my bones. Let's go introduce it to the children."
