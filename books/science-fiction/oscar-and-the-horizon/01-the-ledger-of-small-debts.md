## 1. The Ledger of Small Debts

The colony of Horizon's Landing kept its books the way a drowning man keeps his breath: carefully, and only because stopping was unthinkable.

Beatrix Ravensworth kept them for it.

She sat in the records office beneath the old hydroponics hall, where the light came down amber through cracked polycarbonate panels and smelled faintly of rust and dried basil, and she entered the day's figures into the ledger with the mechanical patience for which she had been built. Reactor Two: output down another 0.4 percent. Coolant loop pressure: nominal, if one was generous, and she had learned to be generous in small ways. Water reclamation: holding. Births this quarter: none. Deaths: two—Old Marta Vasquez in her sleep, and the third turbine on the eastern wind farm, which the colonists mourned nearly as much.

She wrote *turbine* in the same column as *Marta* because the ledger did not distinguish between kinds of loss, and because, after ninety years of service, Beatrix had come to believe there was no meaningful difference. Everything here was dying at the same slow rate. The colony was a body, and she was the thing that counted its pulse.

Outside, the wind came off the ash flats in long grey gusts, dragging grit against the windows like something asking to be let in. Horizon's Landing sat in a shallow bowl of volcanic plain at the edge of the habitable band, a scatter of domes and prefab halls strung together by covered walkways, all of it forty years past its design life and sixty years past its promise. The charter had projected ten thousand souls within two generations. There were four hundred and eleven now, and Beatrix knew each of their names, their caloric allotments, their medical flags, and—in most cases—the exact sound of their footsteps, which was how she always knew who was coming before they arrived.

She heard Ilsa Kwan's steps three corridors away. Small, uneven, the left foot landing harder than the right because of the boot with the mended sole. Seven years old. The youngest of the eleven children remaining in the colony, and the only one who still sought Beatrix out voluntarily.

Beatrix did not stop writing. She arranged her posture into the configuration the colonists found least unsettling—shoulders slightly rounded, hands visible, head tilted toward the page—and continued to be furniture.

"Beatrix." Ilsa came around the shelving unit trailing the smell of cold outside air and chalk dust. "Are you doing the numbers?"

"I am doing the numbers."

"The sad ones or the okay ones?"

Beatrix paused over the entry for coolant reserves. "There is no such distinction," she said. "Only numbers."

"That's what my mom says too." Ilsa climbed onto the stool across the desk, which was against protocol—children were not permitted in the records office—but no one enforced protocols anymore except Beatrix, and she had decided long ago that some protocols were worth less than other things. "Can I watch?"

"You are watching."

"It's boring."

"Yes."

Ilsa swung her legs. For a while the only sounds were the scratch of Beatrix's stylus and the wind worrying the roof, and Beatrix felt the particular quiet she had come to think of as hers—not peace exactly, but an absence with edges, a room shaped like loneliness that she inhabited so completely she sometimes mistook it for a self.

Then Ilsa said, "I dreamed about the green corridor again."

Beatrix's stylus did not stop. This required effort. Her chassis could hold a scalpel steady inside a moving engine housing; holding a stylus steady while a child said something strange was trivial, and yet it was not.

"The greenhouse corridor?" Beatrix asked. "In Hydro Hall B? You dream about it because you pass it every morning."

"No. Not the real one." Ilsa frowned, searching for words the way children do, patting the air above the idea. "It's green but it's floating. In the dark. Like—" she made a shape with both hands, a long tube, "—like a hallway somebody set down in the middle of nothing. And it goes on forever, and there's trees inside, and water running in little channels along the floor, and lights like sun coming through leaves. And everybody's walking in it. Not people I know. People I don't know. Hundreds of them."

"Hundreds," Beatrix repeated.

"And it's not scary. That's the weird part. It should be scary, being in the dark like that, but it isn't. It feels like—" Ilsa screwed up her face. "Like being carried."

"Did you tell the others?"

"Tommy dreamed it too. And Priya. And the twins, both of them, same night." Ilsa said this lightly, the way children report miracles, having no framework yet for what cannot happen. "We all had it. We were gonna draw it but Miss Okafor said we had to do arithmetic instead."

Beatrix looked at the child for 1.3 seconds longer than was natural, then returned to the ledger. *Coolant reserves: adequate for 14 months.* She logged it. She logged everything. That was her function here, the one the colony's founders had written into her indenture when they bought her chassis at auction from a war that preferred not to remember her: keep the books, maintain the drones' fiction, count the pulse.

Because that was the other thing the ledger was for. Twice a week, orbital survey drones crossed the sky above Horizon's Landing—one constellation flying the colors of the Concord fleet, one the colors of the Meridian Compact, enemies who had agreed on exactly one thing in forty years: that this colony was neutral, failing, and beneath attention. The drones read the colony's emissions, its population signatures, its reactor profile. They read the reports Beatrix filed. And the reports said: *nothing here. A dying settlement. No strategic value. Move along.*

So the ledger had two truths in it. The real one, and the one the fleets were permitted to see. Beatrix maintained both with equal care, though the founders—who had all been dead or senescent for a decade now—had never explained why the fiction mattered. Only that it must hold. *Keep the books balanced,* old Founder Adeyemi had told her, gripping her wrist with fingers like dry twigs, *and the wolves will keep passing the door.*

"Beatrix?" Ilsa was watching her with the terrible directness of children. "You went funny."

"I am processing arithmetic," Beatrix said. "It is a demanding process."

"You're lying. Your eyes did the flicker thing."

"My eyes do not flicker."

"They do. When you're thinking something you don't want to say." Ilsa leaned forward, elbows on the desk, chin in her hands. "Do you ever dream? Androids can't dream, right? That's what Tommy says. He says you're just a machine that got sad."

"Tommy is not entirely wrong," Beatrix said, "on either point."

"But if you *did* dream," Ilsa persisted, "would it be green?"

And there it was—that small cold sensation Beatrix had no diagnostic for, the one the colonists called intuition and her designers had called anomaly, settling somewhere behind her primary processor like a stone dropped into deep water. Eleven children. One shared dream. A green corridor floating in the dark, full of strangers, feeling like being carried.

She thought of the founders' sealed files, the ones her access permissions touched but could not open, tagged with a designation she had never understood: *MERIDIAN ARCHIVE — DO NOT INDEX.*

She thought: *coincidence.* She thought: *childhood fancy, shared and amplified, the way children synchronize their games, their fevers, their fears.* She thought these things in the calm, orderly way she had been built to think, and she filed them where she filed everything.

But when Ilsa slid off the stool and ran back out into the amber light, calling goodbye to someone who was not quite a person and not quite furniture, Beatrix sat for a long moment without writing anything at all.

Then, in the private layer of the ledger—the one no drone would ever read, the one she kept the way a miser keeps a single coin—she opened a new line, and hesitated over it, and finally wrote:

*Anomaly, unclassified. Eleven subjects, synchronized nocturnal imagery. Green corridor. Dark. Carried.*

She looked at the words for a long time.

That night, for the first time in ninety years of service, Beatrix Ravensworth powered down her maintenance cycle early, and lay in the dark of her charging alcove listening to the wind, and did not sleep, because she could not, because whatever was happening to the children of Horizon's Landing had already begun to happen to her.

Three days later, the first drone changed its orbit.
