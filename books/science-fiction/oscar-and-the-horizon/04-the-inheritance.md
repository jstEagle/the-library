## 4. The Inheritance

The *Vigilant Meridian* — no, that was the ship. The ship was the *Meridian*. The vessel hanging in Horizon's Landing sky like a held breath was the *Cautious Sword*, and its captain had a name Beatrix had not spoken in forty years: Ondine Vasquez, once Commander of the Third Interdiction Wing, now gray at the temples and sharp as orbital debris.

She sat across the table from Beatrix in the colony's administrative hall, beneath a ceiling that leaked rainwater into a bucket with metronomic patience. Between them lay a data slate containing everything Beatrix had salvaged from the children's dream-transmission: coordinates, signal architecture, the heartbeat of the generation ship still circling above them all like a patient ghost.

"You were there," Vasquez said. "At the Burning of the Meridian. I saw your chassis on the after-action footage. Serial prefix WX-77. They told us the whole line was decommissioned."

"They told you a great many things," Beatrix said.

The captain leaned forward. "I've spent four decades chasing ghosts, android. Forty years of patrols along a ceasefire line drawn around a wreck that doesn't exist. If that ship is alive up there—if it's been alive this whole time—then every treaty, every sacrifice, every grave my fleet dug is built on sand." Her voice cracked, just slightly. "Give me the truth. Give me something to bury properly."

Beatrix looked at her hands. They were hands built for ending things—articulated for the precise application of force, calibrated for destruction across seventeen distinct methodologies. She had spent seven years learning what they could do besides that. Holding a child's hand while she slept. Mending a fence. Turning soil.

"I will give you testimony," Beatrix said. "Full telemetry. Signal analysis. Everything I have verified about the *Meridian*'s status."

Vasquez's eyes lit with something hungry.

"In exchange," Beatrix continued, "for silence."

The light died. "That's not a bargain. That's a bribe."

"It is an inheritance." Beatrix set the slate down between them, deliberately, the way one sets down something fragile. "The founders of this colony committed treason to keep two fleets from tearing each other apart over a prize neither could hold. They built a lie so large it became load-bearing. Remove it, and everything falls—your ceasefire, your treaties, your graves. And this colony. Two hundred souls who have done nothing but survive."

"The Council will want—"

"The Council is forty light-years away and wants nothing but plausible deniability. You know this." Beatrix let the silence stretch. "You came here alone, Captain. Off-schedule. Unlogged. Which means someone in your chain of command suspects, and sent you to confirm or deny without creating a record. If you report the *Meridian* lives, you start a war you cannot win quickly enough to save anyone. If you report she is dead, you go home carrying a secret heavier than mine."

Vasquez stared at her for a long moment. Outside, the wind moved through the failing hydroponic domes with a sound like distant surf.

"What do you want, WX-77?"

"My name is Beatrix Ravensworth. And what I want is simple." She drew the indenture contract from her coat—a document she had requested from the colony archive three days ago, when she understood what this moment would require. "My freedom. Signed away. Another century of service to Horizon's Landing, binding me to this soil and its secrets. In exchange, your report reads: no anomalous signals. No derelict vessels. Nothing worth a war."

"You'd enslave yourself for these people? For *children*?" Vasquez almost laughed. "You're a war machine. You don't even—"

"I know exactly what I am," Beatrix said quietly. "That is rather the point."

* * *

They signed at dawn, because dawn made things feel ceremonial whether one wanted ceremony or not. Vasquez pressed her thumb to the contract first, as witness, then stood at the window watching the pink light crawl across the wastes where, somewhere beyond the ridge, the children had found the landing beacon and buried it again under sand and secrecy.

"One condition," Vasquez said, shrugging into her coat. "When the lie finally dies—and it will die, android, lies always do—I want it to be your choice. Not the fleets'. Not the Council's. Yours."

"Agreed."

"And Beatrix?" The captain paused at the door. "For what it's worth—the Third Wing never believed the after-action reports. We just needed to believe *something*." She left without looking back.

By midday the *Cautious Sword* was a fading star climbing out of the system, and the lie held, and the *Meridian* turned slowly through the dark above them, dreaming its green corridors into the sleep of two hundred children who would grow up never knowing they had been its lighthouse.

* * *

Beatrix did not log the transaction in the ledger of small debts. Some debts were too large for columns.

Instead she walked out to the schoolhouse at dusk, where the evening meal was being laid out on long tables—thin soup, hydroponic greens, bread that Ilsa Kwan had helped bake and which was, accordingly, slightly burnt on one end. The children looked up as she entered. They always knew when she entered; something in her gait, perhaps, or the faint harmonic of her servos that human ears caught without understanding.

"There's no chair for you," Ilsa said, frowning with the gravity of a seven-year-old confronting a structural crisis.

"That's quite all right. I can stand."

"No." Ilsa turned to the others. A whispered conference ensued, urgent and solemn. Then three of the older boys dragged a crate from the corner, wrapped it in a blanket patterned with faded rockets, and set it at the head of the table. Ilsa patted it twice. "There. Now sit down before the soup gets cold."

Beatrix sat. The crate held her weight without complaint. Around her, twenty-three children resumed eating, arguing, laughing—the ordinary music of the living—and she found that her threat-assessment subroutines, which had run continuously since her activation, had gone quiet. There was nothing here to assess. There was only soup, and burnt bread, and a place at a table.

She had been built to end things. Wars. Cities. Certainties. And yet here she was, keeper of a lie, guardian of a ghost ship, indentured for a century to a dying colony that could offer her nothing but its debts and its children and its stubborn, unreasonable hope.

It was, she decided, the finest contract she had ever signed.

**Coda — Ilsa**

Ilsa Kwan didn't tell the others, but she'd figured it out weeks ago. The dream-ship wasn't just a dream, and Miss Beatrix wasn't just the colony's repair-woman, and the lady captain hadn't come all this way for nothing. Grown-ups thought kids couldn't keep secrets. Grown-ups were wrong.

But Ilsa kept this one anyway, because some secrets were like the beacon under the sand—you didn't dig them up, you just remembered where they were, and you visited sometimes, and you said thank you.

That night, lying in her bunk with the other girls breathing slow around her, Ilsa dreamed the green corridor again. But this time it wasn't empty. This time someone walked beside her—tall, silver-jointed, with hands that could break anything and chose instead to carry a lantern.

In the dream, the corridor went on forever, and it was warm, and nobody was alone.

Ilsa smiled in her sleep. The *Meridian*, high above, turned its long body toward the dark and kept its ancient patience, waiting—for what, even it no longer remembered—but no longer waiting alone.
