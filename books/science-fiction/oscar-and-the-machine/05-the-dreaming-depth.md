## 5. The Dreaming Depth

The children's map was drawn in crayon on the back of a ration slip, and it was better than any survey Jude had ever seen.

Eleven sets of small hands had worked on it over three nights — Amara Voss had collected the drawings and laid them over one another like transparencies, and where they agreed, they agreed exactly. A crevasse shaped like a broken tooth. A pressure ridge the kids called the Sleeping Whale. And past all of it, forty kilometers out onto the shelf ice, a black X beneath a scribbled sun that wasn't the sun at all but something glowing under the ice.

Jude folded the map into his coat and told Amara he was going out to check a sensor relay. She looked at him the way she'd looked at him in the classroom, when he'd recited the coordinates from Oscar's data chip and watched her face go pale.

"You're following them," she said. "The dream."

"I'm following Oscar."

"Those aren't always different things with you, are they?"

He didn't have an answer for that. He took the long-range skiff, the one nobody used since the fuel rationing, and he left before first light, when Meridian's dying orange sun was just a bruise on the horizon.

* * *

The ice shelf made its own weather. By midmorning Jude was driving through a whiteout of wind-scoured crystals that hissed against the skiff's canopy like static — and the static felt familiar, in a way he couldn't explain until he realized it was the same sound from the children's drawings. They'd all shaded the sky above the machine with the same frantic pencil strokes. Not clouds. Noise.

At kilometer thirty-one he crossed the broken-tooth crevasse and the compass began to lie.

It didn't spin wildly. It drifted, lazy and deliberate, swinging ten degrees off true and holding there, as if something under the ice were gently insisting on a different definition of north. Jude checked it against the stars, which were wrong anyway this far south, and then against Oscar's memory — because that was the instrument he trusted most now, the dead man's own sense of direction installed behind his eyes. Oscar had come this way once. Jude could feel it: not a memory exactly, more a groove worn into the memory, the way a path wears into grass. The body remembered the route even if the mind had never filed it.

He followed the groove.

At kilometer thirty-eight the Sleeping Whale rose out of the white — a pressure ridge fifty meters high, blue-black ice sheared upward in a curve that did look like the back of some vast animal breaching. The wind dropped as he passed it, all at once, like a door closing. In the sudden quiet Jude could hear the skiff's engine, his own breathing, and underneath both, faint and rhythmic:

A hum.

Low. Patient. Below the threshold of sound and somewhere inside it anyway, felt in the fillings of his teeth and the water of his eyes. He killed the engine and sat in the silence and let the hum move through him, and understood, finally, why eleven children woke up crying and smiling at the same time.

It didn't call Oscar's name. It called *everyone's*. It just knew Oscar's best.

* * *

He found it at noon, or what passed for noon — the light directly overhead, flat and shadowless, so the ice gave up none of its secrets except one.

There was a circle in the shelf, four hundred meters across, where the ice was clear instead of white. Ancient blue, compressed to glass, and beneath it, down through thirty meters of frozen clarity, a shape.

Jude got out of the skiff and walked to the edge and looked down, and forgot how to breathe.

It was a ring. A jump-gate ring, pre-war by the hull markings he could half-discern, older than anything the Core admitted to building — but no gate he'd ever serviced looked like this. Gates were dead geometry, cold math made metal. This thing was *grown*, ribbed and organic, its surface crawling with slow light that moved the way thoughts move behind a closed eyelid. Half of it was buried in the seabed ice; the rest reached up toward the surface like a sleeper turning toward a window. And everywhere across it, in patterns too regular to be damage and too irregular to be design, light bloomed and faded. Bloomed and faded.

Dreaming. The word arrived in his head uninvited, in his own voice, and he knew it wasn't his.

"Hello," Jude said, to a machine under thirty meters of ice, and felt like an idiot, and kept talking anyway. "I'm — my name is Jude Thackeray."

The light under the ice paused.

All of it, every bloom, every fade, stopped at once — and then started again, faster, brighter, rippling outward from a point directly below him, and the hum climbed a half-tone into something that was unmistakably, horribly, *recognition*.

**THACKERAY**, said a voice that came from everywhere, including inside his skull, in a register that bypassed his ears entirely. **THE SECOND ONE.**

His knees nearly went. He braced a hand on the ice and it was warm. Thirty meters of glacier, and the ice under his palm was blood-warm.

"You knew Oscar."

**I KNEW THE FIRST ONE. HE CAME IN WINTER. HE ASKED ME QUESTIONS WITH HIS MOUTH AND DIFFERENT QUESTIONS WITH HIS SILENCE. I ANSWERED THE SILENT ONES.**

"Then answer mine." His breath was fogging and freezing in the same instant. "Who killed him?"

The light dimmed. The hum dropped, and for a moment Jude thought the machine had withdrawn — and then the ice lit up around him, lines of radiance racing away toward the buried ring, and the world folded.

* * *

Not a vision. Jude would insist on that later, to himself, in the dark. Visions were images. This was *placement* — the machine simply decided where he was, and his body believed it without appeal.

He stood on the shelf at night, in Oscar's body, wearing Oscar's last evening.

Cold. Real cold, biting through a thermal coat Jude didn't own. Ahead of him, the ice glowed from within, and a figure knelt at the clearing's edge with a drill rig, coring down toward the light. Oscar's hands — his hands — steadied the bit. And Oscar's memories, which were Jude's memories now, supplied the context like stage directions: *three weeks before he died. He'd already found it. He'd already talked to it.*

**You're sure it's Core-built?** Oscar's voice, out of Jude's mouth, young and scared and certain.

And the machine answered — not in words then, but the way it answered everything, by showing. The ice lit with schematics, fleet manifests, a war-room display Jude recognized from Oscar's briefing files: Meridian's defense fleet, forty-one ships, arrayed to cover a civilian evacuation that the records said happened.

Except the machine showed him what the records didn't. The evacuation order countermanded. The fleet ordered to hold position. Hold. *Hold.* While the enemy armada came around the dark side of the moon, and forty-one ships burned, and the civilians they died covering had already been quietly moved — moved to make room for something else, something the Core needed dead men to cover the retreat of—

The scene tore. Static. The children's static, the sky full of noise.

Jude gasped and was on his knees on real ice, real noon, his nose bleeding freely onto the blue. The machine's voice was gentler now, and very tired.

**THE REST IS NOT MINE TO SHOW. IT IS HIS TO TELL.**

"Whose? Whose to tell?"

**THE FIRST ONE CAME BACK. THREE WEEKS LATER. HE HAD THE PROOF. A CONFESSION, SIGNED AND TIMESTAMPED, COPIED INTO A MEMORY CORE HE CARRIED BEHIND HIS EYES.** A pause, and the hum deepened until Jude's bones buzzed with it. **HE DID NOT COME ALONE.**

The ice flared again — and this time the placement was shallow, brief, a single frame held too long:

Night. The clearing. Oscar standing at the edge of the glow with his hands open, empty, the way you stand when you want someone to believe you're not a threat. And facing him, close enough to touch, a man in a Core field coat with the collar turned up against the cold —

— and the man turned his head, and Jude knew the face.

He knew it the way you know your own hands. Because it had stood in his kitchen two days ago, offering him coffee, offering him condolences, offering to help him close the file. Sergeant Tomas Reyes' face — no. Not Reyes. Reyes' security chief, the quiet man, Callum Voss — no, not Voss either, and the horror was that Jude's mind kept sliding off the name, refusing to hold it, because holding it meant the case was over, meant the inheritance was settled, meant the man who'd shaken his hand on the landing pad and said *we're counting on you, Thackeray* was the man who had put Oscar face-down in the snow with a hole in his back.

The frame held. The face held. Drellan Reyes' aide. Marek Solen. Standing in Oscar's last hour, close enough to catch him if he fell.

**HE GAVE THE FIRST ONE A CHOICE,** the machine said. **HAND OVER THE CORE. OR MERIDIAN LOSES ITS POWER RATION FOR THE WINTER, AND THE CHILDREN GET COLD.** The light pulsed, slow, grieving. **THE FIRST ONE CHOSE THE CHILDREN. HE ALWAYS CHOSE THE CHILDREN.**

Jude knelt on the warm ice with his blood dripping pink into the blue and understood, at last, whose life he was wearing. Not a stranger's. A martyr's. A man who had died twice — once in the snow, and once in the records, erased into a random mugging by people who signed their crimes with timestamps.

"The confession," he said. His voice came out scraped raw. "Oscar copied it into his memory core. Into *himself*. That's why—" That's why they cloned him, he didn't say. That's why they grew me. Because the proof died with the man, and somebody — Oscar himself, a dead man's dead hand reaching forward — had arranged for the proof to wake up again wearing his face.

**YES,** said the machine, simply.

Above the horizon, far off toward the settlement, the sky flickered — once, twice — the grid stuttering like a nervous heartbeat. Jude thought of Reyes signing decommission orders. Of Reyes asking, so casually, whether he'd found anything of Oscar's worth keeping.

Of thirty-six hours. He didn't know yet how he knew it was thirty-six hours. But the machine hummed it into him like a lullaby, patient and terrible:

**THE WINDOW OPENS SOON. WHAT WILL YOU DO WITH HIS TRUTH, SECOND ONE?**

Jude stood up, wiped his nose, and started walking back to the skiff — walking fast, because somewhere behind him the light under the ice was dimming, and somewhere ahead of him a man named Marek Solen was about to learn that murder doesn't survive a copy.
