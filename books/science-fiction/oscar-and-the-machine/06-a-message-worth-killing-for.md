## 6. A Message Worth Killing For

Drellan Reyes had killed a man once before, though the paperwork said otherwise.

It had been eleven years ago, on Ceres Station, in a room that smelled of recycled air and old coffee. A logistics auditor named Pell had found an anomaly in the fleet manifests — a discrepancy of forty-one ships, forty-one crews, forty-one sets of families who had been told their loved ones died with honor at the Battle of Meridian Approach. Pell was not a brave man or a foolish one. He was simply thorough, and thoroughness, in Reyes' experience, was the most dangerous quality a subordinate could possess.

Reyes had not strangled him or shot him. He had done something cleaner. He had written a memo.

*Auditor Pell has demonstrated exceptional aptitude and is hereby reassigned to deep-range survey duty aboard the CSV Long Watch, effective immediately.*

The Long Watch had suffered a reactor casualty fourteen months into its survey. All hands lost. The Core sent condolences to no one, because Pell had no one, which was precisely why he'd been chosen for the audit in the first place.

Reyes remembered the memo more clearly than he remembered his mother's face. It had taken him four minutes to write. He had revised the wording twice — not out of guilt, but because the first draft's phrasing might have invited questions from Personnel. Efficiency demanded precision even in murder. Especially in murder.

He thought about Pell now, standing at the window of the administrative tower on Meridian, watching the ice shelf glitter under a sun that gave less warmth each year. Somewhere out on that white plain, Jude Thackeray was walking toward something he should never have found.

* * *

The report had come in at 0400 local, and Reyes had been awake to receive it, because he was always awake now. Sleep had become a luxury he rationed like everything else on this dying rock.

The drone telemetry was unambiguous. A single figure on foot, moving northeast across the shelf ice along a bearing that terminated — after two hundred and eleven kilometers of featureless glacier — at a set of coordinates that did not appear on any civilian map. Coordinates that appeared in exactly three files in the Core archive, all classified above Reyes' own clearance, all flagged with a designation he had only seen once before in his career: CONTINGENCY MERIDIAN.

The children had led him there. That was the part Reyes found genuinely unsettling, and he resented being unsettled. Eleven colonist children, dreaming in unison of a machine under the ice, calling a dead man's name. He had read Amara Voss's incident reports with the detached interest of a man reading weather data. Anomalies were explainable. Shared dreams among children raised on failing atmospheric processors with leaking coolant lines — there were toxins, there were suggestion effects, there were a dozen tidy explanations he could have put in a report and filed.

But the coordinates matched. And the clone had gone.

"Status on Thackeray," he said to the empty office, and then, because the room's systems were listening, "Query: current position, subject Thackeray, Jude."

The wall display resolved: a thermal ghost crawling across the ice, small and stubborn as a louse on a white sheet. Two hundred kilometers out. Four days back, if he turned around now.

He would not turn around now. Reyes knew this the way he knew the load-bearing walls of his own mind. The clone carried Oscar Thackeray's memories, and Oscar Thackeray had spent the last eight months of his life pulling at a thread that the Core had spent thirty years pretending didn't exist. Clones were supposed to be obedient vessels — that was the entire point of the inheritance program, continuity of expertise without continuity of liability. But nobody had accounted for what happened when you poured a murdered man's obsession into a fresh body and pointed it at the scene of the crime.

Reyes had warned them. He had written the assessment himself, three years ago, when the program proposed re-instantiating Thackeray to close out colonial affairs: *Subject O. Thackeray exhibited persistent investigative fixation prior to termination. Recommend archival storage of engram without deployment.* Headquarters had overruled him. Continuity looked good in the annual review. A familiar face signing the decommission order played better with the holdout colonists than a stranger's.

And so here they were. A dead man's face walking across the ice toward a dead machine, carrying — if Oscar's last transmissions were any indication — enough truth to burn down thirty years of careful architecture.

Reyes poured himself coffee from the carafe that was always warm and always terrible, and began to plan.

* * *

The problem, reduced to its essentials, was simple: information wants to travel, and Meridian was about to lose every road out.

The colony's uplink array handled routine traffic through a relay satellite in decaying orbit — negligible bandwidth, fully monitored, useless for anything large. Real transmission off-world required the jump-gate. That was the irony at the heart of this whole miserable assignment: the gate the Core had officially declared derelict thirty years ago was neither derelict nor entirely dead. It cycled. Once every few years, its ancient systems drew power from sources the Core's engineers had never fully mapped, and for a window measured in hours, it could push a signal through folded space to anywhere in human-settled territory. Unjammable. Untraceable. Undeniable.

The next window opened in nine days. Reyes had confirmed it that morning through back channels, cross-referencing the gate's residual emissions against the cycle tables buried in the wartime files. Nine days was comfortable. Nine days was manageable.

Unless the machine itself chose to transmit.

That was the variable that kept him at the window, watching the ice. The intelligence under the glacier was not a recorder; it was a participant. It had reached into the dreams of eleven children like fingers testing a lock. It had called Oscar by name. If it wanted the confession broadcast — and everything about its behavior suggested that it did — then the jump window wasn't just an opportunity for Jude Thackeray. It was an opportunity for the machine. And machines with grievances did not wait politely for permission.

Which meant the calculation changed. It was no longer *contain the clone*. It was *end the variable entirely*.

Reyes sat down at his desk and opened a file he had hoped never to use: the security annex, the one that existed outside every ledger, funded through a procurement line labeled *atmospheric sensor maintenance*. Six men. Former fleet marines, all of them officially dead or discharged, all of them paid in Core scrip that could only be spent at Core facilities, which made them loyal the way debt makes people loyal. Their team lead answered to the callsign Vantage. Vantage had done work on Ceres Station eleven years ago. Vantage had audited Auditor Pell's travel arrangements without ever knowing why.

He composed the order the way he composed all his orders: precise, deniable, boring.

*Asset Thackeray-J has located sensitive material of concern to ongoing armistice stability. Material must not be transmitted, copied, or permitted to leave the surface. Asset is to be detained and transported to Admin Tower for debriefing. In the event asset has already interfaced with the material —*

He paused, coffee halfway to his mouth.

In the event the material was already inside the clone's head. Because that was how the old technology worked; he'd read the wartime specs. The gate didn't hand you a chip. It wrote itself into you, memory-deep, indistinguishable from recollection. You couldn't confiscate a thing like that. You couldn't redact a man's mind.

You could only stop the man from reaching a transmitter.

*— in the event asset has already interfaced with the material, asset is to be neutralized. Remains to be recovered and incinerated via atmospheric processor intake. Incident to be reported as crevasse fatality during unauthorized excursion.*

Four minutes to write. He revised the wording twice. Not out of guilt. Out of craft.

* * *

There was one more piece, and Reyes handled it personally, because some things you did not delegate.

At 1100 he walked down to the schoolhouse, alone, unarmed, wearing the open, reasonable face he kept polished for exactly these occasions. Amara Voss met him in the doorway with her arms crossed and her chin set at the angle of a woman who had decided in advance not to be managed.

"Administrator," she said. "Come to tell us the power failures are scheduled?"

"The grid is degrading. I've told you the truth about that." He let a beat of silence carry the weight of everything he wasn't saying. "I've actually come about your students. I understand they've been having the dreams."

Something flickered behind her eyes — the quick recalculation of a teacher deciding how much to protect and how much to parley. "Eleven of them. Same dream. Every night."

"That must be frightening for them."

"They're not frightened." Her voice went quiet and fierce. "They're *listening*. There's a difference, Administrator, and I think you know it."

He smiled the smile that had opened a hundred locked doors. "Children are suggestible. Isolation, stress, environmental factors — I'll have the medical team run a full workup, no cost to the colony. But until we understand it, I'd ask you to keep them indoors. The ice shelf is dangerous this time of year. Drifts shift. Crevasses open where none were charted." He held her gaze, mild and sincere. "I'd hate to see any of them wander."

"I keep my students safe," Amara Voss said, in a tone that made it clear she understood exactly what he was telling her, and exactly what she was telling him back.

"Of course you do," Reyes said. "That's why you're the right person for this colony. It's why we're all still here."

He walked back to the tower through the thin cold light, past the greenhouse where the colony's last green things grew under salvaged lamps, past children in the yard who stopped their game to watch him pass — eleven of them, he counted without meaning to, and one of them, a small girl with frost-pale braids, looked at him with an expression no child should have been able to make. Not fear. Recognition. As if she had seen him somewhere before. As if somewhere very far beneath the ice, something had described him perfectly.

Reyes did not break stride. But in the privacy of his own skull, he updated the file.

The machine had shown the children things. Which meant the machine might have shown them other things — faces, names, the shape of what was coming. Loose ends, plural. His order to Vantage would need amending before nightfall.

Back in the tower, he made two calls. The first authorized a "maintenance inspection" of the uplink relay — standard procedure, six hours of downtime, purely coincidental. The second went to Vantage, and it was short.

"Move tonight," Reyes said. "And Vantage — the teacher keeps breathing. For now. Dead teachers make martyrs, and martyrs make questions. But if the children try to leave the settlement—" He watched the thermal ghost of Jude Thackeray crawl another kilometer across the ice, and finished the sentence the way he finished all his sentences, cleanly. "Handle it like the drifts shifted."

He closed the channel and stood a moment in the quiet, listening to the building breathe around him.

Nine days to the window. Four days to intercept. Somewhere under two hundred kilometers of ice, a dead machine was dreaming, and in its dream, Drellan Reyes was already a name on a list.

He went to brief the medical team about the children. There was paperwork to file. There was always paperwork.

The best murders, he had learned long ago on Ceres, were mostly paperwork.
