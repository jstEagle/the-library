## 1. Nine Seconds of Falling

The morning of the ninth of Vesta-Month began the way every morning had begun for eleven years: with the smell of wet basalt and tomato vines, the hum of the nutrient pumps, and the gentle downward insistence of half a standard gravity telling me, as it always did, which way was up.

I had come to trust gravity the way other people trusted God. It never spoke, it never left, and it always told me where I stood.

I was on the third tier of the greenhouse, checking the drip lines on the yam beds, when the universe hiccuped.

There was no warning. There was never going to be a warning — that's the thing about gravity, it doesn't announce itself, it just is, the way the floor is, the way the sky is. One moment I was crouched over the irrigation manifold with a torque wrench in my hand, muttering at a fitting that had developed opinions about staying sealed, and the next moment the fitting, the wrench, the yam beds, the tomato vines, forty years of somebody's careful agricultural planning, and me — all of us were simply *up*.

Not thrown. Not pushed. *Released.* That's the word that keeps coming back to me, even now. Released, like something held too long finally being let go.

The wrench left my hand with the unhurried dignity of an object that has been waiting its whole life for this. I watched it rotate slowly past my face, and I remember thinking, with the strange clarity of a person whose entire world has just stopped making sense: *that's a 12-millimeter socket, and it is floating, and I am floating, and the water is floating, and oh — the water.*

The irrigation lines had ruptured at the couplings. In half a gravity, a leak is a nuisance. In free fall, a leak is a weather system. Water came out of the drip lines in fat trembling spheres, hundreds of them, drifting through the greenhouse like a slow-motion rain that had forgotten which direction rain goes. They caught the grow-lights and turned the whole tier into something almost beautiful — a cathedral of suspended beads, each one holding a small distorted reflection of my own stunned face.

I would like to tell you I appreciated the beauty in the moment. I did not. I thought: *forty years of root stock, drowning in mid-air.*

I grabbed the tier rail — which, thank every bored engineer who ever specified handholds on a station, was exactly where handholds should be — and pulled myself down the aisle, or up the aisle, or *across* the aisle, because down had resigned. Yam seedlings drifted past me in their netting pots, roots trailing like the hair of drowned things. The tomato vines, which I had trained for a decade along guide wires, had begun to coil off them, slow as sleeping snakes, reaching for nothing.

And the fish.

I should explain the fish. Tank Seven, the tilapia, part of the aquaculture loop that keeps the nitrogen cycle honest. In half a gravity, the tilapia are unremarkable fish. In free fall, tilapia discover, with visible horror, that they are not built for the open sky. They hung in their tank in a frozen school, gills working, eyes bulging, a congregation of small silver congregants witnessing a miracle none of them had asked for. One of them — the big male I'd named Aldous, because he always looked vaguely disappointed in me — pressed his nose against the tank glass and stared at me with what I swear was accusation.

"Not my fault," I told him. My voice sounded strange in the zero-G, thinner, like the station itself had gone quiet to listen.

That's when I checked the clock, because a body in free fall starts wanting numbers, anything to hold onto. Station time 0641. I noted it. I'm a keeper; noting things is most of my job.

The gravity came back at 0641 and nine seconds.

I know it was nine seconds because the station's own event log told me so, afterward, in its flat bureaucratic voice. But my body knew it too, in the way it knew the length of the dark between the grow-lights cycling down and up. Nine seconds. I've since spent a great deal of time thinking about nine seconds. It's long enough to fall four hundred meters. It's long enough for light to travel from the Moon to a point a few thousand kilometers short of Earth. It's long enough for a person alone on a station at the edge of everything to feel the floor of her whole life open like a mouth.

When the gravity returned, it returned all at once, everywhere, with a sound I will hear for the rest of my life: the *whump* of an entire greenhouse remembering which way was down. Forty years of water hit the drainage decks. The yam beds settled. The tomato vines came down off their wires in green avalanches. Aldous hit the water of Tank Seven like a dropped stone and shot to the bottom to sulk. And I hit the third-tier grating on my knees, one hand still full of torque wrench — it had come back to me, or I had come back to it, and I've decided not to examine that too closely.

Then the station did the thing stations do when nothing is wrong: nothing. The pumps hummed. The lights cycled. Somewhere in the spine, the air handlers breathed their long mechanical breath. The universe, having hiccuped, pretended it hadn't.

I knelt there in the flooded aisle, soaked to the waist, holding a wrench like a weapon, and I laughed. I want to be honest about that. Eleven years alone does something to a person's sense of proportion, and my first response to a fundamental violation of physical law was a wet, slightly unhinged laugh that echoed off the glass and scared the tilapia all over again.

Then I got up, and I did what keepers do.

* * *

The damage took most of the day to survey, and the survey took most of what I had.

The yam beds survived — root stock is tougher than it looks, and most of the seedlings I could re-seat, their root balls rinsed and tucked back into the medium like patients being re-bandaged. The tomatoes were a loss on the upper tiers; a decade of training, snapped off at the guide wires, and no amount of careful staking was going to convince those vines they'd ever had a direction. I salvaged what cuttings I could and set them in the propagation trays, because that's the whole job in miniature, isn't it? Everything breaks; you root what's left.

Tank Seven lost two fish to the turbulence — not the fall itself, but the chaos after, gills full of air, found floating at morning check. I netted them out and said the small words over the recycling chute, the ones I'd made up years ago because nobody had taught me any. *You grew. You fed the greens. Go on.* Aldous watched me do it from the bottom of the tank with an expression I chose to read as grief rather than resentment.

By late afternoon I was wringing out my second shirt of the day and running the station diagnostics, because a nine-second gravity failure on a station this age is not a curiosity. It's a coroner's summons. Rotational gravity doesn't fail; it's not a thing that can fail. Kepler Station doesn't spin — it sits in the L4 shadow of the big mining platform's orbit, and its "gravity" comes from the same reference network every ship and station in this system uses, the gravitational baseline that tells inertial dampers and spin compensators and every autopilot ever built what *down* means. If my gravity had failed, then the network that defines gravity had failed. Not for me. For everyone.

I pulled the anomaly logs with the pleasant, practiced confidence of a woman who expected to find a blown regulator and go to bed angry at machinery.

The logs returned nothing.

Not an error. Not a fault flag. Nothing. The system's own record of station time 0641 showed a smooth, unbroken gravity trace, flat as a lie, running straight through the nine seconds I had spent floating among my tomatoes. The environmental computer insisted that gravity on Kepler Station had been continuous and nominal for 4,017 days.

I stood in front of the display for a long time, dripping, and felt the first real cold of the day, and it had nothing to do with the greenhouse.

Because here's the thing I knew, and knew absolutely, in the wordless way you know your own heartbeat: the gravity had failed. My body had been in free fall for nine seconds. I had watched a wrench orbit my head. No diagnostic in the world was going to gaslight me out of my own inner ear.

Which meant the station's systems weren't broken. They were *edited.* Something had reached into the logs and smoothed them over, the way you'd smooth a bedsheet over a body.

I ran it again from the backup core. Same flat line. I ran it from the maintenance terminal in the greenhouse, the dumb one, the one that still ran firmware two generations old because I'd never gotten around to updating it and had long ago stopped feeling guilty about that. And there — faint, half-corrupted, a fossil in the old buffer — was my nine seconds. A notch in the gravity trace. Nine seconds of nothing, then recovery, then nothing, as if the newer system had reached backward through time and tidied even the dumb terminal's memory.

I sat down on the grating next to Tank Seven and looked at Aldous.

"Okay," I told him. "So either I'm crazy, or something on this station is lying to me. And you were there. You saw the wrench."

Aldous declined to comment. Fish are notorious for that.

* * *

I filed the anomaly report anyway. Not because I expected anyone to read it — the report queue at Kepler Station goes to an office on Ceres that has, as far as I can tell, been staffed by an autoresponder since before my assignment began — but because filing reports is a ritual, and rituals are load-bearing when you live alone. *Anomalous gravitational event, duration approximately nine seconds, station time 0641. All local systems nominal. All local systems lying. Requesting verification from network-wide telemetry.* I wrote that last part, looked at it, and left it in. Let the autoresponder chew on it.

Then I did the thing I do every evening, the thing that structures my days the way church bells structure a village: I walked the ring corridor to the beacon room and ran the evening check.

The beacon is the reason I'm here. That's the official version, and it happens to be true, though "reason" is doing a lot of work in that sentence. Kepler Station exists to tend the beacon — a navigation transmitter, receive-and-respond, sitting at the edge of the shipping lanes, guiding traffic that stopped coming through this sector decades ago. The garden was added later, a terraforming trial, a promise that the station could one day feed a colony. The colony ship was commissioned. The manifests were drawn up. Birth cohorts were assigned — actual lists of names, children not yet born, scheduled for a world that was going to be made ready for them.

The ship never came. The world was never made ready. But the garden grows, and the beacon transmits, and I tend both, and once a year I read the manifests the way you'd visit graves: slowly, aloud, giving every name its weight.

The beacon room is my favorite room on the station, which is a strange thing to say about a room containing exactly one piece of functioning equipment and a great deal of dust. The transmitter sits in its cradle like a sleeping animal, warm and steady, and its carrier wave goes out and out and out into the dark, and nothing has ever answered it, and I have found a strange comfort in that. It's the loneliest prayer ever prayed, and I get to be its only priest.

I ran the evening check. Power nominal. Carrier wave nominal. Alignment nominal.

And then I did something I hadn't done in eleven years: I pulled the beacon's own event log. Not the station log — the beacon's. Its memory is separate, older, wired into the transmitter core itself, and I had never once had a reason to look inside it. The beacon receives. It listens. It is not supposed to *do* anything but wait.

At station time 0641, during my nine seconds of falling, the beacon's log showed a transmission event.

I stared at it for a long time. The greenhouse was still drying behind me; two tilapia were dead in the recycling; my knees ached where the grating had introduced itself; and the beacon — my beacon, receive-only, patient as a lighthouse, the one honest thing in my life — had *transmitted.*

Outward. During the flicker. Nine seconds of signal, aimed at a patch of empty sky with no name in any chart I own.

The beacon is not supposed to be able to do that. I checked the schematics twice, hands not quite steady, and the schematics agreed with me: receive-only. There is no transmit path. There is no power routing to a transmit path. And yet the log sat there on the screen, calm as scripture: one entry, nine seconds, outbound.

I thought about the flat line in the station's gravity trace. I thought about a system that edits its own memory, and a beacon that speaks when it was built only to listen, and a patch of dead space that apparently deserved nine seconds of my beacon's voice at the exact moment the floor of the universe let go.

I'm a keeper. I keep things. I keep a garden for colonists who will never come, and a beacon for ships that never pass, and a set of manifests for children who were never born, and I have kept all of it faithfully for eleven years without once asking the question that was now sitting in the dark of the beacon room with me, patient and enormous:

*Kept for whom?*

Outside the viewport, the stars did what stars do — burned, indifferent, in their ancient arrangements. Somewhere out there, in the nameless patch of sky my beacon had spoken to, something had heard me.

Or something had used me. I wouldn't know which for nineteen days.

But I'm getting ahead of myself. That night, all I knew was this: the gravity had failed for nine seconds, every system I trusted had lied to me about it, and my beacon — my quiet, faithful, receive-only beacon — had answered.

I turned off the display, and in the dark of the beacon room, with the carrier wave humming its one long note out into the black, I sat with my dead fish and my drowned garden and my flat-lined logs, and for the first time in eleven years, Kepler Station felt less like a home than like a held breath.
