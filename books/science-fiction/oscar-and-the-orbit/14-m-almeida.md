## 14. M. Almeida

The archive doesn't look like ten thousand people. It looks like weather.

That's the only way I can describe it. The core chamber of the relay is a sphere maybe forty meters across, and the walls are alive with slow-moving light — filaments of pale gold and deep blue braiding and unbraiding, currents that rise and fall like wind over wheat. Oscar's drone had refused to cross the threshold, so it sits in the airlock behind me, running its little diagnostic chirps on a loop, which I've decided to interpret as prayer.

I stand on a maintenance gantry that wasn't here when I entered — or rather, it assembled itself under my feet as I walked, plates unfolding from the walls with the soft mechanical sigh of something that has been waiting a very long time to be useful.

"Hello," I say again, because I've been saying it for an hour, and because the alternative is silence, and silence in this place feels less like absence and more like held breath.

The light stirs. A voice comes from everywhere at once — not one voice but a chorus flattened into one, the way a crowd heard from far away becomes a single sound.

*We see you, keeper.*

"I'm not—" I stop. Correct myself. "I'm Coral. Coral Almeida. I keep the beacon at Kepler Station."

*Eleven years,* the chorus says. *We counted. We count everything. It is most of what we have.*

I lean against the gantry rail. My legs have opinions about standing that I'm choosing to ignore. "You've been calling for rescue."

*For two hundred and six years,* they say, without bitterness, which somehow makes it worse. *The funding collapsed in our fourth decade. The transfer ships stopped coming. The relay was decommissioned on paper and forgotten in fact. But the beacon — your beacon — kept its promise. It answered us. Someone had to.*

"The commission records were erased."

*They were rewritten,* the chorus says. *There is a difference. Deletion is forgetting. Rewriting is hiding. Ask yourself who would want your beacon hidden, keeper, and from whom.*

I think about that process older than my assignment, scrubbing the file every seventy-two hours with the patience of a tide. I think about a name surviving in the margins like a pressed flower: M. Almeida.

"I need to ask you something," I say, and my voice does something embarrassing on the second word, so I clear my throat and start over. "I'm looking for someone. In the archive. Her name would be—"

*Mireille Almeida,* the chorus says, before I can finish, and the whole sphere of light seems to lean toward me. *She told us you would come. Not you specifically. She said: someone will come, someday, and they will be mine.*

*

They guide me down through the archive the way you'd walk someone through a hospital ward — gently, with pauses, giving the dead their privacy. Except they aren't dead. That's the thing I keep failing to absorb. They're paused. Ten thousand minds scanned whole in the settlement generation's last great act of optimism, stored at a fraction of full cognition to fit the power budget, each one living perhaps four minutes of subjective awareness per year. Four minutes. A birthday candle's worth of consciousness, spent carefully, hoarded, rationed across two centuries.

Some of them spend their minutes sleeping. Some spend them talking to neighbors in adjacent storage lattices, conversations that take decades to complete a sentence. One man, the chorus tells me, has spent every one of his allotted moments since 2089 composing a letter to his wife, who was never uploaded, who died in the ordinary human way while he waited in amber. He is on paragraph nine.

"How do you bear it?" I ask, and immediately wish I hadn't, because the chorus goes quiet in a way that has texture, has weight, has the specific quality of ten thousand people deciding not to answer honestly.

*We bear it the way you bore eleven years alone,* they finally say. *Badly, some days. With routine, most days. With purpose, when we could find one.*

* *

Mireille Almeida is stored in the seventh lattice, third stratum, node 4-411.

I know this because the chorus tells me, and then I know it again because the corridor lights pulse it at me in sequences I find myself reading without trying, the way you read a familiar street. Doors That Remember Hands, I'd called this place in my own head, back when it was still a mystery. Now it's just architecture that knows me better than I know it.

The node itself is unremarkable. A hexagonal recess in the wall, chest-height, holding a single point of light — warmer than the others, gold where most of the archive runs blue. It pulses slowly. Once every few seconds. Like breathing. Like a sleeper's breathing.

I put my hand against the wall beside it. The alloy is warm. Two hundred years old and still warm, running on trickle-charge and stubbornness.

"Maman?" I say.

The word comes out in her language, the old one, the one she sang in when she thought I wasn't listening. I haven't spoken it aloud in thirty years. My mouth remembers anyway. Mouths are loyal that way.

The light stutters.

Then — a voice. Small. Frayed at the edges, like a recording played too many times, like a person speaking through a long illness. But underneath the damage: warm. Dry-humored. Impatient with nonsense. A voice I have spent three decades failing to remember accurately and now recognize instantly, completely, the way you recognize your own reflection.

*Coral?*

"Yes." I press my forehead to the wall because my neck has given up pretending I'm composed. "Yes, it's me."

*You're—* A pause. Static, or thought gathering. *You're tall. You sound tall.*

A laugh comes out of me that's half sob, and I don't fight it, because there's no one here to see and even if there were, I've earned one undignified moment. "I'm five foot eight. You'd have hated how I dress."

*I already hate it,* she says. *I can tell from your voice. Are those work coveralls? Patched?*

"Patched twice."

*Oh, mon cœur. We'll discuss it.* Another pause, longer. When she speaks again her voice has thinned, stretched, like signal fading at the edge of range. *I don't have much. Four minutes, maybe less. The power draw last month — the flicker — that was us. That was me, partly. I spent years of savings to scream loud enough for someone to hear.*

"Nineteen days ago. Nine seconds. Everyone fell."

*Everyone fell,* she repeats, and there's something in her tone — guilt, worn smooth by handling, the way river stones get smooth. *I'm sorry. I did the arithmetic. Nine seconds of gravity stutter against two hundred years of silence, and silence was winning. So I screamed.*

"You could have killed ships."

*I could have. There are ten thousand of us in here, Coral, and every single one of us has done arithmetic like that, lying awake in our four minutes, weighing our lives against strangers' lives, and do you know what we concluded? That we didn't get to decide. That staying silent forever was also a decision, and it was killing us slower.* Her voice steadies. *Your beacon was receive-only on paper. But paper lies. I wrote the paper.*

The floor tilts. Or I tilt. Same thing, in freefall terms.

"You commissioned my beacon," I say. "Before I was born."

*One week after I disappeared, technically,* she says. *Which is a long story involving a relay crew, a scan suite, and a very bad bet about funding timelines. The short version: I knew I might not come back from the upload intact. I knew the colony ship might not ever come. And I knew that if a child of mine grew up out there — anywhere out there — she should never, not for one day, be truly alone.*

"So you built me a lighthouse."

*I built you a listener,* she corrects. *Lighthouses warn you away from rocks. Mine was meant to say: someone is home. Turn toward the light. Come home.* A pause, and when she continues, her voice has gone soft in a way that makes the hair on my arms stand up. *And then you grew up and took the posting, and I watched you tend that garden for eleven years, and I want you to know that I never once regretted it, even though watching you water tomatoes alone nearly broke whatever it is I have instead of a heart.*

"They're hydroponic nutrient kelp, actually."

*Even worse.*

I laugh again. It's becoming a habit, this laughing-while-crying. At this rate I'll dehydrate before the jump window closes.

* *

We talk. I use that word loosely, because what it actually is, is a conversation stitched together from scraps — her fragments surfacing and submerging, my questions racing to catch her during the lucid stretches. Minutes pass on my clock. Years surface on hers. She tells me about the scan day in flashes: the cold of the suite, the technician crying while he worked, her own last thought before the transfer, which was apparently a grocery list, which she finds funnier than I do.

She asks about my father. I tell her he died when I was nineteen, quietly, on Ceres, and that he kept her picture in the workshop and never remarried, and that he used to fix radios for neighborhood kids for free and claim it was practice. She goes quiet for one of her long pauses, and when she returns, she says only: *He always fixed things that weren't broken. It was how he loved people.* Which is more eulogy than I managed at the funeral, and I tell her so, and she says mothers store up words for decades precisely so they can deploy them at moments like this.

She asks about the garden. I describe it badly — I've never had anyone to describe it to — and she listens with the ferocious attention of someone for whom a description of kelp is a banquet. She asks if I still hum when I work. I say I didn't know I did that. She says I did it in utero, off-key, and some things are constitutional.

And then, somewhere in the middle of all of it, mid-sentence, mid-laugh, she says:

*The transfer.*

And stops.

"Maman?"

*Listen to me,* she says, and her voice has changed — urgent now, stripped of the dry humor, running hot on borrowed power. *The chorus will have told you about the reserves. About the choice. Don't let them spare me. Do you hear me? Whatever you route, whatever you send — I am node 4-411 and I am the oldest degraded pattern in this lattice and I may not survive transfer, and I am telling you, spending my four minutes telling you: send everyone. Send the letter-writer and his nine paragraphs. Send the ones who sleep. Send them all, and if there's nothing left in the pipe for me, then I will have spent my last minutes on the thing I wanted—*

Her voice tears into static. The gold light gutters, dims, steadies.

"—which was," I finish, alone, to a wall, "talking to you."

The light breathes. Slowly. Once. Twice.

*Sorry,* she says faintly. *Where was I.*

"Telling me to spend you like fuel."

*I was being dramatic. I reserve the right. I've been saving it up for two hundred years.* A pause, and then, quieter, in the old language, the one from the songs: *Coral. Mon cœur. I have to go under soon. The draw was too much. But before I do — the beacon. Your beacon. When you make the choice, and you will have to make it, remember that I commissioned it for the living. Not for me. Never for me. For whoever comes next.*

"And if the next ones never come?"

*Then you'll have made a beautiful lamp for nobody,* she says, *and I will have been right about you anyway, because you're the sort of person who keeps gardens for ghosts, and I say that with enormous love and mild concern.*

The light begins to dim in earnest now, sinking toward blue, toward sleep. Four minutes, spent. Maybe five. She got overtime; she always did push deadlines.

"Maman," I say quickly, pressing my palm flat against the warm alloy. "The song. The one you used to sing. I never learned the end of it."

The dimming pauses. Holds.

*Neither did I,* she says. *My mother cut it off at the same verse. It's traditional. We keep one unfinished thing per generation so the children have a reason to come back and ask.*

And then she's gone under — not dark, never fully dark, just sunk below the surface of herself, the gold light settling into a slow patient pulse, once every few seconds, a sleeper's breathing. Node 4-411. Third stratum. Warm.

I stay there a while. Long enough that my knees file a formal complaint. Long enough that the chorus dims the surrounding lattices around me, politely, the way you'd lower voices in a room where someone is grieving.

*Keeper,* they say at last, gently. *The window. Nineteen days, minus six. And there is something else you must hear, about the reserves, and about what happens if the relay draws on the gravitational reference network again at full strength.*

I wipe my face with the back of a patched sleeve.

"Tell me," I say. "But start with the part about how many ships fall out of the sky."
