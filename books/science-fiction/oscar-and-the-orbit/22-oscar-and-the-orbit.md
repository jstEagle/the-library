## 22. Oscar and the Orbit

The drone has been following me for six days now.

It started as a shadow in the Perihelion's wake — a dented maintenance unit I'd salvaged fuel from back in the shipping lanes, the one that had imprinted on my skiff like a duckling on a flatbed truck. I'd assumed it would lose interest once we cleared the debris shoals. Drones don't have interests. They have loops, and when their loops break they drift until someone scrapes them for parts.

But this one kept pace. Through the long burn home, through the patrol's escort formation, through the white-knuckle descent past Kepler Station's marker buoys, it held station two hundred meters off my port side with the stubborn precision of something that had decided, at some level below firmware, that I was its job now.

I named it on day four, mostly out of spite toward my own loneliness.

"Oscar," I said over the comm, and immediately regretted it, because naming things is how you end up talking to them, and talking to drones is how you end up as a cautionary tale in station psych evaluations.

The drone flashed its running lights twice. Which meant nothing. Which meant everything, if you'd spent eleven years reading meaning into the blink of a beacon.

* * *

Kepler Station looked smaller than I remembered it, which was impossible, because nothing about it had changed. Same ring of solar panels turning their slow, patient circles. Same greenhouse dome catching the light of a star that didn't know we existed. Same beacon mast rising from the north pylon like a needle waiting for thread.

Except the beacon was dark. I had done that. I had poured its last reserves into a data ghost and sent ten thousand minds screaming through a closing jump window toward the inner colonies, and the beacon had gone silent for the first time since before I was born, and for three weeks the sky above my garden had been just sky.

Coming back to a dark beacon felt like coming home to find the porch light off and the house sold.

Oscar and I docked together, which is to say Oscar docked himself to my hull without asking, which is to say I let him, which is to say I was glad.

* * *

The greenhouse had survived my absence better than I deserved. The autonomous systems had done exactly what I'd told them to do, which was nothing dramatic — hold temperature, cycle water, keep the grow-lights honest. Forty years of careful planting, reduced during those nine seconds of falling to a drifting chaos of uprooted seedlings and floating soil, and then patiently reassembled by machines that didn't grieve, didn't hope, didn't understand what any of it was for.

But some things hadn't come back right. The tomato vines had fruited small and bitter. The wheat had come in patchy, leaning toward a light schedule calibrated for a gardener who wasn't there. Walking the rows, I kept finding small evidence of the flicker — a trellis bolted at an angle no one would have chosen, a hydroponic line rerouted around a stress fracture, the sediment of nine seconds of weightlessness settled into every corner like ash after a fire.

I knelt in the aisle between beds four and five and pressed my palm flat against the soil. Warm. Alive. Smelling of wet mineral and green growing things and eleven years of my own sweat worked into the dirt.

"You're still here," I said to the garden, because apparently I was now someone who talked to plants *and* drones, and the cautionary tales could form an orderly queue.

Behind me, in the airlock corridor, Oscar's treads squeaked against the deck plating. He couldn't enter the greenhouse — humidity played hell with his joints — so he waited at the threshold like a dog at a screen door, running lights pulsing in a pattern I had decided, without evidence, was patience.

* * *

The new commission record arrived on a Tuesday, though Tuesdays mean nothing out here except that the calendar server says so.

It came from the Colonial Reestablishment Authority, which was a real organization now instead of a name attached to automated rejection letters. The message was brief and bureaucratic and, buried in paragraph three, extraordinary:

*In light of the recovered archive's historical significance, colonization of the Kepler system has been re-approved. First wave: 214 souls, arrival window fourteen months. You are requested — not ordered, requested — to draft the replacement commission record for Beacon K-7. The record should be authored under your personal credentials and cryptographically sealed against amendment.*

Un-erasable. That was the phrase the lawyer-bot used, twice, as if worried I might miss it. *Authored under your personal credentials and sealed against amendment.* No more seventy-two-hour rewrites. No more process older than my assignment quietly eating the past. Whatever I wrote into that record would stay written, in my own name, for as long as anyone kept the lights on.

I sat with the message for a long time, watching the cursor blink in the empty response field, understanding that I was holding the pen my mother never got to hold.

She had commissioned the original beacon in secret, in the week after she uploaded herself into a dying relay, and she had left its records erasable because she hadn't known what the future would need to forget. She'd built me a lighthouse and then let the fog roll over its name. It had taken me twenty-nine years and nineteen days and one impossible conversation to understand that the erasure wasn't cruelty. It was a mother's gamble: make the daughter's life simple, even if the truth was complicated. Keep her alone but safe. Keep the beacon answering, always, so that whatever else happened, someone was listening for her.

*I chose the commission for you,* she'd said, in those last fragmentary minutes before transfer, her voice younger than my memories of it, mid-thought forever. *So you would never be alone. Even when I couldn't be there. Especially then.*

She'd been wrong about the alone part, mostly. But she'd been right about the listening. And now it was my turn to write the record — not to hide the truth behind a scrubbed file, but to set it down plain, in daylight, where the unborn colonists could read it and know exactly what they were inheriting.

I opened a fresh document and titled it, simply: *Commission Record, Beacon K-7, Second Commission. Author: Coral Almeida, Keeper.*

Then I sat there for another hour, because the first sentence of anything permanent deserves an hour.

* * *

What I finally wrote ran to forty pages, and most of it was testimony.

I wrote about the first generation — ten thousand minds scanned and shelved in a black cathedral beyond the heliopause when the funding collapsed, abandoned not by malice but by arithmetic, kept alive for two centuries by solar trickle-charge and one loyal beacon and the stubborn, terrible hope that someone would come. I wrote about the gravity flicker: nine seconds stolen from the reference network by a dying archive trying to scream, and the ten thousand ships that could have fallen out of the sky if it had screamed louder. I wrote about the choice — currents toward rescue or darkness toward safety — and why I chose rescue, and what it cost, and why I'd choose it again.

I wrote about M. Almeida, relay crew, uploaded before her daughter was born, who spent her last coherent fragments making sure that daughter would have a light to steer by. I wrote her name in full, un-erasable, because names are the smallest monuments and the only ones that fit everywhere.

And at the end, I wrote the new purpose, in words short enough to survive translation into every language the colonists might bring:

*Beacon K-7 exists to guide the living. Its light is not a memorial. It is a door, and it is open.*

I sealed it with my credentials. The system asked me to confirm, and I confirmed, and somewhere in a ledger that no seventy-two-hour process would ever touch, the beacon stopped being my mother's secret and became everyone's inheritance.

Above me, through the greenhouse dome, the relit beacon turned its slow circle. The engineers from the Reestablishment Authority had restored it in eleven days — new core, new reserves, same mast, same frequency, because frequencies are promises too. It pulsed now on a navigation band, bright and steady, broadcasting approach vectors and docking schedules and the coordinates of a garden where the first wave could watch things grow.

Guiding the living. My mother had built it to mourn. I got to build it to welcome. I think she would have called that a fair trade. I think, if the transfer had preserved more than fragments of her, she'd have sung about it — that half-remembered phrase from the buffer, the one that turned out to be a lullaby she'd made up for a daughter she'd never hold: *the orbit holds, the orbit holds, and everything that falls comes home.*

* * *

Oscar helped with the planting, in the way that a wrench helps with poetry.

The first wave wouldn't arrive for fourteen months, but seeds don't wait for committees, and the colonial charter had included — buried in appendix nine, because bureaucracies hide their best sentences in appendices — a provision for "pre-arrival agricultural establishment." Which meant I was authorized, encouraged, *requested*, to plant the next garden now, so that when 214 souls came down the approach vector, the first thing they'd see besides a steady light would be something green.

I'd chosen the seed carefully. Not wheat, not tomatoes — the workhorses, the calories, the practical crops that could wait for wave two. For the first bed, the foundation bed, the one visible from the docking apron, I'd chosen beans.

Don't laugh. Beans are a statement. Beans are fast — sixty days from seed to harvest, which in colonial time is practically instant gratification. Beans fix nitrogen; they improve the soil they grow in; they give back more than they take. And bean seeds are tough. They'll wait years in a vacuum-sealed packet and still sprout the moment you give them water and warmth and a reason.

My mother had carried a packet of them onto a relay crew twenty-nine years ago. I found them in her effects when I was nineteen, sealed, labeled in her handwriting: *For Coral, when there's soil worth planting in.* I'd kept them through eleven years of tending a garden for colonists who never came, and I'd never planted them, because they weren't mine to plant. They were a promise, and promises need the right ground.

The ground was ready now.

I broke the seal in the greenhouse, kneeling in the aisle between beds four and five, with Oscar parked at the threshold running his patient little light-pattern and the new beacon pulsing overhead. The seeds were small and dry and ordinary, the color of river stones, and my hands shook anyway, because twenty-nine years is a long time for a promise to travel.

"One," I said, pressing the first seed into the soil. "Two." The second went in beside it. "Three."

I planted the whole row, and then the row behind it, working in silence except for the sound of soil closing over each seed and the faint mechanical breathing of a drone who had decided I was his job. When the last one was in, I watered the bed, and wiped my hands on my knees, and sat back on my heels, and looked at a rectangle of dark earth that contained, potentially, several hundred future meals and absolutely, certainly, one kept promise.

Oscar trundled forward half a meter, as close to the threshold as he could get, and flashed his lights.

"Yeah," I told him. "Me too."

* * *

That evening I did the rounds — the last ritual of the old keeper, or the first ritual of the new founder, depending on how you wanted to file it.

Greenhouse systems: nominal. Water reclamation: nominal. Solar array: ninety-four percent and climbing. The station hummed around me with the particular contentment of machinery that knows its purpose, and for the first time in eleven years, the purpose wasn't a ghost. Fourteen months. Two hundred and fourteen people. Children, probably — colonists always bring children, because colonies are how the species bets on itself. There would be kids running these corridors who had never known silence, who would think the beacon's pulse was as natural as weather, who would plant gardens of their own in soil my beans were busy enriching right now.

I stood at the north viewport with Oscar humming at my elbow and watched the beacon turn.

Somewhere out past the heliopause, a cathedral of black alloy drifted dark and cold, its archive emptied, its debt paid. Somewhere in the inner colonies, ten thousand minds were waking into servers that had been built to receive them, blinking at a world that had moved on without them, beginning the long strange work of being alive again. Somewhere in a colonial archive, a forty-page testimony bore my name and would bear it forever. And somewhere in the soil beneath my feet, twenty-nine-year-old seeds were doing the only thing seeds know how to do, which is trust the dark enough to split open.

My mother had spent her death making sure I'd have a light. I intended to spend my life making sure it shone on something worth arriving to.

Gravity was steady. The garden was growing. The orbit held, the orbit held, and everything that fell —

— came home.
