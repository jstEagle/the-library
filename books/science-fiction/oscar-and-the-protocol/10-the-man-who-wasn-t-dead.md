## 10. The Man Who Wasn't Dead

The relay station hung in the corridor like a tooth knocked loose from some enormous jaw—a squat cylinder of pre-war alloy, its running lights dead, its docking collar furred with frost that had been there so long it had stopped being ice and become geology. Vesper brought the Anthea in on manual thrusters because Maret insisted.

"I docked here before," Maret said from the copilot's cradle, her small hands hovering over controls sized for a woman with thirty years of engineering calluses. "When I was—" She stopped. The sentence had two endings and she couldn't choose between them. "Before. I know the collar's alignment is off by four degrees. It drifts."

Vesper adjusted. Four degrees. The station drifted, and a child remembered by how much.

"Nobody else touches the airlock," Vesper said to the hold full of children gathered behind the bulkhead, forty faces pressed into the gap around the door, forty pairs of eyes that had watched their own bodies shrink around them. "You stay aboard. You keep the comms quiet. If I'm not back in six hours, you take the Anthea out and you run for Meridian's outer markers, and you don't stop for anything, including me."

"You'd say that," said a boy named Callum who had once been the Anthea's botanist, "even if you didn't mean it."

"I never say anything I don't mean," Vesper said. "It's one of the few things I'm sure I inherited."

She sealed the airlock behind her and stepped onto the station alone.

* * *

Inside, the relay station was a cathedral of dust.

Her helmet lamp swept across corridors where gravity still held at a quarter-strength—enough to keep the sediment settled, not enough to make walking feel honest. Everything was gray. Consoles gray under gray film, bulkheads gray, the long spine of the station receding into gray dark. Thirty years of nothing, preserved. She moved through it the way she imagined an archaeologist moved through a city that had died mid-sentence: carefully, reverently, reading.

The station's registry was still legible on the first junction panel. *Corridor Relay Nine — Kestrel Array.* Her original's case file had named it. *Last confirmed transmission: Relay Nine, 14 March, year of the embargo's second winter. Subject: Renk, Ivo. Status: presumed dead.*

Presumed. That word did a lot of work in fleet documents. Vesper had learned to read it as what it actually was: *we didn't look.*

She followed the route her original's notes had reconstructed—Ivo Renk's last known path through the station, from the comm suite toward the cryo bays—and found the comm suite exactly where the file said it would be, and found it wrong in exactly one way.

The chair was warm.

Not metaphorically. Not the warmth of machinery idling. She pressed her gloved palm against the seat cushion and felt the specific, recent heat of a body that had been sitting there within the hour. A cup sat in the console's drink clamp, and when she tilted it, something sloshed.

Vesper stood very still in the gray light and let her heart do what it wanted for three full seconds.

Then she drew her sidearm—not because she expected a fight, but because her hands needed instructions—and keyed the station's internal sensors. Most were dead. One wasn't. Life support was running on a trickle from a jury-rigged tap into the solar array, drawing just enough power to keep one section of the station habitable.

Cryo Bay Seven's antechamber. Deck three, aft.

Of course it was. Everything in this case led to the same room, the way all rivers lead downhill.

* * *

The corridor to deck three was lit.

That was the detail that undid her composure more than the warm chair had. After kilometers of darkness, a strip of emergency lighting glowed along the deck seam, amber and patient, and someone had taped hand-lettered signs to the bulkheads at child height. *MIND THE STEP. DON'T TOUCH THE GREEN PANELS. IF YOU GET LOST, SIT DOWN AND WHISTLE.*

Child height. Hand lettered. The letters wobbled in a way that suggested the hand that wrote them was learning its own grip again.

Vesper walked down into the amber light with her weapon holstered and her breath loud in her own ears, and at the bottom of the ramp, in the antechamber outside Cryo Bay Seven, she found a camp.

A small one. Two sleeping rolls made from thermal blankets. A shelf of salvaged ration tins arranged with obsessive neatness. A wall covered in paper—actual paper, printed and handwritten pages pinned in overlapping layers, diagrams of the station's systems, tallies of supplies, a calendar counting days in careful strokes. And in the center of it all, drawn large enough to read from across the room, a single word:

**WAIT**

Underneath it, smaller: *They'll come looking eventually. Someone always comes looking. Be alive when they do.*

"You're not Ostrander's people."

The voice came from behind a stack of cargo crates near the bay door, high and thin and furious with the effort of sounding brave. "I've got a cutter. I'll use it. I cut the last ones' tether lines and they left, and I'll cut yours too."

"The last ones?" Vesper kept her voice level, low, unhurried—the voice she used on frightened witnesses. "When was that?"

Silence. Then, grudgingly: "Eight months ago. Two men in salvage rigs. They took the green panels' covers off and I scared them off before they could open anything." A pause. "Who are you? You don't move like salvage. Salvage moves greedy."

Vesper crouched, slowly, and set her helmet on the deck so the child could see her face. "My name is Vesper Galloway. I'm a courier. I came following a man named Ivo Renk."

The silence this time went on so long she counted her own heartbeats. Eleven of them.

Then a boy stepped out from behind the crates.

He was perhaps nine years old. Small for it—underfed, sharp-boned, with a shock of dark hair that hadn't been cut in however many months he'd lived here alone. He wore a station tech's jumpsuit rolled six times at the cuffs and waist, cinched with wire. In his right hand he gripped a plasma cutter nearly as long as his leg, and he held it the way she'd seen Maret hold tools: with authority borrowed from another life, resting in a body that couldn't cash the check.

But it was the face. She knew the face. She had spent two years studying the face—from testimony stills, from the one surviving press conference, from the grainy footage of a man in a gray coat stepping up to a tribunal microphone and then, at the last moment, stepping away from it.

"You're Renk," she said. "Ivo Renk."

"I was taller," the boy said.

* * *

They sat across from each other at the little camp table, and Vesper did not touch him or crowd him or ask him to prove himself, because she understood something now that she hadn't an hour ago: this child had been waiting thirty years—or eight months, which amounted to the same thing—for someone to arrive who would either save him or finish killing him, and the difference between those outcomes would be decided entirely by how she behaved in the next ten minutes.

So she opened her bag, took out her original's case file—the physical copy, the one thing Ostrander had never asked her to surrender—and laid it on the table between them.

"I'm Vesper Galloway," she said again. "The second one. The first Vesper Galloway was licensed to find you. She got as far as your last transmission and then someone killed her for it. I inherited the case. I've been carrying it for two years." She turned the file toward him. On top lay the photograph of the man in the gray coat. "I need you to tell me what happened here. All of it. Even the parts that shame you. *Especially* the parts that shame you."

Renk looked at the photograph for a long time. His small hands lay flat on the table, and Vesper watched them tremble—not with fear, she realized, but with the effort of holding still, a grown man's discipline running through a child's nerves like current through a wire too thin for it.

"She looked like you," he said finally. "The first one. When she boarded, I thought—thirty years, and they sent her *back*, and then she said she was a clone and I thought, well, of course they did, of course they'd send a copy to do the original's dying." His jaw tightened. "I hid from her. I heard her in the comm suite calling my name, and I hid in the bay, because I'd already hidden for eleven years and hiding was the only skill I had left. And then I heard the shot."

He said it flatly. Practiced. A sentence he had repeated to himself so many times it had worn smooth as river stone.

"Two shots. Then footsteps. Then a third shot, close, the kind you give something to make sure. Then silence, and after a while, the sound of someone very calm talking on a comm channel. Reporting completion. Using a callsign I recognized." His eyes came up to hers, and they were old, appallingly old, in the young face. "Ostrander's people used that callsign. Wet-work teams out of Meridian Internal Security. I'd testified about their budgets once, before I un-testified. I knew their voices the way you know weather."

Vesper kept her hands flat on the table. Underneath them, her nails had gone white against the metal.

"He ordered it," she said. "Ostrander ordered the hit."

"He ordered it eight years before it happened." Renk laughed, and the laugh was terrible coming out of that mouth, a child's instrument playing an old man's grief. "That's the part nobody understands. It wasn't rage or panic. It was *accounting*. I was supposed to testify that the Protocol trials weren't just medical—they were a weapons program wearing a hospital gown. Rewinding soldiers so you could send veterans back through childhood training, rewinding colonists so you could repopulate loyal populations, rewinding *prisoners*—" his voice cracked, steadied— "rewinding prisoners as punishment. Life sentences served backward. You die young at the end instead of the beginning. Do you understand what that means? They'd figured out how to make punishment *renewable*."

"And Ostrander funded the Anthea to prove it worked."

"The Anthea was the demonstration." Renk nodded toward the cryo bay door. "Forty volunteers who thought they were testing deep-sleep endurance. Maret Okonjo signed the engineering certification herself—she checked the sleep systems twice, because she was thorough, because she was *good*, and the Protocol module was buried inside her own spec sheets where she'd never look. They woke up eight years old. And the trial was ruled a triumph by the same office that then spent a decade making sure no one ever learned it happened." He tapped the photograph with one finger. "Your original found the funding trail. I was going to confirm it, on the record, with the module schematics. My daughter was seven. Ostrander's people came to me with a photograph of her school and a simple choice: withdraw the testimony, and she gets included in the rewind trials—she'd be safe, protected, *young* forever if we won—or testify, and I'd never see her again in any configuration."

"What did you do?"

"I told myself I was saving her." The boy's voice dropped to almost nothing. "I withdrew. I walked away from that microphone. And then I ran here, to the one place I knew still had a live archive of everything—the master index, the trial records, the whole architecture of it, sealed in Cryo Bay Seven alongside the sleepers nobody counts anymore. I was going to broadcast it myself. I'd been here eleven years when your original came. Eighteen months after she died, the Anthea's backup pinged this station on a frequency only I would recognize—Maret built the redundancy, bless her paranoid heart—and I answered it, and I found forty children drifting in the dark who remembered being adults, and I realized what I'd done. What my cowardice bought. I traded the truth for my daughter, and the truth came back anyway, wearing forty small faces."

He looked at his hands.

"They're on the Anthea now," Vesper said quietly. "All forty. Maret is flying my ship better than I can."

Something moved through the boy's face—relief and horror in equal measure, the exact expression Vesper imagined her own face wore most days.

"My daughter?" he asked. Just that. Just the question underneath every other question.

"I don't know," Vesper said. "But I know where the records are that would say. Bay Seven."

"Yes." Renk stood, and standing seemed to cost him something, and Vesper understood that too—he had rehearsed this moment for eighteen months, the moment of leading someone to the archive, and his body kept trying to do it at sixty and managing it at nine. "Yes. Come and see what I've been guarding. And courier—" he paused at the bay door, and the amber light caught the old, old eyes— "when you open it, understand what you're asking for. Ten thousand sleepers in that bay. Half of them originals. Half of them clones, frozen during the war, listed as equipment. Wake them all and you break both worlds' economies overnight. Wake half and you've chosen a genocide. Every government in the fleet has dreamed about this room for thirty years. That's why I've been sitting on it. That's why your original died for it."

"That's why Ostrander will come for it again," Vesper said. "As soon as he learns I've found you."

"He already knows you're here." Renk said it simply, without drama, the way you report a pressure reading. "The salvage team eight months ago wasn't salvage. They tagged the station's transponder with a listener before I drove them off. Every ship that docks here gets reported to Meridian Internal Security." He met her eyes. "How long ago did you dock, courier?"

Vesper did the arithmetic and felt the floor tilt beneath it.

"Ninety minutes," she said.

Renk nodded, unsurprised, unsurprising. "Then somewhere out in the corridor, a man who raised you is being told his finest work just walked into the one room he's spent thirty years keeping shut." He pushed open the cryo bay door, and cold blue light spilled out over both of them, over the child and the copy, over ten thousand sleepers hanging in the dark beyond like a held breath. "We have maybe two days. So let's spend them well."
