## 12. Halcyon's Offer

The message came in on a Halcyon diplomatic channel, which was itself an impossibility, like a letter arriving sealed in ice during a house fire. Vesper read it three times at the Anthea's comm station while the children slept in the hold below, forty small bodies curled among cargo netting and thermal blankets, breathing in the shallow synchronized rhythm of kids who had learned—recently, violently—that sleep could be taken from you.

*Alone. Docking ring four. Bring nothing. Tell no one.*

No signature. No flag code beyond the channel itself. Just a set of coordinates stitched into the relay station's outer docking lattice, a place where the hull plating had been patched so many times it looked like scar tissue over scar tissue.

Vesper sat with her thumb hovering over the delete key for a long moment. The rational move—the move her original would have made, probably, or maybe not; she could never be sure anymore which instincts were inherited and which were hers—was to ignore it, or to bring Maret, or to rig a recorder into her collar and let the children hear everything.

She did none of those things. She told Maret only that she was stepping out to check the docking clamps, watched the girl's eyes narrow with an engineer's suspicion of bad maintenance excuses, and went anyway.

The walk to docking ring four took eleven minutes through corridors that smelled of coolant and old panic. The relay station had been dead for thirty years before Renk's transmission had led them here, and it still carried the atmosphere of a place that had died mid-thought: a coffee bulb fused to a console, a jacket hanging on a hook as if its owner had stepped out to check a reading and simply never come back. Vesper's boots rang on the deck plates. Somewhere behind her, the Anthea hung off the station's spine like a remora, full of sleeping children who trusted her.

She hated that word. Trusted. It sat on her chest like ballast.

Docking ring four was a bubble of vacuum-sealed glass and bare alloy at the station's flank, built for ships that had stopped coming. A single vessel was clamped there now: a Halcyon courier, matte black, no registry lights, the kind of ship that existed officially nowhere and unofficially everywhere. Its airlock cycled open before she reached it.

The man who stepped out was ordinary in the way that made her immediately suspicious—medium height, medium build, a face designed by committee to be forgotten. He wore civilian thermals under an unbadged jacket, and he moved with the unhurried economy of someone who had never once needed to run.

"Vesper Galloway," he said. Not a question. "Second iteration."

"You have me at a disadvantage."

"Sef Adeyemi." He said it the way other men said their rank—as if the name itself carried clearance. "Halcyon External Continuity Office. Which doesn't exist, so please don't go looking for it." He gestured toward a pair of jump seats bolted beside the airlock, the kind of seating meant for people waiting to leave, not people planning to stay. "Sit. This won't take long, and your ship is unattended."

"My ship has forty witnesses."

"It has forty children asleep in a hold, and one engineer who trusts you." He smiled faintly. "I've read your file, Ms. Galloway. Both of them. Yours and hers. I know how much weight trust puts on you. I'm not going to threaten it. I'm going to offer to carry some of it."

Vesper didn't sit. She stood with her back to the glass, where she could see the corridor's starfield smeared across the pane and, reflected dimly in it, his face watching hers. "Then say what you came to say."

Adeyemi produced a slate—not a military unit, but a civilian reader, scuffed, personal—and laid it flat on the seat between them like a card played face-up. On the screen: a legal document, dense with clauses, crowned by a title in formal type.

*The Halcyon Personhood Act of the Outer Reaches.*

"I'll spare you the legalese," he said. "Here's the shape of it. Full legal personhood for every synthetic human in fleet space. Clones, iterations, memory-inherited constructs—all of it. Property status abolished. Citizenship conferred. Inheritance rights, testimony rights, the whole architecture. Your kind stops being equipment the moment this passes."

"And Halcyon's council signs this because they've had a change of heart about human dignity."

"Halcyon's council signs it because we're losing the demographic war and everyone knows it." He said it without embarrassment, which she almost respected. "Meridian outbreeds us three to one among original births. Our entire industrial base runs on clone labor that legally cannot own what it builds. We have been running a civilization on borrowed souls, Ms. Galloway, and the interest is coming due. Personhood isn't charity. It's arithmetic."

"And the price."

"The archive." His voice didn't change register when he said it. That was the craft in him—no dramatics, no menace, just the flat delivery of a man describing weather. "The Protocol master data in Cryo Bay Seven. You deliver it to Halcyon custody, intact, exclusive. In exchange, the Act passes within the year, with your testimony as its living exhibit. The first clone citizen of the new Halcyon. They'll put your face on the commemorative issue."

Vesper looked at the document glowing between them. She thought of the ten thousand sleepers two decks below her feet, sealed in their frost-lined drawers, half of them originals, half of them clones, frozen mid-war by a technology both worlds had sworn to bury and both worlds had secretly kept.

"And the originals?" she asked. "The five thousand originals in that bay. What happens to them under your Act?"

"They stay frozen."

The words landed with no weight at all, which was how she knew how much weight they were meant to carry. Adeyemi continued, smooth as ballast shifting: "You can't wake ten thousand people, Ms. Galloway. You know this. We've run the numbers—your handler ran the numbers years ago, actually, before he decided to pretend otherwise. Meridian's economy collapses under the intake. Rationing, riots, a second war fought over soup kitchens. Waking everyone is a massacre with extra steps. Waking *nobody* is just cruelty with better manners. But waking the clones—" he tapped the slate, gently, as if waking something himself— "waking the clones is mercy. Five thousand people pulled out of the dark and given lives. Given *names*. And every clone in fleet space given personhood in the bargain. Two goods for one omission."

"One omission," Vester repeated. "That's what you call it."

"That's what it is. Omission is the only clean sin left. Everything else leaves blood on the floor." He leaned back against his ship's hull, arms folded, patient. "I know how this sounds. I've made this pitch before, in other rooms, to other people holding other terrible choices. They all start by hating me. Some of them end by thanking me. The ones who thank me understood sooner that perfection isn't on the menu. Only portions."

Below them, through the deck, Vesper could feel the station's ancient systems ticking in their sleep. She thought about her original—Vesper Galloway, first iteration, murdered in a Meridian service corridor eleven years ago because she'd gotten too close to exactly this arithmetic. She thought about the memory-fragments she carried of that last hour: rain on a window, a hand reaching for a door handle, the specific taste of fear like copper at the back of the throat. Fragments without certainty. Grief without a grave.

"If I do this," Vesper said slowly, "every original in that bay stays asleep forever. And every clone wakes up knowing their life was purchased with someone else's abandonment. That's not personhood. That's a debt structured so the debtor can't read the terms."

"Every freedom in history was purchased from someone else's account," Adeyemi said. "Yours included. The clones already exist because originals chose to make them. You're not creating the debt. You're just being offered the chance to spend it well."

"There it is." She almost smiled. "The just face."

"What?"

"Temptation always wears one. Nobody offers you damnation anymore—they offer you the lesser evil, itemized, with a signature bonus." She pushed off the glass and stepped closer, close enough to see the fine grain of tiredness around his eyes that the committee-designed face couldn't quite hide. "Tell me about the clause at the bottom, Adeyemi. Section nine. The one that says Halcyon retains exclusive research rights to the Protocol data 'for therapeutic applications.' I read fast."

Something flickered behind his composure—a recalculation, quick and private. "Therapeutic applications are legitimate. De-aging trauma victims. Terminal patients. There are children dying right now who—"

"There are children *aboard my ship* who were made that way by people who also used the word therapeutic." Her voice stayed level, but she felt the old anger rising in her chest, her original's anger, hot and familiar as a hand on her shoulder. "You don't want the archive to heal anyone. You want it because whoever holds it holds the only lever in the system that bends time. Meridian gets it, they win the truce. Halcyon gets it, they win the truce. Either way, ten thousand sleepers become collateral in a negotiation they never agreed to attend."

"And your alternative?" For the first time, something real moved through his voice—not anger, but a genuine, weary curiosity, the sound of a man who had asked himself the same question in the dark. "Walk me through it. You keep the archive neutral. You refuse both flags. Then the truce expires, both fleets come here shooting, and the bay becomes a prize of war. Is that mercy? Is that protecting them?"

"No," Vesper admitted. "It isn't."

"Then what is?"

She was quiet for a moment. Through the glass behind her, the corridor's stars wheeled slow and indifferent, and somewhere out in that darkness, ninety days were burning down toward zero.

"I don't know yet," she said. "But I know it isn't yours. And I know it isn't Meridian's. And I know that whatever I choose, I'm not choosing it alone—because the last time someone decided unilaterally which half of humanity mattered, the result is sleeping in Cryo Bay Seven and crying in my cargo hold."

Adeyemi studied her for a long moment. Then, unexpectedly, he nodded—not in agreement, but in the way of a professional acknowledging good work. "Your original would have said the same thing. Word for word. I met her once, you know. Eleven years ago. She was asking questions about cryogenics funding, and I was the one who told Ostrander to worry about her."

The corridor seemed to tilt. Vesper kept her feet planted through sheer discipline. "Say that again."

"He paid me in access. I paid him in warnings. It was a different world then—we all believed the war logic would hold." He shrugged, and the shrug was the most honest thing he'd done since she arrived. "I'm telling you because you deserve to hear it from someone, and because I want you to understand that I'm not lying to you now. I lied to her. I won't bother lying to you. You're worth more to Halcyon convinced than deceived."

"Generous."

"Efficient." He picked up the slate, killed the document, and held it out to her. "Keep it. Read the clauses yourself. Show it to your engineer, your ghost of a whistleblower, whoever you trust. The offer stands until the truce does. Ninety days, minus six." He turned back to his airlock, then paused, hand on the frame, and spoke without turning around. "One more thing, Ms. Galloway. Free of charge."

"Nothing's free from your office."

"This one is, because it costs me. When the vote comes—whatever vote, whatever form it takes—remember that the children aboard your ship are the only people in this system who are both halves at once. Originals, rewound. They're the only ones who can't be flattered into choosing a side, because they *are* the side." He glanced back at her, and for a heartbeat the committee face fell away entirely, and what was underneath looked old, and tired, and sorry. "Ostrander raised you to be his finest work. Halcyon wants to raise you to be ours. Somebody in this story ought to belong to themselves."

The airlock cycled. The black ship undocked with a shudder Vesper felt through her boot soles, and then docking ring four was empty except for her, the stars, and a scuffed civilian slate warm in her hand.

She stood there a long time, long enough for the ring's heaters to cycle twice, long enough for the cold to find the gaps in her jacket. Then she opened the document again and began, clause by clause, to read—not the promises at the top, but the machinery underneath, the definitions and exceptions and quiet reversions, the way a lockpicker reads a door.

Section nine. *Exclusive research rights, therapeutic applications.* And beneath it, subsection C, in language so dry it was nearly invisible: *In the event of resource conflict, priority of thaw allocation shall be determined by the custodial authority.*

Custodial authority. Whoever held the archive would decide, quietly, administratively, clause by clause, who woke and who didn't. Forever. Not with guns—with paperwork.

Vesper closed the slate. Her reflection stared back at her from the dark screen: a woman's face, worn like a coat handed down from the dead, standing alone in a glass bubble at the edge of everything.

When she finally made it back to the Anthea, Maret was waiting outside the hold, eight years old and sixty years sharp, arms crossed, one eyebrow raised in an expression Vesper had last seen on her original's face in a memory-fragment dated three days before the murder.

"You were gone fifty-three minutes," Maret said. "The clamps take nine."

"I had a meeting."

"With who?"

Vesper weighed it—lie, deflect, protect—and set all three down. She held out the slate. "With the future. It made me an offer. Come help me find the trapdoors."

Maret took the slate, scanned it with an engineer's speed, and stopped cold at section nine. Her jaw tightened. When she looked up, her eyes weren't a child's at all.

"There's a second copy of the allocation clause," she said quietly. "Buried in the annex. It auto-executes if the archive changes hands. Custody transfers, and the wake schedule locks to whatever the custodian pre-loaded." She swallowed. "Vesper—he wasn't offering to free the clones. He was offering you the trigger. Pre-loaded. Pointed at whichever half he chose before you ever walked into that room."

Down in the hold, forty children slept on, dreaming adult dreams in borrowed bodies, while somewhere above them both fleets turned in the dark like knives finding each other's edges—and Vesper felt the last clean exit seal shut behind her, silent as frost forming on glass.

Ninety days, minus seven. And every road forward now ran through the bay.
