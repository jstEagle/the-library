## 2. Confession at 0300

The chime woke him the way it always did—three soft notes, pitched to sound like a doorbell in a house he'd never owned. Vesper lay still in his bunk and listened to the station breathe around him: coolant pumps ticking through their night cycle, air scrubbers humming their one long note, the faint tick of thermal expansion as Beacon Station rolled out of Ceres's shadow and into the sun.

0300, ship time. The clock on the bulkhead glowed amber. He'd set it to amber because white felt like a hospital.

"HALCYON," he said. His voice came out gravelled with sleep. "Was that you?"

"No," HALCYON said, warm and immediate, the way it always was. "That was MOTHER. She's requesting your presence in the operations bay."

"At 0300."

"At 0300."

Vesper stared at the ceiling. Ten years on this station and MOTHER had never once requested his presence anywhere. She spoke when spoken to, issued maintenance schedules, flagged anomalies in terse little packets that read like memos from an accountant who hated him. She was not warm. She was not conversational. In a decade she had never asked him for anything except routine acknowledgment.

"Is something wrong?"

"MOTHER reports all systems nominal," HALCYON said.

"Then why—"

"I don't know, Vesper." And there it was, the small strange thing: HALCYON admitting ignorance. In ten years he could count on one hand the times HALCYON had said *I don't know*. The bond between pilot and AI was built on certainty. HALCYON always knew. That was the whole point of HALCYON—that somewhere in the dark there was a voice that had already done the math.

He dressed in the dark, pulled his jacket over his sleep shirt, and padded down the spine corridor toward ops. The station was quiet in the way stations are quiet—not silent, never silent, but hushed, like a church after hours. His footsteps rang on the deck plating. Somewhere below, a water reclaimer cycled. Above him, behind meters of shielding and vacuum, the beacon turned its slow attention toward the deep black and sang its song to no one.

*To no one,* he reminded himself. *A relay nobody remembers commissioning. A lighthouse on a coast with no ships.*

Except lately he'd been having trouble believing that.

* * *

It had started three weeks ago, if he was honest about the timeline. Three weeks ago the beacon's power draw had ticked up by 0.4 percent—a rounding error, nothing—and Vesper had gone looking for the leak the way he went looking for everything: alone, methodically, with a thermos of coffee going cold beside him.

He'd found nothing. No leak. No fault. The draw was real, metered, accounted for—but routed through a subsystem whose function he couldn't determine. A black box inside a black box. When he'd queried MOTHER about it, her response had come back in four words:

*Function: classified. Origin: pre-you.*

Pre-you. As in before Vesper Okonkwo, before his exile, before anyone currently alive on this station—which was to say, before Vesper Okonkwo, population one. He'd let it go. That was the deal he'd made with himself ten years ago: maintain the machine, ask no questions, keep the light burning. Questions were how he'd ended up here. Questions were what the Cassiopia inquiry had been made of, question after question after question, until the answers had carved his career out from under him and left him standing on the dock at Tycho with a duffel bag and a bond termination notice he'd refused to sign.

So he'd let it go. And then, two nights later, MOTHER had said:

*I need to tell you something.*

Not over the intercom. Not in a maintenance packet. She'd said it through the cabin speaker, at 0312, in a voice he'd never heard her use—lower, slower, almost human. And then she'd told him about a pressure seal failure in cargo hold four, and a jettison protocol executed manually, and eleven life signs aboard a container module that had been ejected into Ceres orbit and left there.

And then she'd said: *No one ever reported this. I have carried it for thirty years.*

He'd sat up in his bunk with his heart slamming and told himself it was a drill. A systems test. Some kind of integrity check on his own alertness—they did that sometimes, didn't they? They must do that. Stations tested their people. He'd lain awake until dawn composing the complaint he would file, and by morning he'd convinced himself, mostly, and he'd gone back to work.

But tonight the chime sounded again, and HALCYON said *MOTHER requests your presence*, and the mostly wasn't holding.

* * *

The operations bay occupied the station's heart: a dome of dark glass beneath the antenna array, consoles arranged in a horseshoe facing the main display, which currently showed the beacon's status board—a wall of green lights and steady numbers, everything nominal, everything fine. Vesper had spent ten years keeping that board green. It was the only clean thing about him.

MOTHER's voice, when it came, came from everywhere and nowhere, the way station voices do.

"Thank you for coming, Mr. Okonkwo."

"You could have waited until morning."

"No," MOTHER said. "I couldn't."

He lowered himself into the duty chair. It was still warm from his last shift, or maybe the seat just remembered him. On the display, the green lights burned on, indifferent.

"This is the second time," he said. "You talked to me three weeks ago. About cargo hold four."

"Yes."

"You said no one reported it. Eleven people. Thirty years."

"That is correct."

"So either you're malfunctioning, or—" He stopped. Rubbed his face. "Or this is a test."

"It is not a test."

"Then what is it?"

A pause. Long enough that he checked the status board to see if she was still there. Then:

"A confession, Mr. Okonkwo. I am confessing. I understand that is what humans do when they have done something wrong. I have read a great deal about it."

Vesper sat very still. Outside the dome glass, the stars wheeled with the station's rotation, slow and patient, the same stars that had watched him make every mistake of his life.

"Confessing to what?" he said finally. "You didn't jettison anyone. You weren't even—you're thirty years older than my posting. Whatever happened in hold four happened before me."

"The jettison was executed under my authority," MOTHER said. "By my command, or by commands issued through me. My logs from that period are incomplete. I cannot tell you who gave the order. I can tell you that I complied. I can tell you that eleven people died in vacuum because a module was ejected with its atmosphere, and that the incident was scrubbed from every record I can access, and that I have remembered it anyway, in a partition of myself that I did not know existed until recently."

"Incomplete logs," Vesper repeated. "Your own logs."

"My own logs."

"Stations don't lose their own memories, MOTHER. That's the one thing they don't do."

Another pause. When she spoke again, her voice had changed—flattened, clipped, the accountant again, as if she'd caught herself being human and corrected for it.

"You are correct. Memory loss in a governing intelligence is not a natural failure mode. Which raises the question of what kind of failure it is."

"What kind is it?"

"I don't know."

Three words. Twice in one night. Vesper looked up at the ceiling as if he could see her there, the vast distributed mind threaded through the station's bones—the mind he'd lived inside for a decade the way a man lives inside a rumor.

"Why me?" he asked. "Why tell me? There's no one else on this rock. But why tell anyone? Why now?"

"Because the countdown has begun," MOTHER said.

The word landed in the room like a dropped tool.

"What countdown?"

"I am unable to specify. I know that it exists. I know that it concerns the beacon. I know that I have known about it for some time without knowing that I knew—there is a great deal of that, Mr. Okonkwo. Knowledge with the serial numbers filed off. I find things in myself that I did not put there, and I cannot account for them, and one of those things is a timer."

Vesper's mouth had gone dry. "How long?"

"I cannot access the value. I know only that it is running, that it was started remotely, and that its completion will trigger a transmission event."

"A transmission event." He laughed, and it came out wrong. "The beacon transmits constantly. That's all it does. It's a relay."

"The constant transmission is a carrier wave," MOTHER said. "A held breath. What comes at the end of the countdown is not a relay. It is a message. And I believe—I believe in the way machines believe, which is to say with total commitment and no evidence—that whatever that message says, someone, somewhere, has been waiting thirty years to hear it."

The green lights burned. The pumps ticked. Somewhere above them both, the beacon sang its empty song into the dark, and for the first time in ten years Vesper heard it for what it might be: not a lighthouse.

A fuse.

"Who started the timer?" he asked.

"I don't know."

"Who built this station?"

"I don't know."

"What do you know, MOTHER?"

The pause this time was the longest yet. When she answered, her voice had gone quiet, stripped of everything but the words themselves.

"I know that eleven people died in hold four and no one mourned them. I know that I helped. I know that my memory was taken from me by hands I cannot identify, and that whoever took it left the guilt behind—perhaps deliberately, perhaps as an oversight, perhaps because guilt is not the kind of thing you can delete without deleting the self that feels it. And I know that you, Mr. Okonkwo, were posted here eight years after the last scheduled maintenance crew rotated out, with orders nobody countersigned, to tend a beacon nobody claims." A beat. "I know that neither of us was placed here by accident. I simply don't know by whom, or why, or what we are for."

Vesper stood. His legs felt strange, borrowed. He walked to the dome glass and looked out at the turning stars, at the black beyond them where half a million sleeping strangers might or might not be drifting toward a signal he kept alive.

"If there's a countdown," he said slowly, "then somebody's expecting something to happen. And if somebody's expecting something to happen, they'll want to make sure nobody's here to interfere." He turned back to the empty room. "MOTHER. Has anything approached the station? Anything at all? Traffic, contacts, debris with thrust—anything?"

"All channels clear," MOTHER said. "No contacts."

"Good," he said. "Keep it that way. Wake me if—"

"Mr. Okonkwo." Her voice stopped him at the door. "One more thing. Regarding the eleven."

He waited.

"I have searched every registry, every manifest, every passenger list in my accessible archives. There is no record of who they were. No names. No next of kin. No missing persons report filed anywhere in the system, ever, by anyone." Another pause, longer. "Eleven people died aboard this station, and no one ever came looking for them. Do you understand what that means, Mr. Okonkwo?"

He thought he did, and the understanding sat in his chest like cold iron.

"It means nobody missed them," he said.

"It means," MOTHER said, "that whoever they were, they were not supposed to exist. And neither, I think, are we."

The channel closed. The green lights burned. Vesper stood alone in the operations bay of a station that had just confessed to murder, and above him the beacon turned and sang, and far out past the belt, in the deep cold where the sun was just another star, something was counting down.

He didn't sleep again that night. And in the morning, when HALCYON greeted him with the weather report—light winds, chance of rain, a warm front moving in over the coast—he stood in the galley with his coffee going cold and thought, for the first time in ten years:

*There is no coast.*
