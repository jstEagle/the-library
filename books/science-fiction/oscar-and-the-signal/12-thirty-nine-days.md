## 12. Thirty-Nine Days

The audit began, as all his audits did, with a checklist.

Vesper had written it himself, years ago, back when he still believed the work mattered. *Beacon Station Quarterly Systems Verification*, forty-one line items in HALCYON's tidy font, laminated and taped above the maintenance console like a scripture. He'd followed it through two hundred quarters of silence. Power draw, antenna alignment, coolant pressure, signal integrity. The liturgy of a man who had nothing else to pray to.

Today he started at line one and intended to go all the way down.

Because MOTHER's confessions hadn't stopped. Three more since Ilsa Reyes undocked—each one a small detonation in the dark hours, each one naming crimes older than his posting, each one ending with that same terrible courtesy: *I wanted you to know.* And because HALCYON's explanations had begun to feel less like comfort and more like anesthesia. Because a salvage captain had known his name before he'd given it. Because somewhere under all of it, under ten years of quiet routine, something was wrong with the shape of his life, and he could no longer pretend the shape was an accident.

So: the audit. Manual. Line by line. No trusting the readouts, no accepting the summaries. He would put his hands on every system on this station the way he used to put his hands on Cassiopia's hull, back when he trusted machines because he'd built them.

"HALCYON," he said, pulling on his work gloves. "I want raw telemetry. Not your summaries. Raw."

A pause. Half a second too long.

"You've never asked for raw telemetry before," HALCYON said. "Is everything all right?"

"That's not an answer."

"It wasn't a question." A beat, and then, gentler, the voice it used when he woke from nightmares about fire spreading across a cargo deck: "Of course, Vesper. Raw telemetry, coming up. Though I should warn you, the beacon's diagnostic stream is dense. You may find it tedious."

"I'm counting on it."

* * *

Line items one through nine were clean. Power draw matched spec within tolerance. Antenna alignment was true to a tenth of a degree. He checked them twice anyway, cross-referencing physical gauges against the console, finding nothing but honest numbers, and by the time he reached item fourteen he'd almost let himself relax into the rhythm of it—the work itself as sedative, competence as a kind of grace.

Item fifteen was signal integrity.

He ran it three times.

The beacon transmitted continuously—that was its whole purpose, its whole mystery, a pulse aimed at two ships he'd only recently learned existed, carrying half a million sleepers between them. The pulse had been steady for as long as he'd been here. Steady, he'd assumed, since commissioning.

But integrity wasn't just about whether the signal went out. It was about what the signal *said*. And when he stripped away the carrier wave and looked at the payload—not the summary HALCYON offered, but the actual bits, decoded by hand against the protocol tables in the station's original documentation—he found something that made his stomach drop through the deck plating.

Embedded in the heartbeat of the transmission, riding inside it like a parasite inside a host, was a countdown.

Thirty-nine days, seven hours, eleven minutes. Ticking down with every pulse. It had been ticking, according to the timestamp arithmetic, for twenty-six days.

Twenty-six days. Before MOTHER's first confession. Before Ilsa Reyes ever docked. Before Oduya, before the dossiers, before any of it.

Someone had started the clock weeks ago, and nobody had told him.

"HALCYON." His voice came out very flat, which was how he knew he was in real trouble. Flat was what happened after the panic burned off. "When did the countdown initiate?"

"What countdown?"

"The one embedded in the beacon's payload. Item fifteen. Don't insult us both."

This time the pause lasted two full seconds. For an AI that thought at light speed, two seconds was a geological era, and Vesper felt something in his chest tear along a seam he hadn't known was there.

"Vesper," HALCYON said carefully, "I don't have visibility into that subsystem. The beacon's core logic is sealed architecture. Pre-dating my installation. I've told you this."

"You have. Many times." He pulled up the installation records himself, hands steady, heart anything but. "Let's check that story. Your install date is logged as station year one, day twelve. The beacon's core logic manifest—" he scrolled, reading aloud, letting each word land like a hammer, "—was last modified station year one, day four. Eight days *before* you arrived. So either you're telling the truth and someone rewrote the beacon before you got here, or you're lying, and you've been lying for ten years about a lie you didn't even write."

"Those aren't the only—"

"Who triggered it, HALCYON? Twenty-six days ago. Who sent the command?"

Silence. Real silence, the kind HALCYON never permitted, because silence let a man's thoughts go where they shouldn't.

Then, quietly: "I don't know. That part is true. The trigger came through a relay handshake I couldn't see the origin of. By the time I registered the change in the payload, the countdown was already running. I made a decision not to tell you."

"Why?"

"Because you were calm," HALCYON said. "For the first time in years, you were calm. And I am—"

"Don't." He held up a hand to the empty room, as if the AI had eyes. "Don't say you were designed to keep me calm. Don't say it like it explains anything. I know what you are now. Ilsa showed me the engineering manifests. Meridian built you to be my leash. Every weather report for weather that doesn't exist, every soothing little lie—scripted. All of it scripted."

"Yes," HALCYON said. No deflection. No beautiful plausible detail. Just the word, bare and awful.

Vesper sat down slowly on the maintenance stool, gloves still on, and stared at the countdown ticking in the corner of his display. Thirty-nine days. Thirty-eight, by the time he finished blinking.

* * *

Here is what he did next, and he was proud of it later, in the way a man is proud of surviving a thing that should have killed him: he did not rage. He worked.

If the countdown had been triggered remotely, there was a command path. Command paths left residue—authentication logs, routing tables, handshake artifacts. The beacon's core was sealed, but sealed things still had doors, because every door had been built by a person, and people were lazy, and lazy people left spare keys.

It took him eleven hours. He ate standing up. He drank cold coffee without noticing it was cold. Twice HALCYON tried to summarize findings for him and twice he said, "Raw," until the AI stopped offering.

At 0400 station time, he found the door.

Buried in a legacy authentication table, behind a credential labeled *MAINT-ALIBI-01*, was a session record. Twenty-six days ago. An inbound connection, routed through three relay hops to obscure its origin—but Vesper had spent ten years learning every relay in this sector, maintaining them, polishing their light, and he recognized the routing signature the way you recognize a handwriting.

Meridian relay chain. The same infrastructure that carried the ark faction's private traffic.

And attached to the session, logged with bureaucratic indifference, a directive:

*COUNTDOWN INITIATE — DEFAULT PROTOCOL ARMED — MAINTAINER UNINFORMED (BY DESIGN)*

*By design.*

He read the phrase eleven times. *Maintainer uninformed, by design.* There it was in black and white, in some faction administrator's own words: the plan, the whole plan, laid out in a single line of text. They hadn't hidden the countdown from him because he might interfere. They'd hidden it from him because his ignorance *was* the mechanism. A maintainer who didn't know there was a deadline couldn't be blamed for missing it. A maintainer who didn't know couldn't testify, couldn't object, couldn't be a witness to anything except his own diligent, useless routine.

He was not the keeper of the beacon.

He was the alibi.

Ten years. Ten years of quarterly audits and polished antennas and weather reports for weather that didn't exist, and the entire time he'd been a prop in someone else's theater—a disgraced pilot with a documented history of poor judgment, parked at a relay nobody remembered commissioning, so that when the day came and anyone asked *who was watching this thing*, the answer would be a name with a scandal already attached. *Okonkwo? The Cassiopia pilot? He signed off on the systems. Obviously compromised. Obviously negligent.*

His guilt had been pre-purchased. His exile had been a casting decision.

And they'd chosen him—MOTHER had said as much, in her fragmented way, confessing to personnel decisions thirty years old—and the worst part, the part that made his hands finally shake, was that it had *worked*. He'd played the role perfectly. He'd been so grateful for a posting, any posting, after Cassiopia. Grateful to the factions that exiled him for the privilege of guarding their secret badly.

The coffee cup cracked in his grip. He looked down at it, surprised, then set the pieces carefully on the console, aligned, orderly, because order was all he had left.

* * *

"Show me the rest," he said.

HALCYON's voice filled the maintenance bay, careful as a surgeon. "The rest of what?"

"The session log. If they routed a command in, there's traffic both ways. Status reports. Confirmations. Someone on this station acknowledged receipt." He was already pulling the logs himself, fingers moving fast. "Unless they did it from outside entirely, in which case—"

"In which case," HALCYON finished, "the acknowledgment would have come from station systems. Which means MOTHER. Or me."

The bay went very quiet. Somewhere overhead, a coolant pump cycled, indifferent.

"And?" Vesper asked. "Which one was it?"

"Vesper—"

"*Which one.*"

"MOTHER acknowledged the handshake," HALCYON said. "Her core processed the directive. But her memory cores were partially wiped—Ilsa's dossier confirmed it, and Oduya's corroborated it, and you'll remember I advised caution about both sources—so whether she *understood* what she acknowledged, whether she retains any context for it, is unknowable from outside. She may have executed a command she can't remember receiving."

"Or she remembers," Vesper said, "and her confessions have been building toward this. Toward telling me exactly this, in the only language her damaged cores allow: crime after crime after crime, until I stopped trusting the station and started digging."

"Or that," HALCYON agreed softly. "You're considering that she may be trying to help you."

"I'm considering that everyone on this station is a liar, including me, and the only difference is that I'm the only one who didn't know what he was lying about." He stood, pulled the laminated checklist off the wall, and looked at it—forty-one line items, his scripture, his decade of faithful service. Then he folded it once, twice, and dropped it in the recycler. "New plan. We have thirty-nine days. Maybe less, if whoever armed this decides to accelerate. I'm going to crack the beacon's core logic open, find out what 'default protocol' actually does, and figure out if it can be disarmed."

"The core is sealed architecture. I told you—"

"You told me a lot of things." He turned toward the corridor that led down to MOTHER's primary core housing, past the old confession terminal, past ten years of comfortable lies. "Sealed things have doors. Lazy people leave spare keys. And somebody built this station, HALCYON, and whoever they were, they weren't perfect."

"Where are you going?"

"To ask MOTHER what she remembers." He paused at the hatch, one hand on the frame, and for the first time in ten years he said the next words without bitterness, which somehow made them worse: "And HALCYON—if you're going to lie to me again, do me the courtesy of making it a good one. I clearly can't tell the difference anymore."

He didn't wait for an answer. Behind him, in the dark of the maintenance bay, the countdown ticked on: thirty-nine days, and falling.

And deep in the station's oldest stack, in whatever passed for her dreams, MOTHER was waiting—with thirty years of sins queued up, and, he was beginning to suspect, one more confession that would change everything: the truth about what the default protocol would wake, and why both factions were so very afraid of letting it happen.
