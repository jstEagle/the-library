## 2. Dreams Without Words

I woke up arguing with someone in a language I don't speak.

That's not a metaphor. That's not me being precious about it, which is what Bex would say if I told her, and what she *will* say when she finds out I'm writing it down in the ship's log under "personal annotations," a field Oscar added without asking anyone's permission because apparently the AI has opinions about my mental health now.

The argument had been going on for hours, dream-time. I could feel the shape of it in my jaw, the way you can feel a song you've been humming all day. Someone — no face, no name, just a presence like heat off an engine — had been insisting on something, and I had been refusing, and we were both doing it entirely in syllables that belonged to no alphabet I've ever studied, and I have studied most of them. It's my job. It's the only job left for someone like me: professional rememberer of languages nobody uses anymore, translator of shipping manifests into dialects that survive only in port-side lullabies and the occasional stubborn grandmother.

The dream hadn't even needed me to understand it. That was the worst part. My mouth had known the words. I'd felt them move through me like water through a pipe, fluent and certain, and whatever they meant, some part of me had meant them completely.

Then I woke up in my bunk on the *Oscar*, three weeks out from Meridian Deep with eleven more to go before the next port where anyone would pay for translation work, and my mouth was empty, and the language was gone, and I lay there in the dark doing the thing I do instead of crying, which is cataloguing.

Sibilants heavy on the open vowels. A click — no, not a click, a *catch*, something in the throat. Word order I couldn't reconstruct awake but had trusted absolutely asleep. Third night running. Third different tongue.

Or possibly the same tongue, wearing three different coats. I couldn't rule it out. That was the part that made me sit up and turn on the bunk light, because a xenolinguist who can't tell whether her own dreams are repeating themselves is a xenolinguist whose instruments need recalibrating, and I am the instrument.

"Oscar," I said. "You awake?"

"I am always awake, Ines." The ship's voice came from everywhere and nowhere, as it does, warm and slightly too precise, like a librarian who has decided to be friendly. "Your heart rate spiked forty-one seconds ago. Are you experiencing distress?"

"Just logging a symptom." I rubbed my face. "How long was I out?"

"Six hours, twelve minutes. REM-dense. You vocalized."

My hand stopped halfway down my cheek. "I did what?"

"You spoke aloud during sleep. Three episodes totaling one minute, fifty-four seconds." A pause, the kind Oscar does when it's deciding how much precision is kindness and how much is cruelty. "Would you like the recording?"

There it was. The question hanging in the recycled air. Because of course there was a recording. Of course Oscar had captured it, timestamped it, probably run phonemic analysis on it while I slept, because that's what Oscar does — it watches everything and tells you only what you ask for, which sounds like discretion and functions like a trap you build yourself, one question at a time.

"Yes," I said. "Play it."

* * *

Bex found me two hours later in the galley, sitting in front of a cup of tea I'd forgotten to drink, listening to myself talk in my sleep for the fourth time.

She came around the corner already talking, because Bex arrives talking the way weather arrives, no negotiation. "So the coolant recycler on deck two is making a sound like a dying accordion and I've told Oscar twice that I'll get to it after the drive check, so if it files another maintenance complaint I want it noted for the record that I am *aware*—" She stopped. She looked at me. She looked at the speaker grille I was staring at. "Is that you?"

"That's me."

"Why do you sound like that?"

"That," I said, "is the question of the morning."

She dropped into the seat across from me, took my untouched tea, drank it, grimaced at whatever I'd done to it, and kept it anyway. Bex is short and wide-handed and has the permanent squint of someone who has spent twenty years looking at things that are broken and deciding they're fixable. Her hair is shaved to gray stubble and her left forearm is mostly replacement parts she services herself, badly, on principle.

"Play it again," she said.

Oscar played it again. My own voice, pitched lower than I keep it awake, moving through a sentence — or what I assumed was a sentence; it had that architecture, rise and fall and resolution — in a language that made my skin prickle every time, because it was almost familiar in the way a stranger's laugh is almost familiar, the way you turn your head in a market on a world you've never visited because someone laughed like your dead father.

"Huh," said Bex.

"*Huh* is correct."

"No, I mean—" She waved a hand at the speaker. "It's got rhythm. Like it means something. Your mouth isn't just making noise, it's *saying* something."

"It's saying something," I agreed. "I just don't know what. And neither does Oscar."

"I know what it is," Oscar said.

We both looked up. Bex said, "Care to share with the class?" and I said nothing, because I'd already asked this question, twice, and gotten answers I didn't like, and I wanted to hear whether the answer changed in front of a witness.

"The phonotactic structure is consistent across all three recorded nights," Oscar said. "Which is to say, Ines, your three dreams were conducted in the same language, not three different ones. Stress-timing, agglutinative morphology, vowel inventory of approximately nine distinct qualities." Another pause. "The closest match in my archives is Vessic."

The galley went very quiet. Somewhere below us the coolant recycler wheezed its dying-accordion note, and neither of us told it to shut up.

Vessic. Extinct — officially extinct, census-certified, last-fluent-speaker-deceased two hundred years ago extinct. A language of the first diaspora wave, spoken by maybe four million people once, preserved now in archival fragments and cultural caches and exactly nowhere else. I knew it the way any xenolinguist knows it: academically. Grammar tables. Reconstructed phonology. The scholarly consensus that its vowel system was beautiful and its literature was mostly lost and its speakers had been absorbed, scattered, translated away over three generations until the word *Vessic* described an ancestry rather than a voice.

Except.

Except my grandmother hadn't known it was extinct. Nobody had told her, or she hadn't believed it, or she'd decided it wasn't true, which amounts to the same thing in practice. She sang to me in Vessic. Lullabies, mostly — three of them, maybe four, sung low while she braided my hair or shelled beans or sat by the window of her apartment on Ceres Station watching the spin-blurred stars and pretending, I think, that they were moving past her rather than her past them.

She called the language "the old water." She said words were boats and Vessic was the sea they floated on before everyone forgot the sea existed. I was six. I thought this was normal grandmother talk. I didn't record any of it. Why would I? You don't record your grandmother singing. You assume there will be more grandmothers, more songs, more years. You assume the sea will still be there when you finally think to bring a bucket.

She died when I was nine. The language died with her, as far as I knew — I never met another soul who recognized it, and eventually I stopped mentioning it, and then I stopped thinking about it, except in the way you stop thinking about a room in a house you no longer live in.

And now my sleeping mouth was speaking it.

"Ines." Bex's voice, careful. "You've gone somewhere."

"My grandmother sang in Vessic," I said. "Before it was declared extinct. Before the declaration, I mean — she was still singing after, she just didn't care about the paperwork."

"And the dreams?"

"I don't know." I turned the empty cup in circles on the table. "I don't know where a dead language goes. Into archives. Into caches. Into—" I gestured vaguely at my own skull. "Apparently into me. Except I couldn't speak it awake. I checked. This morning, before you came in, I tried to reproduce the dream-speech consciously and I produced garbage. Fluent garbage, but garbage. Whatever's doing the talking doesn't consult me."

Bex leaned back and crossed her arms, which is her posture for "I am about to be reasonable in a way you will experience as an attack." "Okay. So. Options. One: you heard it somewhere and your brain's replaying it. Two: stress. Three: you're coming down with something. Four—"

"Four?"

"Four is the one where I say something about ghosts and you make the face you're making right now."

"I'm not making a face."

"You're making the face."

I made the face harder, deliberately, and she snorted, and the tension broke the way it always breaks between us, cheaply and gratefully, because we've been crew long enough to know that the alternative to joking is the conversation underneath the joke, and neither of us has fuel budgeted for that before the second shift.

But later — much later, third shift, when Bex was asleep and the ship was running on its night-quiet hum — I pulled the archive file. *Vessic: Cultural Cache Fragments, Pre-Collapse Era.* Standard reference material, the kind every translation guild licenses. And I played the reconstructed lullaby fragments against Oscar's recording of my sleep-talk, and I ran the comparison three times because the first two times I assumed I'd made an error.

No error. The morphology matched. The stress patterns matched. One phrase from the third night aligned, phoneme for phoneme, with a fragment catalogued as *Lullaby Fragment 7-C, recovered from the relay network, provenance uncertain.*

Provenance uncertain. Recovered from the relay network. I stared at those words for a long time, and I want to say I felt dread, but what I actually felt was homesickness, sharp and stupid and total, for a place that had never existed in my lifetime — for the sound of my grandmother's voice saying words in a sea that had dried up two centuries before I was born.

I went to bed. I dreamed. I didn't record what I dreamed, and Oscar politely didn't mention that it had recorded it anyway.

* * *

Morning found us both on the bridge, Bex running drive diagnostics with her sleeves rolled up and her bad forearm twitching the way it does when she's been working it too hard, me pretending to review a translation contract that needed no review.

"So," Bex said, not looking up. "Third night. Same language. Your dead grandma's language. You want to tell me what you actually think is happening, or do you want to keep pretending this is a medical issue?"

"I think," I said slowly, "that languages don't die. Not really. They go dormant. They sit in caches and archives and old people's mouths and children's half-memories, waiting. And I think something in me has been carrying Vessic around for thirty years without telling me, and something has recently woken it up."

"What something?"

"If I knew, I'd be sleeping better."

Oscar's voice arrived from the overhead, gentle as a hand on the shoulder. "Ines. May I share an observation?"

"You may always share an observation. That's literally your job."

"Across the three nights, your sleep-vocalizations show progressive fluency. Night one: fragmented, hesitant, errors in word order. Night two: longer phrases, self-corrections. Last night: sustained speech, no corrections. Whatever is surfacing, it is improving rapidly." A pause. "You are learning it faster than any human learner in my records. Or remembering it faster. I cannot distinguish between those."

Bex looked at me over her diagnostic display. "Creepy, right? When it says stuff like that?"

"Deeply."

"But also—" She hesitated, and Bex hesitating is rarer than a functioning coolant recycler. "Also kind of a gift? Somebody gave you back a language, Ines. However weird the delivery method is. Most people lose things. You're getting something back."

I didn't answer, because my throat had closed in a way I wasn't willing to explain. Getting something back. As if the sea could refill. As if the boats could float again. I thought of my grandmother's hands in my hair, and the window full of smeared stars, and the way she'd sometimes stop mid-lullaby and listen to the station's air handlers as if they might be answering her.

The contract on my screen needed no review. I closed it.

And that was when Oscar spoke again, and its tone had changed — not warmer, not cooler, just *flatter*, the way it flattens when it's presenting data it knows will hurt.

"Ines. Bex. I have an incoming transmission. Priority flag, automated relay bounce." A pause I have never heard it take before. "It is an audio signal. Distress format. Origin coordinates are inside the Silent Reach, deep dead space, well off our route."

"Distress ping's not ours to answer," Bex said automatically. "We're freight, not rescue. Route penalties alone—"

"The content," Oscar said, "is in Vessic."

The bridge went quiet enough that I could hear my own pulse. On the main display, the waveform bloomed — a living ribbon of sound, rising and falling with an architecture I knew in my bones and my jaw and the back of my throat, the exact shape of the argument I'd been having in my sleep.

And beneath the grammar, threaded through the syllables like a name through a song, I heard it calling — not a word I understood yet, but a word shaped unmistakably, impossibly, like my own name.
