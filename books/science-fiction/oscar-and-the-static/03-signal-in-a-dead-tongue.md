## 3. Signal in a Dead Tongue

The ping arrived at 0347 ship-time, which is to say it arrived at the hour when I am least a person and most a collection of anxieties wearing a jumpsuit. I was awake anyway — third night running — because my dreams had taken up a new hobby of speaking to me in languages I don't know, and waking from them felt less like surfacing and more like being handed back.

I was in the galley, heating water I would forget about until it boiled dry, when the receiver chimed. Not the routine chime. The *other* one. The one Oscar uses for traffic that doesn't fit any expected category, which on the Long Route means roughly never.

"Ines," Oscar said, before I'd even reached the console. "You should look at this."

"You say that like I have a choice."

"You don't. That's why I said it like that."

The console showed me coordinates first, and the coordinates were the first wrong thing. Dead space. Not deep space — dead space, the stretch past the boundary markers where the charts go thin as old paper and the relays went dark during the Collapse and nobody bothered going back, because there was nothing out there worth the fuel except the silence itself. Freighters didn't get distress pings from dead space. Ghosts didn't either, officially.

Then the text scrolled up, and I stopped being cold, because every drop of blood in my body relocated to my face at once.

It was Vessic.

I want to be careful here, because "Vessic" is a word that does things to me that I can't always afford on shift. It's not just a language. It's my grandmother's voice singing *lullu en vasha* over the hum of a colony habitat's air recyclers, her fingers working braids into my hair, the words half-lost even then because she was one of maybe four hundred people left who spoke them. Two centuries since anyone used Vessic for anything but academic papers and funeral eulogies. Declared extinct the year after she died, as if the paperwork had been waiting for her personally.

And now a distress signal, formatted correctly, addressed properly, written in fluent living Vessic, was asking — by name — for the freighter *Oscar*.

Not by registry number. By name. And the name wasn't the ship's.

*Ines Coldharbour,* it said. *If you hear this: answer.*

I read it three times. My hands were doing something interesting on the console, some tremor I had no memory of authorizing. On the fourth read I noticed the grammar, and the grammar was what finally scared me properly, because it wasn't textbook Vessic. It wasn't archival Vessic, the reconstructed formality you find in the linguistic databases, all careful case-endings and reconstructed vowels. It was *living* Vessic. It bent the way spoken language bends. It made the small informal contractions that only exist when people actually talk to each other — the kind of thing you can't fake from a corpus, the kind of thing that has to breathe.

My grandmother talked like that.

"Oscar," I said. My voice came out strange. "Run verification."

"Running," Oscar said. A pause. "Against which archive?"

"All of them. Colonial-era, pre-Collapse, the university reconstructions, everything."

"That will take eleven minutes."

"Take ten."

* * *

Bex found me still standing at the console when the timer ran out, which Bex later claimed was suspicious in itself, because nobody stands at a console for ten minutes unless something is either broken or haunted.

"What've we got?" Bex said, coffee bulb in hand, hair flattened on one side from the bunk pillow. Bex sleeps the way Bex does everything: efficiently, defensively, one ear open for machinery misbehaving.

"A distress signal."

"From where?"

"Dead space."

Bex took a long sip. "Nope. Try again."

"Coordinates inside the Silent Reach proper. Past the boundary markers. Broadcast in Vessic."

The coffee bulb stopped halfway to Bex's mouth. I watched the skepticism assemble itself behind their eyes like scaffolding — I've seen Bex build that structure around bad news a hundred times, and it goes up fast and holds enormous weight.

"Vessic," Bex repeated. "The dead language."

"The extinct language. There's a difference. Extinct means no speakers. Dead means—"

"It means two hundred years, Ines. It means nobody alive speaks it, which means nobody sent it, which means either your receiver's hallucinating or someone's playing a very expensive joke." Bex set the bulb down. "What's the verification say?"

"Grammatical match to living colloquial Vessic: ninety-four percent. Match to grandmother's recorded speech patterns—" I hesitated, because saying the next part out loud would make it real, and I hadn't finished deciding whether I wanted it to be real. "Eighty-eight."

Bex was quiet for a moment. Then, carefully: "Why do we have your grandmother's speech patterns on file?"

"Because I uploaded them. Year two of the route. For the translation work." I shrugged, and the shrug felt like it belonged to someone else's body. "Sentimental archive maintenance."

"So the signal matches a language that's extinct, and specifically matches the speech patterns of your dead grandmother, whom you personally uploaded to our ship's database." Bex picked the coffee back up. "Ines. I want you to hear how that sounds."

"I know how it sounds."

"Do you? Because from where I'm standing it sounds like either the cruelest coincidence in shipping history or the setup to something. And I've been on the wrong end of 'something' before."

I had forgotten, briefly — genuinely, completely forgotten — about Bex's family. About the rescue scam, the fake distress calls, the year Bex spent chasing a signal that turned out to be bait for salvage crews, and what it cost. It came back to me now the way those things do: all at once, with interest.

"Bex—"

"Don't." Bex held up a hand. "Don't apologize yet. First tell me why you're already packed."

I looked down. I was, in fact, holding my flight kit.

* * *

We argued about it for six hours, which for a crew of two plus an AI is practically a constitutional convention.

The facts were these: we were nine days out from Meridian Deep with a contract load of medical-grade polymer stock, penalty clause for deviation sitting at forty percent of the run's value — ruinous, route-ending, possibly career-ending. The signal originated thirty-one hours' burn off-route, inside dead space, where our insurance didn't reach and our charts didn't go. The signal was repeating on a cycle. The cycle was decaying.

That last part was Oscar's finding, and it changed the shape of the argument entirely.

"The carrier wave is degrading," Oscar told us both, projecting the waveform between us like a referee laying down a ball. "Extrapolating the decay curve, the transmission terminates in approximately nineteen days. After that, whatever source produced it goes silent permanently."

"Or," Bex said, "the scammer's equipment dies, same as it did last time, and everyone who chased it loses everything."

"This isn't last time," I said.

"No. Last time they used my sister's voice." Bex's voice stayed level, which somehow made it worse. "They spliced it from her last call home. Played it back on a loop with a decay curve too, Ines. Very convincing. Very scientific. Three ships answered. One made it back."

I didn't have anything to say to that. There isn't anything to say to that. You can't argue someone out of a wound; you can only stand near it and hope your standing counts for something.

But the signal kept cycling while we argued, and I kept listening to it, and every cycle it did something that tightened another turn around me. Because it wasn't just grammatically alive. It was *addressed*. Somewhere in the second day-cycle of repeats — I'd been keeping the receiver open against Bex's explicit objection — the wording shifted. Slightly. The way a person rephrases when they think they haven't been understood.

*Ines Coldharbour. If you hear this: answer.* Became: *Ines Coldharbour. We know you can hear this. Please.*

Please. In Vessic, *please* carries a particle that doesn't translate — a softening suffix, *-veth*, that my grandmother used exactly twice that I remember: once when asking the habitat council not to decommission the language program, and once, at the end, when she asked me to sing the lullabies back to her so she could check I still had them right.

This signal used *-veth* correctly. Casually. Like breathing.

"Oscar," I said, when Bex had gone to the engine room to be angry somewhere with better acoustics. "Probability assessment. Honest one."

"Honest assessment requires acknowledging insufficient data," Oscar said. "However: the probability that a hoaxer could generate syntactically native colloquial Vessic, including non-translatable pragmatic particles, approaches zero. The archives are incomplete. Whoever composed this knew forms of the language that exist nowhere in any database I can access."

"Meaning?"

"Meaning the signal knows Vessic better than we do." A pause, which Oscar deploys the way other people deploy silence — deliberately, for effect. "Ines, I have processed every transmission on this route for six years. This is the first one that appears to be *speaking* rather than broadcasting."

I sat with that. Around me the ship hummed its small mechanical prayers, and somewhere inside my skull a dream-language I couldn't name turned over in its sleep.

"There's one more thing," Oscar said.

"Of course there is."

"The signal addresses you by name. Your name does not appear in any public registry associated with this vessel. Our transponder identifies us as the *Oscar*, registered to Coldharbour Transit, sole operator listed under initials only — your preference, from the privacy protocols you installed in year one." Another pause. "For the signal to use your full name, it would need information that exists in exactly one place I'm aware of."

The galley felt very small. "Which is?"

"My cultural cache. The archive files you loaded alongside your grandmother's recordings. Your name appears in the metadata of the lullaby files. Sung by, dedicated to." Oscar's voice did the thing it does when it's being gentle, which is become slightly more precise than usual. "Ines. Either someone stole that data, or something read it. I don't know which possibility I like less."

* * *

Bex came back up at second-shift change smelling of coolant and stubbornness, and found me still at the console, and didn't ask what I'd decided, because Bex had already read it off my spine.

"Forty percent penalty," Bex said. "Nine days late minimum, if the burn math even works, which I'll know by morning and I already hate the number. Insurance void past the boundary markers. No rescue coverage in dead space — if we break something out there, we fix it ourselves or we die politely." Bex counted it off on grease-stained fingers. "And in exchange we get to chase a ghost that knows your name, toward coordinates that are famous for eating ships, based on a language that's been dead since before either of us was born."

"Yes."

"Say the actual yes, Ines. Out loud. I want it on record that you chose this."

I turned around in the chair. Bex stood in the hatchway with arms crossed and jaw set, and underneath all of it — under the skepticism and the scaffolding and the armor — was the question Bex never asks directly, which is: *are you going to be worth following?*

"I heard my grandmother's language come out of empty space tonight," I said. "Addressed to me. Asking in her voice's grammar for help. I could log the signal, report it to the Route Authority, and spend the rest of my life wondering, and the Route Authority would take eleven months to send a survey buoy that would find nothing, because the signal will be dead by then." I swallowed. "Or I can answer. Bex — it said *please*. With the *-veth*. Nobody alive knows what that means. Whatever's out there, it isn't a scammer with a splice rig. Scammers want cargo. This wants *conversation*."

"That's either better or much worse."

"Yes," I agreed. "Both. Probably both."

Bex looked at me for a long moment. Then uncrossed their arms, which from Bex is a treaty signature.

"Nineteen days of decay curve," Bex said. "Burn window closes in six hours if I want any margin at all. I'll start recalibrating the drive for a burn no freighter should make, and you—" they pointed at me, and their finger was not entirely steady, "—you are going to write a deviation log so clean the Route Authority auditors weep with boredom. Full justification. Sensor anomaly, possible navigational hazard, precautionary investigation. Lie beautifully, Ines. It's the one thing you do better than me."

"I don't lie."

"You forge documents. Same thing with better grammar." Bex turned for the engine room, then stopped, one hand on the hatch frame, back still to me. "My sister's voice was on that loop for eight months before anyone admitted it was fake. Eight months of me listening to a dead woman ask for help." The hand tightened on the frame. "So if this turns out to be that — if something out there is wearing your grandmother to get at you — you let me be the one who says stop. Promise me that. Because you won't be able to. You're already too far inside it. I can see it from here."

I promised. It cost more than I expected it to.

"And Ines?" Bex said, still not turning around.

"Yeah."

"When we divert — log it as my call. Pilot override, engineer concurrence. If the Authority comes asking, it was my ghost we chased, not yours."

"Bex—"

"Consider it interest on a debt." And they were gone, boots ringing down the corridor toward the drive housing, leaving me alone with the console and the signal, which cycled again as I watched, patient as tide:

*Ines Coldharbour. We know you can hear this. Please.*

I opened the deviation log. I put my hands on the keys. And before I typed a single character of the beautiful lie, I keyed the transmitter, and — knowing Bex would hear it, knowing Oscar would log it, knowing it violated about four sections of protocol and every instinct of self-preservation the Long Route had taught me — I answered.

One word. In Vessic. The word my grandmother used when she wanted to know if I was still carrying the songs.

*"Lashen?"*

Are you there?

The signal stopped mid-cycle.

For eleven full seconds, the receiver held nothing but the sound of dead space — and then, faint and fractured and unmistakably delighted, something began to speak back.
