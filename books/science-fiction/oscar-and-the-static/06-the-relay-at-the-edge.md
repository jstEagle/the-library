## 6. The Relay at the Edge

There is a particular kind of silence that comes when a ship stops pretending it's going anywhere else. The Oscar's drive hummed down to a whisper, the burn ended, and Bex and I stood in the cockpit with our coffee going cold, looking at the thing we had crossed dead space to find.

It hung against nothing like a splinter against skin.

"Is that it?" Bex asked, though obviously it was.

"That's it."

"It's smaller than I expected."

Everything is smaller than you expect when you've spent two weeks imagining it. But the relay was small in the way that old things are small — compacted, dense, built in an era when engineers still believed the future would be grateful for their thrift. A pre-Collapse long-range relay: a spine of trusses maybe four hundred meters end to end, studded with antenna farms that had once combed the dark for voices and now mostly combed it for dust. Its hull wore two centuries of micrometeorite scarring like a face gone gray. Patches of original paint survived in sheltered creases — a corporate livery, faded to the color of an old bruise — and where the sun caught them just right, the whole station glimmered, briefly, like something remembering it used to be beautiful.

And it was transmitting. That was the part that made my chest hurt. Even at this distance, even before Oscar sharpened the feed, I could feel it — not hear it exactly, more like standing near a wall with plumbing behind it. A carrier wave, thin and patient, running on a decay curve so gentle it would take another few days to reach zero. Earth's last transmission, still leaving home, two hundred years late, addressed to everyone and delivered to no one.

Until us.

"Signal source confirmed," Oscar said. "Origin point is interior. Deck three, communications core." A pause — Oscar's pauses were always calibrated, never accidental. "The distress call and the loop are coming from the same room."

"Cozy," said Bex.

I didn't say anything. I was listening to the loop through the good headphones, the ones I used for archival work, and I was trying very hard to be a professional about what I heard.

Here is what professional sounds like: *Carrier wave stable. Modulation consistent with pre-Collapse broadcast standards. Content: human voice, male-presenting, approximately sixty years of age. Emotional register: composed.* Here is what it actually was: a man saying goodbye to a planet that hadn't finished dying yet, his voice worn smooth by repetition the way river stones are worn smooth, and underneath him, faint as breath, a second voice — newer, wronger, stitched into the gaps between his sentences like a bird nesting in a statue's mouth.

"You're doing the face," Bex said.

"What face?"

"The face where you're translating something and it's translating you back."

I took off the headphones. "We should dock."

* * *

Approach took six hours, because I insisted on six hours. Bex wanted two. There is an argument you have on every ship eventually between the person who wants to arrive and the person who wants to arrive *correctly*, and Bex and I have had it enough times that we can conduct it now almost wordlessly — a sigh, a raised eyebrow, a hand drifting meaningfully toward the throttle.

"Final approach vector looks clean," Bex said, which was true, which is why the next sentence landed the way it did. "Docking collar's on the dorsal spine, port side. Standard coupling geometry. This should be boring."

"Boring would be nice," I said. "We've earned some boring."

Oscar ran the approach. I let the ship fly itself for this part because Oscar flies better than I do and because I wanted my hands free — free for the receiver, free for the signal, free for the voice that was getting louder in my headphones with every kilometer, the way a name gets louder as you walk toward the house where the person who says it lives.

The loop, up close, resolved into layers. That's the thing nobody tells you about old recordings: they accrete. Every replay leaves a residue, every re-broadcast picks up noise, and after two hundred years the message wasn't one message anymore but a sediment — the man's farewell at the bottom, then decades of relay chatter fossilized above him, maintenance pings, automated weather reports from a world that no longer had weather worth reporting, and then, near the top, freshest, strangest, the thing that had called us here: Vessic. Fluent, living Vessic, spoken with an accent I knew in my bones because it was my grandmother's accent, because it was the accent of a language that had been dead longer than I'd been alive.

"*Sela veth,*" it said, and my hands went cold on the console. *Come home.* Or possibly *come here*. The grammar allowed both; Vessic had never seen much use for a hard line between the two.

"Ines." Oscar's voice, gentle. "Your heart rate."

"I know."

"You've been clenching your jaw for eleven minutes."

"I *know*, Oscar."

Bex glanced over. Said nothing. Bex has learned when my silences are load-bearing.

We came around the station's spine at fifty meters, close enough to read the scars individually — weld beads from repairs made by hands two hundred years dead, a hull breach sealed with a plate whose serial number matched no registry that still existed, and, near the docking collar, a row of stenciled letters half-eaten by corrosion. Oscar enhanced them. **VESIC CULTURAL RELAY — NODE 7**.

I read it three times. My grandmother's language, stamped on a dying machine at the edge of nowhere, and I swear to you my first thought was not wonder. It was: *she never told me she worked out here.* Which is a stupid thought, because she died when I was nine, and nine-year-olds don't ask their grandmothers about their routing assignments, they ask for another song.

"Preparing docking sequence," Oscar said. "Contact in ninety seconds."

The collar grew in the viewport. Rust-orange, ringed with guide lights, three of which still worked. The others blinked on a delay, ghosts of a full set, and there was something unbearable about those three working lights — the diligence of them, keeping their little appointment with the dark for two hundred years on the chance someone might come.

Seventy seconds. Fifty. The thrusters breathed. Bex called distances in a flat pilot's cadence — *thirty meters, twenty, ten* — and I watched the collar fill the glass and felt, absurdly, like a child approaching a door behind which a parent was waiting, and I want to be honest that I don't know where that feeling came from except everywhere.

At eight meters, everything went wrong at once.

Not explosively. Quietly, which is worse. The Oscar shuddered — a deep structural shiver, like a dog shaking off water — and every magnetic indicator on my board swung red simultaneously. The ship yawed ten degrees without our asking it to. Bex swore with real artistry and killed the approach thrusters, and we hung there, eight meters off the collar, being slowly, patiently pulled sideways by a force that had not been there thirty seconds ago.

"Magnetic shear," Bex snapped. "Off the scale. Where is it even *coming from* — there's no field generator big enough on that hulk to—" The ship lurched again. "Oscar, give me numbers."

"Field strength tripling every forty seconds," Oscar reported. "Source appears to be distributed across the entire station structure. It is not localized. It is—" another pause, and I have known Oscar long enough to hear the machinery behind these pauses, the AI choosing words the way a careful person chooses steps on ice, "—it is behaving less like a field and more like a grip."

"Back us off," I said.

"We are backing off," Bex said, hands moving. "We are backing off *enthusiastically*."

The Oscar fell away from the station, thrusters flaring, and for one long moment the magnets held us anyway — a tug at the hull, insistent, almost personal, like fingers reluctant to open — and then we tore free and drifted out to a safe thousand meters, and the cockpit was very quiet except for Bex's breathing and the tick of cooling systems.

"Well," Bex said finally. "That's new."

"Damage report."

"Hull's fine. Drive's fine. My dignity has a dent in it." Bex turned around in the pilot's chair, and I saw the look underneath the wryness, the one I'd been dreading since the diversion. "Ines. Freighters don't get grabbed by dead stations. That wasn't interference. Interference doesn't *want* anything."

I didn't answer right away, because I was looking at the receiver readout, where the Vessic signal — the distress call, the voice wearing my grandmother's accent — had changed while we were being pushed away. Not stopped. Changed. The loop had been a loop for however long it had been calling: same phrases, same cadence, a prayer worn into a groove. Now the groove was broken. New words. Slower ones. Spaced apart like someone choosing each syllable with tongs.

Oscar translated the phonetics for me, though translation was beside the point. I understood it natively, the way you understand your own name.

"*You came,*" the station said. "*Why did you leave?*"

Bex saw my face and went pale. "What did it say?"

I looked out the viewport at the scarred hull, at the three stubborn guide lights blinking their patient arithmetic, at the monument that refused to finish dying. Two hundred years of goodbye, and the first time anyone answered, we'd pulled away at eight meters.

"It asked why we left," I said.

Bex stared at me. Then at the station. Then at me again, with the expression of a person recalculating every decision that had led them into their current life.

"Nope," Bex said. "No. Absolutely not. We are not having a conversation with a magnet."

"It's not a magnet."

"Then what is it?"

And the honest answer — the answer I could feel sitting in my chest like a swallowed stone, the answer that had been forming since the first night I dreamed in a tongue I didn't know — was one I wasn't ready to say out loud, because saying it would make it real, and because Bex was already at the edge of trusting me, and because some part of me understood, with terrible clarity, that whatever lived inside that station had been alone for two centuries, and I knew what alone did to a mind. I was living proof.

"It's lonely," I said. "It's been lonely for two hundred years, and it doesn't know how to ask people to stay."

Bex opened their mouth. Closed it. Looked at the station for a long moment — at the welded seams, the ghost lights, the stenciled letters carrying my grandmother's language out into the dark like a message in a bottle that had finally, impossibly, washed ashore.

"So we go back," Bex said at last, in the tone of someone agreeing to a thing they will complain about forever. "But if it grabs us again, I'm cutting the collar off with a torch and billing the corpse."

"Fair."

"And Ines?" Bex's voice dropped, lost its armor for exactly one sentence. "Whatever's in there — whatever it is — you talk to it. Not me. Deal?"

"Deal," I said, and turned back to the console, and began, with hands that were not entirely steady, plotting a second approach.

Behind us, patient as tide, the carrier wave went on decaying. Days left. Maybe fewer. And beneath the farewell of a dead world, softer now, almost shy, the voice in the wiring repeated its question to the empty space where our ship had been:

"*You came. Why did you leave?*"
