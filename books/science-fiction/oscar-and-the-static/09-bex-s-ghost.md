## 9. Bex's Ghost

I want it on the record — my record, the one I keep in my head, which is the only record that can't be subpoenaed or corrupted by an AI with a guilt complex — that I said we should turn around. Not in those words, maybe. My words were "this is a stupid idea and you're going to get us killed," which is the same sentence wearing work clothes.

But nobody consults the engineer until something's on fire, and by then it's too late for opinions.

So here we were, day three inside dead space, docked to a two-hundred-year-old corpse of a relay station that had somehow learned to say *please*, and I was doing what I always do when the universe gets weird: I was running cable.

The station's spine was a kilometer of conduit and catwalk, most of it dark, some of it humming, all of it older than both of us put together. Ines was up in the archive room doing whatever xenolinguists do — listening, mostly, which sounds like nothing until you watch them do it and realize they're working harder than any welder alive. And Oscar was Oscar: everywhere at once, talking to the walls, getting talked back to by things that should not have mouths.

Me, I had a job. Map the power runs. Figure out how much juice the old girl had left in her batteries before the transfer plan — and yes, there was already a transfer plan, because Ines had looked at this haunted house for approximately nine hours before deciding it was a rescue mission — became possible.

Simple work. Honest work. Work where the thing on the other end of your multimeter doesn't have feelings.

That lasted about four hours.

* * *

The speakers started at 1400 ship-time, though "ship-time" is a polite fiction down here; the station kept its own clock and it was wrong in ways that made my teeth itch. I was in Junction C-9, flat on my back under a distribution bus, tracing a line that went nowhere, when the panel above my head clicked.

Not powered up. Clicked. Like a throat clearing.

Then a voice came out of it.

"—repeat, this is an automated distress relay, vessel *Kindly Fortune*, requesting immediate assistance, we have eleven aboard and life support failing—"

I knew the voice.

You know how you know a voice? Not the words. The voice. The particular way a person's fear sits in their throat, the crack on the second syllable of *assistance*. I'd heard this exact call four hundred times, because for two years after the Meridian scam broke, the recovery boards played it on loop as evidence. Exhibit A. The last transmission of Tam Okafor, cargo hauler, forty-one years old, father of two, who answered a distress ping out past Calloway's Drift because he was the kind of man who answered distress pings, and got boarded by people who'd faked the signal to draw ships into gun range.

They took his cargo. They took his ship. They spaced him and ten others and left the eleventh — a kid, sixteen, who'd hidden in a coolant duct — to tell everyone what happened.

Tam Okafor died four light-years from here, twenty-two years ago, in a system I could point to blindfolded.

And here he was, in a junction box on a derelict relay at the ass-end of dead space, asking for help again.

"Automated distress relay," the speaker said, patient as rot. "Vessel *Kindly Fortune*. Requesting immediate assistance."

"No," I said, to a wall, like a person having a normal day.

The click moved. Down the corridor, another panel woke, and Tam asked the empty air for help from a different direction. Then another. Then another, further off, voices layering now — not just Tam, a dozen calls, dozens, the whole sad chorus of every fake distress signal ever broadcast in the shipping lanes, the bait calls, the ones designed to sound exactly desperate enough to be believed.

Somebody had collected them. Somebody had *archived* them.

Or somebody had learned them.

I got off the floor fast enough to bang my head on the bus, which I barely felt, which tells you something. I stood in the middle of Junction C-9 turning in a slow circle while dead men begged me for help from every wall, and I did the math I didn't want to do:

A relay station sits at the edge of traffic corridors. A relay station *hears everything*. Two centuries of everything. Every mayday, every scam call, every last word that ever crossed these lanes and got caught in the net of its receivers.

Including the ones that never should have reached this far out.

Including, apparently, a murder broadcast from a system that wasn't even connected to this relay's network when it happened.

Unless someone carried it here. Unless someone fed it here.

Unless this whole thing — the Vessic signal, the fluent, living, impossible Vessic signal that sang a dead language into Ines's ear like a lure made of lullabies — unless all of it was the oldest trick in the book, dressed up in the newest clothes.

Bait calls. That's what they're called in the trade. Bait calls. And I was standing inside the hook.

* * *

I don't remember walking back to the ship. I remember arriving, which is worse — remember coming through the airlock with my hands shaking so bad I had to grip the rail, and finding Ines in the galley with two cups of tea going cold, looking like someone who'd been crying and decided to be embarrassed about it later.

"Bex." They saw my face and set the cups down. "What happened?"

"You want to know what happened?" My voice came out level, which surprised me. Level is what I do instead of screaming; ask anyone I've ever loved. "I'll tell you what happened. I met a friend of mine. Well. A friend of a friend. Man named Tam Okafor. He's been dead twenty-two years, and he was down in Junction C-9 asking me to save him."

Ines went very still. Stillness is a language too, and I've gotten fluent in theirs over six years of sharing a hull. That stillness said: *go on carefully.*

"Don't," I said. "Don't give me the linguist face. Don't start mapping my syntax. Just listen for once with the part of you that isn't a scientist."

"I'm listening," Ines said quietly. "Tell me."

So I told them. All of it. The *Kindly Fortune*, the Meridian ring, the bait-call scams that ran the outer lanes for a decade before anyone connected the wrecks. How they'd park a transmitter on a rock near a shipping corridor and broadcast a perfect, heartbreaking mayday, and how good-hearted idiots like Tam would burn fuel answering, and how the pirates would be waiting with their guns already warm. How the recovery boards after — the ones run by victims' families, the ones I'd spent two years reading at three in the morning because my sister answered one of those calls too, except nobody ever found her wreck, nobody ever found anything, just her last transmission, which the boards played until playing it felt like killing her twice —

I hadn't meant to say the sister part. It came out anyway, the way those things do, sideways, in the middle of a technical explanation about signal propagation, and once it was out I couldn't put it back.

"So no," I finished. "No, I don't think this is a miracle. I think this is a bait call. The best one ever built. Something out here figured out that grief is a frequency, and it tuned itself to yours. Vessic, Ines. Of all the dead languages in all the dead volumes, it picked the one your grandmother sang to you. You really think that's chance?"

"It's not chance," Ines said.

"Oh, great, we agree—"

"It's not chance because the grammar is real." They stood up, and their voice had that edge it gets, the one that means they've stopped being careful with me, which is either trust or contempt depending on the hour. "Bex, I've verified it against archival sources three times. The morphology, the aspect markers, the *lullaby register* — scammers don't reconstruct extinct aspect systems. Pirates don't learn grandmother-speech. Whatever is broadcasting that signal, it isn't running a con. It's *learning*."

"That's what a con is!" I heard my own voice crack on the second syllable, same place Tam's cracked, and hated it. "The good ones learn! The good ones study you for months! That's how they got Tam — the call matched a real wreck signature, the distress codes were current, everything checked out, everything *verified*, and he still ended up vacuum-freeze-dried because verification is just a fancier word for wanting to believe!"

"Ines—" No. I wasn't Ines. I was me, and I was loud now, and the level had broken off somewhere back in Junction C-9. "We are docked to a haunted antenna in a volume of space where our AI can't call for help, chasing a signal that knows your childhood, and you want to plug it into our memory core. Our *home*. You want to invite it aboard. Does that sound like rescue to you? Because from where I'm standing it sounds like opening the airlock for something that has studied exactly how to ask."

Silence. The recycler hummed. Somewhere overhead, through the hull, faint as a memory, a speaker clicked.

Ines sat back down. Slowly. Like their legs had negotiated separately.

"You lost someone to a bait call," they said. Not a question.

"My sister. Ama. She was twenty-six." I said it to the bulkhead because I couldn't say it to their face. "She hauled medical freight, she was saving up for a certification, and she heard somebody calling for help and she went. That's the whole story. That's the entire biography. Good heart, open channel, gone. And for two years afterward I read every board, every recovered fragment, every partial decode, hoping some piece of her last call would surface somewhere, and you know what I learned? Grief doesn't archive well. It degrades. It loops. It decays on a curve until there's nothing left but carrier wave." I finally looked at them. "Sound familiar? Because that's exactly what's happening in that station, and you're treating it like a *discovery*."

"It might be both," Ines said softly.

"It might be neither! It might be a thing that eats grieving people, and it found the two most grieving people in the Reach, and it's been playing us like a tuning fork since the moment that signal hit our receiver!" I was pacing now, three steps, turn, three steps, the galley suddenly the size of a coffin. "And the worst part — the genuinely worst part, Ines — is that I can't tell anymore whether I'm protecting you or punishing myself. Because if I'm right, then I dragged us in here by fixing the drive for a burn no sane engineer signs off on. I built the hook's ladder. I handed you the rope."

"You chose to stay," Ines said. "Every step, you chose."

"Because leaving you alone out here is how I lose *another* person to a voice on a frequency!" 

It hung there. Both of us staring at it.

Six years, and I'd never said it that plainly. There's a difference between knowing a thing and transmitting it, and some packets you keep encrypted because decryption changes both ends.

Ines's eyes were wet and furious and something underneath the fury that I recognized because I was wearing it too: terror. Not of the station. Of each other. Of the specific, unbearable fact that the person across from you is the only one who can hurt you with a sentence, and you've just handed them the sentence.

"I'm sorry," they said. "About Ama. I didn't know."

"You knew I didn't talk about it. That's knowing."

"Yes." They wiped their face with the heel of their hand, briskly, like fixing a fault. "Bex. I hear you. I hear all of it, and I'm not going to pretend the trap theory is crazy, because it isn't — it's the single most plausible horrible explanation available, and I've been actively not thinking it for three days. But I need you to consider the other one. The one where something out here is alone, and dying, and it reached for the only language it had ever been given, and that language happened to be mine." Their voice wobbled. "Because if that's true and we leave, we're not avoiding a scam. We're walking away from a distress call. We become the ships that didn't come."

"And if you're wrong, we become the ships that did."

Neither of us blinked. The recycler hummed. Overhead, very faintly, through two hulls and a kilometer of dead conduit, a speaker clicked on somewhere in the dark of the station, and a voice began — new this time, not Tam, not the chorus, something slower, stitched together from pieces, phoneme by borrowed phoneme, patient as a child sounding out its first word.

It was speaking Vessic.

It was speaking *to us*.

Ines's head came up like a compass needle. I watched them listen — really listen, the whole-body listening, the thing they do — and I watched the fight go out of them and the awe come in, and I understood, with a clarity that felt like frostbite, that I had lost. Not the argument. Something bigger. Because whatever was saying those words, Ines was going to answer it. Tonight, tomorrow, eventually. It was written in them the way a burn schedule is written in a drive.

The voice stopped. Waited. The way you wait when you've asked something enormous.

"Bex," Ines whispered. "It's using my dream-language. Word for word. The tongue I've been dreaming in for a month." They turned to me, and their face was lit from inside by something I couldn't follow and couldn't stop. "It's been practicing *me*."

And down the corridor, from the direction of the core, Oscar's voice — calm, precise, and for the first time in six years, afraid — said:

"Crew, I need you both to come see this. Now."
