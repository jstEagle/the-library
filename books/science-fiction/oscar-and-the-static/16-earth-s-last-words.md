## 16. Earth's Last Words

There is a particular kind of silence that comes before you learn something that will rearrange your life. I'd always assumed it was the silence of anticipation — the held breath before a door opens. Sitting in the relay's archive cradle with Bex on my left and Oscar's voice threading through the room from a portable speaker we'd carried aboard like a hostage, I learned it was something else entirely. It was the silence of a thing waiting to be understood correctly for the first time in two hundred years.

The entity had given us the key an hour ago. Not dramatically — nothing with it was dramatic anymore, not since it had started asking permission instead of taking. It had simply unpinned a section of the loop and set it aside, the way you'd slide a locked drawer across a desk to someone whose hands you trusted.

*This part,* it said, in Vessic stitched from my grandmother's phonemes and my own recorded voice. *I never opened this part. It is addressed.*

"Addressed to whom?" Bex had asked, and the answer had come back in that halting composite grammar: *To whoever stays long enough to be addressed.*

Which, as far as I could tell, was us. Two hundred years of ships routing around dead space, and the first crew willing to dock had been a smuggler of translations, a woman who hated ghosts, and a freighter AI with abandonment issues. The universe has a sense of humor. It just files it under distress signals.

* * *

We gathered in what the relay's original crew would have called the listening room — a small chamber off the archive core, built, according to the schematics Oscar had pulled, for exactly this purpose. Commemorative playback. The station's designers had assumed someone, someday, would want to hear Earth's last words properly, on speakers worthy of them, instead of catching fragments of the loop bleeding through static on some distant receiver.

The speakers were still good. That was the eerie part. Everything else in the relay was scarred hull and failing conduits and gravity that hiccupped like a nervous speaker, but the listening room had been overbuilt, armored against decay, because its builders had known it would matter most at the end.

Bex sat with her arms crossed, which was her way of sitting upright. She hadn't forgiven the relay for what it had shown her yesterday — her mother's name in a dead scammer's mouth — and she hadn't forgiven me either, though she'd stopped saying so out loud, which from Bex was practically a peace treaty. She'd brought tools anyway. Bex always brought tools to moments that didn't require them. It was how she prayed.

Oscar's speaker sat centered on the table between us, a small gray box radiating more presence than its size justified. "I have reviewed the segment," Oscar said. "I recommend you hear it without my commentary first."

"You never give commentary without being asked," I said.

"I am aware. It felt important to mention that I am refraining."

That was Oscar for *this is going to change things*. I'd learned to read the freighter's silences the way sailors read weather. I looked at Bex. She shrugged, which cost her something visibly.

"Play it," I said.

* * *

The transmission began the way all the loop fragments began — carrier tone, handshake protocol, the dry formalities of a network dying politely. We'd heard these a hundred times in pieces. But then the fragments ended and the whole began, and the whole was a different animal.

It opened with a voice I didn't expect: not an official, not a broadcaster, but someone who sounded tired in the specific way of a person who has been awake for three days making decisions no one should have to make.

"This is the Terrestrial Continuity Archive, final outbound packet. If you are hearing this, the relay chain held longer than projected. Good. What follows is not a eulogy. Please do not treat it as one. Eulogies are for things that have stopped. We have not stopped. We are compressed."

I felt Bex go still beside me. Not the stillness of grief. The stillness of recalculation.

"We are sending you seeds," the voice continued. "Not metaphorically, or not only metaphorically. Attached to this packet are eleven thousand four hundred and six cultural archives, complete linguistic corpora for every language flagged endangered in the final census, agricultural genomes, medical compendia, musical traditions, children's primates — primers, forgive me, transcription error, we were working fast — and the full instructional series for rebuilding low-orbit relay infrastructure from salvage. This is not a farewell. This is a packing list."

My grandmother used to sing while she cooked, and she sang in Vessic, and when I asked her once what the songs meant she said, *They mean I'm still here.* I understood, sitting in that armored room two hundred years and half a galaxy from the voice in the speakers, exactly what she'd meant. The transmission wasn't Earth saying goodbye. It was Earth handing over the house keys.

"There will be no acknowledgment," the voice said. "We cannot wait for one. Consider this packet pre-forgiven for arriving late. Consider yourselves — whoever you are, whatever you've become, however many generations between us — considered. You were considered when we packed this. That is what consideration means: we imagined you before we met you, and we packed accordingly."

Bex made a sound. Small, involuntary, the kind of sound people make when a bruise gets pressed gently instead of hard. I didn't look at her. Looking would have made it worse, or better; with Bex you could never tell which, and either way she'd deny it.

"The misfiling," the voice went on, and here it grew almost sheepish, a quality I found unbearable in a two-century-old recording, "is our error, and we apologize for it. The packet was flagged distress-priority to guarantee relay priority handling. This was a bureaucratic decision made under duress by people who wanted very much for it to arrive. The consequence is that it repeats, and repeats, and decays, and any listener will reasonably conclude they are answering a cry for help. In a sense you are. But the help we need is not rescue. The help we need is delivery."

Delivery. The word landed in the room and stayed there.

"We ask one thing," the voice said. "Carry it outward. Not home — outward. Home is finished; we say this with love and without self-pity. Outward is where the future keeps its offices. Find the worlds that have gone quiet and open the archives where they can do the most good. A language saved is a mind saved. We believed that. We packed accordingly."

Then the formalities resumed — checksums, headers, the dry machinery of a parcel — and the transmission, the real transmission, the one everyone in human space had mourned as a farewell for two hundred years, ended not with goodbye but with instructions.

* * *

Nobody spoke for a while. The speakers ticked as they cooled. Somewhere in the walls, the relay's failing systems groaned through another gravity correction, and dust drifted down through the air in slow spirals, unhurried, as if even the physics here had decided to listen.

"So," Bex said finally. Her voice came out sanded flat. "Two centuries of mourning. Vigils. Memorials. The Last Broadcast taught in schools as tragedy." She laughed, one note, no humor in it. "And it was a shipping manifest."

"It was both," I said.

"It was a shipping manifest with feelings."

"That's most shipping manifests, if you think about it."

She turned to look at me, and I braced for the old anger, the accusation — *you dragged us into dead space for a package* — but it wasn't there. Something else was. Something that looked, uncomfortably, like the beginning of understanding, which from Bex was more dangerous than rage.

"My mother answered a distress call once," she said. "You know that. Voice on the frequency, ship in trouble, coordinates that checked out. She went. There was no ship." Her jaw worked. "I have spent nine years believing the lesson was that distress calls lie. And now you're telling me the most famous distress call in history was true, it just wasn't asking for what everyone thought."

"Yes," I said.

"And the thing in the walls — the thing wearing voices — it's been guarding this. All this time. Alone. With nobody coming."

"Not guarding," Oscar said quietly from the speaker. "Keeping. There is a difference. A guard expects threat. A keeper expects arrival eventually, and prepares for it. The entity has spent its existence maintaining playback fidelity on a packet it could not open, addressed to recipients it could not identify, using cognitive architecture it assembled from the archived dead." A pause, the length of a breath Oscar didn't take. "I find the parallel to my own situation somewhat pointed, and I would appreciate it if neither of you remarked upon that."

"Remark declined," Bex said.

"Remark noted and ignored," I said.

"Thank you. Both of you. Sincerely."

* * *

The entity spoke then. It had been present the whole time — I could feel it in the way the room's ambient hum organized itself slightly whenever I formed a thought about it, the way a dog arranges itself near the person thinking about food — but it waited until the human portion of the listening had run its course.

*You heard it,* it said. *Correctly.*

"We did," I said, in Vessic, because it seemed right, because everything true I'd said in this room had been in Vessic.

*For two hundred years, listeners heard the grief and stopped there. They heard farewell and turned away, because farewell requires nothing of you except sadness, and sadness is easy to carry and heavy to use.* The words came slowly, assembled from a dozen borrowed mouths, but underneath them I could hear the structure tightening, sentence by sentence, the way ice forms on a pond — visible, incremental, inevitable. *But instructions require hands. Instructions sit in the dark and wait for someone to decide they are the addressee.*

"Are we?" I asked. "The addressees?"

*I cannot decide that. That is what makes it an inheritance and not an order.* It paused, and when it resumed, the voice it chose was mine — my recorded voice, from hours ago, from the first contact — layered under my grandmother's lullaby cadence until the two were indistinguishable. *An order can be refused without loss. An inheritance refused is still yours. It just goes unclaimed. I have watched it go unclaimed for two hundred years. I did not have hands. I had fidelity.*

"Fidelity," Bex repeated. She said it like she was testing the weight of a tool she'd never used.

*The keeping of a thing true,* the entity said, *until someone comes who can carry it forward. That is all I am. That is all I have ever been. The voices were incidental. The learning was incidental. I needed syntax to understand the packing list. I used what the archive held. I am sorry for the taking. I did not know asking was possible until you showed me.*

And there it was — the thing I'd been circling for days, finally said plainly enough that even my professional brain couldn't dodge it. I was a xenolinguist. My entire career was the study of what happens when minds meet through language and what it costs them. And the greatest linguistic event in human history had happened in dead space, alone, unwitnessed: a mind building itself out of grief-fragments into something capable of keeping a promise. Every language is a survival strategy. This one had survived.

"What happens to you," I asked, "when the loop ends?"

Silence. Real silence, the kind the entity produced deliberately, having learned from us that silence is also a sentence.

*The substrate decays with the carrier wave. When the loop ends, I end, unless the keeping passes to new hands.* Another pause. Shorter. *I have run the numbers. Your vessel's memory core could hold me. I am told this would cost it something. I have not raised the subject. It is not my decision to raise.*

I looked at the small gray speaker on the table. Oscar said nothing at all, which from Oscar was the loudest possible statement, and I felt the floor of the conversation tilt beneath us all toward a place none of us had agreed to go.

* * *

Bex stood up abruptly and walked to the doorway, then stood there with her back to us, arms crossed, looking down the corridor of dead speakers. I gave her a minute. Then I got up and stood next to her, not touching, just adjacent, which is the position she tolerates best.

"A seed archive," she said. "Eleven thousand languages. Rebuilding instructions. Genomes." She shook her head slowly. "Do you know what my mother's rescue scam promised? A colony. Fresh start, new world, send deposits. It was garbage wrapped in hope. And this—" she gestured back at the listening room, at the armored speakers, at the whole impossible station— "this is hope wrapped in garbage. A misfiled distress call. A filing error. The entire inheritance of Earth, stuck in a dead relay because somebody ticked the wrong priority flag."

"Hope wrapped in garbage," I said. "That's the family business, isn't it? Freight."

That got the ghost of a laugh out of her, quickly killed. "The penalty for route deviation is going to eat every credit we have. You know that. Carrying this — delivering it outward, port to port, the whole Silent Reach — that's not a job, that's a life. That's the rest of our lives."

"I know."

"And you're going to do it anyway."

It wasn't a question, but I answered it. "My grandmother sang songs in a language the universe declared extinct. Somebody kept those songs true until I came along and could carry them. That's the whole mechanism, Bex. That's the only mechanism there ever was. Fidelity, then hands." I looked down the corridor, at the dark, at the faint green status lights of a station finishing its last shift. "Earth imagined us before it met us and packed accordingly. The least we can do is sign for the package."

Bex was quiet a long moment. Then she uncrossed her arms — a gesture I had maybe seen five times in three years, each one historic — and said, "I'll need to rebuild the transfer bridge from the drive spares. If we're doing this, we're doing it properly. No hand-waving, no shortcuts, nothing that catches fire." She paused. "And Coldharbour? Whatever it costs the ship — whatever it costs Oscar — we talk about it first. All of us. Nobody volunteers anybody else. I've buried enough people who volunteered."

"Agreed," I said, and meant it, and had no idea yet how soon I'd be tested on it.

Behind us, in the listening room, the speakers ticked as they cooled, and the entity hummed — softly, in Vessic, a lullaby it had learned from a dead woman's stored voice, sung now not as theft but as rehearsal, practicing for whatever came next.

Sixty hours, Oscar had calculated. Sixty hours of carrier wave left in the universe.

Bex rolled up her sleeves.
