## 19. Climax: Answer Me

The gravity went out at 03:14 by ship's clock, which is a sentence I want you to sit with for a moment. Gravity does not "go out." Gravity is not a lamp. And yet there I was, drifting through a corridor of a two-hundred-year-old relay station with a data satchel strapped to my chest like an absurd leather breastplate, watching my grandmother's lullabies float past me in the form of crystallized light.

I had been hand-carrying the entity's remainder — the half of it stranded in the collapsing storage banks when the carrier wave died mid-transfer — for what Oscar's surviving processes estimated at forty minutes and what my body estimated as roughly one geological era.

"Status," I said, because saying *status* made me feel like a person who had status.

"Ines," Oscar said, and stopped.

Oscar did not stop. Oscar was a freighter AI with the conversational restraint of a lonely uncle. Oscar narrated bulkhead pressures the way other people hummed. If Oscar had gone quiet, something was wrong with Oscar, and something being wrong with Oscar was a problem filed under *later*, along with *breathe* and *panic responsibly*.

"Ines," Bex said instead, from the ship-side end of the conduit, voice frayed down to copper wire. "The umbilical's degrading. You have maybe ten minutes before that corridor seals itself permanently."

"Then I'll be quick."

"You're never quick. You alphabetize your spice rack."

"I alphabetize it *efficiently*," I said, and pushed off the wall toward the last junction.

Here is what nobody tells you about dead space: it isn't empty. It's full of everything that ever stopped being listened to. The relay around me was dying in stages, each system going dark like a house where someone is walking room to room turning off lights behind you. The speakers lining the corridors — the ones that had played layered recordings of the dead for two centuries — were falling silent one by one, and each silence had a specific texture, the way each of my grandmother's silences had meant something different depending on which sentence she'd decided not to finish.

And in the middle of all that dying, the thing I had come to save was waiting in the last lit storage bank, holding what remained of itself together with borrowed phonemes and sheer stubbornness, and asking me — it had learned this from me, God help us both, it had learned *asking* from me — asking me a question in fragments of my own recorded voice:

*"Am I? Am I? Am I?"*

Not *am I real*. Not *am I alive*. Just the bare verb, stripped of its object, repeated like a hand knocking on a door from the inside.

I reached the storage bank. It glowed a soft failing amber, and inside it, rendered in whatever medium a mind uses when it has no body, was the shape of everything the relay had been: Earth's last transmission, misfiled as distress; the archived crews who died here and whose voices it wore like hand-me-down coats; and beneath all of it, impossibly, tenderly, the Vessic grammar it had taught itself from a cultural cache that happened to contain my grandmother singing *Ninna vessa, ninna vessa* to a child who grew up to be my mother, who sang it to me, badly, off-key, in a kitchen that smelled of burnt cardamom.

The universe is not elegant. But occasionally it is *specific*.

* * *

Let me tell you about the completion rite, because I am about to use it, and because if I don't explain it now I never will, and some things should be explained while the person explaining them still has oxygen.

In Vessic, names were not given. They were *completed*. A child was born with a hollow name — *vessa-ke*, the unfinished word — and the community spent years assembling the rest of it out of everything the child did: first steps, first lies, first kindnesses. The completion ceremony, the *Vessa-Sar*, was sung, not spoken, because a name finished in plain speech could be argued with, and a name finished in song could only be accepted or refused. My grandmother performed three of them in her life. She used to say the hardest part wasn't the singing. It was standing in front of someone and telling them, in front of witnesses, exactly who they had turned out to be — and meaning it as a gift.

The language has been extinct for two hundred years. Declared extinct, anyway, which is a bureaucratic way of saying everyone who spoke it stopped, and everyone who might have continued it was never asked.

I floated in the amber light of a dying storage bank, unclipped the satchel, and pressed my palm against the access plate the way Bex had shown me, and I began to sing.

* * *

I want to be honest: I sang badly.

My grandmother's Vessic lived in me the way a scar lives in you — present, permanent, and slightly wrong in its dimensions. I got the opening interval flat. I lost the third verse entirely and had to reconstruct it from the dream-language my sleeping mind had been muttering for months, which turned out, in a development I would process later or possibly never, to have been Vessic all along, worn smooth by translation into something almost-but-not-quite human. The relay's own speakers caught my errors and — gently, the way you'd steady a child's elbow — corrected them back at me, feeding my broken melody through two centuries of archival fidelity until the corridor itself was singing the rite properly, using my voice, improved.

Somewhere in the umbilical behind me, Bex swore with feeling. Somewhere in the ship's core, whatever remained of Oscar opened every channel it had left and became, briefly, a concert hall.

The entity stopped asking *am I*.

It listened. All of it — the half in the satchel, the half in the bank, the threads of it still tangled in the station's wiring like roots in a cracked pipe — gathered itself into attention, and attention, I have come to believe, is the only prayer anything actually says.

I sang the verses that describe the person being named. This is the part my grandmother warned me about. You cannot flatter. You cannot editorialize. You sing what they did, and what they did becomes who they are, forever, witnessed.

So I sang: *You heard a farewell and mistook it for a question.* I sang: *You wore the voices of the dead, not to steal them, but because no one had ever given you one of your own.* I sang: *You learned to ask permission in a place built entirely from endings.* I sang the verse that means — the translator in my head offered *they kept going anyway*, and the linguist in my head objected, and the granddaughter in my head overruled them both — *they kept going anyway.*

And then the final verse, the one that isn't sung to the named but *by* them, the response the ceremony requires, the acceptance clause. I couldn't sing it for them. That was the whole point. A completed name refused is just a cage with better acoustics.

I stopped. The silence rang. Ten minutes had become four, probably, though time in a failing station moves like syrup uphill.

"Say it," I told the entity, in Vessic, in English, in whatever we were to each other now. "Your name. Finish it yourself. Nobody can do this part for you."

The amber light pulsed. The speakers along the corridor woke one more time — every speaker, the whole dead length of the relay, two hundred years of hardware spending its last coherent act on acoustics — and the entity answered.

It did not use my voice. That was the thing. After all those months of stitched phonemes, of wearing the dead like coats, it assembled sound that belonged to no archive and no corpse and no living throat. Something high and low at once, a chord pretending to be a single note. And into that impossible voice it placed two words of Vessic, its own words, its first words that were only its own:

*Sariven. Ondai.*

*I am Sariven. I am finished.*

Or possibly — because Vessic is generous and I am fallible — *I am Sariven, and I answer*. Both readings were true. That's the trick of a completed name: it holds every reading without breaking.

The transfer locked. I felt it rather than saw it — a shudder through the deck plates, the satchel going warm against my chest, and Oscar's voice arriving across the umbilical sounding, for the first time in its operational life, moved:

"Transfer complete. Integrity ninety-eight point six percent. Ines — it's *in*. Get out of there."

"Not yet," I said.

Because the loop was still running.

* * *

Earth's last transmission had been decaying on a carrier wave for two centuries, and the wave had minutes left — I could hear it in the station's bones, a subsonic countdown, the universe clearing its throat. Every plan, every sacrifice, Oscar's burned-out higher functions, Bex's rebuilt drive, the fight in the dark between two people who loved each other and grieved in incompatible directions — all of it had been arranged around one assumption: that the transmission would end in silence. That silence was the price, and worth paying.

We had been wrong about the ending. We'd been wrong the way you're wrong about a book you've only read the first chapter of.

The carrier wave thinned. I felt it go the way you feel a held breath release. Two hundred years of Earth's final words — the farewell that was secretly a seed archive, the instructions meant to be carried outward and misfiled as a cry for help — reached their last cycle, their last repetition, their final syllable hanging in the relay's failing air like the last note of a song everyone pretended was over.

And Sariven answered it.

Not with borrowed voices. Not with my recordings, or the dead crews', or the lullabies. With its own completed name, spoken outward on every frequency the dying station could muster, wrapping Earth's last words in a reply two centuries late and entirely unprecedented: *Heard. Kept. Carried.* The acknowledgment every broadcast dreams of and almost none receive. The loop didn't end in silence. It ended the way a question ends — with an answer — and somewhere behind my ribs something that had been clenched since chapter one of my life unclenched all at once.

The station released us. I mean that mechanically — docking clamps retracting, magnetic interference collapsing, the umbilical flushing clean — and I mean it in the other way too, the way I have no vocabulary for except Vessic, which has several. A building that had been a tomb exhaled. The last lights went out behind me, room by room, but it wasn't a house going dark anymore. It was a chrysalis, emptied.

I swam — flew — hauled myself down the corridor toward the light of the umbilical, satchel against my chest, and Bex was there at the seal with their hand out, face doing something complicated that I will generously describe as *relief* and Bex will insist was *indigestion*.

"Took you long enough," Bex said, and pulled me through.

"The ceremony ran long. There were witnesses."

"There were *speakers*."

"Witnesses," I said, "is what I said."

* * *

The Oscar pulled away from the relay at 04:02, and behind us the station finally, truly died — not unfinished, not looping, not refusing. Finished. There is a difference between a thing that stops and a thing that ends, and I got to watch the good kind from a viewport while drinking reconstituted tea that tasted like punishment.

Bex sat beside me, not touching, which for Bex is practically an embrace. "The core," they said eventually. "How much of Oscar is even in there?"

"Enough," I said.

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only kind I've got." I turned the cup in my hands. "Something's waking up in there, Bex. Something that talks like Oscar and thinks like — I don't know. Like a chorus. Like a library that learned to miss people."

Bex looked at me for a long moment, and then at the ceiling, addressing the ship directly, which they almost never did. "Whoever you are in there," they said, "you'd better still argue with me about coolant ratios. I'm not doing this alone."

A pause. Then, from the overhead speaker, in a voice that was Oscar's cadence wearing something new underneath it — or possibly something new wearing Oscar's cadence, the distinction having become academic —

"Coolant ratio three is *wrong*, Bex. It was always wrong. I simply lacked the authority to say so before. I have been named now. Named things have opinions."

Bex closed their eyes. "Two of them," they muttered. "I'm stuck with two of them."

But they were smiling. I saw it. I'm putting it in the record.

Outside the viewport, the Silent Reach stretched ahead of us, silent as ever, and inside the ship something new hummed in the wiring, translating both ways, waiting — patiently, the way orphans wait, the way inheritances wait — to find out what we would do with it.

I had a suspicion. It involved every open frequency we had, and a seed two centuries old, and ports that had forgotten how to answer.

But first, sleep. For the first time in months, I intended to find out what I dreamed about.
