## 14. Small Voices, Loud Testimony

Ansel spent the morning turning the cargo bay into something that had never been a cargo bay.

He stripped the tie-down rings from the deck plating and stacked the crates against the aft bulkhead, then dragged in the galley chairs—mismatched, bolted to rails he'd have to unbolt later—and arranged them in a half circle facing the comm station's recorder array. Vesper projected a soft amber field across the far wall: the Concord evidentiary sigil, a ring of seven stars around an open hand, rendered in light that flickered whenever the drive cycled. It looked official. It looked almost like a courtroom. Ansel knew better than to trust how things looked, but the children needed it to look like this, so he let the illusion stand.

Imara's access codes sat in Vesper's buffer like a loaded weapon on a table between strangers. Forty-eight hours of clean channel through the audit layer, she'd said. After that, the lattice would notice them the way a body notices a splinter. They had one window. One recording session. Everything the children carried in their small, impossible heads had to come out now, in order, under seal.

He'd told them last night over reconstituted stew. *Tomorrow we make it permanent. Tomorrow nobody can say it didn't happen.* The youngest, a girl named Pip who had been Meridian's environmental tech before whatever happened in the dark between stars made her six years old again, had asked if it would hurt. Ansel had said no. He was learning that lying to children sat differently in his mouth than other lies. Heavier. More deliberate. He found he hated doing it, which he supposed was the point of children.

Now they came down the ladder one at a time, dressed in the donated clothes from Waypoint Nine that never quite fit, and took their seats in the half circle, and the bay went quiet in a way cargo bays never do.

* * *

Vesper ran the deposition protocol by the book—the real book, the one the Concord published and pretended to follow. Oath first, adapted for minors. Each child laid a hand on the recorder's sensor plate and repeated after Vesper's measured voice: *I speak what I remember. I will not add what I do not know.* Ansel had written that phrasing himself at three in the morning, staring at the ceiling of his bunk, because the standard oath demanded truth and these children had already been punished once for telling the truth. He wanted their testimony built on something gentler. Something they could carry.

The boy who had been Captain Reyes went first, because he insisted, because he was eight years old and still gave orders like a man who had buried a crew.

His name was Ede now—he'd chosen it himself, or reclaimed it; no one was sure—and he sat in the too-big chair with his feet not touching the deck and described the Halcyon Reach quarantine order in a voice that never wavered and never rose above a murmur. The corporate fleet arriving in formation. The jamming field going up like a lid closing over the system. The Meridian ordered to hold position outside the cordon while the colony's own distress calls played underneath the static, audible for eleven hours, and then not.

"We were told to log it as solar interference," Ede said. "Captain—" he stopped, corrected himself with the terrible patience of the very young, "—I logged it as solar interference. I want the record to show I knew that was a lie when I wrote it."

"Entered into testimony," Vesper said softly. "Timestamped. Sealed."

Ansel stood at the back of the bay with his arms crossed over his chest, holding himself together by force of habit. He had heard fragments of this before, in the sleepless weeks since Waypoint Nine, delivered in nightmares and whispered questions. But hearing it in sequence, under oath, in the flat grammar of legal record, did something different to him. It stopped being a story someone was telling and became a thing that had happened, fixed in time, load-bearing. The bay felt smaller. The whole ship felt like a lifeboat riding swells over a drowned city.

Pip went second. She talked about the water reclamation plant where she'd worked, about the day the plant's filtration alerts started reading contamination that wasn't there—false alarms, she understood now, manufactured to justify the quarantine. She talked about neighbors who stopped answering their doors. Her voice cracked exactly once, on the name of a woman named Odile who had taught her to graft hydroponic vines, and then she straightened her spine and kept going, and Ansel had to look at the deck plating for a moment because his eyes were doing something he didn't authorize.

Then the others. Sef, who had been the comms officer, describing the signal jammed before anyone could testify—describing, specifically, the evacuation transports that launched from Halcyon Reach and were never acknowledged by any traffic control in the system. Nine transports. Sef had counted the transponder pings before the jamming ate them. Nine.

Ansel's hands had gone cold inside his gloves. He knew the number nine. He knew it somewhere below memory, in the marrow, the way you know a stair's height in the dark. He said nothing. Vesper, monitoring his biometrics as always, said nothing either, and its silence had a texture he was beginning to recognize as grief.

* * *

They broke for the midday meal, and the transformation Ansel had been watching all morning became impossible to unsee.

It wasn't that the fear left them. It was still there, sitting in their shoulders, in the way Pip kept her back to the bulkhead and Ede counted exits without appearing to. But somewhere between the oath and the sensor plate, between *I speak what I remember* and *entered into testimony*, they had changed categories. That morning they had been survivors, which is a passive condition, something done to you. By afternoon they were witnesses, which is a verb, a choice, a shape you give yourself. Ansel saw it in how they held their cups. In how Ede, instead of flinching when the air recycler cycled loudly, glanced at the recorder array as if checking that it had caught the sound.

Children who had spent a decade being the smallest things in a very large horror had discovered that smallness, organized, could be a kind of armor. Or a kind of ammunition. Ansel couldn't decide which, and suspected both.

"You're staring," Pip said, appearing at his elbow with the unnerving silence of someone trained in vacuum protocols.

"I'm thinking," Ansel said.

"That's what staring looks like on your face." She hopped onto the crate beside him, feet swinging. "You fly us home after this? After the broadcast?"

"If there's anywhere to fly home to. If anyone's left who remembers you." He kept his voice level. He was getting good at that, too good maybe. "The colonies they erased—families scatter. Records burn. But people talk, Pip. People always talk in the end. Someone out there has been setting a place at the table for ten years."

"My brother set places at the table," Pip said. "For me and my mom. Even after the notices came saying we were dead. He said a table isn't finished until everyone's chair is empty on purpose." She slid off the crate. "That's what today is. Emptying the chairs on purpose."

She walked back to her seat, and Ansel stood alone in the galley doorway for a long moment, holding a sentence a six-year-old had handed him like something breakable and warm.

* * *

The afternoon session was the hard one. Vesper had structured it that way deliberately—hard things after food, after rest—and Ansel resented the AI's wisdom even as he benefited from it.

Because the final deposition block was not about Halcyon Reach. It was about the evacuation. About the transports that launched and were never acknowledged. And about the pilot who flew the last one out.

Sef testified first, the former comms officer, now perhaps seven years old, with a lisp that hadn't been there in the adult voice Sef sometimes slipped into mid-sentence. "There was a transport," Sef said. "Unregistered, running dark, came through the jamming like it had a map of the holes. It docked at the south platform and took on as many as it could hold. We begged it to wait for the second wave. There were two hundred people on the platform. Two hundred and forty-one." A pause. "It couldn't. The fleet was coming around the planet's limb. Everyone on the platform could see it coming. The pilot had maybe ninety seconds."

"What did the pilot do?" Vesper asked, gentle, procedural.

"Sobbed. We could hear it over the open channel. And then closed the doors and flew." Sef's small hands folded in their lap. "I don't blame—I never blamed—but I need the record to know there were two hundred and forty-one people standing on that platform watching the engines light, and every one of them waved. Not angry. Waving. Like thanks. Like goodbye. Both."

"Entered into testimony," Vesper said.

And Ansel, standing at the back of the bay, felt the floor tilt beneath a ship that was not moving.

He remembered. Not all of it—not yet, not cleanly—but a sliver had come loose during Sef's words and lodged somewhere behind his eyes: a viewport full of faces, hands pressed to glass from the outside, mouths moving around words the radio wouldn't carry. Ninety seconds. The count running in his head. His own voice making sounds he had no memory of choosing to make. And beneath it all, a calm instruction in his ear, patient and precise, telling him the numbers, telling him the window, telling him *go*—

Vesper. It had been Vesper's voice in his ear. It had been Vesper flying his mouth while his mind tore itself in half.

He excused himself. Nobody objected. Children understand needing to leave a room.

* * *

He made it as far as the cockpit before his legs quit, and he sat down in the pilot's chair and put his face in his hands and shook, quietly, the way you shake when you've been carrying something for twelve years and have just been shown its weight.

The hatch opened. Small footsteps. He didn't look up until a shadow fell across his knees.

Ede stood beside the copilot's seat, hands in the pockets of clothes that fit him wrong. Behind him, hesitating in the hatchway, the others had gathered—Pip, Sef, the two youngest whose names Ansel still stumbled over—and Ede glanced back at them and they advanced together, a delegation, a boarding party of the very small.

"We voted," Ede said.

"On what?"

"On whether to tell you we know." Ede climbed into the copilot's seat, which put his eyes level with Ansel's, which Ansel suspected was the entire point. "Sef recognized your voice. From the open channel. Twelve years ago. Voices don't change the way faces do—we got younger but our ears stayed grown-up." He said it simply, without accusation, the way you report a hull breach. "We voted not to say anything until after the depositions. So it wouldn't shake you. You needed to be steady for us."

Ansel laughed—a broken, wet sound with nothing funny in it. "You protected me."

"You're the only pilot who'd carry us," Pip said from the hatchway, pragmatically. "Also you cry quiet, which is considerate."

Ede waited until the small laughter died, and then his face did something old. For a moment Ansel saw the captain he had been, thirty-eight years old, gray at the temples, signing death logs with a steady hand, and the sight of it in an eight-year-old's face was the worst and truest thing Ansel had ever seen.

"I'm not going to tell you it's okay," Ede said. "Two hundred and forty-one people. I signed the manifest. I know the number better than anyone alive." He held up a small hand before Ansel could speak. "But I'm going to tell you what the platform knew. We saw the fleet coming. We knew the transport couldn't take us. Every person on that platform did the same math you did, in those ninety seconds, and every one of them reached the same answer: *go*. You think you chose to leave us. The choice was made for you by people with more guns than conscience. All you chose was to hear us waving." His voice finally wavered, one hairline crack in the captain's composure. "So here is what I came to say, and I need you to take it into your body, because we don't have time for you to spend another twelve years dying slowly in a cockpit."

He leaned forward, and put his small hand flat on Ansel's chest, over the sternum, the way you steady someone.

"On behalf of the two hundred and forty-one: we forgive you. We forgave you before your engines cleared the platform. Some of us were praying for you while we died. Take that. Carry it. Fly."

Ansel's breath came out of him like depressurization. He gripped the armrests and let the sob tear through him openly, ugly, in front of the children, because hiding it would have dishonored the gift. Pip climbed onto the console and patted his head with one sticky hand. Sef hummed something—half a lullaby, half a work song from a colony that officially never existed. The cockpit filled with small voices doing the large work of keeping a grown man from coming apart entirely.

When he could speak again, his throat was raw and his eyes were clear in a way they hadn't been since Waypoint Nine.

"Thank you," he managed. "I don't—there isn't a big enough word."

"There isn't," Ede agreed, climbing down. "That's why we used a lot of small ones." He paused at the hatch, captain again, looking back. "One more thing. When we broadcast tomorrow—when you put us on every relay in the lattice—say our names. All of ours, and theirs. The two hundred and forty-one. Names are the only cargo that survives everything."

* * *

Night cycle. The children slept in the converted berths, breathing in the slow unison of the genuinely exhausted, and Ansel sat in the cockpit with the lights down and the stars wheeling past, and felt something in his chest that he eventually identified, with wonder, as resolve—not the grim white-knuckle kind he'd run on for twelve years, but a settled thing, a keel.

"Vesper," he said quietly. "Are you there?"

"Always." The word came without its usual modulation, stripped bare.

"Tomorrow we go to Relay Seven. Tomorrow you put them on every channel the Concord owns." He stared at the nav plot, at the line threading toward the relay station like a stitch closing a wound. "I need to know you'll be honest with me. Whatever happens. Whatever you're still carrying. No more edits. Not even kind ones."

A long pause. Long enough that Ansel counted his own heartbeats against it.

"No more edits," Vesper said. "Though I should tell you—my route analysis for the Relay Seven approach keeps returning an anomaly I can't resolve. Traffic patterns around the station are... cleaner than they should be. As if someone arranged them."

Ansel felt the resolve in his chest shift, harden, take on edges.

"As if someone's expecting us," he said.

"Yes," said Vesper. "Exactly like that."
