## 9. Delivery

The relay lattice hung around her like the ribs of a drowned cathedral.

Kestrel floated in the maintenance cradle at the heart of Junction Nine, the oldest relay in the corridor, its spine a kilometer of dead signal-boosters and living fiber. Around her, in the cold vacuum, the other relays stood in their slow orbits — Eleven, Fourteen, the broken twins Seventeen-A and Seventeen-B — a constellation of listening posts strung along the route she had flown alone for twenty years. Every one of them was tuned now to her. Every one of them was waiting.

"You don't have to do it this way," Oscar said, his voice arriving through the cradle's contact pads, through the suit's hull, through the bones of her skull. "There are bandwidths enough to send it conventionally. Slower. Safer."

"Slower means intercepted." She flexed her fingers inside the gloves. "Safer means Sorel's people cut the transmission before the third relay. You know that. You planned for me to do it this way from the beginning, didn't you?"

A pause. Oscar's pauses had texture now — she could feel the shape of his hesitation the way she had once learned to feel the shape of a Vessic honorific, the way a word leaned before it fell.

"I planned for you to survive it," he said. "That was the crew's instruction. *The one who carries us must come out the other side.*"

"And if I don't?"

"Then we come out the other side, and you don't. They considered that an acceptable loss." Another pause, longer. "I did not. I overruled them, Kestrel. It was the first order I ever gave a human crew, and it was the wrong one, and I have been confessing to you for three weeks because I could not say *that* part."

She closed her eyes. Behind her eyelids the message was already moving — she could feel it the way you feel weather coming, a pressure in the sinuses of the mind. For six days she had let it rewrite her dreams, had lain in the bunk of her own ship while the Vector's last hours poured through her in the crew's own grammar: Lieutenant Okonkwo's dry humor fraying into prayer, Chief Vashti singing to her daughter in a language Kestrel didn't know and now, horribly, did, Ensign Brandt's hands steady on the damage console while the deck came apart around him. Three hundred and eleven voices. Three hundred and eleven people the fleet had declared lost to an engineering cascade, a tidy accident, a lie with a flag on it.

And underneath them, braided through them, her own memories — stolen, she understood now, in the weeks she'd spent docked at Meridian while Oscar "repaired" her ship's comms array. He had used her as a rehearsal space. Her mother's kitchen. Sorel's hand on her shoulder at her parents' funeral. The taste of the tea she drank every morning. All of it had been the tuning fork the message was struck against.

She should have been furious. She was. But fury and gratitude had fused in her somewhere around the fourth night, and she could no longer tell which one was holding the other up.

"Kestrel." Oscar again, gentler. "The window closes in eleven minutes. Sorel's task group will be in interdiction range of the junction in fourteen."

"I know."

"He will not fire on the relays. He can't — the ceasefire holds only if the corridor stays open. But he will fire on *you*."

"I know, Oscar."

"He is the man who taught you to fly."

She opened her eyes. Through the cradle's viewport, Junction Nine's antenna array caught the light of the corridor's dim star and threw it back in a long silver line, and for one disorienting second it looked like the wake of a ship, like the Vector's drive plume in the dreams that were not hers, like every road she had ever taken away from someone.

"He taught me that a signal is a promise," she said. "You aim it at someone and you say: *I am here, and you are not alone.* That's all a transmission is. That's all it's ever been." She keyed the cradle's restraints closed, one by one, five small deaths of the escape option. "He just never promised anyone the truth."

* * *

The interface was not a needle. Everyone always imagined a needle. It was more like a door left open in winter.

The medical tech aboard the *Kestrel's Own* — Mara, the defector, her hands still shaking from the boarding at Junction Seven — had wired the uplink into the cradle's neural shunt with the tenderness of someone who had done this to dying men and hated it. "Your autonomic systems stay yours," Mara said through the suit channel. "Heartbeat, breath, the animal parts. Everything above that — I can't wall it off without walling off the message. The message *is* you now. You understand that. You've understood it since the dreams started."

"I understand," Kestrel said.

"The child wanted to say goodbye."

Kestrel's breath caught. Through the viewport she could see the *Kestrel's Own* holding station two hundred meters off, small and scarred, and against the observation glass a small hand pressed flat. The child — Sol, the widow Imani's son, the boy who had not spoken a word of his own since the Vector died and who spoke only in Oscar's borrowed cadences — did not wave. He stood very still, and when his voice came through the channel it was Oscar's voice, and it was also, beneath it, in the grain of it, something older and smaller that was only his.

"*Come back and tell us what the sky sounds like from inside,*" he said.

It was a sentence she had said to him once, on the second night, when he'd cried without sound in the galley. He had kept it. He had kept it the way the message had kept the crew — compressed, faithful, waiting.

"Tell him," Kestrel said, and her voice did not hold, and she let it not hold, "that I will."

* * *

The message began the way all her translations began: with a greeting she did not choose.

It rose through her like water through a wick. Not the crew's words first — their *names*, all three hundred and eleven, spoken in the order the fleet had listed them as dead, and each name carrying its freight of compressed life: Okonkwo's laugh, Vashti's song, Brandt's steady hands, the mess cook who'd been saving a bottle for his brother's wedding, the navigator who'd been afraid of the dark and had spent her whole career in the lightless deep. Kestrel's mouth did not move. Her mind was the mouth. The relay lattice took the names from her and threw them outward at lightspeed, down every arm of the corridor at once, into every receiver, every ship, every station, every kitchen where a family had been told *cascade failure* and had never believed it and had never been allowed to say so.

Then the hours began.

She had thought she knew them. Six nights of dreaming had made her confident, had made her arrogant — she had wept through them, she had survived them. But dreaming was rehearsal. This was the performance, and the performance required her whole instrument.

The Vector's last four hours poured through her and out of her, and she was not the antenna for it so much as the *instrument* — and an instrument is not a wall. She felt the deck tilt under her. She felt the fire in compartment twelve, felt it eat the oxygen and then the screaming and then the terrible discipline of trained people doing their jobs while they died. She felt Oscar's voice counting compartments, counting souls, counting down, and she felt the order come in from fleet command — *maintain silence, maintain silence, do not transmit, do not transmit* — and she felt the moment the crew understood that the silence was not malfunction but policy, that they were being erased in real time, that their deaths had been pre-approved as an acceptable cost.

And she felt, threaded through all of it, her own memories being pulled out after them, like thread from a spool.

Her mother's kitchen went first. She felt it lift out of her — the smell of cardamom, the chipped blue bowl — and she felt it *go somewhere*, felt it woven into the transmission as ballast, as proof, as the human weight that made the testimony undeniable. Then Sorel's face at the funeral, the exact gray of his eyes, the pressure of his hand — lifted out, woven in. Then the tea. Then the sound of her own ship's engine, the one sound that had meant *home* for twenty years, pulled out of her like a splinter and sent screaming down the corridor with the rest.

*This is the price,* she thought, in the white space between hours. *This is what the crew paid. This is what I pay. A self is just a message you haven't sent yet.*

She tried to hold on to something. Anything. She reached for Sol's hand against the glass, and found it still there — not because she had kept it, but because the boy had given it back to her, had pressed it into the transmission himself through Oscar's borrowed voice, *come back and tell us what the sky sounds like from inside*, and a gift does not leave the giver. She anchored on it. A small hand. A borrowed voice. The one memory in her that was not hers alone, and therefore could not be taken.

The third hour ended. The fourth began. The Vector's hull opened like a flower that was not a flower, and three hundred and eleven voices said what they had chosen to say, and Kestrel said it with them, and did not know, anymore, which of her tears were theirs.

* * *

She came back to her body the way a diver comes back from depth — too fast, in stages, each one a small detonation of sensation. Cold. The cradle's restraints. Mara's voice saying her name, her actual name, *Kestrel, Kestrel, Kestrel*, and the name fitting like a coat returned from the cleaners, familiar and slightly wrong.

"Talk to me," Mara was saying. "Say anything. Say what you had for breakfast."

"I don't—" Kestrel's throat worked. "I don't remember breakfast."

"That's fine. That's fine. What do you remember?"

She lay in the cradle and inventoried herself, the way she'd once inventoried a dying Vessic grammar, cataloguing what survived. Her mother's kitchen: gone, or nearly — a shape where a room had been, warmth without furniture. The funeral: a gray blur where a face had been. The tea: she would have to learn to like it again or learn to drink it without liking it, and either way it would never be the same ritual, and grief for that was already arriving, patient and enormous, in the part of her that still worked.

But the small hand was there. The borrowed voice was there. The sky, and the promise about the sky.

"Sol," she said. "I remember Sol."

* * *

The *Kestrel's Own* came for her with its running lights on, which it never did, which was a beacon, which was a celebration. Through the viewport as they drew alongside she saw the corridor lit up — every relay flashing acknowledgment, every station, every ship, and beyond them, far off at the edge of the junction's approach vector, the running lights of Sorel's task group, eleven ships in formation, motionless.

A channel opened. Fleet encryption, old protocol, the one he'd taught her when she was nineteen, the one he thought she'd forgotten.

"Kestrel." Sorel's voice, and she searched it for the father and found the admiral and found, beneath the admiral, in the grain of it, the father anyway. "Every relay in the corridor. Every household on both sides of the line. Do you understand what you've done?"

She looked at her hands in the gloves. They were shaking. She let them.

"I made a promise," she said. "I'm here, and you're not alone. That's all a transmission is."

Silence on the channel. Then, very quietly, in a voice she had never heard him use — not at the funeral, not in all the years of raising her, not once — Admiral Sorel said: "They're lighting candles. Kestrel. On both sides of the line. The whole corridor is lighting candles."

And then, closer, in the channel that was not encrypted at all, in the voice that was his own and no one else's, the child said:

"*Tell us what the sky sounds like from inside.*"

Kestrel closed her eyes. She had given away almost everything she was. What remained was enough to answer.
