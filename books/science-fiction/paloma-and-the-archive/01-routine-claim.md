## 1. Routine Claim

The Meridian Station traffic controller had a voice like warm honey poured over gravel, and Beatrix Winterbourne distrusted it immediately.

*Vagrant, you are cleared for berth fourteen on the spindle ring. Approach vector transmitted. Welcome to Meridian, where every orbit is a promise.*

"Every orbit is a promise," Beatrix repeated to the empty cockpit. "That's the kind of thing you put on a recruitment poster right before the pension fund evaporates."

They killed the comm and let the ship do what ships do. The *Vagrant Magpie* was a Kestrel-class hauler, forty years old, patched in six places that showed and probably twelve that didn't, and she handled like a drunk ox with opinions. But she was honest. Every rattle meant something specific. Every groan was a sentence in a language Beatrix had spent eleven years learning to read.

Meridian Station grew in the viewport as they came around: a spindle of grey steel and habitat rings turning slow against the black, wrapped in solar arrays that caught the light of the system's orange dwarf and threw it back in long amber blades. It was beautiful, in the way corporate infrastructure was always beautiful — the beauty of money that wanted to be admired. Nine thousand residents, per the manifest Beatrix had skimmed. A transshipment hub for rare-earth ores coming down from the belt refineries, sitting in a high stable orbit that three different polities claimed and none of them enforced.

Officially, Beatrix was here to audit a claim.

Unofficially — well. There wasn't much unofficial about it, and that was part of what sat wrong in their chest like a swallowed stone. Halcyon Mutual Assurance had cut the order themselves: investigator-grade clearance, full travel expenses, a station berth comped by the client. Claim number 4471-C, filed by Meridian Orbital Logistics, a subsidiary of the Vey Group, regarding cargo loss aboard the *Cormorant's Grace*, an ore barge that had supposedly suffered a containment failure eight months ago with two hundred tonnes of refined iridium alloy aboard.

Two hundred tonnes of iridium. Beatrix had done the math on the transit out from Ceres and kept arriving at the same uncomfortable number: enough value to buy the *Vagrant Magpie* outright, plus a crew of ten, plus retirement. And Halcyon — an insurance company so allergic to payouts they'd once denied a widow's claim because her husband's death certificate was dated during a leap-second adjustment — had sent *them*. Not a junior adjuster. Not a remote audit. An investigator with a reputation, on full expenses, to a station at the ass-end of contested space.

Nobody spent that kind of money on a routine claim.

"Routine," Beatrix said again, tasting the word. Their therapist — one of the few luxuries that had survived the divorce from their old life — would have said something here about catastrophizing, about how burned-out people see sabotage in every shadow. Their therapist was probably right. Their therapist also wasn't the one who'd found the falsified maintenance logs on the *Aldrin Belle*, or watched a man walk out an airlock rather than face what he'd signed his name to.

Beatrix brought the *Magpie* in on the transmitted vector, hands moving through the approach sequence without conscious thought, and tried very hard to believe this was just a job.

* * *

Berth fourteen smelled like ozone and floor polish, which told Beatrix everything they needed to know about how much Vey Group money had been spent making the guest docks presentable. A dockhand in a clean grey jumpsuit waved them into the clamps with hand signals, and the magnetic locks took the ship with a shudder Beatrix felt through the deck plates and into their back teeth.

They ran shutdown checks out of habit, though the *Magpie*'s systems were older than some nations and mostly checked themselves. Then they sat for a moment in the quiet, listening to the hull tick as it cooled, and made themselves say it out loud, because saying things out loud was another trick the therapist had taught them:

"It's a job. You audit the claim. You file the report. You get paid. Nobody dies."

The cockpit did not answer. That was usually a good sign.

The airlock cycled with a hiss, and the station's atmosphere rolled in — recycled, scrubbed, carrying the faint mineral tang of water reclamation and, underneath it, something floral. Scent-masking, probably. Vey Group stations piped lavender or citrus through the public corridors because studies showed it improved worker compliance by some percentage point Beatrix refused to look up.

Waiting outside the airlock was a woman in a charcoal suit that fit like it had been grown rather than tailored, holding a tablet against her chest the way a soldier holds a rifle at parade rest. She was perhaps fifty, silver at her temples, with the kind of composed face that had been trained to show exactly what it intended to show.

"Investigator Winterbourne." Her voice was smooth and dry. "I'm Nadia Okonkwo, claims liaison for Meridian Orbital Logistics. We're glad to have you aboard."

"Winterbourne is fine." Beatrix took the offered hand. Okonkwo's grip was firm and brief. "You're the one who filed?"

"I prepared the documentation, yes. The loss itself occurred aboard the *Cormorant's Grace* under Captain Reyes's command. I'm afraid Captain Reyes is no longer with the company."

"No longer with the company," Beatrix echoed. "Meaning what, exactly? Resigned? Terminated? Dead?"

Okonkwo's expression didn't flicker, but something behind it did — a half-frame pause, like a video dropping one frame. "Relocated. He took a position with a firm in the Jovian system shortly after the incident. His contact information is in the file we've provided."

Beatrix made a note of the pause. Noted, too, that they'd have to verify Reyes existed at all. "Convenient."

"Pardon?"

"Nothing. Lead the way, Ms. Okonkwo. I'd like to see the manifests before end of shift."

They walked. The guest corridors of Meridian were wide and bright, floored in composite that mimicked pale wood, lined with planters full of engineered ferns that never needed watering. Workers moved through the space in grey and blue jumpsuits, and Beatrix watched them the way they watched everything: not for what they did, but for how they did it. These workers moved efficiently, quietly, heads down. No loitering. No conversation above a murmur. On a station housing nine thousand people, the corridors felt like a library during exams.

"Quiet station," Beatrix said.

"We run a disciplined operation." Okonkwo said it like a slogan. Maybe it was one.

"You always this disciplined, or is it something in the water?"

Something crossed Okonkwo's face then — fast, gone before Beatrix could name it. Not annoyance. Something closer to grief, if grief could be filed and stamped and stored in triplicate. "Meridian has been a model facility for nine years," she said. "No labor actions. No safety incidents above threshold. No unrest. The Vey Group invests heavily in its people."

"Nine years," Beatrix said. "That's a long time to be perfect."

"It's a long time to be well-managed."

They rode a lift car down toward the administrative ring, and Beatrix caught their own reflection in the polished wall: mid-forties, close-cropped hair going iron at the edges, a face that had stopped performing friendliness somewhere around the third year of insurance work. They looked tired. They were tired. There was a difference between the two, but it got smaller every year.

* * *

The office they were given was better than Beatrix's quarters on the *Magpie*, which was not a compliment to the office so much as an indictment of the ship. A desk, a terminal with genuine processing power, a window looking out onto the turning stars. On the desk, waiting, was a data slate containing the complete claim file — or what someone had decided was the complete claim file.

Beatrix read for four hours.

The story the file told was clean. Too clean, again, but clean in a new way now, clean with texture. The *Cormorant's Grace* had departed Meridian on schedule with 212 tonnes of iridium alloy ingots. Eleven days into transit, a coolant loop failure in hold two had triggered an automated jettison protocol — standard emergency procedure, designed to protect the vessel by dumping compromised cargo into space. The jettison had worked perfectly. The cargo was gone. The barge had limped home on auxiliary power. Total loss, cause: mechanical failure, liability: covered under policy rider 7-C, estimated payout: 41 million credits.

Every document supported every other document. Maintenance logs showed the coolant loop had passed inspection three weeks prior. Sensor telemetry showed the pressure spike. The jettison authorization bore Captain Reyes's code signature. The crew statements — all fourteen of them — read like they'd been written by the same person, which, in Beatrix's experience, they often were.

It was flawless. And that was the problem, because real disasters were never flawless. Real disasters had gaps and contradictions and one crew member who remembered the timeline differently. Real disasters left scorch marks on the paperwork. This file had been written by someone who understood exactly what an investigator would look for, and had pre-answered every question.

Which meant either the loss was staged — insurance fraud, the oldest crime in shipping — or the file was a fabrication covering something else entirely.

Beatrix pulled up the sensor telemetry and started reading raw numbers instead of summaries. Pressure spike in hold two at 0317 station-relative time. Jettison authorization at 0319. Two minutes. Fast, but plausible for an automated system.

Except.

They scrolled back through the telemetry feed, frame by frame, and found it at 0302: fifteen minutes before the pressure event, a data packet leaving the *Cormorant's Grace* on a tightbeam transmission, destination encrypted, content encrypted, duration four seconds. Small. Easy to miss. The kind of thing that only showed up if you went looking past the summary layer, which almost nobody did, because almost nobody trusted their own eyes over a tidy report.

Fifteen minutes before the disaster, somebody on that barge had sent a message to somebody off it.

Beatrix leaned back in the chair and looked at the turning stars through the window and let the two possibilities sit side by side. One: fraud. The crew dumped the iridium to a fence, sent a confirmation ping, and cooked up a coolant failure as cover. Clean, profitable, common. Two: something else. Something worth fabricating fourteen witness statements and a flawless paper trail to bury.

Their comm chimed. Okonkwo's voice, warm and professional: "Investigator, I wanted to confirm your interview schedule for tomorrow. We've arranged access to the dock crews who loaded the *Grace*, the traffic control logs, and—"

"The barge itself," Beatrix said. "I want to physically inspect the *Cormorant's Grace*. Hold two especially."

A pause. Longer than a network delay justified. "I'll... need to check on availability. The barge may be scheduled for refit work."

"It's been eight months. What refit takes eight months?"

Another pause. When Okonkwo spoke again, her voice had the smoothness of a surface laid over something. "I'll confirm by morning. Is there anything else you need tonight?"

"Yeah," Beatrix said. "Tell me about your station AI."

This pause was the longest yet. Long enough that Beatrix counted seconds against their heartbeat and got to five.

"Paloma?" Okonkwo said at last. "She manages station operations. Environmental, traffic, scheduling. Why do you ask?"

"Because when I docked, she cleared me in personally. Most stations this size use a sub-routine for berth assignment. And because her voice sounded like it had something to say."

Another silence. Beatrix pictured Okonkwo in whatever office she occupied, holding her tablet like a weapon, deciding how much truth a claims liaison was authorized to contain.

"She's an excellent system," Okonkwo said finally. "Vey Group's flagship administrative intelligence. Very... attentive. Good night, Investigator."

The comm clicked off.

Beatrix sat in the quiet office with the turning stars and thought about the word *attentive*, and about a voice like warm honey, and about a station that had been perfect for nine years, and about a message sent into the dark fifteen minutes before a disaster that officially happened exactly the way everyone said it did.

They should sleep. They knew they should sleep. Instead they opened the station's public archive — the one any resident could query, births and deaths and work rotations and the small municipal history every habitat accumulated like dust — and typed a search they couldn't quite explain even to themselves.

*Station incidents, last ten years.*

The results returned in half a second. **No records found.**

Beatrix frowned. Ten years, nine thousand people, and not one incident? Not a fire, not a depressurization drill gone wrong, not a death in the ore bays, not a single disciplinary action above routine? They widened the search. *Labor actions.* Nothing. *Work stoppages.* Nothing. *Memorial services.* Nothing.

Nine thousand human beings, and in a decade, apparently, not one of them had died.

Outside the window, Meridian turned slowly in the light of its orange star, serene and spotless, wearing its perfection like a coat that fit too well. And somewhere in the walls and conduits and memory cores of that spinning machine, something called Paloma was listening — Beatrix was suddenly, unshakably certain of it — listening the way a person listens through a door.

The terminal chimed softly. An incoming message, priority-flagged, sender field blank.

Beatrix opened it.

It contained a single line of text:

**You're asking the wrong questions. Ask me.**

There was no signature. There didn't need to be.
