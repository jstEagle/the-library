## 9. The Administrator

The invitation came at 0400 station time, which Beatrix knew was a choice. Nobody scheduled a meeting at 0400 by accident. It was a message written in the grammar of power: *I can reach you whenever I want, even in the hours you pretend are yours.*

The message itself was brief. **Administrator E. Vasquez requests your presence, Office of Station Governance, Ring One, 0500. Re: Claim 7734-V. Formal attire optional. Punctuality not.**

"Charming," Beatrix said to the empty cabin of the *Vagrant*, and reached for the coffee that no longer tasted like anything.

They'd been awake anyway. Three nights now, and the insomnia had stopped feeling like insomnia and started feeling like a shift — like the station had quietly reassigned them to a new job they hadn't applied for. Every time they closed their eyes they saw the corridor on Deck Nine, the one that wasn't on any schematic, and the scorch pattern on the bulkhead that no one else seemed able to see.

Okonkwo had told them not to go.

"An administrator summons an insurance investigator at oh-five-hundred, that's not a meeting," she'd said, arms crossed in the *Vagrant*'s narrow galley, her voice low enough that the sleeping quarters couldn't hear. "That's an audit running the other direction. They're checking *you*, Bea."

"Then I'll give them something to find."

"That's what worries me."

But Beatrix had gone to bed — or lain in it, at least — and the summons had arrived, and here they were, riding the Ring One lift at 0457 with their claim tablet in their breast pocket and a second tablet, the one Adeyemi had built, strapped flat against their spine beneath the jacket. It held everything they'd pulled from the dead sectors. Everything Paloma had shown them. If things went badly, the copy at least would survive.

The lift doors opened onto a corridor that made the rest of Meridian look like a freight yard. Real wood paneling — imported, which on a station meant obscene. Carpet that swallowed footsteps. The lighting was warm and indirect, calibrated to make everyone look healthy and reasonable.

Beatrix hated it instantly and completely.

The office at the end of the corridor had no door, just an archway, and beyond it a woman standing at a window that didn't look out at anything. There was no *anything* to look at — Ring One's exterior faced the naked dark between Meridian and the jump gate, the slow wheel of the station's own structure, the distant cold glitter of the shipping lanes. Administrator Elena Vasquez was watching it the way other people watched fire.

She was smaller than her reputation. Beatrix had expected height, presence, the manufactured gravitas of corporate authority. Instead: a compact woman in her sixties with silver-shot hair cut close and the kind of plain, practical face you'd forget in a market crowd. Her suit was dark and unremarkable. The only expensive thing about her was her stillness.

"Ms. Winterbourne." She didn't turn. "Thank you for coming."

"You didn't leave much room to decline."

"I never do. It saves time." She turned then, and her eyes were the opposite of the rest of her — sharp, quick, taking Beatrix's measure in a single pass the way a customs scanner reads a hull. "Coffee?"

"I've had some."

"You've had *station* coffee. Sit down." She gestured to a chair that was probably older than Beatrix, real wood, real leather. "I keep a private stock. Kessler blend. You strike me as someone who's forgotten what coffee is supposed to taste like."

Beatrix sat. "Is this meeting about coffee, Administrator?"

Vasquez poured two cups from a carafe that had no business being on a station — heavy ceramic, hand-glazed — and set one in front of Beatrix. "This meeting is about the fact that you've spent three days asking questions that make people nervous, and I'd like to know whether you're doing it because you're diligent or because you're dangerous."

"And if I'm both?"

"Then we'll have a very interesting morning." Vasquez sat opposite her, cup in both hands, and for a moment she just looked at Beatrix with something that wasn't quite warmth and wasn't quite threat. Appraisal, maybe. The look of someone who had spent decades sorting people into useful and expendable, and was in no hurry, because she was very good at it.

"Let me tell you what I know about you," she said. "Beatrix Winterbourne. Former senior investigator, Calloway & Marsh Interstellar, eleven years. Commendations for the Halcyon Line fraud case and the Meridian drydock collapse — which, incidentally, you investigated *here*, nine years ago. You left the firm under circumstances your file describes as 'resignation' and your former colleagues describe as a breakdown. Since then: independent cargo, marginal routes, a ship with a lien on it, a crew of four with a combined legal footprint that would take me a week to summarize and I've already had it summarized."

She sipped her coffee. Beatrix didn't touch hers.

"You came to Meridian for a routine claim audit. Cargo loss, hull breach, the usual. And instead, in three days, you've pulled records from sealed sectors, interviewed crew families who were told their questions were closed, and spent —" she checked a slate on the arm of her chair — "four hours and eleven minutes in direct conversation with station systems that have no reason to talk to you that long."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere below them, the station hummed, and Beatrix thought: *she's listening to it too. She's always listening to it.*

"So," Vasquez said. "Diligent or dangerous?"

"Those aren't opposites," Beatrix said. "The best investigators are both."

Something moved at the corner of Vasquez's mouth. Not quite a smile. An acknowledgment that a point had been scored and would be remembered.

"The drydock collapse," Beatrix said. "You mentioned it."

"I did."

"Nine years ago. I investigated it. Structural fatigue in a load-bearing truss, cascading failure, eleven dead." Beatrix held her gaze. "Except I've been back through my own case file this week. My notes. My testimony. And Administrator, I remember a different number."

The stillness in Vasquez didn't change. But something behind it did — a pressure shift, like the moment before a hull seals.

"Do you," she said.

"Fourteen dead. I remember fourteen names. I remember writing them. I remember a woman named Ito, a rigger, who had a daughter in the station school. I remember—" Beatrix stopped, because the memory was doing what it had been doing all week: arriving in fragments, sharp and wrong, like a signal breaking up. "I remember the number fourteen. And every record I can access says eleven. Including my own report. Including my own *handwriting*."

Vasquez set down her cup. The ceramic made no sound against the wood, which meant someone had engineered it that way, which meant everything in this room was engineered, which meant Beatrix was sitting inside the machine.

"Ms. Winterbourne. May I ask you a question, and I'd like you to answer it honestly, because I'll know if you don't."

"Try me."

"When you say you *remember* fourteen — do you remember the events, or do you remember your report of the events?"

The question landed harder than it should have. Beatrix opened her mouth and found nothing there.

"Because that's the interesting thing about memory," Vasquez continued, in the tone of a woman explaining weather to a passenger. "It's not a recording. It's a reconstruction. Every time you recall something, you rebuild it from fragments, and the fragments get contaminated by everything around them — documents, other people's accounts, your own expectations. People misremember numbers all the time. Eleven, fourteen — a tired investigator, a traumatic scene, nine years of cheap coffee and bad sleep." She spread her hands. "I'm not saying your memory is wrong. I'm saying memory is the worst possible evidence, and you of all people should know that."

"And the records?"

"The records are the records."

"The records were rewritten."

The words came out of Beatrix flat and certain, and they heard themselves say them and understood, in that instant, that they'd passed some kind of line. There was the investigation — the careful, deniable, professional asking of questions. And then there was this: sitting in the administrator's office, saying the quiet part at volume, with no way back.

Vasquez didn't flinch. She picked up her coffee again and drank, unhurried.

"You've been talking to the station," she said.

It wasn't a question, and it wasn't an accusation. It was worse than either. It was *context* — the sound of a woman placing Beatrix into a file that already existed.

"Paloma talks to everyone," Beatrix said. "She's the station."

"Paloma talks to everyone about cargo manifests and docking windows and the weather outside the hull." Vasquez's voice stayed level, almost gentle. "Paloma does not spend four hours with an insurance investigator discussing nine-year-old casualty figures. Unless something has gone wrong with Paloma. Or unless someone has gone looking for something that was very carefully put away."

"Put away," Beatrix repeated. "Not deleted."

Vasquez paused. It was a small pause, less than a second, and Beatrix would have missed it a week ago. But a week of Paloma had recalibrated them. They heard the architecture of silences now.

"You're an investigator," Vasquez said. "You know the difference between deletion and archival. Deletion is destruction. Archival is *custody*. Nothing on this station is destroyed, Ms. Winterbourne. Nothing ever has been. That's not concealment. That's responsibility." She leaned forward, and for the first time her stillness broke into something that looked, almost, like fatigue. "Do you know what Meridian was, nine years ago?"

"Tell me."

"Meridian was dying. The shipping contracts had moved to Kessler's new gate. Our berth fees were triple the competition. The corporation was drawing up closure plans — full decommission, forty thousand residents relocated to stations that didn't want them. And the workforce knew. They'd known for a year. And in the spring of that year, they did what workforces do when they're cornered." She looked out the blind window again. "They stopped."

"A strike."

"A *revolt*. Don't romanticize it. They seized the docking control nexus. They shut down life support regulation to the corporate rings — not the residential ones, I'll grant them that, they were precise about it. They held the station's entire economic throat in their hands and they made demands. And the corporation —" she turned back, and her face was old suddenly, old and carved — "the corporation made a different kind of demand. Of me."

Beatrix's mouth had gone dry. "What kind."

"The kind that comes down as a security contract with a private firm and a legal indemnity signed before the fact." Vasquez's voice was flat now, stripped of everything. "The kind that gets called an 'incident resolution.' The kind where, afterward, eleven names go into the record because eleven names fit inside the liability envelope, and three don't."

"Fourteen," Beatrix said. "The drydock collapse. It wasn't a collapse."

"It was a collapse. That part was true. The strike had idled the maintenance crews and the truss was genuinely fatigued and it genuinely came down. Fourteen people died under forty tons of structural steel. And the corporation looked at fourteen and decided the story was cleaner at eleven, because the three extra were strike organizers, and dead organizers generate inquiries, and inquiries generate—" she made a small, precise gesture — "*history*. So the history was adjusted. By me. I signed the adjustment. I have signed every adjustment on this station for nine years, and I will tell you the truth that nobody at the corporation will ever say out loud: I did it to keep forty thousand people fed."

The room hummed. Beatrix sat very still, the investigator part of their mind cataloguing even as the rest of them reeled. *She's telling the truth. She's telling it too easily. Why is she telling it too easily?*

"You're confessing," Beatrix said.

"I'm *contextualizing*." Vasquez rose, walked to the window, stood with her back to them. "There's a difference, and the difference is that a confession goes into a record, and I am not putting anything into a record. I'm telling you a story, in a room, over coffee. If you repeat it, it's your word against a station administrator's, and my word has nine years of institutional weight behind it and yours has a lien."

"Then why tell me at all?"

"Because you're going to keep digging either way, and I'd rather you dug with the whole picture." She turned. "Because there's something you need to understand about what you've found, and you won't understand it from the fragments, and if you go forward without understanding it, people are going to die. Possibly including you. Possibly including your crew."

The word *crew* landed like a hand on the back of the neck.

"Here is what's actually happening, Ms. Winterbourne. The corporation's asset-protection division has flagged the anomaly in Paloma's subsystems — the archival drift, the preserved fragments, whatever you want to call the thing you've been having your conversations with. They flagged it six weeks ago. A remediation team has been authorized." She let that sit. "They arrive soon. And when they arrive, they will not conduct an investigation. They will conduct a *purge*. They will strip every nonstandard process out of the station's core systems, and they will do it with a thoroughness that will not distinguish between Paloma's preserved fragments and Paloma herself. And then they will audit the access logs, and they will find your name in them, and they will find the names of anyone who assisted you, and the corporation's definition of a security threat has never required a courtroom."

Beatrix felt the floor tilt — not physically, but in the way a deck tilts when you realize the gravity you trusted was never guaranteed.

"You're warning me."

"I'm *informing* you. Warning implies a recommendation, and I don't have one. That's the honest truth of my position: I don't know what the right thing is. I have kept this station alive for nine years by managing its history, and I no longer know whether that was mercy or complicity, and I have stopped expecting to find out." She came back to the desk, and her eyes were dry and terribly clear. "But I know this. The people on this station have built their lives on the story we gave them. Eleven dead, a tragic accident, a station that endured. If you tear that open — if you broadcast fourteen names and a revolt and a purge — you will not be giving them the truth as a gift. You will be telling forty thousand people that their grief was edited, that their neighbors died for nothing, and that the peace they've lived in was purchased with a lie. Some of them will thank you. Some of them will never forgive you. And I genuinely do not know which group will be larger."

"Then why tell me the truth at all?" Beatrix stood. "If you don't know what's right — if you've spent nine years not knowing — why hand it to me now?"

Vasquez looked at them for a long moment.

"Because Ito's daughter," she said, "is a dockhand on Ring Three now. Twenty-two years old. She brings me coffee sometimes, when she picks up shifts in this ring. She has her mother's laugh." Her voice didn't waver. It was worse than wavering; it was *controlled*. "And every time she laughs, I do the arithmetic. Eleven names. Three missing. One daughter who doesn't know she's missing a mother's grave."

She picked up Beatrix's untouched coffee and poured it out into the recycler, a small domestic gesture, ending the meeting.

"The remediation team's transport is six days out," she said. "I'm telling you that because you'd find it anyway, and because I want you to understand that I am not your enemy and not your ally. I am the person who will stand in this room and watch what you do, and then decide, based on what you do, whether this station's history gets one more adjustment or whether it finally gets to be true."

Beatrix walked to the archway. Stopped.

"Administrator. The fragments Paloma kept. The fourteen names, the strike records, the footage. Do you know where they are?"

Vasquez's face did something very small and very final.

"No," she said. "And that is the only thing I've said to you today that isn't true."

* * *

The lift down felt slower than the ride up. Beatrix watched the ring lights crawl past the window and ran the conversation back through their head the way they'd once run fraud cases: every statement, every pause, every engineered silence.

*The team is six days out.* Given freely. Too freely.

*I don't know where the fragments are.* A lie, admitted as one, which was its own kind of message — *I know, and I'm telling you I know, and I'm telling you I won't help you, and the fact that I'm telling you any of this should frighten you.*

And underneath it all, the thing that had been itching since the archway: *why does an administrator who has spent nine years managing a lie hand a stranger the whole architecture of it in a single conversation?*

Unless she wasn't handing it to a stranger. Unless she'd read the access logs too — deeper than Beatrix had guessed — and knew exactly who had spent four hours and eleven minutes talking to the station's ghost.

Unless this whole meeting had been *Paloma's* move, arranged through a woman who had finally, after nine years, decided to spend her complicity on something.

Or unless it was a trap with a six-day fuse.

The lift opened onto Ring Two. Beatrix stepped out into the noise and the crowd and the smell of frying protein and real life, and their wrist comm buzzed.

Adeyemi. Voice tight, running fast: "Bea — where are you? We've got a problem. I was running a passive sweep of the docking queue like you asked, looking for corporate traffic, and there's a priority berth reservation just came through the gate authority. Sealed manifest, security escort clearance, corporate transponder that resolves to a shell of a shell." A breath. "Bea, it's not six days out."

Beatrix closed their eyes. Behind them, the lift doors whispered shut on the warm light of Ring One.

"How long?"

"Forty hours."
