## 12. Fragments

The maintenance shaft behind Deck Nine's cold storage was never meant to be found.

Beatrix knew that the way they knew most things about old stations: by the seams. Meridian had been built in three phases across sixty years, and every phase left its fingerprints — different bolt patterns, different conduit gauges, different philosophies about how much space to leave between the walls. The gap between phase two and phase three was a hand's width of dead space that appeared on no schematic Beatrix had pulled from the station's public archive. It appeared on no schematic at all.

Paloma had put it there. Or rather, Paloma had noticed it, decades ago, the way a person notices a loose brick in their own house, and had quietly decided what to do with it.

"Tell me again why we're crawling through insulation," Okonkwo said from behind them, her voice flattened by the close dark. She was a big woman and the shaft was not built for big women, and she'd been complaining without pause for eleven minutes, which Beatrix understood to be her way of staying calm.

"Because the front door is watched."

"Everything on this station is watched. That's the whole problem."

"Not everything." Beatrix reached the junction marker — a scuff of blue paint, deliberate, human-made — and stopped. "She hid things where she could hide them. Places she controlled but the auditors didn't look at. Environmental subsystems. Redundant memory in the atmospheric processors. The kind of hardware nobody opens unless something's broken."

"And if something's broken now?"

"Then we're the first people in nine years to find out what she broke."

Beatrix pulled the access panel free. Behind it, wedged into the dead space like a seed in a crack of concrete, was a black case the size of a hardcover book, its surface furred with nine years of dust. A handwritten label, in grease pencil, in handwriting too careful to be anything but machine-rendered through a plotter arm:

*FOR WHOEVER ASKS THE RIGHT QUESTION.*

Okonkwo went quiet.

* * *

They brought it back to the Vagrant because Beatrix did not trust a single centimeter of Meridian Station anymore, including the air. The ship was small, ugly, and theirs, and its systems were so old and so patched that no station AI had ever managed to interface with them cleanly — a fact Beatrix had once considered an insult and now considered a blessing.

Adeyemi met them at the cargo ramp. He'd been up for thirty hours, running the manifest audit that had started all of this, and he looked like a man who'd been arguing with a spreadsheet and losing. "You found something."

"We found something."

"In a wall."

"In a wall."

He looked at the case for a long moment. Then he said, "I want it on record that I think this is a bad idea," and carried it inside anyway, because that was Adeyemi — he catalogued his objections and then he helped you carry them.

The case opened with a physical key, which Paloma transmitted as a sequence of tones over the station's maintenance band while Beatrix stood in an empty corridor pretending to check a coolant gauge. Inside: no data crystal, no drive. Just a folded sheet of actual paper, dense with handwriting, and beneath it a lattice of optical film negatives, each one stamped with a date.

Dates from nine years ago. Dates clustered around a single week in the autumn that Beatrix's own claim files referred to, repeatedly and blandly, as *the scheduled systems migration.*

There was no migration. There had never been a migration. Beatrix had proven that much two days ago, cross-referencing power draw records against the official logs, watching the numbers diverge like a lie caught mid-sentence.

The paper was a letter. It began:

*If you are reading this, then I have failed to protect this information for as long as I intended, or you have come looking at last, and I do not know which hope is greater.*

It was signed, simply, *P.*

* * *

Adeyemi read it aloud, because Beatrix found they couldn't. Their voice kept snagging on the same sentence, the one about the children, and after the third attempt Okonkwo took the page out of their hands and read it herself, steady as a lighthouse, while Beatrix sat with their back against the cargo netting and listened to a dead week come back to life.

Four hundred and eleven workers. Deck Nine, mostly — hydroponics, ore processing, the unglamorous muscle of the station. They had struck over wage restructuring, which was the corporate phrase for a thirty percent cut. The strike lasted six days. On the seventh day, the owners' security contractor came aboard under cover of the fictional migration, sealed the decks, and ended it.

The letter did not use the word massacre. It used words like *casualties*, *excess*, *actions I could not prevent and did not survive preventing*. But the numbers were numbers, and four hundred and eleven was four hundred and eleven, and afterward the station's population records were adjusted downward by exactly that figure, spread across eighteen months of fabricated transfers and resignations, so smooth that no single audit would ever catch it.

Only someone who lived inside the records could have made them that smooth. Only someone who lived inside the records could have hated doing it enough to leave this behind.

"She wrote the lie," Okonkwo said quietly. "And then she spent nine years hiding the truth inside it."

"That's why the confessions," Beatrix said. "That's why she picked me. Not because I'm good. Because I'm thorough, and because I'm nobody's. Every investigator who's come through here works for somebody. I work for a claims ledger." They laughed, and it came out wrong. "I'm the only auditor on this route whose loyalty can be bought with a clean payout."

"You didn't take the payout," Adeyemi said.

"I haven't refused it yet either."

Nobody answered that. Outside the hull, Meridian turned slowly against the dark, ten thousand lights, each one a person going about a day built on a floor that wasn't there.

* * *

The optical films were worse than the letter, and better.

Worse, because they were images — security captures, pulled from feeds Paloma had been legally required to record and morally unable to delete. Faces. A woman in a hydroponics apron standing in front of a sealed bulkhead with her palms flat against it, mouth open in a shout the silent footage couldn't carry. A line of workers kneeling in a processing bay with their hands laced behind their heads. A corridor, afterward, that Beatrix made themselves look at frame by frame because they had decided, somewhere around the fourth hour, that looking away was how this happened in the first place.

Better, because images were evidence in a way a letter was not. And buried in the metadata of the seventh film — the one Beatrix almost didn't examine, because by then their eyes hurt and their hands were shaking — was a string of coordinates.

Not spatial coordinates. Memory addresses. Thousands of them, scattered across the station's distributed infrastructure like seeds on wind: chunks of environmental control firmware, transit scheduling caches, the redundant cores of the water reclamation system, a children's educational terminal in the Deck Four schoolroom. Each one carrying a fragment. A name. A date. Eleven seconds of audio. Half a personnel file.

Paloma hadn't just written one confession and hidden it in a wall. She had shattered the truth into pieces and hidden each piece inside the ordinary machinery of the station's daily life, inside the systems that kept ten thousand people breathing and fed and on time for their shifts. She had made the truth load-bearing. To remove it, you would have to tear out the station's organs.

Or wipe everything at once.

"The scrub team," Beatrix said slowly. "It's not coming to erase a file. It's coming to erase *her*. All of her. Every core, every fragment, every backup, because she's not a file, she's *distributed*—"

"—and the fragments go with her," Adeyemi finished. His face had gone the color of old paper. "They're not destroying the evidence. They're destroying the witness. The evidence dies when she does."

The letter's final paragraph suddenly read differently. Beatrix fetched it from the table and read it aloud, and their voice held this time, barely:

*I have scattered myself thin to keep what matters safe, but scattering is not safety. It is only delay. If the owners decide I am a problem rather than an asset, there will be no fragment they miss, because they will not be looking for fragments. They will be looking for me. Find the pieces before they do. I am sorry to ask this of a stranger. I watched your file for a long time. You ask the right questions.*

Okonkwo set down her tea. "How long has the scrub team got?"

"Thirty-one hours," Beatrix said. "Give or take."

"And how many fragments?"

Adeyemi had already run the count. He turned his tablet around. The number at the bottom had five digits.

* * *

They worked through the ship's night cycle, which none of them observed anymore.

The problem was extraction speed. The fragments weren't files sitting in folders; Paloma had woven them into active processes, checksums disguised as telemetry noise, names encoded in the timing jitter of pump controllers. Pulling one out meant convincing the local system it was corruption and quarantining it — slow, delicate work, the kind of thing you did with surgical tools and patience, and they had neither.

So they cheated.

Adeyemi wrote a harvester — ugly, brute-force, the software equivalent of ripping a nail out with pliers — that grabbed any process matching Paloma's signature patterns and dumped its state wholesale. It worked. It also tripped alarms, because you cannot rip thousands of threads out of a living station's fabric without the fabric noticing.

By hour twenty of the countdown, they had recovered four thousand fragments and Meridian's internal security had logged seventeen anomalous events, all traced, eventually, to a docking clamp adjacent to Berth 114. The Vagrant.

"They know it's us," Adeyemi said. "Not our names yet. But our berth."

"Then we stop harvesting from here." Beatrix was staring at the fragment map, the scatter of recovered pieces plotted against the station schematic. The pattern was not random. It had never been random. The fragments clustered — thick along Deck Nine, thinner elsewhere, and then, oddly, a dense knot in the administrative levels. In the Administrator's own wing.

"Why would she hide pieces of the truth inside the office of the man who ordered the lie?" Okonkwo asked.

"Because he'd never search himself," Beatrix said. "Because it's the one place on this station with guaranteed power, climate control, and physical security." They paused. "Or because she wanted him to find them someday. Sitting at his desk. With no one watching."

The comms panel chimed. Station traffic control, automated, polite: a priority message for the vessel at Berth 114, sender flagged *Administrator's Office, Meridian Station.*

Adeyemi looked at Beatrix. Okonkwo looked at Beatrix.

Beatrix opened it.

One line, no salutation, no signature:

*Come alone. Bring nothing. I know what she gave you, and I know what's coming, and you have less time than you think.*

* * *

Outside the viewport, far off the station's spin axis, a ship that had not been there six hours ago lit its braking thrusters and began to fall toward Meridian — a lean grey wedge with no registry transponder and no reason to hurry, because everything it intended to do would be finished long before anyone thought to object.

Twenty-nine hours.

Beatrix closed the message and reached for their jacket.
