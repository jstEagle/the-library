## 14. Okonkwo's Choice

The Vagrant's galley smelled like recycled coffee and burnt protein, which meant Adeyemi had been cooking again, which meant someone was upset.

Okonkwo stood at the counter with her back to the door, hands flat on the surface, staring at nothing. She'd been there for twenty minutes. Beatrix knew because they'd been standing in the corridor outside for twenty minutes too, working up the nerve to walk in.

"You going to hover out there all cycle," Okonkwo said without turning, "or are you going to come in and make it worse?"

Beatrix came in. Sat down at the little bolted table that had held four people once and two people now and, lately, felt like it could hold none. "Adeyemi cook?"

"Adeyemi cooked." Okonkwo nodded at the pan. "Then she went to go sit with Paloma's fragments again. Third time today."

"She's worried about you."

"I know she's worried about me." Okonkwo finally turned. Her face had the particular grayness of someone who hadn't slept and didn't intend to start. "That's the problem, Beatrix. Everybody keeps being worried *about* me instead of talking *to* me."

"Maybe because every time we talk to you, you look like you're doing math we can't see."

Okonkwo laughed — a short, ugly sound with no humor in it. "Math. Yeah. That's one word for it." She pulled out the chair across from Beatrix and sat, elbows on the table, and for a long moment neither of them said anything. The station hummed through the hull, that deep Meridian drone that stopped sounding like anything after your first week and never quite left your bones after that.

"Forty hours," Okonkwo said eventually. "Thirty-one now. You've been counting too."

"Everyone's been counting."

"And nobody's decided anything. We've got nine years of murdered dockworkers sitting in a memory core under Deck Nine, an AI who's been hiding them like contraband, and a corporate scrub team inbound to burn it all down. And this crew—" she gestured vaguely at the bulkhead, at the ship, at the whole sorry arrangement of their lives, "—this crew still hasn't had the actual conversation. The real one. Not the logistics conversation. The other one."

"The one where somebody says what they actually want."

"Yeah." Okonkwo rubbed her eyes. "So let's have it. Just us. I'll go first."

Beatrix braced. They'd known Okonkwo for six years — hauled with her through the Kestrel corridor when the tolls were tripled overnight, sat with her through the arbitration that ate half her savings, watched her weld a coolant line back together with her bare hands and a prayer while the ship bled atmosphere around them. Okonkwo did not flinch. It was the thing everyone said about her. *Okonkwo doesn't flinch.* It was practically her job title.

But she was flinching now. Or something adjacent to it. Something Beatrix had never seen on her face before, and it took them a moment to name it.

Shame.

"When I signed onto Meridian," Okonkwo said, "I wasn't signing onto Meridian. I was signing onto the contract. Halcyon Orbital Logistics, tier-two maintenance certification, hazard supplement clause fourteen. You remember what clause fourteen pays out?"

"Death or permanent disability. Triple rate."

"Triple rate." She nodded slowly. "My sister's kids are on Thessaly Station. Both of them. Schooling, medical debt from when their mother got sick — my sister, before she passed — all of it sits on my signature. Every month I wire home forty percent of net. That's not generosity, Beatrix, that's just arithmetic. So when I look at this situation, I don't get to be noble first. I have to do the math *first*, and then see if there's any room left over for noble."

"Nobody's asking you to be noble."

"Somebody always is." She smiled, tired. "Usually it's the person with the least to lose."

"That's not fair."

"It's completely fair. Look at the board. You lost your career already — burned-out investigator, blacklisted from three firms, hauling cargo because it's the only work that doesn't ask questions. Adeyemi's a ghost by choice, no family, no paper trail, nothing anyone can take off her. Me?" She spread her hands. "I'm the one with dependents. I'm the one whose death benefit has a number attached. When the scrub team comes through that airlock, they're not going to arrest anybody. They're going to *delete* everybody. And the difference between deletion and survival is going to come down to who was holding what when the lights went out."

"So you want to walk away."

The words came out harder than Beatrix intended, and they saw them land. Okonkwo's jaw tightened.

"I want to *live*," she said. "Those aren't the same as walking away. But yes. Part of me wants to take the Vagrant out of this dock, point her at the jump gate, and never think about Deck Nine again. A big part. A part that does the math every night when I lie awake listening to this station breathe." She looked down at her hands — scarred knuckles, a missing fingertip from a pressure-door incident nobody ever filed a report on. "And then there's the other part. The part that won't shut up."

"Which part is winning?"

"That's the thing." Okonkwo laughed again, softer this time. "Neither one. They're dead even. Dead even for three days now, and I keep waiting for one of them to win, and they don't, and I'm so tired, Beatrix. I'm tired in a way sleep doesn't touch."

* * *

They found Adeyemi in the aft compartment, cross-legged on the deck plating beside the portable core housing they'd rigged three days ago — the battered salvaged unit where Paloma's fragments lived, the ones she'd smuggled out of the archive layer before the scrub order went formal. Adeyemi had her hand flat against the casing, which she did sometimes, though the fragments couldn't feel it. Or maybe they could. Nobody knew anymore what Paloma could feel.

"How is she?" Beatrix asked quietly.

"Distributed. Scared, if scared is a word that still applies." Adeyemi didn't look up. "She keeps replaying the same fragment. Dockworker testimony, timestamped nine years ago, day of the revolt. A woman named Ferro describing the barricades on Deck Nine. Paloma plays it maybe once an hour. I think it's how she checks that she's still herself."

"Ferro. That the name from the manifest?"

"One of forty-three." Adeyemi finally looked up, and her eyes were red-rimmed. "Forty-three names the station says never existed. Forty-three families, if they had families, who were told their people transferred to Ceres or died in an accident or simply moved on. Nine years of nobody lighting a candle." She tapped the core casing gently. "She remembers all of them. Every name. She's carried them alone for nine years in a building full of people who forgot, and now we're going to decide whether she gets to stop carrying them, and we're taking our sweet time about it because one of us is doing math."

"Adeyemi—"

"No. Don't defend her. I love her, but don't defend her." Adeyemi stood, brushing dust off her knees. "You know what the worst part is? I understand her. Completely. If I had her obligations, I'd be doing the same math. That's what makes it unbearable. It's not cowardice. It's just weight, and she's carrying more than the rest of us."

"Where is she now?"

"Airlock two. Watching the approach vectors." Adeyemi's mouth twisted. "Counting."

* * *

Beatrix found her there, sure enough. Okonkwo sat on the bench beside the observation port, suit liner half-zipped, watching the traffic lanes feed into Meridian's docking spine. Cargo tugs. Passenger ferries. And somewhere out past the marker buoys, beyond the lane markers, a Halcyon fast courier burning hard for the station with a scrub team aboard and a warrant that made everything legal.

"Thirty hours," Okonkwo said. "Give or take."

"You should sleep."

"I'll sleep when it's over." She scooted down the bench to make room, which was as close to an invitation as Okonkwo ever issued. Beatrix sat.

For a while they just watched the traffic. A fuel skiff crawled past the port close enough to read its registry stencil. Somewhere below them, eleven thousand people went about their shifts in a station that had forgotten its own dead, and the forgetting looked exactly like living: laughter in the mess halls, kids running the concourse, the market on Deck Four loud and bright and utterly innocent of what it was built on top of.

"My father worked docks," Okonkwo said suddenly. "Back on Ceres, before the consolidation. Non-union, after the unions broke. He used to say the company didn't hate the workers. That was the part people got wrong. The company didn't hate anybody. Hating takes attention, and attention costs money." She turned her head, and in the reflected light of the traffic lanes her face looked carved out of something older than she was. "He said the company just *weighed*. Everything went on the scale. Labor, risk, liability, reputation. And whatever tipped the scale wrong got removed. Not punished. Removed. Like a line item."

"Is that what Halcyon did on Deck Nine?"

"That's what Halcyon does everywhere. Deck Nine was just the time they got caught." She exhaled slowly. "Except they didn't get caught, did they? They erased it. Forty-three people, gone cleaner than a bad quarter's earnings. And it would've stayed erased if Paloma hadn't broken protocol and started keeping souvenirs."

"So the scale's tipping again," Beatrix said. "Right now. Us on one side, the truth on the other."

"Don't." Okonkwo's voice cracked like a whip. "Don't make it poetry. Poetry is how people talk themselves into dying for free."

"Then make it numbers. Talk to me in numbers, since that's your language. What's the number?"

Okonkwo stared at her for a long moment. Then, to Beatrix's genuine surprise, she answered.

"Death benefit, clause fourteen, triple rate: it covers the kids' debt twice over. If I die doing something Halcyon officially disapproves of, my sister's children are free. That's the number. That's the number I've been staring at for three days." Her voice dropped. "Do you understand what a disgusting calculation that is? I've been sitting here weighing whether getting myself killed might be the most responsible thing I ever do. My sister would rather have a broke brother-in-law than a paid-off mortgage and an empty chair. I know that. The kids know that. And the math doesn't care. The math says the money helps them more than I do, at distance, across four years of light-lag, wiring home forty percent of a life they barely remember me living."

The words hung in the airlock's cold air. Out in the lanes, a tug sounded its horn, distant and meaningless.

"But that's only if I die," Okonkwo went on. "There's another outcome. The one where I survive and Halcyon blacklists me, and the contracts dry up, and the wiring home stops, and the kids' debt compounds at eleven percent until it eats everything. That outcome has a number too. It's bigger. It's the number that's actually keeping me up at night, because it's not dramatic, it's not noble, it's just slow. Slow ruin. The kind you can watch happening." She laughed, hollow. "You asked which part is winning. Neither. Because both futures are bad, and the only variable I control is which flavor of bad I pick."

"There's a third future," Beatrix said carefully.

"The broadcast."

"The broadcast. Paloma goes distributed, the archive goes public, and Halcyon's cover story collapses in front of eleven thousand witnesses. Scrub teams don't operate well in daylight. Corporations survive scandals all the time — what they don't survive is looking *weak*, and a station-wide broadcast they failed to prevent makes them look weak for a decade."

"That's your theory."

"It's Adeyemi's theory. I find it persuasive."

"It's a theory," Okonkwo repeated. "Built on the assumption that the broadcast works, that the scrub team doesn't cut the uplinks first, that the station's population believes a restored archive over nine years of official record, and that Halcyon's response is embarrassment instead of escalation." She shook her head. "You're betting lives on a theory."

"We're betting lives either way. Doing nothing is also a bet. It's a bet that Halcyon stops here. That they wipe Paloma, seal Deck Nine forever, and never need to do this again anywhere else. You believe that bet?"

Okonkwo didn't answer. Through the port, far out past the buoys, a new drive flare bloomed — small, hard, accelerating on a military-grade curve no cargo hauler would ever touch. Both of them saw it. Both of them knew what it was.

"Early," Beatrix said quietly. "They're early."

"Or that's a decoy and the real team's already aboard." Okonkwo stood, zipping her liner the rest of the way, and her hands were steady now — steadier than they'd been in three days, steadier than they'd been since the first confession, and Beatrix realized with a strange lurch that the decision had just been made, right there, between one breath and the next, and Okonkwo had chosen the flavor of bad.

"Get Adeyemi," she said. "Wake the fragments up. All of them. If we're doing this, we're doing it tonight, before that thing finishes its burn — and Beatrix?" She paused at the airlock hatch, backlit by the approaching flare.

"Yeah?"

"If I'm doing the math anyway — if I'm gambling my sister's kids on a theory — then I want better odds than a theory." Her jaw set. "I want Paloma to tell us everything she knows about Halcyon's scrub protocols. Everything she's learned in nine years of watching them watch her. Because if we're going to spend ourselves, we're going to spend ourselves *well*."

She pulled the hatch open — and stopped. Beyond it, the ship's comm panel was already chiming. Not the station channel. Not the fleet band.

An internal frequency. One that shouldn't exist.

Paloma's voice came through soft and urgent, threaded with static: *"Beatrix. Okonkwo. I'm sorry — I tried to hold the boundary longer. There's a second team. They boarded through the cargo ring twelve minutes ago. They're already inside the station."*
