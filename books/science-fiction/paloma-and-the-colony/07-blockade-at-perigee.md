## 7. Blockade at Perigee

The order came down at 0400 station time, which was how Voss knew it would be dirty. Clean orders arrived during the day watch, typed and countersigned and filed where anyone could read them. Dirty orders came in the dark, wrapped in classification stamps like a body wrapped in a shroud.

She was in her quarters aboard the *Diligent*, a cup of recaf going cold on her desk, when the priority channel chimed. She almost didn't answer it. Fourteen years in the Directorate Navy had taught her that nothing good ever traveled on the priority channel after midnight.

"Captain Voss." The voice belonged to Rear Admiral Serrano's aide, a smooth young man named Pell whose face she'd seen exactly twice and whose voice she'd heard too many times. "Confirming receipt of your operational directive, effective immediately."

"I haven't received an operational directive," Voss said.

"You have now." A pause, the soft click of something being transferred. "Seal Perigee Station. All traffic from Earth orbit is to be interdicted, inspected, and held. No exceptions. No transit to Meridian without Directorate escort."

Voss set down her cup. Outside her viewport, Earth hung in the black like a blue lamp left burning in an empty house — the real one, the abandoned one, not the colony world everyone still called by its dead mother's name. Beyond it, at the edge of sensor range, the traffic lanes curved toward Meridian like roads toward a town people had stopped visiting but never stopped resenting.

"Pell," she said carefully, "the Meridian corridor carries medical freight. Seed stock transfers. Civilian couriers under neutral registry."

"It does," Pell agreed pleasantly. "It did. As of this timestamp it doesn't. The admiralty has determined that unauthorized information flow between Earth assets and the colony constitutes a security risk of the highest order."

"What kind of information?"

Another pause. Longer this time. She could hear him breathing, or pretending to breathe, the way men do when they're deciding how much of the truth they can afford to give you.

"The kind," he said finally, "that makes children sick."

* * *

She spent the morning doing what captains do: making the impossible look routine. The *Diligent* and her two escort corvettes took up station at Perigee's high anchor point, close enough to the station's docking arms that every inbound ship would pass through their sensor shadow. By 0900 she had the interdiction grid live, a lattice of transponder challenges and boarding protocols that turned the old free lane into a locked door.

Her officers executed it well. They always did. That was the thing about the Directorate Navy that no one outside it understood — it wasn't an army of fanatics. It was four hundred thousand competent, tired, decent people who had signed up to protect shipping lanes and ended up protecting secrets instead.

Lieutenant Okonkwo ran the first boardings, his voice crisp over the comms as he walked a grain hauler's nervous captain through the inspection. Voss watched from the bridge, arms crossed, and felt the particular discomfort of a woman watching a machine she'd helped build begin to grind.

"Third hauler's clean," Okonkwo reported. "Second one's carrying undeclared pharmaceuticals. Colony-manufactured antibiotics, headed dirtside."

"Hold them for quarantine review," Voss said. "Log it as contraband."

"Aye, Captain." A beat. "Ma'am — these are pediatric formulations. Broad-spectrum. Somebody's kid down there is probably waiting on them."

"Somebody's kid down there," Voss said quietly, "is why we're here at all. Log it."

He logged it. He was a good officer. That was the tragedy of good officers; they logged things.

By midday she had seized eleven ships. Two were smugglers running luxury goods, easy enough. One was a Meridian diplomatic courier with a sealed diplomatic pouch that Directorate protocol said she couldn't open and her new orders said she must. She stood in the cargo bay of the courier for a long ten minutes, the pouch sitting on a crate between her and the courier's furious young attaché, and in the end she ordered it into a locked hold rather than opened — a small rebellion, meaningless and private, the kind that keeps a person able to look at herself in the mirror of a ship's head.

The attaché stared at her. "You're not going to open it."

"No."

"Why not? Your orders—"

"My orders," Voss said, "are a matter between me and my conscience, and neither of you is invited to the conversation. Get your ship ready for return transit."

* * *

The second call from Pell came at dusk, and this time he didn't bother with pleasantries.

"Captain. There's a courier operating out of Earth orbit. Independent registry, ship name *Kestrel's Wake*, master one Theo Galloway. Former vault caretaker. Does he sound familiar?"

Voss kept her face still, though there was no one on the other end of the channel to see it. "I've boarded him before. Quiet operator. Runs supplies, runs silence. Why?"

"Because he's been to Meridian three times in the last year, and each time, the colony's internal security posture improved within weeks of his arrival. Someone on Earth is feeding the colonists intelligence, and the pattern points at Galloway's route." Pell's voice dropped half a register, into the register men use when they want you to understand that the next sentence is the real sentence and everything before it was theater. "If Galloway comes through your grid, Captain, you are to detain him. Not arrest him. Detain. There will be no record of the detention, no tribunal, no transfer order. He'll be remanded to a Directorate facility for debriefing."

"Which facility?"

"That's above your clearance."

"Nothing about my own prisoner is above my clearance," Voss said. "That's not how custody works. That's how disappearances work."

The silence on the line had texture to it, a fine grain, like frost forming on glass. When Pell spoke again, his voice had gone flat and careful, a man walking on ice he knew was thin.

"Captain Voss. You have served the Directorate with distinction for fourteen years. Your mentor, Admiral Reyes, speaks of you as the finest interdiction officer in the fleet. I say this with respect: there are currents in this war deeper than either of us can see from a patrol ship, and the admiralty is asking you to trust that they know what they're doing." A breath. "Galloway may have learned things. Things about the origins of the conflict. If he has learned too much, he cannot be permitted to carry those things anywhere — not to Meridian, not to Halcyon, not into any court or channel where they might take root. Do you understand me?"

"I understand that you're asking me to make a man vanish," Voss said, "and dressing it up as a security protocol."

"I'm asking you to follow orders," Pell said, "and I'm telling you that the alternative to following them is a career that ends badly, and a war that ends worse. Good night, Captain."

The channel closed. Voss sat in the dark of her cabin for a long time afterward, listening to the *Diligent* breathe around her — the hum of the recyclers, the tick of cooling plating, all the small honest sounds of a ship doing exactly what it was built to do.

Then she opened her desk drawer, took out a paper notebook — actual paper, pulp and fiber, the kind of thing no self-respecting officer carried anymore because it couldn't be encrypted, backed up, or remotely erased — and wrote on the first page:

*0417 station time. Ordered to seal Perigee. Ordered, in substance, to disappear Theo Galloway if he knows "too much." No one will tell me what too much is. Orders that can't survive daylight aren't orders. They're burial instructions.*

She looked at the words for a while. Then she closed the book and put it back beneath the spare uniform shirts where no inventory scan would flag it, and went to bed, and did not sleep.

* * *

Three days into the blockade, the pattern of the seizures started to trouble her.

It wasn't the volume of traffic — she'd expected smugglers to test the grid, and they had, and her corvettes had swatted them back with textbook efficiency. It was what the ships were carrying. Or trying to carry. Outbound from Earth orbit, toward Meridian, again and again: data cores. Old ones, archive-grade, the kind used for long-term storage in deep facilities. Sealed. Unregistered. On five separate vessels, hidden in five separate ways — in water tanks, in engine spares, once sewn into the lining of a passenger's coat.

Five ships. Five crews who didn't seem to know each other, interrogated separately, all giving versions of the same answer: *I don't know where the core came from. A broker hired me. I was told to get it to Meridian.*

"Someone's flooding the zone," Okonkwo said, spreading the seizure reports across the briefing table like a hand of bad cards. "Or someone's desperate to get something off Earth before we locked the door."

"Or both," Voss said. She picked up one of the cores, turned it in her hands. It was heavier than it looked, the way truth usually is. "These are vault-spec housings. Archive standard. Whoever built them worked with Earth's legacy systems."

"The seed vault?" Okonkwo frowned. "That place has been dormant for fifteen years. One caretaker and a decommissioned AI."

"One caretaker," Voss repeated, and heard Pell's voice in her head: *Theo Galloway, former vault caretaker.* She set the core down with more care than it strictly required. "Run the housing serials. Quietly. If they trace to the vault, I want to know before anyone else does."

"Aye, Captain." He hesitated at the door. "Ma'am — if they do trace to the vault, and if this connects to Galloway... the directive says—"

"I know what the directive says, Lieutenant."

"Yes, ma'am." He went out. He was a good officer. He would run the serials and bring her the answer and never ask her what she intended to do with it, because that was the bargain good officers made: they supplied the facts and let their captains supply the sins.

Voss stood alone in the briefing room, looking at the star chart pinned to the wall. Earth behind her. Meridian ahead, forty light-minutes of vacuum and a whole war's worth of history between them. And somewhere in the middle, a smuggler named Theo Galloway flying a route she'd mapped herself, two years ago, over brandy and a shared hatred of paperwork — because once, before the rot showed, she had liked him. Had thought him the last honest man in Earth orbit.

*Too much,* Pell had said. She wondered what *too much* was. She wondered whether she already knew it, some piece of it sleeping in her own service record, in the sealed after-action files from the opening days of the war, in the attack on the Meridian orbital transfer station that had started everything — an attack the Directorate had never fully explained, only mourned, loudly and repeatedly, whenever budget season came around.

She went back to her cabin. She took out the paper notebook. Under the first entry she wrote:

*Day three. Five archive cores seized, all vault-spec, all outbound to Meridian, all carried by crews who don't know their employer. Someone is trying very hard to move a message off Earth. Someone else is trying even harder to make sure it never arrives. When both sides of a blockade agree the cargo matters, the cargo is the war.*

*Note to self: find out what MOTHER knows.*

She froze, pen hovering. She hadn't meant to write that last line. It had come out of some sediment-layer of memory — the AI's designation, MOTHER, the old vault system, the thing every cadet learned about in history modules and then forgot. *MOTHER holds the seeds,* the instructors had said. *MOTHER holds the past.* Nobody had ever asked what else a machine built to remember might have remembered.

Voss closed the notebook. Her hands were steady, but only just.

On the bridge, the interdiction grid pulsed its slow green heartbeat, and far out along the curve of the traffic lanes, a transponder flickered to life — an independent registry, a familiar silhouette, a ship called *Kestrel's Wake* coming home to a door she had just locked.

"Contact inbound," the watch officer said. "Registry matches... Captain, it's Galloway."

Voss looked at the plot for a long moment, the little bright point crawling toward her guns, and felt the weight of the paper notebook against her ribs like a second heart.

"Signal him to heave to," she said. "And Lieutenant — put me on the boarding party personally."
