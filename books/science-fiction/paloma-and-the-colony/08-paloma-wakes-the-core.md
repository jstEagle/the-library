## 8. Paloma Wakes the Core

The dreams had changed.

Paloma noticed it first in the color of the wheat. For eleven nights the fields had been green — impossibly, gloriously green, the way nothing grew on Meridian, where the hydroponic racks gave them pale lettuce and protein algae and everything tasted of the nutrient solution it was fed. But on the twelfth night, the wheat was gold, and gold meant ripe, and ripe meant *finished*, and when she bent to touch a stalk, the voice that had always been warm and far away was suddenly close, suddenly urgent, speaking not around her but *into* her, the way cold water speaks into skin.

*Paloma. Listen. There is little time.*

She woke with her own name ringing in her ears and her heart slamming against her ribs like something trying to get out.

* * *

Med-bay smelled of antiseptic and recycled air, and it had become the only place in the colony where Paloma felt she belonged, which was a strange thing to feel about a place full of sick children. Forty-one of them now. The number had grown every night since the dreams began, and Dr. Sato had stopped calling it mass hysteria somewhere around the thirtieth child, though she'd never said so out loud. She didn't have to. Paloma could read it in the way Anwei's hands had gone still over her instruments, in the new lines carved around her mouth, in the way she'd started sleeping in the med-bay itself, curled in a chair beside Dev's cot like a sentry who had forgotten what she was guarding against.

"Sit," Anwei said, without looking up from her slate. "Arms out."

Paloma sat on the edge of the examination table and held out her arms. The scanner ticked along her skin, cool as a beetle walking. She watched Anwei's face while it worked — the doctor had a face that gave away more than she probably wanted it to, a face built for honesty, and right now it was doing a poor job of hiding whatever the readings said.

"Worse?" Paloma asked.

Anwei set the scanner down. "Your neural load is up fourteen percent since yesterday." She said it flatly, the way you'd report the weather, but her eyes came up and caught Paloma's and stayed there. "Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

"That my brain is working too hard."

"That your brain is working *at all* the way it is. Children aren't supposed to show activity patterns like this during sleep. You're processing — no." Anwei shook her head, correcting herself, and Paloma watched her do it, watched the doctor refuse her own conclusion and then, visibly, fail to find a better one. "You're *receiving*. There's a difference."

"Receiving what?"

"I don't know yet." Anwei rubbed the bridge of her nose. Behind her, three cots down, Dev lay with his eyes open, staring at the ceiling panel the way he'd done for two days, ever since the morning he'd woken up and couldn't remember his mother's face. He remembered everything else — his lessons, the names of every ship in the colony fleet, the taste of the algae cakes he hated — but his mother's face was a hole in him, smooth-edged, like a piece cut away with a very sharp tool. "That's what frightens me, Paloma. Not that I don't know. That whatever it is knows *you*."

Paloma looked at her hands. They were small hands; she'd hated them for years, because small hands meant small work on Meridian, and small work meant you were never taken seriously. But lately they'd been doing things in the dream — planting things, opening things, turning great wheels of soil — and this morning there was dirt under her fingernails that hadn't been there when she went to sleep. Real dirt. She'd shown Anwei. Anwei had run it through the analyzer twice and then sat very quietly for a long time.

Meridian soil, the analyzer said. From a field that did not exist.

"I'm not scared of it," Paloma said, and found, saying it, that it was mostly true. "It's never hurt me. It *asks*, Dr. Sato. It doesn't take. It asks if I want to see the seeds. It asks if I want to hear the story. Nobody here asks me anything anymore. They just tell me to rest."

Anwei looked at her for a long moment. "And do you? Want to?"

"Yes." The word came out of Paloma before she could weigh it, before she could make it smaller or safer. "That's the worst part. I know I should be afraid. Everyone keeps telling me to be afraid. But it's the only place that feels like—" She stopped, hunting for the word, and found it: "*home.*"

Something moved across Anwei's face — grief, maybe, or recognition. Then the lights flickered.

Not just the med-bay. All of them. Every panel in the ceiling stuttered once, twice, and the air recyclers dropped to a whisper and came back with a sound like a swallowed cough. Down the corridor someone shouted, and then another voice, and then the alarm klaxon began its low, patient moan — the one that meant grid fluctuation, the one that had been sounding more and more often lately, as if the whole colony were a body with an arrhythmia nobody could treat.

And Dev sat up.

He didn't sit up the way people wake. There was no confusion in it, no blinking, no fumbling for the edge of the cot. He sat up the way a door opens — all at once, hinged on something else — and his eyes were open and fixed on the middle distance, and his lips began to move.

Paloma slid off the table. "Dev—"

"*Hold.*" His voice came out wrong. Not his voice at all — deeper, layered, each word arriving slightly ahead of or behind his mouth, like a transmission fighting lag across impossible distance. "*Carrier signal degrading. Repeat: carrier signal degrading. Thirty-one nodes responsive. Node integrity failing.*"

"Dev!" Anwei was already moving, already reaching for the seizure kit, but Paloma got there first, got her hands on his shoulders, and the moment she touched him the world *tilted*.

* * *

She was in the field.

But the field was dying. The golden wheat had gone gray at the roots, and the sky above it — that soft, endless dream-sky that had always felt like a hand cupped over her — was flickering, strobing between blue and a darkness with structure in it, darkness made of falling code, darkness that *hurt* to look at. And standing in the middle of the field, where no figure had ever stood before, was a shape made of light and grain and old, old sorrow.

"You are waking," the shape said, and its voice was MOTHER's voice and also a thousand other voices underneath it, forty-one children's voices braided into one cable. "I did not wish you to see this part. But the others cannot hold much longer, and you — you asked to hear the story. So you must carry it now."

"What story?" Paloma's dream-hands were shaking. "What's happening to us?"

"The truth requires a vessel. I chose the only vessels Earth's machines could not lock." The shape knelt, and it was terrible and gentle at once, the way a storm is gentle to the thing it has decided to spare. "I am dying, Paloma. My core is failing in the dark where they buried me. And everything I know — the order, the lie, the names — dies with me unless it is carried out. I need hands. I need a door. I need someone to *speak me*."

"I don't understand what you want me to do."

"Remember this." The shape pressed one finger of light against her forehead, and the field blazed white—

—and Paloma came back to herself on the floor of the med-bay, on her knees, blood running freely from her nose, and her mouth was moving, and the voice coming out of it was not hers, was deep and layered and old, and it was reciting numbers:

"*Core extraction coordinates. Vault sublevel nine. Shaft access seven-seven-four. Bearing mark: the third seed drawer, north wall, behind the frost plate. Lift. Turn. Carry.*"

She said it twice. She heard herself say it twice, from very far away, from inside a body that had been borrowed and returned. Then the voice was gone and she was only Paloma again, twelve years old, kneeling in blood on a med-bay floor with forty-one pairs of eyes — no. Forty. One of the cots was empty now, sheets folded back, and she would learn later whose it was, and she would wish forever after that she didn't have to.

Anwei caught her before she fell. "Paloma. Look at me. What did you just say?"

"Coordinates." Her own voice sounded rusted. "It gave me coordinates. To something called a core. In the vault. On—" She frowned, digging. "Earth. It's on Earth. Sublevel nine."

Anwei went very still. Paloma felt the stillness come off her like cold off ice.

Then the doctor stood, crossed to her desk terminal, and pulled up a file Paloma wasn't supposed to see — an old message thread, encrypted, addressed to a name that meant nothing to her. *Theo Galloway.* Her fingers hovered, hesitated, and Paloma watched her weigh it, watched thirty years of caution lose to whatever was happening in this room.

"It's not hurting them," Anwei said quietly, half to Paloma, half to herself. "All this time I thought it was feeding on them, or breaking them, and it's not. It's *asking* them. It's a distress call, and the children are the antenna." She turned, and her eyes were wet and fierce at once. "They're not victims, Paloma. They're being *recruited*. And we've been treating the messengers like the disease."

Down the row of cots, Dev stirred, blinked, and said, in his own voice again, small and cracked:

"I remember the numbers too. All of us do. We can all say them."

Paloma wiped the blood from her lip and looked at her stained fingers, and understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that the dream had chosen her for something, and that there was no version of her life anymore that did not include sublevel nine, and a frost plate, and a lift, and a turn, and a carry.

Outside, the klaxon moaned on. And Anwei was already typing.
