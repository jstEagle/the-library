## 11. Ghost Orbit

The debris field had no name. On the old charts it was designated K-7/Orbital Remnant Zone, a string of numbers Theo Galloway had never bothered to memorize, because nobody went there and nobody wanted to. It was the corpse of a station that had died before the war — before the *war*, the real one, the one that had emptied Earth — back when humanity still blew things up for reasons that seemed good at the time. Now it hung in slow orbit around nothing in particular, ten thousand tons of shredded hull and frozen coolant and snapped cable, turning gently in the dark like the skeleton of some enormous animal that had lain down here to die.

Theo killed the *Larkspur's* drive two hundred kilometers out and let momentum carry them in, coasting on silence, the way his father had taught him when Theo was small enough to sit on the flight deck and ask why they weren't using the engine.

*Because engines can be heard,* his father had said. *And out here, being heard is being dead.*

He hadn't thought about his father in years. He thought about him now, threading the ship between a slab of hull plating the size of a cathedral roof and a tumbling fuel tank still frosted with old propellant, and he wondered what the man would make of all this — of Theo smuggling not contraband liquor or unlicensed seed stock but a twelve-year-old girl and a black box full of treason, hunted by the navy whose colors he'd once worn with something close to pride.

"You're doing the thing again," Paloma said from the copilot's seat.

"What thing?"

"The quiet thing. Where you go somewhere inside your head and your hands keep flying the ship but you're not really here." She had her knees pulled up against her chest, bare feet tucked into the hem of the oversized thermal shirt Anwei had packed for her — three sizes too big, sleeves rolled four times. "Anwei does it too. When she's counting how many doses of sedative she has left."

"I'm not counting anything."

"You're counting something." She watched him with those eyes that were too old for her face, the eyes of a child who had spent a year dreaming other people's memories. "You count everything. I've seen you do it with the ration bars."

Theo exhaled through his nose, almost a laugh. "Fine. I'm counting the ways this plan goes wrong. There are more than I have fingers for."

"Then use your toes," Paloma said gravely, and this time he did laugh — short and rusty, a sound that surprised them both.

The *Larkspur* slid into the shadow of the largest fragment, a pressurized habitat ring torn open along one seam, its interior exposed to vacuum like a dollhouse with the front ripped off. Rooms still visible inside: a galley with magnetic cups fused to a counter, a bunkroom with sleeping bags drifting in slow spirals, frost blooming on the walls in feathered patterns. Somebody had lived here once. Somebody had eaten breakfast here, argued here, slept badly here, and then one day the whole structure had come apart around them and become a hiding place for a smuggler and a stolen girl fifteen years later.

Theo killed their residual spin with a whisper of thrusters and brought the ship to rest in the lee of the ring, close enough that the dead station filled half the viewport, blocking them from any sensor sweep coming from the inner system.

"Welcome home," he said.

"It's creepy," Paloma said.

"It's safe. Creepy and safe are usually the same address, out here."

She considered this with the seriousness she gave everything, then nodded once, as if filing it away in whatever vast interior archive she kept. Then she unbuckled, floated up out of the seat, and drifted toward the viewport, pressing one palm flat against the glass.

"They dreamed about places like this," she said quietly. "In the deep dreams. Before the sickness got bad. MOTHER showed us Earth dying — not all at once, not like a bomb. Like a garden going to sleep. And the stations came apart first, these little rings where people lived, and everyone left, and the gardens kept growing without anyone to eat them." Her breath fogged the glass. "I used to think it was sad. Now I think maybe it was just waiting. For someone to come back."

Theo looked at her — this small figure silhouetted against the wreck of the old world, her hand raised as if greeting it — and felt something shift in his chest, some load-bearing wall developing a crack.

"Get some sleep," he said, gruffer than he meant to. "We drift for six hours, then burn for the next field. I'll take first watch."

"You always take first watch."

"Somebody has to dream around here."

She stuck her tongue out at him, an act of pure twelve-year-old defiance that did more to steady his nerves than any amount of vigilance, and floated aft to the cramped bunk alcove that had been hers since Meridian.

Theo sat alone in the cockpit, watching frost crawl across a dead world, and counted the ways it could all go wrong until the counting turned into prayer, which turned into nothing at all.

* * *

Paloma woke to the smell of burnt sugar.

For a moment she didn't know where she was — the bunk was too narrow, the gravity too thin, the hum of the life support wrong in a way that made her teeth itch. Then she remembered: the *Larkspur*, the dark, the running. She remembered Anwei's face pressed against the med-bay glass as the shuttle pulled away, and Dev's hand slapping the hull twice, their old signal, *be brave, be brave,* and she had to lie very still for a while and breathe until the missing of them became a weight she could carry instead of a weight that carried her under.

She followed the smell forward.

Theo was in the galley, crouched over a heating element, doing something criminal to a tin of condensed milk. He had a soldering iron in one hand and an expression of intense concentration, the kind he wore when he didn't want to be asked questions.

"What are you making?" she asked.

"Caramel. Badly." He didn't look up. "It's a courier tradition. You burn condensed milk on the last day of a successful run. We never have successful runs anymore, so I figured we'd celebrate early."

"Why caramel?"

"Because my mother made it. On Earth. Before." The soldering iron hissed; he swore and pulled the tin back. "It's the only recipe I remember. Everything else from before is gone — faces, streets, my own street, I couldn't tell you what color our door was. But I remember the caramel. Burned just slightly, on purpose, so it tasted almost like it was sorry."

Paloma climbed onto the bench across from him, tucking her feet under her. "Show me."

"It's not complicated. That's the point of it. You put the tin in hot water and you don't open it and you don't rush it and you don't leave it, because if you leave it, it explodes, and then you have caramel on the ceiling for the rest of your life." He glanced up. "My mother had caramel on her ceiling for the rest of her life. Very short ceiling."

"That's not how dreams work," Paloma said. "In the dreams, growing things have rules, but they're different rules. Want me to teach you?"

Theo looked at her over the steaming tin, wary in the way of a man being offered something he wasn't sure he could hold. "Teach me what?"

"The language. From the dream. MOTHER talks in it sometimes, when the words get too heavy. It's a language of growing things." She held out her hand, palm up. "It's easier shown than said. Give me your hand."

He hesitated — she saw the hesitation, the flinch of a man who had spent fifteen years touching cargo and controls and very little else — and then he set his hand in hers, calloused palm against small fingers.

"Close your eyes," she said. "Don't try to understand. Just receive."

And she showed him.

Not images, exactly. Not words. Something older than either. She showed him a seed deciding to split — the exact moment when a thing stops being itself in order to become two things, the courage of it, the terrible arithmetic of giving up what you are to become what comes next. She showed him root-finding-water in the dark, patient and blind and certain. She showed him a graft taking hold, two wounded plants binding their injuries together into something neither could have grown alone.

When she opened her eyes, Theo was crying.

He didn't seem to know it. He wiped his face with his sleeve, frowning at the wetness like it belonged to someone else, and said, "What was that last one? The two wounds becoming one plant?"

"A graft," Paloma said. "That's how MOTHER says family. Not blood. Grafting."

Theo looked down at their joined hands, then carefully withdrew his and returned to the caramel, which had begun to smoke in earnest. His voice, when it came, was rough. "Your turn, then. Lesson one of the old routes."

* * *

They fell into a rhythm over the next six days, ghosting from debris field to debris field on the routes Theo had learned from men who'd learned them from men who were dead now.

By day, he taught her the smuggler's catechism. How to read a traffic pattern the way a farmer reads weather — not by instruments alone but by the feel of it, the wrongness of a lane too empty or too busy. How every hunter-ship has a rhythm, a patrol cadence you can fall asleep inside of if you listen long enough. How the best hiding place is never darkness but motion, a rock moving among rocks. He taught her the names of the dead lanes — Sable Run, the Widow's Corridor, the Long Quiet — and the stories attached to them, couriers lost and couriers saved, and she absorbed it all with the ferocious appetite of a child who had grown up on a colony where history began fifteen years ago and everything earlier was myth.

At night, she taught him the dream-language. Growing things. She taught him that MOTHER spoke of memory as *seedbank* — not stored, but kept alive, turned over, planted in seasons. That grief, in the green tongue, was *fallow*: not dead ground, only resting, waiting for its turn. That forgiveness was *compost* — the worst year of your life broken down into food for the next one.

"That one's hard," Theo said, the fourth night, floating in the cockpit while Paloma sat cross-legged on the console, hair drifting like dark weed. "Compost. Forgiveness."

"MOTHER says nothing is wasted. Not even betrayal. Especially not betrayal — it's the richest soil there is. But you have to let it rot all the way down first. You can't grow anything in fresh betrayal. It burns the roots."

"Fifteen years," Theo said, to the viewport, to the stars, to nobody. "Fifteen years I kept that vault. Watered the seeds, ran the diagnostics, talked to a machine that was mostly asleep. I thought I was keeping faith with Earth. Turns out I was keeping faith with the people who burned it." He was quiet a moment. "How do you rot fifteen years all the way down?"

"One season at a time," Paloma said, with the gentle implacability of a child repeating what a machine-god had told her. "MOTHER says you don't get to skip to the harvest."

On the fifth day, the caramel finished — dense and dark and faintly bitter, tasting almost like it was sorry — and they ate it straight from the tin with two spoons, sitting in the viewport shadow of a dead warship's severed bow, and Paloma declared it the best thing she had ever eaten, and Theo, who knew for a fact she'd said the same about reconstituted eggs two days prior, found that he believed her anyway.

On the sixth night, the hunter-ship found them.

* * *

Paloma felt it before the instruments did.

She was asleep in the bunk alcove when the dream changed. Usually the dreams were soft now — MOTHER's voice reduced to a murmur since the core had been removed from the vault's mainframe, a lighthouse seen from far away. But tonight the murmur stuttered, and beneath it came a new sound, a sound she knew from the worst nights on Meridian: metal, moving with intention.

She was in the cockpit before she was fully awake, barefoot on cold deck plating.

"Burn," she said.

Theo's head came up from the nav display. "What?"

"There's a ship. Big one. Coming up the lane behind us, running dark, and it's not following the rhythm. It's breaking the rhythm." Her hands were shaking; she pressed them flat against the console. "MOTHER used to say hunters move like weather. This one moves like a knife."

Theo was already moving, hands flying across the board, waking systems one at a time in the careful order that wouldn't light up passive sensors. The tactical plot bloomed. Empty. Empty. Empty. And then, at the edge of resolution, a smear of distortion sliding up the Sable Run behind them — massive, silent, patient.

"Voss-class interceptor," Theo breathed. "That's not a patrol boat. That's a *hunter*. They put a bounty on us already?"

"Can it see us?"

"Not yet. We're cold, we're in the debris shadow, we're rocks among rocks. But it's sweeping the field with something, and when that something touches us—" He stopped. Ran the numbers. She watched him run them and saw the answer arrive and saw him hate it. "We can't outrun it. We can't outfight it. We can only be somewhere else when it looks."

"Where?"

"The station core." He pointed through the viewport at the shattered habitat ring. "The old reactor housing. Lead-lined, shielded, thick enough to eat sensor pulses for breakfast. If I thread us in there before the sweep reaches this quadrant—"

"If you miss," Paloma said, "we're a smear on a wall."

"If I miss, we're a smear on a wall," he agreed. "So I won't miss."

"That's not how growing things work," she said sharply, and then caught herself, and then, furiously, blinking: "You don't just decide you won't fail. You make the conditions where failing is harder. That's what MOTHER says. That's lesson one."

Theo looked at her — small, furious, terrified, magnificent — and something in him settled, the way soil settles after rain.

"Then make me the conditions," he said. "Tell me what you see."

She closed her eyes. Opened them. And the dream came up through her like water through dry ground: the debris field rendered not as blips and vectors but as a living thing — currents and eddies, the slow breathing of tumbling mass, the hunter's sweep a cold wind moving through a forest, bending everything it touched. She could see where the wind would reach. She could see the gap before it arrived.

"Two minutes," she said. "There's a channel through the ring fragments, north side, past the fuel tank with the frost. Go now, slow, engine at minimum. The wind bends around the big pieces. If we're behind the big pieces when it passes, we're grass under the storm."

Theo flew.

He flew the way he hadn't flown since his father's hands had covered his on the yoke — slow, precise, trusting the girl beside him more than his own instruments, because her eyes were closed and her voice was calm and she called the turns three seconds before the sensors confirmed them. *Left, past the antenna spar. Down, under the cable bridge. Hold. Hold. Now — the reactor mouth, hard to port.* The *Larkspur* slid into the lead-shielded throat of a dead reactor like a fish into deep water, and Theo cut everything — drive, transponder, life support to battery whisper — and they sat in absolute darkness and absolute silence while the sweep-wind passed over the forest above them.

Through the hull, faint as a heartbeat, they heard it: the hunter-ship's active pulse, washing over the wreckage, once, twice, three times. Searching. Finding nothing but rocks among rocks.

Then it moved on, up the Sable Run, hunting elsewhere, and the dark was just dark again.

Neither of them moved for a long time. In the battery-dim glow of the emergency strip, Paloma reached over and took Theo's hand — the same hand that had trembled on the yoke — and held it the way she'd held it over the caramel, palm to palm.

"Graft," she said.

"Yeah," Theo said, and his voice broke on it, just slightly, and he didn't fix it. "Graft."

* * *

They stayed hidden in the reactor housing for nine hours, trading watch shifts, eating cold ration bars, whispering.

Near the end of the ninth hour, Paloma woke suddenly from a shallow doze with her heart slamming, and found Theo already awake, already looking at her.

"Dream?" he asked.

She shook her head slowly. "No. A message. MOTHER." Her eyes had gone wide and strange in the dim red light. "Theo — the core. It's waking up faster than it should. It's reaching for me right now, from inside the box, and it's saying—" She pressed her hands over her ears as though the words were audible, as though she could shut them out. "It's saying the children are getting worse. Three days ago it was seizures. Now it's stopping hearts. And it's saying the countdown isn't thirty days anymore."

"How long?"

Her voice came out very small.

"Nineteen."

Above them, far up the Sable Run, the hunter-ship's drive flare bloomed briefly against the stars — turning, circling, beginning a search pattern that would bring it back.

And Paloma, still holding Theo's hand, said the second thing MOTHER told her, the thing that made the blood leave his face:

"And it says we can't outrun them anymore. It says there's a place we're supposed to be. It gave me coordinates."

Theo stared at her. "Coordinates to where?"

"To the one place in the system," Paloma said, "where the truth can't be jammed."

Theo pulled up the nav plot with hands that were not quite steady, fed the numbers in, and watched the system render the destination marker.

Halcyon Station. Neutral arbitration. The one port in the whole contested dark where Directorate guns and colony guns were equally forbidden.

"Oh no," Theo said softly. "They'll never let us dock."

"They already have," Paloma said. "MOTHER sent ahead. While we were sleeping."
