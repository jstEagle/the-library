## 17. Homecoming Under Guns

The ship came out of the dark above Meridian with its hull still ticking from the burn, and Theo Galloway's first thought was that the colony looked wrong.

He had been coming here for fifteen years. He knew Meridian the way a man knows his own hands — the crescent of agricultural domes along the equatorial shelf, the smudge of the fishing fleets on the northern sea, the amber glow of the habitat rings at night like a string of lanterns hung in a dark room. He had watched that glow from orbit more times than he could count, usually with cargo holds full of seed stock and medicine and the small mercies Earth no longer sent anyone. It had always looked to him like what it was: the last lit window in a house everyone else had abandoned.

Now the window had bars.

They resolved as the ship dropped lower — gray shapes in high orbit where no gray shapes should be, three Directorate cruisers parked over the colony like crows on a fence, their hulls drinking the light of Meridian's sun and giving nothing back. Drop-ships hung beneath them in a slow carousel. And down on the surface, visible even from this altitude as a constellation of hard white points around the main spaceport and the council complex, were landing lights. Floodlights. The kind of lights you pointed at things you intended to watch.

"They're already here," Paloma said.

She stood beside him at the viewport, small and straight-backed, her hand resting flat against the glass the way she sometimes rested it against the core's cradle in the hold below — as if she could feel through it, some pulse too faint for instruments. She had barely slept in four days. None of them had. But her eyes were clear, and they stayed clear now, fixed on the cruisers without flinching, and Theo found himself drawing something from that he didn't fully understand. A twelve-year-old looking at the guns that had killed three of her friends, and not blinking.

"That's the *Vigilant*," he said. "And those two are *Sable* and *Corregidor*. That's not a patrol. That's an occupation force." He turned toward the cockpit. "Anwei?"

Her voice came through the comm a half-second later, thin with distance and relay lag but steady, the way it always was — the steadiness of a woman who had spent fifteen years delivering babies and setting bones in a place where panic got people killed. "Theo. Thank God. Listen to me before you commit to anything."

"Too late," he said. "I'm committing."

A breath on the line. He could picture her in the med-bay, one hand pressed over her earpiece, the other probably still moving — sorting vials, adjusting a monitor, doing two things at once because the children kept needing her. "There are four hundred marines on the ground. They came in six days ago under a 'protective stabilization order.' The council rolled over so fast you'd think they'd been waiting for permission. Chancellor Osei signed a transfer agreement yesterday. The Directorate gets the core; we get our ration lines restored and a promise that no one investigates how the sickness started." Her voice did not quite stay level on the last part. "They're coming for it tomorrow at 0900. Theo, if you're in system, don't dock. Don't—"

"Where are the children?"

Another silence. Longer. When Anwei spoke again, all the medic's composure had burned away and what was underneath was rawer and more useful. "Med-bay, west wing. Nineteen of them now. We lost a fourth last night. Little girl named Sefa. Dev hasn't left Devi's bedside in two days — he's holding on by spite alone, and I'm not sure how much longer either of them has." A pause. "Paloma there? Put her on."

"She's here." He glanced at Paloma, who shook her head once — *later* — and touched his arm, pointing at the console, at the approach vector he hadn't yet entered. Her expression said what she'd been saying since Halcyon, since Judge Okafor's chamber, since the long blind run home: *We didn't come all this way to hide.*

"Tell her—" Anwei began.

"I know what to tell her." Theo keyed the nav board and began, with the calm of a man signing his own death warrant, to plot a descent profile that would bring them down on the far side of the colony, away from the port, away from the floodlights. "Anwei. The transmitter array at Relay Station Nine. Is it still intact?"

"The old broadcast array? It's been decommissioned for years, it's—"

"Is it *intact*?"

"...Physically, yes. The council never funded the teardown. Why?"

"Because in about twenty hours," Theo said, "it's going to be the loudest thing on this planet."

* * *

They came down through the night side, engines banked low, riding a descent corridor Theo had used a hundred times to slip contraband past customs drones — a smuggler's trick, threading the wake of a decommissioned weather satellite whose orbital decay made a radar ghost wide enough to hide a ship in. The hull groaned. Paloma sat strapped into the jump seat behind him with the core's interface band wrapped around her forearm, and she talked to him the whole way down, quietly, steadily, the way you'd talk to a horse on a bad trail.

"You're drifting left. The dream says the wind off the sea comes in gusts after midnight. MOTHER showed me the weather records from the vault — the station logs. Trust the dream, Theo. Left. Now right. Now *down*, faster than you want to."

He trusted her. That was the strange, simple truth he kept arriving at and being surprised by. Fifteen years of trusting no one but himself and a half-asleep machine, and now he staked his life on a child's shared hallucination, and it held. The ridgeline rose up black against the stars and he pulled the nose up at the last possible second and the ship kissed the earth of a fallow field eight kilometers east of Relay Station Nine with a bump that rattled his teeth and raised a bloom of dust that would be visible from orbit for exactly as long as it took him not to care anymore.

He cut the engines. In the sudden silence, the ship ticked and settled, and somewhere far away — or maybe not far away at all, maybe inside his own chest — he heard the sound of everything he'd spent fifteen years building coming apart.

"Well," he said. "We're home."

Paloma unstrapped and looked at him. "This isn't your home."

"No," Theo agreed, popping the harness. "But it's about to be, one way or another."

* * *

Anwei met them at the edge of the field in a med-bay transport running dark, its running lights taped over, driven by a colony kid of maybe sixteen who looked terrified and thrilled in equal measure. She climbed out before the vehicle fully stopped and crossed the distance to them at a pace just short of running, and then stopped, abruptly, an arm's length away, as if seeing them in person required recalibration.

She was thinner than when he'd left. There were bruise-colored shadows under her eyes deep enough to hold water. Her coat smelled of antiseptic and recycled air and, faintly, of the particular sweetness of a ward where children have been dying for weeks.

"You look terrible," Theo said.

"You look like a man about to do something unforgivably stupid," Anwei said. "Good. That means you're taking this seriously." Her eyes went to Paloma and softened in a way that cracked something open in her whole face. "Come here, sweetheart."

Paloma went. Whatever passed between them happened with their foreheads touching and Anwei's hands cupped around the back of the girl's skull, and Theo looked away, out across the dark fields, because some griefs and some reunions weren't meant for witnesses.

When he looked back, Anwei had her doctor face on again, which meant the other face was folded away somewhere it could bleed in private. "Walk with me. Both of you. We have maybe four hours before the next patrol sweep comes through this sector, and I need you to understand exactly what you're walking into."

They walked. The fallow field gave way to scrubland, then to the gravel apron of the old relay road, and ahead of them, on a low hill against the stars, stood Relay Station Nine — a lattice tower eighty meters tall, guyed with cables gone slack, crowned with the skeletal dish arrays that had once carried Meridian's voice to the outer settlements before the war made every transmission a target. It looked like the ribcage of something enormous and patient, buried to the neck, waiting.

"It's dead," Theo said. "Officially."

"Unofficially?" Anwei asked.

"Unofficially, it needs a new power coupling, a realignment on the primary dish, and someone willing to stand at the top of that tower while three cruisers figure out what's happening." He shrugged. "Give me two hours and a working fusion tap and it'll be the loudest thing on this planet. The question is what we say with it. Because Anwei — listen to me. The moment we key that transmitter, there is no version of this where I walk away clean. The Directorate doesn't leave witnesses to a broadcast like this. And neither does your council, if they decide handing me over buys them goodwill."

"I know." She said it simply, without apology, and he loved her a little for it. "I've done the arithmetic. So has the council — that's the problem. Osei genuinely believes surrendering the core saves the children. He thinks the Directorate will cure them once they have what they want." She stopped walking and turned to face him, and in the starlight her expression was terrible and clear. "He's wrong, Theo. I've seen the Directorate medical protocols. I pulled them off a marine's datapad when he was sedated in my bay last week — broken ankle, nothing serious, but he talked in his sleep and I talked my way into his kit. Their contingency plan for the dream-sickness isn't a cure. It's containment. Sedation, isolation, and if the vectors persist—" Her jaw tightened. "*Termination of compromised assets.* That's the phrase. Assets. Children are assets now."

Paloma made a small sound. Not a cry. Something smaller and harder, like a stone dropping into a well.

"So we're not asking the council," Theo said slowly. "We're going around them."

"We're going around everyone." Anwei started walking again, faster. "Here's what I've built. The med-bay staff — four of us, all of whom have watched children die of a disease that isn't a disease. Two of the relay techs from the old days, Rask and Imani, who can have your power coupling fabricated by morning. And nineteen children in the west wing who are running out of time, plus one friend of theirs who refuses to stay in bed." She glanced sideways at Paloma. "Dev escaped the ward twice yesterday. Second time he made it as far as the perimeter fence before the marines picked him up. He told them — a twelve-year-old boy, surrounded by rifles — he told them he wasn't trying to escape. He was trying to get to the transmitter, because the dream told him it needed to be turned on, and someone had to do it since the grown-ups wouldn't."

Theo stopped walking.

For a moment he couldn't move at all. He stood on the gravel road under the alien stars of a colony he'd helped feed for fifteen years, and he thought about the vault — the quiet, the order, the beautiful loneliness he had called peace — and he thought about a boy walking toward armed soldiers because a dying machine's dream had asked him to, and something in Theo's chest that had been clenched for fifteen years finally, painfully, let go.

"All right," he said. "Then here's how we do it."

* * *

They worked through the small hours, and it was the best work of Theo Galloway's life.

Rask and Imani turned out to be worth their weight in vault gold — between them they had the fusion tap spliced and the coupling fabricated by 0300, working by shielded lamplight in the relay station's gutted control room, arguing in the shorthand of people who had built this grid together thirty years ago. Theo climbed the tower twice in the dark, testing guy wires and dish alignment with a headlamp shuttered to a slit, and each time he paused at the top with the whole colony spread out below him — the habitat rings' amber glow, the white constellations of the marine floodlights, the dark wound of the med-bay's west wing where nineteen children dreamed themselves apart.

From up there he could see the Directorate cruiser in low orbit, a moving star that didn't twinkle. Watching. Always watching.

At 0430, Anwei brought the children.

Not all of them — she wouldn't risk the ones who couldn't walk. But nine of them came, carried and supported and wheeled in two transports with their medical monitors taped over, and Dev led them off the transport on his own feet, hollow-eyed and burning, with Devi's small hand locked in his. They gathered in the control room among the cable runs and the dust, and they looked at the transmitter console the way pilgrims look at a shrine, and Theo — who had spent his whole adult life alone with a machine — felt his throat close.

Paloma knelt in front of them. "You know why we're here," she said.

"The garden," said a girl of six or seven, the youngest, her voice foggy with sedation and fever. "The green place. MOTHER's almost done telling us. But the story needs a door."

"A door," Paloma agreed. "And we're going to open it tonight. All the way. For everyone." She looked up at Theo. "MOTHER says the core can't broadcast through the planetary relay network — the Directorate owns the relays. But the old array transmits raw, wideband, omnidirectional. Every screen, every speaker, every implant receiver on Meridian will pick it up whether the network wants to carry it or not. And the cruisers will hear it too. Everyone will hear it."

"Everyone," Theo said, "is the point."

He sat down at the console. His hands were steady. He noticed that they were steady and understood, distantly, that he would remember this moment — this exact moment, the feel of the keys under his fingers, the smell of dust and ozone and sick children, the weight of Paloma's hand on his shoulder — for whatever remained of his life, however long or short the Directorate chose to make it.

"MOTHER," he said aloud, to the interface band on Paloma's arm, to the core humming in the transport outside, to fifteen years of quiet. "Are you there?"

The answer came through the band, through Paloma, through the nine dreaming children who spoke it together in a soft chorus that raised the hair on his arms:

*I am here, Theo Galloway. I have waited a long time to say what comes next. Do not be afraid for me. Be afraid for them — and then stop being afraid, and speak.*

He keyed the transmitter.

* * *

The array woke with a sound like a struck bell, subsonic, felt in the teeth rather than heard, and the old dishes drank the station's power and threw it at the sky.

On the console, the signal meters climbed. On the viewscreen Theo had wired to the output, the first frames of MOTHER's testimony assembled themselves out of static — timestamped logs, admiralty cipher headers, the false-flag order in the original hand of Fleet Admiral Corvane Duret, the attack on the *Meridian Dawn* that never happened, the fourteen thousand colonists who died in the retaliation that followed, catalogued by name, by age, by the seeds they had carried in their pockets when the missiles came.

And beneath it all, threaded through every channel, the children's dream — the green fields, the growing things, the patient voice saying *I remember, I remember, I was made to remember* — broadcast raw into every receiver on the planet.

In the habitat rings, forty thousand screens lit up unbidden. In the marine barracks, the floodlights flickered as the colony grid surged. In the council chamber, Chancellor Osei stood up from a table where he'd been rehearsing his surrender speech and watched Fleet Admiral Duret sign the death warrant of a ship that never sailed.

And in the control room of Relay Station Nine, Theo Galloway watched the doors.

They came fast — he'd given them ninety seconds before the cruisers triangulated the transmission, and they used seventy of them. Ground vehicles first, then the drop-ship thunder overhead, then marines in the doorway with weapons leveled, and behind them an officer Theo recognized from intelligence briefings — Colonel Vashti Renk, Directorate occupation command, her face rigid with the particular fury of a soldier who has just realized her own chain of command has been lying to her and hates the messenger for making her know it.

"Galloway!" she shouted over the bell-sound of the array. "Shut it down! Shut it down or I will—"

"You'll what?" Theo said. He didn't raise his voice. The transmitter was doing the shouting now, and they all knew it. "Shoot me in front of nine sick children? Go ahead. It's already out. You can't call it back. Every screen on this colony is showing your admiralty's signature right now. Every marine you brought has a receiver in their helmet. Ask them what they're hearing. *Ask them.*"

Some of the marines, he saw, had gone very still. One of them — young, freckled, weapon still aimed but wavering — whispered something to the sergeant beside him, and the sergeant didn't answer, because the sergeant was listening to the same voice in his own ear, the patient voice from the green fields, saying *I remember.*

Renk stepped forward, sidearm drawn. "You are under arrest for sedition, theft of Directorate property, and incitement—"

"Add piracy," Theo said. "I've earned it." He spread his arms, standing between the muzzle and the children, and found that he was smiling, and that the smile cost him nothing at all. "Fifteen years I kept the vault. Fifteen years I kept my mouth shut and my head down and called it peace. This is what I was keeping it *for*. Take me. But the truth's already in the air, Colonel, and air belongs to everyone."

Renk's finger tightened on the guard of her trigger.

Behind her, in the doorway, the freckled young marine lowered his rifle all the way to the deck.

It was, Theo thought, as the marines closed in and the cuffs closed cold around his wrists and Paloma's voice rose above the bell-sound of the transmitter — clear and furious and utterly unafraid — the single finest sound he had ever heard: metal, letting go.

They dragged him toward the drop-ship. He did not resist. There was nothing left to resist *for*; the work was done, or nearly, and the rest belonged to the children.

But at the ramp he twisted around, one last time, and looked back at the tower — at the dishes turning slowly against the stars, pouring the vault's long-held testimony into the sky — and at Paloma standing in the doorway with the dreaming children gathered around her like a garden coming up through concrete.

"Hold the line," he called to her. "Whatever happens — hold the line."

And from the med-bay across the colony, from nineteen beds and one perimeter fence, from every screen and speaker on Meridian, the dream answered him.
