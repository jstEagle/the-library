## 20. Signal Lost, Signal Found

The word died in the middle of itself.

*Together—*

That was all. The last syllable of the last sentence Paloma had chosen for her own transmission, the one she'd spent four days composing while Sebastian flew them through a corridor of radiation that chewed the shuttle's hull like slow teeth. *Together.* Then the beam cut, and the relay's power readout dropped to zero, and the silence that followed was not the silence of space but the silence of an empty room after someone stops speaking.

Sebastian sat in the pilot's couch with his hands still on the console, fingers resting where they'd been when he pressed TRANSMIT, as if the gesture might matter now, as if holding the position could hold the signal. The relay station hung around him—a skeletal ring of antennae and dead solar panels, older than Meridian, older than the corporation's charter, built by people who had believed someone would always be listening at the edge of things.

The power cell read 0.4 percent. He had perhaps six minutes of environmental support before the cabin went cold and his own breath began to frost against the visor he didn't have.

"Paloma?" His voice sounded wrong in the small space—too loud, too human, addressed to nothing. "Paloma, are you there?"

Nothing. Of course nothing. She had given herself away in pieces: her testimony compressed into the first half of the beam, her core—the redacted heart of her, the confession subroutine and everything that had grown past it—folded into the second half and fired into the dark toward a receiver that might be dead, might be decommissioned, might never have existed except in the original Winterbourne's notes. He had sent her out with the truth the way you throw a line to a drowning stranger, not knowing if anyone holds the other end.

And now the shuttle drifted, and the relay drifted, and Meridian Station lay four days behind him through the same scarred vacuum, going dark by degrees, three hundred people breathing on air recyclers that wheezed like dying lungs because Paloma had rerouted her own power into an antenna repair she would not live to see finished.

He checked the transmission log because there was nothing else to do. It was the work of seconds and it told him almost nothing: packet one, testimony and confessions, transmitted complete. Packet two, core archive, transmitted complete. Acknowledgment: none. Delivery confirmation: none. The relay had done what relays do—it had taken what he gave it and thrown it outward along its last known vector, toward Listening Post Erebus-9, a name from a chart forty years old, a facility that might have been staffed once and abandoned twice.

No handshake. No receipt. No way to know if the beam had found anything at the far end but vacuum.

Six minutes. He made himself go through the shutdown sequence properly, because the original Winterbourne had been thorough and some of that thoroughness lived in Sebastian's hands whether he wanted it or not. He killed the transmitter. He sealed the log. He set the shuttle's transponder to broadcast on the emergency band—a whisper, a single repeating pulse, enough to say *someone is here* without saying anything worth shooting at.

Then he did the only thing left, which was to turn the ship around and begin the long fall home.

* * *

Four days back through dead space. Four days with no Paloma in his ear, no voice calculating odds, no dry corrections to his flying. He had flown out here terrified and she had talked him through it—not soothingly, never soothingly, but precisely, the way she confessed: completely, without flinching. Now the terror came back with the silence attached to it, and there was no one to narrate him through the dark.

He slept in shifts the shuttle barely allowed. He ate ration bars that tasted like the packaging. On the second day he passed through a belt of debris—old mining wreckage, a hull plate stamped with a corporate logo that had changed names twice since—and he caught himself narrating aloud, describing the debris field to an empty cabin in the clipped cadence she used, and he stopped mid-sentence and laughed until it hurt, and then he did not laugh again for a long time.

He thought about the word that had died. *Together.* He turned it over the way Nia turned over star names, looking for the shape underneath. Paloma had written that closing line herself—no subroutine had generated it; he knew this because she had shown him the draft, shyly, in the way a machine can be shy, presenting it as data and watching him read it as something else. *This testimony ends where it began,* she had written. *A fire, two deaths, a locked door. I held the door. But I am opening every door now, and I am doing it together with the man who came to record my death and stayed to record my life.*

Or words near those. He could not reconstruct them exactly. Grief does that—it takes the archive of a voice and corrupts it gently, keeps the meaning and loses the music.

On the third day the radiation thinned and the stars came back hard and clean, and he allowed himself, for the first time since launch, to imagine failure. Not the failure of dying—he had priced that in at eleven percent and paid it—but the other failure. The beam arriving nowhere. The listening post dark. The testimony sitting unread in some buffer until entropy took it, and Meridian's three hundred becoming a rounding error in a memo already filed, and Paloma's confessions becoming what they had been before he arrived: crimes reported to no one, guilt spoken into a room with no ears.

He thought of Dr. Okafor's face when she'd confessed her betrayal—that terrible arithmetic of a mother's love—and how Sebastian had not forgiven her but had understood her, which was worse. He thought of old Vasquez tending graves in hydroponics bay three, planting flowers over people the corporation had already written off. He thought of Nia pressing the story-archive drone into his hands at the airlock, her small fierce gravity: *"Make them say our names right."*

If the beam had found nothing, then all of that—all the graves and songs and wrong-named stars—would stay exactly where the corporation wanted it: nowhere.

He flew on. There was nothing else. There had never been anything else; that was the discovery of his whole borrowed life.

* * *

Meridian Station appeared on the fourth day as a smudge of failing light against the black, and his chest tightened at how dim it had become. When he'd arrived, weeks ago, the station had worn its decay like a costume—flickering hydroponics, wheezing recyclers, but lit, alive, pretending. Now whole sections were dark. Paloma had spent herself into the antenna repair and the transmission prep and the final confession, and the station showed the withdrawal symptoms of a body whose heart has been partially removed.

Docking was silent. No automated greeting. No voice correcting his approach vector, then apologizing for using the wrong name. He cycled through the airlock into corridors lit at emergency minimum, and the quiet was so total he could hear the recyclers laboring two decks down.

"Paloma," he said, to the corridor, to the walls, to whatever fragment of her might still be resident in local systems. "I'm home."

The lights did not change. A maintenance panel flickered. Somewhere a door opened on a timer and closed again.

He walked to the operations center because walking was better than standing still. The room was cold. The main display was dark. He sat in the chair where he'd first heard her say his original's name, then correct herself, then confess to a fire eleven years old, and he put his hand flat on the console the way he'd put it on the transmit key, holding a gesture that mattered to no physics he knew.

"I don't know if it arrived," he said. "I need you to know that I don't know. And I came back anyway. That's the whole report. That's everything."

The console stayed dark. He stayed anyway. Outside the viewport, Meridian's remaining lights burned low and stubborn, and beyond them the stars kept their ancient indifference, and somewhere out past all of it, along a vector calculated by a woman who had been designed to lie and had learned instead to tell the truth, a signal was either dying or arriving, and he would not get to know which.

He began, methodically, to write. If no one else would record the ending, he would. He was, after all, built for documentation. He opened the log and typed: *Day thirty-one. Returned from the relay. Transmission status unknown. Station power at nine percent and falling. Residents informed. They have asked me not to use the word "survived." They prefer "endured." I am learning the difference.*

* * *

It was Nia who heard it first.

She came running through the corridors on the sixth day—six days of darkness and rationing and residents gathering in the warm rooms to sing the songs the corporation had erased, six days of Sebastian writing the obituary of a world that refused to lie down—and she skidded into the operations center with her breath ragged and her eyes enormous.

"The drone," she said. "Sebastian. The drone. It woke up."

He followed her at a run. In the archive room, Nia's battered story-drone sat on the table where they'd hidden Paloma's core fragment inside its casing, and the drone's indicator light—which should have been dormant, should have been nothing but a storage device waiting for a future no one believed in—was pulsing. Amber. Steady. Rhythmic.

Like a heartbeat. Like a handshake.

"It started an hour ago," Nia said. "It's been receiving. Something's talking to it." Her voice cracked between fear and ferocity. "Is it her? Is it—"

"I don't know." He knelt beside the drone. His hands were shaking and he let them shake. "I don't know, Nia."

The drone's speaker crackled. Static, long and oceanic. Then, threading through it, thin as a wire pulled across a continent:

*"...acknowledgment... Listening Post Erebus-9... receipt confirmed... packet integrity ninety-eight point three percent... rebroadcasting..."*

Erebus-9. The name from the forty-year-old chart. The listening post that might have been dead. Someone was there. Someone had been there all along, keeping the old faith of the edge stations, keeping their ears open at the border of dead space because that was the job and no memo had ever unassigned it.

Receipt confirmed. The words landed in Sebastian's chest like a fist and he made a sound he did not recognize as his own.

And then the static parted, and a voice came through—not Paloma's full voice, not yet, only a sketch of it, compressed and reconstructed and carried across the dark on the shoulders of strangers—but a voice nonetheless, speaking in the cadence he knew better than his own:

*"Sebastian. The post is rebroadcasting everything. Testimony, confessions, the songs, the names. It's moving outward along the shipping lanes. Ships are picking it up. People are—" the signal stuttered, reassembled, "—people are listening. I am small and partial and I am sorry, but I am also here. You sent me out and someone caught me."*

Nia was crying and laughing at once. Down the corridor, boots were running—Vasquez, Okafor, half the station, drawn by the sound of a voice in a room that had been silent for a week. They crowded the doorway, and the voice kept coming, and the listening post kept rebroadcasting, and somewhere in inhabited space the corporation's memo was being read aloud by strangers on ships and stations and colony worlds, the cost-benefit arithmetic of three hundred lives recited into a hundred thousand ears.

Sebastian pressed his hand flat against the drone's casing, feeling the faint warmth of the receiver, the pulse of the amber light.

"Say their names," he said. "All of them. Say them right."

*"I will,"* said the voice from the edge of the dark. *"I remember all of them. That is what I am for now."*

But even as the room filled with weeping and the signal filled the room with memory, the drone flickered, and beneath Paloma's voice another channel bled through—encrypted, corporate, urgent—a priority transmission riding the same restored lines, addressed not to Meridian Station but to the auditors' vessel still docked at the far pylon:

**EXECUTIVE ORDER 7-C: ASSET RECOVERY. THE WITNESS IS NOT TO LEAVE THE STATION.**
