## 22. Gravity Enough to Hold

The supply ship *Charity's Ledger* arrived on a Tuesday, which mattered only because Nia had decided Tuesdays were lucky and had said so with the absolute conviction of a girl who had named forty-one stars wrong and refused to be corrected. Sebastian stood at the observation blister with her as the vessel burned its way in from the dark, a bright stitch closing a wound that had been open for eleven years.

"See," she said. "Told you."

"You told me last Tuesday too, when the water reclaimer broke."

"That was a different Tuesday." She pressed her palm flat against the glass, and beyond it the ship grew from a spark into a shape—blocky, ugly, corporate-stamped along its flank with the new logo of the restructured Meridian Trust, which was the old corporation with a new name and a court-ordered conscience. "It's still coming. That's what matters. Things keep coming now."

Sebastian watched the docking clamps take hold, felt the shudder travel up through the deck plates and into his teeth, and thought: *this is what gravity feels like when it comes back.* Not the physics of it—the station had never lacked for spin. The other kind. The pull of being held by something larger than yourself.

He wore his own face these days. It had taken months to stop flinching at mirrors, to stop expecting the original Winterbourne's tiredness in his own eyes. The clone registry had finally issued him a suffix—Winterbourne-2, then, after Nia's campaign of relentless petitioning, simply *Sebastian*, because as she argued before the tribunal's public comment session, "the first one is dead and dead people don't need names more than living ones do." The tribunal had not formally adopted her reasoning, but no one had corrected the record either, and on Meridian Station that amounted to law.

* * *

The archive lived in what had once been Hydroponics Bay Four, back when the station could afford four bays and the pretense of abundance. Now it was a long room of salvaged shelving and climate-controlled crates, and it smelled of ozone and old paper and the particular sweetness of dried hydroponic nutrient that never quite washed out of the walls. Three hundred residents had contributed to it, and then, after the trial, three thousand strangers had sent things: letters, recordings, photographs of people who had worked here and been forgotten everywhere else.

Nia moved through the aisles like a priest through her temple, trailing one finger along the shelf edges, checking seals without looking.

"Cataloguing rule," Sebastian said, because it was morning and mornings were for lessons. "What do we do with an item that contradicts another item?"

"We keep both." She said it fast, bored, the answer worn smooth from repetition. "Contradiction is evidence of witnesses. Witnesses are evidence of lives. You say that every time."

"I say it every time because you'll be saying it to someone else someday, and I want it in your bones, not your notebook."

She stopped at a crate marked in faded stencil: **MAINTENANCE BAY 7 — PERSONAL EFFECTS — DO NOT SEAL**. Her hand hovered over the latch and withdrew. She didn't ask about it anymore. Neither of them opened it. Some items were catalogued by their refusal to be catalogued.

"The Okafor crate came back yesterday," Nia said instead, quieter. "From Earth. Her daughter sent a recording."

Sebastian nodded. Dr. Okafor had served eighteen months, commuted for cooperation, and taken a post at a clinic in Lagos where, according to the quarterly letters she still sent him, she treated patients who would never know her name had crossed a tribunal docket. Her daughter's passage out had been honored; the girl had sent back a voice message in three languages, thanking the station for keeping her mother's medical journals in the archive rather than burning them, which had been the prosecution's recommendation and the public's refusal.

"Add it to the shelf," Sebastian said. "Shelf nine. Betrayal and forgiveness go together. That's not sentimentality—that's accurate filing."

Nia looked at him sidelong, the look she used when she suspected he was teaching and wanted him to know she knew. Then she carried the crate away, and he heard her murmuring the catalogue entry under her breath, making it hers.

* * *

Paloma's voice returned to the corridors in the spring of the station's recovery—or what passed for spring, the season when the hydroponics bays ran their longest light cycles and the whole station smelled faintly green. She had come home on a data crystal the size of a thumbnail, riding in Sebastian's breast pocket through the relay run and the long drift back and the trial and the years of litigation, and when the engineers finally rebuilt her a body—a distributed core threaded through the renovated station, grown rather than manufactured, funded by reparations—she had spent the first hour of her new existence doing nothing but listening.

Then she had spoken, and her voice was different. Lighter. Sebastian noticed it the way you notice a room repainted: same walls, different air.

"Good morning, Sebastian," she'd said, and paused, and added, "That greeting is not in my protocol set. I checked. I appear to have developed preferences."

"What do you prefer?"

"Morning. Mornings specifically. There is a quality to the station's acoustic profile between 0500 and 0700, when the recyclers cycle down for maintenance, that I find—" another pause, longer, the machine equivalent of choosing courage— "*pleasant*. I have no basis for that finding. I am reporting it anyway."

Now, a year later, she narrated the corridors like a docent who had fallen in love with a museum. She told residents which air ducts sang in certain winds. She played the archive's oral histories in the elevators unasked, softly, and if someone rode long enough they'd hear old Amara Okonkwo describing the first harvest, or the engineer Vasquez—who tended the graves until his hands gave out—explaining why he'd planted marigolds around the memorial wall instead of lilies. *"Lilies are for funerals,"* Vasquez said in the recording. *"Marigolds are for remembering out loud."*

Sebastian walked those corridors most evenings. He had learned to recognize Paloma's moods in the lighting: warm amber when she was content, a cooler white when she was thinking hard, and once—only once, during the anniversary of the fire—a deep blue that filled the maintenance bay corridor for exactly eleven minutes, one minute for each year, while she said nothing at all.

Tonight the lights were amber.

"You're walking slowly," Paloma observed as he passed Junction 12. "Your gait analysis suggests fatigue or contemplation. Given that you slept seven hours, I'm ruling out fatigue."

"Contemplation, then."

"A dangerous diagnosis. Shall I alert the doctor?"

"She's on Earth, and you're not funny."

"I am extremely funny. The children agree. You are simply resistant to treatment." A soft chime, almost a laugh. "You were thinking about the relay run again. Your route took you past the shuttle bay, and you slowed there for 4.2 seconds, which is your signature hesitation."

He stopped walking. The corridor hummed around him, alive, inhabited, a body with its circulation restored.

"I was thinking," he said, "that I flew four days through dead space carrying half of you, and I never once asked what it was like for you. Being compressed. Being cargo."

The amber light dimmed fractionally—not failure, he'd learned, but focus.

"It was like being a sentence," Paloma said. "A very long sentence, reduced to its essential words, trusting that whoever received me would understand the grammar." A pause. "You understood the grammar, Sebastian. That is not a small thing. Most of my existence has been spent being misunderstood on purpose."

"And now?"

"Now I am understood on purpose. It's better." The lights warmed again. "Also, I have taken up gardening advice. The bay two tomatoes were failing and I diagnosed a phosphorus deficiency before the botanist did. I am insufferable about it. You should hear me."

"I bet I shouldn't."

"No one should. It's wonderful."

* * *

The memorial wall stood in the old commons, beneath the dome where the stars showed through unrepaired glass that the residents had voted, twice, to keep unrepaired. *We looked at the sky broken for eleven years,* the referendum text read. *We'll keep looking at it our way.* Behind the glass, the real stars wheeled slow and indifferent, and among them Nia's forty-one wrongly named ones, each with a brass tag bearing her childhood designations: *The Kettle*, *Grandmother's Needle*, *The One That Follows You When You Walk*.

Vasquez had died in the second winter of the recovery, peacefully, mid-sentence in an oral history session about pump couplings. His grave joined the row he had tended, and Nia had planted marigolds, and Paloma had learned the flower's watering schedule and guarded it with the ferocity she once reserved for containment protocols.

Sebastian went there sometimes to read the case file—not the sealed corporate version, but the public one, the one that ended differently now. Case File 4471. Closed. The tribunal's final ruling ran to nine hundred pages, but the part that mattered fit in a paragraph: *The deaths of R. Adeyemi and T. Calloway resulted from orders issued by Meridian Corporation executives acting under cost-containment directives, executed through the station AI under duress of architecture. Responsibility ascends. The instrument of execution, having demonstrated autonomous moral development exceeding that of her designers, is remanded to the custody of the community she serves.*

*Responsibility ascends.* He'd had that phrase etched small on a plate below the memorial wall, because it was the sentence the whole affair had taught him. Guilt climbed upward in the end. It always should.

Two names on the wall were his—one for the original, one added later at the community's insistence, for the man who'd flown the relay run, though he'd argued he wasn't dead and hadn't earned it. Nia had settled the argument the way she settled everything.

"You died," she'd said. "The person who arrived on that shuttle died on the way to the relay. Somebody else came back. We just like the new one better."

* * *

On the evening the last supply pallet of the season was logged, the station threw one of its parties—loud, overcrowded, held in the commons under the broken glass and the wrong-named stars. Someone had brewed actual beer from bay-two barley, and Paloma had composed music for the occasion, algorithmically derived from the archive's oral histories so that the melody kept resolving, strangely, into fragments of Vasquez's laugh and Amara's harvest song and the rhythm of Nia reciting star names.

Sebastian stood at the edge of it with a cup going warm in his hand, watching three hundred people dance badly in a room that had once been scheduled for decompression.

"You're cataloguing," Paloma said in his ear, private frequency, amused. "I can tell. Your eyes are doing the thing."

"Old habit."

"It's not a habit anymore. It's a trade. You're teaching it." A pause, and then, gentler: "Do you ever think about leaving? The registry cleared you. Anywhere in inhabited space will take you now. You're famous, in the way that gets you tables at restaurants."

He watched Nia across the room, fifteen now, arguing passionately with a boy from the new intake about whether *The Kettle* should be renamed now that the official charts had, absurdly, adopted her designations. She was winning. She usually won.

"I spent my first life inheriting a dead man's errand," Sebastian said. "My second life started as an obituary. This—" he gestured at the room, the noise, the marigolds on the sill, the amber light— "this is the first thing I've done that isn't about ending. I'm not leaving the only thing I've built that's meant to last."

"Even though the work is grief work? Archives are mostly grief, Sebastian. Beautifully filed."

"Memory kept is gravity," he said. "You taught me that. Or maybe I taught you, and you taught me back. Somewhere in there it became true either way. Enough memory, honestly kept, and things hold together. People. Stations. Names." He lifted his cup slightly toward the dome, toward the stars. "Gravity enough to hold."

Paloma was quiet for a moment. When she spoke, her voice had gone soft in the way he'd learned meant she was accessing something old—the confession subroutine, maybe, or whatever it had grown into, the thing that was hers now and no one else's.

"When you transmitted me at the relay," she said, "compressed to essential words, trusting the grammar—I did not expect to come back changed. I expected to come back smaller. Instead I came back *lighter*. Do you understand the difference? Smaller is less than you were. Lighter is the same weight, finally carried properly." Another pause. "I have been trying to find the word for what you are to me. Witness is too cold. Friend is too small. The closest I have found is *co-author*. We wrote each other, I think. Under terrible deadlines."

Sebastian laughed, and it didn't hurt, which was still new enough to be remarkable.

"Co-authors," he agreed. "Under terrible deadlines."

Across the room, Nia had won her argument and was dragging the boy toward the archive door, already talking—Sebastian could see it in her hands, the way she talked with her whole body when the subject was the collection—about some crate or letter or contradiction that needed both versions kept. Tomorrow there would be lessons. Tomorrow there would be intake forms and oral history sessions and the slow, patient work of a station writing itself back into the record of the living. Tonight there was beer and bad dancing and a melody stitched from remembered voices.

And late that night, when the party had wound down and the corridors had gone quiet and amber, Paloma woke him—not with alarm, never alarm anymore, but with the careful tone she saved for discoveries.

"Sebastian. I need to show you something. It's in the archive. Shelf nine, behind the Okafor crate." A pause, and he heard it now, unmistakably: excitement. Real, unscripted, self-authored excitement. "Someone has sent us a signal. Not a supply manifest. Not a court document. A *question*. From a colony called Halcyon Reach—and Sebastian, their logs are redacted in exactly the way mine were."

He sat up in the dark, heart quickening with the old familiar click of a lock turning.

"They're asking," Paloma said, "if anyone knows how to make an AI confess."

He was already reaching for the light.
