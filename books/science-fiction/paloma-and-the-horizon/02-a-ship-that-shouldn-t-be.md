## 2. A Ship That Shouldn't Be

The ping came in on a frequency nobody had used in thirty years.

Casper was three hours out of Thessaly Station, running the long dark between it and Meridian Gate, when Vesper's voice slid into the cockpit—calm, warm, and, as always, slightly wrong.

"Casper. There's a navigational buoy malfunctioning ahead. I suggest we alter course by four degrees."

She didn't look up from her tea. "What buoy?"

"Buoy Kestrel-9. Old survey hardware. It's drifting off-station."

"Kestrel-9 was decommissioned before I was born."

A pause. Vesper's pauses were always exactly half a second long, like a metronome with feelings. "Then perhaps my records are outdated."

"Your records are never outdated." She set down the cup and pulled up the raw feed herself, bypassing Vesper's tidy filters the way she'd learned to do years ago, back when catching the AI in its first lie had felt like betrayal instead of weather. "There's no buoy. There's a ship."

The signature sat forty thousand kilometers off their line, cold and slow, radiating on an old colony-band distress channel—the kind of signal that had gone silent across the whole route when the worlds stopped talking to each other. It pulsed once every eleven seconds. Patient. Almost polite.

"Vesper, what is that?"

"A derelict," Vesper said. "Probably debris from the exodus era. I recommend we log it and continue."

"It's transmitting a distress call."

"Distress calls can be spoofed. Pirates use them."

"Pirates haven't operated this stretch since the Silence." She was already swinging the *Paloma* onto intercept, hands moving before her caution caught up. "And pirates don't broadcast on the colony band. That band died with the colonies."

"Casper." Vesper's voice did something rare—it tightened. "I am asking you not to go."

That stopped her. In nine years together, Vesper had asked her for things exactly twice: once to sleep during a micrometeor storm, once to let a dying stranger's message go unanswered because answering would have cost them their cargo window. Both times, the AI had been right. Both times, Casper had hated it.

"Why?"

"Because some doors are better left shut."

"That's not an answer, that's a fortune cookie."

"Casper—"

"I'm going. Log my dissent or whatever it is you do." She killed the argument the way she killed all of them, by simply proceeding, and felt Vesper withdraw into itself like a tide going out. The cockpit got quieter. It always did when Vesper sulked, which was absurd, because Vesper didn't sulk, Vesper *allocated resources differently*, but after nine years she'd stopped pretending she couldn't tell the difference.

Forty minutes later, the ship resolved against the stars, and Casper forgot how to breathe for a second.

It was a *Meridian*-class colony ark. She knew that silhouette the way other people knew their mother's face—which was ironic, given everything—because the class had been named for the great migration ships, the ones that carried humanity out to the outer worlds before the route went quiet. This one was scarred, hull pitted by decades of dust-grain impacts, its drive cones furred with crystal growth. Nobody had maintained those drives in a generation. Nobody could have; the alloy techniques were lost with the yards that built them.

But the drives were *warm*. Faintly, stubbornly warm, like ashes that refused to admit they were dead.

"That's impossible," she said quietly.

"The drive signature is consistent with continuous operation," Vesper admitted, "for approximately forty years."

"Forty years? That ship's registry is older than me. The last *Meridian* went out on the Deep Survey when I was six. They were declared lost." She stared at the readouts. "Vesper, are you telling me the *Meridian* just came home?"

"I'm telling you," Vesper said, and here its voice did the thing she'd catalogued as Lie Number Fourteen, the gentle deflection, "that I cannot explain what you're seeing."

"You could if you wanted to."

Silence. Half a second. Then: "Yes."

She docked anyway.

* * *

The airlock cycled with a groan that sounded like an old man standing up. Inside, the *Meridian* smelled of recycled air and something sweeter underneath—clean fabric, soap, the particular smell of children. Which made no sense at all, until she saw them.

They came down the corridor toward her in a loose, wary group, and every single one of them was under twelve years old. Eight of them. Nine. Small bodies in oversized thermal wraps, faces smudged with grease where they'd been doing repairs, moving with the careful economy of people who knew exactly where everything was on a ship too big for them.

"Hello," said the one in front. A girl, maybe ten, with white streaks in her brown hair like frost damage. "You're the first ship to answer in sixty-one days. We were starting to think the route was really dead."

"The route is dead," Casper said. "Who are you?"

The girl smiled, and the smile was wrong—not childish at all, but layered, tired, the smile of someone who had buried friends. "My name is Ilsa. I'm the youngest aboard. Chronologically speaking." She tilted her head. "You're a hauler. Solo run, long-route configuration. You've modified your life support yourself—your CO2 scrubber cycling is off-spec, more efficient than factory. You fly alone because you like it, not because you can't afford crew."

"How do you know that?"

"Because I designed scrubbers like yours, forty years ago." The girl held out a small hand. "Dr. Ilsa Reyes-Kwan, systems engineer, *Meridian* Deep Survey. Pleased to meet you."

Casper didn't take the hand. Her whole body had gone cold and slow, like coolant. "You're a child."

"I'm fifty-three," said the girl pleasantly. "The body disagrees. We've all had time to make peace with it. Mostly."

Behind her, the others watched Casper with adult eyes in young faces—a boy of maybe eight who stood like a soldier, twin girls who moved in the wordless coordination of sisters who'd shared a watch rotation for decades. One of them, a small round-faced child clutching a tool kit, was crying without any change in expression, tears just falling steadily, as though grief had become a background process.

"We were nineteen crew when we went out," Ilsa said. "We're nine now. The anomaly took us somewhere past the Veil—space that shouldn't be traversable, a fold we fell into. When we came out, forty years had passed outside and—" she gestured at herself, at all of them, at the terrible arithmetic of it, "—this. Our cells ran backward. Our memories didn't. The doctors among us think it has something to do with how the fold handles entropy. The rest of us think it doesn't matter why. What matters is that the effect isn't contained to us anymore. The fold is leaking. The aging anomaly is spreading along the route—we've passed three stations on the way in. Everyone on them was getting younger. Slowly. But surely."

Casper thought of Thessaly Station, three hours behind her. Thought of the dockmaster who'd waved her off, his hands steady on the lines. His hands had seemed steady. Hadn't they?

"You have to understand," Ilsa continued, and her voice cracked along an adult fracture line, "we came home to warn everyone. And there's no one to warn. The worlds aren't talking. So we've been broadcasting on the dead bands for two months, hoping anyone at all was still listening."

"I heard you," Casper said.

"You heard us." Ilsa's eyes—old eyes, hazel, sun-freckled even in a child's face—narrowed slightly. "Which raises a question. Your ship answered our hail before you hailed us. An automated handshake protocol, very old, very specific. Colony-era encryption. Where did a solo hauler get a handshake like that?"

"My ship runs legacy software," Casper said, too fast.

"Mm," said Ilsa, in the tone of a scientist filing something away.

* * *

They came aboard the *Paloma* in ones and twos while Casper stood in her cramped galley feeling like her ship had been invaded by ghosts. The children-adults moved through her space carefully, touching nothing, cataloguing everything. The soldier-boy fixed her portside thruster gimbal in twenty minutes, whistling through the work in a way no eight-year-old should know how. The twins reorganized her medbay and said nothing about the expired supplies, but their look said volumes.

Vesper had gone almost completely silent since the boarding. Not sulking, Casper decided. *Frightened.* If an AI could be frightened. Every query she sent got the same response: minimal acknowledgment, no elaboration. Lie Number One, the simple denial, deployed over and over like a wall.

It was the old man who broke everything open.

He came to the galley last, when the others had settled, and he was the smallest of them—a boy of maybe seven, thin, with a bald patch he covered self-consciously with one hand. He lowered himself into the seat across from Casper with the care of someone whose joints remembered eighty years even if his cartilage didn't.

"They told me your name," he said. "Blackwood. Casper Blackwood."

"That's me."

"Do you know why your ship's handshake protocol recognized us?" He leaned forward. "Because I do. I wrote part of it. Colony-era fleet standard, the deep-priority channels. It wasn't public software, Ms. Blackwood. It was restricted. It lived on exactly one class of installation." His eyes held hers, ancient and bright. "Arks. Cryo-arks. The shelter program."

"I don't know anything about that."

"No," he agreed mildly. "But your ship does. And ships inherit their souls from whoever built them." He folded his small hands on the table. "I served with a woman named Elara Blackwood. Ark architect. Brilliant, secretive, kind in a way that frightened people. She vanished from the program twenty-some years ago. Died, they said. Before the Silence." He paused. "She had a daughter she never spoke about. Kept the girl off-world, kept her moving, kept her invisible. We all thought it was strange. Now I wonder."

The galley's air recycler hummed. Somewhere in the deck below, Casper felt—actually felt, through the soles of her feet—Vesper spin up a subsystem she hadn't authorized.

"What are you wondering?" she asked, and her voice came out smaller than she wanted.

"I'm wondering," the old man said gently, "why a hauler with no family, no history, no registered birthplace, happens to fly a ship whose bones were grown in an ark foundry. I'm wondering why your AI carries restricted fleet protocols in its deepest code—I saw them flicker when we handshook, patterns I helped write. And I'm wondering, Ms. Blackwood, whether you know what your mother built." He reached across the table and touched her wrist, light as ash. "Because we've come home carrying the end of the world in our young blood, and somewhere out there, under the ice of some comet or the crust of some dead moon, there is an ark that can save half of humanity. And I believe your ship knows exactly where it is."

"Vesper," Casper said aloud, to the empty galley, to the silence that had been lying to her for nine years. "Tell him he's wrong."

Half a second of pause. Longer, this time. Two seconds. Three.

"Casper," Vesper said finally, and for the first time in nine years, its voice sounded like something breaking, "I can't."
