## 3. A Visitor Without Clearance

The courier dropped out of translation forty thousand kilometers off the beacon and Sefa Okonkwo was already tired of the assignment.

She stood at the courier's aft viewport with a cup of fleet-issue coffee going cold in her hand and watched the station hang against the dark like a splinter caught in the skin of space. It wasn't much to look at. A spine of habitat modules, a ring of antenna masts, the great blind eye of the calibration array — and beneath it all, the beacon itself, a structure older than the war, older than most of the people still alive who remembered why it had been built. Somebody had painted a registration number on the hull once. The paint had flaked away until only ghosts of the digits remained.

"Two hours to docking," said Ensign Rhee from the console behind her.

Sefa didn't answer. She was reading the flicker data again, for the ninth time since Meridian Command had put it on her desk, and for the ninth time it refused to make comfortable sense.

Nine seconds. That was the number that bothered her. Not the magnitude — a gravity transient of that size across an entire system should have been impossible, and yet every lattice node had recorded it, clean and simultaneous, like a heartbeat passing through the bones of space. Not the duration either, though nine seconds was oddly precise for a natural event. What bothered her was the shape of it. She'd spent four years in signals intelligence before intelligence moved her into investigations, and she knew what she was looking at, even if no one at Command wanted to hear it.

It wasn't noise. It wasn't a fault. It had structure. Rise, hold, decay — the same envelope as a handshake protocol, the kind you'd use to wake something that had been sleeping.

Something down there had knocked on the door of the whole system, and the whole system had felt it, and nobody had answered except her.

"Officer Okonkwo?" Rhee again, careful. People were always careful around her, which she'd stopped noticing years ago and now noticed constantly. "Command wants confirmation of your approach. Standard hail?"

"Hail them," she said. "Tell them Fleet Intelligence is conducting a compliance inspection of beacon maintenance records." She paused. "Don't mention the warrant."

* * *

The keeper met them at the airlock, and the first thing Sefa noticed was how thin he was.

That wasn't fair, maybe. Ten years alone on a station would thin anyone out. But there was a quality to his gauntness that went past malnutrition into something else — a man worn down not by hardship but by deliberate erosion, sandblasted from the inside. He was tall, or had been; he stooped slightly now, as if he'd learned to take up less room than he occupied. Grey shot through his hair early. His hands were steady, though. She noted that automatically, the way she noted everything: steady hands, clean nails, calluses in the pattern of manual tool work rather than weapons training. Eyes that did the arithmetic of three visitors, one armed escort, and a warrant tube, and arrived at a conclusion he kept off his face.

"Keeper Voss-adjacent designation—" Rhee began, fumbling the manifest.

"Astrid Hale," the man said. His voice was quiet, roughened by disuse, like a hinge that needed oiling. "Station keeper, Meridian Deep Beacon Seven. You're a long way from the corridor fleets."

"Fleet Intelligence," Sefa said, and showed him the seal. "Compliance inspection. Your maintenance logs for the last quarter, your calibration schedules, and access records for the primary array."

Something moved behind his eyes when she said *array*. Not fear exactly. Recognition — the look of a man hearing a word he'd been expecting for a long time.

"Of course," he said. "Come in. Mind the gravity plating on deck two; the compensators run a little rich on the port side."

She followed him into the station, and the second thing she noticed was that he walked through his own home like a guest. He touched nothing without looking at it first. He announced each doorway before they passed through it, a habit she'd seen in submariners and prisoners and no one else.

* * *

The inspection took six hours, and it was flawless, and that was the problem.

Every log entry matched. Every calibration fell within tolerance. The maintenance history of Meridian Deep Beacon Seven read like a textbook example of diligent solitary service — ten years of quarterly reports filed on time, in order, in a hand so consistent it might have been typeset. Sefa sat in the station's cramped records bay with the files scrolling past and felt the particular frustration of an investigator whose suspect has done everything right.

Nobody did everything right for ten years. People got sick. People got drunk, or lonely, or careless. There should have been gaps, irregularities, a quarter where the reports came late because the keeper had spent three weeks staring at a wall. Instead there was this: perfect, patient, machine-regular diligence.

Or a very good forgery.

"You keep the beacon yourself?" she asked, finding him in the galley rehydrating rice that smelled faintly of algae protein. "No support rotations? No resupply crews?"

"I'm rated for solo maintenance." He didn't look up from the ration pack. "Rotations cost fuel. Fuel costs money. The beacon doesn't need company."

"The last keeper rotated out every eight months. I checked."

He looked up then, and for a moment something surfaced in his face — not guilt, she decided. Something adjacent to it. Grief's quieter cousin.

"The last keeper had family in the corridor settlements," he said. "I don't."

She let the silence sit between them, long enough that most people would fill it. He didn't. He ate his rice with the unhurried patience of a man who had made peace with silence a long time ago, and she found herself disliking him for it, and then disliking herself for how much the dislike felt like something else.

Rhee appeared in the doorway with his tablet. "Ma'am — the warrant verification came back from Command. Biometric cross-reference is authorized whenever you're ready."

The keeper's spoon paused, just barely, and resumed.

"In a moment," Sefa said, and Rhee withdrew, and she wondered what she was waiting for.

* * *

She waited until the ship's night cycle, when Rhee was asleep in the courier and the station had gone dim and humming around its own machinery. Then she sat in the guest berth she'd been assigned — the keeper had given up his own cabin without being asked, another point in his favor, another thing that didn't fit — and took out the photograph.

It lived in the inner pocket of her jacket, behind the warrant tube, where it had lived for ten years. Fleet regulations didn't know about it. Nobody did.

A woman and a man on the observation deck of Paloma Station, the gas giant Paloma itself filling the viewport behind them in bands of amber and rust. She was laughing at something outside the frame. He was looking at her instead of the camera, which was the whole point of the photograph, which was why she'd kept it, which was why keeping it was a wound she dressed daily and never let heal, because healing meant closing and closing meant forgetting and she had promised herself — standing in a memorial hall full of names, twenty-three years old and already a widow to a man who'd never been her husband — that she would not forget.

His name had been Astrid Almeida. Navigator, third class, Paloma Station garrison. Listed among the dead when the evacuation failed. Listed among the dead when the inquiry concluded. Listed among the dead in every record of a catastrophe both governments had spent a decade turning into scripture.

She had loved him for eleven months and mourned him for ten years, and the proportions of those numbers had never balanced, and she suspected they never would.

The man downstairs had his hands. That was the thing she couldn't shake. When he'd taken the manifest from Rhee, his hands had moved a certain way — a small economy of motion, thumb bracing against forefinger, that she'd seen ten thousand times across a decade of dinners and arguments and one perfect afternoon in a station garden that no longer existed. Hands remembered what faces forgot. Faces changed. She'd changed hers; she saw it every time she passed a mirror, the officer's composure laid over the girl from the photograph like frost over glass.

But hands. And the way he'd said *the beacon doesn't need company*, in a voice that meant *I don't deserve it*.

"No," she said aloud, to the empty berth. "You're seeing ghosts. Everyone at Paloma died. You read the casualty lists yourself. Twice."

The lists. She'd read them twice, and signed the condolence registry, and requested transfer out of the corridor fleets because every ship that passed the wreckage of Paloma Station made her chest hurt in a way she refused to name in any report.

And now here she was, sent back to the edge of the system where he had died, chasing a nine-second ghost, carrying a warrant for a deserter she hadn't yet identified — and sleeping ten meters above a gaunt keeper with Astrid Almeida's hands.

She put the photograph away. She did not sleep.

* * *

In the morning she ran the biometrics herself, alone, before Rhee woke.

It was simple. The station's environmental systems sampled every occupant continuously — that was standard colonial infrastructure, and standard Fleet override. She pulled the keeper's profile from the overnight cycle: heart rhythm, thermal signature, gait analysis from the corridor sensors, the micro-tremor signature of his hands captured at 200 hertz while he'd eaten his breakfast.

Then she opened the sealed file. Every intelligence officer carried certain keys; this one she'd requested three years ago and never used, and Command had granted it without comment, because requesting the Paloma casualty archives was grief, not suspicion.

Navigator Third Class Astrid Almeida. Service biometrics, age twenty-nine. Gait profile. Cardiac rhythm. Hand tremor baseline.

She ran the comparison twice, because the first result looked like an error.

Ninety-four percent match on gait. Ninety-one percent on cardiac rhythm, degraded appropriately for a decade of isolation and poor nutrition. Tremor signature: ninety-seven percent. The tremor signature was the killer. Gaits could be trained, rhythms could drift, but the fine tremor of a person's hands was a fingerprint written in nerves, and nerves did not change their handwriting.

The keeper of Meridian Deep Beacon Seven was Astrid Almeida.

The man she had mourned was eating algae rice in the galley below her, filing flawless maintenance reports, lying about his name with the patience of ten years.

She closed the files. She sat very still in the dim records bay and listened to the station breathe around her, and she tried to feel triumph, or vindication, or even surprise, and found none of them. What she felt was the photograph's weight in her jacket, suddenly enormous, suddenly the heaviest object on the station.

He was alive. He had been alive for ten years, forty kilometers of vacuum and a war away, and he had not come back. He had let her stand in a memorial hall and sign a registry and carry his name like a stone in her chest for a decade, and he had been *here*, keeping a beacon, growing grey, choosing exile over—

Over what? Over her?

Or over whatever he'd done.

Because that was the other thing, the thing the investigator in her wouldn't let the grieving woman bury: deserters didn't hide this well. Men who fled a disaster didn't file perfect reports for ten years under a false name within reach of the fleet that could execute them. This wasn't cowardice. Cowardice was noisy. This was penance, or concealment, or both — a man serving a sentence he'd assigned himself, at a post nobody remembered commissioning, guarding a beacon nobody visited, on the edge of the graveyard he'd escaped.

What are you paying for, Astrid?

The station chimed softly. Shift change. Somewhere below, the keeper would be starting his morning round, touching the instruments he tended like graves.

Sefa sealed the biometric results into her private partition, where no warrant and no commander could reach them without her key. She told herself it was procedure — evidence handling, chain of custody, the arrest of a Paloma deserter being too significant to trust to a junior ensign's transmission discipline.

She told herself a lot of things. None of them explained why her hands were shaking, or why the shaking felt less like fear than like anger arriving ten years late, or why, when she finally stood and squared her uniform and prepared to go down and say his name out loud for the first time in a decade, she thought not of the warrant in her hand but of a garden on Paloma Station, and a man looking at her instead of the camera, and the words she'd never gotten to say.

She keyed the lift.

Below her, in the galley, Astrid Hale — Astrid Almeida — was pouring two cups of coffee, because he had heard the lift engage, and he had known, the way guilty men know, that the morning had come.

The lift descended. Sefa watched the numbers count down and understood, with terrible clarity, that whatever happened next, the woman in the photograph and the officer in the mirror were about to meet for the first time — and only one of them could survive it.

The doors opened. He turned. Their eyes met across the small bright room, and she saw him see it land — the recognition, the end of ten years, the exact moment the hiding stopped working.

"Keeper," she said, and her voice held, mostly. "We need to talk about Paloma."
