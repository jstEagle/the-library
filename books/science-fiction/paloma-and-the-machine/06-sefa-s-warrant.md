## 6. Sefa's Warrant

The warrant arrived at 0400 station time, which was how Sefa Okonkwo knew it was dirty. Clean orders came during the day shift, with briefings and witnesses and paper trails. Dirty orders came in the dark, addressed to her rank instead of her name, encrypted twice, and signed by a staff judge advocate she'd never met.

*By authority of Fleet Intelligence Directive 11-C, you are instructed to detain and hold for transfer the individual designated KEEPER-7734, Meridian Deep Beacon, on suspicion of desertion under wartime statute 9-A, origin: Paloma Station evacuation.*

She read it three times. The words didn't change. Desertion. Paloma. She set the slate down on the courier's fold-out desk and looked at it the way she'd learned to look at ordnance — from an angle, without breathing.

Ten years. Ten years since anyone had used that word in her presence, and they'd all been careful about it, because Sefa Okonkwo had lost a navigator at Paloma and everyone in Fleet Intelligence knew that grief made officers either soft or sharp, and nobody wanted to find out which way she'd gone.

She'd gone both ways, if they'd asked. Soft where he was concerned. Sharp everywhere else.

The keeper. She thought of him standing in the beacon's maintenance bay with his hands folded like a man waiting for a verdict he'd already heard. Gaunt. Gray at the temples, though he couldn't have been more than forty. A voice like gravel poured over something smoother underneath. He hadn't flinched when she showed the warrant for the flicker investigation. He hadn't flinched at anything.

*Desertion under wartime statute 9-A.* There were four hundred eleven names on the Paloma deserter rolls. She knew because she'd pulled them once, three years into the war, drunk enough to do it and sober enough to remember every line. His name had been on it. *Almeida, Astrid. Lt. Cmdr., Navigation. Status: unaccounted, presumed deceased, Paloma Station incident.*

Presumed deceased. She'd mourned a dead man for a decade, and now Fleet Intelligence — someone in Fleet Intelligence — suspected the gaunt keeper of a rock at the edge of nowhere was him, and their first instinct was to send the one officer in the system who had loved him.

That wasn't procedure. That was cruelty dressed as efficiency, or worse: someone testing her.

She keyed the comm panel. "Bridge, this is Okonkwo. Alter course back to Meridian Deep. I'm executing a detention order."

Her voice came out level. She was almost proud of it.

* * *

The flight back took nineteen hours, and she spent fourteen of them in the courier's archive chair with a biometric cross-reference running against the keeper's intake scan — the one she'd taken herself, standard procedure, thumbprint and retinal and the subdermal lattice pattern that no amount of exile could rewrite.

She told herself it was diligence. A good intelligence officer verified before she arrested. If the man was innocent, she needed to know before she put restraints on him. If he was guilty, she needed the file airtight, because Paloma cases got reviewed by three separate boards and any one of them could smell a sloppy warrant from a system away.

It was diligence. It was also the longest fourteen hours of her life, watching a progress bar crawl while the courier fell through the dark toward the beacon, and she sat with her hands flat on her knees like a woman in a lifeboat keeping herself from reaching over the side.

The lattice match came in at hour nine.

She was eating a ration bar she didn't taste. The screen chimed, polite as a doorbell, and there it was: *Subdermal lattice signature: MATCH, confidence 99.97%. Subject designation: ALMEIDA, ASTRID.*

99.97 percent. The remaining hundredth of a percent was statistical noise, the universe's small courtesy of plausible deniability, and she hated it, because for nine seconds she could pretend there was some other explanation — a cloned lattice, a forged identity, a clerical ghost — and then the retinal comparison finished loading and killed even that.

His eyes looked back at her from a ten-year-old service portrait. Younger face. Whole face, before whatever had hollowed him out. The same eyes she'd last seen across a shuttle bay on Paloma Station, the day the evacuation went wrong, the day he'd turned back toward the core decks and the bulkhead had sealed between them, and she'd spent six years believing he'd died holding a corridor open for strangers, because that was what navigators did, that was what *he* did, and grief needs a shape or it eats you alive.

He hadn't died holding a corridor open. He'd run. And then he'd hidden himself so deep in the dark that not even the war could find him, tending a beacon commissioned under a name nobody remembered — except now she understood why the commissioning records had felt familiar when she'd pulled them yesterday. *Authorized by Office of Navigation Planning.* His old office. His old signature chain. He hadn't just found a hiding place. He'd built it out of his own career, brick by brick, a monument to himself disguised as infrastructure.

Sefa closed the file. Then she opened it again. Then she closed it and sat in the archive chair in the dark of the courier cabin and did not sleep.

* * *

What kept her up wasn't the arrest. The arrest was easy; she'd written it in her head by hour twelve. Approach, inform, detain, transport. Statute 9-A gave her no discretion and she would take none, because discretion was how officers like her became officers like the ones she despised. She would put the cuffs on Astrid Almeida herself, and she would log the time, and she would sit across from him on the long flight back to fleet custody, and neither of them would say what they were to each other, because that word had died at Paloma along with everything else, and corpses don't testify.

What kept her up was the photograph.

It lived in the inner pocket of her go-bag, had lived there for ten years, moving from locker to locker to ship to ship, wrapped in a sleeve of thermal foil like contraband. It was the only picture she had of him that wasn't official. Someone at the station canteen had taken it on a rest day — Astrid mid-laugh, holding a cup of terrible coffee, squinting against the light of the observation deck, wearing the expression of a man who believed the future was a place he was actually going.

She'd shown it to exactly two people in ten years. Once to a grief counselor who'd told her to let it go, and once, accidentally, to a customs clerk on Thessaly who'd asked if the man in the photo was her brother, and she'd said yes, and the lie had fit so well she'd worn it for a week.

Now she took it out and held it under the cabin's dim emergency light and compared it, memory against memory, to the gaunt keeper with his folded hands.

The jaw was the same. The hands — she'd know those hands anywhere, the long navigator's fingers, the burn scar on the left index finger from a training accident he'd lied about being heroic when it was really a stove. The eyes were the same eyes doing different work. The portrait's eyes believed in things. The keeper's eyes had stopped believing in anything except the next calibration cycle, and she recognized that look because she saw it in mirrors, sometimes, on bad nights, before she caught it and put it away.

"You're alive," she said to the empty cabin. Her voice sounded wrong in it. "You son of a bitch. You've been alive this whole time."

The stars outside the viewport didn't answer. Stars never did. That was the thing about the Meridian dark — it held everything you buried and never gave it back until you'd stopped asking.

* * *

At hour sixteen she did the thing she'd been avoiding, which was pull the Paloma incident file itself. Not the public summary — the internal one, the one with the evacuation timeline and the abandonment order and the execution checklist that had been sealed by order of Fleet Command and unsealed only partially, redactions like teeth marks all through it.

She'd read it before. She read it differently now, knowing the man in it was alive, knowing he'd spent ten years alone at the edge of the system doing penance in vacuum.

The timeline said what it always said: reactor cascade, structural failure spreading through the habitat ring, evacuation window collapsing. The abandonment order came down from Fleet Command — Voss's signature, she noticed, Voss's cold clean hand — authorizing withdrawal of rescue assets to protect the transport lanes. The execution checklist followed. Someone had to physically implement it: seal the docking arms, cut the tractor locks, close the corridors.

Someone had to sign the checklist.

The signature block was redacted. It had always been redacted. But above it, in the action log, was a navigation officer's authorization code, and she'd seen that code format a thousand times in ten years of reading fleet traffic, and she knew — had known, probably, since the first year, in the place where knowledge goes when you can't afford it — whose code it was.

She'd chosen not to know. Grief is a kind of choice too. You can grieve a hero or you can grieve a man, and she'd picked the hero because the hero was easier to carry, and now the choice was being taken away from her by a warrant signed in the dark.

Because here was the arithmetic she couldn't escape, sitting in the dark with the file glowing on her knees: if Astrid Almeida was alive, then he hadn't just survived Paloma. He'd *left*. He'd executed the abandonment order with his own hands and then walked away from the wreckage of it into ten years of silence, and every person who'd ever told her *at least he died well* had been consoling her with a story, and the story was a lie, and the liar was the man in the photograph.

And underneath that arithmetic, colder: *why now?* Why did Fleet Intelligence suddenly care about a deserter hiding at a beacon nobody visited? Deserter rolls didn't generate warrants after ten years. Something had changed. The gravity flicker had changed something. Nine seconds of system-wide stutter, logged by a keeper who'd lied about it in his report — she'd read the raw sensor data against his submission, and the discrepancy was small and professional and absolutely deliberate, and she'd thought at the time: *this man is afraid.*

Now she knew what he was afraid of. Not her. Being found.

But by whom, and for what, and why did the flicker matter enough to move Voss's office off its decade of comfortable silence?

She was an intelligence officer. She knew what it meant when the questions started multiplying faster than the answers. It meant the warrant wasn't the operation. The warrant was the *cover* for the operation, and she was the deniable instrument, and somewhere above her pay grade someone wanted Astrid Almeida found — or wanted *her* compromised by finding him — and either way she was already inside the machine, gears turning, whether she cooperated or not.

* * *

Hour nineteen. The beacon's light swelled in the viewport, steady and patient, blinking its idiot faithful rhythm into the dark. Somewhere inside it, a man who'd been dead for ten years was probably calibrating something, or pretending to, or standing at a window watching her courier approach and knowing exactly what it meant.

She laid the warrant on the desk beside the photograph. Two documents, side by side. One of them ordered her to destroy what the other one preserved.

Duty said: arrest him. Duty had carried her through ten years of war, through the interrogations and the surveillance rotations and the slow grinding erosion of everything she'd joined the fleet to protect. Duty was the hull she lived inside. You didn't punch holes in your own hull.

But duty, she thought, watching the beacon blink, had never once asked her what she owed the dead. And the dead — the four hundred eleven names, the crew of Paloma, the version of Astrid Almeida she'd buried with full honors in her heart — the dead deserved better than a story. They deserved the truth, whatever the truth was, even if the truth was that the man she loved had done the unforgivable and then spent a decade trying to become small enough to deserve it.

She could arrest him and never learn it. The warrant would see to that. Transfer, tribunal, sealed records, another redaction where a life used to be. Or she could sit on the knowledge for one more night — one more night, that was all — and walk in tomorrow morning with the cuffs in one hand and the photograph in the other, and watch his face when he saw it, and let his face tell her what the files wouldn't.

It wasn't mercy. She told herself it wasn't mercy. It was interrogation technique: establish rapport, present unexpected stimuli, observe micro-expressions. Every academy cadet knew it. It just happened that the only stimulus in the galaxy capable of breaking Astrid Almeida's composure was her, and the fact that she knew that, and was going to use it, and was grateful for the excuse —

That fact she put away with the photograph, in the inner pocket, against her heart, where she kept everything she couldn't afford.

The courier slid into docking range. The beacon blinked. On her console, the detention order waited for a timestamp, patient as the dark.

"One more night," she said aloud, to no one, to him, to the girl she'd been in a shuttle bay ten years ago watching a bulkhead close.

Then she keyed the log: *Courier Okonkwo arriving Meridian Deep, routine follow-up inspection,* and began composing the message she would send to no one, and did not sleep, and did not reach for the comm, and watched the beacon blink like a heartbeat she'd spent ten years learning to live without — and would spend tonight learning she never had.
