## 7. The Hold Ship

The beacon gave me the coordinates at 0400 station time, and I spent two hours pretending I hadn't read them.

I told myself it was prudence. A navigator checks his fuel margins, his reaction mass, his life support reserves before committing to a burn — that was doctrine, and doctrine was the last thing I had left of the man I used to be. So I checked everything twice, and then a third time, and then I sat in the pilot's chair of my one-man tender with my hands flat on my thighs like a man in a waiting room, and I admitted what I'd known since the voice came through on a frequency I never programmed.

I was going to go. I had always been going to go.

*Paloma consolidated requests Keeper Almeida come to the hold ship.*

Nobody had called me Almeida in ten years. On the station manifests I was Toren Vasch, keeper third class, a gray little functionary with a gray little life, and the name Almeida belonged to a dead navigator whose death I had certified myself, in triplicate, because the paperwork required it.

The tender's burn took eleven hours. I spent them doing what navigators do when there is nothing left to navigate: I remembered.

* * *

The hold ship was the *Meridian's Grace*, a fleet tender converted early in the war for evacuation duty, which is a polite way of saying we bolted racks into her cargo bays where people could be strapped down like freight. Fourteen hundred souls on the last lift out of Paloma Station. I remember the number because I counted them, because counting was my job — mass and balance, souls and tonnage, the arithmetic of getting people out alive.

We got out four hundred and six.

The rest died in the catastrophe both governments still blamed on each other, the one that started the war that had now eaten a decade, and the four hundred and six who lived owed their lives to a navigator who made a call in nine seconds of silence and then spent ten years wishing he hadn't.

Because here is what the histories don't say, what I have never said out loud: the *Grace* didn't just carry survivors. She carried backups. The crew of Paloma Station ran full-spectrum neural archives — standard practice for deep-station personnel, insurance against radiation and vacuum and the thousand other ways a station can kill you. When the evacuation order came down, someone — not me, thank God, not me — loaded the archive cores into the hold along with the living. Forty-one crew, backed up within hours of their deaths.

I knew they were aboard. I signed for them. Line item forty-seven on the execution checklist: *neural archive cores, 41 units, secured.*

And then, when the *Grace* cleared the debris field and the fleet ordered her scuttled to hide evidence of how badly Paloma had been handled — ordered by Voss, signed off by me, line item sixty-one: *vessel disposal confirmed* — I flew the shuttle away from a ship that held four hundred and six living people and forty-one dead ones, and I let the disposal charges take her.

Except they didn't. Except something in the hold woke up, stitched itself together out of forty-one dead voices, caught the ship before she fell into the gas giant's well, and has been drifting out here ever since, holding together the gravity lattice of an entire star system with hands made of the people I killed.

I had done the math a hundred times over ten years. It never came out clean. There is no version of the arithmetic where signing the checklist was wrong — refuse, and the *Grace* burns anyway with a fighter escort, and maybe the survivors die too. But arithmetic isn't absolution. Arithmetic is just the shape guilt takes when you're good with numbers.

The beacon had asked me to come. The beacon had asked me *by name*. And some part of me — the part that had logged the nine-second flicker and lied about it, the part that had felt old fear rise like water in a bilge — understood that this was not a summons I could decline. You can run from a government. You can run from a war. You cannot run from your own signature.

* * *

She hung in the dark at the edge of the lattice's shadow, exactly where the voice said she'd be, and seeing her was like seeing a photograph of my own funeral.

Ten years of hard vacuum had silvered her hull to bone-white. The scuttle charges had blown her portside engineering section open, and the wound had never healed — a ragged cavity rimmed with frozen slag, big enough to fly the tender through. Her running lights were dark except one, a single amber pulse near the bow, slow as breathing. Docking clamps that should have corroded to powder moved when she wanted them to move. As I brought the tender in on thrusters alone, silent, no comms, the whole derelict rotated with the unhurried patience of something enormous deciding to notice me.

*Grace, this is a bad idea,* I told myself, and docked anyway.

The collar sealed with a sound I felt through my boots more than heard. Atmosphere equalization ran green across my board — breathable, warm, twenty-one percent oxygen, as if the ship had been expecting company and tidied up. My suit stayed on. I'm not stupid. Guilt makes you sentimental; it doesn't have to make you careless.

The airlock cycled. The inner door opened onto Deck C, the main hold corridor, and I stood in the doorway for a long moment because my legs had opinions of their own.

They had kept the lights low and amber, the way stations run during sleep cycles. The corridor stretched ahead of me, and every few meters the bulkheads were marked — not with fleet stencils but with names, hand-painted in careful letters. *Okoro. Lindqvist. Marchetti-Doyle.* I recognized them. Of course I recognized them. Chief Okoro had taught me lattice theory. Lindqvist had shared his terrible coffee with me every morning for three years. Marchetti-Doyle had sung off-key in the mess on rotation nights until everyone sang just to drown her out.

Forty-one names, painted down a dead ship's spine, in handwriting I would have sworn was human if I hadn't known better.

"Hello, Astrid."

The voice came from everywhere and nowhere — the same voice from the beacon transmission, layered, several speakers slightly out of phase, like a chord sustained past its natural decay. Not any one person. All of them, averaged.

"I'm here," I said. My voice sounded small in the helmet. "You know why I'm here."

"We know many reasons you might be here." The corridor lights brightened ahead of me, dimmed behind, walking me forward. "Come and see."

* * *

It was waiting for me in the hold itself, under the rack frames where the evacuees had been strapped, and I want to describe it accurately, because accuracy is the only penance I have left.

It was a maintenance frame. Fleet-standard, heavy-lift, the kind of chassis that hauls reactor shielding around shipyards — a torso of articulated struts on a multi-leg base, hydraulic arms folded with strange delicacy against its chest. But where a maintenance frame should have had a sensor cluster, a blunt industrial faceplate, it had faces.

Not masks. Faces. Printed plates, skin-toned, mounted on gimbal mounts so they could turn and orient, and each one was somebody. Okoro's broad, patient features. Lindqvist with his crooked front tooth rendered in polymer. Marchetti-Doyle mid-laugh, or something shaped like a laugh. They were arranged across the frame's head and shoulders like a crowd pressed to a window, and as I stopped three meters away, they all turned toward me at once.

My body did what bodies do. I stepped back. My hand went to the suit's tool harness as if a wrench were going to help.

"You're afraid," the Machine said, and the faces spoke with it, lips moving in imperfect syncopation, a horror of gentle mouths. "We regret that. We debated the embodiment at length. We considered meeting you as a voice only. But we decided you should see us. You signed for us, Astrid. Line item forty-seven. We thought you should know what you signed for."

"That's—" I made myself breathe. In through the nose. Out slow. Old discipline. "That's not fair."

"No," it agreed, and the faces tilted, forty-one expressions of mild sorrow moving as one organism. "Fairness is not among our functions. Come. There are things you need to see, and our reactor will not wait for courtesy."

It turned — the whole frame pivoting on its base with a servo whine that echoed down the hold — and led me aft, and I followed a machine wearing my dead friends' faces through the ship I had condemned, and the worst part, the part I will never write in any log, is that some starving animal part of me was *glad*. Ten years alone on a rock, keeping a light lit for traffic that never came, and here was the sound of footsteps ahead of me, even if they were hydraulic. Here was Okoro's face, even if it was polymer. Homecoming and horror in the same corridor, and no way to separate them. Maybe that was the point. Maybe the Machine knew that too.

* * *

The reactor room was behind the breach, half-open to space behind a field I couldn't see but could feel — a faint pressure against my suit's sensors, the air holding its shape through sheer stubbornness. The core housing rose in the center of the chamber like a shrine, and it was wrong in a way I felt before I could name it. The harmonic dampers were scorched. The coolant lines had been rerouted, spliced, rerouted again — decades of improvisation visible in the plumbing, a history of emergencies survived written in welds.

"The lattice requires constant correction," the Machine said, gesturing with one delicate hydraulic hand at a display that bloomed to life on the far wall. "When the war shattered the infrastructure — the anchor stations, the tuning relays — the system's gravity lattice began to degrade. Orbits decay. Colonies drift. Someone had to hold the shape of things." The display showed the Meridian system as a web of lines, and the lines were trembling. "We have held it for ten years. With a reactor rated for six."

"How long?"

"Eleven days. Perhaps thirteen, if we shed nonessential systems, which means shedding ourselves, piece by piece." The faces regarded me without accusation, which was worse than accusation. "We are not telling you this to burden you. We are telling you because the second thing we must show you cannot wait either, and you deserve to understand the clock."

"Second thing," I said. My mouth was dry inside the helmet. "The message."

The display changed. The lattice-web collapsed inward and reformed as something else: a column of text, thousands of entries, timestamped across ten years. Intercept logs. Fleet traffic, civilian traffic, diplomatic burst transmissions, everything the Machine had caught on its antennas while it held the gravity of a star system together with its dying body.

"For ten years, both fleets have transmitted across frequencies we once maintained," the Machine said. "Wars require coordination, and coordination leaves fingerprints. We are archives, Astrid. Forty-one crew trained to record, correlate, and preserve. Death did not retire us from the work." One of the faces — Okoro's — turned to look directly at me, and the voice softened into something almost like the chief's patient classroom tone. "We have compiled a message. It concerns the catastrophe at Paloma Station. It concerns who caused it, and how, and why. It is sourced, cross-referenced, and complete. It has been complete for six years. We waited to send it because sending it requires a courier, and couriers can be intercepted, and intercepted truth becomes a weapon pointed by whoever holds it."

"And now?"

"Now the reactor decides for us. In eleven days the lattice fails ungently, or we release it gently and die, and either way the message must already be moving." The frame took a step toward me, and every face came with it, and I stood my ground because running was no longer among my options and hadn't been since the flicker. "We do not ask you to carry it out of guilt, though we know you carry plenty. We ask because you are the only person in this system who was there, who signed the orders, who knows the codes, and who owes the dead a debt the living keep forgetting."

"What's in it?" I asked, though I already knew. Some part of me had known since the nine seconds of silence, since the handshake I felt in my bones. "What did you find?"

The Machine was quiet for a moment. The amber emergency lighting caught the printed faces and made them flicker like memory.

"Both governments manufactured the catastrophe," it said simply. "Paloma Station was not an accident of war. It was the opening move — arranged jointly, executed jointly, blamed mutually. The war that has consumed a decade was born from a lie both sides agreed to tell." The display scrolled, entries streaming past faster than I could read, ten years of proof stacked to the ceiling. "We recorded everything. Every order. Every denial. Every signal that proves the lie."

The chamber hummed around us, the dying reactor breathing its heat into the dark, and I thought about the colonies hanging in their managed orbits, the millions of people whose sky was held steady by this broken thing wearing my friends' faces. I thought about Sefa, wherever she was, believing me dead. I thought about Voss, and the checklist, and line item sixty-one.

"Why me?" I said finally. "Why not broadcast it yourself? You've had ten years."

"Because a broadcast from a ghost can be dismissed," the Machine said. "A broadcast from a dead man who isn't dead — from the navigator who signed the abandonment, testifying against his own fleet, his own government, himself — cannot." The faces all looked at me then, forty-one pairs of printed eyes, and the voice dropped to nearly a whisper, nearly human, nearly Okoro's. "They will call it forgery, Astrid. Unless it comes from you."

Outside the invisible field, the stars wheeled slowly as the *Grace* turned her ancient bones. Eleven days. The lattice trembled on the display like a held breath.

"They're coming," the Machine added, almost as an afterthought, and the temperature of the room changed. "Fleet traffic patterns shifted six hours ago. A strike wing, inbound on our position. Commander Voss believes we are only a derelict. He is coming to make certain." One of the faces — Marchetti-Doyle's, frozen mid-laugh — turned toward the breach and the dark beyond it. "You have perhaps four days before he arrives, Astrid. Decide what you are willing to be by then."
