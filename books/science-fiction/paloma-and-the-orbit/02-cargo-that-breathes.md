## 2. Cargo That Breathes

Paloma Delacroix had a rule about impossible things: you catalogued them, you priced them, and you sold them to someone whose curiosity outweighed their sense of self-preservation. Impossible things were just inventory that hadn't found its market yet.

So when the signal from the dead frequency repeated for the ninth time — same cadence, same strange liquid syllables folded into the carrier wave like a voice drowned in honey — Paloma did what any professional would do. They made tea.

Not because the situation called for tea. Because their hands needed something to do that wasn't shaking.

"Right," they told the empty cabin of the *Marrow's Grace*, wrapping both palms around the mug. "You're a smuggling signal. Somebody's moving contraband memories on a dead band, and you're sloppy enough to repeat. That's all. That is *all*."

The signal repeated a tenth time, patient as tide.

Paloma set the tea down untouched and pulled up the decode rig — a salvaged customs-grade analyzer they'd named Gertrude, because naming your illegal equipment after somebody's grandmother made it feel less like a felony and more like family. Gertrude hummed, chewed the signal, and spat out her first verdict in cheerful amber text:

**ENCRYPTION: NONE.**

"No encryption?" Paloma leaned closer. "Who broadcasts unencrypted on a dead frequency? That's not smuggling, that's *shouting*." Unless, some small cold voice suggested, there had been no one left to encrypt it *for*. Unless the sender hadn't been hiding at all, but calling — the way you call into a burning house knowing full well who's inside.

They pushed the thought away and got to work.

* * *

The structure emerged over the next six hours, and it was wrong in ways that made the back of Paloma's neck prickle.

Contraband memory came in standard formats: compressed, chunked, watermarked by whichever syndicate had cut it, wrapped in enough legal deniability to survive a customs scan. This was none of those things. This was one continuous stream, uncompressed, uncut — a single take. Nobody recorded memories in a single take. Memory-hauling was an industry of edits; even the honest grief-sellers trimmed the boring parts.

This recording had no boring parts. It also had no beginning and no end that Gertrude could find. It simply looped, two hundred and eleven seconds of raw sensory data, timestamped in a calendar format Paloma didn't recognize.

"That's not possible," Paloma said conversationally, to Gertrude, to the tea, to God if She was listening. "Timestamps don't come in formats nobody uses anymore. Timestamps are like — I don't know — *spines*. Everything has one."

Gertrude offered no comfort. She never did. Best listener in the system, worst conversationalist.

Paloma fed the format into the cross-reference banks, the ones Mirei maintained off-grid for exactly this kind of headache. The answer took eleven minutes to compile, which meant it had to be dredged up from deep archive strata, the sediment layers of the net where information went to be forgotten politely rather than deleted rudely.

**CALENDAR SYSTEM: VESSARI RECKONING. STATUS: OBSOLETE (c. 200 YRS). SEE: VESSARI.**

The terminal paused there, which terminals did not do. Then:

**SEE: VESSARI — NO ENTRY FOUND.**

Paloma stared at the screen. Read it again. The words didn't rearrange themselves into anything sensible, so they said them out loud instead, slowly, like tasting something suspect.

"There's no entry." A calendar format survived two centuries of drift in the archives, but the civilization that used it didn't rate a stub? Not a disambiguation page, not a "see also," not even one of those grim little notices — *this subject has been removed pursuant to* whatever statute — nothing. It was as if the archive had been asked about the Vessari and had answered with a perfectly straight face: *who?*

Paloma had spent twelve years hauling other people's pasts through contested orbits. They knew every flavor of erasure. There was the cheap kind — records lost, drives wiped, inconvenient witnesses relocated to places without return fares. And there was the expensive kind. The kind where the hole itself got patched over with smooth, plausible nothing, so seamless you only noticed it by the way light bent around the absence.

This was the expensive kind. This was the *very* expensive kind.

"Okay," Paloma said, and drank the now-cold tea in one go, because courage was mostly caffeine and denial in equal parts. "Okay. So somebody faked a Vessari calendar stamp to make their contraband look old. Vintage adds value. Classic grift. Nothing here but commerce."

It was a good theory. It had the great advantage of being boring. Paloma held onto it the way you hold onto a railing in rough weather, and decoded the payload.

* * *

The first thing that came through was rain.

Not real rain — memory-rain, the kind that falls inside a person, carrying the smell of whatever they loved. Wet stone. Some flowering tree Paloma had never smelled and yet recognized instantly, the way you recognize a face from a photograph taken before you were born. Then voices, dozens of them, layered, ordinary, arguing cheerfully about something — the audio resolved just enough to catch a word repeated like a refrain, soft and round, *selu, selu* — and then the ordinary day tilted, the way days do, and everything after was running.

Paloma sat very still while the memory played. Professional stillness. The stillness you learn hauling grief-cargo, because clients could tell if you flinched, and flinching devalued the product.

But this wasn't cargo. Cargo had edges. Cargo had been *edited by someone who survived it*. This was a single take. Whoever had recorded this memory had recorded it live, in the middle of it, and had kept recording, and the recording ended —

— it ended mid-breath. A breath drawn in and never let out. Two hundred years old, and the breath was still caught in it, still waiting.

Paloma discovered they had stood up at some point without deciding to. Their legs had opinions their brain hadn't authorized. They sat back down.

"It's a massacre," they said to the cabin. Their voice came out flat, which was worse than shaking. "Somebody massacred these people, and then somebody else massacred them again — out of the record, out of the language, out of the *calendars* — and now the only place they exist is a distress signal repeating on a frequency nobody monitors, addressed to nobody, because everybody it was addressed to is—"

They stopped. Made tea number two. Drank it standing up, staring at the starfield through the port, doing the arithmetic they didn't want to do.

Two hundred years ago. Contested orbits. Meridian sector. Which colonies had gone dark in that era? The histories said *none*, said the expansion had been smooth as poured glass, said the corporate consolidation period had been — Paloma actually laughed, one short ugly note — "*largely peaceful*." Of course it had. History written by archivists on retainer always read like a brochure.

And yet. And *yet*. Somewhere under the smooth glass, a whole people had lived long enough to have their own way of counting time, their own word for whatever *selu* meant, their own rain-smell. And someone had paid enormously — Paloma understood this now with the clarity of a professional appraising another professional's work — someone had paid *enormously* to make sure no one would ever ask what happened to them.

Which raised the question that mattered, the question with teeth: why was the memory *back*? Erasure this thorough didn't leak. You didn't spend a fortune burying a people and then leave the body breathing on an open channel.

Unless you didn't leave it there. Unless someone else dug it up.

Paloma pulled up their client ledger — the real one, the biometric-locked one, the one that existed nowhere but their own head and a drive sewn into the lining of their coat. Twelve years of names, aliases, drop codes, payment trails. If someone had commissioned a recovery of Vessari material, the commission would have passed through the gray market, and the gray market was Paloma's street.

They scrolled. Kestrel Combine shell accounts. A duchess-in-exile who collected extinct lullabies. Three separate buyers who'd wanted pre-Consolidation military footage and gotten, per the invoice, "historical ambiance, unspecified origin." Nothing flagged. Nothing Vessari. Nothing—

Paloma's thumb stopped scrolling.

There, between a job for a Halcyon University ethnomusicologist and a bulk order of fabricated childhoods for a witness-protection broker, was an entry in their own handwriting. Their own cipher. Their own payment code, verified against the biometric hash, dated fourteen months ago.

**CLIENT: PALOMA DELACROIX.**
**CARGO: FRAGMENTS, ORIGIN UNVERIFIED. PARTIAL PAYMENT ACCEPTED. BALANCE OUTSTANDING.**
**NOTE: CLIENT INSISTED ON REMAINING ANONYMOUS. IDENTITY WITHHELD AT CLIENT'S REQUEST.**

Paloma read it four times. The cabin was very quiet. Somewhere in the walls, the *Grace*'s recyclers breathed their slow mechanical breath, in and out, in and out, and Paloma became aware that they were matching it, that they too had stopped mid-inhale, that the breath caught in the recording two hundred years ago had apparently decided to catch in them as well.

"No," they said. "No, no, no. That's a clerical error. That's me paying myself, that's a laundering pass, that's—" But they knew their own bookkeeping the way they knew their own hands, and it wasn't an error. Fourteen months ago, someone had hired Paloma Delacroix to smuggle fragments of the Vessari massacre, and that someone had used Paloma's own codes, own ciphers, own payment rails, and the ledger — honest, incorruptible, sewn into the coat — insisted the client and the courier were the same person.

Which was impossible. Paloma remembered their clients. Remembering clients was survival; forget a client and you forgot who might kill you. They remembered every face, every alias, every handshake and threat—

Except.

Except there were gaps. Everyone had gaps. Twelve years in the trade, and there were nights that went missing, weeks with a texture like static, a scar on their left forearm they'd never earned a story for. They'd always filed those under *the cost of doing business*. Anesthesia after a botched extraction. A bad batch of smuggled wine. The mind was a hold full of cargo, and some of it shifted.

Hadn't it?

Paloma looked down at their own hands — competent, steady, *stranger's* hands — and felt the first true crack run through the morning's comfortable theory that this was all commerce. Because commerce had a shape, and this had none. Someone had erased a people so thoroughly that the archives said *who?* And someone had recovered the memory of it. And somewhere in between, the trail ran directly through Paloma Delacroix, who could not remember hiring themselves, and whose own past had holes in it shaped exactly like questions.

The signal repeated. Eleventh time. Twelfth. Patient as tide, that caught breath cycling forever: *selu, selu,* and then the running, and then the silence where an exhale should have been.

"You're not cargo," Paloma told it quietly. The admission cost something; they felt it leave. "Cargo doesn't breathe."

Outside the port, Meridian Station turned its slow counterfeit daylight across the docking bays, and customs drones stitched their patient patterns through the traffic lanes, and somewhere in that orderly glitter, presumably, were people who had paid to unmake an entire civilization and would very much like to know who was holding its last breath.

Paloma looked at the ledger entry one more time. **CLIENT: PALOMA DELACROIX.** Their own name, sitting there like a stranger in a familiar coat.

"Fine," they said, to the signal, to the ghost in their own bookkeeping, to whoever was listening on the dead frequency. "Then I guess I'm owed an explanation. From myself."

They reached for the comm panel to ping the one person alive who could pull the archive threads — Mirei, black-market archivist, keeper of what the official record wouldn't say — and that was when the docking bay claxon sounded, polite and terrible, announcing an unscheduled inspection.

Customs. Boarding party inbound. Inspector's warrant already logged.

Paloma closed the ledger, sealed the drive against their sternum where it rode like a second heartbeat, and thought, with the peculiar calm of a person watching the noose arrive ahead of schedule: *they're fast. Whoever buried the Vessari, they're very, very fast.*

The airlock began its cycle.
