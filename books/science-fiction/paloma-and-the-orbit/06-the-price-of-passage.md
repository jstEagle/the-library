## 6. The Price of Passage

The problem with needing money fast was that everyone who had it knew you needed money fast.

Paloma sat in the back booth of the Salted Ion, a dive wedged between a coolant recycler and a pawnshop on Meridian Station's lowest ring, watching a cup of tea they couldn't afford go cold. The tea had been a calculated purchase — four credits for the right to occupy a booth indefinitely, plus the illusion of prosperity. Twelve years of smuggling had taught them that half of any con was looking like you didn't need one.

Across the table, a man named Dov Halloway smiled at them with teeth that were too even to be original.

"So," Halloway said. "You want passage through the Kestrel Corridor."

"I want *through* the Kestrel Corridor," Paloma said. "Passage implies someone's taking me. I fly my own ship."

"Of course." Halloway spread his hands, conciliatory as a landing pad. "But nobody flies the Kestrel alone. One in three ships doesn't come out the far side. That's not a statistic, Delacroix. That's a promise."

"Then your employer should be thrilled. Cheap odds."

Halloway laughed, and it was a good laugh, warm and genuine, which was how Paloma knew he'd practiced it. He was corporate liaison class all the way down: tailored jacket over a soft body, eyes that catalogued exits, a voice pitched exactly between friendly and forgettable. His badge said HALCYON DYNAMICS — LOGISTICS DIVISION, which meant either he moved cargo or he made cargo disappear. With Halcyon, historically, it was both.

"And what," Paloma asked, "does Halcyon Logistics need smuggled? You people own half the customs net on this station. You could walk a freighter through wearing a bell."

"What we own is paperwork," Halloway said. "What we don't own is *attention*. There's a shipment — medical isotopes, technically — that needs to arrive at Ceres Gate without appearing to have left Meridian. No manifest gap. No sensor ghost. Nothing anyone can point at later and say, *look, something passed here.*"

"You want me to make your contraband look like it never existed."

"I want you to do what you do best." He leaned forward. "I did my research. You're very good at making things un-happen."

The words landed somewhere under Paloma's ribs and sat there, cold and specific. They thought of the signal still folded in their ship's memory core, wrapped in three layers of encryption, humming like a thing alive. *The Vessari massacre.* A crime so thoroughly erased that two centuries of records showed nothing but clean vacuum where a civilization used to be.

And now a corporation was offering them a fortune to make something else un-happen.

*Coincidence,* said the part of Paloma that wanted the money.

*Sure,* said the part that had survived twelve years by not believing in coincidences.

"The fee," Halloway said, "is forty thousand. Half up front."

Paloma kept their face pleasant and did the math behind it. Fuel for the Kestrel run: six thousand, minimum, because you bought fuel like you bought parachutes — the expensive kind, from people who looked you in the eye. Hull insurance for a route that ate one ship in three: nobody sold that, which was its own kind of math. Shield capacitors, nav-data bribes, a new coolant loop because theirs sounded like a dying accordion. And beyond the corridor, the coordinates the signal pointed toward — a dead zone three weeks past Ceres Gate where no legitimate captain would follow them even for money.

Forty thousand got them there. Thirty-nine thousand short of comfortable, but there.

"Half up front," Paloma said. "And I choose my own route timing."

"Naturally." Halloway produced a slate, thumbed it across the table. "One condition. No passengers, no side cargo, no deviations. My employers are particular about their shipments arriving *alone*."

Something in the phrasing snagged. *Arriving alone.* Not *arriving intact*, not *on schedule*. Alone — as if the danger wasn't interception but company.

"Who's the client?" Paloma asked lightly. "I like to know whose money I'm spending."

"An internal account." Halloway's smile didn't move but somehow became less warm. "You'll find Halcyon pays better than curiosity does."

*There it is,* thought the suspicious part of Paloma, while the practical part signed the contract with a thumbprint that felt, faintly, like a brand.

* * *

Mirei's shop occupied a former confession booth in the decommissioned chapel deck, which she'd chosen, she claimed, because "people already come here to tell me things they shouldn't." She sat behind a counter built from an altar, sorting data-shards into trays with the tenderness other people reserved for kittens.

She looked up when Paloma came in, and her eyebrows did something complicated.

"You signed something," she said. "I can see it on you. You have the posture of a person who just shook hands with a debt."

"Halcyon contract. Forty thousand to move a ghost shipment through the Kestrel."

Mirei set down her tray. "Through the Kestrel. For Halcyon." She repeated it the way a doctor repeats symptoms. "Darling. Sit down before I say something you'll hate."

Paloma sat on a pew that creaked like it remembered sin. "Say it anyway."

"Halcyon Dynamics holds the archival contracts for the Outer Colonies Compact. Every historical record, every census, every colonial registry east of the Belt — stored, maintained, and *corrected* by Halcyon Information Services." She let that sit. "They are not merely a company that might benefit from erasing the Vessari. They are the eraser. It's their product."

The chapel was quiet except for the ventilation, breathing its slow mechanical breath.

"So I'm taking money from the people who—"

"You're taking money from the people who will then own a piece of you," Mirei said. "That's how it works. Nobody buys a smuggler outright. They buy a run. Then another. Then one day you look up and every job you've taken for two years has been theirs, and you can't remember when you started saying yes." She came around the counter and perched beside Paloma on the pew, close enough that her shoulder touched theirs — Mirei's version of an embrace. "I've watched it happen to better liars than you."

"I need the corridor money."

"I know."

"I need to reach those coordinates."

"I know that too." She sighed. "So here's what I can give you instead of a lecture, since you'll ignore the lecture anyway." She pressed a data-shard into Paloma's palm. "Kestrel nav-trends, last eleven years. Every recorded loss, mapped against debris drift patterns. The corridor isn't random, whatever the charts say. It has moods. Learn its habits and you can be the one ship in three that comes home."

"Mirei—"

"Don't thank me. Thank me by coming back and telling me what you find out there." Her voice went quieter. "Because you *will* find something, won't you. That's why you need the corridor at all."

Paloma closed their hand around the shard. "There's a signal," they said. "In Vessari."

Mirei went very still. In the eleven years Paloma had known her, they had never seen her go still. She was a woman who fidgeted the way other people breathed.

"Two hundred years," she said finally, "since anyone heard that language spoken."

"Someone's still broadcasting."

"Then someone's still *alive*," Mirei said, "or something refuses to stop being true. Either way—" she stood, brisk again, armor back on, "—you take the Halcyon money, you run their dirty isotope crate, and you treat every credit of it as stolen. Because it is. You're robbing the grave-robbers. That's the only way this arrangement stays survivable."

"That's very morally convenient."

"I'm a black-market archivist, darling. Moral convenience is my entire business model."

* * *

Loading the Halcyon shipment took four hours and felt like swallowing something that would rather stay whole.

The crate arrived under seal — a matte-black container the size of a coffin, no markings, no serial, cold enough to frost the gloves. Paloma's cargo handler, a secondhand loader arm named Bess who whistled off-key when she worked, paused over it longer than usual.

"You feel that?" Paloma asked her, mostly joking.

Bess didn't answer, because Bess was an arm, but her servos held a hesitation that felt like an opinion.

Paloma strapped the crate into the hold alongside everything else they owned: the memory core with the Vessari signal nested inside it like a pearl in an oyster, spare fuel cells, water reclaimer parts, and a tin of real coffee they'd been saving for an occasion worth being awake for. They told themselves the coffee was for the far side of the corridor. They told themselves a lot of things.

Pre-flight took two hours. The ship — the *Long Answer*, a converted ore-hauler with the aerodynamics of a filing cabinet and the soul of a much better vessel than she had any right to be — grumbled through her diagnostics complaining about the coolant loop, as predicted, and the number-three thruster, as always.

"Fine," Paloma told her. "We're both running on spite. At least yours is factory-installed."

Meridian control cleared them for departure with a bored voice and a standard vector, and Paloma was ninety seconds into the burn when the comm crackled with a channel they hadn't opened.

"Delacroix." Inspector Rhee Okafor's voice, dry as registry paper. "Lovely evening for a departure."

Paloma's stomach performed a maneuver the *Long Answer* would have envied. They keyed the channel. "Inspector. I'm not carrying anything worth your time."

"No one ever is. That's what makes the job interesting." A pause, filled with the sound of Okafor presumably reading something. "Flight plan filed: Ceres Gate, via standard lane. But your fuel load says otherwise, Delacroix. Nobody buys that much jump fuel for a milk run to Ceres."

"My reclaimer's shot. I'm hauling reserve."

"For a milk run."

"Inspector, do you have a question, or do you just miss me?"

Another pause. When Okafor spoke again, the bureaucratic dryness had thinned, just slightly, into something almost human. "The Kestrel Corridor kills one ship in three. Whatever you're doing out there — however much someone paid you to do it — remember that the dead don't spend their fees." Click. Channel closed.

Paloma sat with that for a long moment, hands light on the controls, the stars sliding past the canopy in their ancient indifferent way.

Then they opened the navigation console, loaded Mirei's shard, and watched eleven years of wreckage bloom across the display — every lost ship a small red mark, scattered at first glance, and then, as the overlay deepened, not scattered at all. Clustered. Patterned. The corridor had habits, all right. It killed along lines, like handwriting.

Paloma spent the first jump leg learning the handwriting, and the second leg teaching themselves the shape of the words underneath it, because the signal was still there in the core, patient as sediment, and during the long dark hours between burns there was nothing to do but listen.

By the third day, without quite deciding to, they had begun to learn the language.

It was a strange tongue — verbs that carried their own tense inside them, a word for grief that doubled as a word for weather. Paloma practiced it aloud in the empty cockpit, clumsy and self-conscious, and found that certain phrases landed in their mouth like they'd been there before, waiting. Like keys fitting locks they'd forgotten they owned.

On the fourth day, approaching the corridor's threshold, they caught themselves murmuring a Vessari greeting under their breath as the debris field rose ahead — a silver river of shattered hulls and frozen atmosphere, beautiful the way wounds are sometimes beautiful.

*Welcome home,* the phrase meant. Roughly. Probably.

Paloma's hands went cold on the yoke, and the *Long Answer* dropped into the Kestrel Corridor at full burn, chasing a fortune they'd already begun to regret earning — while somewhere behind them, in the encrypted dark of the memory core, the dead kept talking.
