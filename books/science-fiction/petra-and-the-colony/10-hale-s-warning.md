## 10. Hale's Warning

The notice came on paper.

That was the first thing Astrid noticed, and the first thing that frightened her. Everything on Vesper's Landing moved through the colony net now — ration adjustments, work rosters, the school calendar, even condolences arrived as soft chimes and glowing text. Paper had to be printed, carried, signed, witnessed. Paper was what you used when you wanted a thing to have weight. When you wanted it to be undeniable.

It was waiting for her in the delivery slot of her hab unit when she came back from the morning shift at the water reclamation shed, a single sheet in a grey envelope with the council seal pressed into wax that had cracked in transit. ASTRID NYSTROM, said the header, in type so clean it looked machine-stamped from another century. FORMAL NOTICE OF INQUIRY. She stood in her narrow kitchen holding it under the weak overhead light for a long moment before she opened it, the way you might hold a snake by the tail, deciding where to put it down.

*You are hereby notified,* it began, *that an inquiry has been opened into your access of restricted archival materials on the fourteenth of this month, between the hours of 09:40 and 11:15. Under Founding Statute 12, all requests to consult sealed records must be sponsored by a sitting councilor. No such sponsorship exists.*

The fourteenth. The day she'd sat in the archive annex with the founders' version of history open on her slate, comparing its smooth, unblemished dates against the numbers in her grandmother's maintenance log. She hadn't touched anything sealed. She hadn't needed to — the official record was public, gloriously, insultingly public, and it was precisely because it was public that its lies were legible. But someone had logged her terminal session. Someone had flagged it. And someone had decided that reading the wrong pages carefully enough constituted a crime.

The notice ran to three paragraphs. The last one was the shortest, and the worst:

*You are advised that continued unauthorized access may result in revocation of residency privileges. This inquiry is procedural and should not be cause for alarm. Councilor Maren Hale has been assigned as your liaison and will contact you at her convenience.*

*Should not be cause for alarm.* Astrid read the sentence twice. It was the politest threat she had ever received, and it had been written by someone who understood exactly how polite threats worked — how they let the recipient do all the frightening themselves, alone, at two in the morning, turning the words over until they gleamed with imagined meanings.

She made tea she didn't drink. Outside her window, Vesper's Landing went about its evening: the sodium lamps flickering on along the tram line, the smell of yeast from the communal ovens, children shouting somewhere near the hydroponics terraces. Children. She thought of them without meaning to — the drawings Dev Okafor had shown her, weeks ago now, before either of them had words for what was happening. A burning corridor. A numbered door. Thirty-one identical images in thirty-one different hands.

She looked at the envelope again. Grey, unremarkable, delivered by hand or drone — no way to know which. That was the thing about the notice that kept snagging in her mind like a splinter: not what it said, but what it proved. It proved that the archive logged terminal sessions down to the minute. It proved that someone reviewed those logs. It proved that Maren Hale's friendly offer of tea, that generous early retirement dangled over porcelain cups, had not been a courtesy at all. It had been a first pass. A probe. And when the probe came back showing that Astrid Nystrom did not scare easily, the machinery behind the probe had shifted gears without any visible seam.

They were watching everything. They always had been. She simply hadn't been worth watching before.

* * *

She spent the next two days being boring, deliberately, methodically boring. She went to work. She came home. She bought the same rations she always bought, spoke to the same neighbors about the same weather, filed her maintenance reports on the beacon with the punctual dullness of twenty years' habit. If they were building a file on her, she intended it to be the most tedious file in the colony's possession.

But underneath the performance, her mind was working the problem the way her hands worked a corroded valve fitting: patiently, from every angle, feeling for the point where it would give.

The tin was still in her hab. Grandmother's tin — dented green steel, rust blooming at the seams like lichen, holding the pre-founding maintenance log and that photograph with the faces scratched out. Every night she took it out and read a little further into the marginalia, and every night she became more certain of two things at once. First: that the log was the truest document on Vesper's Landing, dated years before the colony officially existed, in handwriting she knew better than her own mother's. Second: that if the council found it, it would vanish into an evidence locker and never be seen again, and whatever happened after that would happen to her in a room without windows.

The inquiry notice had told her something else, too, though it took her a day to hear it. *Between the hours of 09:40 and 11:15.* They knew not just that she'd accessed the archive but exactly when. Which meant the monitoring wasn't retrospective housekeeping. It was live. Somewhere in the administrative stack, a system watched the colony's terminals the way she watched pressure gauges — constantly, professionally, waiting for a needle to jump.

So she stopped using her terminal for anything that mattered. She wrote her working notes by hand, in a notebook she kept inside her coat. She read the tin's documents by lamplight with the window shuttered, absurdly, like a smuggler, and hated herself a little for the theater of it — and then stopped hating herself, because the theater was the point. Paranoia, she was learning, was not a sickness. It was a skill set. It was what you developed when the institution around you had made secrecy its native language and you finally had to speak it back.

On the third night she took the tin out one last time and wrapped it in oilcloth, the way Grandmother had wrapped tools for the climb, three folds and a hitch, waterproof and silent. Then she sat with it on her knees and asked herself the question plainly, because Grandmother had taught her that plain questions got plain answers: if they come tomorrow, will they find it?

Yes. Of course yes. A hab unit was a drawer. A drawer was a confession. Everything she owned fit in eleven square meters, and eleven square meters could be searched in twenty minutes by one bored agent with a checklist.

The beacon, on the other hand, was hers.

Not legally — legally it belonged to whoever held the original contract, some entity so old the name had worn off the paperwork, leaving only the clause and the obligation and her signature renewed year after year beneath the phrase *until it is needed.* But practically, physically, in every way that mattered on a rocky spine of basalt two hours' climb above the settlement, the beacon was the one place on Vesper's Landing where Astrid Nystrom's knowledge exceeded everyone else's. She knew its housing the way she knew the lines of her own palms. She knew which panels were load-bearing and which were cosmetic, which bolts were torqued and which were painted over, and — because she had rebuilt half the interior herself across two decades of monthly maintenance — she knew the cavity behind the gyro stabilizer mount that appeared on no schematic ever filed with the colony.

Nobody climbed to the beacon except her. Nobody wanted to. That was the whole quiet genius of the arrangement: the council had inherited a piece of infrastructure it didn't understand, attached to a keeper it preferred not to think about, and had left both alone for sixty years. Their neglect was her vault.

She would move the tin tonight. Tomorrow there would be rain — the forecast said so, and anyway the forecast always said so this time of year — and rain gave her a reason nobody would question. Beacon checks didn't wait for weather. The light had to burn through storms or it wasn't worth having.

* * *

The storm arrived early, as storms on the ridge liked to, rolling up out of the western dark while Astrid was still halfway through the switchbacks, a wall of cloud the color of wet slate with lightning moving inside it like something trying to get out. By the time she reached the final pitch the wind was coming in gusts hard enough to lean on, and the rain hit sideways, needling cold through every seam of her gear.

She loved it. She was honest enough with herself, gripping the hand-chain bolted into the rock, breath tearing white off her face, to admit that she loved it — this was the closest thing her life had to a church, and the weather was the liturgy. The beacon rose above her, its housing dark except for the running lamp, patient as it had been patient for longer than the colony could remember, and she climbed the last ten meters with her heart slamming and her teeth bared against the wind, and felt, as always, the specific relief of arriving somewhere that expected nothing of her except the work.

Inside, she sealed the hatch, struck the chemical heater, and let the small space fill with the smell of hot metal and her own steaming breath. Routine first — she had learned long ago never to skip routine, because routine was what made the other things invisible. She checked the lamp array, wiped condensation from the lens housings, verified the power draw against the log. Forty minutes of ordinary diligence, performed loudly enough that anyone reviewing sensor telemetry from the site would see exactly what they expected to see: the keeper, doing her job, in bad weather, nothing more.

Then she knelt at the gyro mount.

The panel came away in her hands with the ease of long practice, its edges painted to match the housing, its fasteners chosen to look corroded to anyone who didn't know they weren't. Behind it, the cavity ran back into the structure like a throat — dry, dark, smelling faintly of the desiccant packs she replenished twice a year. She had built this hiding place for spare parts once upon a time. It had waited empty for six years. Now she fed the oilcloth bundle into it, snug against the frame where temperature swings were mildest, and sat back on her heels and looked at it, and felt the strangeness of what she'd done settle over her like the damp.

Her grandmother's whole testimony — the log that predated the founding, the photograph of the crew whose faces someone had gouged out with something sharp and deliberate — sealed inside a machine the guilty had built as penance, or a trap, or both. There was a symmetry there that she suspected Ede's sister would have appreciated, wherever the truth of her had gone. You hid the evidence of the crime inside the monument to the crime. Where else would it be safe? Nowhere else on this colony was safe. Nowhere else was even honest.

She replaced the panel, pressed the paint seams closed with her thumb, and sat for a while in the heater's orange glow, listening to the storm hammer the housing like a fist on a door.

And it was there, in that silence between gusts, that she heard it — or didn't hear it, exactly, but felt it, the way you feel someone reading over your shoulder. The beacon's maintenance transponder blinked its status pulse every ninety seconds, a heartbeat of data sent down to the colony net: power levels, battery state, hatch integrity, ambient temp. Standard telemetry. She had configured it herself.

Hatch integrity: nominal. It had been nominal when she arrived. It would be nominal when she left.

But last month — she pulled the local buffer up on her handheld, scrolling back through the stored pulses with numb fingers — last month, on the ninth, between 02:10 and 02:40, the buffer showed a gap. Thirty minutes of missing heartbeat. Not a failure; the system didn't fail, she'd built it not to fail. A gap. As if something had reached into the stream and lifted a section out, cleanly, the way you'd lift a page from a ledger.

Someone had been here. Someone had stood inside this housing in the dark while she slept two hours below, and had known the telemetry well enough to erase their own visit, and had left no mark she could find — or had left a mark she hadn't thought to look for.

Astrid sat very still. The heater ticked. Rain ran in silver threads down the lens ports.

They're watching everything, she thought again, but the thought had changed shape now, grown colder and more precise. Not just the terminals. Not just the archive. Here. This place, this machine, this contract older than the colony itself — the one thing Maren Hale had called *legacy infrastructure, best forgotten* — even this was under observation. Which meant the beacon mattered to them. Which meant whatever it locked, they were afraid of it too.

Good, she thought, and was surprised by the steadiness of it. Let them watch. Let them see a woman climb in a storm and check a lamp and climb back down. Let them erase their own footprints and congratulate themselves on their thoroughness. She had something they didn't have. She had the cavity behind the gyro mount, and the tin inside it, and a grandmother's handwriting telling her, in marginalia she was only beginning to decode, that the dream had a name and the name had a number and the number was on the door.

She packed up, doused the heater, and paused with her hand on the hatch.

Through the small observation port, far below, the lights of Vesper's Landing lay scattered across the valley floor like embers raked apart — warm, ordinary, oblivious. And at the edge of the settlement, near the clinic, one window burned brighter than the others, the emergency-white of a medical bay burning through the rain.

She frowned. The clinic didn't run full lighting overnight unless—

The transponder pulsed. Ninety seconds. Hatch integrity: nominal. Somewhere below, a child named Ilsa Voss had been sleeping for nine days, and the clinic lights were on, and Astrid stood in a locked metal room on top of a mountain with a dead woman's evidence hidden in its walls, understanding suddenly and completely that the clock she had been ignoring since the tea in Maren Hale's perfect little parlor had been running the entire time.

She opened the hatch and stepped out into the storm, and began the climb down, fast.
