## 13. Blackout Night

The clinic at Vesper's Landing had been built to be forgotten. It sat in the lee of the water reclamation plant, its walls the same grey composite as everything else in the settlement, its sign — CLINIC, and beneath it, smaller, HOURS: ALWAYS — faded to a whisper of itself. Dev Okafor had worked there for eleven years, first as an assistant to old Dr. Reyes, then, after she retired to the warmer latitudes of death, as the only medical professional for four hundred colonists. He knew every creak in the floor, every patient who faked coughs to avoid school, every family that paid their bills late and every family that never paid at all.

He did not know what to do with Ilsa Voss.

She lay in the second bed of the observation ward, the one with the window facing east, toward the beacon hill. Nine days now. Her parents had finally consented to bring her in on the sixth day, after she'd stopped responding even to her mother's voice, but they'd come like people surrendering a hostage — reluctantly, watchfully, as if the clinic itself might be part of whatever was happening. Marta Voss had not left the chair beside the bed since. Tomas came each evening after his shift at the hydroponics bays and stood at the foot of the bed with his arms folded, not praying exactly, but holding himself in the shape of a man who wanted to.

Dev checked her vitals out of habit more than hope. Heart rate slow and steady, sixty-two beats a minute, like a metronome set by someone very patient. Respiration shallow but regular. Brain activity — he'd run the scans twice on the ancient imaging unit, which hummed and complained and produced pictures that looked like weather maps — activity that made no sense. The deep structures lit up like aurora. Whatever Ilsa was doing in there, it was not rest. It was work.

"Her fingers moved again," Marta said from the chair. She said it the way other people reported rain. "About an hour ago. All five, all together."

"I saw it on the monitor," Dev said. It was true. He'd watched the little spike of motion cross the screen at 3:14 in the morning and felt something cold walk down his spine. Five fingers moving in unison was not a reflex. It was a gesture. It was the beginning of a sentence someone hadn't finished yet.

Outside the window, the afternoon was doing its best impression of a normal day. Vesper's Landing didn't have weather so much as it had moods — the atmosphere processors kept the air breathable and bland, and the sky cycled through a programmed palette of greys and pale blues that the founding charter had apparently considered cheerful. Children were visible in the distance, small figures on the path between the school annex and the commons, kicking a ball that rose too slowly in the low gravity and hung at the top of its arc like something undecided.

Nine children sleeping. He'd made himself count them again last night, alone in his office with the door locked, going through his own records one name at a time. Ilsa Voss. Petra Lindqvist. The Adeyemi twins, Fola and Femi, who had gone under within hours of each other and whose EEGs, when he overlaid them, matched line for line, peak for peak, as if one mind were running through two bodies. Six others whose families had brought them in quietly, without fanfare, some of them from founding-family households that had never once set foot in his clinic before because they used the private medics retained by the Council.

All nine descendants of Meridian crew. He hadn't told Astrid Nystrom that yet. He wasn't sure he was ready to say it out loud, because saying it would make it a fact, and facts were things you had to do something about.

The lights went out at 4:47 in the afternoon.

Not flickered. Not dimmed. Went out — every light in the clinic, every light in the ward, the monitor screens, the emergency strips over the doors that were supposed to have their own independent power cells. Darkness arrived complete and total, the kind of darkness that doesn't exist on Earth, where cities bleed their glow into the sky forever. Here, when the grid failed, the dark was absolute, older than the colony, patient as stone.

"Dev—" Marta's voice, sharp with a fear that had been waiting just beneath the surface for nine days.

"I'm here." His hand found the edge of the nurse's station. "Stay where you are. Don't try to move in the dark."

He heard Tomas's boots in the corridor, coming fast from the waiting room, and then the sound of the whole building changing around them — the ventilation fans spinning down, the faint subsonic hum that lived in the walls of every structure on Vesper's Landing fading away, leaving a silence so profound that Dev could hear his own blood moving in his ears. Somewhere outside, a child's voice rose in alarm and was gathered up by an adult's. Somewhere farther off, the backup generators at the water plant tried to catch — he heard the stuttering cough of their ignition sequence, once, twice — and failed.

And then Ilsa Voss sat up.

Dev heard it before he could see anything: the whisper of sheets, the soft mechanical sound of the bed frame adjusting to a change in weight. Then Marta's breath catching, a small torn sound, and Tomas saying, "Ilsa? Ilsa, sweetheart—"

"It's okay," Dev said, though nothing about it was okay, though every instinct he owned was screaming. "Don't touch her yet. Let me get to her." He moved by memory, hand along the counter, around the station, three steps into the ward. His shin found the corner of the supply cart and he bit down on the pain.

In the pitch dark, Ilsa was sitting upright. He couldn't see her, but he could feel her presence the way you feel a person standing behind you in an empty room — a displacement in the air, a pressure against the skin. And he could hear her breathing, which had changed. For nine days it had been slow and tidal, the breathing of the deeply asleep. Now it was quick and shallow and deliberate, the breathing of someone concentrating very hard.

"Marta," he said quietly. "Is she — can you see her at all?"

"No. Nothing. Dev, why is she sitting up, why is she—" Marta's voice cracked. "This is what happens in the dream. This is what they describe. The fire comes and then the dark comes and then—"

"The dark comes?" Dev felt the words land somewhere below his ribs. In nine days of recording sleep-talk, of coaxing fragments from the waking dreamers, no one had mentioned darkness. Fire, always fire. A corridor full of smoke and orange light. A numbered door. But no darkness.

"The lights fail," Marta whispered. "In the dream. That's how it starts. The ship goes dark, and then the fire finds them anyway, because fire makes its own light. They told us that. The ones who wake up. They said the dark comes first and you think maybe it will be okay, maybe the fire can't find you in the dark, and then the corridor lights up orange from the far end and you know it's coming for everyone."

Tomas had reached the bed. Dev heard him stop, heard the hesitation in his breathing — a father wanting to gather his daughter up and a father afraid that touching her might break something. "Ilsa," he said, very gently. "Papa's here."

Ilsa did not answer him. But she spoke.

It was not her voice. Dev had treated this child since she was two years old; he knew the particular bright rasp of her, the slight lisp she'd carried since the loss of her front teeth, the way she flattened her vowels the way all the Landing kids did. What came out of the darkness was a woman's voice, low and measured and strange, with an accent none of the colony children had — vowels rounded differently, consonants placed precisely, the accent of somewhere else, of a place that existed only in recordings and history files.

"Seven minutes," the voice said. "Hold the seal for seven minutes. Ede, count with me. Ede, are you counting?"

Marta made a sound like she'd been struck.

"Ede," the voice said again, and now there was something in it that Dev recognized from a hundred sickrooms, from a hundred vigils — the particular tenderness of someone trying to comfort another person while being terrified themselves. "Count with me. Out loud. So we know we're both still here. One. Two."

Around them, in the dark, other voices answered.

They came from everywhere and nowhere — thin, distant, layered, as if broadcast through walls, as if the whole colony were speaking through its sleeping children at once. Dev understood, with a lurch, that they weren't hearing echoes in the clinic. Somewhere across the settlement, in bedrooms and private wards and shuttered rooms, the other eight sleepers were sitting up in their own darkness, and the words Ilsa spoke were being spoken simultaneously by all of them, in unison, in that same borrowed woman's voice.

"Three. Four." The counting filled the ward. It seemed to come from inside the walls themselves. "Keep your hand flat against the door. Feel for the heat. If the metal sings, pull back. If it stays quiet, we hold. We hold until the seven minutes are done and the launch bay clears and then—" The voice caught. Steadied. "Then we open it for whoever's left. We promised. Forty-one of us, we promised."

"Five," said the darkness, in thirty-one sleeping mouths across Vesper's Landing.

Dev's hands were shaking. He pressed them flat against the bed rail to still them and felt the vibration coming up through the frame — a fine tremor, rhythmic, like the whole structure of the bed was resonating with something. With the counting. With whatever frequency the dead had found to speak on.

"Six. Seven." A pause, long enough that Marta began to sob openly, quietly, into her sleeve. "Ede, talk to me. Tell me about the garden again. Tell me what the soil smells like. I want to be thinking about soil when it comes."

Another voice joined then — younger, lighter, a girl's voice or a young woman's, and Dev thought of a photograph in a tin box he had never seen, of a name Astrid Nystrom had not yet spoken aloud to anyone. He didn't know why he thought it. He simply knew, the way you know a room has someone in it before you see them.

"Soil," the second voice said, warm and close, as if leaning against the first speaker's shoulder. "Wet, after the first rain. Like iron and green things. Like the beginning of something. Petra, listen — when the launch bay clears, don't wait for me. Take the others and go. Promise me you'll stand on real ground. Promise me you'll smell it for both of us."

"Eight," said the corridor of sleeping children.

And then the fire came.

There was no heat. There was no light. But every hair on Dev's arms stood up, and the air in the ward took on a texture — a pressure, a dryness, a smell of scorched polymer and burnt insulation that had no source in the sealed, cold room. Marta cried out. Tomas was saying his daughter's name over and over, a rosary of it. And the counting continued, faster now, the voices fraying at the edges, layering over each other, the calm woman's voice and the warm girl's voice and dozens of others rising beneath them — men, women, voices of every age, all counting, all holding, all keeping their hands flat against a door that Dev suddenly understood with total, physical certainty was real, had been real, was still real somewhere, was perhaps the door he had seen drawn forty times in crayon by children who had never been aboard any ship.

"Nine. Ten. Eleven. Twelve—"

The lights came back.

Every one of them, all at once, full brightness, so that the ward snapped into existence around them like a photograph developing: the white walls, the monitors, Marta with her hands over her mouth, Tomas gripping the bed rail, and Ilsa — Ilsa lying flat again, eyes closed, hands at her sides, her face slack and peaceful as a christening portrait. As if she had never moved. As if the last seven minutes had happened entirely elsewhere and her body had merely been the antenna.

Dev looked at the wall clock. 4:54. Seven minutes. To the second.

* * *

They sat in his office afterward, the three of them, because Marta's legs wouldn't hold her and Tomas wouldn't leave her side and neither of them would leave the clinic while Ilsa slept on. Dev made tea that nobody drank. The emergency lights had come up an hour later, the grid restored by technicians working through the night, and the official explanation — issued within ninety minutes, which was itself suspicious, Dev thought, official explanations usually took days — was a cascade fault in the primary distribution junction, rare, regrettable, already corrected.

"A cascade fault," Tomas said. He turned his cup slowly on its saucer. "That's what they're saying."

"That's what they're saying."

"My grandfather worked the grid for twenty years. He used to say the Landing's power system was boring on purpose. Redundant on purpose. He said the founders built it so that nothing could ever take the whole grid down at once, not a storm, not a strike, not a war. 'Seven minutes of total blackout,' he called it once. 'The one thing that must never happen.'" Tomas looked up. "Why would my dead grandfather use those exact words, Dev? Why would that phrase even exist?"

Dev had no answer. He had something worse than no answer: he had a hypothesis, and the hypothesis had teeth.

Because he'd done the arithmetic already, sitting in the dark with his back against the nurse's station, listening to the dead count. The dreamers described a fire that lasted seven minutes. Every one of them, independently, gave the same duration — the waking ones drew it, the sleeping ones counted it, and when he'd timed the sleep-talking episodes against the drawings' annotations (children annotated their nightmares with timestamps; it was the sort of detail that broke your heart), the span was always the same. Seven minutes of fire. Seven minutes of darkness. Seven minutes of holding a door.

And tonight the grid had failed for exactly seven minutes, and every sleeper in the colony had sat upright in the same instant, and spoken with the voices of people who had died before this colony was founded.

"You recorded it," Marta said. It wasn't a question. She'd seen the recorder on his desk many times; she'd signed the consent form herself, weeks ago, back when this had still seemed like a medical mystery instead of a haunting. "You'll play it for me. I need to hear it again. I need to know I didn't imagine the name."

"Ede," Dev said.

Marta closed her eyes. "My grandmother's name was Ede. My grandmother — Marta Ede Voss. I was named for her middle name. Nobody talks about her. You know how the founding families are, there are portraits in the hall and histories in the archive, and my grandmother is in none of them. When I asked my mother, growing up, she said Ede died before the Landing, on the crossing, that it was sad and that we didn't discuss it." Her hands were trembling around the untouched cup. "But tonight, that voice — that poor, brave, terrified voice — asked for Ede like Ede was standing right beside her. Like they were holding hands in the dark."

The office was very quiet. Through the window, Dev could see the beacon hill, black against the deepening sky, and at its crown the beacon itself, dark now with the grid down — no, wait. It wasn't dark. He leaned forward. Atop the hill, small and steady, a single point of light burned. The beacon ran on its own isolated cell, he knew that much from the maintenance filings; it was the one structure on Vester's Landing that the grid failure hadn't touched. While the whole colony had plunged into the past's darkness, the beacon had kept its vigil, unchanged, unblinking, as it presumably had every night for decades, broadcasting whatever it broadcast to whoever had ordered it maintained.

Someone was up there. He could see the light shift, briefly, as a figure passed between the lamp and the ridge line. Astrid Nystrom, climbing in the aftermath of a blackout, checking her charge, because that was what keepers did. Because her contract said *until it is needed*, and Dev was beginning to suspect that the need had arrived, and that the paperwork had known before any of them.

"Tomas," he said. "Your grandfather's phrase. Seven minutes. Where exactly did he say it? Was there a document? A logbook, a training manual, anything?"

"He kept a personal log. Handwritten. It's in a chest at our house with his tools." Tomas frowned. "Why?"

"Because I think your grandfather knew something," Dev said slowly, "and I think he wasn't the only one, and I think the reason nobody talks about your grandmother is the same reason nine children are asleep in my clinic speaking with the voices of the dead." He stood, crossed to the window, and looked at the light on the hill. "And I think there's a woman up on that beacon who's been sitting on the answer her whole life without knowing it."

Marta followed his gaze. "The keeper," she said. "The Nystrom girl. My mother always got a strange look when the Nystroms came up. Like they were owed something. Or owed something to us." She set down the cup, and when she lifted her chin, Dev saw something new in her face — not the helpless fear of the past nine days, but its opposite, its engine. "Take me with you. Tomorrow. Both of us. Whatever you say to her, we say it too. Our daughter is lying in that bed counting numbers for a woman who's been dead for sixty years, and I am done being polite about it."

Down in the ward, unnoticed by any of them, Ilsa Voss's right hand had curled loosely against the sheet — five fingers, half-closed, in the shape of someone holding on to another hand in the dark. And on the monitor, in the deep structures of her sleeping brain, the aurora brightened, and held, and waited.
