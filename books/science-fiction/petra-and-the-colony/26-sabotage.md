## 26. Sabotage

The storm came in off the ice shelf the way storms always did at Vesper's Landing — without announcement, without mercy, as if the weather itself had learned its manners from the founders.

Astrid felt it before she saw it: a pressure change in her teeth, a low ache in the old break she'd suffered falling from the beacon's third ladder rung two winters ago. She was standing at her kitchen window with a mug of tea going cold in her hands when the first gust hit the settlement's outer panels and made them sing — a long, keening note that the colonists had named *the widow's voice* back when naming things was still allowed to be honest.

She checked the beacon feed out of habit. The maintenance signal pulsed green on her console, steady as a heartbeat. Every ninety seconds, a small packet of data went out into the dark: telemetry, power draw, core temperature, and buried in the noise floor where no one but she would ever think to look, the copied archive — Grandmother's log, the photograph, the tally marks from the old habitat wall, every scrap of truth Astrid had managed to save since the council began closing its fist.

Forty-one names now. She'd found them all in the vault logs Dev had transcribed, and each night the signal carried them out like a lullaby sung to no one.

The green light blinked.

Astrid set down the mug. Blinked again herself, certain it was fatigue. The signal had never missed a pulse in eleven years of her keeping it — not during the whiteout of '43, not when lightning had kissed the tower's upper mast and left the housing smelling of ozone for a week. The beacon did not blink. It was built by guilty hands to be unblinking; penance did not permit interruption.

It blinked a third time, and then the icon went amber, and then it went grey, and the console read:

**SIGNAL LOST — LAST PULSE 22:14:07**

Outside, the storm screamed down the valley like something let out of a cage.

* * *

She dressed for the climb the way she'd done a hundred times, but her hands were shaking so badly she had to start over on the inner glove seals. Insulated shell, harness, headlamp, spare lamp, the emergency kit she kept packed by the door because Clause Seven had taught her that preparation was the only prayer worth saying. She hesitated over the comms unit. The council had locked her out of official channels after the retraction — Maren's face on every screen in the settlement, calm and sorrowful, calling Astrid Nystrom a fabricator, an alarmist, a woman who endangered children with stories. Half the colony believed it. The other half wasn't sure, and unsure people kept their doors shut.

But there was one channel still open, because Dev Okafor had refused — publicly, stubbornly, at cost to himself — to close it. She keyed it.

"Dev. The beacon's down. Signal cut at twenty-two fourteen."

Static, then his voice, tight with sleep and something sharper underneath. "Cut? Storms don't cut it. You told me yourself it rode out the whiteout."

"I know what I told you." She looked at the grey icon and felt the cold coming through the window glass as if it were already inside her. "Someone turned it off, Dev. And if it stays off through the activation window tonight—" She stopped. Neither of them liked saying it aloud. Three children still slept. Ilsa Voss among them. Whatever held them was tethered to the beacon, and the beacon was tethered to the tower on the ridge, and the tower was four kilometers up a switchback trail that would be a river of ice by now.

"The window opens at oh-three-hundred," Dev said. "That's five hours."

"Four and a half, if I don't stop to be sensible."

"Astrid—"

"If it goes dark through the window," she said quietly, "we lose them. That's what decommissioning does. That's what the vote was for. Someone just decided not to wait six days." She heard him breathing, heard the sound of him getting up, of a child stirring somewhere behind him — his daughter, home now, awake, whole, sleeping in her own bed every night since the names were read aloud. "I'm going. I wanted someone to know where my body would be if I'm stupid about it."

"You're always stupid about it," Dev said. "That's why it works. Go. I'll wake the Vosses."

* * *

The trail was gone.

Not closed, not drifted — *gone*, erased under a skin of black ice laid down by sleet that had frozen mid-fall, so that the switchbacks gleamed like the ribs of some enormous animal stripped bare. Her headlamp threw a cone of light into horizontal rain that turned to snow ten meters up and vanished into a darkness that swallowed even the beam. Wind hit her from the left in gusts she could lean her whole weight into, the way sailors leaned, and between gusts the silence was worse — a held breath, a waiting.

She climbed by count. Two hundred steps to the first cairn. Rest. Two hundred more. It was a trick her grandmother had taught her, though Grandmother had taught it for counting stitches, for counting the days between supply drops, for counting anything that needed to be gotten through rather than understood. *We keep what we're given and ask nothing.* Astrid had spent months hating those words. Now they were just a rhythm, and rhythm was all that stood between her and the wind's arithmetic, which said: *one small woman, four kilometers, minus one.*

At the second cairn her boot found ice instead of rock and her right leg shot out sideways and the world became a smear of white and black and pain as her shoulder took the slope. She lay there for a moment, cheek against the frozen ground, breath tearing at her throat, and considered — honestly, calmly, the way one considers anything at the bottom of a fall — staying. The ground was cold but not lethal yet. Sleep was right there, offered, warm-seeming, a lie the body tells in the cold. The beacon was still three kilometers away. The children were still asleep either way. What difference—

No. She got the words out loud, because aloud was harder to ignore. "What difference is everything, you coward. Get up."

She got up. Her shoulder sang its broken-glass song. She counted steps.

* * *

The sabotage revealed itself at kilometer three, and it was worse than she'd feared, because it was careful.

The main power line ran along the ridge on stanchions, armored cable, rated for exactly this kind of weather. Someone hadn't cut it. They had *unbolted* it — a clean disconnection at junction box seven, panel closed afterward, bolts finger-tightened, nothing left swinging. No sparks, no drama. A maintenance crew's work, done by someone who knew the junction boxes, who knew which one fed the beacon, who knew that a severed line in a storm would look like storm damage to any investigator who bothered to squint.

Astrid stood in the lashing rain and looked at the neat, polite, murderous little disconnection and felt something in her chest go very quiet and very hard.

They hadn't sent thugs. They hadn't sent security. They had sent competence. Because that was how the founding families had always worked — she understood it fully now, standing in the weather their ancestors had crossed a burning ship to escape — not with violence, not usually, but with paperwork and precision and the absolute confidence that no one would ever check. The massacre aboard the *Meridian* had been followed by forty years of immaculate minutes. The cover-up had been followed by a beacon with a keeper clause. Everything tidy. Everything deniable. Everything bolted shut and finger-tightened.

Except her. Except the clause. Except the dream that wouldn't stay dead.

She got the panel open with numb fingers, spliced the line with the emergency kit's repair sleeve, and hit the reconnect. Nothing. The breaker at the tower end must have tripped — which meant the manual reset, which meant the last kilometer, which meant the exposed spine of the ridge where the wind came off the shelf with nothing between it and her but forty years of other keepers' stubbornness.

Her headlamp flickered.

She checked the battery gauge. Fine. Checked the spare in her pack. Fine. The flicker came again — longer this time — and she stood very still in the roaring dark and understood that the lamp wasn't failing. Something was draining it. Some interference rolling off the ridge ahead, the same signature she'd felt in her teeth on blackout night, the same presence that pressed against the inside of dreams.

*We're still here,* the flicker said, in the language of dying light. *Hurry.*

* * *

The last stretch took everything.

She climbed the ridge spine on hands and knees in places, clipped to the guide cable her grandmother's generation had bolted down, moving one carabiner-length at a time while the wind tried to peel her off the mountain like a label off a jar. Twice her feet went out and the harness caught her and she hung above a drop she couldn't see the bottom of, swinging, counting, breathing. Once she simply stopped — thirty seconds, maybe forty — forehead against the ice-slick cable, and let the storm have her weight without letting it have the rest.

*Ilsa is nine years old,* she told herself. *Ilsa drew the corridor in blue crayon because that was the crayon she had. Ilsa has been asleep for nineteen days and her mother sits beside her every night reading the vault logs aloud because Dev said the names might reach her, and Sanna Voss has not missed a single night.*

She moved.

The tower rose out of the dark all at once, the way mountains do — one moment absence, the next a vast vertical fact humming faintly under the gale. She reached the access hatch, fought it open against the pressure differential, and hauled herself into the base chamber, where the wind abruptly ceased and the silence rang like a struck bell.

The breaker panel was open. The main throw was down.

And beside it, on the dust of the housing ledge, someone had left a message. Not written — *arranged*. A strip of torn paper, weighted with a bolt, and on it, in block letters cut from a printed notice:

**LET THEM GO. YOU CAN'T WAKE THE DEAD AND NEITHER CAN IT.**

Astrid stared at it for a long moment. Then she picked it up, folded it with deliberate care, and put it in her breast pocket, over her heart, where evidence lived.

"No," she said to the empty chamber, to the council, to forty years of tidy men. "You've had your version. It's our turn."

She threw the breaker.

* * *

The beacon woke like a lung filling.

Power surged up through the tower's bones and out through the mast, and even inside the base chamber Astrid felt it — a deep subsonic thrum in the floor plates, in her sternum, in the old break in her shoulder, which had begun to speak a language of its own. On her wrist console, the signal icon bloomed green. Pulse one. Ninety seconds later, pulse two. The archive went back out into the dark, forty-one names riding the carrier wave, and somewhere below in the settlement she imagined — she chose to imagine — three sleeping children shifting slightly, like swimmers turning toward shore.

Then the lights in the chamber dimmed, steadied, dimmed again, and the thrum stuttered.

Astrid's stomach dropped. She spun to the diagnostics board and read the cascade of warnings with a clarity that frightened her by being calm: **LOAD IMBALANCE. RESERVE CELL DISCHARGING. ESTIMATED BACKUP RESERVE: 41 MINUTES.** The main line was live — she'd verified it — but something downstream was bleeding the power faster than the line could feed it. Damage. Or more sabotage, deeper in, a second cut laid behind the first by someone who assumed she'd fix the obvious one and die of satisfaction.

Forty-one minutes. The number sat in her chest like a stone. Even the numbers were mocking her now.

She got back into the storm.

* * *

The reserve cells were housed in the secondary bunker fifty meters down the ridge's far side — a crawl she should have refused, a crawl she did anyway, because the alternative was doing the arithmetic of what forty-one minutes meant for a nine-year-old girl and refusing to do it. The wind on the far side was worse, funneled, personal. Ice had welded the bunker hatch shut and she broke it free with the pry bar, losing the pry bar to the dark in the process, and dropped inside onto a floor slick with condensation.

The cells were fine. The bus bar connecting them was not — corroded through at a joint that inspection records said had been replaced six years ago. Records lied. Records always lied. She laughed once, high and unhinged, at the sheer consistency of it, and got out the splice kit, and discovered her hands could no longer feel the difference between the tool and her own fingers.

That was the danger sign. She knew it. Frostnip sliding toward frostbite, fine motor control going, and the splice required precision — seating the bus bar, torquing the collar, sealing the joint — work for warm hands, for steady hands, for hands attached to a person who had not just climbed a ridge in a hurricane on a vow and a countdown.

She got the bar seated. She got the collar half-torqued. And then the world tilted — not the floor, her blood pressure, her blood sugar, the long debt of the climb coming due all at once — and she went down onto one knee, then both, vision graying at the edges, the splice kit open and useless beside her like a toy beside a corpse.

*Get up.* She couldn't. *Crawl.* She did, two meters, toward the hatch, toward the storm, toward help that was four kilometers and a lifetime away, and then the gray closed in from all sides and the last thing she thought, with terrible, distant politeness, was: *so this is what the clause meant. Until it is needed. Until it is needed, and then until it isn't.*

The hatch opened. Cold air poured in — and with it, a voice.

"Astrid. Astrid Nystrom, look at me."

Hands on her shoulders. Strong hands, chapped from dishwater and clinic laundry and nineteen nights of sitting vigil. She forced her eyes open and the gray parted around a face she knew from the school gates, from the memorial gatherings, from the front row of every night the names were read: Sanna Voss, hood blown back, ice in her eyebrows, fury and terror fighting for possession of her mouth.

"How—" Astrid's lips barely worked.

"Dev called us. Ilsa's father took the sled for Dev's daughter's inhaler — pharmacy run, the storm caught him short of the turnoff — and I heard the call and I *ran*, you impossible woman, I ran the lower trail because I couldn't sit in that room one more minute listening to my daughter breathe in her sleep." Sanna was already working — pulling Astrid's gloves off with her teeth, chafing her fingers, pressing heat packs from her own kit against Astrid's palms, movements quick and brutal and practiced, the muscle memory of a mother who had spent nineteen days learning that helplessness was a choice. "Finish the splice. Your hands are done, mine aren't. Tell me what to do."

"It needs—" Astrid's vision swam. She made herself point. "Bus bar. Collar. Full torque, clockwise, until the mark aligns. Then sealant, the grey tube, full bead around the joint. If you cross the threads it arcs and we're both—"

"Then I won't cross the threads." Sanna Voss knelt in the freezing bunker of a sabotaged beacon in the worst storm in six years and picked up the torque wrench as if she'd been born holding one. "Nineteen days I've watched everyone tell me what can't be done. Watch the collar, Astrid. Tell me when."

Astrid watched. Through the gray, through the ringing, through the pain singing up both arms, she watched Ilsa Voss's mother seat the collar true, bring the wrench around slow and even, and stop it precisely at the alignment mark on the first try.

"Now the sealant. Bead, continuous, don't breathe the fumes."

Sanna sealed it. Set the tube down. Looked at Astrid with an expression that had passed through fury and come out the far side into something harder and quieter.

"Tell me this finishes it," she said. "Tell me my daughter comes home."

Above them, through fifty meters of rock and storm, the beacon's hum changed pitch — steadied, deepened, settled into the long unbroken tone that meant the reserves were charging, the signal was whole, the lock was holding. Pulse after pulse after pulse, green and green and green.

Astrid lay back on the cold floor and laughed, or wept, or both, and Sanna Voss pulled the emergency blanket around them both and sat with her back against the cell bank like a woman guarding a gate.

It was Sanna who saw it first — the thing tucked in the corner behind the reserve cells, half-hidden by a maintenance tarp that didn't belong there. She stood, pulled the tarp aside, and went very still.

"Astrid," she said. "There's a stencil on this wall. Under the paint. It's a serial number." She brushed at it with her thumb, and flakes of old whitewash fell away, and the digits emerged letter by letter into the lamplight. "It matches the one on your beacon housing. I've seen it every night for weeks — it's stamped on the vault door frames too. Downstairs. Under the habitat."

Astrid closed her eyes. The storm roared overhead, indifferent, and beneath it the beacon hummed its faithful note, and somewhere in the space between the two sounds a door she had never known existed waited to be found.

"Then tomorrow," she said, "we go downstairs."
