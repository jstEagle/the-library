## 37. What the Children Kept

The first thing Ilsa Voss brought back from the dead was a song.

Dev Okafor heard it on a Tuesday morning, three weeks after the tribunal convened, standing in the doorway of the school's music room with a stack of attendance sheets he no longer trusted himself to hold. The room had been rearranged since the sleepers woke — desks pushed to the walls, chairs in a loose ring, because the returned children could not bear to sit in rows anymore. They sat facing each other now, all of them, as if they'd spent too long already with their backs turned.

Ilsa stood at the center, nine years old and thinner than she'd been before the dream, her hair still growing back where the clinic had shaved patches for monitoring leads. She was singing. Not a colony song — Dev knew every song taught in Vesper's Landing schools, had taught half of them himself — but something older, slower, built on a minor key that seemed to lean against the air. The words were in no language the curriculum covered. And around her, six of the other returned children had picked it up without being taught, their voices finding the harmonies like water finding channels, and Dev stood in the doorway with his heart hammering and understood that none of them had ever heard this melody before in their lives.

Except that they had. Just not here. Not awake.

He did not interrupt. He stood until the song ended — it ended the way tides end, not stopping so much as arriving somewhere — and then Ilsa turned and looked at him with her own eyes again, the ones that had said *thank you* at the last, and said, "Mr. Okafor, do you know what this one is called?"

"No," Dev said honestly. "Do you?"

She frowned, and the frown was nine years old, entirely a child's frown, and that was somehow the worst part. "It has a name," she said. "But the name belongs to someone else. I just have the shape of it left over." She touched her sternum, lightly, the way you'd touch a bruise. "Like when you carry something for someone so long your arms remember it after you've set it down."

* * *

The tribunal had been running for three weeks by then, and it had settled into a rhythm that surprised everyone who'd expected spectacle. There were no mobs outside the assembly hall. There were clerks. There were long tables of records brought up from the vault beneath the old habitat, and archivists who wept quietly over forty-one final messages they transcribed twice, once for the record and once, they said, for themselves. Maren Hale testified for eleven hours across two days, her voice never rising, naming names including her own ancestor's, and when she finished the hall was so silent Dev could hear the ventilation cycling.

But the tribunal kept circling back, session after session, to the children. Because the children would not stop bringing things back.

Tomas Reyes, seven years old, who had slept nineteen days, came back knowing how to splice cable in a style no one on Vesper's Landing used — a style the vault logs identified, within a week, as the Meridian's engineering standard, obsolete for sixty years. He demonstrated it in front of the tribunal with small competent hands while grown engineers took notes, and afterward his mother found him sitting alone behind the assembly hall, turning a scrap of wire over and over.

"I don't want it," he told her, when she asked what was wrong. "But I can't put it down."

Mira Chen, ten, came back with a recipe. She wrote it out in careful schoolroom letters: quantities in units the colony hadn't used since rationing ended, techniques described in terms that made the colony's oldest cooks go pale and then go quiet. It was bread. Some kind of ship-bread, dense, meant to keep for months, meant to be baked in the Meridian's galley ovens. When the colony bakery made a test batch, Mira tasted it and started to cry — not the crying of a child disappointed in a flavor, but the crying of someone meeting a smell from a burning house.

"It's right," she said. "That's what's wrong with it. It's exactly right."

And Ede — Ede's laugh. That was the one that undid people. Amara Sefu, eight years old, had come back from thirty-one days under with a laugh that wasn't hers. It was a woman's laugh, low and sudden and ending on an upward break, and the first time Amara laughed that way in public — at some small joke in the market, nothing — an old man dropped a crate of fruit. He was ninety-one years old, one of the last colonists born aboard the Meridian itself, and he told the tribunal later, in a voice that shook, that he had known a woman named Ede Nystrom for four months of his childhood aboard the ship, and that she had laughed exactly like that, and that he had spent sixty years unable to remember the sound no matter how he tried, and then a child laughed in the market and there it was, whole, preserved, carried out of the fire by a girl who had never met either of them.

Dev sat in the tribunal gallery the day that testimony was given and watched Astrid Nystrom listen to it. She sat very straight. She did not cry. But her hands, folded on the rail in front of her, went white at the knuckles, and afterward she walked out past him without seeing him, and he let her go, because some griefs need the corridor empty.

* * *

Not everyone received it as gift. That was the argument, and it split the colony down seams nobody had known were there.

The formal question before the tribunal — submitted, absurdly, in writing, as if the colony's machinery could only process grief through paperwork — was this: *What is owed to knowledge carried by children who cannot consent to carrying it?*

There were those who wanted it taken away. A faction of families, mostly those whose children had *not* slept, petitioned the council to establish a program: counseling, suppression therapy, "integration support" — words that everyone understood meant making the children forget. The dead were dead; the argument ran; sixty years of peace had been built on the founding story, however false, and now the truth was out and good, justice done, monument carved — but the residue, the fragments living in children's heads like shrapnel, served no one. Let the children be children. Let Tomas put down the wire. Let Amara have her own laugh back.

Dev attended the session where that petition was debated, and he listened to a woman named Greta Holm — whose son had never dreamed, never slept, and who loved him with a ferocity Dev did not doubt — stand before the assembly and say: "My boy watches the others the way you watch people speaking a language you were locked out of. He's begun to pretend. He hums the song wrong on purpose so they'll correct him. Is that what we want? A colony of children divided into those who remember and those who must fake it?"

And an engineer named Sorrel — one of the ones who'd taken notes during Tomas's demonstration — stood and answered, not unkindly: "Your boy will learn the splicing technique in a month, from Tomas, if Tomas teaches him. What he will never learn from any book is what Tomas knows without knowing how he knows it: which joint fails first in a fire. Do we take that from the child? From us? Knowledge bought at that price should not be paid for twice by throwing it away."

Then Ilsa Voss's father stood up, and the room went quiet, because Henrik Voss had spent the whole crisis as a man barely holding his shape together, and no one expected eloquence from him.

"My daughter died," he said. "For thirty-one days my daughter was gone, and what came back was wearing her, and I will spend the rest of my life learning the difference between those two sentences. So believe me when I tell you I have thought about taking things from her. Every night I think about it." His hands gripped the edge of the podium. "But last week she sang me the song, all of it, and she held my hand through the part that hurts, the way you hold someone's hand at a funeral, and she said, *Papa, this part is sad because it's somebody's sad, and if I give it away then nobody has it.*" He looked out at the assembly. "She's nine. She understands something about inheritance that this colony spent sixty years refusing to understand. We don't get to choose what we're given. We only get to choose whether we keep it or bury it. This colony buried it once. Look what that cost."

The vote, when it finally came — not a binding vote, the tribunal chair insisted, but a *sense of the colony*, because some things shouldn't be decided by arithmetic — was overwhelming. Keep it. All of it. The songs would be transcribed. The recipe would enter the colony cookbook with its history printed beside it, the fire acknowledged in the margins. Tomas's splicing standard would be taught in trade school, credited, in the course catalog, to *the crew of the Meridian, recovered through T. Reyes*. And Amara's laugh —

Amara's laugh needed no policy. It simply stayed, the way laughter does, moving through the schoolyard, startling old men into dropping fruit and then helping them pick it up, and slowly the colony learned to hear it without flinching, learned to say *there goes Ede's laugh* the way you say the name of a river or a wind — something that belonged to the place now, something the place had been given.

* * *

Dev's daughter Anaya was not among the sleepers. Her name had been on the list — descendant of the forty-one, called home — but the door had opened before her number came, and she had woken every morning of the crisis ordinary and untouched, and Dev had learned that survival can be its own kind of exclusion.

She came to him one evening in the weeks after the vote, when he was grading papers he couldn't concentrate on, and stood in his study doorway with the particular stiffness of a child preparing to ask something enormous.

"Papa," she said. "Can I learn the song?"

He set down his pen. "Of course. Ilsa would teach you tomorrow if—"

"Not from Ilsa." She came into the room and climbed onto the chair across from his desk, and looked at him with her mother's level gaze. "From the recordings. The vault messages. There are forty-one of them and most of them haven't been listened to more than once, because listening is hard, everyone says so. But Auntie Camille in the archive says some of them have singing in the background. Ship sounds. People humming while they worked, before the end." Her chin lifted slightly. "If the others got theirs from inside, mine should come from outside. From the record. Someone should learn it from the record, so both sides exist." She paused. "So it's true instead of just remembered."

Dev looked at his daughter — nine years old, too young by every measure except the one that mattered — and thought of Astrid saying, in the corridor of the beacon, in the days before everything: *the dead don't need rescuing, they need witnessing.* He thought of a wall of tally marks behind a panel. Forty-one scratches, counted by someone who refused to let the count be lost.

"Okay," he said, and his voice cracked on it, and Anaya pretended very politely not to notice. "We'll listen together. As many evenings as it takes."

"That might be a lot of evenings," she warned him.

"I know," he said. "I'm counting on it."

* * *

Astrid found him at the school two days later, in the late afternoon light, when the corridors emptied and the building belonged to dust motes and the distant thump of someone's after-hours drum practice. She leaned in his doorway the way he'd leaned in the music room's, and for a moment neither of them spoke, and the silence between them had become — he noticed this with quiet wonder — easy. Worn smooth, like a stone two people had carried between them for a long way.

"I heard the council ratified the cookbook entry," she said. "Bread with a provenance."

"They're calling it Meridian Loaf. Mira insists it be shaped wrong — flat on one side, 'because of the oven rack.' The bakers obeyed. You can't argue with a primary source."

Astrid smiled, and then the smile settled into something more careful. "Dev. Can I ask you something? As the person who sees them most?"

"Anything."

"The fragments. The skills, the songs, the—" she hesitated, "—the laugh. In your professional judgment. Are they wounds that healed around something, or seeds that happened to land in scorched ground?"

Dev considered the question the way he tried to consider all questions that deserved it: slowly, and without flinching.

"When Ilsa sings," he said, "she holds the sadness *for* someone, and she knows she's holding it, and she chose to keep holding it. That's not a wound. Wounds don't consent." He turned his pen over in his fingers. "But I'll tell you what I actually think, which is less tidy. I think we've been asking the wrong question all along. Gift or wound — the founders asked that same question about the truth itself, sixty years ago, and they decided it was a wound, and they cut it out, and the wound didn't close. It just went somewhere else and waited for children to find it." He looked up at her. "Maybe inheritance isn't a thing you evaluate. Maybe it's a thing you steward. You don't ask whether a river is a gift or a wound. You build the banks. You teach the children to swim."

Astrid was quiet for a long moment. Then she reached into her coat pocket and set something on his desk: a photograph, old, creased white along one fold. Crew members in front of the Meridian's airlock, faces bright with the particular confidence of people who did not yet know how their story would end. One face had been scratched out long ago — but beside it, in fresh ink, in Astrid's handwriting, someone had written a name.

"Ede," Astrid said. "Restored. The tribunal approved the restoration of all forty-one photographs yesterday. They're going to hang them in the school corridor. Where the children walk every day." She paused. "I argued for it. I want you to know I argued for it, and I'd argue again, even though some mornings I stand in that corridor and I can't breathe. Because my grandmother spent her whole life teaching me that we keep what we're given and ask nothing — and she meant silence, and shame, and a locked tin. But the sentence was true anyway. She just never knew what we'd actually be given." She tapped the photograph once, gently, above the restored name. "This is what we were given. All of it. Even the parts that hurt to carry."

She left the photograph on his desk — the archive had copies, she said; this one was for the teacher, for the classroom, for the record that lived outside the record — and Dev sat with it after she'd gone, in the failing light, while the drums thumped somewhere far off and the dust motes turned gold.

* * *

The school corridor was hung with the photographs on the last day of term. Forty-one frames, evenly spaced, each with a name plate beneath it in letters large enough for a child to read without stopping. The colony's sculptor had offered to make the frames identical, austere, uniform — and the children, consulted, had refused. Each frame was different. Someone's grandmother contributed fabric. Someone else carved small birds into the corners of four of them, "because Ede liked birds, I know that, I don't know how I know that."

On the morning they were hung, Dev stood at the end of the corridor and watched the children arrive.

They did not hush, as adults had feared. They did not file past in solemn procession. They streamed down the corridor the way children stream anywhere, loud and jostling, backpacks swinging — and then, one by one, unprompted, they began to slow. To stop. To look up. Tomas Reyes planted himself in front of frame seventeen and stood with his hands in his pockets, studying the face there, and Dev saw his lips move and caught, as he passed, the faintest thread of sound: the boy was humming the song. Correctly. To the photograph. Like a greeting.

Amara Sefu stopped at frame twenty-three, tilted her head, and laughed — her laugh, Ede's laugh, low and sudden and breaking upward — and the sound went down the corridor like a struck bell, and three other children laughed too, ordinary laughs, and the corridor filled with all of it mixed together, the living and the carried, indistinguishable now, which was perhaps the entire point.

Anaya found Dev by the window. She was holding a folder of papers — the transcript of message thirty-six, she said, the one with the humming in the background; she and her father had isolated it over five evenings, and she'd written out the notes, and she wanted, if it was all right, to teach it to the class after the break.

"It's a work song," she said, with the gravity of a scholar presenting findings. "They sang it while repairing the hydroponics seals. Message thirty-six is dated eleven days before the lots were drawn. They didn't know yet." She looked up at him. "So it's a happy version. There's a happy version, Papa. Before everything. Should we teach the happy one first?"

Dev crouched down to her height, this daughter of his who had chosen to learn from the record, and felt something in his chest that had no better name than the future arriving.

"Yes," he said. "Teach the happy one first."

Down the corridor, Ilsa Voss had reached the last frame, and she stood before it a long time, and then she raised her hand — not waving, Dev realized, not signaling anyone — just resting her palm flat against the glass, the way you'd rest your hand on a door.

And that evening, when the lamps came on along the ridge path and the beacon's light swept its steady arc over Vesper's Landing, Astrid Nystrom stood her watch beneath it as she always did — but tonight she was not alone on the climb. Word had gone round the colony, plain and unforced: the keeper's rota was open, witnessed, signed. And waiting for Astrid at the beacon's base, holding a thermos and a maintenance manual she was too young to legally possess, was a girl from Dev's own class, who looked up at the sweeping light and said, without a trace of fear:

"I've been having a dream. About the door. But in my dream, it isn't locked anymore. It's just open. And someone inside is asking if we're coming."

Astrid looked at the child for a long moment. Then she shouldered her pack, and held out her hand, and began the climb.
