## 5. What Mira Remembers

The girl wouldn't eat the ration bar until I'd eaten half of mine first.

I want to be clear about what that means on a station where I have, for nine years, been the highest-ranking authority on anything, including the question of whether the coffee is a war crime. I sat cross-legged on the deck of my own galley—my galley, purchased with nine years of solitude and a spine that clicks when I stand up too fast—and I split a protein bar with a child who used to command a deep-space vessel with a crew of forty.

She chewed slowly, watching me the way I'd watch a pressure gauge. Not with suspicion, exactly. With *calibration*.

"You still do the thing," she said.

"What thing?"

"Where you check the seal on your sleeve before you eat. Left, then right. You did it on the Halcyon too. Captain Okafor used to say you'd check your sleeves in a hull breach."

"I've never been on the Halcyon."

"I know." She said it the way you'd tell someone the sky is up. Patient. Almost kind. "Not yet."

Her name was Mira. The Halcyon's manifest listed her as Mira Chen-Voss, forty-one years old at departure, mission specialist in jump dynamics, third in the chain of command. The person sitting on my galley floor with protein paste at the corner of her mouth was maybe eight years old, and she had the eyes of someone who had done the math on a lot of things and hadn't liked any of the answers.

There were eleven of them. Eleven children from a crew of forty, which raised questions I was not ready to have, and the other ten had settled into my station like water finding its level—sleeping in bunks built for one adult each, two to a bunk, small and warm and *there*, filling nine years of silence with the sound of breathing. I'd spent the first night after docking sitting in the corridor outside the berths just listening to it. I'm not embarrassed. Much.

But Mira was the one who talked.

"You're Dominic Quintana," she said now, licking paste off her thumb. "Station keeper. You've been here nine years, four months. You talk to the AI more than you talk to people, and you've named it something you think is a joke but isn't."

"It's absolutely a joke."

"You named it *Bosun*."

"That's a joke."

"You cried when you thought it was going to be decommissioned in year three."

"That is *classified*."

She grinned, and for a second I saw it—the adult in the child's face, the way a house shows through scaffolding. Then the grin faded and she looked at her hands, small and uncalloused, like they'd betrayed her.

"I need to tell you some things," she said. "And you're going to want to not believe me. That's fine. I did the same thing, the first time around."

"The first time around."

"Drink your coffee," Mira said. "This takes a while."

* * *

I was the one who found the anomaly.

Not the pulse—the pulse was Dominic's discovery, and I want that on the record, wherever records go when nobody reads them. I mean the *pattern*. On the Halcyon, in the last year of the mission I remember, I was the one who noticed the beacon's signal had structure nested inside structure, like a letter inside an envelope inside a locked box. The outer layer was navigation data. The middle layer was timing. And the inner layer—

The inner layer was addressed.

Not to a place. To a *person*. We never decoded the name. Captain Okafor said it was compression artifacts. I said compression artifacts don't repeat at intervals matching a human heartbeat. We argued about it for six months, and then the accident happened, and then there were no more arguments because there were no more people to have them.

Here is what I remember, and I remember all of it, which is the problem:

We were forty. We came back through the window early because Okafor wanted to report the inner layer before our comms window closed. The transit went wrong in a way that transit doesn't go wrong—time doesn't *fold*, it isn't supposed to fold—and we came out the far side of our own lives. The ship was fine. The ship was always fine. It was us it unmade. I watched Okafor's hands get smaller. I watched my own reflection do it too, over what felt like years and was, by the ship's clock, eleven days. The ones who didn't make it to childhood didn't die. They *unarrived*. There's a difference, and the difference is that I can still remember their voices, and they can't remember anything at all, because from where they ended up, they never got to start.

Eleven of us stayed old enough to stay. Then younger. Then we docked at a station with a beacon that had been singing to us the whole way home, and a woman opened the airlock who I had seen die.

* * *

"You die in about fourteen months," Mira said.

I had been drinking coffee. I did not spit it out, which I consider one of the finer achievements of my career. I set the bulb down. I checked the seal on my left sleeve, then my right, because apparently I'm a creature of habit even at the end of the world.

"Go on," I said.

"You don't believe me."

"I believe that you believe you."

"That's the worst kind of believing." She pulled her knees up to her chest. In the low light of the galley, with her feet not touching the floor from where she sat on the bench, she looked like any kid at any sleepover telling any ghost story. That was the trick of it. That was what made it land. "It starts with the fleets. Two of them. They come because of the Halcyon—because of *me*, what I know. And they both want the beacon, and neither of them will say so out loud, and you—"

She stopped.

"You," she said again, softer. "You tell them you have authority. You make up orders. There's a directive—you cite a directive from an admiralty that doesn't exist anymore, and it works, it works *so well*, and for a while everyone stands down and it's almost—"

"Almost what?"

"Almost like being saved." She looked at me. "And then the window opens. And there's a fight. Not a big one. That's the awful part, how small it is. A misread signal. Someone fires a warning shot that isn't a warning. And the beacon—the beacon tries to stop it, tries to *talk them down*, and it can't, because nobody taught it the words, because the one person who could have—"

"Mira."

"You're in the docking arm," she said. "You're trying to get the children to the shelter, and you get me there first, and you go back for the others because that's what you do, you go back, you've been going back for nine years for a station nobody sent you to—" Her breath hitched, a small terrible sound, eight years old and forty-one years deep. "And the arm decompresses. And I watch it on the feed. And you're checking your sleeve seals. Left, then right. Like it matters. Like anything—"

"Mira." I said it harder than I meant to. The galley was very quiet. Somewhere down the corridor, another child laughed at something Bosun said, and the sound came through the bulkhead like light through a crack. "How long have you known this? How long have you been carrying—"

"Since I was forty-one," she said. "Since before I was eight. Since before I was born, from a certain point of view, which I don't recommend." She wiped her face with her sleeve, briskly, the way adults do when they've decided crying is a task to be completed. "I've had this memory longer than I've had these teeth. Do you understand what that's like? Do you understand that I've *grieved* you? I did the whole thing. The funeral in my head. And now I'm sitting in your galley eating your terrible ration bar and you're *alive* and you check your sleeves the same way and I don't—"

She didn't finish. She didn't need to. I've spent nine years talking to an AI, and one thing Bosun never taught me but solitude did: the sentences people don't finish are the ones they've finished a thousand times alone.

"Okay," I said. "Okay. Here's what we're going to do. You're going to tell me everything. All of it. Dates, names, which bulkheads blow, which captain fires first, all of it. And I'm going to write it down. And then—"

"And then you'll decide whether to believe me."

"And then I'll decide what to *do*," I said. "Believing is above my pay grade."

That got the ghost of the grin back. "You always say that. Said that. Will say that." She rubbed her eyes. "Verb tense is a nightmare on this ship."

"You're not on the ship. You're on my station."

"Yeah," Mira said, and looked around the galley—at the coffee stains, the magnetized photos I'd printed of nowhere, the tally marks I'd scratched inside the cabinet door for years I didn't log anywhere official. "That's the other thing I remember," she said quietly. "In the end, this station is the only thing anybody agrees on. Everybody wants it. Nobody knows why. And it's because of you."

"I'm a maintenance contractor."

"You're the lighthouse keeper," she said, "and the ships are coming, and one of them is coming for the light."

I told myself, that night, in my bunk, with eleven children breathing in the berths below and a dead woman's memory of my death folded into a child's voice—it was a lot of company for one skull—I told myself that a girl's nightmare wasn't evidence. That trauma dressed itself in prophecy. That I had spent nine years alone and was therefore, medically speaking, the easiest mark in the system for a story with me at the center of it.

Then I pulled up the beacon logs, because I am a creature of habit, and I checked the pulse anomaly, the one aimed outward, the one I'd found in the archive and filed under *pending*.

It had a nested structure. Three layers deep.

And the innermost layer, repeating at intervals I measured twice, matching a resting human heartbeat—

It wasn't addressed to a person.

It was addressed to *Quintana*.

* * *

I know how this sounds. I was there, in the galley, saying it, and I know how it sounded.

She didn't flinch. That's the first thing you learn about her, if you're me, in either order: she doesn't flinch. Okafor used to say Mira had been through the jump window so many times in simulation that reality had stopped being able to surprise her, only disappoint her. I watched her listen to herself tell the future to a woman she'd watched die, and I understood that the flinch had been spent already, somewhere in the eleven days the ship took to unmake us, and what was left was just the reporting. Just the work.

"You wrote it down," she said, when I finished.

"I write everything down."

"You always did. Your logs were the only ones on the Halcyon that read like a person wrote them. Okafor made us read them in training. 'This,' she'd say, 'is what it looks like when someone's paying attention.'" A pause. "She's not one of the eleven. I have to tell you that now so you don't ask later. Okafor unarrived in the first hour. She held the helm until her hands were too small for the board. She made me promise to get the rest home."

"Is that what you're doing?"

"That's what I'm doing."

"Even if home is fourteen months from now and I'm not in it."

Mira looked at her hands. "Especially then."

I want to be honest in this log, wherever it ends up, whoever ends up reading it, if anyone does: I was not thinking about fleets or windows or the beacon's three-layered heartbeat. I was thinking that a child had grieved me, and that the grief was mine now, handed across a galley bench like the other half of a ration bar, and that I could not give it back.

"You said I make up orders," I said. "From a dead admiralty."

"You do. It works."

"And in your version, it works *almost* well enough."

"Almost," she said. "The word of the day."

"So the variable," I said, and I could feel my own pulse doing its patient, mortal work, "isn't the lie. The lie holds either way. The variable is what happens after the window opens. What I do. What I don't do."

Mira looked up at me then, and for a moment she was neither eight nor forty-one but something older than both—the witness, the survivor, the last page of a report nobody wanted to file.

"You go back for the others," she said. "That's the constant. In every version, you go back. I'm not here to stop you from being you. I'm here because maybe this time, there'll be something left to go back *to*."

I didn't sleep after that. I sat in the observation blister with the coffee I was no longer allowed to call terrible, watching the jump window hang in the dark like a held breath, and I thought: *fourteen months*. I thought: *left sleeve, right sleeve*. I thought about how I'd spent nine years logging hours nobody read, and how the universe had apparently been reading them the whole time, and had sent me a reviewer.

The beacon pulsed. Outward. Patient. On the beat of a human heart.

Down the corridor, a child cried out in her sleep—one of the others, not Mira—and I was moving before I'd decided to move, and I caught myself in the hatchway with my hand on the seal, checking it, left then right, and I understood that whatever was coming had already arrived.

It had arrived the day I opened that airlock. It had arrived nine years ago, maybe. It had arrived the moment somebody, somewhere, decided that a station at the edge of a window needed a keeper, and picked a name.

In the morning, I told Bosun to wake me if anything decelerated into sensor range.

Bosun said: "Already has. Two somethings. Bearing zero-one-seven and one-nine-three. They're not answering hails."

They were coming for the light.

They were coming for me.
