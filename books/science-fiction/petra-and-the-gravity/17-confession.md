## 17. Confession

There is a particular quality to the silence of a station that is about to stop being yours.

I'd lived with Petra's silences for nine years—the hum of the coolant loops, the tick of the beacon's phase-lock, the way the AI cleared its throat with a sound like a spoon in an empty cup when it wanted my attention. I knew them the way you know a house you've stopped seeing. But this silence was different. This was the silence of a held breath.

On the display above me, the jump window had begun to open.

It didn't look like much, honestly. Nine years of imagining, and what I got was a smudge of wrongness against the stars, a lens-shaped absence where the background radiation bent in a way background radiation has no business bending. The beacon was singing to it—a structured pulse I hadn't ordered, counting down from a number I couldn't parse, in a mathematics that made my teeth ache the way a filling does before a storm.

And around that smudge, arranged like chess pieces in the final act of a very tired game, were forty-one warships.

Concord on one side. Meridian on the other. Both of them obeying me.

Both of them obeying a lie.

* * *

Let me back up, because confession is a discipline, and I intend to do it properly.

My name is Dominic Quintana. I am the sole caretaker of Relay Station Petra, a posting nobody applied for, in a system nobody charted, maintaining a beacon whose purpose appears in no document I have ever been able to find. For nine years I logged my hours into an archive nobody read. I calibrated. I replaced seals. I talked to the AI, whose name is—was—simply PETRA, because whoever built this place had a genius for the unimaginative.

And eleven days ago, when two fleets arrived and demanded the beacon's codes from a woman with no rank, no authority, and no plan, I invented one.

I invented Rear Admiral E. Vashti of the Ninth Deep Survey Admiralty, defunct these sixty years. I invented Emergency Directive 114-C, granting custodial authority to the senior station officer of record—which was me, by a margin of one. I invented a chain of command with exactly one link, and I am the link.

It worked. That was the horror of it. It kept working. Concord's Captain Oyelaran saluted me. Meridian's Captain Reyes—a woman who could smell weakness through vacuum, I'd have sworn—accepted my quarantine order with a nod. Two fleets, forty-one ships, eleven thousand personnel, all of them holding their fire because a caretaker in a patched thermal suit told them a dead admiralty would be very disappointed in them.

And now the window was opening, and the beacon's makers were answering, and Voss—the Concord records officer with the face of a man doing arithmetic he enjoyed—was moving on my station with a boarding party, and Mira, nine years old and forty years old and formerly the captain of a ship that had aged her backward into childhood, had told me with the terrible clarity only children and the dying are permitted: *In the version I remember, you lied better. And everyone died.*

* * *

I found her in the hydroponics bay, because of course I did. The children had colonized it the way children colonize anywhere soft and green. Mira was sitting under the tomato vines with her knees drawn up, watching the window's smudge on the wall display with an expression no nine-year-old should own.

"You're going to do it," she said. Not a question.

"I'm going to try."

"It won't work." She said it flatly, without cruelty. That was the worst part—she'd stopped trying to spare me. "In the other time, you doubled down. You invented more orders. You were very good at it. You bought three more days, and then the window opened and nobody knew what it wanted, and Concord fired on Meridian because Voss told Oyelaran you'd sold the beacon to them, and Meridian fired back because they already believed it, and the makers' ship came through into a battlefield and left again, and—" She stopped. Her hands were fists in her lap. "It took the Halcyon with it when it went. What was left of the Halcyon."

I crouched down to her level. My knees cracked. Nine years of ladder-work.

"And this time?"

"This time I don't know. This is the part I never saw. Because in the other time, you never told the truth." She looked up at me, and her eyes were old in a way that had nothing to do with her body. "I used to think that was the difference. One honest sentence. That's all it ever takes to fork a life."

"One honest sentence," I said. "I've got a whole broadcast prepared. You're telling me I only need the first line?"

"I'm telling you the first line is the only one that's yours." She unfolded, stood, and did something that undid me completely: she straightened my collar, the way a captain does to a subordinate before a hearing, or a parent does before a funeral. "Whatever happens after you say it—whatever they do to you—I want you to know that in every version I remember, even the bad ones, you were the person who stayed. That's not nothing. That might be everything."

"Mira—"

"Go," she said. "You have a station to confess to."

* * *

The comm room had one chair, and I had sat in it for nine years talking to no one. Now the displays showed me faces: Oyelaran on the left, gray and precise, her flag bridge arranged behind her like a stage set. Reyes on the right, arms crossed, her flotilla's running lights strobing behind her in a pattern I recognized as a Meridian readiness signal. And below them, smaller, Voss, in a Concord boarding skiff forty meters off my hull, close enough that I could see the arithmetic still running behind his eyes.

"Station Petra," Oyelaran said, "your beacon is transmitting an unauthorized countdown. Explain."

"Station Petra," Reyes said over her, "your window is destabilizing on a schedule consistent with a mass transit event. Explain."

"Station Petra," Voss said, quiet, and I felt the hook go in, "your Directive 114-C does not exist. I've checked every archive Concord holds. I've checked the ones it doesn't hold. Explain *that*."

There it was. The moment. In every version of the last eleven days I'd rehearsed, I had a deflection ready—another layer of fiction, another invented signature, another dead admiral to hide behind. My mouth knew the shapes of the lies. They fit like old boots.

I took a breath, and took the boots off.

"My name," I said, "is Dominic Quintana. I am the caretaker of this station, and I am about to waste your time, because everything else I've told you is a waste of time, and I'd like to stop."

The silence that followed had texture. I could feel forty-one ships listening.

"There is no Directive 114-C. There is no Ninth Deep Survey Admiralty—or if there was, I never served it, and neither did anyone else. Rear Admiral Vashti is a woman I invented in about four seconds while Captain Oyelaran's hail was still playing, because I am a relay caretaker with no rank and no authority, and the alternative to lying to you was watching you shoot each other over a station neither of you understands. I forged the directive. I forged the signatures. I forged the chain of command, and the chain has one link, and it's me, and I am so far out of my depth that I can see the surface from here."

Reyes's arms came uncrossed. Oyelaran's jaw did something small and structural.

"I don't know what the beacon is for," I went on, because once the dam breaks you might as well swim in the flood. "I've kept it for nine years and I don't know. I don't know who built it. I don't know what's coming through that window. The Halcyon's crew can tell you things I can't—they've seen a version of this that went wrong, and they were children when they got here, and I have been trying to keep them fed and safe and un-traumatized while pretending to be an admiral, and I have failed at approximately all of it. The only true thing I have ever told either of your fleets is that you should not fire on each other. I said it for the wrong reasons, in a voice that wasn't mine, under a name that doesn't exist. But I said it. And you listened. And I am asking you—God, I am *begging* you—to keep listening, just once, to the true version."

I stopped. My hands were shaking. On the display, the countdown smudge pulsed, indifferent.

"There," I said. "That's the confession. Do your worst. I'll be here. I'm always here."

* * *

What happens in the silence after a confession is that you find out what you actually believe.

I had expected fury. I had expected Oyelaran to cut the channel and Reyes to start moving ships. I had expected Voss to smile the smile of a man whose leverage had just matured. I had braced for all of it, and instead—

Instead, Captain Oyelaran looked at Captain Reyes across a channel they'd refused to share for eleven days, and said, "Meridian Actual. Did you know?"

"I suspected," Reyes said slowly. "The directive's formatting was wrong. Pre-Consolidation style. Nobody under sixty writes like that." A pause, and then, dry as vacuum: "I assumed she was a plant. Concord plant, badly trained."

"I assumed she was Meridian's," Oyelaran said.

And then—this is the part I will be turning over for the rest of my life—*they both laughed*. Short, bitter, exhausted, the laugh of two people who have been staring at each other across a gun sight for eleven days and have just been told the gun was never loaded. It wasn't forgiveness. It wasn't peace. It was the sound of two professionals discovering they'd been played by an amateur, and discovering, in the same instant, that they'd both wanted an excuse to stop.

"You lied to two fleets," Reyes said, turning to me, "to keep us from killing each other."

"Yes, Captain."

"Over a station you don't understand."

"Yes, Captain."

"And the countdown. The window. The ship that's coming." Her eyes narrowed. "What do you actually know?"

"Nothing," I said. "That's the whole of my expertise. Nine years of nothing, maintained daily."

The channel went quiet again, but it was a different quiet. And then Oyelaran said, in the tone of a woman reading a report she doesn't yet believe, "Concord Actual to all units: hold position. Hold fire. Pending—pending *further developments*." The pause where *Directive 114-C* should have gone was the loudest thing I'd ever heard.

"Meridian Actual concurs," Reyes said. "Hold position. Hold fire." And then, to me, quietly, off the fleet channel: "You're still a fraud, Quintana."

"Yes, Captain."

"But you're the fraud both of us obeyed. That's going to matter. I don't know how yet. But it's going to matter."

* * *

Voss didn't laugh.

I watched his face on the small display—the only face that hadn't moved—and I understood, with the clarity that comes only after you've burned your own disguise, that I had just destroyed the only thing he'd come here for. Leverage over a fraud is worth a career. Leverage over a confessed fraud is worth nothing. I had, in the space of one broadcast, spent his currency in front of both fleets.

"Officer Voss," I said, "your boarding party is welcome aboard. There's nothing left to seize, but the tea is decent."

"Station Petra," he said, and his voice was careful, "you understand that confession doesn't erase the record. Eleven days of false authority. Fleet movements. Resource expenditures—"

"Voss." It was Oyelaran, cutting across him, and something in her voice made even me sit straighter. "Stand down your skiff."

"Captain, with respect, the legal—"

"The legal question," Oyelaran said, "is whether a fabricated order that prevented a fleet engagement constitutes a crime or a service, and that question is *above your pay grade and mine*, and it will be decided by people who were not here. Stand. Down. Your skiff."

The pause lasted four seconds. I counted. Then Voss said, "Skiff holding," and his face went carefully blank, and I thought: *he hasn't decided anything. He's just moved the decision to a quieter room.* Some men you defeat. Voss you merely interrupt.

* * *

The window pulsed.

Not the slow countdown pulse—the beacon's rhythm, which I'd learned like a heartbeat—but something new, something that came through the station's hull as much as through the speakers, a pressure in the teeth, a change in the weight of the air. PETRA's voice arrived with the spoon-in-the-cup sound I'd learned to dread.

"Dominic. The beacon has received a reply."

I stood in the comm room, stripped of every rank I'd ever worn, real or invented, and looked at the display where the smudge of wrongness had begun—very slightly, unmistakably—to *brighten*.

"Put it through," I said.

"It isn't words," PETRA said. "It's a signature. Arrival telemetry. Something is matching our beacon, pulse for pulse." A pause, the length of which told me the station AI was frightened too, if machines can be, and after nine years I will testify that they can. "Dominic. It is answering *us*. It is answering as if we called it. As if someone here asked it to come."

The display brightened. Behind me, in the corridor, I could hear the children running toward the comm room, small feet on metal, Mira's voice calling my name.

And on the fleet channels, forty-one ships went to alert at once—both fleets, all of them, every gun and every grudge suddenly irrelevant—because whatever was coming through the window was no longer a countdown.

It was a door, and it was opening, and everyone in the room had heard the knock.
