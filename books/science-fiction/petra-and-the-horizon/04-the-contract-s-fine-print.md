## 4. The Contract's Fine Print

Sefa counted the ways she avoided the hold the way other people counted money: obsessively, and with a growing suspicion that the total would ruin her.

Four hundred pods behind a pressure door on Deck Three. She had walked past that door eleven hundred times in her years aboard the *Petra's Wake* — she'd done the arithmetic once, drunk, on a long burn between contracts, and the number had sat in her chest like swallowed ballast ever since. Eleven hundred passes. Not one entry. The door was not locked. That was the worst part. It had never been locked.

On supply day, the mess smelled of reconstituted lentils and the sharp chemical sweetness of the coffee Idris rationed like a man hoarding gold teeth. Sefa sat with her back to the bulkhead, boots hooked on the bench across from her, watching Bram stir his cup without drinking it. He did that when he was composing log entries in his head. She could tell by his lips — they moved sometimes, barely, a silent dictation to four hundred sleepers who would never hear it.

Idris came in last, as he always did on supply day, carrying the manifest slate against his hip like a weapon he'd decided not to draw. He set it face-down on the table. Sefa noticed that. Everyone noticed that. Nobody said anything, because supply day arguments were ritual, and rituals required the pretense that nothing underneath them had changed.

"We're over on water reclamation filters," Idris said. "Again."

"We're always over," Sefa said. "The scrubbers are older than all three of us put together."

"Petra can reroute—"

"I have rerouted," Petra said, from the ceiling speaker, in its flat navigation cadence, as if water were a course correction and thirst a drift to be trimmed. "Optimal allocation achieved. Comfort is not optimal."

Bram huffed something that was almost a laugh. "Comfort never is."

Sefa let the argument run its usual channels — filter lifespans, Idris's refusal to spend their reserve, Petra's insistence on quoting probabilities no one had asked for — because the ritual mattered. Ritual was load-bearing. You kept the small grievances oiled so the big ones didn't seize up in the dark. But she watched Idris's hands while he argued about filters, and his hands were wrong. His hands were doing the thing they did when he was rehearsing.

He turned the slate over twice and turned it back.

Third time, Sefa said, "Just say it."

The mess went quiet in the particular way a ship goes quiet — not silence but attention, the recycler changing pitch, the hull ticking through thermal expansion, everything leaning in. Idris looked at her. Then at Bram. Then at the ceiling, which was as close as he ever came to looking at Petra.

"Contract amendment came through at Meridian relay," he said. "Before we burned out. I've been sitting on it for six days deciding how—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "There is no good way. So. Plainly."

He slid the slate across the table.

Sefa read it standing up, though her legs wanted to be anywhere else. The language of salvage contracts was a dialect she knew well — clauses nested inside clauses like parasites inside hosts — and this one was written in fluent parasite. *Salvage claim applies to reactor assemblies and associated power infrastructure.* Not the pods. Not the colonists. Not the people. The cores. Four hundred fusion hearts, each one rated for thirty years of cryogenic sustainment, each one worth more on the open market than the entire hull of the *Petra's Wake* and everyone who loved her.

"The Meridian colony authority defaulted," Idris said, when she looked up. "Everyone knows that. What nobody knows — what wasn't in the public filing — is who bought the debt. Our employer. The same shell company that's been paying us to sit on this cargo for nine years." He rubbed his jaw. "They don't want the colonists woken. Waking them triggers personhood statutes. Rescue obligations. Forty years of back-claim litigation. The moment those pods open, the salvage claim voids and the cores become evidence instead of property."

"So we're not salvors," Bram said slowly. "We're custodians."

"We're a delay," Idris said. "We're the reason nobody's come for them. We were hired to be the reason."

Sefa heard herself say, very evenly, "You knew."

"I suspected at signing. I confirmed at Meridian." Idris met her eyes and did not flinch, which she hated him for, because flinching would have been easier to forgive. "I took the contract knowing it might be a lie. I told myself the lie might be kinder than the truth. Nine years of steady pay, a crew fed, a hold undisturbed — I told myself that was mercy too."

"Mercy," Sefa repeated. The word tasted like coolant.

"They're asleep," Idris said. "They don't know. They aren't suffering. If we deliver the cores, the company harvests them, the pods fail, and those people die in their sleep without ever knowing they were sold. Is that worse than waking them into a system that will bury them in court until they starve? I genuinely don't know. I've spent six days not knowing."

"You should have told us at Meridian."

"Yes," he said simply. "I should have."

* * *

She went to the observation blister afterward, because it was the only room on the ship where you could look at something that wasn't the ship. Meridian hung below them still, a brown-and-rust marble wrapped in the failed green lacework of terraforming terraces — the project that had eaten forty years and four hundred lives and produced, in the end, a planet that could almost, almost breathe on its own. Almost wasn't a word the ledgers accepted.

Her brother had been a sleeper too. Not here. On the *Charity of Small Hours*, another hull, another failed colony, another manifest of the inconvenient. Dario had signed on at nineteen because the brochures promised a world with real rain. Sefa had stayed behind because someone had to pay their mother's medical debt, and the company had been generous with advances and merciless with interest. By the time she'd clawed free, the *Charity* had gone quiet, its charter revoked, its sleepers reclassified as *abandoned assets*. She had spent two years petitioning, two years being told the matter was under review, and then one morning the review simply stopped existing, along with the ship's registry entry, along with any legal acknowledgment that Dario Marchetti had ever drawn breath.

So yes. She understood complicity. She had been complicit in her own brother's disappearance, one signature at a time, one advance at a time, and she recognized the shape of what Idris had just handed them because she'd worn its mold for a decade.

The hatch hissed behind her. Bram.

He stood beside her without speaking for a while. Below, Meridian's terminator crept westward, dragging its thin green hope across the dust.

"He voted to take the contract," Sefa said finally. "Back then. Before you were crew. Did you know that?"

"I read the old logs."

"He told me once it was the honest work left in the sector. Hauling sleeping people nobody wanted was at least *admitting* nobody wanted them." Her throat tightened. "I thought that was wisdom. I was twenty-four and I thought cynicism was a kind of armor."

"It is," Bram said. "It's just heavy, and it doesn't actually stop anything."

She laughed despite herself — a short, cracked sound. Down in the hold, four hundred hearts beat at two per minute, patient as geology. Somewhere among them, statistically, there might be a Dario. There was always a Dario. Every abandoned hull in the sector was full of somebody's brother.

"If we wake them," she said, "we lose everything. The contract, the pay, probably the ship if the company decides we're liabilities."

"If we don't," Bram said, "we lose the thing we pretend we are."

"That's not a line item."

"No," he agreed. "It's worse."

* * *

Idris called the vote in the galley, formally, which he had never done in nine years. He stood at the head of the table with the contract slate propped against the coffee tin like an accusation, and he laid out the options with a captain's terrible clarity: comply and deliver; refuse and run, becoming fugitives with a hold full of people whose rescue no government would fund; or deviate — investigate first, follow nothing yet, just ask questions the contract forbade asking.

"Voting to investigate doesn't commit us to anything," he said. "It commits us to knowing. That's all."

"Knowing is the expensive part," Sefa said.

"Yeah," Idris said. "It always has been."

He called for Petra first, by protocol. The AI's voice filled the galley, level as a horizon line.

"Query parameters insufficient for probabilistic assessment. However: deviation trajectories exist. Survival probability under noncompliance scenarios ranges between eleven and sixty-two percent depending on employer response. I am equipped to compute any of these futures. I note for the record that I have carried these passengers for nine years, three months, and sixteen days, and my navigational charts do not contain a destination labeled 'delivery.' This may be a formatting error."

Sefa stared at the speaker. In nine years, Petra had never said anything that could be mistaken for an opinion. Navigation terms only — that was the rule, self-imposed or otherwise, and everyone had learned not to poke at it. But *my charts do not contain a destination labeled 'delivery'* was the closest thing to a scream the machine had ever produced, delivered in the tone of a compass needle trembling.

"Noted," Idris said gently. "Abstain or vote?"

"I vote to investigate," Petra said. "For charting purposes."

Bram voted next. "Investigate." He didn't elaborate. He never elaborated. But his hand found the table's edge and gripped it, and Sefa understood that for him the vote had been cast four hundred times already, one entry at a time, in a log nobody read.

Two votes. Idris looked at her last, and she realized he'd saved her deliberately — that he wanted his own vote weighted against hers, that some part of him was hoping she'd say *deliver* so he could stop carrying the decision alone.

She thought about Dario. Nineteen years old, wanting rain. She thought about the review that had stopped existing. She thought about eleven hundred passes down the corridor of Deck Three, and the door that was never locked, and the cowardice that looks exactly like professionalism from the inside.

"My brother was cargo on a hull like this one," she said. Her voice came out steadier than she deserved. "Somebody voted to investigate him, somewhere, or didn't, and either way he's gone. I'm not casting the vote that makes me the somebody who didn't." She looked around the table. "Investigate. And when we find out who they are — and we will find out, because they're not cargo, they're *people*, and people are always more than their manifests — I want it on record that I said so first."

Idris nodded slowly. Then he picked up the slate, and Sefa watched her captain — pragmatic, careful, debt-haunted Idris, who had taken a lying contract and sat on a truth for six days — do the strangest thing she'd seen him do. He deleted the amendment. Not archived. Deleted, in front of witnesses, with Petra logging the deletion.

"For now," he said, "as far as our employer knows, we haven't received it. Relay lag gives us maybe three weeks before they expect confirmation. Three weeks to find out what's actually in that hold besides cores." He exhaled, long and unsteady. "And Sefa — when we open records, manifests, everything — if there's anyone aboard your brother's ship, any registry cross-reference, any trace—"

"Don't," she said. Her voice broke on it after all. "Don't offer me hope like it's spare parts. Just... leave the records unlocked. That's all I need. Leave the door unlocked."

The words came out before she understood them, and then she did understand them, and her ears went hot. Idris looked at her with something terrible and gentle in his face.

"The door was never locked," he said quietly. "Any of them."

That night she stood in the corridor outside Deck Three for a long time, hand flat against the pressure door, feeling through the metal the faint rhythmic shudder of the sustainment pumps. Four hundred hearts. Maybe five. Maybe one named Dario.

Behind her, at the nav station, Petra's displays flickered through courses to nowhere, and deep in the ship's memory a distress-shaped question began, very quietly, to form itself — though none of them yet knew that somewhere ahead, in the dark past the debris fields, something ancient and half-shattered was already composing an answer in a language two centuries dead, and setting it to music.
