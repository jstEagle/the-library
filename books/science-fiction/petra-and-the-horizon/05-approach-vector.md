## 5. Approach Vector

I want you to know what it looks like from out here, the place your signal comes from.

You can't see it. I know that. You're four hundred heartbeats slowed to a crawl in the dark of our hold, and the only sky you've ever known is the inside of a pod lid. But I've started narrating things to you again — Sefa would say it's unprofessional, and she'd be right, and I'd do it anyway — because someone should witness this for you, and I'm the one holding the pen.

So: there is a gas giant ahead of us called Vesper-9 on the charts and called nothing at all by anyone who ever loved it, and around its equator, where a ring of ice and stone should be, there is instead a ring of *architecture*. Half of it is gone. The other half is still transmitting.

And the transmission is singing.

* * *

Petra flagged the pattern forty hours after we committed to the burn, back when the source was still an abstraction — a coordinate, a vector, a problem in arithmetic. Now it's a shape filling my viewport, and I understand that everything we thought we knew about the signal was wrong, or rather, was too small.

"It's not just a distress call," Petra said, in her way, which is to say she said it as if reporting weather. "Course correction recommended. Debris density exceeds safe threshold at all standard approach lanes."

"Then find me a non-standard one," Idris said from the command chair, and went back to staring at the ring like a man reading a letter he wasn't sure he wanted to open.

But I was already looking at the waveform. I'd been looking at it for two days. And here is what I found, the thing I haven't said aloud yet because saying it makes it real:

The lullaby isn't hidden in the signal. The lullaby *is* the signal.

* * *

Let me explain, because you deserve the whole of it, even the parts that frighten me.

The distress burst repeats every eleven minutes and six seconds. That's an odd number. Distress protocols use round numbers — sixty seconds, three hundred, anything a machine can count on its fingers. Eleven minutes six seconds is a human number. It's the length of something someone chose.

And when I laid the carrier wave's modulation against the cultural manifest in your cargo file — entry 447, "Lullaby (Meridian Traditional), audio artifact, priority low" — they matched. Not approximately. Exactly. Every rise and fall of the melody, every held note, every rest, encoded in the spacing between the bursts. Someone took the song your mothers sang on Meridian before the terraforming failed, and they built a beacon out of it, and they've been singing it into the dark for long enough that the signal's edge has traveled farther than any ship we know of.

It's a distress call. It's also a map. Because a song has structure, and structure can carry information, and whoever built this wanted to make sure that only people who knew the song could follow it home.

We know the song. It's in your manifest. Which means, whether they intended it or not, they were singing to *you*.

* * *

"Walk me through it," Idris said, and I brought the waveform up on the main display, and I watched his face do the thing it does when he's trying very hard to look unmoved.

"The bursts are the melody," I said. "The gaps between them are the harmony line. And the micro-jitter in the timing — look here, and here — it's not noise. It repeats across cycles. It's a second layer of data riding under the music."

"What kind of data?"

"I don't know yet. But it correlates with the debris field." I pulled up the navigation overlay, the slow-turning cloud of shattered metal and frozen atmosphere around Vesper-9, and set the jitter pattern against it, and there it was, plain as a signature: the timing variations mapped onto clear corridors through the wreckage. Safe passage, written in rests. "They didn't just want to be found. They wanted to be found *navigably*. Anyone who knows the song can thread the needle."

Sefa stood in the doorway with a coolant coupling in her hand and her jaw set the way it gets when she's about to say something she's rehearsed. "Or," she said, "anyone who knows the song gets invited somewhere they shouldn't go."

"Sefa—"

"No. Hear me. A distress call in a dead language was already strange. A distress call that's also a treasure map through a minefield? That's not desperation. That's *design*. Somebody wanted specific visitors, arriving a specific way, and everyone else discouraged." She set the coupling down harder than she needed to. "My brother's hull answered a signal once. You know how that ended."

We all knew how that ended. Nobody said it.

Idris looked at me. "Bram. Can you fly it?"

I thought about lying. I thought about the honest answer, which is that I don't know, which is that no navigator alive has flown a corridor defined by a lullaby, which is that the last time I trusted a rhythm I couldn't verify, I was nine years old and my grandmother was singing to me during a storm on Ceres and the storm won anyway.

"Yes," I said. "If the map stays current. If whatever's transmitting keeps updating the jitter to match the drift." I paused. "Which means it's not a recording. Something over there is still *singing*. Live."

That sat in the room with us for a while, the way heavy things do.

"Then sing back," Idris said finally. "On approach. Let them know we heard."

* * *

Here is what flying the corridor is like. I'm going to tell you in detail because I want you to have it, stored somewhere, even if you never wake to use it.

The debris field around the ring is not random. That's the first thing. Wreckage scatters chaotically, normally — shrapnel physics, no memory. But this field has *lanes*. Broad avenues swept clean, walled by tumbling hulks and sheets of hull plating that catch the light of the gas giant and throw it back amber and bruise-purple. Flying through it feels less like navigating wreckage and more like walking through a ruined cathedral: the destruction is old, deliberate, and somehow still reverent.

The second thing: the field is moving. Slowly, but moving — orbital mechanics doing their patient work, the whole graveyard migrating around the planet on a cycle of years. A static map would be useless within months. So the signal updates. Every repetition of the lullaby carries fresh timing, fresh corridors, the singer adjusting the song to match the drift of the dead.

Which means I don't fly by instruments alone. I fly by ear.

I have Petra feeding me the decoded melody through my headset, and I have the nav display showing me the corridor walls, and I hold the two together in my hands — throttle and yoke, thrust and trust — and when the song holds a note, I hold my line; when it rests, I correct. There's a moment, maybe twenty minutes into the first pass, where I stop translating. Where the music stops being instructions and becomes just *music*, and my hands know what to do the way they know how to breathe, and I am flying a hauler full of sleeping colonists through the corpse of a civilization guided by a children's song, and it is the most beautiful and most terrible thing I have ever done.

I'm sorry. I know I keep using that word. I'll find better ones eventually. For now: I'm sorry, and it's beautiful, and I'm terrified, and I wouldn't turn back if you paid me.

* * *

The closer we get, the more the ring resolves, and the more it resolves, the worse it gets.

From a distance it looked intact — a bright arc against the planet's banded face, catching light along its spine. At ten thousand kilometers the illusion fails. The ring is broken. Not damaged — *broken*, snapped in four places, the severed ends trailing cables and habitat modules like torn ligament. Whole sections hang at wrong angles, their rotation stopped, their windows dark. One segment has drifted inward and is being slowly devoured by the gas giant's upper atmosphere, a smear of glowing metal drawing a line across the clouds like a wound that won't close.

And the surviving sections — the parts still lit, still pressurized, still *alive* — are patched. That's the word that keeps coming back to me. Patched. Hull plates welded over hull plates in mismatched alloys. Solar sails stitched from sailcloth that was clearly something else first. Antennas built from antenna fragments, spires assembled from the bones of other spires. Whoever lives there has spent generations making something whole out of pieces, and the pieces came from the ring itself.

"They ate their own ship," Sefa said quietly, at my shoulder. She'd come to the cockpit without my noticing, which she does when she's worried. "Look at the construction. That's cannibalization. They've been stripping their own infrastructure to keep the rest running."

"You can tell that from here?"

"I can tell that from anywhere." Her voice did something complicated. "I grew up on a station that did the same thing. Slower. We called it maintenance. It was really just deciding, piece by piece, what we could afford to stop being."

I didn't have an answer for that. I logged it instead. That's what I do with things I can't answer — I write them down and address them to you, four hundred sleepers who may never read a word, and I tell myself it counts as testimony.

Maybe someday it will.

* * *

At five thousand kilometers, Petra spoke without being asked, which she almost never does.

"Anomaly," she said. "The signal has changed."

Every head on the bridge turned. On the main display, the waveform re-rendered itself, and I felt my stomach drop before my mind caught up.

The lullaby had gained a verse.

Not new material — the same melody, the same key, but extended, doubled, carrying twice the data. And woven into the new section, unmistakable now that I knew to listen: a counter-melody. A second voice. Two singers, harmonizing, where before there had been one.

"They know we're here," I said. "They've seen us on approach, and they're—" I stopped. Started again. "They're answering. They're singing *with* us."

"With us?" Sefa frowned. "We haven't transmitted yet."

"We did." I pulled the log. Three days ago, per Idris's order, I'd broadcast the lullaby back on a wide beam — a courtesy call, a knock on the door. I'd forgotten about it in the noise of the burn. "Three days ago. At light-speed delay, they'd have received it..." I ran the numbers and my mouth went dry. "They received it. They listened. They learned the harmony. And they've been practicing."

Nobody said anything for a moment. Out the viewport, the ring turned, patched and broken and lit from within, and somewhere inside it, someone had taken a song from a dead world and taught it to a second voice, and sung it back across the gulf to a ship full of strangers.

"That's not a trap," Sefa said, but her voice shook, and I think she was trying to convince herself. "Traps don't harmonize."

"Traps do," Idris said softly, from the command chair. He hadn't moved in an hour. He was watching the ring the way you watch a person sleeping. "The best ones do. That's what bait is — something beautiful, placed exactly where you can't help going." He rubbed his eyes. "Keep flying, Bram. But arm the point defenses, and nobody docks until I say."

"Aye, Captain."

"And Bram?" He waited until I looked at him. "Log everything. Whatever's over there — however this goes — somebody has to remember it straight."

Somebody has to remember it straight. I've been turning that phrase over for hours now, because I hear what's underneath it. Idris knows what I keep in this log. He signed off on the contract that says otherwise. When he says *remember it straight*, he isn't giving me an order. He's asking me for something he can't ask for out loud.

I'm logging it straight, Captain. For what it's worth. For whatever it costs either of us later.

* * *

Final approach. The corridor narrows.

The last stretch of the debris field funnels toward a docking structure on the ring's largest surviving segment — a bay door half again too big for the Petra's Wake, sized for ships that don't exist anymore, framed in scaffolding that has been repaired so many times it's more weld than original metal. The corridor walls close in around us: a shattered tug the size of a village, rotating slowly, its cargo clamps still gripping containers stamped with Meridian Colonial Authority seals. A field of personal effects — I don't know how else to describe it — hundreds of small objects drifting in a loose cloud: tools, toys, a child's jacket turning end over end in the starlight, frost blooming and sublimating off its fabric in a slow breath.

Your people's things. Or their descendants' things. Out here, after two hundred years, I'm not sure the distinction still means what it used to.

The song guides us through the last turns. Note, hold, rest. Hold, rest, note. My hands move and the ship moves and the wrecked cathedral opens its final aisle, and then the bay is in front of us, floodlit from within, and standing in the light —

People.

Small figures at the bay's edge, dozens of them, arranged in a line. Too far to see faces. Close enough to see that they're not armed, or that they're hiding it well. Close enough to see that some of them are children, held by the hand, lifted onto shoulders to see.

And as Petra's Wake crossed the threshold of the bay — as my thrusters fired the last gentle correction and the deck hummed beneath four hundred sleepers and three awake souls — the figures began to sing.

Not the lullaby. The *other* part. The counter-melody, the second voice, the verse that was added three days ago when they heard our broadcast and answered. They sang it live now, unamplified, and the bay's acoustics carried it up through the hull and into the ship and into my bones, and I sat in my chair with my hands hovering over controls I no longer needed and let a song I'd only known as data become, suddenly and irreversibly, a room full of voices.

Sefa made a sound beside me. Small. I didn't look at her. Some mercies are private.

"Petra," I said, and my voice wasn't steady, and I let it not be. "Are we receiving anything else? Any transmission, any telemetry, anything besides the song?"

"One item," Petra said. "Low-bandwidth text, Pre-Collapse Standard. Repeating."

"Put it up."

It appeared on the display, in characters two centuries dead, translated by a language model trained on archives neither of us had ever expected to matter:

**YOU BROUGHT THEM HOME. COME DOWN. WE HAVE SO MUCH TO SHOW YOU — AND ONE THING WE MUST ASK.**

The bay doors began to close behind us, slow and smooth, with the sound of something enormous being made welcome.

Or made captive. I honestly couldn't tell you which, and I've stared at that sentence for a long time, and I still can't.

The song went on. We went down.
