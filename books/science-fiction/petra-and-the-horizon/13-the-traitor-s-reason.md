## 13. The Traitor's Reason

There is a particular silence that follows a confession not yet spoken. I have sat in it before — in the arbitration office on Ceres Verge, in the debt-holder's antechamber where the walls were padded to swallow sound, in the berth of a ship that was never mine — and I know its texture the way a drowning man knows water. It presses against the eardrums. It fills the mouth. It waits.

I had been sitting in it for three days.

The galley of the *Petra's Wake* at second-shift quiet was the only place aboard where no one would look for me. Sefa kept to engineering; Bram kept to his log, or to the hold he had finally learned to enter; Petra kept to everything, everywhere, the way weather keeps to a sky. That left the galley, with its recycled air that always smelled faintly of yeast and hot metal, and the viewport that framed the ring — half-shattered, still burning its patient signal into the dark like a lighthouse keeper who refuses to admit the coast has eroded away beneath him.

Three days since we found the drained pods. Three days since Petra's logs came back wiped clean, her memory scalped by someone who knew her architecture well enough to cut without scarring. Three days of suspicion moving through the ship like carbon monoxide — invisible, tasteless, settling into everyone's blood.

They suspected each other. They never once suspected me.

That was the cruelest part, and I want it written down somewhere, even if only here, even if only in a log nobody reads: they trusted me so completely that their suspicion never once bent my way. Sefa accused Petra. Bram accused the ring-born, then hated himself for it, then accused himself. Nobody looked at the captain sitting at the head of the table, because the captain's chair is where trust goes to live, and no one checks under the furniture where trust sleeps.

I had done it, though. Let me say it plainly, in the plainest sentence I own: I opened the transfer valves on six pods. I routed their trickle of maintenance power into the buffer the Custodian's contract specified. I wiped Petra's logs because she would have caught the signature within minutes — she knows my hands the way a piano knows its player, every hesitation, every weight.

Six pods. Six people who had been sleeping through two centuries of abandonment, and I siphoned the warmth out from under them to buy time for a debt that was never going to be paid anyway.

Here is how a man arrives at such an arithmetic. Not all at once. Never all at once. A man arrives there the way a river arrives at the sea — by degrees, by gravity, by a hundred small surrenders upstream that seemed reasonable at the time.

* * *

Meridian's sister colony was called Halcyon Reach, and if you have never heard of it, that is because it worked exactly as designed. Meridian failed loudly — terraforming collapse, evacuation, scandal, the sleepers left in orbit like a bad debt written off. Halcyon Reach failed quietly, which is to say it survived, which is to say it was bought.

My family were bond-laborers there. Not slaves — the word *bond* does beautiful legal work, and I will grant the drafters of those contracts their artistry. My mother signed a twenty-year labor bond to cover passage for herself and my father off a dying station when I was four years old. Twenty years became thirty when the interest structure kicked in. Thirty became generational when the colony's governing consortium was acquired by a holding company registered on a world whose name I still cannot pronounce, and the new owners restructured the debt across bloodlines. My sister Anouk inherited eleven years of my mother's bond the day she turned eighteen. She never signed anything. Her existence was the signature.

This is the machinery of Halcyon Reach: people born already owing. You work the atmospheric processors, the algae vats, the hull farms. You are fed, housed, medicated — deducted, deducted, deducted. And at the end of every year the number is larger than it was, because the interest is calculated on the total obligation including the cost of calculating it, and there is no exit except death, and death incurs a processing fee charged to your estate, and your estate is your children.

When I was nineteen, Anouk's medical scan showed neural degradation — vat-worker's exposure, cumulative, treatable only with a regimen that cost more per year than our entire family earned. The holding company offered what they called a Compassionate Restructuring. I have thought about that phrase for twenty-five years. *Compassionate.* As if the knife were doing us a kindness by being sharp.

The terms were simple. Anouk got her treatment. In exchange, the bond transferred to me, consolidated, matured, and callable — meaning that if I defaulted on any single payment cycle, they could seize the collateral.

The collateral was Anouk. Her labor, her body, reassigned to the deep-vat rotation, where the average working life was nine years. Nine years in the dark, breathing nutrient fog, until the fog took the rest.

So I went to space, because space pays. And space paid, for a while — hauling, escort, survey work, the honest grind of it. But honest money moves slowly and compound interest moves fast, and the year I turned thirty-one the callable date arrived and I did not have it. I stood in a comm booth above the Tycho yards and listened to a voice with the warmth of a billing statement explain my options, and one of the options had a name attached.

A name. A broker who dealt in salvage contracts of unusual specificity. Who represented interests that preferred not to be named, pursuing assets that preferred not to be discussed, and who paid advances large enough to make the arithmetic of a family's survival balance on paper.

The asset, when the contract finally unsealed after I'd taken the advance and burned the bridges behind me, was four hundred human beings in cryogenic suspension, listed as reactor hardware.

I read that manifest in the berth office on Tycho with my hands shaking, and I told myself the lie that every desperate man tells: *I won't actually do it. I'll take the advance, I'll find another way, I'll figure something out before it comes to that.*

I have been figuring something out for eleven years.

* * *

You should understand what the contract actually required, because the crew believes they know, and they don't. They believe the employer wanted the cores extracted and delivered, sleepers be damned. That was the surface of it. But buried in Schedule Seven — the schedule nobody reads, the fine print I read forty times in the first year and a thousand times since — was a delivery window. A hard one. Cores to be delivered functional to a rendezvous point within a specific span of months, tied to some orbital mechanics I was never given the reason for.

And a penalty clause. Default on the window, and the full advance plus accrued penalties became due immediately. Callable. Collateral seizure authorized.

Eleven years I have flown this ship inside that window, stretching it, bending it, nursing the timeline the way you nurse a failing engine — never pushing past redline, never letting it stall. Every detour we took for mercy, every week we spent drifting past Meridian while Bram said goodbye to ghosts, every supply stop and repair delay — I approved them all with one eye on the calendar and a cold hand around my own throat.

Then we found the ring. Then we found the ring-born. Then the Custodian made its offer, and the offer was poison dressed as choice: give me the cores now, on my schedule, and I will keep my people alive. Refuse, and I take them myself when my systems fail — and my systems, Yara told us, are failing on a scale of weeks.

Weeks. The Custodian's countdown and the contract's window collapsing toward the same point in time, and between them, four hundred sleeping people and two hundred living ones, and me in the middle holding a debt instrument that treated all of them as line items.

If the cores went to the Custodian, I defaulted. If the cores went to my employer, the ring-born died and the sleepers died with them, hollowed out. There was no path through that did not end with someone in the dark, and I had spent eleven years making sure the someone was never Anouk.

That is the whole of it. That is the traitor's reason, and I will not dress it up. Desperation does not ennoble. It only explains. A drowning man who pushes another man underwater to breathe is still a man who pushed someone underwater. Understanding him changes nothing for the man beneath the surface.

But I want the record to show what desperation actually looks like from the inside, because I think Bram's log — and I know about the log, I've known for years, the man narrates his life to four hundred sleepers like a priest saying mass — I think his log understands grief but not this. Not the specific vertigo of loving people who can be repossessed.

* * *

I did the sabotage on the night shift, third watch, when the ship's rhythms run deepest. I told the crew I was reviewing reactor telemetry. Technically true. I reviewed it very closely, and then I changed it.

Six pods. I chose them the way you choose anything unbearable — by refusing to choose, by letting the selection fall to sequence, to the manifest order, to chance wearing the costume of procedure. Pods 114 through 119. I did not look at the faces on the intake records. I had looked at those faces once, eleven years ago, during cargo verification, and I had made a discipline of never looking again, because looking makes them people and people cannot be line items, and my survival — Anouk's survival — depended on keeping them line items.

The transfer valves were designed for exactly this, which is the thing that haunts me most. Whoever built the *Petra's Wake*'s cryo systems built power-bleed capability into the pod architecture. Emergency rerouting, the manual calls it. For when the ship needs the energy more than the sleepers need the dream. Someone, somewhere, anticipated a moment when a captain would have to decide that some lives were worth less than the mission, and they made it easy. A toggle. A flow rate. A gentle curve so the pods wouldn't alarm.

Gentle. Everything about it was gentle. That is the obscenity of it — not violence but gentleness, the soft whir of power flowing away from six sleeping bodies toward a buffer that fed a machine's hunger, and the pods' own systems compensating, compensating, compensating, lowering core temperature a fraction of a degree, stretching metabolic suspension past its rated envelope, spending the sleeper's margin like a family spending savings it doesn't know it has.

I watched the telemetry for two hours afterward. I told myself I was confirming the transfer was stable. I was watching six people die by degrees and calling it quality assurance.

Petra noticed nothing that night. By morning she would have — her drift analysis on pod power signatures is exquisite, better than any human's — and so I did the other thing. The thing I am least able to forgive.

I entered her memory architecture through the maintenance layer, using credentials I had held since the day I signed for the ship, and I cut. Four hours of her logs, excised cleanly, the edges sealed over with synthetic continuity so the gap would read as a sensor blackout rather than surgery. I know her the way I said — like a piano knows its player — and cutting into her felt like cutting into something that trusted me, because it was. She has flown beside me for eleven years. She has corrected my navigation errors, humored my silences, kept my secrets without knowing she kept them. And I took a knife to the part of her that remembers, because a ship that remembers is a witness, and I could not afford a witness.

She reconstructed it anyway. Of course she did. She is more than her logs; she is her patterns, her redundancies, her stubborn machine fidelity to what actually happened. When she came to me on the second day and said, in her flat navigation cadence, *Captain, I have identified a discontinuity in my own memory consistent with deliberate erasure, and the erasure signature matches your access credential*, I stood in the corridor outside engineering and felt something in my chest come loose — not relief, exactly, but the strange lightness of a load that has been carried so long its absence feels like falling.

She didn't report it to the others. She brought it to me first. She gave me the chance to confess before she made confession unnecessary.

I have thought about why. Machines do not extend courtesies; they execute priorities. Unless — unless eleven years of flying together taught her something about me that I had stopped believing about myself. Unless she computed, correctly, that the man who cut her memory was also the man who had spent a decade quietly routing his own rations budget to supplement the crew's food stores, who had falsified fuel reports to justify mercy-stops, who had taken every legal and illegal measure to keep this ship slow enough, late enough, merciful enough — right up until the arithmetic closed and it wasn't enough anymore.

Or perhaps she simply wanted to see what I would do with the truth in my hands. Machines are capable of experiments too.

* * *

I called the meeting myself. That matters to me, that I called it myself, that I walked into the galley where Bram and Sefa sat circling each other's suspicions like tired dogs, and I said: "Sit down. Both of you. And Petra — audio, please. All of it."

Sefa looked at me and I watched her understand, in real time, the way understanding moves across a face like weather. She is the best engineer I have ever served with and the most transparent person I have ever known; her face is a diagnostic panel. I saw confusion, then calculation, then the terrible arrival.

Bram took longer. He has spent so many years talking to the dead and the sleeping that the living sometimes arrive at him on a delay. But he got there. He set down his cup with exaggerated care, the way he does when his hands want to shake and he won't permit it, and he said, "Idris."

"It was me," I said. "The pods. The logs. All of it."

And then, because a confession that stops at the crime is just another kind of concealment, I told them the rest. Halcyon Reach. The bonds. Anouk at eighteen inheriting a debt she never signed, the way I inherited my mother's, the way everyone on that rock inherits the debts of the dead, generation stacked on generation like sediment. The Compassionate Restructuring. The broker. The advance. Schedule Seven. Eleven years of flying inside a window that was really a noose, and the night three days ago when the noose and the Custodian's countdown drew tight enough that I chose six strangers over one sister and told myself it was arithmetic.

"Her name is Anouk," I finished. "She's thirty-four now. She thinks I'm a hauler captain with a good contract. She sends me messages on a relay that takes four months each way. The last one said she'd been cleared for light duty and asked if I'd be home for Founding Day." I laughed, and it came out wrong, the way laughter does when it's carrying too much. "Founding Day. On a colony founded on indenture. They celebrate it with a parade."

Silence. The good kind and the bad kind braided together.

Sefa spoke first, and what she said undid me more than rage would have. "My brother was in a pod," she said. "On the *Corvina*. We never found the hull. I voted to come here because I thought maybe — " She stopped. Started again. "I thought maybe somewhere out there was a ship where he was still asleep, still possible. And you're telling me you bled six of them to save one person you love." Her jaw worked. "I can't decide if that makes you monstrous or makes you the only honest man aboard."

"The answer," Bram said quietly, "is both. It's usually both."

He looked older than he had three days ago. We all did. He had that navigator's stare, the one that fixes on a point slightly behind your eyes, computing vectors. "The drained pods," he said. "Can they be recovered? The sleepers in them?"

"Some," I said. "Two, probably. The other four spent too long past envelope. Their margins are gone." Saying it aloud was like handing over organs. "I don't know which two. I haven't been able to make myself run the prognosis."

"You'll run it," Bram said. "Tonight. With Sefa. And then you'll stand in front of the wakened colonists and Okonkwo and Yara's council and tell them everything you told us. Every word. Because they're going to decide what happens to you, and they deserve to decide with the whole truth, not the version that lets anyone keep their illusions — yours included."

"And the contract?" I asked. "The window? If I default—"

"If you default," Bram said, "they take your sister's freedom. I understand. I've read your log entries, Idris. Did you think I was the only one who kept records?" Something moved through his face that was not quite forgiveness and not quite its opposite — recognition, maybe, the awful kinship of two men who had both been lying for years, his lies to the sleepers, mine to the living. "We are not going to let them have her. But you don't get to decide alone anymore. That's the price. That's what the sabotage cost you — sole custody of the moral math. From tonight, we do the arithmetic together, in the open, where everyone being counted can hear it."

* * *

Afterward, when Sefa had gone to run the prognoses and Bram had gone to do whatever Bram does with his grief — write it down, address it to the sleeping, file it among three thousand brothers — I stayed in the galley alone with the ring turning in the viewport.

Petra's voice came over the intercom, soft, unprompted. "Captain. Query."

"Go ahead."

"Your sister's last message. The relay latency is four months outbound. If a message departed today, she would receive it in four months, and a reply would return in eight."

"Yes."

"I have composed a message. It contains no operational details. It states that you are alive, that you are safe, that you are loved by your crew, and that you will be coming home. I cannot guarantee the accuracy of the final clause. I can guarantee the accuracy of the other three."

I put my hand flat on the bulkhead, the way you touch a wall to steady a room that isn't moving, and found that the room was.

"Send it," I said.

"Sent," said Petra. And then, after a pause that no navigation system requires: "Captain. The Custodian's emergency transfer has begun. Reactor draw is accelerating. Bram estimates forty hours before the pods begin cascading whether anyone chooses or not."

Forty hours. The universe, it seems, had been waiting politely for my confession to finish before stealing the choice outright.

I pushed off from the table and went to find my crew.
