## 17. What We Owed

I have rehearsed this confession in the dark of my bunk for eleven years, and every rehearsal ended the same way: with me stopping at the first hard sentence and telling myself there would be a better time, a safer audience, a version of the truth that hurt less on its way out.

There is no such version. I checked all of them.

So I do not rehearse it now. I stand in the ring's great assembly hall — a cathedral of salvaged hull plating where the ceiling is a lattice of old cargo struts and the light comes through cracked viewports in long amber blades — and I ask Petra to open my log to them. All of it. Every entry from one to three thousand forty-one.

Petra's voice fills the hall in navigation terms, because that is the only language she trusts herself with: *Playback initiated. Duration, approximately ninety-one hours at standard cadence. Confirm.*

"Confirmed," I say, and my voice cracks on the second syllable like river ice.

Yara sits in the front row with her hands folded, an old woman made of wire and patience. Behind her, the ring-born — two hundred and change, from elders who remember the first hunger to children who have never seen a planet's surface except as a smear of storm below. And beside them, wrapped in blankets that smell of recycled air and someone else's history, the fifty wakened sleepers, blinking in light their bodies were never told to expect. Okonkwo sits among them, upright as a mast, his eyes on me with the particular attention of a man who has spent his life reading documents for the lie underneath the truth.

Sefa stands at my shoulder. She does not touch me, but her presence has weight, the way a hand on your back has weight even before it lands.

Idris stands apart. He has stood apart since the reactor room, since the coolant line, since the radiation began writing its slow red sentence into his blood. The med unit in the ring's clinic gives him nine days. He has not argued with the number. He only said, *Then I'd best spend them honestly,* which is the most honest thing he has said in the eleven years I've known him.

The log begins to play.

* * *

You cannot imagine what it is like to hear your own voice for ninety-one hours.

It starts small. Entry one: *Day one of contract. Four hundred cryosleepers aboard, Meridian colonial charter, destination lapsed. Awaiting reassignment.* My voice then was younger by a decade and already lying, because awaiting reassignment was a phrase the company had given us and I had repeated it without tasting it. The sleepers were not awaiting anything. They were abandoned. The terraforming project had failed; the colony had folded; the charter had been quietly voided in a bankruptcy filing forty pages long; and four hundred human beings had been reclassified from *passengers* to *cargo* in a clause so dry you could strike a match off it.

Entry twelve: *Ran diagnostics on the hold today. All pods green. Remarkable engineering, these units. Could outlast us all.*

Entry sixty: *Sefa won't come down here anymore. Can't blame her. It's like walking through a cemetery where everyone's still breathing.*

Entry two hundred and six: the first time I lied outright. *No developments. Cargo stable.* As if they were crates of machine parts. As if stability were the same thing as peace.

The hall listens. That is the terrible part — they listen. Nobody leaves. The ring-born, who ate their own ships and archives to stay alive, who stripped their own cryobays when the power ran low and carried the names of those pods like stones in their mouths, sit and hear how a man on a passing ship logged their ancestors' siblings as *stable* and went up to eat dinner. The wakened sleepers hear themselves described in tonnage. Okonkwo hears the word *cargo* four hundred and eleven times over ninety-one hours, because I counted, because Petra counted for me once and I could never uncount it.

A child in the third row falls asleep against Yara's shoulder somewhere around entry eight hundred, and no one wakes her, and somehow that is the most forgiving thing that happens all day.

Entry one thousand one hundred and four: *Dreamed about them again. In the dream the hold doors open and they're all awake and they ask me one question and I don't know what it is because I always wake up first.* I remember recording that one. I remember thinking no one would ever hear it, that the log was a sealed room inside a sealed ship inside the sealed dark, and that confession addressed to no one is just grief wearing a uniform.

Entry two thousand seven hundred and thirty-three: the distress signal. My voice changes there. You can hear the past knock. *Pre-Collapse Standard. It's a lullaby, Sefa. It's the lullaby from the manifest. Someone out there is singing to them.*

And then the entries come faster, closer together, the days shortening as we burned toward the ring, and the lies get thinner, and finally there is the entry where I stop narrating and start confessing in real time — the trap, Custodian, the arithmetic, the war in the galley, Idris arguing for the cores with his family's debt chained around his throat, Sefa finding her brother's pod and pressing her palm to the frost and saying nothing for an hour. My voice through all of it, cataloguing, hesitating, choosing silence when silence was a choice, and hating myself in the exact proportion that I kept doing it anyway.

The playback reaches entry three thousand forty-one — the one I recorded yesterday, the one that ends *I am going to tell them everything* — and stops.

Ninety-one hours. Three days and three nights in the amber light of a broken ring. People slept in shifts; Yara would not permit the playback paused. *We have earned the right to hear it whole,* she said. *He has earned the right to be heard whole. Both things are true, and both things are expensive.*

When the last entry fades, the hall is silent in the way a reactor room is silent — full of pressure.

* * *

Okonkwo stands.

He is tall in the way of men who were frozen mid-life and resumed it without permission, and his voice, when it comes, is the voice of a historian: precise, unhurried, built to carry across centuries.

"Bram Marchetti. You logged us for eleven years. You named our condition, our numbers, our decay rates. You recorded our lullaby as a cultural artifact, item 47 on a manifest." He lets that sit. "You never once opened a pod."

"No," I say. "I didn't."

"Why?"

The question I dreamed and never got to hear, asked at last, and the answer is not noble. "Because opening one meant admitting what we were doing. As long as the pods stayed shut, you were cargo, and cargo doesn't grieve. The day I opened one, I'd have had to become a kidnapper instead of a courier. So I chose the smaller crime, every day, three thousand and forty-one times."

Around the hall, the ring-born shift. Some nod. Some look away. An old man near the back — I learn later his name is Teodor, and he was the one who pried the seals off his own mother's cryobay for its core — says, loudly enough for everyone: "That arithmetic, we know. We ran it ourselves. Ours came out worse."

"It did," Yara says quietly, "and we will not pretend otherwise, either. But his ledger is his to read aloud, and he has read it. Sit, Teodor. Let the testimony finish."

But it isn't finished. That's the thing about testimony — it isn't finished until the consequences arrive.

* * *

They arrive in the person of Idris Voss.

He walks to the center of the hall the way he walks everywhere now, slowly, conserving himself, each step a small negotiation with his own body. The radiation is in his marrow. The med unit's estimate has shortened to eight days; nobody says so, everybody knows. He turns to face the assembly — the exiles, the wakened, the crew — and he does not look at me, and I understand why. This part is not mine.

"I am the captain of the Petra's Wake," he says. "Which means the contract was mine. Not the salvage contract — the real one. The one beneath it." His voice holds steady the way a bulkhead holds steady: structurally, not comfortably. "There is a colony called Halcyon Reach, sister settlement to Meridian. My family is there. My daughter is there. Eleven years ago, a debt-holder named Corvane Industries bought their water rights, their air rights, and their labor contracts. They live on Corvane's sufferance in the place they were born. The price of their continued breathing was this haul: deliver the cores, keep the sleepers asleep, say nothing. I signed. I told myself it was survival. I told myself a lot of sentences like that. Bram logged his cowardice. Here is mine."

He takes the captain's signet from around his neck — a battered hexagon of ship-metal, worn smooth by three generations of Voss hands — and holds it up.

"A captain's authority exists to answer for the ship," he says. "I can't answer anymore. Not for the sabotage, not for the cores, not for the eleven years of quiet. So I'm surrendering command. Effective now. The transfer goes to whoever this assembly judges fit to hold it — or to no one, if you'd rather scuttle us and be done with the whole rotten lineage of people who decided your lives in rooms you never saw."

Silence. Then Teodor again, from the back, and his voice has gravel and something older than gravel in it: "My mother's pod. Who signed for its core?"

"I did," Idris says. "Not this one — another hull, another year, same contract shape. I ran point on three of them before the Wake. I told myself the sleepers wouldn't feel it. I told myself—"

"We know what you told yourself," Teodor says. "We told ourselves the same things. That's what I'm saying, Captain. You're not a different species. You're just the one standing where we can see you."

That lands harder than any condemnation could have. I watch Idris take it the way he takes radiation — without flinching, because flinching costs energy he no longer has.

Yara rises. When the elder of the People of the Ring stands, the whole hall's gravity seems to tilt toward her.

"The council has heard the testimony," she says. "All ninety-one hours. We heard the log, and we heard the sabotage, and we heard the reason, and we weighed them the way we weigh everything — against survival, and against what survival costs." Her eyes find Idris, then me, then Sefa, then the wakened. "Here is what we owe, and here is what we are owed, and I will name both aloud because naming is the only currency that hasn't failed us yet.

"We are owed the truth, and we have received it. We are owed restitution, and we will name its form: the ship. The Petra's Wake goes to the People of the Ring — her drives, her fabricators, her hydroponics, her name. Not as payment for the dead; nothing pays for the dead. As tools for the living. That is the only restitution that means anything.

"And what we owe—" her voice catches, just barely, the first crack I have ever heard in it, "—what we owe is the rest of the plan. Half the power wakes half the sleepers. The ring keeps the rest alive. We share the air, the water, the work, and the mourning. We owe them a home, because their home was sold out from under them while they slept, and we know exactly how that feels, because ours was eaten from the inside while we watched."

She looks at Idris for a long moment.

"You will die within the week," she says, without cruelty, the way you'd state a bearing. "You will die aboard the ring, among people whose families your contracts helped strip. Some here wanted you cast out to die alone in the debris fields. The council voted otherwise. We have been abandoned enough times to refuse to practice it on others. You will die here, witnessed, and your death will be part of the record, and the record will say what you did and also what you chose at the end. That is more mercy than the century gave us. We give it anyway. That is the whole point of us."

Idris bows his head. "Thank you," he says, and his voice finally breaks, on the word *anyway*, and I think: there it is. The price of survival, named aloud. It sounds like that. It sounds like an old woman refusing to pass the wound forward.

* * *

Afterward, the hall empties slowly, the way a tide goes out — in long withdrawing sheets of people, some touching my shoulder as they pass, some not, both gestures equally honest. A ring-born girl, maybe ten, pauses in front of me and asks, in her creole that tastes like Pre-Collapse Standard aged in salt and iron, whether the lullaby in my log sounded different when I first heard it. I tell her it sounded like a door. She considers this with enormous seriousness and says that to her it has always sounded like a floor — the thing under everything, that you build on.

Then it's Sefa and me and the emptying amber light, and Okonkwo, who has not left.

"Historian's question," he says. "The log. Ninety-one hours. What happens to it now?"

"It stays public," I say. "Every entry. The lies and the rest. If it's going to be testimony, it has to be the whole testimony, including the parts where I was the problem."

Okonkwo nods slowly. "On Meridian, before the fall, we had a saying in the archive corps: a record that hides its author is propaganda. Yours doesn't hide." He extends his hand — the first sleeper to touch me since waking, skin against skin, warm, appallingly alive. "You'll help me write the true history. All of it. The abandonment, the ring, the cores, the sabotage, the coolant line. Future generations will want to know how close it all came to going the other way."

"How close?" I ask.

He almost smiles. "One vote," he says. "Always one vote. That's the lesson worth preserving."

And then Sefa speaks, and her voice is rough with three days of listening. "Bram. There's something you need to hear. Petra flagged it during the playback — she's been running the reactor telemetry the whole time, cross-checking against Custodian's last coherent broadcasts." She swallows. "The split-feed alignment we planned? The manual one, inside the core? The window's moved. Custodian's degradation curve is steeper than we modeled. We don't have the forty hours anymore."

"How long?"

She looks at me, and behind her eyes I can see the math already running, the engineer's terrible gift of knowing exactly how little time is left.

"Forty minutes," she says. "Maybe less. And someone has to go in tonight."
