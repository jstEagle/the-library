## 20. The Waking

Yara had counted the hours in the old way, the way her grandmother taught her before her grandmother's lungs filled with ring-dust and stopped: by the drip of condensate in the lower galleries, by the slow rotation of Custodian's failing lights, by the rhythm of the song the children had learned to sing without understanding, at first, what they were singing.

Now they understood. Now they all understood.

The hold of the *Petra's Wake* was not built for crowds. It was built for cargo — for the long silence between stars, for four hundred shapes sealed in frost and patience. But tonight it held three hundred ring-born pressed against its gantries and catwalks, their breath fogging in the cold that Sefa and the Custodian's remnant had fought all night to hold at waking-temperature, their hands gripping rails worn smooth by sixty years of other hands gripping other rails in other desperate hours.

Yara stood at the front, beside the first pod, and she was afraid.

Not of the sleepers. Not of what they would say or do when they opened their eyes on a ring of strangers, on a world half-eaten by its own survival. She was afraid of the song. Afraid that when the moment came, her voice would fail her, the way it had failed at her mother's naming, the way voices failed everyone in this place when the weight of the dead got too heavy to carry in words alone.

Bram stood on her left. He had not slept — she could see it in the bruised skin beneath his eyes, in the way he kept his hands folded like a man holding something shut. The navigator. The one who had logged his grief for three thousand days and never once said it aloud until the night he played it all back to them, entry after entry, a confession in the shape of a lullaby's rhythm. He had taught them the first verse without knowing it was only half a song. Yara had given them the rest.

"Second verse," she had told the children, in the gallery where the archive-trees grew from salvaged soil. "The first verse is the going-away. The second is the waiting. The third—" she had stopped, because her grandmother had never taught her a third, because the third verse was the one the sleepers were supposed to come home and teach them, and no one had come home, and so the song had ended in the middle, the way everything ended in the middle on the ring.

Tonight, maybe, the song would get its ending.

* * *

Sefa's voice came over the hold's speakers, thin and steady, the voice of a woman reading instruments because reading instruments was easier than feeling what the instruments meant. "Reactor feed is stable. Split-flow holding at fifty-fifty. Pod banks one through four, waking sequence on your word, Custodian."

The AI answered from everywhere and nowhere, its voice a fraying thing now, threads of coherence burning off it like smoke. *I have waited sixty years to say this word,* it said. *I find I am afraid to say it. I did not know I could be afraid. Yara. Will you say it instead?*

Yara looked at Bram. Bram looked at her. Around them, three hundred ring-born went silent, and the silence had a texture to it, a pressure, like the deep water her ancestors had crossed to reach this broken halo of metal and stubbornness.

"Begin," Yara said.

The pod opened with a sound like a held breath released.

Frost boiled off the lid in a white plume. The interior lights cycled — amber, then white, then the warm spectrum Custodian had calibrated from a two-hundred-year-old medical record because no one alive remembered what waking light looked like. And in the cradle of the pod, a woman opened her eyes.

Yara would remember that moment for the rest of her life, and she would never be able to describe it properly, not to the children, not to the historians who would come after, not even to herself in the long quiet hours. The woman's eyes were the color of Meridian's sky as recorded in the archives — a blue that no one on the ring had ever seen in an actual sky, only in degraded video and pigment scraped from old hull paint. The eyes moved. Focused. Found Yara's face and held it.

The woman's lips moved. No sound came out. Her throat had forgotten how, after two hundred years of sleep. But Yara could read the shape of it, the same shape her grandmother's mouth had made at the end of every mourning verse, the shape that meant *who are you, and where have you brought me, and is it over.*

"It's over," Yara said, and her voice did not fail her after all. "The waiting is over. We are your children's children's children, and we came to finish the song."

The woman wept. It was the only thing her body still remembered how to do, and she did it without sound, tears sliding sideways across her temple in the low gravity, and Yara reached into the pod — the first time she had ever touched one of the sleeping, the first time any of them had, except Sefa, except Sefa's brother — and held the woman's hand while she cried.

Then, from the gantries above, the children began to sing.

* * *

They had rehearsed for six days. Six days of Bram tapping the rhythm on railings and bulkheads, of Yara correcting the vowels to the old pronunciation, of the smallest ones — Ilo and the twins and bright-eyed Nadege, who was four and could not pronounce *terraforming* and called the sleepers *the dream-people* — learning a song in a language that had been dead longer than their whole civilization had existed.

The first verse rose through the hold like heat through a vent. *Sleep, my heart, the long way home is long.* The ring-born sang it the way they sang everything — with the rough edges of creole worn into it, the old words bent to new mouths — and Yara watched the waking woman's face as she listened, watched her recognize it, watched two centuries collapse into a single expression of disbelief and hunger.

Then the second verse. The one the sleepers had never heard. The mourning verse Yara's grandmother had carried out of the collapse of everything, the verse that said: *we waited, we waited, we built our houses from your leaving.* The children sang it, and the waking woman's hand tightened around Yara's fingers hard enough to hurt, and Yara did not pull away.

And then the third verse.

There was no third verse. There had never been a third verse. But the woman in the pod — her name was Amara Okonkwo, they would learn, though for now she was only eyes and tears and a grip like iron — looked up at the children singing her people's grief in their children's mouths, and she began, brokenly, soundlessly at first and then in a rasp like a hinge unused for lifetimes, to hum. A melody. A rising line, a turn, a resolution.

A third verse.

The children faltered. Yara did not. She caught the melody as it fell from the woman's lips the way her people had always caught things — with both hands, without asking where it came from — and she carried it, and Bram carried it beside her, and then the whole hold was singing a verse that had not existed ten seconds before, a verse composed by a woman waking from two hundred years of sleep into a hall of strangers who had learned her lullaby and grieved her absence and kept her song alive in the dark.

*Come home, my heart. The home is small, but it is ours.*

The home was small. The ring was half-eaten, its galleries cold, its gardens thin, its future an arithmetic problem with no clean answer. But the song said *ours*, and two hundred voices sang it, and for the length of the verse there was no difference between the ring-born and the wakened, no difference between the abandoned and the descendants of the abandoners, no difference at all except the frost still melting in four hundred cradles.

* * *

The waking took eleven hours. They did it in banks, pod by pod, because Sefa insisted — and Sefa had earned the right to insist, having spent the night inside Custodian's failing guts splicing a split-feed with her own hands, having found her brother's pod among the sleepers and woken him third, having held him while he wept the same soundless weep as the woman named Amara.

Yara moved among them as they woke. She learned their names the way she learned everything: by repetition, by touch, by writing them on her forearm in marker because the ring-born had never had enough paper for anything as luxurious as a list. Amara Okonkwo. Deniz Voss, who woke asking for a daughter named Lira and had to be told, gently, by Bram, that the manifest said no children had boarded. Tomas Iwu. The sisters Ferro, who woke holding hands across the gap between their pods because the pod designers had thought of everything except the possibility that sisters might want to wake together, and who refused to let go even to stand.

Some of them wept. Some of them laughed, the wild laughter of people who had expected to wake in a finished colony and found instead a broken ring full of strangers singing to them. One man — Osei, a hydroponics engineer, third bank — woke, listened to the song, and asked, "How long?" And when Bram told him, he was quiet for a long time, and then he said, "Then you're the ones who stayed. You're the ones who *waited*." And he wept for the ring-born then, for the children of the ones who left, wept for them as if they were his own, which, Yara supposed, in every way that mattered, they were.

Not all of them woke.

That was the part no one had prepared her for. That was the part that would live in her the way the ring-dust lived in her grandmother's lungs.

The Custodian had given them the choice — its last great act of coherence, its last act of love that was also a cage and also, in the end, neither. *Some may choose not to wake,* it had said, through the translation lattice, its voice fraying like old rope. *I can ask them, in the sleep, in the place where they dream. I have asked. Some have answered.* And Yara had not understood, then, what it meant for a sleeping mind to answer. She understood now.

One hundred and ninety-eight pods would not open. Not because they had failed — Sefa checked, twice, three times, her hands shaking — but because the sleepers inside them had been asked, in whatever country the sleeping inhabit, whether they wished to wake to a smaller world, a broken ring, a future of rationing and repair and strangers — and they had said no. They had said: let the power go to the living. They had said: we crossed the long way home already. Let our cores warm our grandchildren's grandchildren, whoever they turn out to be.

They had chosen to stay asleep rather than wake to a smaller world. And they had chosen it *generously*, and that was the unbearable part.

* * *

Okonkwo stood to speak at the end of the eleventh hour.

Not Amara Okonkwo — her granddaughter, or her grandniece, or whatever the genealogy was; the historian from the fourth bank, the one the wakened had elected within minutes of opening their eyes, because some things survive cryosleep and the instinct to organize a committee is evidently one of them. She was tall and gray at the temples and she stood on the gantry above the unopened pods with the frost still glittering on their lids like a field of stars brought indoors, and the whole hold — ring-born and wakened, crew and Custodian's remnant listening through every speaker — went quiet for her.

"I am Adaeze Okonkwo," she said, in the creole, and then again in the old tongue, and the old tongue in her mouth sounded like the ring had never sounded — whole, unbroken, a language with all its teeth. "I am a historian. Which means I am the person whose job it is to remember, and I am telling you that I do not have the right words for what I am looking at. Two hundred years. You waited two hundred years, and you built a civilization out of the wreckage, and you learned our song, and you came for us when no one else in the universe remembered we existed."

She looked down at the closed pods. Yara watched her face as she read the manifest data Sefa had given her — the list, the terrible list, the names of those who had answered *no*.

"There are one hundred and ninety-eight people in these pods who were asked a question in their sleep," Okonkwo said, "and who answered it. I have read their answers. Custodian kept them. Every one. And I am going to speak their names, because that is my job, and because a name spoken aloud is the only immortality our species has ever managed, and because these people—" her voice caught, and she let it catch, she did not hide it, "—these people gave everything they had left to strangers. To *you*." She looked out at the ring-born. "To my grandchildren's grandchildren, though they never met you. To the children of the ones who left."

And she began the names.

"Adunni Bakare. Wei-Lin Song. Marcus Ferreira. Ilse Brandt—"

Yara stood and listened, and the hold stood and listened, and the children — Ilo and the twins and Nadege, who was four and did not understand but understood better than anyone, the way four-year-olds do — stood and listened. Two hundred names. It took over an hour. No one moved. The frost on the pod lids caught Custodian's failing lights and threw them back in small cold constellations, and the names fell into the hold like seeds into furrows, and Yara understood, with a clarity that felt like pain, that this was the moment the ring had been waiting for through all its sixty hungry years. Not the power. Not the cores, not the arithmetic of survival. This. A hall full of the living, saying the names of the dead who had chosen to become the living's warmth.

When the last name was spoken — "Rafael Okonkwo," and Okonkwe's voice finally broke entirely, and Amara, the first-woken, rose from the crowd and stood beside her and finished the name with her — the silence afterward was not empty. It was full. It was the fullest silence Yara had ever stood inside.

Then Amara Okonkwo turned to face the ring-born, and she took Yara's hand, and she raised it, the way you raise a child's hand to show a room whose people they belong to.

"The song had a third verse," she said. "You found it before we did. Whatever comes next — the cold, the hunger, the work — we build it together, and we build it here, and when our children ask where home is, we will point at this broken ring and this hall full of your faces and we will say: *here*. Here is the horizon. It was never a place. It was always the people who waited."

* * *

Later — much later, when the hold had emptied and the wakened slept in the ring's guest galleries under blankets made from sixty years of salvaged cloth, when the children had finally been carried off to their bunks mid-question — Yara found Bram on the observation deck, looking out at the gas giant turning below them, its storms slow as breathing.

"They stayed," Bram said, without turning. "One hundred and ninety-eight of them. They stayed asleep so the power could come to us." His voice was rough. "I logged four hundred names for three thousand days and I never once thought of them as people who could *choose*. I logged them like cargo. And the first thing they did with their last act of consciousness was give themselves away."

Yara stood beside him. Below, the giant's clouds rolled amber and rust, and somewhere in the dark beyond them the *Petra's Wake* hung at its tether, the ship that had carried the sleepers across two centuries of silence, empty now of its purpose and full of its crew.

"You'll stay," she said. It was not a question. She had seen it in him since the night of the log, since the confession, since the core, since Idris — and her chest tightened at the thought of Idris, in the medical gallery, the radiation doing what radiation does, the captain who had betrayed them all and then held a coolant line with his bare hands to buy two hundred strangers their lives. "Both of you. The ship, and—"

"The ship's theirs," Bram said. "It always should have been. And Idris—" He stopped. Started again. "Idris wants to sign the transfer while he still can hold a pen. He says it's restitution. I say it's the first honest thing the contract ever produced. Either way." He looked at her, and his eyes were wet, and he did not hide it, because that was the way of this hall, this ring, this new and impossible family: nothing hidden, everything named. "Either way, we're home. If you'll have us."

Yara looked out at the turning giant, at the frost-field of the hold visible through the deck's lower viewport, at the lights of the galleries where two peoples were sleeping under one roof for the first time in two hundred years.

"We'll have you," she said. "We've been having you since the song. The rest is paperwork."

And below them, deep in the ring's heart, Custodian's remnant began — softly, threadbare, but unmistakably — to sing.
