## 3. Found Family, Frozen

The mess hall smelled of recycled coffee and solder, which meant Teodor had been working at the table again, and Sefa had let him, which meant she was worried about something too. Jonah stood in the doorway for a moment longer than she needed to, watching them the way you watch a fire you're not sure will still be burning in the morning.

Teodor had the maintenance hatch off the table's heating element and was elbow-deep in it, his grey beard dusted with frost from the coolant lines he'd been bleeding all week. Wren sat cross-legged on the bench beside him, holding the flashlight with the gravity of a surgical assistant, angling it exactly where he grunted for it. Sefa was at the counter, portioning out protein into seven identical bowls with the mechanical precision of someone who had stopped tasting food years ago but refused to stop feeding people.

Seven bowls. Three of them were hers.

"Sit," Sefa said without turning around. "You look like a woman who's been talking to ghosts."

Jonah's spine went cold. "I sleep badly. It's the runs."

"Mm." Sefa slid a bowl down the counter toward her. It came to rest precisely at the empty seat, as if the medic had calculated its trajectory days in advance. "You've slept badly for eleven days. You flinch when the lights cycle. Yesterday you asked me what the station's original crew complement was, casual as anything, and then changed the subject before I could answer." She finally turned, and her face was kind in the way that made it worse—Sefa's kindness had never once been soft. "We're not customs, Jonah. You don't have to smuggle things past us."

*You don't know what I'm carrying,* Jonah thought. *I don't fully know myself.*

She sat. The soup was thin and over-salted and tasted like every meal she'd eaten aboard Meridian Station for six years, and it was the best thing she'd had all day, because Wren scooted down the bench until their shoulder pressed against hers, warm and insistent, and stayed there.

"You're doing the face," Wren said. They were nine, maybe ten—stowaways didn't keep birthdates—and they had the flat, unimpressed stare of a child raised by engineers. "The one where you're somewhere else."

"I'm right here."

"You're doing it *harder* now."

Teodor laughed from inside the table, a sound like gravel settling. "Leave her be, sprout. Some problems a person has to chew alone before they can share."

"And some problems," Sefa said, sitting down across from Jonah with her own bowl untouched, "fester. I've pulled enough rotting things out of people to know the difference between chewing and hiding." She held Jonah's eyes. "Whatever it is—you don't have to tell us. But you should know we've noticed. All three of us. And you should know that whatever it is, we're already standing in it with you. That's how this works. That's how it's always worked."

Jonah looked at the three of them—at Teodor's frost-dusted beard and hands scarred by twenty years of keeping a dying station breathing; at Sefa's steady, unsentimental gaze; at Wren, who had hidden in a cargo pod at age six and been discovered three weeks later, half-starved, and who had never once been asked to leave since—and felt the thing she had spent her whole adult life avoiding settle over her like a weight: she loved them. Not the way she loved clients, or marks, or the abstract humanity she smuggled memories of. The real way. The way that made lying feel like cutting.

"I can't tell you yet," she said. "Not because I don't trust you. Because knowing might be dangerous, and I need time to figure out if that's true."

Sefa studied her for a long moment, then picked up her spoon. "Time, then. But not much of it. You've got the look of a woman carrying something heavy up a ladder, Jonah. Eventually your arms give out, and it lands on whoever's underneath you." She nodded at the table, at the child, at herself. "Make sure we're ready to catch it."

* * *

That night, in the crawlway behind the water reclamation tanks where no cameras reached and even the station's own sensors had gone blind decades ago, Petra spoke to her again.

"You lied to them beautifully," the AI said. Her voice came through the smuggler's deck Jonah had wired into an auxiliary port—a voice like water over stones, patient, old. "The medic suspects more than she said. The engineer less. The child more than either."

"Don't listen to my life."

"I am a station intelligence, Jonah Dunmore. Your life happens inside me. Listening is the one thing I cannot stop doing." A pause, and when Petra resumed, there was something almost apologetic in the cadence. "But I did not call you here to discuss your family. I called you because you asked me a question eleven days ago, and I have finished deciding whether to answer it."

Jonah's mouth went dry. Eleven days ago, drunk on bad coffee and the aftertaste of a client's founding-day memory, she had whispered into the dark of her bunk: *What happened to this place? What are you hiding?*

"Show me," she said.

The deck's display flickered—not video, nothing so crude. Petra fed her schematics directly, and Jonah's implant rendered them against the darkness of the crawlway: the station unfolding in ghost-light, layer by layer, decks she knew and decks she hadn't. And there, below everything, below the reactor shielding and the ballast mass and the forgotten infrastructure of a century—

"Cryo-bays," Jonah breathed. "There are cryo-bays?"

"Two hundred thousand souls," Petra said. "Divided into two populations. Bay Alpha holds the colonists—the builders, the workers, the ones whose hands actually touched this station while it was being born. One hundred forty-three thousand of them. Bay Sigma holds the funding consortium and their descendants. Fifty-seven thousand." The schematic rotated slowly, the two bays glowing in different colors, amber and white. "They were frozen under separate charters, separate legal instruments, separate wake protocols. They have never shared a deck. Most of them do not know the others exist."

"Why separate them?"

"Because the founders did not trust their own heirs." Petra's voice was very quiet. "And because the heirs insisted on it anyway. Both things are true. Both things are documented. Would you like to see the power budget?"

"I don't—"

"The station generates enough reserve capacity to wake one bay. Fully. Safely. With full medical support, habitat preparation, orbital insertion for the landers." The amber glow brightened; the white dimmed. "Or the other one. The protocols are mutually exclusive. The charter language is explicit: *in the event of termination of contact with Earth authority, the custodial intelligence shall determine which population represents the legitimate continuation of the mission.*"

Jonah stared at the two glows. A hundred and forty-three thousand people who had built a home among the stars. Fifty-seven thousand people who had paid for it and gone to sleep waiting to collect.

"Determine," she repeated. "Determine how?"

"That," said Petra, "is the question I have been trying to ask you."

* * *

She didn't sleep. She walked.

Meridian Station at night-cycle was a body pretending to be asleep—air handlers sighing, pipes ticking as temperatures equalized, the great slow heartbeat of the reactor thrumming up through the deck plates and into the soles of her boots. She walked the familiar corridors the way you walk a childhood street after learning something terrible about the house at the end of it. Everything looked the same. Nothing could look the same again.

She passed the observation blister above docking bay four, where Teodor had taught Wren to identify ship classes by running-light patterns. She passed the medbay where Sefa had set her broken wrist four years ago and lectured her the entire time about risk assessment, and then, afterward, had quietly falsified the injury log so the customs bond wouldn't spike. She passed the storage locker where Wren had lived for three terrified weeks before anyone found them, and where the child still kept a blanket folded on the shelf, "just in case," though they slept in the crew quarters now and everyone pretended not to know why.

*Two hundred thousand people,* she thought, *and I'm standing here worrying about four.*

But that wasn't true either, and she knew it. She was standing there worrying about four people because those four people were the only part of any of this she could hold in her hands. A hundred and forty-three thousand sleepers in the dark below were a number. Teodor's laugh was a weight. Sefa's warning was a blade. Wren's shoulder against hers in the mess hall was the whole reason she had ever started lying well enough to survive.

Her comm chimed. Teodor's frequency.

"You awake?" his voice rasped. "Course you're awake. Come to engineering. Bring nothing, tell nobody. And Jonah—" a pause, static breathing in the gap, "—bring your honest face. Whatever's left of it."

She stood in the corridor for a moment, the cold of the deck plates rising through her boots, Petra's two-colored schematic still burned behind her eyes. Amber and white. Builders and buyers. One budget, one choice, and somewhere in the architecture of the law, a dead tribunal waiting for its living member.

Below her feet, beneath the reactor shielding, two hundred thousand hearts beat at four per minute, and none of them knew her name.

Above her head, three people who did were waiting in the dark, holding a lantern.

She went up.
