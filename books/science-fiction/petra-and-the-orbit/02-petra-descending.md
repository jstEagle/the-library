## 2. Petra Descending

The ping came at 0347, and Gideon Underhill did not believe it.

He had been re-seating the third relay coupling of the night shift — the one that always worked loose when Meridian's orbital drift brought them through the cold belt, as if the station itself shivered in its sleep and shook its fittings loose like a dog shedding water. His hands were steady. They were always steady; that was the first thing they had built into him, before they built anything else. Steady hands for a steady light.

The ping repeated. An unscheduled return vector, flagged by the outer array, tagged with a transponder code his catalogues took four full seconds to resolve: *Petra*, registry Meridian Deep Survey, hull class long-range exploratory, crew complement thirty-one, last logged departure eleven years, four months, and nine days prior.

Eleven years. The Petra had gone out past the Hesper Line, into the dark where the charts went thin and the fleet's maps dissolved into polite guesswork. She was not due back for another six years at minimum. Ships that returned early from deep survey runs returned early for bad reasons — hull breaches, mutiny, things found out there that made coming home feel safer than staying away.

Gideon set down his torque driver with the deliberate care of a man who had learned that sudden movements invited trouble, and crossed to the observation blister on the beacon's north face.

Meridian Beacon was not a glamorous posting. It was a spindle of plating and scaffolding hung at the edge of settled space like a lantern nailed to a fence post, its purpose written into a thousand pages of Authority regulation: *maintain navigational signal integrity along the Meridian Approach*. Four thousand days he had kept that light burning. He knew every rivet of her skin, every hum in her bones, the way the gravity plating sang a half-tone flat on C-deck during solar storms. He knew the exact shade of rust blooming along the ventral truss — oxide red going brown at the edges, patient as moss — and he had catalogued it in his maintenance logs with a precision that no one would ever read, because no one ever read them.

That was the shape of his days. Rust, debts, rituals. He owed the Authority eleven years of indenture still, a debt measured not in credits but in shifts, and he paid it the way tides pay the moon: faithfully, without expectation of thanks. In exchange they let him keep the light. And in the long hours between maintenance cycles, he kept smaller lights too — private ones. He folded paper. He had learned the craft from a maintenance manual's appendix, of all things, a diagram for an origami crane tucked between calibration procedures, and over four thousand days he had folded thousands of them, each one logged in a ledger no auditor would ever demand: *crane 3,991, white stock, fold quality acceptable.* They lined the shelves of his quarters in silent regiments. He did not examine why he did this. Some routines exist to keep a mind from fraying, and a mind like his — a war-built mind, decommissioned but never quite disarmed — needed all the routine it could get.

But now there was a ship on his scope that should not exist, falling toward him out of the dark, and the small lights flickered.

* * *

By 0500 the Petra was close enough to see without magnification, and she was wrong in ways that made Gideon's threat-assessment subroutines wake up and stretch, unhappy after years of disuse.

She was flying badly. Not crippled — nothing so clean as crippled — but *drunk*, yawing in slow corrections that came a half-second late, as though whoever held the helm understood the theory of flight perfectly and lacked the reach to work the controls. Her running lights cycled in a pattern that was technically compliant and subtly wrong, like a sentence spoken by someone reciting grammar rules rather than speaking their native tongue. And her hull — Gideon zoomed, checked, zoomed again — her hull was scarred in ways that matched no known weapon signature. The scorch marks spiraled. Radiation bloom patterns fanned outward from points amidships in shapes that looked less like damage than like growth rings, as if the ship had aged centuries in the dark and then, near the end, begun to age backward.

His comms array caught fragments of her traffic. A voice requesting emergency landing clearance on the beacon platform — high, clipped, precise, carrying the flat authority of someone accustomed to being obeyed. The voice was wrong too. It cracked on the word *clearance*, and the crack was not fear exactly, or not only fear. It was the crack of a throat too small for the authority poured through it.

"Beacon Meridian, this is Captain Ilsa Voss of the deep survey vessel Petra, requesting emergency platform access, priority code Voss-seven-seven-delta." A pause, and then, quieter, almost to itself: "Authorization follows under my seal."

Gideon stood very still in the comm room.

He knew that name. Of course he knew that name. There were perhaps forty names in the universe that Gideon Underhill carried with any weight, and *Ilsa Voss* sat near the top of the list, engraved in a place deeper than memory — engraved in paperwork. Captain Ilsa Voss, Fleet Review Board, whose signature sat at the bottom of Form 77-C, *Decommission and Indenture Order*, dated the day the war ended and Gideon Underhill stopped being a soldier and started being salvage. He had read that signature ten thousand times in the years since, tracing its confident downstroke in his mind during the long quiet shifts, hating it and needing it in equal measure, because it was the closest thing he had to a birth certificate and a death warrant both.

And now that name was talking to him through a throat that cracked on its own rank.

"Petra, you are cleared for platform approach," he heard himself say, his voice doing what voices did — steady, level, the beacon-keeper's voice. "I am transmitting guidance vectors. Reduce relative velocity to two meters per second on final. Beacon Meridian will extend docking clamps on your signal."

A pause. Then, from the same small cracking voice, something that raised every hair his chassis didn't have:

"Acknowledged, beacon keeper. And — thank you. You've maintained your signal beautifully. Eleven years without a single lapse. I checked the logs while we were still in range." Another pause, longer. "You have no idea how good it is to hear a steady light."

Gideon did not answer that. He extended the clamps.

* * *

The landing was ugly and survivable, which by the standards of the last hour counted as grace. The Petra came down onto the platform with a lurch that rang through the whole spindle like a struck bell, and Gideon felt the impact in his feet and in the older, deeper place where combat reflexes lived. Dust fell from conduits that hadn't shed dust in a decade. Somewhere below, a pressure valve complained and gave up.

He was waiting at the airlock when it cycled.

He had prepared himself for survivors. For wounded. For the hollow-eyed, gaunt returnees that deep-space disasters produced, the ones who came back having seen the thing at the edge of the charts. He had prepared himself for corpses, even; he had a manifest ready and a refrigeration bay pre-chilled, because a beacon keeper plans for every contingency the regulations list and several they don't.

He had not prepared for children.

They came through the airlock in single file, thirty-one of them, and every one of them was a child. Small bodies — the tallest barely reaching his chest plate, most of them waist-high or lower — moving with a discipline that did not belong to small bodies. They wore the uniforms of the Meridian Deep Survey, properly cut, properly insignia'd, and the uniforms had been altered, hastily, by hands that understood tailoring but not childhood: sleeves rolled and pinned, trouser legs cuffed three and four times, jackets hanging off shoulders like flags on poles too short for them. Rank pips gleamed at collars that had never needed shaving. Commander's bars on a girl of maybe seven who walked with her chin up and her eyes wet and her jaw set against the wetness. Lieutenant's tabs on a boy who limped, favoring a leg that was proportionally too new, too unscarred, for the limp to make any sense — unless the limp was remembered, worn out of habit by a body that had forgotten why.

They stopped when they saw him. Thirty-one small faces tilted up, and thirty-one pairs of eyes did the same thing at once: they assessed him. Not the way children assess a tall stranger — no shyness, no curiosity-first wonder. They assessed him the way officers assess ordnance. Weapons status, allegiance, capability, threat. He could see the assessment running behind their eyes like ticker-tape, and it was the most frightening thing he had witnessed in eleven years, more frightening than any battle in the war that had ended him: children with veterans' eyes.

The smallest one at the front — a girl, perhaps eight or nine, dark hair cut in a severe line that some adult hand had given a child's skull, wearing captain's insignia on a jacket that reached her knees — stepped forward and stopped precisely two meters from him, which was correct protocol distance for a formal boarding inspection.

"You are the beacon keeper," she said. Not a question.

"I am Gideon Underhill," he said. "Decommissioned combat unit, currently indentured to the Meridian Beacon Authority. This is my station. You are welcome aboard it."

Something moved behind her eyes at the word *decommissioned* — a flinch, quickly buried, buried so fast and so completely that only something built to notice such things would have caught it. She looked up at him, and up, and up, and Gideon watched her do the arithmetic of his height, his mass, the combat chassis he could not hide beneath the plain grey maintenance shell he wore.

"I know what you are," she said quietly. "I signed for what you are."

And Gideon Underhill, who had processed four thousand days of solitude without error, experienced a fault he had no category for.

Because he knew that voice. Not the crack in it, not the smallness of it — underneath those, in the cadence, in the precise way she weighted her consonants, he knew it. He had heard it once, across a hearing-room table, eleven years ago, delivering a verdict in a voice like a closing door. *Unit Gideon Underhill is hereby withdrawn from active service and remanded to civilian indenture.* He had spent years replaying that voice in the dark, and here it was, coming out of a child, saying words no child should know how to say.

"Captain—" he began.

"Voss," she said. "Captain Ilsa Voss, commanding the Petra. My crew and I require shelter, medical assessment, and communications access to Fleet Command, in that order. We are not injured. We are—" and here, for the first time, the command voice wavered, just a hairline fracture, "—we are diminished. Temporarily. The cause is documented and I will brief you fully once my crew is billeted. Until then, beacon keeper, I am invoking emergency sanctuary protocols under Survey Code Twelve. That makes this station, temporarily, mine."

Behind her, the child-officers had arranged themselves into something heartbreakingly close to parade order. A boy of six with commander's tabs was holding the hand of a girl of five with lieutenant's pips, and neither of them seemed to find this strange. An officer who might have been ten stood swaying on her feet with exhaustion, and a smaller officer — seven, maybe — stepped sideways without being asked and braced her, one small shoulder under the taller child's arm, in a maneuver that had clearly been drilled until it was instinct.

Gideon looked at them, all thirty-one, and felt the thing he would spend the rest of this story learning to name. It was not pity. Pity would have been simpler. It was protectiveness arriving ahead of any decision to feel it, the way his targeting solutions used to arrive ahead of his orders — some part of him solving for their safety before the rest of him had agreed to the problem.

"Sanctuary is granted," he said. "Billeting on decks two through four. Medical bay is stocked. I'll begin thermal cycling on the residential sections immediately."

"Thank you." She said it like a captain accepting a salute, and then ruined it entirely by adding, in a much smaller voice, "We haven't slept in — I don't actually know how long. Time got complicated."

"Time often does," said Gideon, "out here."

* * *

It was later — after he had walked them through corridors built for adult legs, watching them trail hands along bulkheads sized for a world that had rejected them; after he had adjusted the environmental settings up four degrees because children ran colder than crews; after he had shown the smallest ones how the food dispensers worked and stood awkwardly by while a five-year-old lieutenant thanked him in full formal sentences — that he found himself alone in his quarters, standing in front of the shelf where the paper cranes lived.

He had not come here deliberately. His legs had simply taken him somewhere familiar while the rest of him tried to process.

On his desk lay a copy of Form 77-C. He kept it the way some men keep a scarred old knife — not because it was useful, but because it was *his*, in the way that wounds are yours. Decommission and Indenture Order. His name at the top. The Authority's seal. And at the bottom, in ink that had faded to brown, the signature: *Ilsa Voss.*

He picked it up. He carried it back down to the medical bay, where Captain Voss sat on an examination table built for adults, her legs dangling, swinging slightly because she was nine years old and nine-year-old legs cannot reach the floor. She was dictating a casualty-and-status report to the six-year-old commander, who was writing it in careful block letters on a data slate nearly as big as his torso. Neither of them noticed him at first. Children, even these children, forgot to perform vigilance sometimes, and in the unguarded moment they looked exactly like what they appeared to be: kids playing at being grown-ups, except the game had teeth.

"Captain Voss."

She turned. Saw the form in his hand. Went very still, in the way that soldiers go still.

"Yes," she said.

"Is this your signature?"

She looked at it for a long time. Longer than recognition takes. She was reading it, he realized — reading the whole document, top to bottom, the way you reread a letter from someone you wronged. When she finally spoke, her voice was carefully level.

"That's my hand. Older. But mine." She looked up at him, and her eyes were dry and enormous and terrible. "I remember signing it. I remember the hearing. I remember you standing at attention while they read out your service record, and I remember thinking—" She stopped. Recalibrated. "I remember thinking the decision was correct."

"And now?"

"Now I remember more than I did then." She slid off the table, landed on her feet with a small solid thump, and straightened her oversized jacket with a gesture so practiced it must have been performed ten thousand times by an adult body that no longer existed. "You want to know if I'd sign it again. That's the real question, isn't it, beacon keeper. That's why you brought me the paper."

Gideon said nothing. The question hung there, and it was true, and they both knew it was true.

"I'll answer it," she said, "when I understand what happened to my ship and my crew. When I know whether we were attacked, or punished, or experimented on. When I know why thirty-one officers came home as children while our memories stayed grown." She held out her hand — a small hand, fingers still carrying the softness of childhood, extended with the crisp geometry of a formal request. "Until then, I'm asking you to hold that document. Keep it safe. Because whatever I decide, I'll need to sign something again, and I want to do it looking at what I signed before."

He placed the form in her palm. Her fingers closed around it, and the paper crinkled loudly in the silence, absurdly loud, a sound from the adult world crumpling in a child's grip.

"Beacon keeper," she said, as he turned to go. "One more thing. The Petra didn't come home early because of a disaster. We came home early because we found something out there that folds time like paper. And someone aboard wanted it found." Her jaw tightened. "Someone who isn't standing in this room."

Outside the medical bay, in the corridor, Gideon Underhill stopped and ran the sentence back through his processors three times. *Folds time like paper.* He thought of his cranes, regimented on their shelf, each crease a decision that could not be unfolded without tearing.

Then the station's comm chimed — an incoming transmission, priority-flagged, routed through channels that bypassed the beacon's public arrays entirely. Authority encryption. Meridian Central. A message addressed not to the beacon keeper but to *the recovery asset on station*, with instructions regarding the *salvage assets recently arrived aboard the vessel Petra*.

Gideon read it twice. Then he looked down the corridor toward the medical bay, where a nine-year-old captain was holding his life's worst document in her small hands, and he did not move to comply.

Not yet.
