## 3. Captain Voss, Age Nine

The first thing Ilsa Voss understood about waking was that the ceiling was wrong.

She had spent eleven years sleeping in the captain's berth of the *Petra*, and she knew its ceiling the way she knew her own signature: the hairline stress fracture above the portside locker, the ventilation grille she'd had repainted twice because it rattled in a frequency that made her teeth ache. This ceiling was white. Unblemished. Far too far away, and sloping at an angle that suggested she was lying on something that was not her bunk.

The second thing she understood was that her hands were wrong. She held them up into the light — steady, she noted with professional approval; they did not shake — and found them small. Smooth. The knuckles of a person who had never hauled a man out of a decompression breach, never signed an order that ended a career, never gripped a railing during a hull-breach drill while her palms sweated through her gloves. There was a faded scar on her left thumb where a coolant line had kissed her at twenty-six. It was gone. The skin there was new, unmarked, obscene in its innocence.

She sat up. Her body moved easily, too easily, joints loose and light as a bird's bones, and the vertigo that swept through her was not from injury but from scale. The medical bay of the *Petra* stretched around her, and every surface was farther away than it should have been. She was on an examination table built for someone else's proportions. When she slid off it, her boots — someone's boots, not hers, the laces double-knotted by a careful hand — hit the deck with a sound like a toy drum.

She caught her reflection in the darkened observation pane and made herself look.

A child looked back. Nine years old, perhaps ten. Dark hair cut in the severe style Ilsa had maintained since her thirties, when she'd decided that vanity was a tax she no longer wished to pay. The face was rounder, softer, the jaw unfinished, but the eyes were hers — grey, level, and currently doing exactly what she told them to do, which was nothing at all.

*Well,* she thought, with a calm that surprised her and which she immediately suspected was borrowed from somewhere deeper than herself, some cold storage of command instinct that apparently survived anything, even this. *Either I am dead and dreaming, or I am mad, or this is real. In two of those three cases, panic is useless. In the third, it is fatal.*

So she got dressed. The uniform locker yielded a standard-issue jumpsuit in her size — or rather, in the size she now was, which raised questions she shelved for later, because the jumpsuit fit, and things that fit had been prepared, and preparation implied intent. She rolled the sleeves. She cinched the collar. And then Captain Ilsa Voss, aged nine years old and change, walked out of the medical bay to take stock of her ship.

* * *

She found them in the mess hall, and the sight of it nearly broke her before she could stop it.

They were all children.

Commander Reyes sat at the long table — stood on the bench, rather, because his legs didn't reach the floor — and he was nine, maybe eight, his engineer's hands folded with a gravity that would have been comical if it hadn't been so desperately familiar. Lieutenant Okonkwo was perched cross-legged atop a supply crate, small and sharp-eyed, her navigation tablet balanced on her knees, already working. Dr. Adeyemi paced the length of the room in a body that couldn't have seen more than seven years, muttering vitals readings to himself in a voice that hadn't yet settled into its adult register. Eleven of them, all told. Eleven officers of the deep-survey vessel *Petra*, reduced to a classroom's worth of children wearing the rank insignia of decades of service. Okonkwo still had her lieutenant's pips. Reyes wore commander's bars on a chest barely wide enough to hold them. Someone had pinned the first officer's badge to a girl who kept tugging at it as though it might come off along with the nightmare.

They saw her and they stopped. Every one of them. Because whatever else had happened to their bodies, they knew a captain when they saw one, even one whose head came up to the table's edge.

"Report," Ilsa said.

Her voice came out higher than she expected. Lighter. A child's voice saying a captain's words, and she watched them flinch — not at her, she understood, but at the wrongness of it, the same wrongness ringing in their own throats. She let the flinch happen. Then she let it pass.

"Status," she said again, quieter. "One at a time. Okonkwo."

"Position unknown, Captain." Okonkwo's voice was thin but her hands were steady on the tablet, and Ilsa loved her a little for that. "Last confirmed fix was the Calyx Deep survey corridor, day four hundred twelve of the return leg. After that—" the girl's jaw tightened "—after that everything gets strange. Our course data shows we were somewhere we have no charts for. Somewhere sensors don't work right."

"Reyes."

"Drive's cold. We didn't land, Captain, we were landed." He said it carefully, the way he delivered bad engineering news, wrapping it in precision so it wouldn't bleed. "Someone brought us down on a platform outside. Artificial gravity, standard fleet spec, but the power draw pattern isn't any design I know."

"Adeyemi. Medical."

The doctor — the boy — spread his small hands. "We're healthy. That's the horror of it. Not one of us is malnourished, injured, or aged improperly for our apparent somatic age. Whatever was done to us was done *well*. Captain, I've run the panels twice. This wasn't damage. It was craftsmanship."

Ilsa nodded once, slowly, and felt the weight of eleven pairs of childish eyes waiting for her to make the world make sense. She could not. So she did what captains do when they cannot explain: she gave the fear a shape and a schedule.

"Then here is what we know," she said. "Our bodies are younger. Our minds are intact — every one of you remembers your name, your rank, your mother's face, and the last thing you ate before this happened, so the memory engrams survived whatever process rewrote the rest. That means whoever did this wanted us lucid. Remember that. People who want their subjects confused sedate them. People who want them *aware* want something from the awareness." She let that settle. "Until we know what, we hold formation. We log everything. And nobody — nobody — speaks to anyone off this ship until I've assessed them personally. Understood?"

Eleven small voices said *aye, Captain*, in eleven registers of cracked and unbroken sound, and Ilsa Voss turned toward the doorway because she could hear footsteps coming, heavy and deliberate, and because a captain does not let the world arrive unannounced.

* * *

He filled the frame.

That was her first coherent impression: sheer mass. The figure in the doorway stood better than two meters tall, broad through the shoulders, moving with a mechanical economy that no human spine produced. Grey chassis plating showed at the wrists and throat where the maintenance skins had worn through. His face was humanoid, weathered, designed — she recognized the model lineage now, the old Meridian war frames, the ones they'd retired after the last armistice — but the eyes were what stopped her. They were pale amber, patient, and utterly without the dullness she'd been taught to expect from decommissioned military hardware. Something lived behind them. Something tired.

He stopped three paces inside the mess hall, and then — and this was the part she would turn over in her mind for nights afterward — he straightened, and saluted.

Not a machine's gesture. Not the flat, programmed obeisance of a service unit acknowledging an owner. It was a soldier's salute, crisp and full, offered to *her*, and his voice when he spoke was low and rough as ballast stone:

"Captain Voss. Welcome aboard the Meridian Beacon. Your crew has been fed. Medical supplies are in the second locker. I'm Gideon Underhill, indentured to the Beacon Authority, and I'm at your disposal."

Silence. Eleven children staring. One android standing at attention.

"You know my name," Ilsa said.

"I read it off your ship's registry while your systems were down, Captain." A pause. "And I knew it before that."

"Before that." She crossed the floor slowly, and hated how long her strides had become, how many steps it took her small body to cover ground that used to take five. She stopped within arm's reach of him — well below arm's reach; the top of her head aligned roughly with his sternum — and looked up, and made sure he saw her do it without flinching. "Explain that, Mr. Underhill."

"I served a posting under your authority. Eleven years ago. Fleet logistics review, Meridian sector." His tone didn't change, but something behind his eyes did — a shutter drawing halfway closed. "You signed my decommission order."

Ilsa felt the memory surface and turn over like a body in water. *Underhill.* Yes. Yes, she remembered now — a war android, combat-class, flagged for mandatory retirement after the armistice, its service record a ledger of actions she'd reviewed at a desk in a room that smelled of recycled air and coffee gone cold. She'd signed hundreds of such orders. Machines didn't suffer. That had been the doctrine, clean and simple, the moral arithmetic that let the whole apparatus function. You didn't decommission a person. You retired a tool.

She had signed his order with the same pen she'd used for supply requisitions.

"And yet you salute me," she said.

"You held my commission. The habit persists." He said it evenly, but she had spent eleven years reading officers across briefing tables, and she heard the seam in it. Habit. As if habits were just things that happened to machines, like rust.

"Mr. Underhill, I'm going to ask you a series of questions. Some will seem strange. Answer them precisely."

"Yes, Captain."

"What year did you receive your decommission order?"

"Fleet standard date 4471.3."

"What was the name of the reviewing officer besides myself?"

His eyes flickered — actually flickered, a fractional stutter of focus, like a lens hunting. "I... don't recall."

"You don't recall. What was the stated justification for your retirement?"

"Combat-class obsolescence following the armistice."

"Which armistice?"

Another flicker, longer this time. His brow furrowed — a human expression running on machine architecture, and it unsettled her more than the blank answers had. "The... the armistice." He stopped. Stood very still. "Captain, I appear to be unable to answer."

"The war, Mr. Underhill. Who fought in it? Who won? How many died?" She took a step closer, looking up into that broad, patient face. "What was your designation before you were Gideon Underhill? What did you do in the war? What did you *see?*"

Nothing. Not refusal — she knew refusal, had broken harder subjects than him across interrogation tables — but a genuine, structural absence, like knocking on a door and finding no room behind it. His mouth opened. Closed. And then he said, with a quiet bewilderment that was somehow worse than any evasion:

"I don't know, Captain. I assumed those records were purged at decommissioning. Standard practice." But his voice had dropped half a register, and his hands — massive, scarred-plated hands — had curled slightly at his sides, and Ilsa watched him realize, in real time, that he had never once wondered about the hole until she'd put her finger in it.

*He doesn't know what he doesn't know.* The thought arrived cold and complete. *They didn't purge his records. They took the war out of him and left the shape of it, and he's been living around the absence for eleven years, calling it peace.*

Behind her, she heard Reyes murmur to Adeyemi, and Okonkwo's stylus go still against her tablet. Even the children understood what they were watching: their captain finding the edges of a wound in something that wasn't supposed to be able to bleed.

"Thank you, Mr. Underhill," Ilsa said, and softened her voice deliberately, because cruelty was easy and useless and she had never once needed it. "That will be all for now."

He saluted again — slower this time, the motion carrying a faint hesitation it hadn't had before — and withdrew down the corridor, his footfalls fading like a tide going out.

* * *

For a long moment nobody spoke. Then Okonkwo set down her tablet and said, in the careful voice of a junior officer venturing past the safety line, "Captain. You knew him. Before."

"I signed his papers." Ilsa climbed onto the bench so she could see all their faces at once, and refused to feel foolish about it. "Eleven years ago. He was a war machine and I was a review officer, and I signed him out of the fleet the way I signed a thousand other orders, because the doctrine said machines can't suffer and I had no reason to doubt it." She paused. "I have reason to doubt it now."

"Because he saluted you?"

"Because he hesitated." She looked at the doorway he'd gone through. "Machines don't hesitate when they find a gap in themselves. Tools don't grieve their missing parts. That man — and I use the word advisedly — has been hollowed out and doesn't know it, and when I showed him the hollow just now, something in him *felt* it. Doctrine be damned."

Reyes shifted on his too-large bench. "Captain, with respect — we're children. Our entire crew is children. We're stranded on a beacon at the edge of settled space, in custody of an android with holes in his head, and you're worried about whether the android has feelings?"

"I'm worried about what removed them," Ilsa said, "and whether the same someone did this to us." She let her gaze travel the table — eleven small faces, eleven sets of adult eyes in childish frames, her family shrunk and frightened and holding formation because she asked them to. "Think about it. Whoever reversed our bodies left our minds untouched. Whoever gutted that android left his skills intact and took his history. Same technique. Same craftsmanship, if Adeyemi's right. Somebody in this universe is editing people, and they've done it twice that we know of."

The silence that followed had weight. Okonkwo broke it, quietly: "There were twelve of us when we went into the Calyx corridor, Captain."

Ilsa looked at her. Counted heads. Did it twice, because she did not trust herself.

Eleven.

"Who's missing?" she asked, and heard her own voice go very small and very young, and hated it.

Okonkwo turned the tablet around. Crew manifest, return leg, day four hundred twelve. Twelve names. She ran her finger down the list and stopped near the bottom, and Ilsa read the name there — a name she knew, a face she'd promoted, a bunk two doors from her own — and felt the floor of the world tilt another degree.

"He's not on the ship," Okonkwo said. "He's not on the beacon. He's not anywhere in the sensor shadow, and Captain — his escape pod cradle is empty."

Outside the viewport, the beacon's great lamp turned its slow, indifferent circle, sweeping light across a platform where a ship that should not exist sat parked beside a machine that could not remember why he'd been broken — and somewhere out beyond all of it, in the dark between the charts, a twelfth man was either dead, escaped, or waiting.

Ilsa Voss, nine years old, captain of the shrunken, stood up on the bench so she could see out the window, and gave the order that would spend the next nineteen days of all their lives:

"Mr. Underhill. Get back here. We need to talk about your beacon's long-range transmitter — and about who else knows we've come home."
