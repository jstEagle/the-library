## 5. What the Crew Remembers

The Petra's mess hall had been built for forty adults and now held thirty-one children who remembered being those adults, and the arithmetic of it sat in Ilsa Voss's chest like a stone she kept trying to swallow.

She stood at the head of the long table on a chair that had been stacked with two seat cushions so she could see over the tabletop — a humiliation she had decided, in the first hour of her new smallness, to treat as a command decision rather than an indignity. Captains did not sit where they could not see their people. So: cushions. So: elevation. If anyone smiled about it, they were wise enough not to let her catch them.

The overhead lights ran at emergency amber because the ship's power plant was still sulking after the crash-landing, and in that light the faces around the table looked like portraits of her officers painted by someone who loved them but had never met them. Lieutenant Okonkwo, nine years old, with his adult hands' habits already showing through — he drummed his fingers in the same triple-tap pattern he'd used during the Kestrel negotiations, when he'd been forty-six and the terror of three fleets. Doctor Amara Reyes, seven, feet swinging above the deck, her surgical precision intact and her body barely tall enough for the instruments. Navigator Sefu Brandt, eleven, who had cried for twenty minutes when they'd landed and then stopped all at once, mid-sob, the way soldiers learn to.

And Ensign Tallis, six years old, the youngest of them, who had been twenty-eight when the Petra folded.

Ilsa made herself look at each face in turn. Command habit. You counted your people the way you counted rounds: constantly, ritually, because the moment you assumed the number was the moment it changed.

Thirty-one. The Petra had left Meridian with thirty-two.

* * *

"Start from the fold," she said. "Not from launch. We've done launch twice. Start from the thing itself."

Brandt raised his hand — then caught himself, scowled at his own reflex, and lowered it. "We were eleven days into dead space. Past the Relay Corridor, past anything charted. Sensors had been lying to us for two days — mass readings that didn't agree with themselves, star positions drifting when we weren't moving." He paused, and Ilsa watched him reach for precision the way a man reaches for a handrail in the dark. "Then space ahead of us went wrong."

"Wrong how?" Okonkwo asked. He was taking notes in a ledger meant for adult hands; his letters came out enormous, childish, and every few words he'd stop and stare at them as if they'd betrayed him personally.

"Folded," Brandt said. "That's the only word that fits. Not a wormhole — there was no throat, no mouth, no gradient. It was more like..." He searched. "You know when you fold a map, and two places that were a thousand kilometers apart are suddenly touching? Space did that. A region maybe four hundred klicks across, and inside it, time wasn't running in one direction. Our own telemetry came back to us before we sent it. We received our future sensor logs."

Silence around the table. In the amber light, Ensign Tallis pulled her knees up onto her chair and wrapped her arms around them, making herself even smaller than she already was.

"We should have gone around," Reyes said quietly. It was not an accusation. It was worse than an accusation — it was the flat tone of a doctor reading a chart she'd read too many times. "Every protocol we have says you do not enter an uncharted spacetime anomaly with a civilian-grade hull. And we went in."

"We went in," Ilsa said, "because I ordered it."

She let that sit. She had learned, somewhere in her forties — in the body she no longer occupied — that a captain who never said *my order, my fault* trained her crew to hide things from her. And right now, hiding things was the most dangerous activity aboard.

"I ordered it because the fold was emitting a structured signal. Repeating. Mathematical. Someone or something was announcing itself, and I judged that learning what it was outweighed the risk." She looked down the table. "I still think that judgment was correct. What happened next is the part I need from all of you, because my memory of it has gaps."

"Gaps how?" Okonkwo's pen had stopped.

"Gaps like missing pages. I remember entering the fold. I remember the signal resolving into something like a lattice — a structure, geometric, deliberate. I remember the ship shuddering and every clock aboard disagreeing with every other clock. And then I remember waking up in the med bay with these—" she lifted one small hand, turned it in the amber light, watched the fingers flex, absurdly smooth, absurdly new, "—and eleven days gone that nobody can account for."

"Not nobody," Brandt said. "Tallis remembers some of it."

Every head turned. Tallis pressed her face closer to her knees.

"Tallis." Ilsa kept her voice level, warm, the voice she'd used on frightened junior officers through two wars. "Whatever you remember, I need it. Even if it doesn't make sense. Especially then."

The girl — the ensign — the twenty-eight-year-old intelligence analyst wearing a six-year-old's body — raised her head slowly. Her eyes were dry but red-rimmed, the eyes of someone who had run out of tears and kept the rest of the grief.

"The eleven days are backwards," she said. "For me. I lived them in reverse. I remember the end first and the beginning last, and the middle keeps... reordering itself when I try to hold it." She took a breath that shook only a little. "But I remember the lattice. It wasn't a structure, Captain. It was a machine. Or the corpse of one. Something ancient sitting in the dead space doing — something — to time. And I remember that we didn't find it by accident."

The stone in Ilsa's chest shifted.

"What do you mean?"

"Our course correction. Day nine. The one that put us on intercept with the fold." Tallis's voice had gone very quiet, very steady, the way voices go when the speaker has rehearsed this. "It didn't come from navigation. It came from below decks. From the data core. Someone fed us a falsified survey ping — a ghost contact — and navigation chased it. I found the spoof signature afterward, in the reversed days. It was military-grade. Fleet intelligence encryption, the new cipher suite, the one only three ships in the fleet carry."

* * *

The silence that followed lasted long enough for Ilsa to hear the ship herself: the tick of cooling metal in the hull, the wheeze of the environmental scrubbers running at reduced capacity, the distant groan of the beacon platform beneath them bearing a weight it had never been asked to hold.

One name moved through her mind, and she hated it for moving there, and she could not stop it.

"Crewman Aldous Verren," she said.

Reyes flinched. Okonkwo's pen went still. Brandt closed his eyes briefly, like a man hearing a diagnosis he'd already suspected.

"He was systems integration," Okonkwo said carefully. "Data core access. He had the clearance."

"He had the temperament," Reyes said, sharper than she probably intended. She caught herself, glanced at Ilsa, and continued more gently. "I'm not saying guilty. I'm saying — Verren volunteered for this voyage when nobody else wanted it. He was quiet. He kept to himself. He spent more time in the data core than anywhere, and when any of us came near he'd close whatever he was working on. I told myself it was shyness." Her small hands flattened on the table. "I told myself a lot of things about Verren."

"When did anyone last see him?" Ilsa asked.

"That's the thing," Brandt said. "That's the thing that's eating me alive, Captain." He pulled up his slate — comically oversized against his eleven-year-old frame — and turned it so the table could see the crew manifest, thirty-two names, thirty-one highlighted present. "Verren isn't just missing. His absence is *clean*. No suit logged out of the airlock. No shuttle departed. No escape pod jettisoned — I've checked the mechanical logs, not just the software. The pod bay shows zero launches since the fold. But his biometric tag dropped off internal sensors at day fourteen of the return leg, three hours before we emerged, and it has not reappeared. A person does not stop existing on a sealed ship."

"A person stops existing," Reyes said slowly, "if the ship's timeline folds around them. If part of the crew aged backward and part of the ship's history got... rewritten..."

"Then why are we all here?" Okonkwo demanded. "Why are we children instead of scattered across eleven days like ash?"

Nobody answered. The scrubbers wheezed. Somewhere aft, a bulkhead ticked as it cooled, patient as a clock.

Ilsa became aware, then, of a presence at the mess hall's doorway — the android, Gideon, standing with the particular stillness that machines have and living things only pretend to. He had been there, she realized, for some time. Long enough to hear the name Verren. Long enough to hear *fleet intelligence* spoken aloud in a room full of children.

He didn't ask permission to speak. She would learn later that this was unusual for him; in that moment it simply registered as insolence, and then, immediately, as courage.

"Begging the captain's pardon," he said, and his voice was deep and unhurried, a voice built for orders and somehow gentler than its manufacture, "but the Authority pings this platform every six hours, and the next sweep is in forty minutes. Whatever you decide about Crewman Verren, decide it knowing that in forty minutes I have to file a report about what this beacon detected. And what this beacon detected," he paused, and Ilsa watched him choose his words with visible care, "was a warship-class vessel returning along a vector that officially does not exist, carrying a crew complement of thirty-two life signs that now reads thirty-one."

"You counted us," Ilsa said.

"I count everything, Captain. It's what I'm for." Something flickered behind his optics — not quite humor, not quite apology. "The question the Authority will ask is where the thirty-second life sign went. They'll ask it harder than you're asking it. And unlike you, they won't care about the answer. Only the asset value."

"They can't claim us," Okonkwo started, and then stopped, because everyone at that table knew exactly how much the Authority could claim, given paperwork and indifference.

Gideon's gaze came back to Ilsa. Waiting. An android, waiting on the word of a nine-year-old, and meaning it — she could see that he meant it, and the strangeness of that lodged somewhere under her ribs beside the stone.

"Forty minutes," she said. "Thank you. Stand by."

He inclined his head and withdrew. His footsteps made no sound. That, too, she noted, and filed away: a war android walking silently, as though he'd learned somewhere that loud machines get decommissioned.

* * *

When he was gone, she turned back to her officers — her shrunken, brilliant, terrified family — and made herself say the hard thing before anyone else could soften it.

"Here's where we stand. Someone aboard — likely Verren, possibly not alone — steered us into that fold deliberately, using fleet intelligence ciphers. The fold aged us backward while leaving our minds intact, which means whoever designed this wanted experienced minds in portable bodies, or wanted witnesses who couldn't be disbelieved, or wanted something I haven't thought of yet. Verren vanished without using a pod, which means either the fold took him or he had means none of us know about. And the truce between the fleets expires in nineteen days, with both sides hunting whatever did this to us." She looked down the length of the table, at the small hands and old eyes. "So we have a saboteur we can't find, a phenomenon we don't understand, a deadline we can't extend, and a landlord who wants to sell us. Questions?"

Ensign Tallis raised her hand. Six years old, arm straight, formal as a parade.

"Tallis."

"Captain." The girl's voice wavered once and steadied. "In the reversed days, I remember one more thing. I didn't want to say it in front of—" a glance toward the empty doorway, "—in front of him. The android." She swallowed. "The falsified ping that lured us into the fold? It didn't originate from the data core. I was wrong about that, or the reordered days corrected me. It originated from outside the ship. From the direction of the Meridian Beacon."

The stone in Ilsa's chest went cold.

"This beacon," Brandt whispered. "The one we crashed on. The one we're sitting on right now."

Outside the viewport, the beacon's great lamp turned its slow, faithful circle, sweeping its light across the dark the way it had for decades, illuminating nothing, seeing nothing, asking nothing of anyone.

Ilsa looked at it and felt, for the first time since waking small, that she was the one being watched.
