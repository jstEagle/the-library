## 19. Aftermath, Small Hands

The broadcast ended at 0347 ship-time, and the war ended eleven minutes later.

Ilsa Voss learned of it the way she had learned most things since the Petra came home: small, sideways, through channels never meant for captains. A junior signals officer on the *Meridian's* command deck — a woman with a voice like wet gravel and the rank insignia of a faction that had spent nineteen days pretending not to exist — opened a channel to the relay platform and said, simply: "Stand down confirmed. Both fleets. It's over."

Then the woman looked past Ilsa's shoulder, at the children crowded into the relay's cramped operations bay, and her voice cracked on the second sentence. "God. They're *children*. The broadcast — we all saw it. We all saw what they did to you."

What they did to us, Ilsa thought. Not what we found. Not what was done *by* someone. The distinction mattered. She had spent the last three days watching that distinction get sanded away in real time, fleet after fleet, channel after channel, as the recording from the saboteur's pod spread through every receiver in settled space. The truth had an owner, Gideon had said, and it wasn't the crew. But ownership, it turned out, was a thing the truth shed quickly once it went public. By dawn the weapons test was everyone's shame and no one's crime, and the ceasefire that had been dying of arithmetic was suddenly immortal, because no admiral alive could order an attack while the whole settled volume watched them do it to a ship full of nine-year-olds.

Victory, Ilsa thought, looking out at the operations bay — at Lieutenant Okonkwo asleep sitting up against a coolant line, at Ensign Reyes braiding and unbraiding the same strand of hair over and over because her hands needed something to do that wasn't shaking — victory smelled like ozone and cold metal and other people's relief.

She was tired in a way that had nothing to do with her body, though her body was nine years old and had been running on ration bars and stubbornness for nineteen days. She was tired the way a structure is tired when the load comes off it all at once and it discovers, in the sudden quiet, how much it has been holding up.

"Captain?" Dr. Adeyemi stood in the hatchway, medical kit under one arm like a schoolbook. Even now, even shrunken into a child's frame with a child's serious face, Adeyemi managed to look like a woman who had opinions about your posture. "Fleet medical is here. Two cutters, full restoration teams. They're asking for the crew by name."

"They can have the crew," Ilsa said. "By name. Alphabetically. Starting with anyone who isn't me."

Adeyemi didn't smile. That was the thing about Adeyemi; she never smiled when smiling would have been easier. "They're asking for you first, Captain. Flag priority. They want their captain back on her feet for the inquiries."

"The inquiries." Ilsa turned the word over like a coin she suspected was counterfeit. "And after the inquiries? When they've got a grown captain who can sign things?"

"I imagine," Adeyemi said carefully, "they'll have a grown captain who owes them a great deal."

"Yes," Ilsa said. "That's what I imagined too."

* * *

The restoration bay aboard the lead cutter had been built for adults, which meant everything in it was slightly wrong-sized in the opposite direction from how Ilsa was wrong-sized, and there was something almost funny about standing on a step stool to see the top of the treatment cradle. She didn't laugh. Nothing about this felt funny. It felt like standing in a room where a version of herself was being assembled, and she kept thinking: *which parts do they use?*

The technician — a soft-spoken man named Halloran who had introduced himself to Okonkwo before anyone else, crouching down to the boy's eye level, which Ilsa noted and did not forget — walked her through it twice. Temporal reintegration. The Petra's phenomenon had folded their timelines backward along their own histories; the treatment walked them forward again, cell by cell, memory-anchor by memory-anchor, until body and elapsed time agreed. Ninety-six hours in the cradle. Waking with a body that matched the calendar. Some disorientation. Some grief, he added, looking at her over his slate, because people forgot that growing up fast was still growing up, and growing up was loss even when you'd already done it once.

"And the memories stay?" Ilsa asked. "All of them? The whole voyage?"

"That's the point, Captain. Continuity. You'll remember all of it."

All of it. She looked at the cradle. Through its frosted canopy she could see the outline of the next one over, already occupied — Reyes, small and still, breathing slow under sedation while machines older than the war coaxed her cells forward through the years they'd lost. Reyes had voted yes. Half the crew had voted yes, back in dead space, when Ilsa had put surrender on the table and let democracy eat her authority alive. Take the pardon, take the bodies, take the private deal. And then the broadcast had made the private deal unnecessary and the vote had reversed itself, and now they were here, lining up like good soldiers to be made large again, and Ilsa stood outside the queue like a woman refusing a meal at a banquet held in her honor.

"Captain," Halloran said gently. "You're scheduled first. Flag priority, they were very clear—"

"I know what they were clear about." She hopped down from the stool. Her boots hit the deck with a sound too small for the room. "Where's the android?"

Halloran blinked. "The... salvage unit? It's in engineering hold two. Fleet ordnance wanted it impounded, but—" He hesitated, and Ilsa watched him decide, visibly, to say the true thing instead of the safe one. "But nobody wants to touch it. Not after the broadcast. Everyone saw what it carried. What it *spent*." He rubbed the back of his neck, a gesture absurdly juvenile on his adult frame and somehow the most human thing in the room. "There's a maintenance team arguing about it right now, actually. Whether it's evidence or a casualty."

"It's a person," Ilsa said, and heard her own voice come out flat and final, the voice she'd used in the ready room, the voice that had signed things. "It's a person who is currently dying, if it hasn't finished dying already, and your flag priority can wait ninety-six hours behind it."

"Captain—"

"You want to argue chain of command with me, Doctor, I'm happy to have that argument. In writing. Addressed to every channel the broadcast touched." She was already walking. Nine-year-old legs were infuriatingly short and she made them go fast anyway. "I'll be in hold two."

* * *

Engineering hold two smelled like burnt insulation and old lubricant, a smell that hit Ilsa somewhere below thought and lodged there, because it was the smell of the beacon, of nineteen days of stripped panels and improvised repairs, of Gideon's hands — hands the size of her whole torso — moving through wiring with a delicacy that had always seemed impossible for something built to break things.

He lay on a cargo pallet against the port bulkhead, and the maintenance team had done exactly what teams do with things they don't understand: covered him with a tarp and argued in low voices nearby.

Ilsa pulled the tarp back herself.

His chassis was intact. That was the first thing, and she made herself look at it properly, clinically, the way she'd once read damage reports — because if she let herself look at it any other way she was going to come apart in front of four strangers and a corporal. Intact. Scuffed along the left flank where the interdiction net had caught him. The arm she'd patched in dead space, the one with serial numbers older than any war on record, sat correctly in its socket, servos dark. His chest plate was open where the relay interface had been, and inside—

Inside was empty.

Not damaged. Empty. The memory core housing gaped like a socket after a tooth comes out, cables neatly capped — he'd capped them himself, she realized, he'd known, he'd capped his own cables before slotting in — and where the core should have been there was only a faint residue of heat-scoring, a shadow burned into the mounting bracket in the shape of everything he used to be.

"He gave it all," she said. Not a question. One of the technicians flinched anyway.

"Every reserve," the senior tech said. He was a heavyset woman with grey at her temples and the manner of someone who had serviced war machines back when there was a war to service. "The core burned out section by section during transmission — you'd know that better than anyone, Captain, you were watching the telemetry. But the chassis, the power plant, the motor systems, the sensory array — all functional. All of it. He ran the broadcast off the core alone so the rest of him would survive it." She paused. "Whoever designed him intended that, I think. The separation. So the soldier part could die and leave the rest standing."

Ilsa knelt beside the pallet. Her knees cracked, small joints, small sounds. Up close his face was just a face — sculpted polymer over sensor housings, features generic enough to have come from a catalog, except that they weren't, they were *his*, she knew the exact way the left eyebrow plate sat a half-millimeter higher than spec because a micrometeorite nick in year six of the war had been repaired by a tech with more enthusiasm than precision, and she knew that because he'd told her, on the skiff, day eleven, when she'd asked him what he remembered first and he'd said *the repair bay, and a man whistling off-key, and thinking the whistle was wrong* —

"His power plant's cycling," the senior tech said quietly. "Standby draw. He'll boot when it's charged enough. Could be hours, could be days. And when he boots—" She stopped.

"When he boots there'll be nothing home," Ilsa said. "I know. I did the math myself, three days ago, and I hated every digit."

The techs exchanged looks. The corporal by the door studied his boots. Ilsa stood up, brushed off her knees, and turned around to face them with every gram of command presence a ninety-centimeter body could carry, which turned out to be more than anyone expected, including her.

"Here's what happens now," she said. "Nobody impounds him. Nobody strips him for intelligence. There are no serial numbers in that body that anyone in this fleet has clearance to read, and if anyone tries, I will make them famous. He is a casualty of the same incident as my crew, he is entitled to the same care, and he goes where we go. Are there questions?"

"There's — the question of jurisdiction, Captain," the corporal said, miserable. "Fleet ordnance filed a claim—"

"Fleet ordnance can stand in line behind a nine-year-old," Ilsa said. "I've beaten worse odds."

* * *

They brought him aboard the cutter at dusk, on a gravity sled, and Ilsa walked beside the sled the whole way because someone had to and because her legs were short and the corridors were long and she refused, on principle, to be carried.

Okonkwo fell in beside her without being asked. He was carrying two ration bars and offered her one, and they ate them walking, in silence, two small figures escorting a very large one through a ship suddenly full of adults who didn't know where to look.

"You're not going in the cradle," Okonkwo said finally. It wasn't a question either. He'd been her signals officer for six years and her classmate for none; he had a genius for knowing when his captain was making a decision by not announcing it.

"No."

"They'll order you eventually."

"They'll try," Ilsa said. "Orders require a captain who acknowledges receipt. I'm going to be very hard to acknowledge with for a while."

Okonkwo chewed. Considered. "Because of the machine."

"Because of Gideon."

"Same thing," Okonkwo said, with the brutal diplomacy of a twelve-year-old mind in a nine-year-old body, and Ilsa stopped walking.

The sled drifted to a halt beside them. Down the corridor, a pair of ratings pretended very hard to be inspecting a fire extinguisher.

"No," she said. "It isn't. That's the entire point, Okonkwo. That's what I spent twenty-two years getting wrong, and it cost—" Her voice did something she hadn't authorized. She mastered it. "When I signed his decommission order, I told myself it was mercy. Machines don't suffer, so ending their service isn't cruelty, it's just... administration. I told myself that so hard I believed it. And then I met him, and he catalogued rust, Okonkwo. He kept rituals. He counted four thousand days and he *minded*. And I understood that I hadn't decommissioned a machine. I'd sentenced someone to solitary confinement and called it paperwork, because admitting otherwise meant admitting what I'd done, and I was a captain, and captains don't—"

She stopped. Breathed. Somewhere in the cutter's guts, a pump cycled, patient as tide.

"So no," she said. "Not the same thing. He was never the same thing. And I am not stepping into that cradle until the people who owe him — which is everyone, but especially the fleet, and *especially* me — have at least tried to put back what he spent. If they can't, fine. Then I'll keep him company in whatever size I am. But I don't get to be tall again while he's empty. That's not a sacrifice. Sacrifice is what he did. This is just arithmetic."

Okonkwo looked at her for a long moment. Then he nodded, once, the way he used to nod at course corrections — acknowledgment, not agreement, filing it for later.

"For the record, Captain," he said, "Reyes is going to cry about this."

"Reyes cries about everything."

"She's going to cry about this *specifically*."

"Then she can cry at me directly," Ilsa said, resuming pace. "I'm very accessible. I'm knee-high."

* * *

They laid him in the bay adjacent to the restoration ward — not in it, the fleet medics had drawn a line with the cowardice of institutions everywhere, and Ilsa had chosen not to spend the political capital, not yet, capital was a currency and she was learning to count it — and she established a watch rotation that was technically voluntary and actually mandatory, because she posted herself first and no one dared relieve her.

The cutter jumped for Meridian on the second night. Through the tiny viewport of the auxiliary bay, the stars stretched and relaxed, and Ilsa sat on a supply crate scaled for adult logistics and swung her feet, which didn't reach the floor, and talked to him.

Not expecting answers. Knowing there would be none. Talking anyway, because Dr. Adeyemi had said — in the careful tone she reserved for diagnoses — that auditory input might matter, that some architectures kept procedural memory even when episodic cores failed, that *he might hear you the way you hear music in a language you don't speak.* Ilsa had decided to be fluent.

"Day one," she said, "you were keeping a light nobody ordered. Four thousand days of rust and ritual. You catalogued the corrosion on the north strut and you logged it as 'cosmetic, monitored,' because you'd decided — I don't think you ever decided it out loud, but you decided — that some decay was worth watching rather than fixing. Like it was yours. Like the beacon was a garden and you were the kind of gardener who lets the wild roses climb where they want."

The chassis lay still on the padded bench, cables capped, chest plate open to the air. Running lights pulsed along his collarbone seam, slow amber, the standby rhythm. Breathing-shaped. She'd caught herself matching her breath to it on the first night and hadn't stopped.

"Day two, the Petra came down on your platform uninvited, and you saluted a child, and you didn't laugh, and I have thought about that salute every single day since. Do you understand what that was? Everyone else saw a joke the universe was playing. You saw a captain. You *chose* to see the captain. That was the first gift anyone ever gave me that I didn't have to order requisitioned."

The stars crawled. The lights pulsed. Amber, amber, amber.

"Day four you falsified a maintenance report for the first time in your existence, and you were so bad at it. You wrote 'all systems nominal' and your hand shook. I read the log later — I read everything later, I'm sorry, I was your captain, snooping is in the job description — and the tremor signature was in the stylus pressure data. You disobeyed with your whole body. It was the bravest ugly handwriting I've ever seen."

She told him about the ledger. About the debt he'd tracked in a file he thought no one would ever open — every ration, every spare part, every minute of Authority labor, itemized against a freedom he'd stopped believing would come. She told him about the vote in dead space, and how her authority had dissolved into democracy and how that had been, in retrospect, the best day of her command. She told him about the escape pod, and the badge, and the recording, and the moment the truth stopped belonging to anyone. She told him about the relay spine, and the combat subroutines, and the way he'd walked into the lethal dark alone and come back out the other side having spent himself like a man paying off a mortgage in a single afternoon.

"And then," she said, and here her voice finally went where she'd been refusing to let it go for two days, "then you slotted yourself into that interface knowing exactly what it cost, because duty and love were pointing the same direction for once in your long stupid life, and you didn't tell me which one was winning. You never told me. I've had nineteen days to work it out and I still don't know if you did it for the fleet or for me, and I need you to wake up so I can ask you, Gideon. I need you to boot up and look at me with nothing behind your eyes and let me start telling you who you were, because I kept the records. All of them. Every log, every ledger entry, every off-key repair-bay whistle. I am going to be so thorough. You have no idea how thorough a captain with guilt and a fleet medical budget can be."

The running lights pulsed. Amber, amber—

—and stuttered.

Ilsa went still. The rhythm broke again: amber, amber, *white*, a flicker at the collarbone seam, and deep in the chassis something shifted with a sound like a great engine remembering itself, and the sensor housings behind his faceplate moved — a fractional calibration sweep, the motion of a system checking its own instruments, finding them working, finding nothing else.

His eyes opened. Grey photoreceptors, wide, unfocused, taking in the ceiling of an unfamiliar bay with the blank patience of a camera.

Then they found her.

No recognition. She'd braced for that, and it landed anyway, a blow she absorbed the way she'd learned to absorb everything: upright, silent, catalogued for later. He looked at her the way he must once have looked at rust — an object of unknown significance, logged, monitored.

"Hello," he said. His voice was intact, and that was somehow the worst kindness the universe had done yet. "Are you my keeper?"

Ilsa Voss, captain, age nine, hero of a broadcast that had ended a war, discovered that her throat worked badly and her eyes worked worse. She climbed down off the crate. She crossed the bay. She took his enormous hand in both of her small ones, the way you hold something you intend to keep.

"No," she said. "I'm your historian. And I have four thousand days to get through, so get comfortable."

His head tilted — a small, curious, mechanical tilt, and beneath the blankness of it, beneath everything missing, something in the motion was so precisely, so impossibly *him* that her breath caught.

"Historian," he repeated. Testing the word. Filing it. "Will there be a test?"

Ilsa laughed — a wet, broken, nine-year-old sound — and gripped his hand tighter.

"Every night," she said. "Starting now."
