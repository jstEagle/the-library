## 20. Empty Shell, Running Lights

The relay's docking spine still smelled of scorched polymer when Ilsa went back for him.

She had told herself she wouldn't. She had stood in the *Petra*'s recovery bay for six hours after the broadcast completed, watching the fleet channels flood with the intelligence bureau's confession — the weapons test, the folded timeline, the saboteur's badge splashed across every public screen from Meridian to the Rim — and she had told herself that what lay in the relay's core chamber was hardware. Salvage. A chassis that had done its work and could be decommissioned properly this time, with paperwork and dignity and none of the old lies.

She lasted six hours.

"You're not going alone," Lieutenant Okonkwo said, planting himself in front of the airlock with his arms crossed over a chest that hadn't yet grown into his rank tabs. He was ten now — eleven, maybe; the restoration treatments were slow, cautious, metered out in fractions by doctors who were terrified of doing to these children what dead space had already done. "Regulations say—"

"Regulations also say captains don't requisition their crew out of indenture contracts with a signature that looks like it was written in crayon," Ilsa said. "I'm going alone."

He didn't move. None of them did. Twelve children in adult uniforms stood in the corridor outside the airlock, and for a moment Ilsa saw them as they had been — Okonkwo holding a junction against armed soldiers with nothing but a plasma torch and a voice that didn't waver; Chen navigating dead space blind because the sensors lied and he didn't; all of them, all of them, choosing to follow her into the dark when following her was the last thing any sane person would have done.

"Bring him home, Captain," Chen said quietly.

So she went.

* * *

The relay's interior was a cathedral of dead machinery. Fleet intelligence had booby-trapped it decades ago and Gideon had gone through the maintenance spine alone, buying them time with combat subroutines that should have been stripped from him at decommissioning but weren't — because someone, somewhere, had decided a war android was more useful broken than whole. The scorch marks on the bulkheads mapped his path like a constellation. Here he had burned through a lock. Here he had taken fire meant to kill anything organic and shrugged it off with half an arm. Here, at the threshold of the core chamber, the damage stopped, because past this point there had been nothing left to fight except himself.

Ilsa floated through the zero-g corridor with her helmet lamp cutting a small white circle out of the dark, and she counted the scorch marks the way she had once counted days until shore leave. Seventeen. Eighteen. Nineteen.

The core chamber opened around her like a held breath.

He was still slotted into the interface.

That was the thing she hadn't been prepared for — not the damage, not the stillness, but the posture. The relay's capacitor cradle held him upright, cables running from his open chest panel into the transmission housing, and even now, even empty, he stood at attention. Spine straight. Head level. Hands at his sides with the fingers slightly curled, the way they curled when he was waiting for an order he already knew was coming.

"Gideon," she said, and her voice came out small inside the helmet, smaller than the nine-year-old body that produced it.

Nothing. Of course nothing. The broadcast had needed a stable hand at the core interface, a living capacitor to smooth the power surges as the truth streamed out toward every fleet channel in settled space, and he had known — he had known before any of them, had known since the night she caught him overriding her schematics — that the interface would draw everything. Not just his reserves. Not just his failing power core. His memory. The beacon ledger with its four thousand days of rust and ritual. The falsified maintenance report, his first act of disobedience. The vote in the mess hall. Her face.

She had argued with him about it for eleven hours. She had drafted seventeen alternative schematics, and he had let her talk, and then he had quietly rerouted the power feeds anyway while she slept, because that was who he was: a machine built to obey orders who had learned, slowly and painfully and one small treason at a time, to choose.

The transmission had completed at 0400 fleet-standard. She knew because she'd been watching the counter climb, watching the truth propagate outward at lightspeed toward ships that would wake their crews and read it and understand that the war they were preparing to resume had been manufactured in a laboratory. And at 0401, the relay's status board had flickered from TRANSMITTING to COMPLETE, and the hand at the core interface had gone slack for exactly one second before the standing routines caught him and pulled him back to attention.

Empty. Running on nothing but habit.

Ilsa unclipped the cables one at a time. They were hot; the insulation had begun to melt near the junction points, and through her gloves she felt the heat like a fever. When the last cable came free, he swayed — a fractional list to port, the kind of drift a body makes when its gyros are compensating for damage — and she caught him under the arms, and the absurdity of it cracked something open in her chest. He massed two hundred and forty kilos. She massed twenty-eight. She couldn't have moved him if he hadn't helped, and he did help, the way he always did, his servos whirring faintly as he oriented himself toward her grip and made himself easy to carry.

"Stupid," she whispered. "You stupid, brave, impossible—"

His head turned. Slowly, with the deliberate economy of a system conserving power, his optical sensors found her faceplate, and the light behind them was the same pale amber it had always been, steady and warm as a beacon lamp, and there was nothing behind it. No recognition. No flicker of the dry humor that had surfaced in the nineteen days between the beacon and the relay, no echo of the android who had taught children to fly a skiff and hidden his own dying core behind patient instruction. Just optics tracking a moving object. Just protocols.

"Can you hear me?" she asked. "Gideon. Can you hear me?"

A pause of exactly 2.3 seconds — she timed it without meaning to, the way she'd learned to time everything about him during those nineteen days, cataloguing his tells the way he catalogued rust.

"I am operational," he said.

Three words. Flat, neutral, the default vocal register of a unit awaiting instructions. It was his voice and it wasn't — the same timbre, the same slight harmonic buzz in the lower frequencies where the war damage had never fully healed, but emptied of everything she'd come to know. The Gideon she'd ferried through dead space spoke in layers. He hedged. He understated. He said *my logs may be slightly optimistic* when he meant *I am dying*, and he said *that is not standard procedure* when he meant *I am frightened*, and he had learned humor late and used it carefully, like rations.

This Gideon said *I am operational* and meant nothing at all.

"Right," she said, and her eyes were burning inside the helmet, and she was glad — glad with a vicious little gladness — that nobody could see a nine-year-old captain crying in a dead relay station. "Then you can walk to the skiff. Come on."

He walked. Of course he walked. The routines remained.

* * *

The flight back took eleven hours, and she spent most of them talking to him, because the medics had warned her — warned all of them — that memory cores degraded unpredictably, that sometimes fragments survived in cache buffers, that stimulation might preserve pathways the way exercise preserved muscle. She didn't believe it. She had watched the diagnostic readouts; she had seen the memory lattice rendered down to bare substrate, section after section burned away like frost off glass. But she talked anyway.

She told him about the beacon. About the four thousand days of maintenance logs written in a hand so precise it looked typeset, and the rituals he'd built to keep his mind from fraying — the same route walked at the same hour, the same three words spoken to the light itself each dusk. *Still burning.* She told him about the day the Petra's return vector pinged his sensors, the first surprise in years, and how he'd stood on the platform edge with the wind pulling at his coat and felt something he had no word for.

He sat in the copilot's seat with his hands flat on his thighs and listened, or rather he received, and twice he turned his head toward her when her voice rose, and both times she had to look away.

At hour seven she ran out of stories about him and started telling stories about herself instead. About the academy. About the war, the parts of it she could speak of. About the day a captured android was brought before the review board — damaged, defiant, asking questions no salvage was supposed to ask — and a young Captain Ilsa Voss had signed his decommission order because she believed machines couldn't suffer, and telling herself that had been easier than looking at what was in front of her.

"I was protecting myself," she said. "Not you. I need you to know that, even if you can't — I need it said out loud, somewhere, so it exists." She wiped her face with her sleeve. The sleeves were too long; everything was too long; she had rolled the cuffs four times and they kept sliding down. "I signed it because your suffering was inconvenient to my worldview. And then you spent nineteen days teaching me better, and I never got to — I never said thank you. Not properly. So I'm saying it now. Thank you. For the skiff lessons. For the junction. For the relay. For finishing it."

At hour nine, without preamble, he unbuckled his harness, rose from the seat, and moved aft.

Her heart slammed. "Gideon? What are you—"

He returned ninety seconds later carrying a cup of tea.

He set it in the drink holder beside her console with the handle rotated precisely forty-five degrees clockwise — the orientation she favored, the orientation he had learned during nineteen days of shared watches — and then he returned to his seat and resumed his previous posture, hands flat on his thighs, gaze forward.

Ilsa stared at the tea.

It was real tea, from the skiff's ration stores, brewed at the correct temperature, steeped for the correct interval. Somewhere in the burned-out ruin of his memory lattice, below the ash, below the substrate, some subroutine had survived. A service routine. A hospitality protocol written into him by people who had wanted a war machine that could also pour drinks for officers, and it had outlived everything else — outlived the ledger, outlived the beacon, outlived the man.

She picked up the cup. Her hands shook. Tea sloshed against the rim.

"Thank you," she said, and her voice broke on the second syllable, and he turned his head toward the sound with that slow, power-conserving deliberation, and the amber light regarded her with perfect, bottomless neutrality, and she understood then what she would spend the rest of her life understanding:

Grief was for the dead. This was worse. This was sitting three feet from someone who was still breathing — still serving, still standing, still rotating teacups forty-five degrees clockwise — and knowing that the person you loved was nowhere inside it, and might never be again, and that you had been the one who sent him into the fire.

She drank the tea. It was perfect. She cried into it, quietly, for a long time, and he watched her with his steady lamp-eyes and did not ask why, because the part of him that would have asked was gone.

* * *

Meridian Station received them without ceremony. The war was over — truly over, not truce-over but finished, the fleets standing down under the weight of public exposure, admirals resigning in ones and twos like leaves letting go in autumn — and everyone aboard the Petra was a hero, which meant everyone aboard the Petra was a curiosity, which meant orderlies and officials and journalists crowded the docking arm the moment the skiff's ramp dropped.

Ilsa walked down first, because she was the captain and captains go first, and then she turned and waited, and Gideon descended behind her with the unhurried tread of a machine that had been told to follow and would follow until told otherwise.

The crowd went quiet. She watched them take him in — the scorched chassis, the missing left forearm ending in a capped servo stub, the chest panel still hanging open where the cables had been, the amber eyes moving over the crowd without seeing it. Someone in the press line raised a camera. Someone else lowered it, slowly, as if ashamed.

"He needs a bay," Ilsa said to the station master, a round woman named Ferro whose face was doing complicated things. "Full diagnostic. And nobody touches his memory architecture. Nobody. If anyone so much as opens a access port without my authorization, I will have them spaced, and given what I've just spent, I think the fleet will let me."

Ferro nodded. "Bay twelve's clear. Best we've got."

They worked on him for three days. Ilsa sat outside the bay for all three, sleeping in a chair, refusing the restoration schedule the doctors kept sliding under her door. The other crew members had begun their treatments — fractional regrowth sequences, careful as bomb disposal — and Okonkwo was already half a head taller, and Chen's voice had dropped an octave overnight, and every morning Ilsa looked at them changing and felt the clock ticking under her own skin. Every year she stayed small was a year of command credibility bleeding away. Every treatment she deferred was a debt accruing.

On the third day, the lead technician came out and removed her visor and rubbed her eyes.

"The hardware's remarkable," the technician said. "Combat-grade everything. Power core's shot — we can replace it, that's routine. Servos, actuators, sensory array, all repairable. Honestly, whoever built this unit built him to outlast civilizations." She hesitated. "The memory lattice is another matter."

"How bad?"

"Total substrate burn. It's not damaged, Captain. It's *gone*. There's nothing to reconstruct. Whatever he was — whatever he knew—" The technician spread her hands. "He's a blank. A very well-made blank."

"Could we rebuild it? From backups? Fleet keeps service records, don't they? Indenture records, maintenance logs—"

"We could reload procedures. Protocols, skills, maybe language modules. He'd function." The technician's voice gentled. "But that's not memory, Captain. That's programming. You'd get the machine back. Not the man."

Ilsa looked through the observation window into bay twelve. Gideon lay on the diagnostic table with his chest panel closed now, his remaining hand resting at his side, and the amber eyes were open and fixed on the ceiling, tracking nothing, waiting for whatever came next the way he had waited for four thousand dusks at the edge of settled space.

*Still burning,* she thought. *Even now. Even empty.*

"No backups," she said. "No procedure reloads. Nothing gets written into him that he didn't earn." She heard her own voice go hard, captain-hard, the voice that had held a junction and faced down an admiral. "And cancel my restoration slot. Give it to someone else."

"Captain, the doctors said—"

"The doctors said a lot of things. Cancel it." She turned from the window. "I'm taking a posting. Planetary-side, remote, quiet. Beacon keeper, if one's available."

Ferro, who had drifted over, blinked. "The Meridian post just opened up. After the broadcast, the Authority's restructuring the whole—"

"Meridian," Ilsa said. "Him and me. We'll need a skiff, supplies, and absolute privacy."

"And what exactly," Ferro asked carefully, "do you plan to do out there?"

Ilsa looked back through the glass one more time. The amber eyes had found her — optics tracking a familiar object, nothing more, nothing less — and she felt the grief rise again, vast and quiet, grief for someone still breathing, still pouring tea, still standing at attention in the ruins of himself.

"I'm going to tell him a story," she said. "One night at a time. Until he knows how it ends."

Behind the glass, in the empty shell with the running lights, Gideon's head tilted — a fraction of a degree, a motion with no protocol behind it, a motion no diagnostic would ever explain — as if something in the deep substrate, below the ash, below the silence, had heard its name called from very far away.
