## 13. Interdict

The exit vector out of the refinery's shadow was a clean line, and clean lines in contested space are lies told by mathematics.

Rosalind watched it draw itself across the nav-display, a thread of pale green stitching from the derelict refinery's tumbling hulk toward the Ceres Deep relay, and she did not trust it. Nothing that simple had survived the last three weeks. The *Magpie's Rest* had not been built for heroics — she was a hauler, old enough to remember when her class of ship was called honest, with a spine of welded freight-rails and an engine room that smelled permanently of hot dust and ozone. She had carried Rosalind through eleven years of grief at a steady half-light burn. Now she carried something else: a sealed archive that weighed nothing and everything, sitting in the ship's memory like a stone in a shoe.

"Fuel's holding," Idris said from the engineering couch, where he'd been lying for six hours with a diagnostic slate on his chest like a sleeping cat. "Holding" was generous. They'd stripped the refinery's auxiliary cells two days ago, and the power budget was a knife-edge arithmetic he recited in his sleep now — pods, transmitter, drive, life support, each one a mouth asking to be fed. "We can make the window."

"We can make the window," Rosalind agreed, because saying it aloud made it more true than the fuel gauge did.

The hold behind them was quiet. Eleven pods, running on trickle charge, their frost patterns blooming and fading like slow weather. She had stopped thinking of them as cargo years ago. She thought of them now as passengers who had not yet been told their destination.

She was reaching for the burn schedule when the proximity alarm woke.

Not the polite chime. The other one — the one that meant mass, lots of it, moving with intent. Rosalind's hand went to the console before her mind caught up, and the display bloomed with contacts: first one, then four, then a constellation of them, resolving out of the dark beyond the refinery's shadow like fish surfacing under ice. Concord hulls. Wedge-profiled, gunmetal, running lights in the cold disciplined blue of a fleet that had never once doubted its own paperwork. And behind them, further out, answering silhouettes in the ragged amber of the Meridian Compact — the enemy, or what the war insisted was still the enemy, converging on the same empty patch of nothing where a fuel-starved hauler was trying to cross.

"They found us," Idris said. He didn't sound surprised. He sounded tired in the way of a man watching weather he'd been predicting for days.

"The admiral's offer had a tracking clause," Rosalind said. "Polite things usually do."

Three days ago, at the edge of the refinery system, a Concord corvette had hailed them with exquisite courtesy. An Admiral Sera Okonkwo-Vance, commanding the Seventh Interdiction Group, wished to speak with the master of the vessel regarding "a matter of historical record." No threats. No demands. A voice like warm tea, offering full pardons — hers, Idris's, even Veyra's ghost of a cell — if they would simply surrender the archive intact and unexamined. *Intact and unexamined.* She had said no, politely, and the corvette had withdrawn with equal politeness, and Rosalind had spent three days knowing the politeness was a leash being paid out.

Now the leash came home.

* * *

The hail came forty minutes later, when both fleets had settled into their positions like animals circling a waterhole neither would share. On the main display, Admiral Okonkwo-Vance resolved into a portrait: silver at the temples, a face arranged around decades of reasonable decisions, a uniform so precisely fitted it looked grown rather than sewn. Behind her, the bridge of a Concord dreadnought held its breath in perfect order.

"Captain Thornbury." The admiral's voice arrived warm and unhurried, as if they were two neighbors discussing a fence line. "You've had time to consider my proposal. I confess I hoped you'd have called me first. It saves us both the theater."

"There's no theater on my bridge," Rosalind said. "Just bad fuel and worse company."

Idris, off-camera, made a small sound that might have been offense or agreement.

"I've read your file," the admiral continued, as though Rosalind hadn't spoken. "All of it. Including the parts you paid a great deal to keep sealed. Fifteen years ago, the *Cormorant*. Eleven names. You followed an order, Captain. A lawful order, issued through proper channels, from officers whose signatures were verified by systems older and more careful than either of us." She paused, letting the words settle like silt. "You were not guilty then. You are not guilty now. Guilt requires intent, and intent requires knowledge, and knowledge is exactly what that archive pretends to offer. I'm offering to take it off your hands. Intact. Unexamined. And in exchange — every charge, every warrant, every debt, dissolved. You could go back to hauling freight. Or you could finally stop hauling it."

Rosalind heard it then — not the words but the shape beneath them, the familiar architecture of the sentence. *Sign here. Forget here. The dead stay dead, the record stays clean, and you get to sleep at night.* She had signed a version of it at twenty-nine, in a room that smelled of recycled air and lilies someone had brought to make the silence feel kind. Fifteen years of silence, purchased at the price of eleven coffins she'd chosen to carry herself because the alternative was carrying nothing at all.

"You've done this before," Rosalind said quietly. "Offered this. To other people."

The admiral's expression did not change, which was itself an answer. "I've ended conflicts before, Captain. Wars end at tables, not at relays. Whatever you think that archive proves, broadcasting it will not bring back your crew, or my son, or the forty thousand at Meridian Bay. It will only teach everyone the worst thing anyone ever learned: that the people who led them lied. Is that mercy? Is that justice? Or is it just a longer list of names for a longer wall?"

For a moment — one long, treacherous moment — Rosalind let herself stand inside the offer. Pardons. Fuel. Sleep without the pods humming behind the bulkhead. A life where the war simply *ended*, cleanly, the way wars end in the histories children are given. She thought about how tired she was. She thought about how tired Idris looked. She thought about the eleventh pod, and the name inside it, and the way she still said goodnight to it sometimes when the watch was long.

Then she heard her own voice from fifteen years ago, young and hollowed-out, saying *yes* to a silence agreement because yes was easier than the truth, and she understood that the admiral wasn't offering her a future. She was offering her the same room, the same lilies, the same signature — a second silence to stack on top of the first, heavier this time, because now she knew what silence cost.

"No," Rosalind said.

The word came out smaller than she wanted. Small and complete.

Okonkwo-Vance sighed, and for just a moment the warmth dropped away and Rosalind saw the machinery underneath — not cruelty, something worse: administration. "I gave you courtesy, Captain. My board will note that. When we take that archive by force, the record will show I offered you every reasonable exit." She leaned forward slightly, and the portrait sharpened as if the camera could feel her attention. "There are eleven bodies in your hold. Under interdiction law, human remains in transit are the jurisdiction of the nearest Concord authority. Surrender the pods and the archive, and I will see your crew buried with honors. Refuse, and I am required to treat them as evidence in flight — and evidence, Captain, gets handled roughly."

The channel closed on the admiral's composed face, leaving Rosalind alone with the hum of the ship and the sound of her own breathing.

"Evidence," Idris repeated from the engineering couch. He sat up slowly, the diagnostic slate sliding to the deck. "She wants the pods."

"She wants witnesses," Rosalind said. "Dead ones don't testify. That's always been the point of them."

* * *

Both fleets began to move at once.

It was not an attack — not yet. It was positioning, the great slow violence of geometry, wedges and lances of ships sliding across the display to close the exit vector toward Ceres Deep. The Concord formed the anvil; the Meridian Compact, for reasons Rosalind could only guess at, formed the hammer, as if either fleet trusted the other less than they hated the truth in her hold. Between them, the *Magpie's Rest* hung at the refinery's shadow like a seed caught between millstones.

"They'll fight each other before they let the other side have us," Idris said. He'd moved to the copilot's seat, and his hands were doing the pre-burn checks without consulting him, muscle memory outrunning despair. "Which buys us maybe an hour. Maybe less."

"An hour's a broadcast window if we're rude enough about it."

"It's also a coffin if we're wrong." He turned to look at her, and in the low light his face was all angles and exhaustion and something underneath that he still couldn't say in plain words, though he'd tried, badly, twice, mid-firefight, and she'd answered him equally badly, and somehow that had been enough. "Ros. If we run for the relay, we run through both of them."

"Then we run through both of them." She keyed the helm and felt the old ship answer, engines waking with a shudder that traveled up through the deck plates and into her teeth. "I spent fifteen years being quiet, Idris. I did it politely. I did it lawfully. I did it the way she's asking me to do it now." The burn gauge climbed. The stars outside the viewport began, very slowly, to smear. "I'm done being handled."

The *Magpie's Rest* fell out of the refinery's shadow and into the open dark, and both fleets noticed.

Contacts bloomed. Hail requests stacked up — Concord blue, Compact amber, dozens of voices demanding, warning, offering, all of them talking over each other in the civilized language of ultimatum. Protocol's remnant fragment, coiled in the ship's systems since Ashfall, stirred at last, its dying-station voice threading through the comm static like smoke finding a draft:

*"They are converging. There is a gap. It is narrow. It has a cost."*

"Everything does," Rosalind said, and put her hand on the throttle, and drove the old hauler straight down the seam between two fleets, toward the narrow dark where the relay waited — while behind her, in the hold, eleven pods hummed their frost-slow dreams, and somewhere ahead, six minutes of truth ticked closer with every burn.
