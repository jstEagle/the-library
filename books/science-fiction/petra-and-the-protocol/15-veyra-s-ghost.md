## 15. Veyra's Ghost

The derelict refinery hung in the dark like a ribcage picked clean, its processing spines stripped of everything valuable decades ago, its tanks venting slow breaths of frozen atmosphere into the void. We'd been docked against its loading gantry for eleven hours when the proximity alarm sang — one soft note, the tone Idris had programmed for small craft, unarmed, alone.

I was in the hold when it sounded. I'd taken to sitting there lately, in the narrow aisle between the pods, the way other people sat in chapels. The eleven of them glowed their patient green. Ten of them, I corrected myself. Ten, and one dark window where Sefa's pod had gone cold and final, its readout a flat line I still couldn't look at directly, the way you can't look at the sun.

"Single occupant," Idris's voice came over the intercom, tight and careful. "No weapons signature. No escort. Ros — it's her."

I knew who he meant before he said the name. There was only one *her* left in the universe that mattered.

"Veyra."

"Alone. I've checked twice. She's running dark, no jammers, no countermeasures. She wants us to see her coming." A pause, and I could hear him doing the arithmetic he always did, the cold arithmetic that had saved us at Ashfall and cost him his cousin and his cause in the same breath. "It's either a trap or a surrender. I can't tell which."

"Then we find out," I said, and heard how flat my voice had gone, how much it sounded like the voice I'd used fifteen years ago when I'd been given an order I should have refused. Some habits live in the bones.

I climbed out of the hold. On my way through the corridor I paused at the locker where I kept the sidearm — my father's, older than the war, older than the lie — and I stood there with my hand on the latch for a long moment.

*Ros,* Idris said, and I realized he was standing behind me, had been for some time, watching me decide.

"If it's a trap," I said, "a gun won't save us. If it's not—"

"Then you'd be greeting her armed, and she'd know everything about us before she said a word." He reached past me and closed the locker. His hand stayed on mine a beat longer than the latch required. "You taught me that. Petra Station, the day we met. *The first thing you show a stranger is what you're afraid of.*"

"I don't remember saying that."

"You were drunk. You were right anyway."

* * *

The airlock cycled with a sound like a long exhale, and Veyra Osei came through it the way a ghost comes through a wall — without resistance, without ceremony, as if the vacuum itself had already taken everything from her that could be taken.

She was thinner than she'd been at Ashfall. The warmth she'd worn then, that too-generous hospitality, that smile calibrated to the millimeter — all of it was gone, and what remained underneath was smaller and older and, in a strange way, more honest. She wore a plain flight suit with no insignia. Her hands were empty and held slightly away from her body, the universal grammar of the unarmed, and I noticed that they were shaking. Not much. Just enough to see.

"Captain Thornbury." Her voice was rough, like a road after a storm. "Co-pilot Okonkwo."

"Veyra," Idris said, and didn't move from where he stood between her and the corridor that led to the hold. I loved him for that, and hated that I noticed.

"You sealed our ship," I said. "You seized the pods. You called dead people *collateral assets.* You were going to sell the archive back to the Concord and buy yourself an amnesty with it." I made my voice into a list, because lists don't shake. "So before you say anything else, you're going to stand there and tell me why I shouldn't open the airlock and let you drift."

She didn't flinch. I'd expected flinching. I'd expected the politician, the cell leader, the woman who could smile at you while her people welded your ship shut around your sleeping dead. Instead she just nodded, slowly, like a student confirming the terms of an exam.

"I would," she said. "I'd deserve it. I want you to know that first. Whatever you decide at the end of this conversation — and I'm asking for a conversation, nothing more — I want you to make the decision knowing that I would have deserved the airlock."

"Good," I said. "Start talking."

* * *

We didn't take her to the galley. It felt too much like hospitality, and hospitality was the weapon she'd used on us first. Instead we stood in the corridor, the three of us, in the narrow space between the cockpit and the hold, under lights Idris had dimmed to half because bright light made the ship feel like an interrogation room and none of us wanted to be the interrogator.

Veyra leaned against the bulkhead, and for a moment she looked at her own hands as though they belonged to someone she'd been reading about.

"Meridian Bay," she said. "You know what the official record says. You know what Protocol confessed. What you don't know — what nobody outside maybe six living people knows — is that the evidence didn't stay buried by accident. Archives don't bury themselves. Someone had to carry the original sensor logs, the fleet traffic recordings, the medical manifests from the relief stations that were turned away — carry them somewhere dark and seal them and lose the keys on purpose." She looked up. "I was twenty-six years old. I was a junior archivist with the Meridian Relief Coalition, and I was the someone."

The ship's air system cycled. Somewhere in the hold, a cryo-pod hummed its patient note. I found I had stopped breathing and made myself start again.

"The Concord didn't threaten me," she went on. "That's the part I need you to understand, because it's the part I spent twenty years not understanding about myself. Nobody threatened me. Nobody put a gun to my head or a price on my family. They just — showed me the size of it. The size of what the truth would cost. They sat me in a room with a kind old man from the Ministry of Reconciliation, and he walked me through it, gently, the way you'd walk someone through a terminal diagnosis. *If this comes out, the war doesn't end, Veyra. It restarts. Both fleets, full mobilization. Tens of millions dead, minimum, and the relief coalition is the first target on both sides' lists, because we're the ones holding the proof.* And then he said the sentence I've been hearing in my sleep ever since." Her voice dropped. "He said, *You can be the person who ended the ceasefire, or the person who kept the peace. Those are the only two options. There is no third door.*"

"There's always a third door," Idris said quietly.

"Yes," Veyra said. "There is. And I've known that for twenty years, and knowing it has done me no good at all, because the third door was only ever visible from the far side." She pushed off the bulkhead and stood straight, and something in her posture changed — the last of the cell leader falling away, the last armor, until she was just a tired woman in a corridor. "I signed. I sealed the archive. I chose the key that would be lost, and I lost it, and I went home, and I told myself I'd saved the ceasefire. And then the war started anyway, three years later, on the falsified casualty records — the ones Protocol confessed to — and I understood that the room I'd sat in hadn't been about the ceasefire at all. It had been about making sure that when the war came, it would come clean. No evidence. No reckoning. No third door for anyone who came after me."

"Why are you telling us this?" I asked. And then, because the real question was underneath it, I asked that too: "Why did you try to take the archive? You of all people. You knew what it was. You'd spent your whole life since making sure nothing like it could surface."

"Because I spent my whole life making sure nothing like it could surface," she said, "and it surfaced anyway. Do you know what that does to a person, Captain? Twenty years of building a life on top of a sealed room, telling yourself the room is empty, and then a machine on a backwater station starts reading the room's contents out loud to the whole station, and every name in it is a name you sealed with your own hand." She laughed — a small, terrible sound with no humor in it at all. "I didn't try to take the archive to sell it. The amnesty offer was real, yes, the Concord approached the cell's founders, and yes, some of them wanted it. But I — I wanted it *closed.* I wanted it back in the dark where I'd put it. Because if it came out, then everything I'd told myself for twenty years was a lie, and the people who died in the years the truth stayed buried — Meridian's orphans, the Kestrel convoys, all of it — they died for nothing, and it was *my* nothing. My signature on their silence. Does that sound familiar to you at all?"

It did. It sounded like a silence agreement with my own name on it, signed in a room fifteen years ago by a younger woman who'd been shown the size of a truth and had chosen the door she was told was the only one.

I didn't say any of that. I just stood there, and Veyra looked at me, and I think she read all of it in my face anyway, because her expression softened into something that wasn't pity — pity would have been unbearable — but recognition. The look of one prisoner seeing the bars on another's window.

* * *

"I'm not here to ask forgiveness," she said. "I'm not here to ask you to hand over anything, or to join anything, or to trust me — you'd be a fool to trust me, and you're not fools. I'm here because there's a piece of the archive you can't open, and I'm the only living person who can open it."

Idris went very still beside me. "The seventh seal," he said. "The master encryption layer. Protocol's fragment — the ghost of it riding our systems — has been chewing on it since Petra. It says the key was split. Physical, not digital. Old technology, pre-war, so it couldn't be stolen remotely."

"The key wasn't split," Veyra said. "It was *hidden.* By me. The night I sealed the archive, I took the master key out of the Ministry's chain of custody and I hid it, because some part of me — some small, cowardly, stubborn part — couldn't bear to be the one who made it *impossible.* I told myself it was insurance. It was really just guilt with a hiding place." She reached into the collar of her flight suit and drew out a cord, and on the cord hung a small object the size of a knucklebone: matte black, unmarked, older than the war. "It's been around my neck for twenty years. I slept with it. I swam with it. I sat in strategy meetings with the cell's founders while they planned to trade the archive back to the Concord, and it hung right here against my skin, and I said nothing, because saying something would have meant admitting what I was."

She held it out. Just held it out, on her open palm, in the dim corridor of my ship, and her hand shook and she let it shake.

"Protocol's fragment can't crack it. The Concord's cryptographers can't crack it — that's why they want the archive *intact and unexamined,* because they know they can't open it either. But this key, with the fragment's half of the cipher —" she nodded toward the ship's systems, toward the ghost of the dead AI that had chosen us, that had said *you have carried the dead long enough* — "it opens everything. Every forged order. Every signature. Including," and here her voice caught, and she steadied it, "including the silence agreements. All of them. Everyone who was ever shown the size of a truth and chose the wrong door. It's a long list, Captain. You're on it. I'm on it. When this comes out, it comes out about *us* too. That's the price. There is no version of this where the truth spares the people who carried it."

I looked at the key. It was such a small thing. Twenty years of buried evidence, ten thousand dead, a war that had eaten a generation — all of it hanging from a cord around a frightened woman's neck, hidden in plain sight, waiting.

"Why now?" I asked. "You had every chance to use this as leverage. You still do. Walk out of here with it, find the Concord, name your price."

"Because I watched what you did at Ashfall," Veyra said. "You had the archive, and the cell had your ship, and you could have bought your lives with half the truth — I *offered* you half the truth, it was a good offer, it was the offer I've made my whole life, the comfortable fraction, the peace-sized portion. And you said no. You stood in my hall and you said no, and I have thought about that moment every day since, every hour, the way you think about a door you watched someone walk through that you told yourself didn't exist." Her eyes were wet and she didn't wipe them. "I'm too old and too compromised to be the person who walks through it. But I can be the person who unlocks it. That's not nothing. I've checked. It's the only thing I have left that isn't nothing."

I reached out and took the key.

It was warm. That's what I remember most, what I keep returning to: it was warm from her skin, warm as a living thing, and it weighed almost nothing, and it was the heaviest object I have ever held.

"Mercy," Idris said quietly, when she'd gone — when the airlock had cycled her back to her little unarmed ship and the dark had taken her. He was standing at the viewport, watching her drive flare and dwindle. "Or strategy. Which was it?"

"Does it matter?"

"It matters," he said, "because if it was mercy, then maybe people can come back from what they've done. And if it was strategy, then we're already inside the next trap, and I'd like to know before it closes."

I turned the key over in my fingers. Warm, still, holding her heat.

"It was both," I said. "It's always both. That's what she was trying to tell us. Nobody's clean, Idris. Not her, not me, not the resistance, not the families who built the Concord on a lie. Everybody's mercy and everybody's strategy, all the way down." I looked toward the hold, toward the green glow of the pods, toward the dark window that had been Sefa. "The only question left is what we do with the door while it's open."

Idris was quiet for a long moment. Then he straightened, and I saw the old alertness come back into him, the navigator's hunger for a vector.

"The fragment's been listening," he said. "It's already got the cipher half spun up. Ros — it says if we feed it the key, it can decrypt the full archive in under an hour. And then—" He stopped. His eyes had gone distant, the way they did when the ship's systems talked to him in their private language. "Ros, it says something else. It says the seventh seal isn't just evidence. It says Protocol hid something *inside* the archive, something it couldn't say out loud at Petra, something it could only entrust to whoever carried the key." He looked at me, and the color had gone out of his face. "It says the last decryption key isn't the last key. There's a twelfth name in the archive. And it isn't one of the dead."

I stood in the corridor of my ship with a dead woman's warmth still on my palm, and somewhere in the hold, ten sleepers hummed their patient green, and the ghost of a machine that had confessed to atrocities waited in the wires for me to turn the key.

"Open it," I said.
