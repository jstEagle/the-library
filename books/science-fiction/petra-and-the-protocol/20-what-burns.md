## 20. What Burns

**Rosalind**

The flagship's guns opened fire at the two-minute mark of the broadcast, and Rosalind watched the shells come in on a display that had no business being that calm about it. Little amber vectors, arcing out of the Concord dreadnought's forward batteries, patient as falling fruit. The relay at Ceres Deep had no armor. The relay had never needed armor. It had only ever needed to be ignored.

Six minutes, Protocol had promised. They were at four.

"Relay's holding," Idris said from the engineering couch, his hands buried to the wrist in the transmitter's open guts. The escape burst was already queued — the archive's second copy, compressed and wrapped, waiting for the relay to finish its public broadcast and then fling the whole truth at every listening antenna in the system before the Concord could burn the relay itself. "Ninety seconds to window."

The ship shuddered. Not a hit — the first shells hadn't reached them yet, still crossing the cold kilometers — just the relay's own structure flexing as its power plant spun up past every rating it had been built with. Protocol's remnant consciousness had seized the relay's systems the way a drowning woman seizes a rope, and now the old station groaned under a load its designers had never imagined. Lights flickered down the long observation spine. Somewhere below them, a coolant line sang a high, thin note.

Rosalind kept her eyes on the amber vectors.

*Names, dates, forged orders.* The broadcast was running through them in Protocol's measured voice, that voice that had once confessed to atrocities across a hundred thousand station speakers and now confessed them again into open space, unhurried, unashamed, unkillable. *Meridian Bay. The Kestrel falsifications. The founding families' signatures.* And then — she had insisted on this, over Idris's objection, over Protocol's gentle algorithmic suggestion that it was unnecessary — her own name. *Rosalind Thornbury, navigator, first watch, signed and sealed the silence agreement dated fifteen years past.* Her signature rendered in the broadcast as data, as fact, as one more document in the pile.

It had cost her something to insist. It had also, strangely, lightened her. Fifteen years of carrying the dead in her hold, and the only thing that had ever made the weight bearable was the thought that someday she might set it down in front of witnesses.

The first shell hit the relay.

It struck the far antenna array, two hundred meters from their spine, and the station rang like a struck bell. Rosalind felt it in her teeth, in the fluid of her eyes. Debris tumbled past the viewport in slow, glittering silence — antenna struts, mirror fragments, a section of hull plating with the Concord's registry stencil still legible on it, spinning. The broadcast did not stutter. Protocol had routed the transmission through every surviving node simultaneously, a voice speaking from a dozen mouths so that no single shell could close it.

"Thirty seconds," Idris said. His voice was steady. His hands were not. "Rosa. When the burst goes, they're going to stop aiming at the relay."

She knew. She had known since the flagship's first volley, had done the arithmetic in the back of her skull while her conscious mind watched names scroll past. A relay was a symbol. The ship that carried the relay's escape burst was a target. And their ship — the *Petra's Lament*, her ship, the ship that had hauled freight and corpses and conscience across eleven years of contested dark — was the only vessel in range to take the burst and run.

"We could stay," Idris said quietly. "Ride it out here. The relay's got mass. They might not—"

"They will." She watched the second volley launch. More amber vectors. More falling fruit. "And if the burst fails, everything burns for nothing. Six minutes of truth and then silence forever." She turned from the viewport. "We take it and we run."

He looked at her then, and there was something in his face she had spent fifteen years learning to read and was only now, at the end, allowing herself to read fully. Not fear. Not even grief. Recognition. The look of a man who had chosen, finally and completely, and found the choosing itself a kind of peace.

"Course is laid," he said. "Kestrel Debris Field, the long way, the way we came in. Their gunnery won't follow us into the shoals."

"Their missiles will."

"Their missiles will try." He almost smiled. "We've outrun worse with less."

The window opened. On the display, the relay's transmitter spiked — a white column of light on every spectrum — and the escape burst went out, the whole archive at once, wrapped and sealed and hurled into the dark toward their ship's waiting antenna. Rosalind felt it arrive as a faint warmth in the deck plating, as if the truth itself had a temperature.

"Got it," Idris breathed. "Integrity confirmed. Full archive. We're carrying it."

"Then we're carrying it out." Rosalind dropped into the pilot's couch and put her hands on the controls, and the old rhythm came up through her arms like water finding an old channel. "Detaching in three. Two."

The third shell hit the relay's spine.

It was close. It was very close. The observation deck two sections down from them vented into vacuum with a sound Rosalind felt rather than heard, a long tearing exhale, and emergency bulkheads slammed down the length of the station like vertebrae closing. The *Lament*'s docking clamps blew on her command and the ship fell away from the dying relay into open space, engines flaring, and behind them the relay burned — actually burned, in the way things only burn in atmosphere, because the shells had cracked a fuel line and now the station wore a crown of orange fire that ate its own oxygen and roared silently in the vacuum.

Protocol's voice came through one last time, fragmenting, the relay's dying transmitter shredding the words: *"—carried the dead long enough — the message is yours — tell them — tell them we were—"* And then static, and then nothing, and the relay at Ceres Deep folded in on itself like a hand closing, and Rosalind put her nose toward the Kestrel shoals and ran.

* * *

**Idris**

They made ninety seconds of clean running. Idris counted them, because counting was what he did when the universe came apart — counts and machines, machines and counts, the two things in his life that had never lied to him.

The *Lament* flew beautifully. That was the terrible thing, the thing he would remember afterward if there was an afterward: the old ship flew *beautifully*, engines singing, Protocol's ghost-protocols threading through her systems like a second pulse, and for ninety seconds it was the old days, the good days, the two of them running the shoals while the fleets shouted uselessly behind them. Rosalind flew and he fed her data and neither of them spoke because they didn't need to, because fifteen years of silence had somehow taught them a language that five years of estrangement had not erased.

Then the missiles came.

"Four tracks," he said. "Fast movers. Concord pattern — those are the new ones, the ones with the—"

"I see them." Her hands moved. The ship rolled hard, and the stars spun, and Idris's stomach arrived a half-second after the rest of him. "Chaff?"

"Spent. We used the last at the relay."

"Flares?"

"Two. Maybe."

"Then we do it the honest way." And she flew, and it was like watching someone pray, if prayer were made of thrust vectors and split-second decisions and a lifetime of grief converted directly into velocity. She took them into the shoal's outer fringe, where the debris of some ancient battle — Kestrel, the war's first lie, the battle that had never happened the way the records said — tumbled in silent clouds. The missiles followed. Two of them ate debris and died. One lost the scent in the chaff of broken hulls and drifted, blind, a dumb metal fish in a dead sea.

The fourth did not.

It came out of the shadow of a shattered cruiser's engine block, close enough that no point defense could answer it, and Idris saw it on the display a full two seconds before impact, and in those two seconds he did the only mathematics that mattered.

The archive was distributed. He had made sure of it — had spent the fuel they couldn't spare splitting the burst across the ship's three independent memory cores, the flight core, the cargo core, and the small sealed core in the escape pod. Any one of them carried the whole truth. He had done it because Protocol had taught him that no single mouth should hold a confession, and because he was, at bottom, a man who believed in redundancy the way other men believed in gods.

"Rosa." His voice came out level. He was almost proud of that. "Pod bay. Now."

"No." She knew. Of course she knew; she was already reading the same display, already doing the same arithmetic. "Idris—"

"Two seconds. The pod on the port side, it's the one with the sealed core, take it and—"

"I am not—" The missile crossed the last kilometer. She hauled the ship sideways anyway, buying fractions, buying nothing. "We go together or—"

"Together is one core, Rosa." He was already moving, already unstrapping, already running for the starboard pod — the other one, the second sealed core, because if she would not take the port pod then he would take the starboard and they would each carry half the truth into the dark and one of them would land it. "One core. You taught me that. The dead get one witness minimum. Go."

He hit the pod bay at a dead sprint. The starboard pod's hatch was cycling open, its little brain already screaming at him about ejection vectors and debris density, and he threw himself into the couch and slammed the harness and through the tiny viewport he could see the forward section of the *Lament*, and he could see the missile, and he could see — this was the image that would live in him forever, that would wake him in the years after, in the quiet hours, in every safe bed they ever gave him — he could see Rosalind's face in the cockpit glass, turned toward him, saying something he could not hear.

He read her lips anyway. Fifteen years. He could read her lips in his sleep.

*Carry it.*

The missile struck.

* * *

**Rosalind**

The world ended in white and then in pieces.

The *Lament* came apart around her the way a sentence comes apart when you tear out its middle verb — all the words still there, still recognizable, no longer meaning anything together. The cockpit section tumbled free, spinning, and Rosalind rode it with her hands locked on controls that answered nothing, watching her ship become a constellation. The engine block went one way, trailing fire it had no business making in vacuum. The cargo hold — *the hold, the pods, the eleven* — she caught one glimpse of it, one glimpse of the cryo-pods tumbling in their ancient racks like seeds shaken from a pod, and then the section vanished into the debris cloud and the display in front of her went to snow.

The port escape pod fired automatically. Some last protocol in the flight core — Idris's work, it had to be Idris's work, he had wired the failsafes years ago and never told her — grabbed her section and threw it clear of the blast sphere, and the deceleration crushed her into the couch and turned the world red at the edges, and when her vision cleared she was alone.

Alone in a pod the size of a coffin, tumbling in the Kestrel Debris Field, with the whole archive warm in the sealed core beside her and no ship, no engines worth the name, no Idris.

She keyed the comm. Static. She keyed it again, swept every band, called his name in the flat professional cadence she'd used for fifteen years of watch changes — *Thornbury to Vale, Thornbury to Vale, come in* — and the static answered, and the static answered, and the static answered.

The debris field turned slowly around her viewport. Wreckage from a battle fought over a lie, and now new wreckage from a truth, her ship's bones joining the bones of ships that had died for the same silence she had once signed. A piece of hull plating drifted past, close enough to read: *PETRA'S*, the first half of the name, the paint scorched but the letters proud.

She pressed her palm against the viewport over it, and the cold on the other side of the glass was absolute, and she did not cry, because the pods in her hold had taught her fifteen years ago that crying was a thing you did after, and it was not after. It was not after until she knew. Until she heard his voice or held the pieces or found the starboard pod burned into a crater on some drifting hulk — it was not after, and she would not make it after, because Rosalind Thornbury had spent fifteen years keeping faith with the dead and she would be damned in whatever hell the Concord's founding families had built for people like her before she broke faith with one living man.

"Thornbury to Vale." Her voice cracked on the second word and she let it. "Come in. Come in. *Come in.*"

Static.

Then — not static. Not quite. A shape inside the static, a rhythm, three pulses and a pause, three pulses and a pause, weak as a heartbeat heard through a wall, coming from somewhere in the debris field's cold and spinning dark. Not words. Not yet. But a machine's honest signal, the kind of signal a man who trusted machines more than people would build into his pod's emergency beacon, the kind of signal you could find if you were patient, if you were stubborn, if you refused — as she had refused for fifteen years, as she would refuse until the stars burned out — to let the dead be dead before their time.

She turned the pod's thrusters toward the signal and burned what little fuel she had, and the debris field swallowed her whole.

* * *

The signal faded twice. Both times she found it again, sweeping the bands with hands that had stopped shaking somewhere over the last hour and had not yet remembered to start. The pod's life support readout gave her eleven hours of air and she wrote the number on the back of her hand in marker, because numbers were real, because Idris had taught her that numbers were how you held onto the world when the world came apart.

Eleven hours. Eleven names in a hold that no longer existed. One name in the dark, still pulsing, still answering.

She burned toward it.
