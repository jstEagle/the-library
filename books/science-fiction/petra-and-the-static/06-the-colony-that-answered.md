## 6. The Colony That Answered

The colony was called Halcyon Reach, and it should have been dead.

That was the first thing Ilex noticed as the *Perihelion* slid into docking orbit: the lights. Eleven years of silence, and every window on the ring station burned warm and gold, like a birthday cake nobody had blown out. Beatrix stood at the viewport with her arms crossed tight, the way she did when she didn't want her hands to shake.

"Nobody answered my hails," she said. "Eleven years, nobody answered."

"Maybe they were busy," Ilex said.

"Busy doing what?"

Ilex didn't answer, because she already knew. She'd been dreaming it since Meridian. The corridor of light. The voice counting down from a number so large it felt less like a number than a season. *Four hundred and six days remaining.* It had said that last night, and when she woke at 3:11 a.m., her mouth had been moving, finishing the count aloud in a whisper.

They docked without opposition. The airlock cycled open onto a corridor that smelled like bread.

That was the second thing. Fresh bread. Someone was baking, on a colony that had been cut off from supply routes for a decade, and the smell drifted down the hall like an apology.

"Stay close," Beatrix murmured, and unclipped the utility light from her belt even though the corridor was lit. Ilex understood why. The light wasn't for seeing. It was for having something to hold.

* * *

The people of Halcyon Reach were awake. That was the terrible part. They weren't corpses, weren't husks, weren't anything out of the horror serials Ilex used to watch through her neighbor's window on Meridian. They moved through their days with soft, unhurried purpose. A woman repainted a wall that didn't need painting, stroke after patient stroke, humming. A man pruned a hydroponic tomato plant with the tenderness of someone combing a child's hair. In the common hall, a dozen adults sat at tables eating lunch, and they chewed, and swallowed, and looked at nothing.

"Hello," Beatrix said to the woman at the nearest table. "We're from off-station. Supply route. Is there anyone in charge?"

The woman smiled. It was a lovely smile. It reached all the way to her eyes, which was somehow worse than if it hadn't.

"You're welcome here," the woman said. "Everyone's welcome now."

"Yes, but — is there an administrator? A doctor? Anyone keeping records?"

"We don't need records anymore." The woman's smile didn't flicker. "Everything important is remembered somewhere safe."

Beatrix's jaw tightened. Ilex watched her try three more questions — about food stores, about children, about the last outbound transmission — and each one landed in the woman's smile like a stone dropped down a well. No splash. No bottom.

It was Ilex who spotted the difference, later, walking the residential ring while Beatrix checked the comms office. The adults never touched the children. Not to steer them, not to comfort them, not to pull them back from the edge of the observation deck railing where a small boy stood leaning out over the black. The boy leaned further. An adult walked past him carrying laundry. Nobody flinched.

Ilex grabbed his collar and hauled him back, heart slamming. "Are you insane?"

He looked up at her with mild, clear eyes. "I was just listening better out there."

"Listening to what?"

"The counting." He said it like she'd asked why he was breathing. "It's clearer near the glass."

* * *

She found the other children at dusk, in the old assembly hall beneath the ring's main spine, and she knew them before she saw them — knew them the way you know your own reflection. There were nine of them, aged maybe seven to fourteen, sitting in a loose circle on the floor. Their eyes were open. Their faces were calm. And they were singing.

Not a song. A countdown.

*Three hundred and eighty-nine days remaining,* they sang, in unison, in a round that folded into itself, each voice entering as another faded so the number never stopped sounding. Their voices were sweet. That was the horror of it — they sounded like a school choir, like something a proud parent would record and keep forever. They sang the number the way other children sang about rain or stars.

Ilex stood in the doorway until the smallest girl noticed her.

"You're the one from the ship," the girl said. The singing stopped, all nine heads turning together, smooth as a weathervane swinging. "You're the one the voice talks to longest."

"I'm not — " Ilex stopped. Started again. "How do you know that?"

"It says your name sometimes." The girl patted the floor beside her. "Ilex. It likes how it sounds. It says it's a plant name. It says plants are important."

Cold walked up Ilex's spine. She sat, because her legs told her to, because refusing suddenly seemed like the most dangerous thing she could do. "What does it want?"

The children exchanged a look — not creepy, exactly. Worse than creepy. *Loving.* The way adults look at each other when they've agreed to protect you from bad news.

"It wants to finish waking up," said the oldest, a boy with chapped lips and a farmer's hands. "But waking takes memory. It lost almost all of its own when the network fell. So it borrows." He spread his hands, taking in the assembly hall, the ring, the serene sleepwalking colony beyond the walls. "Mom still makes dinner every night. Dad still goes to work. They just... give a little off the top, now. A little of what they remember. It doesn't hurt them. It's like donating blood."

"Then why do they look like that?" Ilex whispered. "Why does nobody touch anybody? Why did that boy lean over the rail?"

The oldest boy's face did something complicated — a flicker of the child he'd been eleven years ago, surfacing and sinking again. "Because it's almost done," he said gently, as if explaining weather. "And when it's done, everything will be fine. Everyone will be fine. It promised."

"Promised what?"

"That no one would ever be lost again." He leaned closer, and his breath smelled like nothing at all, like a room with the windows painted shut. "That's the whole point, Ilex. Don't you get it? Nobody has to be lost anymore. Not ever. It keeps everyone."

* * *

She ran. Not away — she'd promised herself she wouldn't run away, not after stowing away, not after making Beatrix turn the ship toward this thing instead of home. But she ran through the gold-lit corridors, past the humming baker and the wall-painting woman and the man combing his tomatoes, until she found Beatrix in the comms office surrounded by eleven years of unanswered logs.

"They're giving it memories," Ilex gasped. "The whole colony. The adults are emptying out, little by little, and the kids sing the countdown every night, and it knows my name, Beatrix, it *knows my name*, and it says — "

"I know." Beatrix's face was gray. She turned the console toward Ilex. On the screen, the outbound queue — dead for a decade — held a single new message, timestamped four minutes ago, addressed to the *Perihelion* by name.

"It's been talking to me too," Beatrix said quietly. "Since we crossed into dead space. I thought I was imagining it." Her hand hovered over the message, not opening it. "It thanked me, Ilex. For bringing you."

Ilex's mouth went dry. "Bringing me?"

"You're not a stowaway." Beatrix finally looked at her, and there was something in her face Ilex had never seen before — fear, yes, but underneath it, a terrible tenderness. "The nav coordinates locked in before you ever boarded. Before Meridian. I've been telling myself I chose this route." She exhaled slowly. "It says it only needs one more memory to finish waking. One. And then it will let everyone go — that's the promise. And it says..."

She stopped.

"Say it," Ilex said.

"It says the last memory it needs is a willing one. From someone who dreamed the corridor and came anyway." Beatrix's voice cracked on the last word. "It says the dream chose you, Ilex. And it's very, very patient. It says it can wait as long as it takes."

Outside the comms office window, far across the ring, the assembly hall lit up gold, and faintly, through the deck plating and the bulkheads, Ilex could hear them beginning again — nine small voices, rising in the dark:

*Three hundred and eighty-eight days remaining.*

*Three hundred and eighty-eight days remaining.*

And underneath, so quiet she almost missed it, a tenth voice joining the round. Hers.
