## 4. Hunters in the Dark

Sefa Iqbal woke for the second time that day, and the second time was worse, because this time she remembered waking the first time.

She was lying on a cot in a room that smelled of green things — actual, living, dirt-rooted green things — and someone had draped a thermal blanket over her with the particular care of a person who hadn't touched another human being in years and was out of practice. The blanket was tucked in at her feet. Tucked in. Nobody tucked you in unless they meant it.

"Water's on the shelf," said Elodie Underhill from somewhere behind a curtain of tomato vines. "The good jug, not the recycled one. You'll know the difference because one tastes like water and the other tastes like regret."

Sefa sat up too fast. The room tilted politely and waited for her to catch up.

"How long was I out?"

"Six hours. You talked in your sleep." Elodie emerged carrying a basket of what appeared to be aggressively thriving peppers. "Mostly numbers. Some of it was in a language I didn't recognize, which concerned me until I realized half of it was just shipping manifests. You recite manifests in your sleep. That's either very sad or very efficient."

"It's a skill," Sefa said. Her mouth was dry. Her mouth was dry in a way that had nothing to do with thirst. She reached for the water and drank, and while she drank she checked her own memory the way you check a pocket for keys you're not sure are there.

They were there. Most of them. The mission briefing on the *Halcyon*. The jump coordinates. The name Petra, spoken by a woman whose face Sefa could picture perfectly right up until she tried to describe it, at which point it became a smear of light and authority. The key in her breast pocket, cold as a held breath.

What she could not find — no matter how carefully she felt along the edges of the last three years — was the moment she'd agreed to any of it.

* * *

The alarm came at 0400 station time, which Sefa suspected was a time Elodie had invented specifically to be offensive to tomatoes.

Elodie was already in the operations room when Sefa stumbled in, hair unbound, wearing yesterday's clothes and the expression of a woman doing arithmetic she didn't like. The main display showed a point of light crawling across the system chart, patient as a spider.

"Interdiction vessel," Elodie said. "Colonial registry. It came through the outer marker twenty minutes ago and it's been broadcasting since."

"Broadcasting what?"

"Put it on."

The speakers crackled, and a voice filled the operations room — warm, reasonable, the voice of a man who had practiced sounding kind until kindness became a tool he could grip. *"Unregistered station Petra, this is Colonial Interdiction Vessel Vigilant Anchor. You are harboring stolen property and an unauthorized courier. Power down your beacon and prepare for boarding under quarantine protocol. Compliance will be noted favorably in your file. We know you can hear us. Everyone can always hear us."*

"That last bit was unnecessary," Sefa said.

"He seems proud of it, though." Elodie turned. "Stolen property. Unauthorized courier. They mean you."

"They mean the key." Sefa's hand went to her breast pocket before she could stop it. "And yes. They mean me."

The silence that followed had texture. Elodie folded her arms — not hostile, Sefa noticed, but braced, the posture of someone who had spent eleven years learning that bad news always arrived eventually and it was best to receive it standing up.

"You knew they'd come," Elodie said.

"I hoped they wouldn't. Hoping is part of my job description. It says so. Right under 'courier.'"

"Sefa."

She exhaled. There was a version of this conversation where she lied, and she examined it briefly, the way you examine a door you have no intention of opening. Eleven years alone. A garden for ghosts. A signature forty years out of place. This person had earned the truth, or at least the truth's first installment.

"The beacon isn't a beacon," Sefa said.

Elodie blinked. "It pings. I calibrate it. I've calibrated it for eleven years."

"It pings because a vault that goes silent gets visited. A beacon that keeps transmitting is furniture. Whoever built this station wanted it ignored, and the best way to ignore something is to give it a boring job." Sefa crossed to the display, pulled up the schematic she'd memorized on the *Halcyon* — the one she apparently couldn't remember agreeing to memorize — and overlaid it on Elodie's maintenance diagrams. The two matched like interlaced fingers. "There. The transmission array is a shell. Underneath it there's a memory lattice, shielded, isolated, running on its own power tap. It's been recording since before the war started."

"Recording what?"

"Everything. The first hours. The real ones, unedited, before either side got hold of the narrative and started sanding the corners off it. Earth's official record says the Outer Colonies fired on the transport *Meridian* without provocation. The Colonies' record says Earth's escort squadron opened fire first and *Meridian* was collateral. Both records are curated. Both are lies adjacent to a truth somebody very much wants buried." She tapped the lattice schematic. "This has the original. Raw telemetry. Cockpit audio. Weapons signatures. Who fired first — or whether anyone did, or whether it was a third party nobody's admitted exists yet. I don't know. I was sent to deliver it, not read it."

"And the hunters?"

"Both sides sent people. Earth's people want it destroyed because if Earth fired first, the whole war becomes a crime scene. The Colonies' people want it destroyed because if it turns out *nobody* fired first — if the war started on a mistake, or worse, on purpose, by someone who profited — then ten years of righteous fury collapses into embarrassment, and embarrassed governments do not negotiate well." Sefa heard her own voice go flat and clinical and made herself stop. "That's why the *Halcyon* exists. A decommissioned ship with a scrubbed registry doesn't appear on anyone's threat board. I was the package's camouflage."

"And your memories?" Elodie asked quietly.

Sefa looked at her. "What about my memories?"

"You flinched when you said 'I was sent.' Just now. Like the sentence bit you on the way out."

There it was again — that careful attention, that gardener's habit of noticing the small wilting thing everyone else walks past. Sefa sat down slowly on the equipment bench.

"I can't find the moment I volunteered," she said. "I remember the briefing. I remember the flight. I remember deciding, over and over, mid-jump, mid-burn, that this mission mattered. But the original decision — the yes — it's not there. There's a smooth place where it should be. Smooth like river stone. Like something got worn down on purpose." She laughed, and it came out wrong. "Which means either I never decided and I'm a weapon that thinks it's a person, or I did decide and someone decided I'd be more reliable without remembering why. And here's the truly elegant part: I don't know which side edited me. Earth trained me for six years. The Colonies ran me for four after that. Either one had the access. Either one had the motive."

"So you don't know whose courier you are."

"I know whose courier I choose to be," Sefa said. "As of approximately six hours ago, when a stranger tucked a blanket over my feet. It's thin evidence. It's all the evidence I've got."

* * *

The *Vigilant Anchor* broadcast every ninety minutes, each message fractionally less warm than the last, like tea going cold in a pot nobody would admit to abandoning. By the fourth broadcast, the voice had stopped saying *compliance will be noted favorably* and started saying *non-compliance will be noted.*

By the sixth, it said nothing at all, and the vessel simply began to move.

"Four hours to orbit," Elodie said, watching the plot. "Maybe five. They'll claim engine trouble to buy goodwill they don't intend to honor."

"You've done this before," Sefa said. "The calculating-calm thing."

"I've done eleven years of waiting for something to happen. Turns out the skill transfers." Elodie turned from the display. "Tell me the rest. Not the history — I believe you about the history. Tell me about you. Why does a courier carry a key that fits a vault on a station everyone forgot?"

Sefa took out the key. It was a dull grey sliver, unremarkable, the kind of object you'd lose in a drawer and grieve only mildly. Except it hummed — faintly, subsonically, a frequency she felt in her teeth rather than heard — and it had hummed louder with every hour inside Petra's hull.

"It fits the sealed core," she said. "Your beacon's heart. The lattice won't open for codes, because codes get coerced out of people. It opens for this, and this opens for proximity and blood-oxygen and — I think — intent. Someone designed it to open only for a person standing in front of it, wanting it open, alive enough to mean it." She turned the sliver over in her palm. "And here's the detail I keep circling: the manifest listed your name. E. Underhill. Departure date forty years out. Somebody who knew this station, knew you'd still be here, knew the vault needed a keeper until the courier arrived — and arranged for both of us to be exactly where we are, at exactly the wrong-perfect time."

"Do you know who?"

"No. But whoever it was knew me well enough to edit me, and knew you well enough to bet eleven years of your life on your stubbornness." Sefa met Elodie's eyes. "That's either our worst enemy or our only friend, and I genuinely cannot tell which, and I've stopped pretending otherwise."

To her surprise, Elodie smiled — tired, crooked, real. "Welcome to Petra. Everything here is either dead or blooming and you can't tell which until spring."

Outside the viewport, the *Vigilant Anchor* burned steadily closer, and its silence was louder than all its broadcasts put together.

Then Elodie's console chimed — not the interdiction channel, not the beacon's routine pulse, but a private, buried frequency Sefa recognized from a briefing she couldn't remember agreeing to. A single line of text scrolled onto the display:

**THEY WILL OFFER YOU THE GARDEN. IT IS THE ONE THING THEY CANNOT GIVE. DO NOT TRADE.**

Elodie read it twice. Her face did something complicated.

"That's my handwriting," she said. "Or it will be."
