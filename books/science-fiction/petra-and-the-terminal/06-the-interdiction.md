## 6. The Interdiction

Sefa woke to the sound of Elodie arguing with a door.

Not a person. A door. The station's main airlock had begun cycling on its own, and Elodie was standing in front of it with a mug of something steaming and an expression of profound personal offense.

"It's not supposed to do that," they said, without turning around. "I've maintained that seal for eleven years. It has never once opened for anyone. I've half convinced myself it's shy."

"Maybe it's just polite," Sefa said. Her mouth felt like she'd been chewing insulation. The sleep — if you could call it that, that gray shallow drifting full of other people's memories wearing her face — had left her feeling like a document that had been photocopied too many times. "What's it being polite to?"

Elodie didn't answer. They were looking at the external feed instead, and their face had gone the particular color of someone watching a very large animal approach a very small campsite.

The Colonial interdiction vessel *Vigilant Meridian* hung in Petra's sky like a held breath. Nine hundred meters of gunmetal certainty, bristling with the kind of antennae that could read your pulse from orbit. Its hull bore the trident-and-wreath of the Outer Colonies Compact, freshly painted, which meant recently at war, which meant recently losing or winning enough to care about one terraforming station in the middle of nowhere.

"They're hailing," Elodie said. "Automated quarantine notice. Claims we've been exposed to unregistered biota."

"We *are* unregistered biota."

"Yes, well." Elodie took a long sip of whatever was in the mug. "I don't appreciate being cited by my own government for existing. I did that for years before the war, thank you very much."

The airlock finished its cycle. Sefa's hands started shaking before she understood why — some buried part of her recognizing the boarding protocol chime, the specific three-note cadence of a Compact military entry, before her conscious mind caught up. She'd heard it before. Many times. In rooms she couldn't picture, beside people whose faces slid off her memory like water off glass.

*You've been edited,* she reminded herself. *Your own head is a contested archive.*

Four marines came through in matte-gray armor, weapons slung low in the universal posture of *we are friendly until we decide otherwise*. And behind them, unhurried, came a tall man in a commander's coat with his rank tabs removed, as though he wanted to be addressed as a person first and an instrument of state second. He was maybe fifty-five, silver at the temples, with the kind of tired, intelligent face that made you want to confess things to him.

His eyes found Sefa across the corridor and stopped.

Everything about him went still — not shocked, exactly. More like a man who has spent years listening for one specific footstep and finally hears it.

"Sefa," he said quietly.

Her whole body flinched. Not because he knew her name. Because of *how* he said it — soft, familiar, the way you say the name of someone you've tucked into bed, or briefed at 0300, or watched walk into danger on your orders. The name landed in her chest like a key finding a lock she hadn't known was there.

"Do I know you?" she asked. Her voice came out steadier than she felt.

"You did." He spread his hands, palms out, disarming. "Commander Tomas Adeyemi. Compact External Operations." A pause, and then, gently: "I was your handler. For six years."

The corridor seemed to tilt. Sefa reached back through herself — through the fog, the gaps, the places where her memory had been neatly excised like bad fruit cut from a branch — and found nothing. No face. No voice. But her body remembered him; her body had gone loose and safe in a way it hadn't since she'd woken aboard the *Halcyon*, and that frightened her more than any weapon in the room.

"I don't remember you," she said.

"I know." Something moved behind his eyes — regret, or a very good imitation of it. "That wasn't your choice. Or mine."

Behind her, Elodie made a small sound. When Sefa glanced over, they were gripping their mug with both hands like a talisman, and their expression had shifted from offense to something sharper. Watchfulness. The look of a gardener who has spotted an insect on a leaf and is deciding whether it's harmless.

* * *

They were escorted — politely, warmly, in the manner of people who have already won — to the station's common room, which Elodie had furnished over the years with mismatched chairs, a hand-painted mural of a coastline neither of them had ever seen, and a shelf of seed tins labeled in Elodie's careful handwriting. Adeyemi sat down in the chair by the window without asking, which told Sefa he'd read the station schematics, and dismissed his marines, which told her he thought he wouldn't need them.

He was probably right. That was the worst part.

"Petra Station," he said, looking around with genuine appreciation. "Eleven years of continuous operation by a single caretaker. Do you know what the Ministry calls this place, officially?"

"A rounding error," Elodie said.

"A miracle of frugality." He smiled, and it was a good smile, warm at the edges. "The garden is remarkable, by the way. My grandmother kept tomatoes on Ceres. She talked to them. Swore they grew better."

"She was right," Elodie said, in a tone that permitted no further discussion of tomato slander. "Plants respond to attention. Everything living does."

"Mm." Adeyemi turned that smile toward Sefa, and she felt it land like a hand on her shoulder. "And you've been attending to our wayward courier. How is she? Any memory instability? Episodes?"

"I'm sitting right here," Sefa said.

"I'm aware. It's one of my favorite things about you." He said it so naturally that she almost laughed, and the almost-laugh scared her — because that was intimacy, reflexive and easy, the kind you only build with someone over years of trust. Whoever this man was to her past self, he had been *close*. "Sefa. Listen to me. You were sent here by people who used you. The same people who wiped your memory when the mission became inconvenient. Whatever you think you're carrying, whatever you were told about that beacon—"

"Vault," Sefa said, and watched him go carefully, deliberately blank.

"—it was never meant to help you," he continued smoothly, as though she hadn't spoken. "It was meant to be a weapon. And weapons, in the wrong moment, kill the people holding them."

"And in the right moment?" Elodie asked.

Adeyemi looked at them for a long moment. Then he leaned forward, elbows on knees, and became — or performed becoming — entirely sincere.

"In the right moment, it saves everyone. That's why I'm here. Not to destroy anything. To *retrieve* it. Intact. Sealed. Delivered to people who can study it properly, verify it, release it through channels that won't shatter a fragile ceasefire process into a thousand shards of mutual accusation." He paused. "And I'm authorized to offer you both something extraordinary in return."

He laid it out like a man dealing cards he knew were all aces. Full amnesty for Sefa — charges expunged, service record restored, treatment for her memory damage by actual Compact doctors rather than whatever fieldwork she'd cobbled together. And for Elodie:

"A colony berth. Priority placement. Meridian-4, second wave, launching in eight months. Habitats, atmosphere, open sky." His voice softened. "A place where your garden actually goes somewhere. Where the seeds you've kept alive for eleven years get planted in real soil, under a real sun, for the people they were always meant for. That's what this station was built for, isn't it? A future that never arrived. We're offering you the future. All you have to do is give us the core. Sealed. Unopened."

The room went very quiet.

Sefa watched Elodie's face and saw the temptation hit like weather — saw it move across them in stages, want and grief and hope tangled together. Eleven years alone. A garden seeded for ghosts. An entire planet's worth of *yes* offered in exchange for one sealed box.

"That's generous," Elodie said finally.

"It's sincere."

"Mm." Elodie set down the mug. "Can I ask you something, Commander? If the message inside that vault is true — genuinely true, verified, the whole thing — why does it need to be delivered sealed? Why does it need to be released 'through channels'? Who releases a truth through channels except people who want to decide how much of it gets believed?"

Adeyemi's smile stayed exactly where it was. That was the tell. A surprised man's smile moves; a prepared man's doesn't.

"The verification process exists to protect the truth from distortion—"

"No," Elodie said, gently, terribly. "Verification processes exist to protect institutions from truths. I've read the Ministry's agricultural protocols. Same structure. You don't release a crop report until you've decided what the harvest should say." They stood, slowly, and looked down at him with eleven years of quiet loneliness distilled into something unexpectedly hard. "Everyone who comes for that core wants it buried, Commander. Earth wants it buried. Your Compact wants it curated. The only people who want it *open* are sitting in this room, and one of us can't remember why."

For the first time, Adeyemi looked tired. Genuinely tired, the performance slipping like a coat off one shoulder.

"Sefa," he said, ignoring Elodie entirely, leaning toward her. "Six years. I taught you dead-drop tradecraft in the Tycho rain. I carried you out of Ganymede with a punctured lung. Whatever they put in your head, whatever story you've been handed — I am asking you, as the person who knows you best in this universe, to let me take it from here. Please."

And God help her, every cell in Sefa's body wanted to say yes. The word was right there, warm and easy, resting against her teeth like a coin from home.

Then she noticed her own hands. They weren't reaching for him. They were flat on the table, steady, and somewhere beneath the fog and the edits and the six missing years, something in her had already voted.

"I believe you knew me," she said quietly. "I believe you might even have loved me, in whatever way handlers love their people. But you just asked me to hand over the only thing in my life I can't remember losing." She stood. "And you haven't once asked what's *in* the vault. Not once, Commander. In two hours. Doesn't that strike you as strange? It strikes me as strange. It strikes me as the strangest thing a man who claims to want the truth would ever do."

Adeyemi rose too, unhurried, and the warmth drained out of the room like water through soil.

"I'd hoped we could do this civilly," he said. "For old times' sake."

"So did I," Sefa said. "But I don't remember the old times. So I'm going to have to judge you by the new ones."

At the doorway, he paused. Didn't turn around.

"For what it's worth," he said, "the vessel has been ordered to hold position for twelve hours. Quarantine regulations. After that... I'm afraid protocol takes over." A beat. "I really did teach you the Tycho drops, Sefa. Which means I know exactly how good you are. And I know you'll need more than twelve hours."

He left. The airlock sighed shut behind him, and Elodie stared at it for a long moment.

"Well," they said at last. "He's not wrong about one thing."

"What's that?"

Elodie turned, and their face had gone pale and set, the look of someone who has just done arithmetic they didn't want to finish.

"Twelve hours isn't enough time to open that vault properly." They swallowed. "But it might be just barely enough time to open it *badly*."
