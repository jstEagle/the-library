## 2. A Ship Older Than Its Crew

The scope woke him at 0347 station time, which was a fiction Ulyana had invented himself and therefore could not resent.

He had been dreaming of rain — real rain, the kind that fell on Earth in sheets and smelled of hot asphalt and cut grass, not the polite misting lines of the garden's irrigation lattice — and the dream dissolved the way dreams do, all at once and leaving nothing behind but a damp ache behind his eyes. He lay in his bunk in the habitat ring's narrow crew quarters and listened to the station breathe: the whisper of air handlers, the tick of thermal expansion as Kepler-442c's long night gave way to its longer dawn, the faint subsonic hum of Beacon 7 transmitting its eternal, unexplained pulse from the ridge twelve kilometers south.

Then the scope's alert tone sounded again, patient and insistent, and Ulyana Thackeray understood that something had changed.

In eleven years on-station, the deep-space array had woken him exactly four times. Once for a comet fragment burning up in the upper atmosphere, once for a solar flare he already knew about, once for a fault in the array's own cooling loop, and once for nothing at all — a ghost return the technicians on Earth later attributed to gravitational lensing off an uncharted brown dwarf. Four times in eleven years. He dressed in the dark out of habit rather than urgency, pulled his jacket over his sleep shirt, and took the spiral stair down to the observatory two steps at a time, his bare feet cold on the metal treads, telling himself it was nothing, it was always nothing, the universe was mostly nothing punctuated by rare and merciful interruptions.

The scope feed was already up on the main display when he reached it, the array having helpfully queued the contact with a confidence rating of 99.2 percent.

Ulyana stood very still and looked at it for a long time before he let himself believe it.

A ship. A real ship, inbound, decelerating hard against a fusion torch that bloomed on the display like a white flower opening in time-lapse. The trajectory solution resolved itself line by line across the bottom of the screen: origin point beyond the heliopause of Sol, transit vector through the deep dark between stars, arrival window — and here Ulyana felt the first cold thread of wrongness slide down his spine — *now*. This year. This month. Nineteen days out at current deceleration.

Because the Petra was not due back for fifty years.

Everyone on Kepler-442c knew about the Petra, though no one currently alive on the planet had seen her leave. She was a legend with a hull number: the deep-space vessel launched sixty-two years ago on a survey-and-contact mission to the Gliese system, crewed by forty volunteers who had said goodbye to their families knowing that relativistic time dilation would make the voyage a one-way door in everything but name. Fifty years outbound, fifty years back, by ship's clock barely twenty. The children of the Petra's crew were grandparents now, or dead. There were memorials. There was a school on the southern continent named after her captain, Anselm Voss, whose face was on the recruitment posters still hanging, sun-faded and curling, in the transit authority offices of Earth.

And she was coming home nineteen days early, as if the last half-century had been a rounding error someone had generously forgiven.

Ulyana sat down slowly in the observer's chair, the old leather exhaling under him, and began, with hands that were steadier than he felt, to pull the telemetry apart.

* * *

By 0600 he had stopped trying to make the numbers behave and started trying to understand them instead, which was a different discipline entirely and required coffee.

The transponder was the first impossibility. Deep-space vessels broadcast a registry signature woven into every handshake packet — ship's name, launch registry, crew manifest hash, mission authorization countersigned by both Earth Command and the Colonial Assembly. Forgery was theoretically possible and practically suicidal; the cryptographic chain ran through keys held by institutions on two worlds, and any tampering would announce itself like a scream. The Petra's signature came through clean. Not just clean — *pristine*, every checksum validating, every timestamp internally consistent, the whole thing humming with the smug integrity of a document that had never been touched by anything but its own issuing authority.

Except that the timestamps said she had been underway for eleven years, four months, and change.

Eleven years. At the velocities implied by her deceleration profile — velocities that would have turned her fuel margins into a bad joke — the Petra could not have reached even the edge of the Gliese corridor, let alone surveyed it and come home. It was as if she had flown out into the dark, turned around almost immediately, and raced back, and yet her transponder carried the full, unbroken cryptographic history of a legitimate deep-space deployment, countersigned and sealed, as though the universe itself had notarized a voyage that could not have happened.

Ulyana drank his coffee and stared at the hull imagery coming in off the optical array, grainy at this range but sharpening by the hour as the distance closed.

She looked new.

That was the second impossibility, and it was somehow worse than the first. Ships aged. Anyone who had spent time around spacecraft knew it in their bones the way farmers knew weather: micrometeorite pitting along the leading edges, the slow bleaching of hull coatings under hard radiation, the patchwork of replaced panels and re-welded seams that accumulated over decades like scars on an old working animal. The archival images of the Petra at launch showed a beautiful ship, yes, but a ship built to be used — matte-grey ablative skin, sensor masts bristling, the honest clutter of a vessel meant to survive fifty years away from any yard that could repair her.

The ship on his display gleamed. Even at this resolution, even through the atmospheric distortion of imaging from orbit, the difference was unmistakable. Her hull was unmarked. Her panels matched. Her paint — actual paint, the ceremonial blue-white livery of the exploration fleet that no working vessel kept past its first year — was unchipped, unfaded, fresh as the day it went on. She looked less like a returning veteran than like a museum replica, or a memory of herself, or —

Or like something that had been made new again.

Ulyana set down his cup because his hands had begun, very slightly, to shake.

He thought about Beacon 7. He thought about it more often than he admitted to anyone, including himself — the structure he tended without understanding, commissioned by no one, explained by nothing, older in its purpose than his posting and silent about both. For eleven years he had maintained its systems, calibrated its emitters, logged its pulse, and filed his reports into an archive that never seemed to read them. He had made his peace with the mystery the way one makes peace with weather. Some things simply were.

But standing in the observatory with the impossible ship growing brighter on his display, Ulyana felt that peace develop a hairline crack. Because if a ship could come home fifty years early wearing a hull younger than her own logbooks, then the universe was not the orderly ledger he had trusted it to be. Then records could lie. Then time itself might be a thing with seams, and things might be moved along those seams by hands he could not see.

He thought, absurdly, of his mother's lullaby, the one about the river that runs backward, and pushed the thought away.

* * *

He did what procedure told him to do, because procedure was a handrail and he needed one. He flagged the contact priority-one to the colonial relay at Meridian Station, composed a report so dry it could have desiccated fruit, attached the telemetry, and requested guidance. Then he watched the acknowledgment queue and understood, watching it, that there was no one left to give guidance. Meridian was a data node, not a government. The nearest human beings besides himself were four hundred colonists in cryostasis aboard the seed ships still crawling in from Sol, decades out, asleep, unborn into this world in every sense that mattered. The planetary council existed as a charter and a schedule, convening for the first time when the first wave woke.

Which meant that when the Petra arrived at Kepler-442c — and she was decelerating on an intercept with his station, there was no ambiguity about that, no other rock or ring or beacon worth her braking burn — she would be arriving at him. At a gardener. At a man whose official responsibilities included seed-vault humidity control and the maintenance of a beacon no archive could explain.

Ulyana laughed once, alone in the observatory, and the laugh came out wrong.

* * *

Nineteen days is a long time to spend alone with a dread you cannot name. He kept the garden. That was the discipline that saved him, he thought later — the rows of terraced beds under the great dome, the seedlings of wheat and amaranth and the slow-growing ironwood trees that would shade streets not yet paved, for people not yet awake. Every morning he walked the rows with his moisture probe and his pruning shears and he touched the leaves of things that had never been touched by anyone else, and he told himself: *this is real. This grows. Whatever comes down out of the sky, the roots are in the ground.*

But at night he climbed to the observatory, and the ship grew.

On day six, the optical array resolved individual hull features, and Ulyana found himself looking at the Petra's bridge windows and feeling, unaccountably, watched. On day nine, her comm laser acquired his station and the handshake protocols began — formal, courteous, automated, the ship identifying herself in flawless registry language and requesting docking parameters for Beacon Station Primary, which was his station, which had never once in its eleven-year operational life received a request for docking parameters, because no ship had ever come.

He sent them. What else would he do?

On day fourteen, a voice channel opened. Not automated. A voice, human, slightly too loud in the quiet of the observatory, slightly too careful in its diction, the way people speak when they have rehearsed.

"Beacon Station Primary, this is deep-space vessel Petra, requesting final approach clearance." A pause. "Over."

Ulyana keyed the channel. His mouth was dry. "Petra, Beacon Station Primary. Clearance granted. You are—" He glanced at the trajectory plot, the numbers he had checked a hundred times. "You are cleared for approach on the standard corridor. Welcome home."

Another pause, longer this time. When the voice came back, it had changed — thinner, younger, threaded with something that might have been relief or might have been fear.

"Thank you," the voice said. "Thank you. We've been— it's been a long trip. Over."

And it was only then, alone in the observatory above his sleeping garden, with the impossible ship hanging in his sky like a second moon, that Ulyana noticed what he should have noticed the moment the channel opened, what the waveform analysis would have flagged if he had run it, what his own ears had registered and his mind had refused:

The voice was high. Light. Unbroken by years or smoke or gravity.

It was the voice of a child.

* * *

Docking day dawned clear over the garden dome, the orange light of 442c's primary slanting long across the terraces, and Ulyana stood at the observation blister of the docking arm with his heart going like a fist on a door.

The Petra filled the view.

At close range she was worse than she had been at range — worse because she was beautiful, because every meter of her hull confirmed what the optics had whispered: unscarred, unweathered, new. Her drive bells were pristine. Her name was painted on her flank in letters tall as buildings, PETRA, the R's leg crisp, the paint so fresh it seemed to hold the morning light inside it. Sixty-two years since her launch. Eleven years by her own logs. And she looked like she had rolled out of the Luna yards yesterday.

Clamps engaged with a shudder Ulyana felt through the deck plates, through his boots, through his teeth. Pressure equalization sang in the docking collar. The inner seal cycled, and atmosphere rushed between ship and station for the first time, and Ulyana stood at parade rest because he could not think of anything else to do with his body, waiting for the hatch.

Forty crew, the archives said. Forty volunteers, average age thirty-five at departure. Their grandchildren were older now than they had been.

The hatch opened.

They came out in single file, the way crews disembark, and Ulyana's mind simply refused the sight for three full seconds, presenting him instead with a series of corrections — *they're kneeling, they're children of the crew come to greet the ship, there's been some ceremony, some mistake* — before the truth arrived all at once and knocked the breath out of him.

Eight of them. Eight children, walking in step down the Petra's boarding ramp with the grave, deliberate bearing of people performing a duty far larger than their bodies. They wore shipboard jumpsuits scaled down and cinched with improvised knots, and over the jumpsuits — this was the detail that would live in Ulyana's memory for the rest of his life, whatever length that turned out to be — they wore their insignia. Real insignia, full-sized, transferred onto small shoulders: the crossed-orbits badge of command, the silver bar of the executive officer, the technical roses of engineering, the caduceus-and-star of medical. Captain's pins on collars that came up barely higher than Ulyana's waist.

The one in front — a girl, perhaps eight years old, dark-haired, with a face that was trying very hard to be calm and almost succeeding — stopped at the bottom of the ramp. Behind her, seven others halted in perfect formation. She looked up at Ulyana. Her eyes were red-rimmed, exhausted, and impossibly, terribly old.

"Are you the station commander?" she asked. Her voice was steady the way a wire under load is steady.

"I'm—" Ulyana heard his own voice come out strange. "I'm Ulyana Thackeray. I maintain the station. I'm the only one here."

The girl nodded, as if this confirmed something she had already calculated. She straightened her shoulders, and the captain's pin on her collar caught the light.

"Commander Ilsa Voss," she said, "of the deep-space vessel Petra. Requesting permission to come aboard." A breath, barely visible. "We need to talk to you about the beacon. And we don't have much time."

Behind her, one of the smaller children — a boy holding a medical kit nearly as big as he was — began, very quietly, to cry. None of the others looked at him. They had clearly agreed, somewhere in the long dark of their impossible voyage, that no matter what happened, they would hold formation.

Ulyana knelt down slowly, until his eyes were level with the child who commanded a starship, and the wonder that had carried him for nineteen days curdled, finally and completely, into dread.

"Permission granted," he said.
