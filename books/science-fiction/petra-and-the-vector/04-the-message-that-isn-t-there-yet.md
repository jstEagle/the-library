## 4. The Message That Isn't There Yet

Ilsa Voss had commanded a deep-space vessel for eleven years, and in all that time she had never once been afraid of a room.

Now she was eight years old, standing in the terraforming station's central briefing chamber with her shoes not quite touching the floor from the chair, and she was afraid of one. It wasn't the room itself — it was the way the room looked at her. Every surface of it was calibrated for adults: the table too high, the chairs built for longer legs, the light panels set at a height that made her squint upward like a child looking at weather. She had spent fifty years in rooms built for her body. This one reminded her, gently and constantly, that she no longer lived in it.

The others felt it too. She could tell by the way they arranged themselves — Okonkwo and Reyes sitting shoulder to shoulder because small bodies sought warmth and contact without shame, little Adeyemi standing at parade rest beside the door because even at eight his spine remembered forty years of military discipline. They were children wearing the postures of elders, and the dissonance of it made Ilsa's chest ache in a way she had no medical term for.

Ulyana Thackeray sat across the table from her, and he was watching her the way he watched his seedlings: with patience, with something close to tenderness, and with a wariness he probably believed he was hiding.

"Commander Voss," he said. "You said you wanted to show me something."

"I did." Her voice came out higher than she intended. She hated that. She took a breath and pushed on, because a captain pushes on; that was the whole of the profession, distilled. "Before we do, I want you to understand what we've already told you isn't the half of it. The reversal — our bodies, our memories — that's the part you can see. There's a part you can't."

"The message," Ulyana said.

So he'd been thinking about it too. Of course he had. He was a man who maintained an orphaned beacon and tended a garden for the unborn; he was constitutionally incapable of leaving a question unwatered.

"You know about the message," Ilsa said carefully.

"You mentioned it when you docked. Encrypted cargo, priority of origin. You said—" and here he paused, and she watched him choose whether to say it, watched the sentence cost him something, "—you said it was addressed here. To Beacon 7."

"It is." Ilsa folded her hands on the table's edge, which put her chin nearly level with it. She decided she didn't care how she looked. Dignity was a currency she'd have to spend differently now. "But there's more, and I've been trying for three days to decide how to tell you, because every way I say it sounds either impossible or accusatory, and I don't want it to be either."

"Try the impossible first," Ulyana said. "I live next to a beacon nobody can explain. Impossible and I are old friends."

Despite everything, Ilsa almost smiled. *He talks like a man who has been alone so long that conversation has become a garden he tends.* She recognized the cadence. Fifty years ago — her fifty years ago — she'd known someone who talked like that. The thought arrived and then slid sideways out of reach, the way so many thoughts did now, her memories like fish in dark water, visible only as disturbance.

She keyed the table display. It rose obligingly to adult height, and she stood on the chair to reach it, and if Ulyana moved to help her she pretended not to notice, because some part of her command still needed to be hers.

"This is the Petra's cargo manifest as logged at departure," she said. The display bloomed: columns of shipping codes, mass figures, cryo-status flags. "Standard mission inventory. Geological cores, biological samples, navigation data, crew records. And this." She highlighted a single line near the bottom. **SEALED PAYLOAD — ORIGIN: OUTER COLONIES JOINT COMMAND — DESTINATION: BEACON 7, KEPLER-442C — ACCESS PROTOCOL: VOICE-KEYED.**

"Voice-keyed," Ulyana repeated.

"To a specific individual." Ilsa turned to face him fully, and let herself look at him — really look, the way she'd been avoiding for three days because looking at him produced feelings she couldn't account for. He was perhaps fifty, gray coming into his beard, hands stained faintly green at the cuticles the way a gardener's hands always were. He was a stranger. He was also, in some register beneath language, familiar in a way that made her throat tighten.

"The access protocol doesn't ask for a name," she said. "It asks for a phrase. Or — no. That's wrong. Let me be precise, because precision is all I have left." She pulled up the second file, the one she'd opened and closed forty times in three days, the one whose contents she knew by heart. "When we tried to access the payload, the system didn't request credentials. It displayed a message. A dedication line. Address lines, really. Two of them."

She brought it up. Black text on amber, the Outer Colonies' standard field format, unadorned:

**FOR BEACON 7, KEPLER-442C.**
**AND FOR U.T., WHO WILL UNDERSTAND.**

The silence that followed had texture. Ilsa heard the station around them — the soft respiration of its air handlers, the distant tick of hydroponic pumps, the enormous patient quiet of a world being built one atmosphere at a time. She watched Ulyana Thackeray read those two lines, and she watched him do something she had seen a thousand officers do before bad news landed: nothing. Perfect stillness. The body's oldest armor.

"That's not my name," he said.

"No," Ilsa agreed. "Your name is Ulyana Thackeray. U.T."

"Half the alphabet ends up U.T. if you squeeze it. Umar Tan. Ursula Teng. It's a coincidence of initials, Commander, not a—"

"Captain," she said quietly. "You may call me Captain, if it helps. I held the rank for nine years."

Something flickered across his face — not offense; recognition, maybe, or grief. He sat back down slowly, and his hands found each other on the tabletop and gripped.

"All right," he said. "All right. Say the rest of it."

* * *

Here is what Ilsa Voss remembered about delivering hard news, from the long ago when her hands were large enough to hold a stylus properly: you deliver it in order, without ornament, and you do not flinch from the parts that implicate you. So she gave it to him in order.

The payload had come aboard at Junction Station, the last Outer Colony port before the Petra's long fall toward the war's forgotten middle. The orders had been strange even then — sealed by joint command rather than any single colony, couriered aboard by an officer none of them had met, logged under a clearance code that their own captain, sixty-year-old Ilsa Voss, had verified personally and refused to discuss. The crew had assumed diplomatic cargo. Treaty materials, maybe. Everyone had been so tired of the war by then that treaty materials were the closest thing the Outer Colonies had to a religion.

Then the temporal event — whatever it had been, the thing that had reached into their ship and run their lives backward like film through a projector — and the payload's containment had tripped its failsafe. Not opened. Just... announced itself. The dedication line appeared on every screen aboard simultaneously, in the middle of the worst hours of their existence, while eight sixty-year-old professionals were waking up in the bodies of children and screaming, some of them, or going very quiet, like Adeyemi, or doing both in turns, like Ilsa herself.

"And it was addressed to Beacon 7," Ulyana said. His voice was very controlled. "A structure on this planet with no commissioning record. No purpose on file. No explanation in any archive I've ever queried."

"Yes."

"And to U.T., who will understand." He said it flatly, testing the words for weight. "And I'm telling you, Captain — I don't understand. I've maintained that beacon for six years. I climb its maintenance shafts quarterly. I've replaced its power couplings with my own hands. There is no payload bay, no receiver of that class, nothing in it but the original relay core and dust. If your ship carried a message meant for me, then whoever wrote those address lines was writing to a man who doesn't exist."

"Doesn't exist," Ilsa repeated. "Or doesn't remember?"

The question landed between them like a dropped tool. Ulyana went still again — that same terrible stillness — and Ilsa pressed on, hating herself a little, knowing it had to be done.

"Our doctor, Okonkwo — she was our doctor, she's eight now, she does arithmetic on her fingers and hates that she needs to — she ran the temporal decay profiles on us. On what happened to us. The field that reversed us didn't just pass through the Petra, Ulyana. It passed through everything in a cone roughly two light-decades wide, attenuated by distance. We're the only human beings who were inside its tightest focus. But the field had structure. It wasn't random radiation. It was *shaped*. Deliberately shaped, along trajectories that — and I want to be careful here, because I've read her report nine times and I still barely believe it — along trajectories consistent with a transmission originating from this system. From Kepler-442c. From somewhere near your beacon."

"That's not possible." He was on his feet now, though she hadn't seen him stand. "Beacon 7 is a relay. It's a *relay*. I've read its schematics down to the solder—"

"Have you?" Ilsa asked, and kept her voice gentle, because gentleness was the only weapon she had left that fit her hands. "Read schematics written by whom? Commissioned by whom? You told us yourself, the day we docked. Every record is blank. Every archive comes back empty. A structure that size doesn't get built by nobody, Ulyana. Somebody built it. Somebody erased themselves from the paperwork so thoroughly that even the erasure is invisible. And somebody — sixty years ago, by our count, right around when the Petra would have been passing through the field's origin point — addressed a sealed message to that structure, and to a man with your initials, and trusted him to *understand*."

"I would understand," Ulyana said. "If it were mine. Don't you think I'd know? You're asking me to believe I'm a man who builds weapons-grade relays and scrubs his own history and forgets — what, conveniently? Amnesias don't work like that. Minds don't work like that."

"No," Ilsa agreed softly. "Minds don't. But fields do."

She let that sit. Then she brought up the last piece, the piece she had saved, the piece that had finally broken her own resistance and sent her to him tonight instead of waiting another polite week.

"When we scanned the payload's outer casing — external imaging only, we never broke the seal — Okonkwo found residue in the containment matrix. Temporal charge. The same signature as the field that reversed us, diluted ten-thousandfold. The message wasn't just *near* the event, Ulyana. It was *part* of it. Whatever happened to us, whatever turned sixty-year-old officers into children, the message rode the same wave. Which means whoever sent it knew exactly what the field would do. Knew we'd survive it. Maybe—" and here her voice did fail her, briefly, and she steadied it against the table's edge, "—maybe designed it so we would. Eight witnesses, aged backward past the reach of anything that might want them dead. Children are the one thing even a war hesitates to shoot."

Ulyana had turned away from her. He stood facing the chamber's broad viewport, where Kepler-442c's green-and-rust surface curved away below, and somewhere down there his garden, and beyond the horizon, the black spire of Beacon 7 catching the system's light.

"If I am that man," he said, without turning around, "then I'm the man who did this to you. To all of you. Turned you into—" his hand moved toward them, toward the small bodies and old eyes, and couldn't finish.

"You're also possibly the man who ended the war," Ilsa said. "Both things can be true. Most true things travel in pairs."

* * *

Afterward — after he had asked to be excused, politely, like a man leaving a funeral early, and she had said of course, captain's privilege, go — Ilsa remained in the briefing chamber with the amber text still glowing on the display.

**AND FOR U.T., WHO WILL UNDERSTAND.**

She thought about denial. She was an expert in it, suddenly; she'd watched it operate at close range all evening, watched it move through Ulyana Thackeray like weather through a valley — first the flat refusal, then the rationalizing, then the anger, and then, in the final minutes, the thing underneath all of it, which was fear. Not fear of guilt. Fear of *recognition*. Because twice during her explanation, when she'd used certain phrases — once when she'd described the payload officer at Junction Station, once when she'd quoted the dedication line aloud in her ruined child's voice — Ulyana's face had done something involuntary. A micro-expression, there and gone. Not confusion.

*Familiarity.*

He knew something. Some sediment of memory, some dream he'd woken from and dismissed, some phrase that lived in him without provenance, the way a lullaby lives in a person who can't recall learning it. Denial wasn't lying. Denial was a man defending the borders of himself against a map that showed different borders entirely.

Ilsa understood that defense intimately. She had spent three days defending her own borders against the map of a body that wasn't hers, a rank she couldn't fill, a reflection that saluted her with a child's hand.

She killed the display. The dedication line vanished, but of course it didn't, not really; it was printed now on the inside of her skull, along with everything else the Petra carried.

In the corridor outside, she heard Ulyana's footsteps receding toward the habitat ring — quick, uneven, the walk of a man going to interrogate his own house. Going, she suspected, to the archives. Going to pull the beacon's files again, all of them, every blank and redaction and absence, and stare at the emptiness until it confessed.

Good, she thought. Go look. Find the hole where you used to be.

Because tomorrow she would have to tell him the rest — the detail she'd withheld, the one Okonkwo had flagged in the payload's metadata and Ilsa had sat on for two days like a coal in her cupped hands. The message's encryption header contained a timestamp. Not a send-date. An *authorization* date — the date the sending authority had signed off on the payload's creation.

By the Petra's reckoning, that date was thirty-one years ago.

By the calendar of Kepler-442c — the calendar Ulyana Thackeray had lived under for six years, tending his garden, climbing his orphaned beacon — that date was still four years in the future.

Ilsa stood alone in the adult-sized room, a child in a captain's silence, and waited for morning to make a liar or a prophet of the man who tended the unborn.
