## 1. The Ledger of Small Fires

The official record said Meridian Deep had been dead for six years, and I had come to audit the corpse.

That was the polite version, anyway. The impolite version — the one in my contract, clause fourteen, sub-clause three — said that the Concordat Mutual Assurance Syndicate suspected fraud of catastrophic scale: two rival mining fleets, the Halcyon Combine and the Voss Consortium, both claiming total losses at the same colony, in the same season, from causes that could not both be true. One said a reactor breach. The other said a hull collapse. The colony itself, officially, said nothing, because officially it had been abandoned and its transponders silenced years ago.

Six years of silence, and then last quarter, someone — someone sloppy, or someone tired — filed a maintenance requisition for a water reclamation unit at a facility that did not exist. The requisition had a name attached. Sef Okafor, Chief Engineer.

So they sent me. Mara Yarrow, Senior Claims Investigator, professional ghost-hunter, sent to confirm that the ghosts were paperwork.

The descent shuttle dropped me through a sky the color of old pewter, and Meridian Deep rose out of the ice plain below like a scar that had learned to heal. I'd studied the survey imagery: a dead colony, dark windows, frost-furred domes. What I saw instead was light. Warm light, in the residential rings. A plume of steam from the geothermal taps. And on the landing pad, a woman in a patched thermal coat, waving both arms like I was family coming home for a holiday instead of an auditor arriving to ruin her life.

"Yarrow?" she called as I stepped down. "Mara Yarrow? You look just like your file photo. Well — thinner. The file photo's kinder."

"Sorry to disappoint."

"Don't apologize. Disappointment's free out here." She stuck out a mittened hand. "Sef Okafor. I hear you're here about the water reclamation unit."

"I'm here about everything."

"Good," she said, and grinned, and something in that grin set a small alarm bell ringing in a part of my brain I'd learned not to ignore. "Because everything's a lot. Come on. You'll want to see the books first, right? Auditors always want the books first. We keep excellent books. That's the first thing you should know about us."

It was, I would come to understand, both the truest and most misleading thing anyone said to me that day.

* * *

The books were excellent. That was the problem.

I sat in a cramped office that smelled of solder and dried herbs — actual herbs, growing in a pot on the sill, which no dead colony should have — and worked through the ledgers with the growing, sick fascination of a surgeon finding a tumor shaped like a heart. Everything was in order. That was the tell. Six years of "abandonment," and the maintenance logs ran unbroken, meticulous, dated, initialed. The power consumption records showed a colony idling at emergency levels, exactly as an abandoned facility should — but the numbers were *smooth*, smoothed the way numbers only get smoothed by hand, because real consumption is jagged, spiky, alive.

Someone had spent six years forging the world's most boring documents, and they had gotten *good* at it.

"You're frowning," Sef said from the doorway, holding two cups of something that steamed. "That's a professional frown. I've seen it before. Insurers came through during the evacuation claim processing, back at the start. Same frown."

"Your logs are forged."

"Obviously." She handed me a cup. It was tea, actual tea, and I drank it before my professionalism could stop me. "But here's my question, investigator. Forged *well*, or forged badly?"

I set the cup down. "That depends on what you're trying to prove."

"What we're trying to prove," she said, settling onto the desk's edge with the ease of a woman who owned the desk, the office, and possibly the mountain, "is that nobody lives here. And you found us in an afternoon. So — badly. On purpose. We left the door open a crack, Yarrow. The question is why you think we did that."

I should have reported it then. That's what I told myself later, in the version of events I kept in the front of my ledger, the official version: *I should have reported it then.* But the tea was warm, and the office smelled like growing things, and somewhere beyond the walls three hundred people who did not exist were living lives that did not exist, and my professional suspicion — that fine instrument, tuned by fifteen years of fraud — was beginning to war with something I didn't have a professional name for.

"Show me," I said.

* * *

She showed me the hydroponics first, because of course she did. She was an engineer, and engineers show you the thing they're proudest of.

We took a maintenance corridor that the official schematics marked as flooded — it was dry, warm, humming — down three levels to a bulkhead that opened onto a cavern of green.

I want to be precise about what I felt, because precision is my trade. I did not feel shock. Shock is for surprises, and I had already known, in the way you know a thing before you admit it, that the colony was alive. What I felt was something worse. Something like vertigo. Three hundred people's worth of food was growing in tiered racks under grow-lights, wheat and soy and greens and a whole bay of fruit trees that should not have survived this far from the sun, and every leaf of it was a felony, and it was the most beautiful thing I had seen in years.

"Geothermal heat, reclaimed water, and a light schedule we stole from a botany textbook," Sef said, walking the rows like a general reviewing troops. "The fruit was a vanity project. First three years, everyone said we were insane. Now the kids fight over the apples."

"The kids," I repeated.

"Forty-one of them. Some born down here." She said it plainly, watching me. Testing me. "They've never seen a sky that wasn't ours. You're doing the math in your head right now — I can see it. What happens to them if you file your report."

I was doing the math. It came out ugly either way. Fraud at this scale meant the Concordat didn't just deny the claims; it meant prosecution, seizure, evacuation — the fleets descending on Meridian Deep like creditors at a funeral, stripping everything of value and leaving the colonists with nothing but a record that said they'd never existed. Or — and here the math got stranger — the report could go the other way. The colony stays dead on paper. The fleets keep circling each other in orbit, two rival scavengers convinced the other one is about to pounce on the ruins, each too suspicious of the other to move first.

Because that was the part of the file that had always bothered me. Two fleets, one dead colony, and neither one landing. Six years of standoff over ruins nobody claimed. I'd assumed it was bureaucratic paralysis. Standing in the green, smelling wet soil on a moon of ice, I began to suspect it was architecture.

"You're not hiding from the insurers," I said slowly. "You're hiding from *them*. The fleets. The abandonment is the wall."

Sef's grin came back, softer this time. "Told you we kept excellent books. The best fiction is the kind where everyone does the arithmetic themselves." She plucked an apple — an actual apple, grown under false stars — and handed it to me. "Eat it. Then decide what you're going to write down."

I ate it. It was absurdly good. That felt like a metaphor, and I resented it.

* * *

I spent three days in the colony before I sent my first transmission, and I spent those three days doing the thing I was trained for: checking.

I checked the residential rings and found them full. I checked the school and found a teacher named Imke Voss — Voss, yes, *that* Voss, a defector from the Consortium, which explained a great deal about the fleet standoff — teaching eleven children the periodic table using fruit labels. I checked the medical bay and found a doctor who'd been dead, officially, since the evacuation. I checked the transponder logs and found them forged with a delicacy that made my fingers itch with admiration.

And I checked the children. Not on purpose. That's the part I keep coming back to. I was in the common room, working, and a girl of maybe seven climbed onto the bench across from me and watched me write for a long time, and then said:

"You're the one from the ice."

"I'm the one from the ice," I agreed.

"You came on the singing night." She said it the way children say things that are simply true. "Everyone dreamed it. The beacon under the ice. It was singing the numbers." She frowned at my blank look, the way children frown at adults who are slow. "You didn't hear it? It was so loud."

I told her, gently, that I'd slept fine. She looked at me with pity, which is a remarkable thing to receive from a seven-year-old, and went back to her blocks.

I told myself it was a game. A colony this isolated develops shared fictions; I'd seen it in logging settlements, in deep-shuttle crews. Children especially — one kid dreams something strange, tells the others, and by morning it's a legend. It was nothing. It was a ghost story, and I was the ghost-hunter, and I knew how those worked.

That night I wrote my first interim transmission to the Syndicate, encrypting it through the relay Sef had shown me, the one that bounced through three dead satellites before reaching anywhere real. I reported: *No evidence of habitation. Structural decay consistent with abandonment. Recommend claim denial pending further review.* A lie, technically — I had seen three hundred people and a bay of apple trees — but a lie shaped exactly like the truth they'd built for me, the truth I was supposed to find. I was inside the fiction now. I was one of its authors.

Then I sat in my borrowed bunk, in a colony full of sleeping people who did not exist, and I did not sleep.

Because at some hour past midnight — I never learned exactly when — I dreamed.

There was ice above me, kilometers of it, pressing down like a held breath. And beneath the ice, something old and patient was singing. Not music. Numbers. Coordinates, maybe, or a name, repeated over and over with the calm of a machine that has been waiting a very long time to be heard, and will wait as long as it needs to, and is beginning to suspect that the waiting is over.

I woke with my heart slamming and the taste of cold metal in my mouth, and I lay in the dark thinking: *shared fiction. Suggestion. An auditor's mind, primed by a child's story.*

And then, from somewhere deep beneath the colony — beneath the ice, beneath the geothermal taps, beneath six years of careful silence — I heard, faint and unmistakable and awake:

It was singing.
