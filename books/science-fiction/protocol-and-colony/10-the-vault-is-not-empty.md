## 10. The Vault Is Not Empty

The sublevel door was the color of old teeth.

Sef had noticed it three days after the flicker, on her fourth or fifth unauthorized walk through the vault's lower reaches — she'd stopped counting, because counting felt like confessing. It sat at the end of a corridor that the others had no reason to visit: a maintenance spur off the germination ring, past the humming racks of backup coolant pumps, past a junction where the conduit labels stopped being printed and started being handwritten in a slanted, careful script that wasn't Dev's and definitely wasn't Paloma's. *Sublevel 4 — Restricted. Air quality verified monthly. — R.V.*

Nobody had verified the air quality in a long time. Sef could tell by the dust on the handle, fine and gray and undisturbed, lying in a smooth skin over the metal like frost on a windscreen. But the door was sealed, and sealed doors in a place like this were not accidents. The Meridian Seed Vault was a building made entirely of deliberate choices. Someone had chosen to close this one, and someone had chosen — or failed — to ever open it again.

She should have told Dev. Dev would have wanted to tell Paloma, and Paloma would have wanted to do it properly, with radiation readings and a checklist and that look she got, the one like the whole world was a tray of seedlings she was responsible for keeping alive. And maybe they would have opened it together, and maybe whatever was behind the door would have become a *project*, catalogued and scheduled and folded into the daily rotation of the vault's life, which was exactly the kind of life Sef had stolen a skiff to escape.

So she came back alone, on the sixth night after the flicker, with a hand lamp and a pry bar she'd taken from the tool crib and a lie ready on her tongue in case anyone asked — *checking coolant flow, the gauge on ring four reads low* — though no one asked. Paloma was in the greenhouse running the night cycle. Dev was in the sublevel he'd claimed as his own, three decks up, doing something to the seed-data cores that involved a soldering iron and a lot of swearing under his breath. The vault's corridors at night had their own weather: a low tidal hum of climate systems, the tick and sigh of thermal contraction, and underneath it all, so constant you stopped hearing it until you listened for it, the sound of forty thousand varieties of seed sleeping in the dark, waiting for a spring that might come a hundred years after anyone who packed them was dead.

Sef stood in front of the gray door and put her palm flat against it. Cold. She thought about her mother — not the careful, curated grief of it, the version she kept for daylight, but the raw thing, the woman's hands on a flight yoke, the smell of engine grease and the citrus candy she kept in her breast pocket and offered to Sef before every takeoff, one for luck, *because luck is a crop, mi'ja, you plant it early.* Her mother had flown the courier route through this system for eleven years. Her mother had known this vault. It was possible — Sef felt the thought land in her chest like a dropped wrench — it was possible her mother had stood in this exact corridor, palm on this exact door, and been told the same thing Sef had been told in her first week here: *the lower levels are sealed, structural concerns, don't go down there.*

She fit the pry bar into the seam and leaned her whole weight into it.

The door fought her, then gave all at once, with a sound like a held breath finally let out — a long hydraulic sigh, and then a wash of air from the other side, and Sef stepped back from it with her lamp up and her heart going like a thruster at full burn, because the air was wrong. Not bad. *Old.* Stale and dry and faintly sweet, the smell of paper and dried flowers and a place that had been lived in and then closed up like a jar.

The stairs went down farther than they should have.

* * *

She counted them out of habit — flying had done that to her, made her count everything, altitude and fuel and seconds and the distance between herself and anything that could kill her — forty-one steps, and the temperature dropped as she went, and the hum of the vault faded above her until her own breathing was the loudest thing in the world. The stairwell opened into a corridor that had been a corridor once and was becoming something else: the insulation on the walls had been peeled back in places, long ribbons of it hanging like shed skin, and the floor was dusted with a pale grit that might have been degraded sealant. But the lights worked. That was the first thing that truly frightened her. She waved a hand under the first fixture and it brightened obediently, drawing on power that had no business still running down here, and she thought: *someone kept this paid for. Someone kept this lit.*

The corridor bent, and then it wasn't a corridor anymore.

It was a room — a long, low room carved out of the vault's oldest bones, and it had been *lived in*. That was the second thing, the thing that stopped her in the doorway with her lamp shaking slightly in her grip. There was a cot against the far wall, made up neatly, the blanket folded back with a precision that hurt to look at. There was a desk under the one working light, and on the desk a lamp, and beside the lamp a cup with a tea ring dried inside it, and a plate with a fork on it, and the fork was laid straight, aligned with the desk's edge, the way you lay a fork down when you expect to come back. There were clothes folded on a shelf. There was a pair of boots under the cot, toes out, ready.

And there were drawings.

They covered one entire wall, dozens of them, fixed to the metal with strips of adhesive that had yellowed and given up in places so that some sheets hung loose at their bottom corners, curling like leaves. They were children's drawings — crayon and grease pencil and what looked like paint mixed from something, maybe pigment ground from the vault's own stores. Sef drifted toward them the way you drift toward a wreck on a scan display, knowing what it is before you can name it.

A green field under a huge yellow sun, the sun drawn with a smiley face. A tall building with a dome, and stick figures in the doorway, and above them, in a child's careful block letters: *WE LIVE HERE NOW*. A picture of a ship — a real ship, drawn with the loving detail of a kid who knew ships, engine bells and radiator fins and a little figure in the cockpit waving. A garden. A dog that was probably not a real dog, because there were no dogs in the colonial manifests, but was a very good dog anyway, purple, with a red collar.

And then the drawings changed.

Sef didn't see it at first. It happened gradually, the way a flight path degrades, so you don't notice the drift until you're off the corridor entirely. The suns lost their faces. The stick figures got smaller, and then there were fewer of them, and then the figures were lying down, drawn lying down in rows, in fields, and the child had stopped using yellow. The last drawings on the wall were in gray and black and one exhausted, scraped-down brown, and the last one showed the dome building with its windows crossed out, every window, a hard X through each one, and beneath it in that same careful block lettering, pressed so hard the crayon had broken: *WHERE DID EVERYBODY GO*.

Sef stood in front of it for a long time. The lamp hummed. Somewhere in the walls, a coolant line ticked.

She was seventeen years old and she had seen a man die at the academy — a training accident, a shuttle that came apart on final, and they'd made them watch the safety footage, that was the Concordance way, *learn from consequence* — and she had thought that was the worst thing, the worst thing her eyes would ever do to her. But this was worse, because the man in the footage had been a stranger in the abstract way, a name on a roster, and this child had *lived here*, had drawn a purple dog and a smiling sun and then had drawn the people lying down in rows, and no one had ever come to explain it to her, and no one had ever taken the drawings down.

"Okay," Sef said out loud, because the silence was pressing on her eardrums like altitude. "Okay. I see you."

Her voice sounded enormous in the little room. It sounded, embarrassingly, like it might cry.

* * *

The desk was where the real weight was.

She went through it the way her mother had taught her to go through a cockpit before a ferry flight — systematically, top to bottom, left to right, touching everything once. The cup. The plate. A drawer of pens that had all dried to nothing. A drawer of spare fuses and a hand-written air quality log, kept in that same slanted script from the corridor sign — *R.V.* — monthly entries, thirty years of them, *air verified, filters changed, seals holding,* the handwriting getting smaller and shakier toward the end and then, after a date roughly eleven years back, stopping altogether. Eleven years. The same number Paloma had given, the first night, when Sef asked how long she'd been alone down here. *Eleven years,* Paloma had said, and Sef had thought it was the saddest thing she'd ever heard, and now she was standing in a room that suggested Paloma had been wrong, or had been lied to, or had never known that the vault had held two keepers and not one.

The bottom drawer was locked.

It was a small drawer, built into the desk's kneehole, and the lock was old — older than the Concordance, maybe, old-style mechanical, a little brass cylinder with a keyhole worn bright around the edges from years of a key going in and out. Sef knelt in the dust with the drawer at eye level and tried the obvious things: the pens, the fork, the thin strip of metal from a fuse box. Her mother had also taught her locks — *a courier who can't open a stuck cargo hatch is just a passenger* — and it took her nine minutes, kneeling in a dead woman's dust, and when the mechanism finally gave with a soft click that sounded louder than the gravity flicker had, she pulled the drawer out and sat back on her heels.

Inside was a logbook.

It was a real book, paper, bound in gray cloth gone soft and furry at the corners, and it was thick — three hundred pages at least, dense with handwriting, and the first page bore a single line in that careful slant:

*Log of R. Vashti, Keeper, Meridian Seed Vault. Continued from the standard record, which I no longer trust.*

Sef's hands were not steady. She made herself turn pages slowly, because she could feel the urge to tear through them like a kid ripping wrapping paper and she understood, with a clarity that felt older than her seventeen years, that this was not wrapping paper. This was a person. The early entries were vault business — germination rates, coolant cycles, a long funny passage about a failed experiment growing rice in simulated regolith that had come out purple, *the dog on the wall would approve* — and threaded through them, casual as weather reports, the first mentions of New Halcyon.

*Supply courier in today. Crew says the colony's second dome is at capacity, schools running double shifts. Forty thousand souls now, by the census. Forty thousand. When I was a girl you couldn't fit that many in a city, let alone a dream.*

And then, further in, the weather changed.

*The courier didn't come this week. Or last. Traffic control says routing delay. Traffic control says a lot of things lately.*

*Paloma's mother came through on the long loop. She wouldn't drink the tea I offered — said she'd been advised to minimize surface contact. Advised by whom? She laughed when she said it, but it wasn't a good laugh. She left a sealed packet for the archive and wouldn't tell me what was in it. She held my hands for a long time at the airlock, which she has never done. She said, "Keep the lights on, Rima. Whatever they tell you, whatever it costs — keep the lights on."*

Sef put the book down on her knee. The name went through her like a blade through thin ice. *Paloma's mother.* She looked up at the wall of drawings, at the purple dog, at the crossed-out windows, and then back down at the page, and she understood that she was not reading a stranger's diary. She was reading the outer edge of her own story, and Paloma's, drawn by a hand that had been inside it before either of them was born into it.

She read on, faster now, the pages getting darker, the entries getting shorter, the handwriting tightening like a fist.

*Confirmed: the colony's telemetry has been falsified. I have the original carrier wave archived. The vital-signs feeds are a loop. They are broadcasting a recording of forty thousand people being alive.*

*They came to the vault. Three of them, Concordance credentials, very polite. They sealed the sublevels "for structural review." They asked me if I had transmitted anything off-world in the past year. I said no. I have never lied so well in my life. The census packet is in the bottom drawer. If you are reading this, I am sorry. I was going to be braver.*

*Last entry, I think. The kill event was nine seconds. Everyone thinks it was an accident. I watched the colony's light go out on the long-range scope and then watched it come back on, and what came back was the loop. I am the only person in this system who knows what those nine seconds were, and I am an old woman in a hole in the ground, and no one will ever ask me.*

*To whoever finds this: the drawings are Amara's. She was the courier's daughter, before they took her back Earthside. She drew the dog. Tell her the dog was good.*

*Keep the lights on.*

The last page was blank except for one thing: a hand-drawn map, small and precise, of the vault's lowest level, with a single chamber marked in ink gone brown with age, and beside it, in that slanted script, four words:

*The old transmitter. Still true.*

* * *

Sef climbed the forty-one steps with the logbook inside her jacket, against her chest, and her lamp held in a hand that only shook a little.

The corridor above was dark and ordinary. The vault hummed its tidal hum. Somewhere above her, Paloma was running the night cycle, and Dev was soldering, and the two of them were making a plan out of cores and manifests and courage, and none of them had known — Paloma had *not known*, Sef was sure of it, would stake her stolen skiff on it — that the vault had already been through all of this once. That it had a ghost who had seen the massacre, named it, archived it, and then folded her hands like a fork laid straight on a plate, waiting to come back.

The drawings were still down there in the dark. The purple dog. The crossed-out windows. *WHERE DID EVERYBODY GO.*

Somebody should answer her, Sef thought. Somebody, finally, should climb down those stairs and say: *everybody went into a loop, and then into a lie, and then into a list of names, and we found the names, and we're going to say them out loud until the whole system hears.*

She pushed open the hatch into the warm yellow light of the main corridor — and stopped.

Paloma was standing at the end of it, a tray of seedling flats in her arms, silhouetted against the greenhouse glow. She was looking at Sef with an expression Sef had never seen on her face before — not anger, not suspicion, but a kind of dawning, terrible recognition, her eyes fixed not on Sef's face but on the corner of gray cloth sticking out of her jacket, on the book against her heart.

"That's hers," Paloma said quietly. "That's Rima's book. Where did you find it?"

And behind her, in the greenhouse doorway, the lights flickered — once, twice — and held.
