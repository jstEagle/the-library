## 14. The Courier Route

Sef had learned to fly the way other children learned to pray — by repetition, in the dark, with someone's voice guiding her.

Her mother's voice, mostly. *Trim your drift before you trim your speed. A pilot who chases velocity is chasing her own tail.* The words lived in Sef's skull now the way a song lives there, worn smooth from being played too many times on nights when sleep wouldn't come. Three years since the academy. Two years since she'd stopped flinching at the memory of her mother's hands on the yoke of their little courier skiff, the *Meridian Rose*, flying seed packets between farm stations while Sef did homework in the jump seat and pretended not to be bored.

She hadn't been bored. She'd just been nine, and nine-year-olds don't know they're happy until it's over.

Now she sat cross-legged on the vault's plotting table — an actual table, scarred wood, older than the Concordance itself — with three data slates fanned out around her like tarot cards, and she was trying to remember a route her mother had never written down because writing it down would have been the one thing that got them both killed.

"You're making that face again," Dev said from across the room.

"What face?"

"The face where you're arguing with someone who isn't here."

Sef looked up. Dev was elbow-deep in the guts of the skiff's transponder housing, which he'd hauled inside on a gravity sled two days ago and had been performing surgery on ever since. His hands were black to the wrist with conductive grease. There was a smear of it on his cheekbone that he didn't know about, and Sef had decided hours ago that telling him would be a waste of a perfectly good distraction.

"My mother flew courier runs," Sef said. "Before the academy took me. Before—" She waved a hand at everything: the vault, the blockade, the nine seconds that had broken the world open like a seed casing. "Before all of it."

Dev kept working. He'd gotten good, these past weeks, at not looking at her directly when she talked about her family, the way you don't look straight at a bird that might startle. "Courier runs for the vault?"

"For the consortium that built the vault. The same people who founded New Halcyon." Sef pulled the oldest slate toward her and woke its screen. "Paloma found the shipping manifests in the deep archive. Seed deliveries, quarterly, forty years of them. Every delivery has a flight plan attached."

"Standard ones?"

"That's the thing." Sef turned the slate so he could see. "Look at the routing. These aren't standard lanes. They don't follow the beacon corridors at all. They cut through—" she traced the line with her finger, "—here. The shoals between Meridian and the Kestrel debris field. Then a dogleg south through the cometary scatter, then this long shallow arc that hugs the magnetopause of the gas giant before coming back up through the farm belt."

Dev set down his tools. That was how she knew she had his attention; Dev Okafor treated every problem like a machine, and when he stopped touching machines, it meant the problem had become something else. "That's a terrible route," he said. "It adds what, sixty hours per run? Maybe more?"

"Sixty-three, average. My mother logged it once." Sef swallowed. "I found her old flight book when I was going through the skiff after the crash. She kept it hidden under the coolant lines. I thought it was just sentiment." She laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "It wasn't sentiment. It was tradecraft."

* * *

They spread everything out on the plotting table that evening, when Paloma came up from the archive stacks carrying a lamp and the particular expression she wore when she'd found something that frightened her.

"The manifests match," Paloma said without preamble. She set her lamp down and its light threw her shadow huge against the seed racks, so that for a moment she looked like exactly what she was — a woman who had spent eleven years becoming part of a building. "Every nonstandard route in the consortium's delivery logs terminates at a relay station. Station Vesper. Independent charter, registered to a farming cooperative out of the Halcyon system." Her voice caught slightly on the name, the way it always did now, as if the word itself had edges. "The cooperative was dissolved after the colony was. But the station's registration was never revoked."

"Because officially there was nothing to revoke," Dev said quietly. "No colony, no consortium, no reason for a station."

"No reason except seeds," Sef said. "Seeds are the one cargo the Concordance never fully nationalized. Too many farms, too much tradition, too many old contracts. They can't stop the seed traffic without stopping agriculture. So the couriers kept flying. And nobody looked too hard at a route that was slow and strange and went nowhere anyone important wanted to go."

Paloma sat down heavily across from her. In the lamplight Sef could see how tired she was — not the tiredness of the body but of someone who had carried a weight alone for so long that setting it down felt more dangerous than holding it. "You think your mother knew," Paloma said. It wasn't quite a question.

"I think my mother flew census data disguised as genome files through a corridor designed to look boring, to a station registered to dead people, and she never once told me because knowing would have made me a target." Sef's voice stayed level. She'd practiced that. At the academy they taught you that a pilot's voice was a control surface, same as the rudder. "And I think she died still keeping that secret, because the manifest I saw at the academy — the one that made me run — was addressed to New Halcyon, decommissioned, and it was routed through Station Vesper."

Silence. Somewhere below them, the vault's environmental systems cycled, a sound like slow breathing.

"So we fly it," Dev said.

"We fly it," Sef agreed. "But not tonight. Not even this week. First I have to rebuild the route from memory and from her flight book, and the flight book is—" she pressed her palms flat against the table, "—it's shorthand. Half of it's numbers, half of it's things like 'the third rock sings low' and 'if the wind is wrong, wait.' She wrote it for herself. Nobody else was ever supposed to fly it."

"Then teach yourself first," Paloma said. "Then teach us."

* * *

Sef worked for six days.

She slept four hours a night and spent the rest of them bent over the plotting table with her mother's flight book propped open beside the consortium's delivery manifests, cross-referencing coordinates that were forty years stale against sensor readings she took herself, alone, in the skiff, flying short calibration loops within the vault's sensor shadow while the blockade hung overhead like a held breath.

The squadron had gone quiet since the last recalibration pulse. Quiet in the way a predator is quiet. Their broadcasts repeated on schedule — *gravitational calibration anomaly, all stations maintain silence, cooperation ensures continued operation* — but between the scheduled transmissions there was nothing, and Sef had grown up enough around hunters to know that the nothing was worse than the noise.

Each pulse degraded the seed cores a little more. Dev had measured it: a fractional corruption spreading through the census files like frost through glass. Seventy-two hours remained before the final purge. Maybe less. The Concordance didn't negotiate with data.

On the fourth night, Paloma found her asleep at the table with her cheek on the flight book, and instead of waking her, draped a blanket over her shoulders and left a cup of tea cooling beside her elbow. Sef woke at dawn with the taste of cold chamomile in her mouth and the sudden, stupid, overwhelming urge to cry, which she ignored, because crying was not on the flight plan.

On the fifth day, she found the blind spots.

They were beautiful. That was the word her mind kept offering and she kept refusing, but it was true — the corridor her mother had threaded was beautiful the way a mountain path is beautiful, which is to say it was beautiful because it was lethal. The Kestrel debris field was a graveyard of ships and stations from some war so old nobody Sef's age even knew its name, and its wreckage scattered radar returns into static. Inside the static, a small ship running dark could move like a fish through reeds. The cometary scatter added thermal noise. And the long arc along the gas giant's magnetopause — *the long walk*, her mother had called it, in the flight book, no explanation — put the whole planet's radiation scream between the courier and any listening array.

Three blind spots, stitched together by sixty-three hours of careful, patient, invisible flying.

"It's not a hole in their coverage," Sef explained, standing at the plotting table with Paloma and Dev on either side of her, her finger tracing the route one more time. "It's a seam. Coverage is always a patchwork — different arrays, different priorities, gaps between jurisdictions. The Concordance patched most of the seams after the war. But this one they never closed, because closing it would mean admitting there was something worth hiding along it." She looked up. "There was something worth hiding along it. Forty thousand someones."

"And Station Vesper?" Paloma asked.

"Still there. I checked the registry fragments Dev pulled. Last maintenance beacon pinged eleven months ago. Somebody's keeping it alive." Sef hesitated. "Or something is. We won't know until we're close."

"Then we go and find out," Dev said. He'd finished the transponder surgery that afternoon — rebuilt it to broadcast a false signature, a legitimate agricultural courier out of the farm belt, one of hundreds. It wouldn't survive a hard scan, but it didn't have to. It only had to survive a glance. "We load the cores, we fly the seam, we reach Vesper before the purge pulse, and we hand the truth to whoever's still listening at the end of your mother's road."

"And if nobody's there?" Paloma asked. Not challenging — genuinely asking, the way she asked everything, as if the universe owed her honesty and she intended to collect.

Sef thought about her mother's hands on the yoke. About the flight book hidden under the coolant lines, waiting twenty years for someone to need it. About the phrase she'd read a hundred times on the last page, in handwriting gone hurried, as if written in a cockpit with the engines already hot:

*If you're reading this, finish the route.*

"If nobody's there," Sef said, "then we'll be the ones who are there. And we'll keep the station alive ourselves until someone comes."

* * *

Loading took all of the sixth day.

They moved the seed-data cores up from the sublevel on the gravity sled, wrapped in insulation blankets, cradled like infants — which, Sef supposed, was what they were. Forty thousand lives folded into genome files. Thirteen names threaded through the telemetry headers like gold wire through cloth. Dev's testimony, compressed and encrypted, riding in the checksums where no casual inspection would ever think to look.

Paloma handled each core personally. She didn't say anything while she did it, but Sef watched her lips move once or twice, and understood that she was reading names. Saying them. Keeping the count the way she'd kept it for eleven years, one log entry at a time.

By dusk the skiff stood loaded in the launch bay, its belly full of the dead, its transponder wearing a borrowed name. Sef ran the preflight with her mother's checklist open on the slate beside the official one, merging the two the way you'd merge two languages — *check the coolant lines, check them twice, the third rock sings low, trim your drift before you trim your speed.*

Dev climbed aboard last. He paused at the hatch and looked back at the vault — at the green glow of the grow-lights spilling up the stairwell, at Paloma's greenhouse with its ridiculous, stubborn, beautiful rows of living green — and something crossed his face that Sef recognized, because she'd seen it in mirrors: the look of someone leaving a place that had kept them alive and wondering if they were allowed to want more than survival.

"She'll hold," Sef said. "The vault. She held eleven years without us. She'll hold seventy-two hours."

"I know," Dev said. "That's not what I'm looking at."

Sef didn't ask what he was looking at. Some things you didn't ask about. You just logged them, the way her mother logged weather, and waited to see if they turned into something.

They lifted at 0300, ship's time, running dark, transponder cold until they cleared the vault's sensor shadow. Below them Meridian fell away — the ice, the rock, the buried green — and above them the blockade sat in its perfect, patient constellation, forty warships arranged in an orbit that said *nothing leaves*.

Sef turned the skiff away from all of it, toward the Kestrel debris field and the dark seam beyond, and keyed the throttle with her mother's voice steady in her head.

*Trim your drift before you trim your speed.*

The first wreckage of the old war slid past the viewport like bones. Somewhere behind them, the countdown to the final pulse ticked down. Somewhere ahead, a station registered to dead people kept a light burning for reasons nobody remembered.

Sef flew into the seam, and for the first time since the academy, since the manifests, since the running — she wasn't fleeing anything.

She was finishing a route.

Forty minutes into the debris field, with the static singing sweetly off the hull and the skiff threading wreckage like a needle through lace, the passive array picked up a transmission. Faint. Fragmentary. On an old courier frequency her mother's flight book had marked with a single word: *home*.

It wasn't a beacon.

It was a voice — human, ragged, repeating the same phrase over and over into the dark:

"—anyone on this channel, Vesper is receiving, Vesper is receiving, we heard the names, we heard the names—"

Sef's hands went still on the yoke.

Someone at Station Vesper had already heard.
