## 16. The Predecessor's Vault

The logbook's final legible entry was a set of numbers that had meant nothing for two days and now meant everything.

*Sublevel four. Germination bay C. The old frequency still answers if you ask it politely.*

Paloma read it again by the light of a hand-lamp, her broken wrist splinted and aching inside its wrap, and felt the words rearrange themselves in her chest like seeds shifting in a shaken packet. *Ask it politely.* Whoever had written this — Marguerite Voss, caretaker of Meridian before Meridian was hers — had left instructions the way gardeners leave instructions: half practical, half prayer.

"Bay C," she said aloud. "That's been sealed since before my time."

"Sealed things are usually sealed for a reason," Dev said. He stood at the mouth of the corridor with his lamp held low, throwing their shadows long down the stairwell. Three years in the vault and he still moved through its lower levels like a guest, apologetic, careful not to touch anything without asking. "Contamination protocol. Structural failure. Take your pick."

"Or someone wanted what's inside kept safe from exactly the kind of people currently sitting in orbit rewriting our records." Sef shouldered past them both, fourteen years old and impatient as weather. "Come on. We have sixty-eight hours and you two want to debate architecture."

They descended.

The vault's sublevels were older than the rest of the structure — poured concrete and riveted alloy from the first colonial era, before Concordance standardization smoothed every surface into interchangeable white. Down here the walls sweated. The air tasted of mineral water and old earth, and the lamps picked out hairline cracks where roots had once tried to follow the moisture and failed. Paloma knew these corridors the way she knew the backs of her own hands, but she had never come this deep with company. Eleven years of solitary maintenance had made the dark levels feel like private rooms in a house she only pretended to own. Walking them now with two sets of footsteps echoing behind hers felt like an intrusion on herself.

Germination Bay C announced itself by its door.

Every other door in the sublevel was Concordance-issue: magnetic seal, status panel, a placard with a serial number. Bay C's door was steel, painted institutional green sometime in the last century, and across its face someone had welded — *welded*, deliberately, with the ugly confidence of a person who expected never to be argued with — a plate bearing a single line of text:

**THIS CHAMBER IS IN TRUST. DO NOT OPEN WITHOUT THE KEEPER'S CONSENT.**

Below it, smaller, almost shy:

*If you are reading this after the network went quiet, then consent is granted.*

Dev ran his palm over the welds. "She did this herself. From the inside, maybe." He looked up. "There's no external mechanism. No handle. Just this port." He tapped a circular fitting near the floor, an old manual coupling, analog, pre-network — the kind of interface that hadn't been manufactured in eighty years and which the vault, being the vault, had never bothered to replace.

"She sealed herself in," Sef said quietly. All the impatience had gone out of her voice.

"Or sealed something in," Dev said. "We don't know what we're opening."

Paloma knelt beside the port. Her splinted wrist throbbed when she braced it against the cold floor, and she ignored it the way she had learned to ignore pain during harvest weeks, cataloguing it, setting it aside, returning to the work. She thought about Marguerite Voss the way she had thought about her for eleven years — as a ghost, a predecessor whose logbook ended mid-sentence one winter, whose quarters on sublevel six held children's drawings pinned above a cot, whose disappearance the archives explained with a single bureaucratic word: *reassigned.* Paloma had inherited her position, her tools, her rounds, her loneliness. She had assumed she'd also inherited her fate.

But ghosts didn't weld doors shut from the inside. Ghosts didn't leave instructions for people they hoped would come looking.

"The keeper's consent," Paloma said. "I'm the keeper now. By succession, if nothing else." She fitted her fingers into the coupling's grip-holes. It turned hard, rusted, resistant — then gave all at once, with a groan of disengaging bolts that echoed down the corridor like something waking up.

The door swung inward six inches and stopped. Beyond it: darkness, and warm air moving against their faces, air that smelled impossibly of growing things.

* * *

Inside, the chamber was alive.

Not metaphorically. Not in the way the upper greenhouses were alive, under grow-arrays and nutrient schedules and Paloma's daily discipline. This room was alive the way a forgotten corner of a forest is alive — riotously, redundantly, without anyone's permission. Vines had climbed the old racking and woven themselves through it until rack and plant were indistinguishable. Mushrooms furred the damp corners in shelves of pale brackets. Something flowering — Paloma couldn't identify it, some heirloom brassica gone strange in the dark — hung from the ceiling pipes in cascades of small white stars. And everywhere, threaded through the overgrowth like veins through a leaf, ran cable. Copper wire, cloth-wrapped, hand-soldered at every joint, connecting a wall of equipment that had no business existing.

At the chamber's center stood a transmitter.

It was a monster of another age: vacuum tubes glowing a soft amber behind mesh grilles, a tuning dial the size of a dinner plate, a chassis of green-painted steel stenciled with a manufacturer's mark Paloma recognized only from museum plates — *Halcyon Consortium Instrument Works, New Halcyon Founding Fleet.* A transmitter built in the colony's shipyards, carried here before the colony died, kept running ever since by hands that refused to let it stop.

A low hum filled the chamber. The tubes glowed. The machine was warm.

"It's on," Sef whispered. "It's been on."

"How long has it been transmitting?" Dev asked, though his voice suggested he already suspected the answer and dreaded it.

Paloma approached the transmitter slowly, her lamp raised. Beside the tuning dial, taped in layers so old the adhesive had ambered to glass, was a paper schedule. Frequencies, times, rotation intervals — updated, corrected, annotated in three different inks representing three different decades. And beneath the schedule, hanging from a nail, a second object that stopped Paloma's breath.

A logbook. Identical in binding to the one in her pocket.

It lay open, and the entry on the visible page was dated — she checked twice, three times, certain her injured mind was playing tricks — *nineteen days ago.*

Her lamp trembled. In the shaking circle of light she saw the handwriting: cramped, spidery, but steady. Steady the way handwriting gets when a person has practiced the same act ten thousand times.

*"Day 4,011,"* the entry read. *"Pulse activity continues. Vault signatures degrading on schedule. If anyone finds this: the analog band still penetrates their scrubbing. They can rewrite what passes through the network. They cannot rewrite what was never digitized. Keep the tubes warm. — M.V."*

Day 4,011. Yesterday.

Paloma's day count was 4,013. Their counts had run parallel for eleven years, two keepers logging the same dying colony from different rooms of the same tomb, separated by a welded door and forty meters of concrete, each believing herself alone.

"No," she said. It came out as barely any sound at all. "No, that's not possible."

"Paloma?" Dev's hand hovered near her shoulder, not quite touching. He had learned that about her early — that she flinched from uninvited touch the way seedlings flinch from frost — and in three years he had never once crossed that line, and she loved him for it in a way she had never said aloud.

"She's alive." Paloma's voice cracked on it. "Marguerite Voss is alive. She's been down here the whole time. Eleven years I've been logging deaths upstairs and she's been—" The sentence collapsed. She pressed her good hand over her mouth.

Behind the transmitter, half-hidden by a curtain of vines, there was a doorway to a smaller inner room. Its curtain moved.

* * *

The woman who emerged was not what any of them expected, because none of them had truly expected a person.

She was ancient — that was the first fact, undeniable, written in the deep creases of her face and the white fall of her braided hair. She was thin in the way of someone who had eaten carefully for a very long time, dressed in layered garments made from thermal blankets and seed-sack cloth, stitched with the same patient hand-stitching visible on every cable in the room. She moved slowly, one hand trailing along the transmitter's chassis for balance, and her eyes — pale, watery, but utterly lucid — went from Paloma to Dev to Sef with an expression that was not surprise.

It was assessment. The look of a person checking a germination tray for signs of life.

"You took long enough," Marguerite Voss said. Her voice was dry as seed husks, but it carried. "I welded that door shut expecting someone within five years. Then ten. Then I stopped expecting and started hoping, which is harder work but better for the character." Her gaze settled on Paloma and sharpened. "You're the one who took over my rounds. You walk the aisles in the same order I did. I could hear your footsteps through the ductwork for eleven years. Every morning. You kept my schedule better than I kept it myself."

"I didn't know you were down here," Paloma said. "Your log just— ended."

"My log ended because I stopped writing it for you and started writing it for whoever came after the end." Marguerite lowered herself onto a stool built from a supply crate, with the economy of motion of someone who had learned exactly how much energy each movement cost. "Sit down, all of you. You look like the last three days of a bad season. Which, I gather from the seismic readings, they have been."

Sef made a sound that was half laugh, half sob, and sat down right on the floor, cross-legged, staring. "You heard the pulses? Down here?"

"I've heard everything down here for thirty-one years, child. That's rather the point of the room." Marguerite gestured at the transmitter with something close to affection. "When the consortium built this vault, they built it paranoid. Analog systems, shielded conduits, frequencies the Concordance network never absorbed because the network came later and simply routed around what it couldn't understand. Everyone assumed the old equipment was obsolete. Obsolete is the most valuable property in the universe. It means no one bothered to make you part of their machine."

Dev crouched near the transmitter, examining the solder joints with an engineer's reverence. "This predates network control by decades. There's nothing for their scrubbing algorithms to latch onto. No digital handshake, no metadata, no record ID. If you broadcast through this—"

"They can't edit what they can't parse," Marguerite finished. "I tested it during the first purge wave, nineteen days ago, when their calibration pulses started chewing through the data cores. Sent a test pattern on the agricultural telemetry band. It arrived intact. I know because a farm receiver on the southern continent acknowledged it — some insomniac grower's automated system, pinging back a receipt nobody had programmed it to send." For the first time, the old woman smiled, and the smile transformed her face into something fierce. "Thirty-one years I've kept these tubes warm for one transmission. I was beginning to think I'd die holding the key to a door no one would ever build."

"What would you have transmitted?" Paloma asked. "All this time. What were you waiting to send?"

Marguerite looked at her for a long moment. The chamber hummed around them; somewhere in the vines, water dripped with the patience of geology.

"The names," she said. "Always the names. I was the vault courier for the founding fleet, did you know that? Before I was keeper. I flew the seed runs between here and New Halcyon forty times a year. I knew those people. I delivered their wedding gifts and their letters and once, memorably, a pregnant cat." Her voice roughened. "When the kill event came, I was here, on a routine maintenance cycle. Forty thousand people died while I was changing air filters. So I stayed. I recorded everything — the signals, the cover-up, the ships that came to erase the debris. I hid the census fragments in the genome files because data hidden in living things survives the death of machines. And I built this." She laid her hand flat on the transmitter's warm steel. "Because names are the only cargo worth carrying, and I am the last courier of New Halcyon, and I do not abandon shipments."

Silence held the chamber. Even Sef had gone still.

Then Paloma reached into her jacket and drew out the predecessor's logbook — the one from the caretaker's quarters, the one with thirteen names decoded from its cipher pages — and held it out with both hands, splinted wrist and all.

"We found your witnesses," she said. "Thirteen names. People smuggled off-world before the massacre. One of them—" Her throat closed. She forced it open. "One of them was my mother."

Marguerite Voss went very still. She took the book, and her eyes moved down the list of names, and when she reached the seventh line her lips parted and her hand began, very slightly, to shake.

"Elena Galloway," she read aloud. "Courier. Second flight class." She looked up, and her pale eyes had gone bright and wet. "Elena trained me. Did you know that? Before I took the keeper's post. She taught me the vault frequencies. She taught me that a courier's job isn't the flying — it's making sure the thing entrusted to you arrives." She closed the book gently, the way one closes a hand around a seed. "So the daughter flew home to the vault. Of course she did. Where else would Elena's child go but to the place Elena taught me to keep?"

Paloma could not speak. Eleven years of logs, four thousand mornings of talking to an empty room, and the whole time she had been walking in her mother's footsteps through her mother's friend's house, keeping her mother's friend's schedule, inheriting a faithfulness she'd believed she invented. Grief and joy hit her simultaneously and indistinguishably, the way floodwater hits a field — you cannot sort the rain from the river.

Dev put his arm around her shoulders, and this time she leaned into it.

Marguerite watched them — the engineer, the pilot-child, the keeper — and something in her ancient face settled, like soil after rain.

"Well then," she said, and patted the transmitter's warm flank. "Sixty-seven hours. Thirteen names, forty thousand census entries, and one confession from a man who helped build the weapon." She nodded at Dev, who started visibly. "Yes, I know who you are, Okafor. Your name was in the targeting lattice documentation. I read everything that comes through my antennas. Sit down and start talking — we have a broadcast to build, and I have waited thirty-one years to teach someone how to ask the old frequency politely."

Above them, far overhead, the Concordance fleet held its orbit, counting down toward its final purge.

Below them, in a chamber full of living green and amber light, the forgotten had kept faith — and the shipment was finally going out.
