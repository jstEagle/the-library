## 22. Planting Season

The morning of the first planting, Paloma woke before the sun and lay still in her narrow bed, listening to the vault breathe.

It had been breathing for a year now — a sound she had never heard in her eleven years alone. Footsteps on the sublevel stairs. The clank and hiss of Dev's rebuilt transmitter array cycling through its dawn diagnostics. Sef's voice, hoarse with sleep, calling roll for the morning flight class even though the students wouldn't arrive for another two hours. The small percussion of other people living their lives around hers, overlapping, imperfect, constant.

She had spent so long learning to sleep through silence that she now had to relearn how to sleep through company. Some nights she still woke at 0300 out of habit, certain something was wrong, and lay listening until she identified each sound — the coolant pumps, the wind chimes Sef had welded from wrecked drone plating outside the east door, Dev humming an old Lagos hymn under his breath while he soldered. Then she would close her eyes again, and the wrongness would resolve into what it had always been: not danger, but abundance.

Today there would be no log entry. Or rather — today, everything would be the log entry.

* * *

By first light the whole valley knew what day it was. The Meridian Seed Vault no longer hid behind its ridge; the Concordance inquiry had seen to that, dragging its coordinates into every newsfeed in the system as evidence exhibit one. What the inquiry had not anticipated was that people would come. First investigators, then journalists, then pilgrims, then farmers who simply wanted to see the place where forty thousand names had poured out of the ground and refused to be deleted.

They came in couriers and grain haulers and patched-together skiffs, and they kept coming, until someone — Dev, though he still denied it — began building them somewhere to sleep. The old caretaker's quarters were a memorial now, the children's drawings preserved under glass, the predecessor's logbook displayed open to the page where she had written, in a hand gone shaky with cold: *If anyone finds this, the count is wrong. It is always wrong. Count again.* School groups filed past it in reverent silence, and then burst into noise the moment they were outside, because they were children, and Paloma loved them for it.

The vault itself had become a school. Not by decree — the provisional council had offered a charter three times, and three times Paloma had declined to sign anything that gave the Concordance's successor bureaucracy a claim on the place. Instead it happened the way seeds happen: informally, inevitably, in soil. Sef taught flying to any teenager who could hold a wrench, using the courier route as her syllabus, drilling them on the blind corridors her mother had mapped until the kids could thread the blockade canyons in their sleep. "You fly low," Paloma had overheard her say last week, "because low is where the truth lives. High is where they edit you." She said it like a joke. None of the students laughed. All of them wrote it down.

Dev taught engineering in the mornings and testimony in the afternoons. He had never stopped being surprised by his own clemency — checked the terms monthly, Paloma suspected, waiting for the fine print to rot through. But the tribunal had been clear: the man who built the targeting lattice and then spent a year helping to broadcast its victims' names was worth more free than imprisoned, and more dangerous free than silent. So he stood in the old germination hall with a chalkboard salvaged from a colony ship and taught young engineers how to build things that could not be quietly repurposed. *Design for witness*, was the first rule on his board, underlined twice. *If your machine cannot testify to what it has done, you have not finished designing it.*

And Paloma — Paloma gardened. That was all. That was everything.

* * *

The field lay on the south slope, four hectares of ground that had never been farmed, broken and turned over the previous autumn by volunteers who came from as far as the relay stations. The recovered genomes had spent a year in quarantine germination, coaxed back from their long sleep in the seed-data cores — the same cores that had carried the census, the dead hidden inside the living, now emptied of grief and refilled with growth. Forty thousand entries. And threaded among them, the crop lines New Halcyon had been founded to grow: drought-wheat bred for a valley that no longer existed, salt-tolerant greens, a barley whose genome carried, in its junk sequences, the founding consortium's true census — the inheritance of the dead buried in living things, as Dev had said that first terrible week, when none of them had yet learned whether the truth would save them or only cost them everything.

It had cost them nearly everything. It had also saved them. Paloma had stopped trying to separate the two accounts; they balanced the way a field balances, messily, season to season.

The wheat went in first. She knelt at the head row with a handful of seed in her palm — ordinary-looking, unremarkable, which was the point. Nothing about the dead should be ornamental. Behind her, the crowd waited: three hundred people, maybe more, farmers and pilots and students and the families of squadron crew who had turned their ships rather than fire on the vault, some of whom still flinched at loud noises. Sef stood at the front with her cadets in formation that wasn't quite a formation, because Sef refused to teach parade discipline. Dev stood beside Paloma, offering his arm without being asked, and she took it, and together they walked the first furrow.

"You don't have to read all of them," he said quietly. It was the fourth time he'd said it.

"I know."

"It'll take eleven hours at reading pace. Your voice won't—"

"Dev." She stopped at the head of the row and looked up at him — at this man who had fallen through her roof and her solitude and her defenses, who had read her logs in secret for three years because loneliness recognized loneliness, who had confessed his guilt in her greenhouse and paid for it with a year of tribunals. "I have been talking to no one for eleven years. I have practice."

He laughed — that low, startled laugh she had learned to wait for — and kissed her forehead, and stepped back, and the crowd hushed.

Paloma opened the predecessor's logbook to its final blank page, where the transcription committee had copied the full census in columns fine as scripture. She took up a pinch of seed. She pressed it into the earth.

"Abadi, Tesfaye," she read. "Colonist. Hydroponics technician, third ring." Seed into soil. "Abbas, Maryam. Colonist. Age six."

The names went down into the ground one by one, and the crowd did what she had asked them to do, which was nothing. No ceremony beyond the sowing. When a name landed in a family's ear — a relative, a neighbor, a child grown old in exile — that family knelt where they stood and put their own hands in the dirt, and planted the next row themselves, and read the next names themselves, and so the reading spread down the slope like water finding its level, hundreds of voices braided into one long recitation that did not pause for weeping and did not hurry past it either.

Paloma read for hours. Her wrist — the one she'd broken catching the archive door nine seconds before the world changed — ached in the cold, and she was glad of it. Pain was a kind of memory the blockade had never figured out how to rewrite.

* * *

At midday she rested, sitting against the south wall with a cup of tea, watching Sef run her cadets through a low pass over the valley. The skiffs came in under the ridge line exactly as the old courier route demanded, engines throttled to a whisper, and pulled up in a staggered line that Sef had named, to the students' delight, *the manifest formation* — because, she told them, "we are the cargo now, and we are delivering ourselves."

One of the cadets broke formation. A girl of maybe fourteen, flying too high, showing off, and Sef's voice crackled across the training band sharp enough that Paloma heard it from the wall: "Altitude. Down. Now." And then, softer, when the girl had dropped back into line: "High is where they edit you. Say it."

"Low is where the truth lives," fifteen voices chorused across the valley, and Paloma closed her eyes and let the words wash over her like weather.

Her mother had flown that route once. Ilse Galloway, vault courier, who vanished when Paloma was nine, who turned out to have been one of thirteen witnesses smuggled off-world before the kill event — a name in the predecessor's logbook that had undone Paloma entirely, that first night of decoding, until Dev had sat with her until dawn saying nothing, because there was nothing to say, only presence, only company. Her mother had never been found. The inquiry's best reconstruction held that the courier ship had been intercepted at the system's edge, its records purged, its pilot listed as *unregistered personnel — decommissioned*. The same word. Always the same word.

But the route survived. The route survived in Sef's mother's maps and in Sef's hands and now in fifteen pairs of teenage hands, and every time those skiffs flew low over the valley, Paloma chose to believe her mother could see them. Belief was a crop too. You planted it without proof and tended it anyway.

* * *

In the late afternoon, Dev found her at the transmitter array.

He had rebuilt it honestly — that was his phrase, and he used it every time, *rebuilt it honestly*, as if honesty were a structural specification, load-bearing. The old seed-packet transmitters that had carried the truth out on agricultural frequencies still ran, but now they ran open-source, their code published, their logs public, any farmer in the system able to verify every packet. Thousands of farms still received them passively, as they always had. The difference was that now the farms knew what they were receiving, and had organized, and had votes, and were not afraid.

"The council sent another request," Dev said, settling beside her on the array housing. "Directorship. Formal this time. There's a hearing next month."

"I know."

"They'll keep asking."

"They will." Paloma watched the antenna turn slowly against the darkening sky, tracking the relay stations, keeping faith with the horizon. For eleven years she had been a caretaker — of seeds, of logs, of a grief no one else would carry. Caretaking had been a way of hiding. Tending something small enough that the world couldn't ask anything larger of her.

But the world had asked. And she had answered. And the woman who had held a transmitter coupling open with her own body while forty thousand names burned through the purge pulse — that woman could not go back to being a ghost who gardened.

"I'm going to accept," she said. "But on my terms. The vault stays a school and a memorial. The genomes stay public domain. And the field—" she nodded toward the south slope, where the last light was picking out the rows, and the scattered figures still kneeling in them, still reading, "—the field gets expanded every year. One hectare per thousand names, until all forty thousand have ground of their own. Let the Concordance's successors fund topsoil instead of blockades. Let them watch it grow."

Dev was quiet a moment. Then: "Ilse Galloway's daughter, director of Meridian."

"Don't." But she was smiling, and he could hear it, and he laughed again, and leaned his shoulder against hers, and they watched the stars come out over the field together, two exiles who had chosen each other, which was the only kind of choosing that had ever mattered.

* * *

She went back out at dusk, alone, when the crowd had gone and the students had grounded their skiffs and the valley had settled into that deep communal quiet she was still learning to trust.

The first rows were already up — not sprouted, not yet, but visible, the seed tucked into its furrows, patient, certain, in the way of all living things that have survived worse than winter. Paloma knelt at the head of the field where she had begun, and pressed her palm flat against the soil, and felt the faint warmth the earth holds after a long day of sun.

"Forty thousand," she said aloud, to the field, to the dead, to the dark coming on gentle over the ridge. "Count again. It is always wrong. Count again."

And from the school wing, faint through the stone, she heard Sef laughing with her cadets over dinner, and Dev's hymn rising from the workshop, off-key and unhurried, and the coolant pumps, and the wind chimes made of wrecked drones ringing in the evening wind — the vault breathing, all of them inside it, all of it alive.

In the spring, the wheat would come up green over forty thousand names, and it would be ordinary wheat, bread wheat, the kind you grind and share, and that would be the whole of the miracle: that the dead had been returned to the common, the daily, the shared loaf. That remembering could be as plain as planting, and as endless.

Paloma Galloway stayed on her knees in the dark a while longer, sowing the last row by feel, naming the last names softly into the ground — and the ground, as it always does, as it always has, kept them.
