## 2. Nine Seconds of Falling

The flicker came at 0347 station time, which is to say it came in the hour Paloma had privately renamed *the listening dark* — the stretch when Kestrel's ventilation cycled down to a whisper and the only sound in the world was the beacon's carrier hum, so low she felt it in her sternum more than heard it, like a second heartbeat that had moved in without asking.

She was awake because she was always awake then. She had stopped fighting it months ago. Sleep came to her now in shallow, languageless tides — no Portuguese, no English, none of the fleet's clipped Standard, not even the old Meridian creole her grandmother had sung into her hair. Just pressure. Just shapes moving behind her eyes with intent but no words, like fish passing under ice. She had logged it once, early on, in the dry clinical voice she used for anomalies: *Subject reports cessation of dream-linguistic activity. No cognitive distress noted.* The lie of that last clause sat in her chest every time she thought of it.

So she was awake, standing barefoot in the observation blister with a cup of cold rooibos going bitter in her hand, watching the gas giant Vesperal turn its slow amber wheel below, when gravity forgot about them for nine seconds.

It did not fail dramatically. That was the horror of it. There was no klaxon, no lurch, no dramatic cascade of floating cutlery. Gravity simply *thinned*, the way light thins behind a cloud, and for nine seconds everything on Kestrel became slightly untrue. Her tea climbed out of the cup in a trembling amber rope. The loose coil of her hair lifted off her neck. Dust that had settled in the blister's seams for years rose in a fine grey exhalation, as if the station itself were breathing out after holding its breath for decades.

Paloma's stomach dropped — not the rollercoaster drop, but something deeper, something vestibular and ancient, the body's oldest alarm: *the ground is lying to you.*

She gripped the rail. She counted. It was instinct; xenolinguists count everything, syllables, pauses, the intervals between gestures. One. Two. Three. The tea-rope broke into beads that drifted like brown planets. Four. Five. Somewhere below her, in the maintenance shafts, a wrench she'd left on a magnetic rack began to sing against its clamps. Six. Seven. Out beyond the blister glass, Vesperal's cloud bands — those patient, millennial rivers of ammonia — *shivered*. She saw it with her own eyes, a ripple crossing the face of the giant like a flinch, like something enormous being touched in its sleep. Eight.

Nine.

Gravity remembered itself. The tea beads fell, all of them at once, a small brown rain pattering across the deck plates. Her hair dropped. The dust resettled. The wrench went silent mid-note. And in the sudden restored weight, Paloma realized two things in the same breathless instant: that her hands were shaking hard enough to rattle the cup against its saucer, and that the beacon — the beacon nobody ordered, the beacon older than the fleet's own archives — was awake.

She knew it before any console told her. The carrier hum had changed pitch. For three years it had been a single sustained note, a monk's drone, the sound of a machine holding one vowel forever. Now there was modulation in it. Now it was *saying something.*

She ran. Barefoot down the corridor, tea abandoned, the soles of her feet slapping cold deck plate, past the galley where the gravity hiccup had knocked a magnetized pan to the floor, into the comms bay where the beacon's telemetry lived in a nest of screens she'd long since memorized the way other people memorize faces.

Every display was lit. Every display was wrong.

The power draw read as if the beacon had opened every channel it possessed simultaneously — channels Paloma hadn't known it had, bands buried in its firmware beneath strata of code older than the fleet charter. The transmission log, which had shown nothing but the carrier tone for thirty-one months, was filling line by line, timestamped entries scrolling too fast to read, and the signal strength meter was pinned so hard against its ceiling that the needle had bent, actually *bent*, a physical needle in an age of digital readouts, as if the instrument itself wanted to leave its body.

And the waveform on the main screen — she would remember this shape for the rest of her life — was not the smooth sine of a carrier wave. It was jagged, layered, braided. Harmonics stacked on harmonics, overtones folding into undertones, a structure so dense it looked less like a signal than like a throat.

Paloma put on the headset because her hands made the decision before her mind consented to it.

The first thing she heard was breathing. Slow, unhurried, human-shaped breathing, the kind with a slight catch at the top of each inhale. She knew that catch. She heard it in her own recordings, played back during her certification reviews fifteen years ago, the examiner circling it in red: *notable inspiratory hitch, no clinical significance.*

Then the voice began, and it was hers.

Not similar. Not reminiscent. *Hers* — her exact timbre, the faint rasp at the bottom of her register, the way she rounded her vowels a half-tone too far back from the teeth, a habit her grandmother's Meridian creole had carved into her mouth before Standard ever got there. But aged. The voice coming through the headset was hers with twenty or thirty years laid over it like sediment, sanded smooth in places, deepened in others, carrying a weight her living throat had never been asked to hold.

And threaded through it, woven into the spaces between her own phonemes, were sounds no human throat makes.

She heard them the way you hear a wrong note in a familiar song — instantly, bodily, before analysis catches up. Clicks articulated somewhere behind the soft palate. A trilled fricative that seemed to be produced by two parts of the vocal tract disagreeing with each other. A vowel that glided through four positions without ever landing, a sound like a word trying to decide what it meant. They weren't appended to her speech. They were *inflected into it*, the way emotion is inflected into speech, invisible in the transcript and unmistakable in the ear.

The words themselves, when she could separate them from the alien phonemes wrapped around them, were worse.

*"—counted nine,"* the voice said, in her voice, *"but the ninth is not a number. The ninth is a door. Tell them the ninth is a door—"*

Paloma tore the headset off. It clattered against the console. She stood in the comms bay with her heart slamming and the station humming around her and the whole system, apparently, ringing like a struck bell, and she made herself breathe the way she taught apprentices to breathe before field contact work: four counts in, seven held, eight out. The body first. The body is the instrument. Calm the instrument and the interpretation will follow.

The interpretation did not follow.

Because here was the thing she could not say aloud, could barely frame even in the privacy of her own skull: the voice was terrified. Underneath the authority of it, underneath the strange doubled diction, she could hear fear — her own particular fear, the one she recognized from bad nights, from the dreamless tides, the fear of speaking into a silence that might answer. Whoever that voice belonged to, whatever it was, it was afraid of exactly what she was afraid of.

Recognition is a kind of terror. Nobody warns you. You spend your life studying the moment when a species hears itself in another species' call — the startle response, the pupil dilation, the involuntary stillness — and you think you understand it academically. Then it happens to you, alone, at 0347, in your own voice, and you understand that recognition is the universe reaching into your chest and rearranging something.

She should have cut the transmission. Every protocol said so. Anomalous broadcast, unverified source, possible spoofing, possible hostile action — seal the channel, freeze the logs, notify fleet command and let the people with rank and armor decide. Her hand was actually on the isolation switch. She could feel the cool plastic of it under her fingertip.

Instead she reached past it and hit record.

*Preserve the artifact,* her grandmother used to say, back on Meridian stories and kitchen-table linguistics, back before the ship vanished from all charts and most memories. *You can always destroy a thing later. You can never un-destroy it.* Paloma had thought she was quoting archival doctrine. Standing there, recording her own future voice reciting a grammar of doors, she understood her grandmother had been talking about something else entirely.

The data stream found her then. She would struggle, later, to describe it honestly — *pulled* was the closest verb, though nothing touched her. The console displays began to scroll in patterns that resolved, against every probability, into legibility. Not text. *Structure.* The signal wasn't just broadcasting; it was offering itself, opening like a hand, and the terminal interface softened around it the way ice softens around a warm palm, and Paloma felt — this was the part she would never put in an official log — felt *invited*. Felt the way a word feels when it finally finds its sentence.

On the main screen, the waveform braided itself tighter, and inside the braid she could see it now, could see the architecture: repetition with variation, call and return, a phrase stated, restated, restated again with one element changed each time. Any first-year student of xenolinguistics would have known the shape. It was how you teach a language to a stranger. It was how you teach a child.

It was a lullaby. Whatever else it was, whoever's voice it wore, the signal was structured like a song sung to someone falling asleep — and Paloma felt the dreamless tides behind her eyes stir for the first time in two years, felt the wordless shapes down there turn toward the surface like fish rising toward light, and one of them almost had a shape she knew, almost had a—

The station lights died.

All of them, every panel, every strip, plunging the comms bay into absolute black except the screens, which burned on, fed by something that was not Kestrel's grid. In the dark, the beacon's hum swelled, and the voice — her voice, old and unafraid now, tender now, terribly tender — spoke directly into the silence:

*"You counted with me. Good. Now I will count with you."*

Nine seconds, Paloma thought. Nine seconds of falling. And somewhere in the black, in the marrow-deep quiet where her dreams used to live, something answered in a language she had never learned and somehow already knew.

The comm board lit with an incoming priority hail. Fleet encryption. Commander's band.

They had felt it too.
