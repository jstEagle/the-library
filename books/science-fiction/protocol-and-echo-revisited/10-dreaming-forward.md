## 10. Dreaming Forward

The dreams came back in spring — or what passed for spring on Kestrel, when the station's thermal regulators eased their hum by a half-tone and the hydroponics bay exhaled the smell of wet green things into corridors that had forgotten seasons. Paloma woke on the third morning of that season with a word resting on her tongue like a smooth stone, and she lay still for a long time before she dared to say it aloud.

It was not a human word. It was not an alien one either. It was hers.

She said it once, quietly, to the dark ceiling of her quarters, and the station's ambient systems — the beacon's voice threaded through every bulkhead now, public and unhidden — answered with a single soft pulse, like a parent acknowledging a child who has finally spoken its first true sentence.

She got up and made tea and did not write the word down. Some things you keep in the body.

* * *

The fleet had spent seven months learning how to be embarrassed in public, which was, Paloma thought, the most hopeful thing she had ever witnessed an institution do.

The broadcast from the relay — nine seconds of her own living voice spliced into three generations of inherited protocol, heard simultaneously by every faction in the system — had not ended the truce so much as made it obsolete. You could not threaten a rival over comms when both of you had just wept at the same transmission. The Meridian's last linguist, whose recorded echo still tended the relay out past dead space, had understood this: shame shared is not shame; it is memory, and memory can be built upon.

The Concord and the Rimward Compact now held their negotiations in the open, on a station neither owned, with transcripts published within the hour. Delegates argued over water rights and patrol lanes with the same old venom, but beneath it ran something new: the knowledge that they had all been raised, without knowing it, on the same lullaby. Three generations of children sung to sleep by a protocol designed to make strangers into kin. The aliens — whoever they had been, wherever they had gone — had written patience into the very structure of the signal. A grammar that could only be learned slowly. A syntax that rewarded listening.

Paloma attended the sessions sometimes, as a consultant, as a witness, as the woman whose voice everyone recognized and no one quite knew how to address. Commander Ivo Reyes attended more often. He sat through the long afternoons in his dress grays with his career formally ended and his conscience formally intact, taking notes no one had asked him for, and afterward he would find her in the corridor outside the chamber and say nothing, and the nothing would be warm.

"You look tired," he said today, falling into step beside her.

"I slept well," she said. "That's different."

He glanced at her sideways. "The dreams again?"

"The dream." She smiled. "It's only ever the one dream now. But it keeps getting longer."

* * *

Kestrel had changed too. The beacon nobody ordered now had a visitor's gallery, a plaque, and a queue — tourists drifting past the maintenance cradle where the great antenna hung like a sleeping animal, reading the placard that explained, in careful diplomatic language, almost nothing. The commissioning records were public now. All of them. Every fragment the archives had swallowed, every redacted line, laid end to end across a display wall that visitors photographed and mostly did not understand.

The records did not name a commissioner. They named a date — a date eleven years in Paloma's future — and a signature that the forensic linguists had spent four months trying to dismiss as forgery, and had finally, reluctantly, authenticated.

Her handwriting. Her hand, aged. On a document older than her grandmother's service aboard the *Meridian*.

She stood before the display wall some evenings after the gallery closed, when the lights dimmed to preservation mode and the beacon overhead breathed its slow thermal breath, and she read the signature until it stopped looking like hers and started looking like a promise someone else had kept on her behalf.

* * *

The apprentice arrived on a Tuesday shuttle: a thin, serious young woman named Sefa Okonkwo-Vale, twenty-three, with a xenolinguistics degree earned partly through correspondence because her home habitat couldn't afford a full faculty, and a scar along her jaw she didn't explain, and eyes that went immediately, hungrily, to the antenna.

"You're the one who dreamed it," she said, shaking Paloma's hand with a grip too firm for her frame. "The ninth second. Everyone says you dreamed the grammar before you spoke it."

"Everyone is wrong," Paloma said. "I dreamed it my whole life. I just forgot I was allowed to remember." She watched the girl absorb this, watched her decide whether to be intimidated. "You'll forget things here too. That's part of the training. The forgetting is where the answers go to wait."

Sefa frowned. "That doesn't sound like any methodology paper I've read."

"No," Paloma agreed. "It wouldn't be. Put your bag down. I'll show you the maintenance rituals."

* * *

She taught her the way the echo had taught her, the way the *Meridian*'s last linguist had taught the echo, the way — she was increasingly certain — someone yet to come had taught them all. Oil the gimbal bearings on the odd days. Sing to the calibration array in whatever language you dream in; it doesn't matter which, only that it's true. Log everything, especially the things that frighten you, because fear is data wearing a disguise.

On the fourth evening she took Sefa up to the observation blister above the antenna cradle, where the stars came down close and the beacon's voice was loudest, a subsonic warmth in the floor plates, in the bones, in the fluid of the inner ear.

"Do you hear it?" Paloma asked.

"It sounds like a choir holding one note forever."

"That's what it sounds like. Now listen to what it means."

And she put her hand flat against the cold viewport, the old gesture, the inherited one, and let the signal rise through her palm and up the arm and into the chest cavity where language actually lives, and she translated — not word for word, never word for word, but truly — the way you'd translate a mother's humming for someone who had never been hummed to.

Sefa listened for a long time. When she finally spoke, her voice had gone small. "It's waiting for someone."

"Yes."

"Not us. Someone who hasn't—"

"Yes," Paloma said again. "Now you understand why the records are signed in the future. The beacon wasn't commissioned by the past, child. It was commissioned by whoever finishes it. The signature is a door, not a receipt."

Sefa looked at her, and Paloma watched the exact moment the girl's career plans rearranged themselves around a longer horizon. It looked like grief and relief arriving together, holding hands.

* * *

Her last night on Kestrel, Paloma dreamed the whole dream, unbroken, for the first time.

She stood in a room that was the beacon's heart rendered as architecture — a cathedral of standing waves, walls of light that moved when she breathed — and across the room stood a woman with her own face, older, silver at the temples, wearing the faded uniform of a fleet that no longer existed under that name. The woman was writing. When she finished, she turned the page around so Paloma could see the signature, and she said, in the language that belonged to neither humans nor the vanished ones but to the space between:

*"You asked me once who commissioned the beacon. I'm answering late. Forgive me. Time is a translation problem, and I was always better at listening than at explaining."*

*"What did I send?"* Paloma asked in the dream.

*"Everything you were given. Everything you earned. And one thing extra — the flaw. Your bilingual stammer, your imperfect human throat. That's the part that worked. Perfection was the trap. The echo offered me permanence and I nearly took it. You chose the flawed voice instead, and the flawed voice is the only kind that can be inherited. Remember that when you train her. Don't polish her. Let her crack in her own places."*

The dream-woman smiled then, and it was Paloma's smile, worn soft with use, and she folded the page and slid it into an envelope that was also, somehow, a gravity flicker, nine seconds long, and handed it backward through the dark.

Paloma woke with salt on her lips and the taste of a word she had not yet invented, and she lay in the grey pre-dawn of Kestrel's artificial morning and laughed until she cried, quietly, so as not to wake the station, though she suspected the station was awake anyway, listening, keeping the answer safe for the day she would need to give it.

* * *

She left on the same Tuesday shuttle route, a month later, with one bag and no ceremony. Reyes met her at the airlock, out of uniform for the first time she'd ever seen, holding two cups of terrible station coffee.

"Where does a retired xenolinguist go," he asked, "when she's finished inheriting?"

"Outward," she said. "There's a relay past dead space with a custodian who's been alone a very long time. The echo can't leave — it's structure, not soul. But someone can visit. Someone should. And there are other signals, Ivo. Fainter ones, further out. Other lullabies. If the *Meridian* inherited its protocol, someone gave it to them, and I want to know who sang first."

He nodded slowly. "And if the fleet asks you to come back? The new charter has a seat open. They're calling it the Office of Shared Memory. They want you to head it."

"Tell them the office already exists," she said. "It's called childhood, and it's staffed. Every lullaby is a treaty. They'll figure it out." She took the coffee, drank it, grimaced, drank more. "Besides — I have an apprentice running the beacon now, and a debt to pay forward to a woman with my face. Loyalty isn't a building, Ivo. It's a direction."

They stood a moment in the noise of the docking ring, two people who had lied politely across a table once and had spent every day since telling each other the truth in smaller and smaller pieces, until almost none were left hidden.

"Come back sometime," he said.

"I will," she said, and meant it in the tense that has no human name yet — the one the beacon uses, the one that points at a moment that is already remembering you.

The shuttle released Kestrel's spin-gravity with a gentle shudder, and below her the station turned, and the antenna turned with it, singing its one long note to the dark. Through the viewport she watched it shrink to a spark, then a rumor, then a faith.

In the seat beside her, her travel case held a single sheet of paper — the transcript of the commissioning record, the authentic one, the impossible one — and as the ship cleared the station's shadow and the stars came full and hard, she took it out and read the signature one last time.

Then she took out a pen — an actual pen, ink, an heirloom absurdity — and beneath the old signature, in the margin, in her own living hand, she wrote the word she had woken with in the spring. The one that was neither human nor alien. The one that was hers.

It translated, roughly, as: *received, and passing on.*

Somewhere ahead of her, fainter than faint, another signal was clearing its throat.

She leaned back, closed her eyes, and began, deliberately, to dream toward it.
