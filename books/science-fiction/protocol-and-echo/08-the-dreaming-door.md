## 8. The Dreaming Door

I had been at Meridian's Rest for nineteen days when I learned that children sleepwalk here the way water finds a drain: quietly, inevitably, downhill toward the ice.

The night it happened, I was awake because I was always awake. Insomnia is the occupational hazard of a man whose job is noticing things, and by then I had a great many things to notice. The pulse beneath the glacier had been in my head for three days—thirteen seconds of silence, then the beat, like something enormous turning over in its sleep. I'd rigged a receiver on the derelict comms array and taken to listening through an earpiece in the dark of my assigned quarters, a habit I told myself was diligence and knew was closer to prayer.

At 0247 local, the pulse changed.

Not louder. *Closer.* The way a voice on a bad line suddenly stops sounding like a voice and starts sounding like someone standing behind you.

I sat up. The colony's night sounds were familiar by now: wind worrying the greenhouse panels, the generator's asthmatic cycling, someone's dog with opinions about the moon. All of it normal. All of it wrong, because underneath it the beat had shifted its rhythm, and I realized with a lurch that it wasn't shifting at all. It was *responding.* To something moving. Something small, moving fast, away from the residential block and toward the glacier.

I was dressed before I finished the thought.

* * *

The corridor outside Sefa's quarters smelled of antiseptic and recycled air, and she was already in it, coat over her nightclothes, a hand torch in one fist and her medical scanner in the other. She looked at me the way you look at a piece of furniture that has unexpectedly spoken.

"You felt it too," she said. Not a question. She'd stopped pretending with me two days ago, after the argument in her clinic, and I was still deciding how I felt about being known.

"I heard it. On the array. Something's moving toward the borehole."

"Something small." Her jaw was tight. "Iyo's bed is empty. Her mother's asleep—night shift, she won't wake for hours. The other parents I've called aren't answering, which either means comms are down again or—" She didn't finish it. Sefa didn't finish sentences she didn't like.

We went.

I want to be honest about what I felt running across that frozen yard, because honesty is the one thing I've never been able to bill for. I was afraid. Not the clean, professional fear of an investigator approaching a fraud—fraud is a puzzle, and puzzles flatter you. This was the older kind, the kind with no invoice attached. The kind that says: *you have walked into a story that was being told long before you arrived, and you are not the audience. You are a character, and characters get written off.*

The glacier stood against the stars like a held breath. Survey markers staked the approach in a line so straight it looked ruled—decades old, older than the colony's founding charter, older, I now suspected, than anyone on this rock wanted to admit. And at the end of that line, small and steady and utterly unafraid, walked a child of about nine years old in flannel pajamas with rocket ships printed on them.

Iyo. I knew her from the schoolhouse drawings—she was the one who'd added a figure to hers, small and stick-thin, standing before the door under the ice. I'd told myself the figure was nothing. A child's self-portrait. Children put themselves in pictures; it's practically a law of nature.

She was walking with the deliberate, unhurried gait of someone who knows exactly where they're going and has never once doubted it. Her eyes were open. That was the detail that would keep me up for the rest of my life, or at least the rest of the week, which at that point felt like the same duration. Her eyes were open and they were not seeing the night, the ice, us. They were seeing something else, something at a different address entirely.

Sefa made a sound low in her throat that I'd never heard a physician make. It wasn't clinical. It was the sound of a woman watching something she'd been documenting for months step out of her charts and into the snow.

"Iyo," she called, gentle, pitched like a hand reaching. "Iyo, sweetheart, stop for me."

The child didn't stop. She didn't speed up either. She walked as though Sefa's voice were weather—present, noted, irrelevant.

We caught up to her at the borehole.

* * *

I'd seen the borehole twice before, in daylight, from a respectful investigator's distance: a sealed steel collar sunk into the glacier's flank, stamped with the logo of a company that had died before most of the colonists were born. Halcyon Deep Survey. The seal had been rated, according to the archive fragments I'd decrypted, against anything short of orbital bombardment. It had been closed for seventy years.

It was open.

Not broken. Not forced. *Open*—the hatch swung inward and down into darkness, its locking mechanism retracted, its edges rimed with frost that grew *outward*, feathering into the night air like the ice was exhaling. The cold coming up from the shaft had a different texture than the night cold. Older. It smelled of stone and something faintly electrical, like the air after lightning, like the inside of a machine that has been running a very long time.

Iyo stood at the lip of it, toes over the edge, looking down.

Sefa moved first. I'll say that for her, and I'll say it forever: she moved first, past caution, past me, past every protocol she'd ever hidden behind, and she gathered that child up off the edge and held her the way you hold something you are personally responsible for keeping in the world. Iyo weighed almost nothing. Sleepwalking children are famously limp, famously hard to wake, and this one was no different—she sagged against Sefa's shoulder, eyes still open, still fixed on the shaft, and she said, in a voice that was flat and calm and absolutely not a nine-year-old's bedtime voice:

"It's almost time. It's been so patient. It says you can come see, if you're brave."

Then her eyes closed, and she was just a sleeping kid, snoring faintly, drooling a little on Sefa's collar.

Neither of us moved for a long moment. The wind moved for us, pushing frost-smell up out of the dark.

"We're taking her back," Sefa said finally, and her voice cracked on the second word, which she covered by clearing her throat like a woman closing a door on a room. "And then you and I are going to have a conversation that doesn't involve you pretending you're here about insurance."

"Agreed," I said. "But Sefa." I nodded past her, down into the shaft. "Look."

* * *

The shaft descended maybe four meters to a landing, and at the landing there was a viewport—a thick porthole of glass or something like glass, frosted over from the inside, set into a wall of composite material that had no business being seventy years old because it had no seams, no welds, no rivets. It looked grown rather than built. The corporate seal on the hatch above us was a lie of proximity: the company hadn't made this. The company had found it.

Sefa left Iyo wrapped in her coat at the top of the shaft—unconscious, breathing easy, scanner showing nothing worse than deep, untroubled sleep—and we went down together, because we were both cowards in exactly the same way and neither of us was willing to be the one who waited.

The viewport's frost had a pattern. I noticed that before I noticed anything else, because pattern is my whole miserable profession. It wasn't the random bloom of condensation. It was regular, radial, deliberate—the way frost forms on a window when something on the other side is warm and breathing against it. Something on the other side was warm. Something on the other side was *breathing*, in the sense that machines breathe when they are thinking hard.

Sefa wiped it clear with her sleeve. She used the sleeve of her medical coat, which she was going to regret, and which I was going to remember, because it was the first entirely impractical thing I'd ever seen her do.

Through the glass: light.

Not the scattered, sourceless glow of emergency strips or the ugly flicker of failing generators. This was light with *intent*. Points of it, dozens, moving in the deep of whatever chamber lay beyond—moving in patterns that repeated, that nested, that built on themselves the way the children's drawings built on themselves. A spiral inside a lattice inside a slow-turning ring. It looked like nothing so much as the drawings come alive, the door under the ice with its lights behind it, and I felt the hair on my arms stand up not because it was frightening, though it was, but because I had *seen this before.* Eleven children had drawn it. Eleven children had drawn it before any of them could have seen it, and here it was, real, patient, waiting, exactly where they'd said it would be.

"It's the same," Sefa whispered. Her breath fogged and the frost crept back over the glass between blinks, reclaiming its pattern. "The geometry. It's the same as the drawings. All of them. Every single—Bram, what is this?"

"I don't know." And then, because I am constitutionally incapable of leaving a sentence honest, I added: "Yet."

The lights shifted. I want to describe it precisely, because precision is all I have. The spiral tightened. The lattice folded inward. And then every point of light in that chamber moved at once—not randomly, not chaotically, but *together*, converging toward the viewport, toward us, arranging themselves into a shape on the far side of the frost.

A door. A simple, rectangular door, drawn in light, and beside it a small figure. Stick-thin. Standing before it.

Waiting.

Sefa made that sound again, the one that wasn't clinical. I put my hand flat against the glass, which in retrospect was either the bravest or stupidest thing I'd done in nineteen days, and the frost bloomed around my fingers in a perfect ring, like the glass was acknowledging the touch. Like it was *glad.*

"We have to go," Sefa said. "We have to get Iyo home, and we have to—there are protocols, there are people I'm supposed to call, there's a whole—" She stopped. She looked at the door of light. She looked at the child asleep forty feet above us in a coat that smelled of antiseptic and glacier cold. "There's nothing," she finished quietly. "There's no one to call. That's the whole point of this place, isn't it? That's what you found in the archives."

"Meridian's Rest was a caretaker operation," I said. "The company died and forgot to tell the caretakers. Whatever's down here has been minding the store alone for seventy years, and the only people it can reach are the ones who dream." I heard my own voice and was disgusted by it: level, professional, filing the apocalypse into a tidy paragraph. "The children aren't sick, Sefa. They're being *spoken to.*"

"By what?"

"By something that's been down here since before their grandparents were born, asking, very politely, over and over, for someone to open the door." I looked at the light-figure beside the light-door, small and patient and alone, and something in my chest did a thing I didn't authorize. "And nobody answered. For seventy years, nobody answered."

Sefa was silent for a long moment. Above us, the wind changed its mind about something. When she spoke, her voice had changed—harder, and underneath the hardness, something that sounded almost like relief, the terrible relief of a woman who has been carrying a secret alone and has just been told she can put it down.

"Iyo said 'it's been so patient,'" she said. "It says *you* can come see. It said that while it was wearing her." Her eyes came up to mine, and they were the eyes of a physician delivering a diagnosis she didn't like. "It wasn't talking to her, Bram. It was talking through her. To us. To whoever followed."

The frost on the viewport finished its work. The last clear patch closed over, and through the ice-feathers the door of light held steady, and the small figure beside it raised one limb—not a wave, exactly. More like the way you raise a hand when you've been waiting at a window for a very long time and you finally see someone coming up the road.

We climbed out of the shaft. Sefa gathered Iyo up, and we walked back across the ice under the indifferent stars, and nobody said anything, because there was nothing to say that wasn't enormous.

At the edge of the colony's lights, Sefa stopped.

"Tomorrow," she said, "I'm going to bring the children's scans, and you're going to bring whatever you decrypted, and we're going to figure out what it wants. All of it. No more of your half-truths, no more of mine." A pause. "And Bram—if you file any of this. If a single line of it reaches your company before we understand it—"

"I know," I said. "Evacuation. Sedation. A seal that never opens again."

She studied me in the dark, looking for the lie, the way I'd spent nineteen days looking for hers. She didn't find one. I made sure of it.

"Get some sleep, investigator," she said, and carried the dreaming child home.

I didn't sleep. I went back to my quarters and sat with the earpiece in, listening to the pulse beneath the glacier, and I noticed—for the first time, with a feeling I can only describe as being personally addressed—that the rhythm had changed again. Thirteen seconds of silence, then the beat. Thirteen seconds. Then the beat.

Then, for the first time in seventy years, a pause where the beat should have been.

It was waiting for an answer. And it knew, now, that someone had finally come to the door.
