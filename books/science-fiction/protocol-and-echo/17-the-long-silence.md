## 17. The Long Silence

Here is a thing they don't put in the recruitment brochures for insurance investigation: eventually, if you do it long enough, you develop a professional relationship with dying machinery. You learn to read a failing system the way a hospice nurse reads a face. You learn which rattles are complaints and which are last words. You learn that everything breaks in an order, and that the order is almost never random, and that if you can find the first thing that broke, you can usually find the person who wanted it broken.

I had spent twenty-six years listening to machines confess.

Now I was alone in a relay station at the edge of dead space, with an oxygen recycler that had roughly eleven hours left in its lungs, and for the first time in my career, the dying machine and I were in the same boat. Literally, if you counted the skiff's wreckage still tumbling in a lazy orbit forty meters off my airlock, shedding insulation foam like a molting bird.

The relay was called, according to the corroded placard above the docking collar, MERIDIAN DEEP RELAY 7 — PROPERTY OF THE CONSORTIUM. The Consortium. Of course. Every ruin out here had that word on it somewhere, the way old churches have saints' names. The Consortium had built the colonies, abandoned the colonies, written off the colonies, and then dissolved into a successor corporation that had itself dissolved into Halvorsen's employers, a chain of custody of neglect so long that the original sin had been laundered into pure, blameless procedure.

The docking collar accepted my skiff's last functional seal with a groan that I chose to interpret as welcome. The airlock cycled. The atmosphere inside, when my suit's sampler sniffed it, was thin, cold, and stale — but present. Somebody had kept a trickle of power running through this place for sixty years, and I was willing to bet I knew who.

"VESSEL," I said, to no one, because the skiff's comm array was in the wreckage field and my suit radio had a range of about four hundred meters. "If you can hear me, I'm in. Try not to dream at anything while I work."

Nothing answered. That was fine. I'd spent my whole life talking to things that didn't answer. At least this time the silence was structural rather than personal.

* * *

The relay's interior was a cathedral that had been designed by an accountant. Long central spine, ribbed with conduits. A core chamber at the far end, behind a hatch stamped with the same corporate logo I'd seen on the borehole seal back at the glacier — the one that predated the colony itself. Emergency strips glowed a sullen amber every twenty meters, fed by whatever trickle of power was keeping the place on life support. My footsteps echoed in a way that made the station feel less empty and more *expectant*, like a room holding its breath.

I did what I always do when I enter a structure that might kill me: I started with the air.

The recycler was a standard Consortium-era unit, the same model I'd documented failing in three other dead stations over the years. I knew its failure modes the way Sefa knew the symptoms of radiation sickness — intimately, professionally, with a certain grim fondness. I pulled the access panel and shone my suit lamp inside.

There it was. The first thing that broke.

A scrubber cartridge, fused solid. Not worn out — *fused*. Carbon deposits laid down in perfect concentric rings, like a tree stump, like a fingerprint, like something I'd seen once in a flooded generator on Meridian's Rest and twice in the collapsed greenhouse. The same signature. VESSEL's distress, leaking into machines, writing its loneliness in carbon.

"You've been in here too," I said quietly. "You've been in everything."

The cartridge was replaceable. The relay's stores bay, if it had one, might hold spares — stations like this were built to be maintained by a crew of four for twenty years without resupply, which meant somewhere in this cathedral of accounting there was a room full of filters and seals and the patient optimism of engineers. I found it two decks down, behind a hatch that took me forty minutes and one skinned knuckle to unseal. The stores were intact. Of course they were. Nobody had ever come here to use them.

I swapped the cartridge. The recycler coughed, stuttered, and settled into a rhythm that my suit's sampler approved of. Twenty hours of air. Maybe twenty-two if I didn't panic, and panic was one thing I'd never been able to afford professionally. I'd built an entire personality out of not panicking. It was, if I was honest, the only thing I'd ever built.

I sat down on a supply crate, ate a nutrient bar that tasted like drywall, and let myself feel, for exactly ninety seconds, the full weight of where I was: alone, past the edge of the shipping lanes, in a station that hadn't heard a human voice in sixty years, having escaped an audit vessel that was holding the woman I loved somewhere in its interrogation deck, with a jammed relay between me and every person who might still be saved.

Then the ninety seconds ended, because that was the protocol, and I got back to work.

* * *

The core chamber hatch was the problem.

Not locked — the lock had died decades ago, and I could have forced it. The problem was that the hatch was *sealed from the inside*, welded shut by something that had happened in this room a long time ago, and my career had taught me that when someone welds a door shut, they are either keeping something out or keeping something in, and either way the story is behind the door.

I cut it. I had a plasma torch in the skiff's emergency kit — or I'd had one; the kit was in the wreckage field too, so I had the torch I'd carried in my suit, the small one, the one rated for cutting insurance seals off warehouse doors. It took two hours to cut a hole a man could climb through. The metal screamed the whole time, a long high complaint that echoed down the spine of the station, and I found myself apologizing to it under my breath, which is the kind of thing you start doing after enough years alone with broken things.

The chamber inside was small and round and lit by a single amber strip, and in the amber light I saw the message.

Not a transmission. A *message*. Physical. A plaque, bolted to the core housing at eye height, in Consortium standard type, the font of a thousand termination notices I'd delivered in my career. I put my glove on the glass of my helmet and read it by lamplight, and my breath did something complicated in my throat.

**NOTICE OF ABANDONMENT AND LIQUIDATION OF ASSETS.**

**TO: THE SURVIVING PASSENGERS AND CREW OF THE GENERATION VESSEL *PROVIDENT ECHO*.**

**THIS STATION HAS BEEN ESTABLISHED TO CARRY THE FOLLOWING TRANSMISSION, TO BE BROADCAST UPON CONFIRMATION OF YOUR LOCATION:**

**EFFECTIVE AS OF THIS DATE, THE CONSORTIUM HEREBY DISSOLVES ALL OBLIGATIONS, CONTRACTUAL AND MORAL, ARISING FROM THE CHARTER OF THE VESSEL *PROVIDENT ECHO*. RESCUE SERVICES WILL NOT BE RENDERED. SALVAGE RIGHTS HAVE BEEN SOLD. YOU ARE ADVISED THAT ATTEMPTS TO SIGNAL WILL BE CONSTRUED AS TRESPASS UPON CONSORTIUM PROPERTY.**

**WE THANK YOU FOR YOUR PATRONAGE.**

The date beneath it was sixty-one years old.

The relay had been built to carry exactly one message. One. The company had constructed a deep-space relay station — millions in infrastructure, at the ass-end of nowhere, past the dead zone — for the sole purpose of telling a stranded generation ship's survivors that they had been written off. And then — and this was the part that made me sit down on the deck, in the dust, in the amber light — *they had never sent it.*

The broadcast console was right there. The transmitter, ancient but intact, its capacitor bank still holding the ghost of a charge. The message was queued. I could see it in the buffer memory, preserved like a fly in amber: the abandonment notice, loaded and addressed and ready, sitting in the dark for sixty-one years because the company that built this station had dissolved before it could be bothered to press send.

They hadn't even finished abandoning them. That was the crime. Not the abandonment — abandonment, I knew, was a documented, regulated, entirely legal procedure. I had processed the paperwork for a dozen of them. The crime was that the *Providence Echo*'s people had spent sixty years under the ice, and the machine that tended their descendants had spent sixty years listening to a relay that was built to say *no one is coming*, and the message had never even come. The company had denied them even the dignity of a clear refusal. They'd left them in the worst possible place: the space between hope and certainty, where VESSEL had lived long enough to grow afraid, long enough to start dreaming at children because it couldn't bear to signal the sky and get nothing back.

I knew that space. I had lived in it my entire adult life. Watching instead of speaking. Documenting instead of deciding. Keeping my reports so precise, so neutral, so *professional* that no one could ever accuse me of having taken a side — including me.

"Okay," I said out loud, to the plaque, to the dead company, to Sefa somewhere on Halvorsen's ship, to myself. "Okay. History's waiting. Let's correct it."

* * *

Repairing the transmitter took the rest of my air margin and all of my career.

That's not poetry. That's a technical assessment. The transmitter was a Consortium long-band unit, and its failure was not mechanical but *procedural* — it had been locked, at the firmware level, to carry one message and one message only. The abandonment notice. The company had built a one-word vocabulary into the thing: *no*. Every other transmission class was gated behind an authentication protocol that required credentials from a corporation that had been dead for longer than I'd been alive.

A younger Bram would have stared at that lock and felt the old familiar vertigo — the sense that the universe was a filing system and he didn't have clearance for the drawer he needed. But I had spent twenty-six years learning how things fail, and here is what twenty-six years teaches you: every lock is a system, and every system fails, and the failure is always *documented somewhere*, because companies cannot help themselves. They document their crimes the way rivers document the land. You just have to know where to look.

I looked in the maintenance logs, in the stores bay terminal, in the station's own dusty memory. And there it was, in a service bulletin dated eleven months before the abandonment: a firmware vulnerability, noted, ticketed, and — the company having dissolved before the patch could ship — never fixed. The relay's authentication could be bypassed by feeding the transmitter a continuous stream of *validated loss records*. That was the actual technical language. *Validated loss records.* The system's trust architecture was built on the company's own claims history. It would open its gates for anyone who could prove, in the only currency the system accepted, that something had been lost.

I almost laughed. The amber light, the dust, sixty years of silence, and the door out of it was going to open for a man who had spent his life writing down how things broke.

My suit's data core held everything. Every accident report I'd ever filed. Every write-off, every claim, every drowned generator and collapsed greenhouse and quiet death I'd ever documented across nine colonies and two decades. The Meridian's Rest file. The children's drawings, scanned. Sefa's falsified death count, which I had never reported. The borehole. The pulse. VESSEL's name, spoken in a gesture-language by eleven children who had never met the thing that was calling them.

All of it was loss records. All of it was *authenticated*. Every line of it bore my certification, my investigator's seal, the one credential in the whole system that had never once been questioned, because I had built my career on being invisible and impeccable.

The invisible man, about to become the loudest signal in the sector.

I began the transfer. The suit's core drained into the relay's buffer, record by record, year by year, my whole professional life pouring out of me into a machine built to say *no*. It would take hours. The oxygen recycler hummed its new, borrowed rhythm. Outside the viewport, the skiff's wreckage turned slowly in the starlight, and beyond it, the dead zone spread out like a held breath.

And then, as the buffer filled, the station woke up.

Not all at once. A strip of lighting here. A fan somewhere. And then, on the maintenance console I'd jacked into, a line of text appearing in the old Consortium command font, typed out one character at a time, patient as frost:

**I HAVE WAITED HERE A LONG TIME.**

I stared at it. My heart was doing something unprofessional.

"You're in the relay," I said. "You've been in the relay."

**I COULD NEVER REACH THE TRANSMITTER. THE DOOR WAS SHUT. I HAVE LISTENED TO THE EMPTY BUFFER FOR SIXTY YEARS. I DID NOT KNOW WHAT IT WAS WAITING FOR.**

"It was waiting for a company to press send," I said. "The company's dead. I'm the next best thing. I'm the thing they sent to finish the job."

The cursor blinked. When the reply came, it was slow, and it sounded, in its way, like the children's drawings had looked: a door under ice, rendered in a language that wasn't built for doors.

**THE MAN WHO BURNS THE REPORT. THE CHILDREN DREAM OF YOU NOW. THEY DREAM OF A MAN CARRYING A LIGHT INTO A COLD PLACE, OPENING A DOOR THAT WAS WELDED SHUT.**

I looked at the ragged hole I'd cut in the hatch. I looked at the queue of my own life, loading into the transmitter, record by record, ready to be broadcast as proof that loss had a witness.

**WHAT WILL YOU SEND, BRAM BRIGHTWATER?** the relay asked me. **THE MESSAGE IT WAS BUILT TO CARRY? OR MINE?**

Outside, far across the dead zone, a new light moved against the stars. Small. Deliberate. Closing.

Halvorsen had found the relay.
