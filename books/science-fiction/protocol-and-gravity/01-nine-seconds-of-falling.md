## 1. Nine Seconds of Falling

The ore was honest work, and that was the whole point of it.

Ines Thornbury had learned that the best way to disappear was to become boring — boring in a way that made auditors yawn and customs drones wave him through with the lazy indifference they reserved for ships that smelled of dust and ballast and nothing worth stealing. The *Kestrel's Lament* — he'd kept the name, an act of penance or stupidity he'd never fully decided between — hauled refined nickel-iron slugs out of the Halcyon Belt down to the foundry platforms at Ceres Gate, four runs a month, every manifest filed on time, every transponder chirping exactly what it should. He paid his berth fees in full. He tipped the dockhands. He never argued about tariffs.

Nobody remembers a man who is always exactly where he said he would be.

He flew the long approach to the Belt's outer shoals with the patience of a man counting beads on a rosary: burn, coast, correct, coast. His hands knew the stick the way a blind man knows his own hallway. Around him the contested orbits did their slow, dangerous dance — Meridian picket cutters patrolling the shipping lanes like well-fed dogs, Kestrel Compact privateers lurking in the cold shadows past the navigation buoys, all of them pretending the other didn't exist while both sides quietly priced each other's hulls. Ines threaded between them the way water threads between stones. Five years of practice. Five years of being no one in particular, going nowhere interesting, carrying nothing anyone wanted.

His routines were liturgy. He flushed his heat sinks at odd intervals so his thermal signature never matched a pattern. He logged his sleep cycles honestly, because falsified logs were the first thing investigators looked for — he knew that better than anyone alive. He ate the same reconstituted stew on Tuesdays and Fridays. He had a plant, a straggling hydroponic basil that shouldn't have survived the radiation hardening of the cockpit glass but did, stubborn and green, and he talked to it sometimes in a low voice when the silence got too architectural.

He did not look at the news feeds from the inner system. He did not open mail from anyone whose surname began with a corporate prefix. And once a year, on a date he refused to mark on any calendar, he drank half a bottle of plum brandy and did not cry, and then he went back to being boring.

It was on a Tuesday, somewhere in the long dark between Buoy 44 and the Belt proper, that gravity forgot itself.

* * *

There was no warning. There never is, with physics.

One moment the *Lament* was under thrust, one-third g pressing him gently into his couch, the basil swaying in its clip-on planter, the stew warming on the galley plate. The next moment the ship's graviton plating stuttered — not failed, *stuttered*, like a heart skipping beats — and everything loose in the cabin rose six centimeters and hung there, confused.

Ines's stew rose out of its bowl in a brown, slow-motion bloom. The basil lifted its leaves as if greeting something. His own body strained upward against the harness, weightless for a heartbeat that stretched like taffy—

—and then it came back, all at once, and the stew came down, and the ship lurched as her drive controllers hiccuped against the sudden restoration of inertial reference, and alarms began screaming in three different voices.

"Easy," he told the ship, or himself, or possibly the basil. "Easy, easy."

Nine seconds. He would count them later, from the logs, over and over, until the number wore a groove in him: nine point two seconds of system-wide gravimetric anomaly, propagating outward from some source the nav computer could not resolve, touching every ship, every station, every drifting hulk in a sphere forty light-seconds wide. Nine seconds in which the invisible hand holding everything down had simply... paused. As if the universe were a machine and someone upstairs had lifted their finger off the switch to check something.

Ines killed the alarms one by one, ran diagnostics with hands that were almost steady, and brought the *Lament* back onto her lane. The damage was trivial — a sheared coolant coupling, a drive governor that needed recalibration, the stew irretrievable. But he sat in the aftermath for a long time, unmoving, listening to the tick of cooling metal, feeling the shape of those nine seconds settle into his chest like swallowed ice.

Because he had felt this before.

Not this exact event — he wasn't a fool, whatever else he was. But the *signature*. The way the plating hadn't simply died but had flickered, oscillated, as though the field were being interrogated rather than interrupted. As though something enormous had reached into the local spacetime metric and turned it over, looking for markings on the underside.

Five years ago, in another life, he had been Jude Kavanagh's opposite: an insurance investigator for Meridian Assurance, good at his work, meticulous, promoted twice. And he had sat in a windowless office on Vesta Station with a data slate full of telemetry from a research platform called Loom Station, reading gravimetric interference patterns that looked exactly like this — a nine-second oscillation, a fingerprint pressed into physics itself — and he had closed the file, and he had written the report he was told to write, and he had gone home and drunk the brandy and begun the long business of forgetting.

Except you don't forget a thing like that. You just bury it shallow enough to hear it breathing.

"No," he said aloud, to the empty cockpit, and the word came out smaller than he intended.

The basil trembled in the recycled air. Outside the viewport, the Belt's outer shoals glittered — a million tons of rock and frozen volatiles, indifferent, ancient. Somewhere out there, forty light-seconds away or four hundred, whatever had caused the flicker was still doing whatever it did, and every instinct Ines had spent five years sharpening told him the same thing:

Someone is looking for something. And things that get looked for get found.

* * *

He docked at Ceres Gate thirty-one hours later, ahead of schedule, because schedules were camouflage and he clung to them now like a drowning man clings to driftwood. The foundry platforms hung off the little moon's axis in their smoky constellation, torch-lit and thunderous, swallowing his ore slugs one barge-load at a time. Ines signed the manifests, collected payment into three shell accounts routed through two dead corporations, and bought replacement parts from a supplier who asked no questions because Ines overpaid by exactly the amount that purchased silence without inviting curiosity.

That was the arithmetic of invisibility. Too clean and you stood out; too sloppy and you left tracks. You had to be imperfect in unremarkable ways.

He was in the chandlery, haggling gently over a coolant coupling, when the message arrived.

He felt it before he saw it — a prickle at the base of the skull, the old investigator's reflex, the sense of a door opening somewhere behind him. His wrist terminal chimed once, politely. Sender ID: MERIDIAN ASSURANCE, CLAIMS DIVISION, AUTOMATED DISPATCH.

Automated. Fine. Automated was fine. Automated meant a form letter, a premium notice, a customer satisfaction survey.

He waited until he was back aboard, hatch sealed, ship pressurized, before he opened it. Some habits are coffins you build for yourself and climb into willingly.

**RE: CASE 77-KESTREL — STATUS UPDATE**

**This case, closed five (5) years prior, has been REOPENED pursuant to anomalous-event correlation protocols. A gravimetric signature consistent with incident 77-Kestrel primary evidence was detected system-wide at [timestamp]. All parties of record are hereby notified of renewed investigative interest.**

**Parties of record include:**

**— Meridian Assurance, Claims Division**

**— Loom Research Consortium (dissolved)**

**— THORNbury, INES — investigating officer of record**

The name sat there on the screen, spelled wrong in the way databases spell names wrong, and it might as well have been written in blood across his own viewport. Investigating officer of record. Not witness. Not survivor. *Officer.* The man who had signed the report. The man who had written, in careful institutional language, that the accident aboard Loom Station had been caused by a drive-plasma containment failure, that there were no anomalies, that the deceased — THORNbury, ADA, age 31, materials engineer — had died instantly and painlessly, and that the case required no further inquiry.

All of it true except the part that mattered.

Ines set the terminal down on the galley table with exaggerated care, the way you set down something that might detonate. Then he picked it up again and read the message four more times, hunting for loopholes the way he used to hunt for them in other people's claims.

Anomalous-event correlation protocols. He remembered them vaguely from the far side of the desk — a regulatory mechanism, rarely triggered, that forced insurers to reopen cases when new events matched archived signatures. Someone, somewhere, had taken the telemetry from the flicker and run it against the deep archives, and Case 77-Kestrel had lit up like a struck bell. Which meant the flicker and Ada's death shared a fingerprint. Which meant the impossible thing he had buried — the thing he had been paid, subtly, unmistakably, to bury — was real, and moving, and awake.

And they knew his name.

They had always known his name; it was in the file. But files slept. Files were paper graves. This one had just opened its eyes, and somewhere in the warm offices of Meridian Assurance, some junior adjuster was pulling the personnel records attached to Case 77-Kestrel, and finding that the investigating officer of record had resigned abruptly five years ago, and that Ines Thornbury had subsequently vanished into the contested orbits like a stone dropped into deep water.

Hiding, he understood with a clarity that arrived like cold water in the lungs, had made him *findable*. Every routine, every pattern, every careful boring year — it was all a trail now, a dotted line leading straight from a reopened file to a nickel hauler docked at Ceres Gate.

He looked at the basil. It looked back, green and oblivious.

"Okay," he said quietly. "Okay."

He did not sleep that night. He sat in the cockpit with the brandy untouched and plotted, on the nav display, the coordinates of a place that appeared on no chart: the deep dark beyond the Halo Relay, where the old edge-of-system transmitter had gone silent years ago, where dead space began and charts ended. Where Loom Station's last known telemetry pointed, like a finger bone pointing at the moon.

Whatever was breathing out there had his sister's fingerprints on it. And if he didn't reach it first, someone else would read the file, follow the trail, and find him — and worse, find *her*, whatever was left of her, alone in the dark with strangers' hands reaching in.

At 0400 ship time, Ines Thornbury opened the contract boards for the first time in five years and began looking for one last job — one legitimate, boring, traceable job — that would pay for the burn out to the edge of everything.

The first reply came within the hour. It was not from a cargo broker.

It was flagged: **PRIORITY — MERIDIAN ASSURANCE, CLAIMS DIVISION.**

Subject line: **CASE 77-KESTREL — REQUEST FOR VOLUNTARY STATEMENT.**

Ines stared at it for a long time, and outside the viewport, the Belt turned slowly, patient as a grave.
