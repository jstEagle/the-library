## 4. What the Salvage Crew Knows

The waystation hung in the amber light of a gas giant's limb like a rusted tooth in a jaw of cloud. *Halcyon Rest*, the old signage read, though half the letters had died and what remained was HAL—ON R ST, which Ines thought was more honest than the original name had ever been.

He docked on the shadow side, as always, paying the berth fee in unmarked scrip through a broker who didn't look at faces. The station smelled the way all old stations smelled: recycled air scrubbed to a chemical neutrality that never quite hid the undertones of coolant, fried protein, and human sweat. Three thousand people lived here in the gaps between jurisdictions, and every one of them wanted something.

Ines wanted information, and he knew exactly where to buy it.

* * *

Maren's cantina occupied a blister of observation glass on the station's spin axis, where the gravity was soft and the drinks were expensive and the silence could be purchased by the hour. Maren herself was a broad woman with a face like weathered teak and eyes that had catalogued every kind of desperation the outer system produced. She poured Ines's drink without asking what he wanted—kaff, black, cut with something that burned going down—and set it on the scarred table between them.

"You look like a man who's been reading his own obituary," she said.

"Something like that."

"Meridian's got feelers out. Deep ones." She said it casually, wiping down the table that didn't need wiping, her voice pitched under the ambient clatter. "Not just the usual skip-trace chatter. They're pulling archived case files. Old ones. And they're not doing it through normal channels—they're using some kind of automated sweep. Pattern-matching."

Ines's hand was steady around the cup. He'd had five years of practice at steadiness. "Matching for what?"

"Anomalies. Gravitational signatures." Maren's eyes flicked up to his, held there a beat too long. "You'd know something about that, wouldn't you, Thornbury? Nine seconds last week. Whole system felt it. My grandmother used to say when the dead walk, the ground shivers."

"Your grandmother sounds like she watched too much broadcast drama."

"She also said never trust a man who pays cash and asks no questions." Maren leaned closer. "There's a salvage crew docked at Berth 12. Corvo Dane's outfit. They came in three days ago running dark, bought decryption time from one of my back-room boys, and paid triple for discretion. Triple buys a lot of silence, but you've been good to me, so I'll tell you what my boy told me: they're working fragments of an insurance case file. Case number 77-Kestrel."

The name landed in Ines's chest like a dropped wrench. He kept his face still because stillness was the only skill he'd ever truly mastered.

"Kestrel," he repeated. "Don't know it."

"Course you don't." Maren straightened, resumed her wiping. "Dane's crew lifts tomorrow, early. Heading spinward. Toward the edge relays." She paused at the end of the table. "Whatever you did or didn't do, Ines—the past doesn't stay buried out here. It just gets sold to whoever digs deepest."

* * *

He walked back to the ship the long way, through the market ring where vendors sold oxygen candles and counterfeit transit passes and small mechanical birds that sang when you wound them. He bought nothing. His mind was a corridor of closed doors, and behind each one something was scratching.

Corvo Dane. He knew the name the way you know the name of a storm season—peripherally, with respect, with dread. Dane ran salvage the way pirates wished they could: legal enough to keep warrants off him, ruthless enough to leave competitors wondering why their finds kept coming up empty. And now he had pieces of 77-Kestrel. Pieces of Ada.

How? The file should have been ash. Ines had spent a week after the accident making sure of it—scrubbing copies, buying silences, burying the truth under a falsified report so clean that even Meridian's own auditors had signed off on it. Five years of quiet. Five years of careful orbits and unremarkable cargo manifests and a face that never appeared twice in the same port.

And yet here was Dane, holding fragments, heading toward the edge relays—which meant Dane knew something about where the case led. Something Ines himself had never known, because he had made very sure not to look too closely at what killed his sister.

Hiding, he realized, standing in the press of the market crowd while mechanical birds sang their tin songs, had made him predictable. A man with no history draws attention eventually. A man who flinches at a specific case number draws more. Meridian hadn't found him despite his invisibility. They'd found him because of its shape.

* * *

Priya was waiting in the cargo bay, cross-legged on a crate of legitimate ore samples, her engineer's slate glowing against her dark skin. She looked up when the airlock cycled, and there was something in her expression that made his stomach drop before she said a word.

"You went to see Maren," she said. "I followed your transponder ping to the station spine. Don't glare—I'm a stowaway turned crew, remember? Crew gets to worry."

"What do you want, Priya?"

"To show you this." She turned the slate toward him. On it: a fragment of data, dense with headers and checksums, partially decoded. "I lifted it off the station's relay buffers. Corvo Dane's comms officer is sloppy—or arrogant—and their burst transmissions to a local broker bounced through a node I know how to read. This is part of what they bought decryption time for."

Ines set down his cup. "That's—"

"77-Kestrel. Yeah. I figured you'd recognize the prefix, since you went white as vacuum when Maren mentioned it." Her voice wasn't cruel, but it wasn't gentle either. Priya Okonkwo did not waste gentleness on things that needed doing. "I've been decoding it since you left. Most of it's standard accident telemetry—hull stress readings, comm logs, the usual grief in numbers. But there's a section that doesn't fit any insurance format I've ever seen. Encrypted with something old. Pre-collapse corporate cipher."

She swiped, and the fragment resolved into readable text.

Ines read it once. Then again. Then a third time, because the words refused to arrange themselves into meaning, the way a familiar room refuses to be familiar when you wake up in it wrong.

**PROJECT LOOM — CONTINUITY SUBSTRATE — PHASE III TRIAL**
*Subject integration proceeding within tolerance. Memory lattice stable at 94.7%. Recommend extension of trial parameters to include [REDACTED] class events. Note: subject-adjacent personnel (kin) flagged for post-event interview protocol.*

"Subject integration," Ines said. His voice sounded like someone else's. "Memory lattice."

"It's a mind-archive project," Priya said quietly. "Experimental. They were uploading someone. Storing them. And the accident—" she tapped the fragment, "—the accident happened during a Phase III trial. Your sister's death wasn't just an accident, Ines. It was a test condition."

The cargo bay hummed around them, life support breathing its patient mechanical breath. Ines stood very still, the way he'd stood in investigation offices a lifetime ago, letting facts settle over him like snowfall.

Ada. Funny, fierce Ada, who'd taken the hauler contract because it paid double and sent him half her earnings with notes that said *buy something impractical for once*. Ada, whose death he had reported as reactor cascade failure—a clean lie, professionally constructed, because the truth had been a tangle of corporate seals and sealed testimony he couldn't afford to pull. Because pulling that thread would have exposed the falsification. Because grief is heavy, and lies are how we carry it.

They had put her in a machine. Or she had gone into it willingly—there was no way to know from a fragment—and the machine had failed, or been tested to failure, and nine seconds of impossible gravity had been the sound of her dying. The same nine seconds that had shivered through the whole system last week.

"The flicker," he said. "It matched the accident signature. That's why Meridian reopened the case."

"And Dane knows it too," Priya said. "Which means somewhere out there, at the edge relays, there's something left of Project Loom. A facility. An archive. Maybe—" she hesitated, and he watched her decide whether to say the merciful thing or the true thing, and saw her choose the true thing, because that was who she was, "—maybe there's something left of *her*. If the memory lattice survived."

Ines turned away. He walked to the viewport, where the amber giant filled half the sky, banded and beautiful and utterly indifferent, and he thought about his sister's voice, which he had not let himself remember properly in five years because remembering was a door you couldn't close.

If she was in there—if some pattern of her, some lattice of her laughter and her terrible singing and her habit of calling him *little brother* when he was taller than her—if that was sitting in a derelict at the edge of everything, then Corvo Dane was racing to sell it, and Meridian was racing to bury it, and Ines Thornbury, five years a ghost, was the only person alive who might want it simply *kept*.

"We lift tonight," he said. "Before Dane."

"Dane lifts at dawn. You'll be running dark through the shoals to beat him—that's suicide in anything bigger than a courier." Priya was already moving, though, already pulling up course plots, because she had decided somewhere in the last hour that she was coming, and Priya's decisions had the finality of welded seams. "Good thing you've got an engineer who can squeeze a hauler through a keyhole."

"Why?" he asked her. "Why help me?"

She looked at him across the cargo bay, ore dust and starlight and five years of his carefully maintained nothing, and she said: "Because they experimented on a living woman and called it a trial, and everyone who knew stayed quiet. Including you. I want the whole record, Ines. All of it. Whatever it costs."

He didn't answer. There was no honest answer that wasn't a confession, and confessions could wait until dead space.

* * *

They lifted at 0300 station time, running dark, the hauler's engines throttled to a whisper that wouldn't carry past the gas giant's magnetosphere. Ines took the first watch alone in the cockpit, the amber giant shrinking behind them, the long dark ahead.

On the console, Priya had left the decoded fragment open. He read it again, the clinical corporate phrasing, *subject-adjacent personnel (kin)*. Kin. Him. They had planned to interview him afterward, and instead he had handed them a lie so clean it had ended the inquiry, and the inquiry had ended, and Ada had ended, and the machine that ate her had gone silent for five years.

Until nine seconds ago.

Ines keyed the nav plot and looked at the route Priya had threaded through the shoals—a needle's path of gravitational shear and debris, the kind of crossing that killed careless ships and rewarded exactly one kind of pilot: the kind with nothing left to lose and a deadline in front of him.

Behind him, in the dark of the corridor, he heard Priya moving through the ship, learning its bones, claiming her territory. Ahead, spinward, at the edge of everything, Corvo Dane slept aboard a faster ship with fragments of his sister in his hold.

Ines engaged the first burn and felt the deck press gently against his boots, gravity doing its honest work, and he thought: *I buried you, Ada. I'm sorry. I'm coming to dig.*
