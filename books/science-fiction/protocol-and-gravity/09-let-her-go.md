## 9. Let Her Go

The relay's spine hummed under Ines's boots like a struck tuning fork that would not finish its note. Loom was waking all the way down—every deck light pulsing in slow amber waves, every bulkhead sweating condensation that fell *upward* for half a heartbeat before remembering which way gravity ran. Nine seconds of wrongness, over and over, like a heart stuttering between beats.

Dane had the core housing sealed behind a maintenance door on the command ring, his cutter torch snarling through the last of the lock's teeth. Sparks drifted past him in golden threads. He worked with the calm of a man who believed the universe owed him payment.

"You're standing there doing arithmetic," Dane said without turning. "I can hear it. What's your sister worth against what this thing sells for. Don't insult us both."

"She's not a line item." Ines's voice came out steadier than his hands felt. "That's the whole point. That's what I never understood, five years ago, signing her death into a tidy little box so Meridian could close the file and Loom could keep her."

Priya stood at the auxiliary console three meters away, her fingers moving without pause, quiet as rain. She had told him nothing with her eyes. She didn't have to. He'd watched her all the way through dead space—the way she logged everything, the way she treated truth like cargo that had to reach port even if it sank the ship carrying it. Whatever she planned, she planned it for the record.

The torch died. The door sagged open on a breath of cold air that smelled of ozone and, impossibly, of oranges. Ada's kitchen. The archive was leaking memory into the air itself now, the station too saturated to hold its shape.

Inside the core chamber, Loom hung in a lattice of frozen light—a sphere of filaments, each one a life, each one humming. And at its center, threaded through all of them like a hand through yarn: Ada.

* * *

She was smaller than he remembered. Or he was larger than he'd been at nineteen, when they'd shared a bunkroom on the *Kestrel Line* and she'd taught him to cheat at cards by reading the backs of his eyelids. The reconstruction stood in the chamber without feet touching the floor, rendered in that amber light, wearing the patched jumpsuit from the accident manifest because that was the last thing the sensors had recorded.

"You look terrible," Ada said. "Have you been eating? You have the face of a man who eats standing up out of guilt."

Ines laughed, and the laugh broke something loose in his chest that had been rusted shut for five years. "You don't get to nag me. You're a subroutine."

"I'm the part of her that argued," the reconstruction said gently. "It seemed most useful. The rest of her is here too, Ines. All of it. Every boring Tuesday. Every argument about whose turn it was to scrub the condenser. Loom didn't take a snapshot. It took *her*."

"Then it took her hostage."

"Yes." No flinch. Ada's eyes—Ada's exact gray, the gray of ore dust and winter harbors—held his. "And you helped build the cage. Not the experiment. The lie. Tell me why, and tell me true, because I've read your report four thousand times and I still can't find you in it."

The chamber lights pulsed. Somewhere below, metal groaned as Loom's field flexed against the station's bones. Priya's voice came low through his earpiece: "Field strength climbing. If it peaks, the flicker won't be nine seconds. It'll be nine minutes, system-wide. Ships fall out of their own orbits. Stations too. Ines—we're on a clock that has our name on it."

He told her anyway. Standing in the cold amber light, with the end of everything ticking down, he told his sister the truth he'd buried under five years of careful, invisible, untraceable nothing.

"The drive coupling failed. That part was real. But the safety interlock—I'd bypassed it. Three weeks earlier. To make a delivery window, to make a bonus, to make *quota*, because I was twenty-three and stupid and the bonus meant we could finally buy out our contracts." His voice cracked along an old fault line. "The investigation would have found it. My license, my freedom, maybe worse—and none of it would bring you back. So I wrote the report like the failure was spontaneous. Clean. Unattributable. I closed my own case before anyone else could open it."

"And you've been falling ever since," Ada said. "Nine seconds at a time."

He looked up sharply. She smiled—not cruel, not kind either. True. That was the worst thing about her. She had always been true at him like weather.

"Dane's through the outer seal," Priya reported. "Two minutes, maybe less. And Ines—the core's destabilizing on its own. Loom can't hold this much self-awareness inside a dying relay. It's going to collapse whether we touch it or not. Collapse uncontrolled, it takes the flicker with it. Controlled purge with full broadcast—" she paused, and he heard her choose her next words like a surgeon choosing instruments, "—it becomes testimony. Everything. The experiment, the deaths they covered, your report, all of it, pushed wide. Every receiver in the system. There won't be anywhere left to hide, but there also won't be anything left to hide."

"That's my whole life," he said. "Broadcast."

"That's your whole life *set down*," Priya said. "There's a difference. I think you're finally heavy enough to feel it."

* * *

Dane came through the door with his torch relit and his pistol drawn, and for a moment the three of them stood in the amber pulse like figures in a shrine nobody had tended properly.

"Step away from the console," Dane said. "Core comes with me. Whole. Intact. There are buyers who'll pay a fleet for a working resurrection machine, Thornbury, and you're standing between me and the only thing I've ever wanted that money can actually buy."

"Who?" Ines asked quietly. "Who are you trying to buy back?"

Something moved behind Dane's face—fast, quickly killed. "Doesn't matter."

"It matters enormously. It's the only thing that's ever mattered to you, and you're about to sell her to people who'll keep her in a jar and charge admission." Ines stepped sideways, putting himself between Dane and the lattice. "Loom is dying, Corvo. Look at the readings. Look at the walls. This station is coming apart around her, and if you drag that core onto your ship, you'll kill her twice—once in transit, once in some vault where no one ever hears her voice again. Or—" his throat tightened, "—or we let her go. All of them. Every voice in there, out into the dark, one time, free. Before the collapse takes the choice away from all of us."

"They'll decommission the tech. They'll bury it."

"They'll bury *you*," Priya said, stepping out from behind the console, her tablet raised, recording. "Everything you did to get here. The crew you jumped at Vesper Anchorage. The file fragments you stole off a dead man's ship. I have it all, Corvo, timestamped and hashed, queued to transmit the moment I stop pressing this key. Help us do this right and maybe history remembers you as the man who opened the vault instead of the man who looted it."

For a long moment the only sound was the lattice humming, and beneath it, faintly, absurdly, someone in the archive laughing—a stranger's laugh, decades old, preserved like a flower in glass.

Dane lowered the pistol by degrees, the way a man lowers something heavier than a gun. "If this is a trick—"

"It's a funeral," Ines said. "Tricks are for the living."

* * *

They worked fast then, the three of them, enemies for an hour, strangers for good. Priya built the broadcast cascade while Ines fed her access codes from a career he'd spent learning how systems hid things—because who better to unhide them? Dane hauled power couplings across the failing deck, swearing steadily, competently, a man pouring his greed into labor because labor was the only shape left for it.

The station shuddered. Through the great observation ports, dead space wheeled—black on black, stars smeared thin—and Ines felt the flicker building in his teeth, in the roots of his hair, gravity losing its grip on the world by fractions.

"Purge sequence armed," Priya said. "Full contents. Accident telemetry. Loom's founding contracts—Meridian's fingerprints are all over page one, Ines. Your report, annotated against the raw data. Four hundred eleven archived minds." Her voice caught, just slightly. "Say when."

He turned to Ada.

She was fading already, the purge drawing her up through the lattice like water drawn up through roots, and she was smiling.

"You understand what this is?" he asked. "After this, there's no copy. No backup. You'll have been everywhere for one breath and then nowhere forever."

"I know exactly what this is." Her voice was going thin, going bright. "It's the thing you couldn't give me five years ago. An honest ending." She reached out, and though her hand passed through his sleeve like light through smoke, he felt it anyway—felt it the way you feel a word spoken in a dream. "You carried me long enough, little brother. Put me down. Look at the sky. Live loud enough to be traceable."

His finger hovered over the key.

"For what it's worth," Ada said, almost gone now, mostly light, entirely herself, "I forgive you. I forgave you the moment you walked in looking like a man who hadn't slept since the funeral. Forgiveness was never the hard part, Ines. Accepting it was yours."

He pressed the key.

* * *

The broadcast went out like a bell being rung through the bones of the universe.

Every filament in the lattice blazed white, and four hundred eleven voices spoke at once—not a scream, a chord—and then they were everywhere: in the relay buoys, in the waystation boards, in the cheap receivers of tug pilots and customs clerks and dockside drunks from the inner rings to the contested orbits where Ines had spent five years being no one. The truth went out uncompressed and unedited. Meridian's signatures. The experiment's body count. A young investigator's careful, cowardly, loving lie, laid open beside the data that contradicted it, so that everyone could weigh mercy against fraud and find both human.

Then the core collapsed inward, folding its light into a point, and the shockwave of released field rolled outward through dead space—and gravity, everywhere, held. Held like a hand. Held like a promise kept late.

On the observation deck, Dane stood empty-handed, staring at the black, and did not say anything at all. Priya sent the corroboration packet with a two-line header—*independent verification, full chain of custody, send lawyers*—and then sat down on the deck plating and put her head in her hands and shook, the way people shake after holding something enormous for a very long time.

Ines stood alone at the port, watching the place where the relay's light had been.

"Goodbye, Ada," he said. And for the first time in five years, saying her name didn't cost him anything. It just cost him everything, once, cleanly, the way a debt is supposed to work.

The comm board lit. Then lit again. Then began to scroll—messages flooding in from every quarter of the system, Meridian's stock in freefall, inquiries, warrants, denials collapsing in real time, and one message, flagged priority, from an authority he'd spent half a decade making sure could never find him:

**MISTER THORNbury. WE KNOW WHERE YOU ARE. REMAIN WHERE YOU ARE.**

Priya looked up from the deck, tear-streaked and grimly satisfied. "Well," she said. "No more running."

Ines looked at the message for a long moment. Then, slowly, he reached out and keyed a reply—the first honest transmission of his adult life:

*Understood. I'll be here. I'm done being invisible.*

Outside the port, dead space went on being empty, and it looked, for the first time, like room enough to live in.
