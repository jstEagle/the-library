## 5. What the Original Knew

The private logs were encrypted with a cipher the original had invented himself, which told me two things before I'd cracked a single byte: he was paranoid, and he was vain.

Paranoid men use standard encryption. Vain men write their own.

It took me eleven hours to break it, and most of that was spent admiring his arrogance. He'd built the key out of his own case number — the murder file I'd been decanted to inherit — layered through a hash of his own genome. The message was unmistakable: *only I can read this.* Which raised the obvious question of why he'd bothered encrypting anything at all if he intended to be dead by the time anyone found it, but grief and guilt don't run logic checks. I know this now in a way I didn't when I woke up on the transport with someone else's memories sloshing around my skull like bilge water.

I sat in the archive room with the colony's night cycle humming through the walls, a cup of reconstituted tea going cold beside me, and I opened the first log dated four days after his arrival at Meridian Hollow. Twelve years ago. Before Sefa Okafor died. Before the vault door sealed itself behind whatever happened in those underlevels.

The timestamp read: *Day 4. Personal. Do not sync.*

And then the original August Thackeray said, in his own flat dictation voice — a voice I recognized because it was, down to the slight rasp on the sibilants, my voice:

*"They lied to me. Not the colony. Central. The briefing said dissident holdouts, ideological residue, a settlement of people who refused Consolidation on principle and were left to rot as an example. That's the official line and I recorded it verbatim in my field notes like a good little instrument."*

He paused there. I could hear him breathing, deciding whether to continue.

*"The founders of Meridian Hollow weren't dissidents. They were cargo."*

* * *

I want to be precise about what I felt when I heard that word, because precision is the only inheritance I trust. It wasn't horror. Not yet. Horror requires understanding, and understanding arrived later, in installments, each one worse than the last. What I felt first was something smaller and more professional: the click of a case changing shape under your hands. Every investigator knows it. The moment the geometry stops matching the story you were handed.

The original kept talking.

*"There were eleven transports out of the Ceres arcology during the Purge years. Official record says seven. The other four never existed. No manifests, no destinations, no passengers. I've been here four days and I've counted the founding families of this colony against the demographic drift models and the numbers only work one way: these people are from the ships that never existed. Three hundred souls minimum, unregistered, unrecorded, erased so thoroughly that even the erasure was classified."*

Three hundred.

The number landed in me like a coin dropped down a dry well. I heard it fall for a long time before it hit bottom. Three hundred was the capacity figure stamped on the stasis pods Imani Okafor and I had found beneath the reactor — the second vault, the one that wasn't in any schematic, the one whose existence the original's case file referenced in a single redacted line. I'd stood in that frozen hall two nights ago looking at faces behind frost, men and women in Pre-Consolidation dress, and I'd thought: *refugees, probably. Fugitives, maybe.*

I hadn't thought: *evidence.* I hadn't understood that they weren't hiding from history. They *were* the history — the part someone had paid my original to make sure stayed buried.

*"Central didn't lose track of them,"* the log continued. *"You can't lose track of four ships. They tracked them all the way down. And then they sent me."*

He stopped the recording there. The next entry was six days later, and when his voice came back it had changed — tighter, quieter, the way a man sounds when he's started sleeping badly.

* * *

*Day 10. Personal. Do not sync.*

*"Her name is Sefa Okafor. She's the reactor engineer's wife, though that's the least of what she is. She came to my quarters tonight — walked past my door security like it was decorative — and she asked me one question: 'Did they send you to finish it, or to watch it finish?'*

*I told her I didn't know what she meant. She looked at me the way you look at a machine that's lying about being a machine, and she said: 'The vault. Under the reactor. Did they send you to open it or to seal it?'*

"I said there is no vault in the colony records. She smiled. I am writing this down because the smile frightened me more than the question. She said, 'Of course there isn't. We built it that way.'"*

I paused the playback. My tea had gone fully cold, which felt appropriate. In the dark of the archive room, the terminal's glow made my hands look like someone else's hands, which, in fairness, has been true since the day I was printed.

Sefa Okafor. The victim. The woman whose unsolved death was the entire reason I existed — the reason a laboratory somewhere had grown a copy of a tired investigator and shipped him to a dying colony with a case file and a mandate. For twelve years her file had been the spine of everything I knew about this place. Widow of the reactor chief. Mother of one daughter. Keeper of maintenance schedules nobody read. A colonist who died in the underlevels near a door that shouldn't exist, in an accident the original had never managed to close.

And now here she was in the original's private logs, twelve years before her death, already knowing things about the vault that the vault's own blueprints denied. Walking through security. Asking questions with knives in them.

She hadn't been a bystander to the mystery. She'd been its custodian.

* * *

The logs ran long that night, and I let them. Sleep is a resource and I have always been stingy with it; the original apparently shared the habit, because his entries got longer as the weeks turned over, dictated in the small hours in a voice growing steadily less certain of itself.

*Day 31. Personal. Do not sync.*

*"I reported to Central today. Standard check-in, encrypted channel, the usual theater. And then, off-script, I asked about the fourth transport. Just one question, casual, the kind of thing a bored investigator asks to fill silence.*

"The channel went quiet for nine seconds. Nine seconds is a long time on a lightspeed lag. Then a voice I didn't recognize — not my handler, someone above my handler — said: 'Investigator Thackeray, your mandate is the homicide. Your mandate is not provenance.' And then they closed the channel from their end, which they have never done, because closing channels leaves a flag in the routing table and careful people don't leave flags.*

"They're not careless people. So the flag was a message. The message was: stop digging, or become a hole yourself."*

I listened to that twice. There's a particular species of dread that comes from hearing your own voice describe being afraid, and I'll admit I sat with it a while. This was the man whose memories I carried — not all of them, the lab had stripped the personal archive down to professional competence and procedural instinct, thank whatever gods oversee cloning ethics — but enough of him lived in my reflexes that his fear found familiar grooves to run along. I knew exactly how his jaw would have tightened. Because it was, in every way that mattered mechanically, my jaw.

*"Here's what I understand now,"* he went on, later in the same entry. *"The Consolidation didn't just purge the Pre-Consolidation loyalists. It purged the memory of them. Whole communities rewritten as 'ideological residue.' Ships deleted from registries. Families issued new histories or no histories. And Meridian Hollow — Meridian Hollow was supposed to be the proof of concept. Take three hundred people from the ships that never existed, settle them somewhere marginal, let them build a life, and then wait a generation until nobody alive remembers they were ever anyone else. Assimilation by attrition. The colony doesn't know what it is because it was designed not to know.*

"But some of them knew. The founders' inner circle. They built the second vault and filled it with sleepers — the elders, the archivists, the ones who still spoke the old tongue — and they hid it under their own reactor, under their own children's feet, and they waited for the world to forget hard enough that the vault would be safe forever.*

"And then Central noticed the demographic anomaly anyway, and sent me. Not to investigate a murder. The murder — Okafor's death, whenever it comes, and God help me I think they intend it to come — the murder is cover. A homicide investigation is the perfect instrument for burying a secret. You dig everywhere except where the truth is, loudly, for years, and everyone watches you dig."*

He stopped. When his voice resumed, it was barely above a whisper, and I leaned toward the speaker like a man leaning into wind.

*"I have to decide what I am. That's the whole of it. I'm either the shovel or the ground it's dug into, and I have maybe a week to choose."*

* * *

I shut the playback off and sat in the dark for a long time.

Somewhere above me, the colony slept its rationed sleep. Imani Okafor was presumably awake — she was always awake, the reactor owned her hours the way it had owned her mother's — running her failing core through another night of diminishing returns. Three hundred sleepers lay in their frost below her feet, and she didn't know they were there. Hadn't known, when we found the vault together, what they meant. Neither had I, not really. I'd filed them as *motive, redacted* and moved on, because moving on is what I was printed for.

Now the redaction had a face, and the face was mine.

That's the sentence I kept circling, the one I couldn't land. *The original was sent to bury them.* Not to find them. Not to protect them. Bury. Erase the ships, erase the founders' true history, erase three hundred sleeping people so thoroughly that even their descendants would inherit nothing but dust and a grudge against a dying reactor. And when a woman named Sefa Okafor — keeper of the vault, guardian of the erased — stood between him and that mandate, the investigation stalled at a locked door and she ended up dead in the underlevels, and the case never closed, and a clone was grown to keep the not-closing going forever.

My bloodline wasn't blood. It was worse. It was *assignment.* Somewhere in a Central archive there was a personnel decision that had produced me, and that decision had been made by the same machinery that deleted four ships from history, and I had woken up inside it calling it a career.

I thought about how the colonists looked at me. The flinch when I passed. The word they used when they thought I couldn't hear — *the copy,* spat like a bad ration. I'd catalogued it in week one as resentment of outside authority, noted it, moved on. But it wasn't that, was it. Or it wasn't only that. Some of them — the old ones, the ones whose grandparents might have been in the inner circle — had looked at my face and seen the man who came to bury them. Seen the shovel.

Loneliness disguised as procedure, I'd written in my early logs. I'd had it backwards. It was procedure disguised as loneliness, and underneath both, all along, had been a guilt I didn't yet own, accruing interest across a decade, waiting for the copy to arrive and collect it.

* * *

There were forty-one entries left. I played them all. I owed the dead that much, and I owed myself the truth, even if the truth was going to cost me the comfortable fiction that I'd inherited a mystery rather than a crime.

The middle entries charted the original's collapse in careful increments. Day 44: he falsified his first report to Central, describing the vault search as "inconclusive, probable structural void, no significance." Day 58: he met Sefa Okafor again, and this time the log recorded actual dialogue, actual negotiation — she'd tested him for weeks, and somewhere in the testing he'd crossed over. *I'm not the shovel,* he wrote. *I've decided. Whatever it costs.* Day 71: he began quietly diverting his investigator's stipend into the colony's infrastructure accounts, routed through three shells, funding repairs on systems he officially had no reason to care about. Reactor coolant lines. Vault-adjacent conduits. The handwriting of a guilty man laundering conscience into plumbing.

And then Day 89. The last full entry before the gap — the twelve-day hole in the record that ended with Sefa Okafor dead and the case file born.

*"Something's wrong at the vault. Sefa flagged a power draw anomaly three days ago and won't tell me what it is. She says if it's what she thinks it is, we have very little time, and she says it in the tone of a woman who has been right about everything so far. I'm going down tonight. If this entry is the last coherent thing I record, whoever's listening — and I suppose I mean me, the me that comes after, if there is one — know this: I did not do it. Whatever they say I did. Whatever the file says. I need you to know that I did not do it.*

"Or maybe I need you to doubt it. Maybe doubt is the only protection I can leave them. Think about that. Then go look at the door."*

The recording ended. After that: static, timestamps with no audio, and then the official case file opening like a wound, and a colony learning to hate the investigator who wouldn't solve the murder of the woman who'd trusted him.

I sat there until the night cycle bled into morning, doing the arithmetic of my inheritance. A guilty mandate. A buried people. A dead keeper who had known everything. An original who had chosen the ground over the shovel and then vanished into a gap in his own record, leaving behind a locked door, a redacted motive, and a clone.

*Go look at the door,* he'd said.

I was already reaching for my coat when the terminal chimed. A priority ping from the reactor level. Imani Okafor, three words, no greeting:

*"August. The signal changed."*

The distress call from the antenna array — the dead language, the fragment I'd translated as *we are still here* — had stopped repeating.

It had started counting.
