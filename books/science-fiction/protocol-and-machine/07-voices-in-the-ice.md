## 7. Voices in the Ice

There are three universal constants in the universe, according to the original's field notes: entropy, bureaucracy, and the fact that any secret worth keeping will eventually require someone to lie about it at two in the morning.

I was lying about it at 2:14 a.m., colony standard, which felt appropriately precise. The lie was small and procedural in shape — a maintenance log entry reading *cryopod diagnostic cycle, pod 114, no anomalies* — and like all good lies, it was technically true. I *was* running a diagnostic on pod 114. The anomaly was me.

The second vault sat beneath the reactor like a swallowed secret, three hundred sleepers in their frost-rimed pods, arranged in rows that reminded me of nothing so much as a library where every book was holding its breath. I'd been down here four nights running now, after Imani went off-shift, telling myself each time that this would be the night I just looked.

Tonight I stopped looking and started doing.

"Pod 114," I said to the empty room, because talking to yourself is either madness or method, and the original's notes suggested he'd never learned to tell the difference either. "Subject: female, estimated biological age sixty-eight at time of stasis. Name on manifest: Ash, Yewande. Doctor of something. The scan doesn't say what."

The scan didn't say a lot of things. That was the problem with Pre-Consolidation records — half of them were encoded in formats nobody had touched in two centuries, and the other half were written in that dead dialect with its soft consonants and its stubborn refusal to compress meaning into anything efficient. *We are still here.* Four words. Two hundred years old. Repeating across the dark on an antenna array scheduled for scrap.

I had translated the fragment in an afternoon. What I hadn't translated was why my hands shook while I did it.

Here is what I knew, catalogued in the clinical voice I used when the other voice got too loud:

One: the signal originated from inside this vault. Triangulation doesn't lie; people do, but math has no motive.

Two: the signal was still transmitting, which meant something down here was still powered, which meant the founders had built a second power system beneath the first one, hidden from the colonists above, hidden from the original August Thackeray when he walked these same corridors twelve years ago investigating a murder that stalled at a locked door I now stood on the far side of.

Three: I was about to wake up a stranger from a dead century without authorization, without a medical team, and without telling the one person on this colony who might actually help me, because Imani Okafor had looked at me yesterday across the reactor floor and said, "You have his face. You know that, right? Every time you think hard, you make his face."

I hadn't asked whose face she meant. We both knew.

* * *

Waking a sleeper from two-hundred-year stasis is not, as the entertainment feeds would have you believe, a matter of pressing a button and watching someone gasp dramatically into consciousness. It is a controlled metabolic unwinding, a slow negotiation between the machine and the meat. The pod's interface was archaic but intact — Pre-Consolidation engineering had a reputation for outlasting the civilizations that built it, which said something depressing about civilizations — and the thaw sequence wanted, of all things, a verbal confirmation phrase.

I tried Standard. Nothing. I tried the colony's creolized trade-tongue. Nothing. The pod sat there, patient as geology, waiting for a language that had been dead longer than the colony had existed.

Which was, I suppose, the point. A vault full of refugees hiding from a purge would want locks only they could open.

I pulled up the signal transcript again. *We are still here.* In the original dialect it was five syllables, a rising-falling cadence I'd listened to four hundred times, until it had worn a groove in me like water on stone. I played it back through the pod's own receiver, letting the vault hear its own voice.

The confirmation light turned amber. Then green.

"Well," I said, to the room, to the sleepers, to whatever version of myself was going to have to live with this. "That's either very good or very bad."

The thaw took six hours. I spent them badly — pacing, re-reading the redacted case file for the fortieth time, eating a nutrient bar I didn't taste. Sefa Okafor's name sat in the file like a splinter. Murdered twelve years ago. Investigation terminated at a sealed door. And now here I was, on the other side of that door, opening it one body at a time, and the file had nothing to say about it because someone had burned out the sections that mattered.

At hour six, Dr. Yewande Ash opened her eyes.

* * *

She did not gasp. She did not panic. She lay in the pod for a long moment, breathing in the shallow, deliberate rhythm of someone relearning that breathing was optional again, and then she turned her head and looked directly at me with eyes the color of dark honey and an expression I could only describe as *deeply unimpressed.*

"You're not one of ours," she said. In the dead language. Perfectly fluent, perfectly clear, the way a bell is clear.

"No," I answered, in the same tongue, my accent presumably mangled beyond redemption. "I'm — "

"You're a copy." She said it flatly, without malice, the way you'd note the weather. "Thackeray. There were posters of your face in the transit stations before the Purges reached the outer rings. *Ask August Thackeray. He'll find the truth.*" Her mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "Propaganda ages poorly."

"I'm not him. I'm — "

"A clone grown from his pattern, imprinted with his memories, sent to finish his work." She pushed herself upright, moving carefully, testing joints against two centuries of disuse. "Yes. I read the protocols too, before they froze us. Sit down, copy. My legs need a minute and you look like a man who hasn't slept in four days, which means you're about to be useful or collapse, and I'd rather plan for the former."

I sat. It seemed wise.

"How do you know what I am?" I asked.

"Because we built this place to survive people like him." She flexed her fingers, watched them obey. "And because the signal woke me on purpose. Pod 114 isn't random. It's mine. The system was told: if anyone ever translates the beacon, wake Yewande. Only someone who could translate it would come looking, and only someone worth talking to would bother learning a dead language first." She looked around the vault, at the rows of sleeping faces under their rime of frost, and something in her expression cracked — just slightly, just for a moment — before the clinical calm reassembled itself. "Three hundred and eleven of us came down. Three hundred are still here. I assume you can count."

"The eleventh," I said quietly. "Sefa Okafor."

Something moved behind her eyes. "So you know that name."

"She died twelve years ago. Above us. Her death is the reason I exist — the original was sent to investigate, and the case was never closed."

"And you've come to close it." She said it without inflection, which somehow made it worse. "A murder investigation. Twelve years late. Conducted by a photocopy." She swung her legs over the edge of the pod, wincing. "Copy, I am one hundred and nine years old, I have been frozen since before your colony poured its first foundation, and I have exactly one thing to say to your investigation, so listen carefully."

She leaned forward, gripped my wrist with a hand still cold from the ice, and her voice dropped into the register people use for the sentences they've rehearsed for decades.

"It wasn't a distress call."

* * *

I want to record, for the archive I may or may not live long enough to write, that in that moment I felt the specific vertigo of a man discovering the floor of his understanding was a painted backdrop. I had built four days of certainty on that signal. *We are still here.* A cry for help across the centuries. A mystery with a heartbeat in it. Something to translate, something to solve, something clean.

"There's a secondary layer," Dr. Ash continued, releasing my wrist. "Under the repeating phrase. Encoded in the carrier wave's phase modulation — you'd need native fluency to even suspect it was there, because it's hidden in how the syllables breathe. We designed it that way deliberately. A distress call invites rescue. Rescue invites attention. Attention, in the years we fled, meant purge ships." She tapped her temple. "But a countdown — a countdown only matters to someone already standing next to the thing counting down."

"Counting down to what?"

"Ninety days." She said it simply, terribly. "Give or take the drift. The stasis systems weren't built to last two centuries, copy. They were built to last fifty, with redundancy stretching to eighty if we were lucky. We gambled that someone would come back for us. Nobody came. The systems have been cannibalizing themselves for decades — rerouting power, degrading gracefully, buying time with decay. The beacon is the last subsystem with anything left to give, and it's been spending its final reserves to tell anyone listening: *we are still here, and here is ending.*"

"Ninety days," I repeated.

"Eighty-seven, by my estimate of the drift, but let's keep the round number for morale." She stood, slowly, one hand on the pod's rim, and surveyed her sleeping countrymen — three hundred faces under frost, three hundred heartbeats slowed to geological patience. "In ninety days, the stasis fields fail. Not gently. Cascade failure — every pod within minutes of every other pod. Three hundred people will die in the time it takes you to brew bad coffee, and they will not wake up first. They will simply stop."

The vault was silent except for the hum of dying machines pretending to be healthy.

"Why wake me and not the others?" I asked. "If the clock's running —"

"Because waking all of them requires power this vault no longer has, and the transfer protocols require a living administrator from outside. Me, I'm the interpreter. You—" she looked at me with those honey-dark eyes, and I had the uncomfortable sensation of being inventoried, "—you're the resource. Your colony has a reactor. Your colony has cryopods. Your colony, copy, has a choice to make, and I needed someone awake to help force it."

"The colony doesn't know you exist."

"No," she agreed. "And there's the difficulty. Because the last time your colony's founders interacted with our generation, the interaction involved soldiers." She paused. "And because your predecessor sealed this vault's existence behind a murder investigation and a wall of redactions, and I'd very much like to know which side of that seal he was standing on when he did it."

I thought of the case file. Of the locked door. Of Imani's voice: *you make his face.*

"So did I," I said.

Dr. Ash studied me for a long moment, and then, unexpectedly, she laughed — a dry, rusted sound, like a hinge remembering motion. "You really aren't him. He would have lied to me by now. Flattered me, managed me, filed me under assets." She extended her hand, formal, archaic. "Dr. Yewande Ash. Historian, linguist, and as of ninety days ago, apparently, alarm clock. And you are?"

I took her hand. It was still cold.

"August Thackeray," I said. "The second one. And I think, Doctor, you should know that eighty-seven days is not nearly enough time to convince three hundred frightened colonists to share their reactor with three hundred strangers from a war they've spent two generations trying to forget."

"No," she said pleasantly. "It isn't. Which is why we're going to start tonight."

She nodded past me, toward the vault's far wall — and I turned, and saw, for the first time, the thing I had missed in four nights of searching. A panel, faintly glowing, its surface crawling with the same dead script as the beacon. And beneath the script, in newer letters, scratched by hand into the metal with something sharp, in the colony's own trade-tongue:

*Sefa was here. Tell her daughter I kept the count.*

The handwriting was the original's.

* * *

Dr. Ash read the scratched message twice, her lips moving silently, translating even though the words were already in her language's youngest grandchild. When she finished, she was quiet for a long time.

"Okafor," she said finally. "That was the eleventh sleeper's family name. She refused the pod, you know. Insisted on staying awake, on being the keeper. Said someone had to count the days out loud." Her jaw tightened. "She walked out of this vault twelve years ago to check the relay junctions, and she never walked back in."

"No," I said. "She didn't."

"And your predecessor wrote this." She touched the scratched letters, lightly, the way you touch a wound to confirm it hurts. "*Tell her daughter I kept the count.* Kept what count, copy? Counted what?"

Above us, faintly, through meters of rock and steel, the reactor hummed its failing hum. Somewhere up there, Imani Okafor was asleep in a bed she paid for in reactor shifts, dreaming, maybe, of a mother she'd lost to a locked door and a case file full of holes.

"I don't know yet," I said. "But I think I know who to ask."

Dr. Ash raised an eyebrow — a gesture that had clearly survived two centuries unchanged, because some things are load-bearing.

"Then we'd better hurry," she said. "Eighty-six days. And copy — the next time you decide to wake a stranger in the middle of the night, bring coffee. Some things survived the Purges, and bad coffee is one of them."

I was already climbing the ladder toward the reactor level, toward the shift logs, toward Imani, toward the conversation I had been dreading since the day I was decanted — and behind me, in the cold and humming dark, three hundred sleepers breathed on, ninety days from silence, waiting for a colony that didn't know they existed to decide they mattered.

The signal pulsed once more through the antenna array overhead, spending its last watts like a dying man spending his last coin:

*We are still here.*

Not for long, I thought. Not unless I move fast, lie better, and find out what the original August Thackeray was counting — before the count runs out.
