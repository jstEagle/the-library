## 14. The Vote

The colony hall had never held this many people. I'd checked the specs during my first week—Meridian Hollow's assembly space was rated for two hundred and forty occupants, a number the original's files described with the dry optimism of someone who'd never seen it full. Tonight there were three hundred and eleven bodies in it, counting Dr. Ash, who sat wrapped in two blankets near the front like a small, ancient bird that had outlived every other kind of weather.

They'd set up the vote on the old tally system because the network couldn't be trusted anymore. Three boards, three questions, three columns of names. Wake the vault. Convert the sleepers. Let it fail.

Someone had chalked a fourth option on the wall by the door—*kill the copy*—and someone else had scrubbed it half away, so it read like a scar: *kill the—*. I decided to take that as data rather than prophecy.

I stood at the back. That was deliberate. Whatever I was to these people—a murderer's echo, an archive with legs, a bargaining chip that had already been spent—I didn't think my face at the front of the room would help anyone think clearly. Including me.

Imani stood at the podium, and she looked terrible, which is to say she looked like someone who had slept four hours in nine days and had stopped pretending otherwise. She'd spent the week before the vote doing something I recognized from the case files: building her mother's counter-logs into a presentation. Sefa Okafor's voice, recovered from corrupted storage, line by line, would speak tonight if Imani had anything to say about it.

The hall's moderator was old Tomas Reyes, who ran the water reclamation plant and had the particular authority of a man everyone needed and no one liked. He banged a wrench on the podium instead of a gavel, which told you everything about Meridian Hollow's civic traditions.

"This meeting is open," he said. "Three hundred and eleven present. Quorum exceeded. We vote on the vault question tonight, and whatever we decide, we live with." He looked around the room with the expression of a man who had personally calculated how many liters of recycled water each person in it consumed per day. "First speaker: Chief Okonkwo."

Dara Okonkwo took the podium to scattered applause—the real kind, not polite. She'd been the reactor's chief for nineteen years before Imani replaced her, and she commanded the loyalty of maybe a third of the room, which after the sabotage was either impressive or damning depending on where you stood. She hadn't been charged. The council had decided that fear wasn't a crime, only what you did with it, and Dara had done her sabotaging through intermediaries who'd confessed fast enough to keep her name technically clean.

"Let's not pretend," she said, "that we're choosing between options. We're choosing between futures. Option one"—she tapped the first board—"we wake three hundred strangers from a century nobody remembers fondly, people who will need food, water, medical care, housing, all of it drawn from reserves that are already rationed to the bone. Option two, we convert their pods into regulators and keep our own lights on. Option three, we let the vault fail and stop lying to ourselves about having resources to spare."

She paused, and I'll give her this: she knew how to hold a silence.

"I've heard the argument that waking them is justice. Maybe it is. But justice doesn't have a caloric value. My grandchildren eat calories. The vault has been sealed for ninety years without my help, and it can fail for another ninety without my guilt."

"It fails in seventy-one days," Imani said quietly, from the podium's edge. "Dr. Ash confirmed the countdown. After that it's not failure, it's a mass grave."

"Then it's a grave we didn't dig," Dara said, and a hard knot of the room murmured agreement, and I understood something I should have understood weeks ago: the saboteurs had never been a fringe. They were a sample. They were what Meridian Hollow looked like when you scraped off the politeness.

Tomas banged the wrench. "Next speaker."

*

They came up one after another, and the speeches blurred into a taxonomy of fear.

A hydroponics tech named Vess argued for conversion with the flat pragmatism of someone reading a maintenance manual: three hundred pods as distributed regulators, each sleeper kept in low-power stasis as living circuitry, the colony's grid stabilized for a generation. "They don't suffer," she said. "They don't even dream. It's not killing them. It's borrowing them."

"Borrowing," repeated a woman in the third row, in a voice like a door closing. "That's what the Consolidation called it when they took the border worlds. Borrowing."

An old man named Ferro stood for option three with the serenity of the truly exhausted. "My father helped build this colony. He never mentioned any second vault. You know why? Because the founders decided some things were better buried. We're not murderers for letting the past stay past. We're just honest."

And then a young man I didn't know—sixteen, maybe, all elbows and fury—took the podium and pointed at me across the room. "Before anyone votes, ask the copy what's in his head. Ask him what his original did to Sefa Okafor. Ask him why we're even debating whether to save the people his boss buried!"

The room turned toward me like a body turning its head.

I could have stayed silent. Procedure suggested silence; the accused speaks last, if at all. But I'd spent the previous night writing and discarding six versions of a statement, and what survived the drafts was short.

"The original's logs are public now," I said. "All of them. He didn't kill Sefa Okafor. He covered for her accident so the vault would stay protected, and he spent eleven years secretly funding its upkeep because he couldn't undo what the Consolidation made him do. If you want to hate him, hate him accurately. And if you want to know what I am—" I shrugged, because there was no dignified way to say it. "I'm the receipt. That's all."

Silence, of the kind that has weight. Then Tomas banged the wrench and called Imani Okafor to the podium, and the room settled the way water settles after a stone.

*

She didn't start with her speech. She started with her mother's voice.

The hall's ancient speakers crackled, and then Sefa Okafor filled the room—not the file version, not the victim, not the case number, but a woman mid-sentence, annoyed at a stuck seal, laughing at her own cursing. Three hundred people flinched at the sound of a dead woman being alive.

Imani let it play for ten seconds. Fifteen. Long enough for the room to remember that grief has a voice before it has a grave.

Then she killed the audio and spoke.

"My mother died in the underlevels twelve years ago, and for twelve years this colony believed a lie about how. Not because anyone lied to us—because the truth was inconvenient, and inconvenience is the one thing Meridian Hollow has always rationed hardest." Her voice was steady. Too steady. The steadiness of a load-bearing wall. "We've heard the arithmetic tonight. Calories per capita. Kilowatt-hours per sleeper. Days remaining. I'm not going to argue with the math. The math is right. Every number they gave you is correct."

She paused.

"What the numbers leave out is what they're *for*. Dara says justice doesn't have a caloric value. She's right. Neither does mercy. Neither does the truth. Neither does the fact that every one of those three hundred sleepers is descended from the same refugees whose labor built the reactor Imani—that *I*—have been running since my mother died. This colony exists because their grandparents' generation hid them here and paid for the hiding. We are the interest on a debt we never audited."

Ferro started to rise; Imani raised a hand, not aggressively, just the gesture of someone holding a place in line.

"And yes—my mother died keeping that vault alive. I've read her counter-logs more times than I've slept this month. Do you know what she wrote in the last entry? Not instructions. Not warnings. A list. Names. All three hundred. She kept the list current for thirty years, updating it whenever the stasis systems drifted, because she said—" Imani's voice caught, once, precisely, like a circuit tripping and resetting, "—she said *a keeper who forgets the names is just a janitor*."

She looked out at the room, and I watched her stop performing steadiness and simply be steady, which is harder and rarer.

"So here is my position, and I'll make it short, because you've suffered enough speeches. The vote tonight asks us to choose: wake them, use them, or bury them. I'm voting against the question itself. Because my mother did not die choosing between halves of humanity. She died keeping both halves alive. The colonists above and the sleepers below—one ledger, one debt, one colony. The moment we pick a side, we finish the murder. Whatever we call it—rationing, realism, honesty—we'll have done what the Consolidation wanted and couldn't: we'll have made the refugees disappear, and we'll have made ourselves the kind of people who let it happen and filed it under necessity."

She stepped back from the podium. "Vote however your conscience allows. But if you write my mother's name on the 'let it fail' board, know that you're writing it on the side opposite hers."

*

The voting took forty minutes, because every name was written by hand, in chalk, on boards older than most of the voters, and because nobody trusted the tally to anyone else.

Wake the vault: one hundred and twenty-two.
Convert the sleepers: eighty-nine.
Let it fail: sixty-seven.

Thirty-three abstentions, most of them names added to no board at all, written instead on scraps of paper that Tomas collected in an empty water jug "for the record," as though the record might someday be kind.

Not a majority. Not close to the two-thirds the charter required for any binding action on cryogenic assets. The vote failed to produce a decision, which meant the default held, which meant the vault would fail in seventy days unless something changed.

The room began to dissolve into argument clusters, and I was moving toward Imani when the lights went out.

Not dimmed. Not browned. Out—all of them, the hall, the corridor strips, the hum of the ventilation dropping to nothing in a single instant, three hundred and eleven people plunged into a darkness so total that for a moment the only sound was breathing.

Then, from somewhere beneath our feet—from the direction of the reactor, from the direction of the vault—a sound I felt through the floor before I heard it: a long, descending groan of machinery surrendering, followed by the high, thin alarm tone of a stasis system announcing cascade failure.

Seventy days had just become a much smaller number.

In the dark, Imani's hand found my sleeve. "August," she said. "The reactor."

I was already moving.
