## 16. The Murder, Solved

The water in the antechamber came up to my knees and smelled like the inside of a very old battery—copper, ozone, and something organic that had given up on being anything else. My hand-cranked rig hummed against my back, feeding trickle current into the vault's dying bus through a cable I'd spliced with shaking hands two hours ago. Two hours. It felt like a decade. Somewhere above me, the colony was voting on whether I deserved to exist, and somewhere below me, three hundred people were freezing to death at a rate measured in minutes.

And directly in front of me, half-submerged, was the answer to a murder twelve years cold.

I want to tell you I recognized it immediately. That the truth announced itself with trumpets. It didn't. The truth was a sealed maintenance panel, corroded shut, sitting behind Sefa Okafor's old work station like it had been waiting for someone with nothing left to lose and a decent pry bar.

I had both.

* * *

Let me back up, because the descent deserves recording, even if the only witness is me and a waterproof recorder running on fumes.

When the saboteurs hit the reactor during the vote, the lights didn't just go out—they went out *opinionatedly*, with a sound like the whole colony clearing its throat before saying something unforgivable. Emergency power held for the life support ring and then started bleeding into the vault's cascade failure through some coupling nobody had mapped since founding. Imani's voice came over the comm in the dark: "August, the vault's drawing from the same bus. If it cascades, it takes the sleepers and half of Level Three with it."

"And if I cut it loose?"

"Three hundred people die quietly instead."

"So the choice is loud or quiet."

"The choice is always loud or quiet," she said, and there was something in her voice that sounded like her mother's logs must have sounded—tired arithmetic delivered without apology. "Go down there. Manual override's in the antechamber. And August—" a pause long enough for me to hear the reactor groaning through the deck plates, "—whatever you find down there, find it fast."

I took the hand-cranked rig because the lifts were dead and because there is a particular kind of stubbornness that says: if physics wants a fight, physics can have one, but it'll be on foot.

The underlevels were flooded to varying degrees—the vault antechamber worst, because it sat lowest and its sump pumps had been cannibalized years ago for parts that were probably, at this moment, keeping somebody's hydroponics warm. I waded by chemlight. The water was warm, which was worse than cold. Cold water means a leak. Warm water means the coolant loop is bleeding into places it should never be, which means the vault wasn't just failing—it was failing *creatively*.

Sefa Okafor's work station was the first thing I saw when my light swept the antechamber. It had been preserved—I realized this with a small shock—dust-sheeted, tools racked, a mug still on the desk with a fossilized ring of coffee inside it. Someone had treated this room like a shrine. Or a crime scene. With the original August Thackeray, those categories had always overlapped.

That was the man I was here to bury, after all. The murderer whose memories I carried like a stone in my chest. I had confessed them to the colony an hour before the vote—every redacted log, every cover-up, the whole ugly architecture of what he'd done to Meridian Hollow. I'd offered his guilt as currency and the colony had been counting it when the lights went out.

So when I found the panel, I pried it open expecting confirmation. Expecting the mechanism of murder. Expecting, frankly, to finally understand the man whose face I shaved every morning.

What I found was a maintenance log, handwritten, in Pre-Consolidation English.

* * *

Dr. Yewande Ash had taught me enough of the dead tongue to read slowly, the way you read a letter from someone who died before you were born—which, in fairness, she had. The handwriting was precise. Engineer's hand. Dated twelve years ago, the week Sefa Okafor died.

I read it twice by chemlight, standing knee-deep in cooling water, while the vault's stasis systems ticked down around me like a bomb with good manners.

It was not a confession. It was a *work order*.

*Sefa Okafor — Vault Keeper, Third Generation.* That was the first line, and it stopped me cold, because the official colony records listed Sefa as a hydroponics technician. A nobody. A colonist who died in an industrial accident that the investigating officer—August Thackeray, original—had closed with suspicious speed and zero arrests.

But this log knew her title. Knew her *function*. Every entry was countersigned in a second hand I recognized from the original's private files: neat, bureaucratic, deliberately unmemorable. His.

The entries told a story in the flattest possible language, which is how engineers tell the most important stories:

*Day 1: Stasis regulator seven showing cascade drift. Root cause: coolant coupling failure, original manufacture defect, 200-year service life exceeded. Replacement parts do not exist. Options: (a) allow cascade, loss of all sleepers; (b) manual bypass routing live reactor power through regulator array, permanent commitment, requires continuous human oversight.*

*Day 4: Okafor elects option b. States: "They kept us alive when we were the ones hiding. We don't get to forget them now that we're the ones comfortable."*

*Day 9: Bypass holding. Okafor notes the coupling is degrading faster under load. Estimates weeks, not months.*

*Day 11: Coupling failure during manual re-route. Electrical discharge. Okafor killed instantly performing emergency isolation. Her actions contained the discharge to the antechamber. Vault integrity preserved. All three hundred sleepers stable.*

*Day 12: Investigating officer arrived from Central. Officer reviewed logs. Officer understood immediately what this vault was, who these sleepers were, and what Consolidation would do if the Purge refugees' survival was documented. Officer proposed the following record: industrial accident, victim a hydroponics tech, investigation closed, no further inquiry. In exchange, officer assumed personal oversight of vault maintenance and funding, off-book, indefinitely.*

*Final entry, second hand:* *She died saving them. The colony will hate me for the lie, and that is acceptable. Hatred directed at me is attention not directed at this door. Let them think I killed her. Let them think I'm a monster who closes cases. Monsters don't get audited.*

I stood there until my chemlight flickered.

Then I read it a third time, because the first two readings hadn't finished breaking me yet.

* * *

Here is what I know about being a copy: you inherit a face, a set of reflexes, a memory archive—and a story about yourself that you never got to write. For eight months I had lived inside the story where August Thackeray original was a murderer. It was a terrible story, but it had a shape. Guilt has a shape. You can carry guilt. It fits in the arms.

This was worse. This was a man who had looked at a dead woman, at a room full of sleeping refugees from a genocide he'd been sent to erase, and chosen to become the villain in his own case file so that the door would stay shut and the sleepers would stay safe. He'd let Imani grow up hating him. He'd let the whole colony curdle around the mystery of Sefa Okafor's death, taking the poison into himself, year after year, funding pumps and couplings out of a salary he officially spent on nothing.

He hadn't solved the murder because he *was* the solution. The case was closed the day he wrote the false report. Everything after was just—maintenance. Twelve years of maintenance on a secret, alone, hated.

I thought about the memory archive burning in my chest. The colony wanted it destroyed, and I'd offered it gladly, because I'd thought it was a weapon. Evidence of a crime. It wasn't evidence. It was a eulogy written by a man who couldn't attend the funeral without blowing the cover.

"Imani," I said to the comm. Static answered. Of course it did. The universe has excellent comedic timing but terrible customer service.

* * *

The manual override was where the log said it would be, behind a panel stamped with the Consolidation eagle—a symbol that down here, in the dark, in the water, looked less like an empire and more like a warning label. I cranked the rig, fed current into the bypass, and watched the stasis regulators climb out of cascade one degree at a time. Slow. Agonizing. The kind of progress you have to believe in rather than see.

While I worked, I talked. Not to the sleepers, exactly. To Sefa.

"You rigged the whole thing," I said, splicing, cranking. "You found the vault, learned the dead language, took the keeper's job nobody knew existed, and when the hardware failed you didn't file a request—you put your hands on live current." The splice held. "And then you made sure your daughter would find the counter-logs eventually, because you're the only person I've ever heard of who planned her own posthumous vindication down to the filing system."

The regulators steadied. Ninety-one percent. Ninety-three.

"I spent eight months trying to solve your murder," I told her. "Turns out the murder was a press release. Turns out the killer was a eulogy." I laughed, and it came out wrong, wet, echoing off the flooded walls. "Your daughter is going to be furious. She built twelve years of grief on the version of events I helped—" I caught myself. "*He* helped write. And the grief was real. The grief was always real. It was just pointed at the wrong man."

Because that was the part that lodged in me like shrapnel: the hatred hadn't been wasted. Imani's anger, the colony's resentment, the cold shoulders and the closed doors—all of it had been load-bearing. Every ounce of suspicion aimed at the original August was a degree of heat kept off that door. He'd weaponized their contempt. He'd taken everything they had to give—a copy of a bureaucrat, easy to hate—and spent it on strangers from a dead century.

The inheritance inverted. I'd come here to close a murder case. Instead I was standing in the murderer's alibi, and the alibi was love, wearing the uniform of cowardice so well that even I, with his entire memory archive in my skull, had believed it.

Maybe especially I. Maybe he'd written the false logs knowing a copy would read them someday. Knowing I'd need the truth to be ugly right up until the moment it couldn't afford to be.

* * *

The vault stabilized at ninety-eight percent at roughly the same moment the comm crackled back to life.

"—ust? August, come in. Reactor's holding on auxiliary. The vote—" Imani's voice, ragged, "—the vote happened. They chose to wake the vault. Barely. Ash gave a speech in the dead language and half the room cried and the other half called it witchcraft, which is basically consensus for Meridian Hollow."

"It's done," I said. "Vault's stable. Cascade reversed." I looked at the water, the shrine-room, the mug with its fossilized coffee. "Imani. There's something down here you need to see. About your mother."

Silence. Long enough that I checked the comm's power cell.

"If you tell me she was working with him," Imani said finally, "if you tell me my mother helped the man who—"

"She died saving the vault," I said. "There was no murder. There was a coupling failure and a woman who wouldn't stand aside, and then there was a man who lied to everyone—including you—so that the thing she died for would survive. He took the blame because blame was armor. As long as the colony watched him, nobody watched the door."

The static breathed.

"He funded this place for twelve years," I went on, because once the dam breaks you might as well drown honestly. "Off-book. Out of guilt, yes—but mostly out of duty to her. Your mother's last act was to save three hundred strangers, and his next twelve years were making sure it counted."

Another silence. When Imani spoke again, her voice had gone somewhere I recognized from the mirror—past anger, past grief, into the flat country where you rebuild yourself from inventory.

"How do I know you're telling me the truth?"

"You don't," I said. "I'm carrying a murderer's memories and a liar's face. But the log is down here in her handwriting, and it's countersigned in his, and it says exactly what I said. Come read it yourself. Bring Ash to translate. Bring the whole colony if they fit in the corridor."

"They won't fit in the corridor."

"Then bring the ones who matter."

"That's everyone, August. That's the whole point of—" She stopped. I heard her take a breath, the kind you take before lifting something heavy. "I'm coming down. Don't touch anything else."

"Too late," I said. "I touched everything. It's sort of my protocol."

* * *

She arrived forty minutes later with Dr. Ash and a portable lamp, wading into the antechamber like a woman entering a church she'd been told was haunted. Ash read the log aloud in the dead tongue first—softly, the way you speak over graves—and then translated, and I watched Imani's face do the arithmetic of a lifetime collapsing into a different sum.

She didn't cry. I'd expected crying. Instead she took off her glove, knelt in the floodwater beside her mother's work station, and pressed her bare palm flat against the desk, the way you check whether a machine is still warm.

"She planned this," Imani said at last. "The counter-logs in her quarters, the vault log down here—she built a paper trail in two languages so that someday someone would have to know. She didn't trust the truth to survive on its own. She *archived* it." A breath that was almost a laugh. "She was the colony's real investigator. Twelve years before you ever got here."

"Then I'm relieved of duty," I said. "Good. I was never much for solving things. I'm better at cataloguing."

Ash looked up from the log, and the old woman's eyes were bright and terrible. "There is a final page," she said. "Sealed. Addressed not to the investigating officer." She turned it toward me. "To the copy."

The paper was dry—she'd kept it in a sleeve, of course she had, Sefa Okafor archived everything—and the handwriting was the engineer's hand, steady to the end.

*If you are reading this, then either the vault woke or it failed, and either way someone came looking. To whoever wears his face: he will have told you he was a monster, because monsters are simpler and safer. Do not believe him. Do not forgive him too quickly either—he did lie, and lies have interest charges. But understand what he bought with them. And when the day comes that you must choose between halves of humanity, remember that we refused to choose. That was the whole protocol. That was always the whole protocol.*

*We are still here. Make it mean something.*

The signal. The distress call that wasn't a countdown and wasn't a plea—it was her message, broadcast on a two-hundred-year-old frequency in a dead language, waiting for someone trained in dead dialects to arrive and listen. The original hadn't sent it. *She* had. From beyond death, via timer, the colony's true keeper had summoned her own detective.

I laughed again, and this time it came out right.

Above us, faint through the deck plates, the reactor coughed and held. Below us, three hundred hearts beat in their ice, ninety days from waking or ninety minutes from oblivion—we'd bought time, not victory. And in my hand, a dead woman's last instruction told me the choice I was going to have to make before this night ended, and warned me that refusing to choose was the only answer she'd accept.

Imani read the page over my shoulder. Then she looked at me—really looked, for the first time since we'd met, not at his face but at whatever was behind it now.

"The surge," she said quietly. "When we wake them. There's only enough power for one vault or the colony's life support. Isn't there."

"Yes," I said.

"And you're already planning something stupid."

"Imani," I said, rolling up my sleeves in the flooded dark, "stupidity is the family business. But I think I finally understand the protocol."
