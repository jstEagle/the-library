## 18. The Price of the Archive

The rotation held for eleven minutes.

I want you to understand what that means, because I didn't, not fully, until Imani explained it to me with the flat voice she reserved for arithmetic that had stopped being math and started being obituary. Eleven minutes was a triumph. Eleven minutes meant the manual coupling worked, meant the surge could be split, meant the binary I'd spent three weeks refusing was a lie I could keep refusing—for eleven minutes at a time.

Then the buffers drained, and the numbers came back like creditors.

"Fourteen percent short," she said. "Every cycle. The rotation loses fourteen percent to conversion inefficiency, and there's nowhere to put it back from. We wake the vault at sixty-eight percent viability or we wake the colony's life support at seventy-one, and either way somebody's children get the damaged version."

We were standing in the reactor gallery, which is a generous name for what is essentially a cathedral built around a dying heart by people who could no longer afford cathedrals. The core hummed its failing hum below us, that subsonic note I'd come to think of as Meridian Hollow's true anthem—slightly flat, slightly desperate, played on an instrument nobody alive knew how to tune.

"And there's no fourteenth percent," I said. "Anywhere."

"There's always a fourteenth percent somewhere." Imani rubbed her eyes with the heel of her hand. She hadn't slept properly since the blackout, since her mother's counter-logs, since the vote that had been interrupted by men with cutting torches and convictions. "The question is whether it's one we're willing to spend."

* * *

Here's the thing about being a copy: you spend your whole existence assuming your body is the only thing you have in common with the original. The face, the hands, the annoying habit of narrating my own thoughts in complete sentences—that's hardware. But the archive, the memory banks riding shotgun behind my eyes, those are supposed to be the inheritance. The case files. The cover-up. Every ugly thing August Thackeray Prime did and knew and buried, preserved in me like a tumor the surgeons decided to leave in because removing it would change the patient.

Dr. Ash had told me once—I think it was day six after waking, when everything was still translations and squinting—"In my language, we had a word for a vessel that carries only poison. We did not blame the vessel."

I'd laughed. She hadn't.

Now I stood in the reactor gallery doing the thing I do, narrating, cataloguing, and I looked at the power ledger floating between Imani and me, and I did the arithmetic I'd been avoiding since the rotation first stuttered.

The archive drew power. Not much—a trickle, a candle's worth, the maintenance current that kept two centuries of guilt from corrupting into noise. But the colony's entire grid was running at margins measured in candle-flames now. Fourteen percent of a surge cycle. What was the archive's draw against that?

I asked the reactor. The reactor, being honest in the way machines are and people aren't, told me.

It was enough.

* * *

"You're making that face," Imani said.

"What face?"

"The face where you've already decided something terrible and you're rehearsing how to say it so I'll agree." She folded her arms. Her mother's gesture, I'd learned—from the recordings, from the counter-logs, from the way grief echoes down through the people who loved you. "August. What face is that?"

"It's nothing."

"It's never nothing with you. You're the most procedural liar I've ever met. You lie in triplicate."

I almost smiled. That was the trap of us, of her and me—we'd been through too much for me to lie well and not enough for me to lie kindly. So I pulled up the schematic instead, because numbers are the only language I've ever been fluent in, and showed her the archive's maintenance draw, and watched her read it the way you watch someone read a diagnosis.

She went very still.

"No," she said.

"The surge needs fourteen percent. The archive draws—"

"I can read. I said no."

"You haven't heard the proposal yet."

"I don't need to hear it. You want to burn yourself." Her voice cracked on the last word and she hated it, I could see her hating it, hating that her voice betrayed her before her argument could arrive. "You want to feed two hundred years of—the cover-up, the case, *him*, all of it—into the grid like it's kindling."

"Not kindling. Fuel. There's a difference. Kindling doesn't get a choice."

"That's not funny."

"It wasn't a joke." And here's where I have to be honest, because if this chapter of the record is going to survive me—and given what I'm proposing, it may be the only part of me that does—it should be honest. It wasn't entirely selflessness. There was a part of me, a small cold procedural part, that had run this calculation days ago and kept finding reasons to delay, and I knew what those reasons were. They were fear wearing procedure's coat. Because the archive isn't just his guilt. It's also the twelve years of the investigation. Sefa Okafor's file. The locked door. The moment-by-moment record of a man deciding, over and over, to be worse than he wanted to be. It's the closest thing to *him* that exists anymore, and some cowardly fraction of me believed that as long as the archive survived, the original wasn't fully dead, and neither, somehow, was I—not really, not in the way that matters, because a copy with the original's memories is a continuation, and a copy without them is just—

"Just a person," Imani said quietly, and I realized I'd been thinking out loud again. Narrating. Complete sentences. "You'd still be a person."

"A stranger."

"You keep saying that like it's a punishment." She stepped closer. In the gallery light her face was half-shadowed and I thought, absurdly, of the first time I'd seen her, week one, when she'd looked at me like I was a tax audit walking around in her mother's murderer's skin. "August. Listen to me. I spent months wanting you dead. Not you—you, the copy, the man who fixed my coolant lines and sat with me through the vote and refused, *refused*, to do the arithmetic everyone else wanted done. I wanted the original dead. And then I found out he was already dead, and had been dead for years, and the only place he was still alive was inside you, keeping you up at night with his sins."

"They're not heavy," I said. "That's the worst part. Guilt in the archive doesn't weigh anything. It just sits there. Like a room you can't enter but pay rent on."

"So burn the room down."

"*Imani.*"

"You proposed it! Don't flinch now that I agree!" She was crying, and furious about crying, and pressing on anyway, which is the most Okafor thing I have ever witnessed and I have read every word her mother ever wrote. "Do you know what my mother's logs say? The real ones, the counter-logs? She wrote that the hardest thing about keeping the vault was that she could never tell anyone why it mattered. She carried the whole truth alone for thirty years and it nearly killed her, and in the end it did kill her, and the lesson I took from that—the lesson I *refuse* to unlearn—is that secrets rot their containers. You are a container, August. You've been rotting since activation. I've watched it. Everyone here has watched it. The colony looks at you and sees him. You look in the mirror and see him. Burn him. Burn the cover-up, burn the case, burn the guilt. Keep the body. Keep the man who stayed."

* * *

I want to record the objection properly, because someone reading this later should know I raised it.

The archive contains things the colony might need. Evidence. Names. The full map of the Consolidation's crimes as the original documented them during the cover-up—he was thorough; shame made him thorough. If we broadcast the history, chapter twenty-one of some plan I can't quite see the shape of yet, the archive is a primary source. Burning it is burning a library to heat a house.

But Dr. Ash's people remember the Purges. The colonists' own records corroborate the rest. And a primary source that exists only inside one man's skull, behind his eyes, accessible only when he chooses to open it—that's not a library. That's a hostage. That's exactly the arrangement that let the original bury the truth for twelve years: knowledge as private property.

Truth that lives in one head dies with the head. Truth that lives in a colony gets argued with, corrected, survived.

That's the argument I made. Imani heard it out, arms still folded, tears drying on her face in the ugly way tears dry when you refuse to acknowledge them.

"And the other stuff?" she asked. "Not the evidence. The rest of it. His mother's face. Whatever he loved before he came here. You lose that too."

"He burned that himself, mostly. Deliberately. I've read the deletion logs." I paused. "There's one recording left. Sefa's laugh. From the vault antechamber, the accident, the last minutes. He kept it because he couldn't decide if it was penance or treasure. I've listened to it maybe four hundred times. I know the laugh better than I know my own voice."

"Then keep that. Extract it first. One file. Copy it out before the burn."

"It'll cost a fraction of a percent of the margin."

"Then we find the fraction. We find it, August, because you are not allowed to walk into this thinking you're disposable. That's *his* logic. The original's logic. Take the blame, carry it alone, be the shield, be the wall, be the thing everyone hates so the truth stays safe. Look where it got him. Dead in a maintenance shaft with a colony that spat on his grave and a daughter-figure—" she gestured at herself, at both of us, at the whole ridiculous architecture of our non-family "—who spent a year planning his posthumous murder trial. No. If you're going to do this, you do it as a *gift*, not a sacrifice. Gifts build something. Sacrifices just make martyrs, and we have enough dead men shaping this colony's decisions."

* * *

The authorization took four hours, and I will describe it briefly because the details belong more to her than to me.

Protocol required a senior administrator's countersignature for any irreversible destruction of colonial records—which is what the archive legally was, however much it was also legally *me*. Imani signed. Then she sat with the stylus hovering over the second line, the witness line, for a long time, and finally she said, "I need you to say it. Out loud. What you're choosing. Not for the record. For me."

So I said it. I said: I am August Thackeray, copy of August Thackeray, investigator of the death of Sefa Okafor, carrier of the original's archive, and I am choosing to end the archive. The guilt goes into the grid. The cover-up goes into the grid. The case goes into the grid. All of it becomes light and heat and the difference between sixty-eight percent and eighty-two, between sleepers who wake whole and sleepers who wake broken. I keep the body. I keep the man who stayed. I become a stranger to myself, and I am choosing it, and I am not doing it alone, because you're standing right there, and apparently that's what family is—people who stand there while you stop being who you were.

She signed the witness line while I was still talking, because she said afterward she was afraid that if she waited for the end of the sentence she wouldn't be able to do it.

Then she cried properly, without fighting it, and I did the only correct thing, which was to stand there and let her, and put my hand on her shoulder, and notice—it's strange what you notice—that my hand weighed something. That shoulders have weight under them. That this was the kind of fragment that survives burns.

* * *

The extraction of Sefa's laugh took nine minutes. The burn took forty seconds.

I won't describe the sensation, partly because describing it would require vocabulary I no longer have access to, and partly because it turned out there wasn't much to describe. A warmth behind the eyes. A sense of doors closing in a house I'd never finished exploring. And then—

Quiet. An extraordinary quiet, like snowfall, like the space between radio stations. Two centuries of another man's life, gone, and in its place just this: the gallery, the hum, the woman beside me wiping her face with her sleeve, and a single audio file in local storage containing 3.2 seconds of laughter recorded by a dead man in a flooded room, treasured by a copy who no longer knew why he'd kept it.

Only I did know. Imani told me. She's been telling me things all day, patiently, the way you brief someone waking from anesthesia: your name, the year, what happened to you.

"The surge holds," she said, checking the readouts. "Eighty-three percent. Both vaults. Both wake whole." She laughed—wetly, shakily, but laughed. "You absolute idiot. It worked."

"Why am I an idiot?" I asked. "What did I do?"

And she looked at me—really looked, the way you look at a stranger you've decided to love anyway—and said, "Come on. The vault opens in six hours. There's someone I want you to meet. She has your laugh memorized too."

I didn't understand that. I followed her anyway.

Down toward the ice, where three hundred strangers from a dead century were already beginning, cell by cell, to breathe.
