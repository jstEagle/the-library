## 2. The Long Quiet

The manifest was still on my screen when I crossed out of Calloway customs range, and I told myself the same thing I'd been telling it for six hours: clerical error.

Clerical errors happen. Somewhere in the bureaucracy of a war that's been grinding for eleven years, a clerk fat-fingers a name into a decommissioned ship's registry, and a dead man rides a vessel that never flew. It's the kind of mistake that happens every day in a system that processes forty thousand manifests a cycle. Somebody's brother, somebody's son, somebody's ghost gets filed under the wrong hull number, and nobody notices because the dead don't complain.

Except Tomas wasn't somebody's brother. Tomas was mine. And the *Meridian's Grace* — hull 7741, decommissioned eight months before the first exchange, stripped and sold for scrap in a yard I could probably name if I let myself think about it — had no business carrying his name on any ledger, anywhere, in any hand, human or machine.

I killed the screen. That's the first rule of hauling contraband memory through the Corridor: what you don't look at can't be logged. The second rule is that everything gets logged anyway, somewhere, by someone, and the trick is making sure it's not logged by anyone who matters.

The *Kestrel* hummed around me, her old drives singing their one-note song, and I settled in for the long quiet. Fourteen hours of nothing between the Calloway checkpoint and the first Meridian buoy. Fourteen hours of my own company, which is a cargo I've never learned to pack properly.

* * *

The war didn't start with a battle. That's the thing nobody tells you. Wars in the histories start with battles because battles make good stories, but the real ones start with paperwork.

Calloway and Meridian were twin colonies, once. Same generation ship, same founding families, same stubborn streak that made both worlds habitable when the survey teams said they shouldn't be. They traded for two hundred years. My grandmother crossed the Corridor eleven times a year hauling Meridian silk-weave and Calloway grain alloy, and she used to say the two worlds were less like neighbors and more like a married couple who'd stopped talking but still reached for each other in the dark.

Then the Foundry Accords expired.

I was nine. I remember my mother explaining it at the kitchen table on our station, her hands flat on the metal like she could hold the whole situation down by pressing on it. The Accords governed who could refine what, who could sell to whom, and for two centuries the answer had been *everyone, everything, at fair rates*. When they expired, both governments sat down to renegotiate, and both governments discovered they'd spent two hundred years building arsenals they'd never needed before.

The first shot wasn't fired. It was filed. A Calloway customs seizure of a Meridian ore shipment, ruled illegal by a Meridian trade court, answered by a Meridian tariff, answered by a Calloway embargo, answered by — and this is the part that should have embarrassed everyone but didn't — the mining of the Corridor's central lanes. Both sides did it. Both sides called it defensive. Eleven years later, the shipping lanes that had carried two worlds' worth of commerce were a graveyard of hulks and sensor ghosts, and the only people crossing were people like me.

People like me, and people like the ones who died in the first exchange.

Because there *was* a first exchange. Three months into the embargo, before anyone had admitted there was a war, a Calloway patrol frigate and a Meridian escort cutter met in the dark near the old relay buoys, and both captains — I've read the declassified logs, I've read them more times than is healthy — both captains thought the other was about to fire. Neither was. Both did. Forty-one dead in ninety seconds, and after that the arithmetic took over, because you can't let forty-one of yours die without answering, and you can't answer without them answering back, and so on, forever, until the whole thing runs out of people or money or both.

The *Kestrel* was on that first patrol. Not mine — my father's. He came home. His navigator didn't.

Tomas wasn't there either. Tomas was nineteen and working the Meridian side, because our family, like a lot of Corridor families, had roots in both soils. He was a relay technician. Civilian. The kind of job that comes with a laminated neutrality card and a promise from both governments that civilians wouldn't be touched.

The first exchange happened near his relay.

I've done the timeline so many times it's worn smooth, like a stone I carry in my pocket. The exchange started at 0312. Tomas's relay went dark at 0314. The official Calloway report — the one they gave my mother, the one with the ribbon and the folded flag made of synthetic cloth because there wasn't a body — says the relay was caught in the crossfire. The official Meridian report says the same thing in different words. Two governments, one sentence, agreed.

The crossfire lasted two minutes. The relay had been standing in that spot for sixty years. It's a strange kind of crossfire that finds one civilian installation in a whole empty stretch of dark, but I was nineteen too, and nineteen is not an age built for asking that kind of question. Nineteen is an age built for surviving, and then, later, for hauling, and then, much later, for asking.

* * *

The long quiet has its own geography. Four hours out of Calloway space you pass the wreck field — the hulks from the early years, when both sides still fought like they meant it, before the war settled into its current arrangement, which is less a war than a habit with a budget. I keep the *Kestrel* wide of the wrecks. Not because of the old mines, though there are mines. Because of the transponders.

Dead ships keep broadcasting. Nobody tells you that either. A hulk's emergency beacon will run for decades on residual charge, calling for a rescue that came and went a lifetime ago, and if you drift close enough, your own receiver picks them up, and you spend the next stretch of dark listening to dead ships ask for help. I made that mistake my first solo year. I heard forty minutes of a Meridian freighter's mayday, the same thirty-second loop, the same frightened voice, over and over, and I finally understood that the war doesn't end for the ships. The war keeps going inside them, one thirty-second loop at a time, forever.

I gave the wreck field its berth and settled into the long middle of the haul, where there's nothing to do but run diagnostics and not think, and I'm very good at not thinking. I've had eleven years of practice. Grief, in my experience, is a lot like contraband. You can't destroy it, you can't deliver it, so you pack it in proper containers, you label it clearly, you keep it in the hold where it can't shift in transit, and you never, never open it mid-flight.

But the manifest kept leaking through the container walls.

Hull 7741. *Meridian's Grace.* Decommissioned before the first exchange. Tomas's name in the cargo column, in a registry that should have been purged, sealed, and shredded a decade ago. And here's the detail I kept circling back to, the one that wouldn't pack down flat no matter how I pressed it: the manifest I'd seen wasn't an archive. It was an *active* file. Live in the customs system. Flagged for routine review, the way live files are. Somebody had entered Tomas Dunmore into a working ledger, recently, deliberately, aboard a ship that had been scrap before he died.

Clerks don't do that by accident. Clerks don't reach into a decade-dead registry and pull out one specific name and attach it to one specific ghost ship. That takes intent. That takes someone who knew the name mattered, and knew it mattered to *me*.

Or — and here's the thought I kept putting back in its container, the one that shifted around no matter how I lashed it down — someone who wanted the name found, and knew the only person who'd go looking was his sister.

I ran the diagnostics twice. Both clean. The *Kestrel* is a good ship; she's the only honest thing in my life.

* * *

Six hours in, somewhere in the empty middle, I did the thing I never do mid-flight. I opened a container.

Not the grief one. The other one. The small canister I keep in the cabin safe, the one that's technically my most valuable cargo and legally doesn't exist. It holds ninety seconds of Tomas. A memory recording, one of the old civilian ones, made on his twenty-first birthday, three weeks before the relay. He's standing on the relay platform with the whole dark behind him, and he's laughing at whoever's holding the camera, and he says, *Wren, if you ever actually listen to this, it means you finally saved up for a decent receiver, which means you're rich, which means you owe me dinner.*

I've played it maybe two hundred times. I know the exact pitch of his laugh. I know that the wind on the platform catches on the second word. I know that at second sixty-one, someone off-camera — I've never been able to identify who — says something that makes him turn his head, and the recording catches the relay's old hum, the one they used to call the singing wire, and then he's back, grinning, and the ninety seconds end.

I played it once, there in the dark, and put it away, and told myself: clerical error. A clerk with a grudge, a clerk with a glitch, a clerk cleaning out old files who made a mistake and hasn't been caught. The universe is full of clerks and full of mistakes, and the war is full of dead men whose names surface in wrong places, and if I start chasing every ghost in the ledger I'll end up like the wreck-field ships, looping one thirty-second question for the rest of my life.

That's what I told myself. And here's the thing about telling yourself things in the long quiet: the quiet hears you, and it doesn't answer, and the not-answering is its own kind of verdict.

Because there was one more detail. I'd checked it before departure, standing in the customs hall with my heart doing its professional job of staying slow. The entry wasn't just Tomas's name. It was Tomas's name with a service number. A Calloway fleet service number.

Tomas never served. Tomas was a civilian relay tech with a laminated neutrality card and a promise. He had no service number, because the fleet never gave him one.

But somebody gave him one *after* he died. Somebody posthumously enlisted my brother in the fleet that let his relay burn, filed him aboard a ship that never flew, and left the file live where a smuggler with the right access codes — codes I definitely didn't obtain legitimately, but that's another story — could find it.

You don't do that by accident. You don't even do that by malice. Malice erases. That was *preservation*. Somebody built a false record to keep my brother's name alive in a system that eats names, and they built it badly enough to be found, and the only explanation I could live with was the one I liked least:

They wanted me to find it. And they knew I'd be looking.

* * *

Eight hours in, the long quiet started to end. The Meridian buoy chain flickered up on the long-range display, a string of cold lights marking the edge of the other world's patience, and I began the slow professional business of becoming nobody: transponder spoofed, cargo manifest scrubbed, flight plan filed under a name that belongs to a woman who died in the wreck field years ago and has never once been asked for dinner.

I should have felt better. A clean approach, a routine crossing, another haul survived. Instead I sat in the pilot's chair with my hands light on the controls and felt the thing I'd packed so carefully shifting in its hold, pressing against the container walls.

Because I'd lied to myself, back there in the dark, and I knew it. I don't chase ghosts. That's true. I don't chase ghosts because I can't afford to — because grief is a luxury for people with stationary lives, and mine is measured in fuel and customs windows and the exact number of hours before someone checks my hold.

But a clerical error doesn't have a service number. A clerical error doesn't wait, live and flagged, in an active file. A clerical error doesn't know my brother's name.

Somebody put Tomas on a ship that never flew, and they did it on purpose, and they did it recently, and I was going to find out who, and I was going to find out *why* — right after this crossing, right after I delivered the memories that pay for my fuel, right after I did the responsible thing, the professional thing, the thing that keeps me alive and flying and not asking questions.

The Meridian checkpoint pinged me. I answered with the dead woman's voice, all business, nothing to declare.

And then the checkpoint answered back with something I wasn't expecting, in a message that had nothing to do with my cargo and everything to do with my family:

**"Kestrel, be advised: a Calloway customs inquiry flag has been placed on your vessel. Reason code: manifest anomaly. Expect inspection at your next Calloway port of call."**

Manifest anomaly.

They'd already seen it. Whatever ghost somebody had filed under hull 7741, it wasn't sitting quietly in an archive waiting for me to stumble across it. It was *moving*. It had my ship's name on it now, and Calloway customs was asking questions, and I hadn't even started asking mine.

I killed the message log, spoofed my acknowledgment, and flew on into Meridian space with my heart doing something very unprofessional.

Somebody wanted me looking at that manifest. And now somebody wanted to know if I'd looked.
