## 16. Nine Days

There's a particular kind of arithmetic that smugglers do that no one teaches in any academy. It's not navigation and it's not logistics, exactly. It's the math of what you're willing to lose, run backward from the answer until you arrive at a number you can live with — or discover there isn't one.

I spent four hours doing that math in the cockpit of the *Magpie's Regret*, with Tomas's failsafe ticking down on my console like a patient debt collector.

Nine days.

The reactor beneath the main Static array would blow unless the peace accord broadcast from a transmitter powerful enough to punch through both governments' jamming grids. And here was the joke, the beautiful, obscene joke of it: the only transmitters strong enough were military-grade. Calloway's fleet wouldn't carry the message. Meridian's navy wouldn't either. Which left exactly one class of hardware on the Meridian side of the line that could do the job — an old civilian relay tower outside the city of Vess, decommissioned but never dismantled, because Meridian never dismantled anything it might want to point at Calloway someday.

A transmitter on Meridian itself.

I laughed when I worked that out. It came out wrong, more like a cough. Because I'd spent eleven years running the corridor between those two worlds, and I knew better than anyone alive what stood between the Calloway side and Vess.

Everything. The answer to "what stands between me and Vess" was everything.

* * *

Let me walk you through the blockade, since I'm about to spend nine days trying to die through it.

When the war went hot, both sides did the obvious thing: they mined the corridor and pointed guns at each other across it. That was fifteen years ago, and for most of that time the blockade had been a performance — patrols that kept predictable schedules, checkpoints where a smuggler with the right codes and the right bribe could slip through like water through fingers. Ash's safe-passage codes had made it almost boring. Almost comfortable. I'd gotten soft enough to think of the corridor as mine.

That was over now. Sennet's people had hit my safehouse, burned half my stock, and put a tracker on my hull that I hadn't found yet — though I'd find out about that later, and God help me, later was going to hurt. What I knew then was simpler: the patrols had changed shape. Routes that had been quiet for years were suddenly hot. Checkpoint rotations that used to drift had snapped tight as a drumhead. Someone at the top wanted the corridor sealed, and someone at the top had the authority to actually do it.

Which meant the window between Calloway and Vess was now the tightest blockade either world had ever flown. And I was going to fly through it carrying the one piece of cargo that every gun on both sides had been built, in one way or another, to prevent.

I pulled up the corridor chart and started marking kills.

Not literal kills — chokepoints. Places where the geometry of the mines and the patrol lanes funneled everything alive into a narrow channel, and the channel was watched. There were seven of them between my berth and the Meridian boundary. Seven doors, each one guarded, each one requiring a different key.

Door one: the Kestrel Shoals, a debris field from the first exchange of the war — the same exchange that killed Tomas, though I tried not to think of it that way while I plotted a course through his grave. Patrols swept it twice daily on a pattern I used to know by heart. If the new schedule held to old habits, there was a gap at the third hour after each sweep. If it didn't hold, I'd be a smear on a chunk of hull plating that some other smuggler would salvage and sell.

Door two: the tollgate at Calloway Deep, the formal checkpoint. No sneaking this one; too much sensor coverage, too many eyes. This door opened with paperwork. I'd need a cargo manifest so boring it made inspectors yawn, and a transponder signature clean enough to survive a deep scan. My ship currently carried a tracker I didn't know about and a hold full of nothing legal, so this door was going to cost me either a fortune in forged documentation or a favor I couldn't afford to owe.

Doors three through five: open space, which sounds easy and isn't. Open space is where the picket ships live, the fast interceptors that don't patrol so much as hunt. You cross open space by being somewhere else when they look. Timing, decoys, cold-running with systems dark and your life support eating batteries you can't recharge. I'd done forty-hour dark runs before. I'd never done them with a countdown in my ear.

Door six: the Meridian picket line, which was its own special problem, because Meridian knew my face. Not Ash's face — Meridian didn't care about Calloway officers — but Wren Dunmore's face, attached to a smuggling record on their side of the line that included three impoundments, one prison stint, and a standing grudge held by a man named Corvo Sass who ran fuel depots out of the Verge and whom I owed money to in a way that had teeth. Meridian customs didn't scare me. Meridian customs I could talk to. Corvo Sass scared me, because Corvo Sass didn't take bribes. He took payment, in full, with interest calculated by someone who clearly enjoyed their work.

Door seven: Vess itself, and the transmitter tower, and whatever garrison or militia or plain ordinary suspicious citizens stood between me and a machine built to shout across a war.

Seven doors. Nine days. One ship, one pilot, and a dead man's message.

I sat back and looked at the route glowing on my display, all its little red marks and amber warnings, and I thought: this is a suicide run with extra steps.

Then I thought: good thing I've got nothing left to lose except the things worth losing everything for.

* * *

Here's what nobody tells you about grief managed like cargo: it doesn't stay packed.

I'd spent eleven years keeping Tomas in a sealed container in the back of my skull, labeled and manifested and never opened in transit. First exchange of the war. The *Calloway's Grace*, a civilian hauler caught in the opening volley because somebody's targeting computer had a bad day and somebody's commanding officer decided a dead freighter made better propaganda than an apology. Two hundred and six souls. My brother was nineteen. He'd taken the job to pay for my first ship.

I told myself the manifest ghost was a clerical error. I told myself that for weeks, right up until I found the ship that never flew and the canister with my childhood nickname stenciled on it, right up until I heard his voice in the Static's archives saying *they buried the accord, Wren, they buried it and they're going to keep burying it as long as the war pays.*

So the container had come open. And what spilled out wasn't just grief. It was fury, and fury is a fuel, and I was running my whole route plan on it.

But grief has a way of leaking into the calculations anyway. I caught myself, somewhere around hour five, plotting the Kestrel Shoals approach along the exact lane the *Grace* had been flying when she died. Some part of me wanted to fly through the place he died. Some part of me thought maybe if I flew it perfectly, cleanly, the way he never got the chance to, something would balance out.

I deleted the waypoint and re-plotted. Sentiment is a fine thing in its place. Its place is not inside a blockade-run solution vector.

"Sorry, Tom," I said to the empty cockpit. "I'll visit properly. After."

The console blinked at me. Eight days, twenty-one hours. The failsafe didn't care about my feelings. Honestly, that was kind of a relief.

* * *

I made lists. Smugglers live and die by lists.

Fuel: enough for the full run plus thirty percent margin, which meant topping off at a depot, which meant exposure, which meant I'd do it last-minute and ugly. Supplies: forty hours of dark-run consumables, doubled, because I'd learned in prison that optimism is just planning with the numbers rounded down. Decoy drones: I had two. I needed six. Money: I counted what was left after the safehouse fire and the number made me laugh again, that same broken cough. I'd been rich once, by smuggler standards. The conspiracy had cost me a fortune before I'd even known it existed. There's a certain symmetry there I chose not to appreciate.

The manifest for the tollgate took me six hours to forge, and I have to say it was a masterpiece. Medical supplies — real ones, I'd sell them on the far side to fund the return trip — bound for a Meridian charity clinic with a director whose cousin I'd once smuggled out of a Calloway labor levy. Boring. Verifiable. The kind of cargo that makes an inspector want to stamp you through and get back to their coffee. I gave the *Magpie's Regret* a fresh transponder wash and a hull serial that belonged to a ship scrapped eight years ago, which was either brilliant or a felony depending on which customs officer scanned me.

Both, probably. Everything worth doing is both.

The hard problem — the one I circled for hours, the one that finally made me push away from the console and press my palms against my eyes — was door six. The Meridian picket. Because Meridian's grid was the one system I couldn't forge, bribe, or charm my way through, not anymore, not with my face on their watchlists and my debts walking around loose. And even if I slipped the pickets, even if I reached Vess, the transmitter tower sat in a city district that had been militarized since the war's second year. Getting to it would mean landing, moving on foot, breaking into a facility, and broadcasting — all while carrying proof that Meridian's own government had buried a peace accord alongside Calloway's.

Because that was the part I kept almost forgetting, the part that made the plan worse than suicide: the message implicated *both* sides. Tomas's evidence didn't spare Meridian. The accord had been buried jointly, profitably, by people on both worlds who'd decided that two hundred thousand dead was an acceptable price for another decade of wartime contracts. I wasn't carrying Calloway's shame to Meridian. I was carrying everyone's shame to everyone.

And the people of Meridian, hearing it, might well shoot the messenger on principle. Both governments would want me dead. Both governments' enemies would want me silenced for different reasons. Somewhere in the middle was a narrow band of people who'd want the truth — and most of them didn't know it existed yet.

I looked at the route one more time. Seven doors. Nine days. A cargo that would end the war or end me, possibly in that order, possibly simultaneously.

"You'd have hated this," I told Tomas. "You always said I should get a real job." A pause. "You also always said I was the stubborn one. So. Point me."

* * *

It was nearly dawn-shift when I finished — ship prepped, route locked, contingencies nested inside contingencies like bitter little dolls. I allowed myself one luxury: real coffee, heated slow, drunk looking out the canopy at the berth lights of Calloway Station, all that warm human clutter hanging in the dark.

That's when the comm pinged. Encrypted burst, official formatting, the kind of traffic that makes a smuggler's spine go cold before her brain catches up.

Not Ash. His cipher had a rhythm I'd know anywhere, and this wasn't it.

I decoded it with my heart doing something unprofessional against my ribs. It was short. It was unsigned. And it was addressed to me by a name I hadn't used since before the war — my mother's name for me, the one that existed nowhere in any registry, the one only two living people had ever known.

One of them was dead.

The message read: *You're being watched. Not by who you think. Docking bay twelve, station-side, before you fly. Come alone or don't come.*

Below it, a set of coordinates in the static band, and a single word:

*Vale.*

I set down the coffee. Outside, the station lights burned on, indifferent, and somewhere in the dark between here and Vess a reactor was counting down my brother's patience with the universe.

Eight days, nineteen hours.

I went to find out whether Inspector Idris Vale was about to become my ally, my executioner, or — knowing my luck lately — all three.
