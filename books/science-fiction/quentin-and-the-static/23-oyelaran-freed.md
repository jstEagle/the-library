## 23. Oyelaran Freed

There is a particular dignity in being traded like a crate of engine parts, and I have spent eleven days failing to find it.

The Meridian holding facility calls itself an "administrative processing center," which is what bureaucracies call a cage when they want to sleep well at night. I have been processed administratively for eleven days: interrogated under a light the color of weak tea, offered meals with the nutritional enthusiasm of wet cardboard, and asked — this is my favorite part — the same seventeen questions in rotating order by three different officers who clearly did not compare notes.

"Again," said the latest one, a young woman with the weary eyes of someone who joined an intelligence service expecting glamour and got paperwork. "Tell me about your client list."

"My dear," I said, arranging myself on the bolted chair as though it were a chaise in a better life, "I am a *fence*. A broker of pre-owned memories, a curator of other people's pasts. My client list is not a list. It is a rumor I cultivate carefully and know almost nothing about. That is the entire architecture of my profession. If I knew my clients, I would be a liability instead of a businessman."

"You're a criminal."

"I'm a *service industry*," I corrected. "The difference is licensing."

She wrote something down. They always write something down. In my experience, the note-taking is less about information and more about giving the interrogator something to do with their hands while they decide whether to threaten you again.

Here is what they actually wanted, though none of them had the wit to ask it plainly: they wanted Wren Dunmore. Or rather, they wanted whatever had made Calloway customs suddenly so interested in a scrapyard foreman, a decommissioned freighter, and a memory broker whose premises had been raided with a thoroughness that suggested someone upstairs had gotten frightened.

Frightened people are my favorite kind. Frightened people tell you where the treasure is buried by how carefully they dig around it.

So I gave them nothing, but I gave it beautifully. I gave them half-truths wrapped in complaints about my knees. I told them about a fictional shipment of wedding memories bound for a widow on Halcyon Ring — technically true, three years old, and useless. I described Wren in terms so generic she could have been any of forty smugglers I've worked with: tall-ish, competent, emotionally constipated. The interrogators nodded along, satisfied, writing down descriptions of a woman who did not exist while the woman who did was presumably out there doing something reckless with a canister.

Eleven days. And then, on the twelfth, the universe remembered I existed.

* * *

The prisoner swap happened at a docking arm between jurisdictions, which is diplomacy's way of saying "a hallway nobody wants to claim." Six of us from the Meridian side, six from Calloway, walking past each other in the middle like two schools of fish deciding, briefly, not to be enemies.

My counterpart was a Calloway quartermaster who'd been caught selling fuel allotments to both sides simultaneously, which I found genuinely admirable. We nodded at each other with the professional respect of one tradesman recognizing another.

"Oyelaran," said the escort officer, checking me off a slate. "You're being released to Calloway administrative jurisdiction pending—"

"I know what 'pending' means," I said. "It means you haven't decided yet."

He didn't dignify that. Fine. Dignity is overrated; survival is a full-time occupation.

What the escort officer did not know — what nobody in either government's paperwork seemed to know — was that three days before the raid, a package had arrived at my market stall wrapped in plain cloth and paid for in untraceable scrip. Inside: a memory canister, military-grade encryption, and no note except a single word in handwriting I recognized from a decade of shipping manifests.

*Tomas.*

Wren's brother. Dead eight years, officially. Alive in that canister, unmistakably — I'd skimmed the surface layer just enough to confirm provenance before the encryption walls went up, because a fence who doesn't verify his goods deserves to be robbed. What I'd seen in those few seconds had been enough to rearrange my understanding of the war: a man recording coordinates into the static band, alive after his death date, speaking with the urgency of someone who knows he is running out of time to be heard.

And beneath the coordinates, buried deeper than I could reach without proper equipment — names. I could feel them in the structure of the thing, the way you can feel a second floor above a shop. Encrypted. Waiting.

Then Calloway agents kicked my door in, confiscated everything including my good kettle, and put me in a room with weak tea light.

They thought they were stopping a smuggler. What they'd actually done was interrupt a delivery. And now here I was, walking free across a diplomatic hallway with nothing but the clothes I'd been arrested in and a decoded segment sitting in the only place they never searched: my own head.

You see, I don't keep copies of sensitive material on drives anymore. Drives get confiscated. No — I memorize. Eleven days in a cell with nothing to do but recite decryption keys against the wall until they wore grooves in my mind. My captors believed they were breaking my will. In fact they were providing me with uninterrupted study time. There is a lesson there about interrogation, and I intend to charge someone for it eventually.

* * *

Calloway "pending" turned out to mean a transit hotel near the Calloway side of the corridor — the kind of place with a bed bolted to the frame and a window that looked out on a wall, in case freedom tempted me toward views. They'd taken my wristband, my accounts, and my ship berthing rights. What they had not taken, because they didn't know it existed, was a debt.

Oyelaran does not forget debts. It is the load-bearing wall of my reputation.

Wren Dunmore owed me for the Kestrel job — the one where I ate six months of heat so her cargo could move clean through the Meridian quarantine. She'd never mentioned it since, which was correct. Debts spoken aloud become currency, and currency gets stolen. But the ledger in my head was clear, and the ledger said: when Oyelaran walks out of a cell with something that matters, it goes to Wren Dunmore first, free of charge, because that is what the Kestrel job bought.

Besides. Tomas Dunmore had once fixed my atmospheric recycler for free, back when we were all younger and the war was a thing that happened to other people. He'd hummed while he worked. I remember thinking: this one will die old, in a chair, complaining about his back.

The war had other plans. Wars generally do.

So: I needed a transmitter, a cipher key, and approximately four hours of privacy, and I had none of the above. What I had was a hotel room, a wall for a view, and the specific arrogance of a man who has been underestimated his entire career.

The transmitter took two days. There is a maintenance shaft behind the bathroom paneling — there is always a maintenance shaft behind the bathroom paneling; buildings are honest even when governments aren't — and spliced into the building's comm relay, with a signal bounce routed through a laundry barge and a weather satellite that hasn't been maintained since the war began, I could send a burst that would take three forensic specialists a week to trace, by which point I intended to be somewhere else entirely.

The cipher key was harder. Wren and I had agreed, years ago, on a rolling code based on shipping manifest entries — she'd insisted, back when she still laughed at my jokes, that anything worth encrypting should look like boring commerce. So I pulled up the public port ledgers — freely available, gloriously dull — and worked the sequence: entry 4471, the grain shipment, tonnage 12,000 minus the declared 11,340, times the day-code, cross-referenced against the harbor fee schedule. Bureaucracy as cryptography. Somewhere, a dead clerk is proud of us.

By the third night I had the final segment laid out across my hotel bed like a spread of cards, and I will confess something: my hands shook.

Not from fear. Fear and I are old colleagues; we've shared many rooms. This was something else. This was the shaking of a man reading a list and finding, at the bottom of it, a name he wished weren't there.

* * *

The decoded segment resolved into three parts.

First: confirmation. The peace accord was real. Negotiated in the second year of the war by a joint civilian delegation — farmers' unions, shipping consortiums, religious councils, people with no armies and therefore no profit in continued fighting. Both governments had received it. Both governments had buried it. The war was eight years old because eight years of wartime contracts, emergency powers, and blockade economics were worth more to certain men than eight years of peace.

Second: proof. Shipping manifests, payment trails, communications intercepts — the boring, devastating paper skeleton of the conspiracy. Tomas hadn't just found the accord; he'd documented its funeral.

Third: the names.

Seven officers. I read them twice, then a third time, hoping the letters would rearrange themselves out of charity. They did not.

Six of the names meant nothing to me personally — admirals and directors, the architecture of both governments, men whose faces appeared on propaganda posters looking trustworthy. One name, though. One name sat at the top of the list like a keystone, and I recognized it because everyone in the corridor recognized it, because it was stamped on every safe-passage code Wren had ever used, every patrol schedule that had mysteriously opened for her, every encrypted burst that kept her ships alive through waters that killed other smugglers.

Admiral Sennet. Ash Corven's commanding officer. The man whose codes had, for years, been the difference between Wren Dunmore flying home and Wren Dunmore becoming debris.

I sat on the edge of that terrible hotel bed for a long time.

Here is the arithmetic that kept me company: if I sent her the whole truth, she would learn that the man she loved — and do not tell me she doesn't love him; I've watched her decode his messages with the tenderness most people reserve for love letters — that the man she loved served, loyally, under the architect of her brother's murder. Every safe passage he ever gave her was, unknowingly or not, a leash held by the hand that killed Tomas. She would fly into the last stretch of her mission carrying that weight, and grief makes pilots sloppy, and sloppy pilots die.

If I withheld the name — sent the accord, the proof, the six lesser names, and quietly amputated Sennet from the list — I would be doing exactly what the conspiracy did. Editing the record for someone else's emotional convenience. Burying a truth because the truth was expensive. I would be a small, well-meaning Sennet, and I would rather be shot.

Loyalty versus truth. Made literal. Sitting on a hotel bed in borrowed socks.

In the end, the decision made itself, the way real decisions usually do — not through wisdom but through disgust. I looked at that list, at the neat officer's handwriting of history being falsified, and I felt the same thing Tomas must have felt recording his coordinates: that some things do not belong to us. The truth belongs to the dead. We are only couriers.

And I am, whatever else may be said of me, a very good courier.

* * *

The transmission went out at 0300 station time, compressed into a laundry-barge maintenance ping, encrypted in our manifest cipher, addressed to a relay drop only Wren would check. I included everything. The accord. The proof. All seven names, Sennet at the top, unamputated, unedited, ugly and whole.

Then, because I am who I am, I added a postscript. Not encrypted — embedded in the padding, where only someone who knew my habits would think to look:

*Kid — the last name is going to hurt more than the rest combined. Read it sitting down. And whatever you decide it means about the Corven boy, remember: a man is not his uniform's owner. Judge him by what he does in the next nine days. Everyone else will.*

Nine days. Yes, I knew about the countdown — Tomas had built it into the message structure, a failsafe with a fuse, because he understood governments the way I understand clients: assume good faith, verify with explosives. The reactor beneath the main array. I'd seen the schematics in the deeper layers and understood immediately why Wren was going to do something heroic and stupid, because in my experience heroic and stupid are conjoined twins and neither survives without the other.

I packed my single bag. Through the window-with-a-view-of-a-wall, I could hear the station's morning cycle beginning: cargo cranes, shift whistles, the great indifferent machinery of two economies still pretending to be at war.

Somewhere out there, Wren Dunmore was loading a message into her hold. Somewhere closer, Admiral Sennet was signing orders I could guess the shape of. And somewhere in between, a fleet officer was about to discover that his admiral's name headed a list of traitors, and that the woman he loved was the one carrying the evidence.

I settled my account at the front desk with cash, tipped generously — a man should leave every room better than he found it, even rooms with walls for windows — and walked out into the corridor traffic wearing the expression I save for border crossings: mildly bored, faintly disappointed, absolutely unmemorable.

Behind me, the transmission crawled outward at the speed of light, carrying seven names toward a woman with burns on her hands and a brother's voice in her hold.

I gave it four hours before she heard from me. I gave it four days before everything changed.

Both estimates, I suspected, were generous.
