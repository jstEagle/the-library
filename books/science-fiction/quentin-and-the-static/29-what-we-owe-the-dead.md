## 29. What We Owe the Dead

**Wren**

The fuel depot's med bay smelled like antiseptic and burnt insulation, which I suppose is what victory smells like when you scrape the confetti off it. I sat on a cot with my arm wrapped in synth-skin and watched Ash Corven — decorated officer of the Calloway fleet, traitor to his admiral, man who had just shot two of Sennet's agents to keep me breathing — try to figure out how a coffee dispenser worked.

"You have to select a setting," I said.

"I did."

"You selected *decontamination cycle*."

He pressed another button. The machine made a noise like a dying compressor and produced something brownish. He handed me the cup anyway, because that was the kind of man he was: he'd give you the wrong coffee with total sincerity and stand there while you drank it.

"Thanks," I said, and meant about six different things by it.

He sat down on the cot across from mine, close enough that our knees almost touched. For a while neither of us said anything. The depot hummed around us — pumps, recyclers, the low complaint of a station that had been through a firefight and wanted everyone to know it.

Four days left. That was the number sitting in the room with us, uninvited, taking up all the air. Four days until the reactor under the Static array went up whether Tomas's message flew or not. Three days and change, if you counted honestly, and I had stopped counting dishonestly somewhere around the second ambush.

"We should talk," Ash said.

"That's ominous."

"Wren."

"I know." I looked into the bad coffee. "I know. We should."

Here's the thing nobody tells you about love in wartime: it doesn't fail dramatically. It fails in the gaps. In the things you don't say because saying them would make them real, or make them targets, or make you responsible for them. Ash and I had spent two years conducting an affair in encrypted bursts and stolen dock hours, and we had gotten so good at omission that we'd forgotten how to do anything else. He hadn't told me about the strike orders. I hadn't told him about the canister. We'd each been protecting the other from the truth, and between us we'd built a wall out of kindness, and now here we were, both of us singed, both of us alive, sitting three feet apart with nothing left to hide except how scared we were.

So I put the coffee down and started.

"Tomas was twenty-two," I said. "When he died. You know that?"

"I read the file."

"Nobody reads the file right. The file says he died in the first exchange over the Verity Corridor, and that's technically true, but it makes it sound like a thing that happened, like weather. It wasn't weather. He was on a transport called the *Larkspur*, and he'd signed on because the pay was good and he wanted to buy our mother a house with actual gravity calibration, and the first exchange happened *around* him. Nobody targeted the *Larkspur*. That's the part that used to keep me up at night. He wasn't killed. He was *spilled*. Like cargo that got loose in a hard turn." My voice did something I didn't authorize. I let it. "And then four years later his name shows up on a ship that never flew, and it turns out he was alive after he was dead, recording coordinates, carrying a message that could have ended all of this before it ate half a generation. And somebody buried it. Somebody looked at a peace accord and did the math and decided the war paid better."

Ash was very still. Stillness was a discipline for him; I'd seen him hold it through customs inspections and artillery and one memorable dinner with my fence Oyelaran, who had asked him increasingly personal questions as a loyalty test. But this stillness was different. This was the stillness of a man taking inventory of himself.

"I knew command buried things," he said finally. "Everyone knows. It's the water you swim in. You tell yourself it's small things, logistics, embarrassments. You tell yourself the war is real even if the paperwork isn't." He rubbed a hand over his face. "I told myself that for eleven years, Wren. And then your brother — a dead man — handed me coordinates, and I checked them against fleet traffic, and I found the strike orders, and I sat in my quarters for two hours knowing exactly what I was looking at, and I reported nothing. Do you understand? I committed treason in decimal places and the worst part is it didn't feel like treason. It felt like *correction*."

"It felt like Tomas," I said.

He looked at me. "Yes. It felt like Tomas."

"And now?"

"Now I owe him." He said it plainly, no ceremony, the way you'd state a heading or a fuel load. "I owe him the message. I owe him the broadcast. And I owe—" He stopped. Started again. "I owe you the truth, which is that when I sabotaged the targeting data, I told myself it was for the mission. For the accord. For the dead. All true. But underneath it, in the place where I keep the things I don't say, it was for you. I would have missed that node for you even if the node were empty. That's not duty. That's not principle. That's just love wearing duty's uniform, and I think you deserve to know the difference."

The depot hummed. Somewhere a bulkhead ticked as it cooled.

"Ash," I said, "when I cut you out — chapter whatever, day whatever, when I decided to fly this alone — I told myself it was tactics. Keep the officer clean, keep the smuggler deniable, one person can slip a blockade where two get caught. And all of that was true too. But underneath it, in the place where I keep my things, it was because I couldn't watch you die for my brother. Because I already lost one man I loved to this war's arithmetic, and I could not—" My voice quit on me. I finished it quieter. "I could not do it twice."

"So we're both liars," he said.

"Professionally."

"Then let's lie to ourselves together, once, just briefly — let's pretend there's a version of the next four days where we both survive, and plan for that one. And if it turns out we were lying, at least we'll have lied with open eyes instead of closed ones."

I reached across the gap and took his hand. His knuckles were split from the fight; my new synth-skin itched. It was the least romantic touch of my life and possibly the most important.

"Okay," I said. "Open eyes. Here's what I see."

**Ash**

She laid it out on the depot's planning table the way she must have laid out a thousand smuggling runs: no sentiment, no theater, just geometry and timing and risk priced honestly. I've sat through fleet briefings delivered by admirals with three decades of command experience, and none of them could draw a corridor like Wren Dunmore drawing a corridor.

"The array sits in the static band, equidistant from both jamming shells. Meridian side is my way in — they know my face, but they know my face as a *payer*, not a target. Calloway side is yours, and it's worse, because Sennet has your name on an arrest order as of yesterday." She tapped the table. "But Sennet's problem is that he thinks like an admiral. He'll guard the approaches he understands. He'll mass ships where a fleet would come from. He will not expect one burned-out freighter coming up the Meridian meridian—"

"Don't."

"—the Meridian *axis*, thank you, flying a fuel profile that looks like a suicide note, because it mostly is."

"And the lattice?" I asked. "Even if you reach the transmitter, the jamming shell keeps the signal inside the band. It dies in the array's own throat unless someone opens the lattice manually from inside."

Her eyes came up to mine. She'd known. Of course she'd known; she'd run the numbers before I ever offered, and she'd been carrying the answer around like a stone, waiting to see if I'd pick it up myself.

"Someone opens the lattice from inside," she agreed quietly. "From the array itself. Which means being on the array when the reactor hits zero."

"No," I said. "It means being *near* the array when the reactor hits zero. There's a difference, and the difference is measured in escape velocity. Your brother built a failsafe, but he also built the array's maintenance systems, and maintenance systems have lifeboats. Old ones. Ugly ones. Ones nobody's maintained since the war started because nobody remembered the array existed." I pulled the schematic up on the table display — I'd pulled it three nights ago, alone in my quarters, doing the same math I was pretending to do for the first time now. "Pod bay seven. It still shows green on the derelict registry. If I open the lattice and get to pod bay seven in under ninety seconds—"

"Ninety seconds." Her jaw tightened. "You've never been inside the array."

"No. But I've spent eleven years learning how Calloway engineers build things, and Tomas learned from the same manuals. His work will be legible to me. Sloppy where he was rushed, elegant where he had time. I'll find the lattice controls the way you find a lock — by assuming the builder wanted it findable."

"And if you're wrong?"

"Then I'm wrong at high speed, and you fly the message home, and you tell the story however you want. Make me heroic. I know you'll be tempted to make me an idiot instead."

"I would never." A beat. "Publicly."

That almost got a laugh out of both of us, and the laugh broke something loose, and for a moment we just stood there on opposite sides of the table with a suicide run drawn between us, holding onto the joke like a rail.

Then her face changed. Not hardened — I'd seen her harden a hundred times, in customs queues and firefights, armor going up plate by plate. This was the opposite. This was armor coming off, deliberately, in a room with no exits, which for Wren Dunmore was probably the bravest thing she'd done all week, and she'd outrun a kill zone on Tuesday.

"Ash. Before we plan any further. I need to say the rest of it."

"Say it."

"What we owe the dead. You said it earlier and I've been chewing on it since, because I don't think we owe Tomas the message. I think the message takes care of itself — it wants to be heard, messages like that always do. What we owe him is smaller and harder. We owe him the *truth about ourselves*. That we spent years choosing safe lies over dangerous truths because the lies let us keep each other without risking anything. He died carrying a truth neither government wanted, and we honored him by—" She exhaled. "By hiding in plain sight together. So that's what I owe you now. No more omissions dressed as protection. Whatever happens in the next four days, you get all of me, including the parts that are terrified, including the part that wants to lock you in a hold and fly away from all of it. Especially that part. That part is very loud."

"I know," I said. "I have a matching one. Mine suggests we steal a ship and go run honest cargo past the edge of charted space and let the worlds sort out their own war."

"Mine suggests the exact same thing. Word for word. It's very suspicious."

"They may be the same voice. We may have been sharing it for years."

We looked at each other across the table, across the corridor map, across eleven years of his silence and two of mine, and I understood — finally, completely, the way you understand a heading once you commit to it — that we were not choosing each other despite the danger. We were choosing each other *inside* it, with full knowledge, the way you choose anything worth having. Love re-chosen, not out of habit or heat, but out of evidence. The evidence was a burned depot and a dead man's canister and a shared voice suggesting desertion, and it was enough. It was more than enough.

"Then it's settled," she said. "Two days to position. Day three, I run the Meridian axis. Day four, you're on the array, opening my sky."

"Day four," I said. "Ninety seconds. Pod bay seven."

"If you're late, I'll never forgive you."

"If I'm on time, you'll have to forgive me for everything else. Seems fair."

She came around the table and kissed me, and it wasn't the desperate kissing of stolen hours or the careful kissing of encrypted goodbyes. It was slow. It was a signature on a document. When she pulled back, her eyes were wet and furious about it, and she was smiling, and I thought: *whatever else the next four days take from us, they don't get this. This is already saved.*

"One condition," she said against my mouth.

"Name it."

"When this is over — whichever way it goes — you learn how the coffee dispenser works. I refuse to have survived a war only to keep drinking decontamination cycle."

"Deal," I said, and meant every word of it, which was how I knew I was finally telling the truth.

Outside the med bay viewport, the static band hung in the dark like a held breath, and somewhere beneath its oldest tower, a reactor counted down our remaining time with the patience of the dead. Four days. We turned toward it together.
