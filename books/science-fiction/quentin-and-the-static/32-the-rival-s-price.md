## 32. The Rival's Price

The Meridian side of the corridor had a smell I'd forgotten: ozone and old coffee and the particular staleness of a station that had been under blockade so long it had stopped pretending to be anything else. Port Verity hung in the shadow of its own moon like a thing trying not to be noticed, which was exactly how I remembered it, and exactly why I'd spent six years avoiding it.

Two days left on Tomas's clock. The reactor beneath the Static array didn't care about my feelings about Port Verity.

I docked at Berth 19 because it was cheap and because Berth 19 had always been cheap, and because some part of me — the part that manages grief like cargo, sealed and labeled and stacked where it can't shift in transit — thought that docking in the same berth as a hundred other nights might make this feel like any other run.

It didn't.

* * *

She found me before I'd finished the post-dock checklist. Of course she did. Sefa Maro had eyes in every berth on Verity and probably in every berth on every station from here to the Calloway line, and she had never once needed to knock.

"Wren Dunmore." Her voice came through my airlock speaker, warm as engine coolant. "You have no idea how much it hurts my feelings that you didn't announce yourself."

"Sefa." I opened the inner hatch but left the outer sealed, an old habit that said *we're talking through glass, literally and otherwise.* "Your feelings survive. They always do."

"Six years." She leaned against the corridor wall outside, arms crossed, silver rings catching the berth lights. She'd aged well, the way certain weapons age — cleaner lines, same intent. "Six years you fly Calloway runs, you wear their codes, you take their money, and then one day your transponder pings the Verity approach like nothing ever happened."

"I need a corridor."

"You need a lot of things." She smiled. It didn't reach anywhere near her eyes. "Word travels fast on a blockaded station, Wren. Faster than ships, these days. And the word is that you're running something hot enough that Calloway strike wings are repositioning along the Meridian approaches. Something worth burning patrol fuel over. Do you know what that does to my business? Every smuggler with a working drive is grounded or hiding, because the whole corridor's gone tight as a drum around whatever you're carrying."

"That's not—"

"And here's the part I love." She held up a hand, rings flashing. "Here's the part that keeps me up at night, drinking the good brandy I can no longer afford to import. The word is that whatever you're carrying, you're carrying it *to* someone. You're not selling. You're delivering. Which means you don't need to be on Verity long. Which means—" she tapped the glass between us, once, gently, like knocking on a coffin lid, "—you need a corridor. One clean corridor, out of Verity space, past the Meridian pickets who know your face and owe me favors, all the way to wherever you're going. And you need it in the next forty-eight hours, or whatever you're carrying stops mattering."

I stood very still. Through the outer viewport, past her shoulder, I could see the picket frigates hanging in their holding patterns, patient as vultures that had learned to wait instead of circle.

"How much?" I asked, because there was no point pretending I hadn't already decided.

Sefa's smile finally arrived at her eyes, and I wished it hadn't.

* * *

Her office was above the old market hall, in the room where Oyelaran used to keep his decoding rigs before the raid took him. She'd repainted. The walls were a color that wanted to be cream and had settled for surrender. She poured two glasses of the good brandy — the last of it, by the dust on the bottle — and set one in front of me without asking if I drank.

I drank.

"The number," she said, settling across from me, "is everything."

I put the glass down. "Everything is not a number."

"It is tonight." She spread her hands. "You want to know what you cost me, Wren? When you vanished off my books six years ago? Half my Meridian contacts assumed you'd been turned by Calloway intelligence. The other half assumed you were dead. Either way, I spent a year rebuilding trust you walked away from. And now you come back needing the single most expensive favor on this station — a clean corridor through a blockade that's tighter than it's been since the first exchange — and you want to talk numbers?"

"I'm listening to numbers."

"Then hear them." She counted on ringed fingers. "Your ship's escrow account on Verity — yes, I know about it, I've known about it since you opened it, don't make that face. Your contraband reserve. The memory-stock you've got sealed in your hold, even the stuff you think I don't know about, especially the Kestrel-grade emotional stock that fetches a fortune on the black market when people are scared enough to want to feel someone else's happiness. Everything. Every credit, every canister, every asset you have within reach of this station. In exchange: one corridor, swept of my people's obligations, guaranteed quiet from the Verity gate to the deep-band relay. Forty-eight hours of nobody knowing where you are."

I looked at her for a long moment. Outside, somewhere below us, the market hall murmured — the sound of people trading in small illegalities because the large legal ones had failed them.

"You know what I'm carrying," I said quietly.

"I know it's got Calloway moving fleets."

"It could end the war, Sefa."

Something flickered across her face — quick, quickly buried. Grief has a way of surfacing in people who've lost someone to a war that won't end, and Sefa had lost people. Everyone on Verity had lost people. That was practically the station charter.

"The war," she said, "has been very good to certain people. Me included, though I'll deny saying that. And it has been very bad to everyone else, which is also me, though I'll deny that harder." She refilled my glass. "So here's my confession, since we're being honest, which I hate: I don't actually want your money, Wren. I want your money *and* to feel like I got something back for six years of hurt feelings. Pay me everything, and I'll throw in the feeling. Consider it a package deal."

"That's extortion with sentimentality."

"That's Meridian," she said. "We never had Calloway's gift for doing cruelty cleanly."

* * *

I spent an hour in my ship transferring assets, and I'm not going to dress it up, because the whole point of sacrifice — real sacrifice, the kind that costs — is that it doesn't get ceremony. There was no dramatic moment. No music. Just me in the pilot's chair with a transfer interface glowing, watching numbers drain out of accounts I'd built one dangerous haul at a time across six years of a war I told myself I wasn't part of.

The escrow first. Eleven years of smuggling, and the escrow was the closest thing I had to savings — the fund I'd told myself, in weaker moments, would someday buy a berth somewhere quiet, somewhere the war couldn't follow, somewhere I could open Tomas's memory canister and just *listen* without a countdown running underneath it.

Gone. Transferred. The interface chirped politely, like a shop assistant.

Then the contraband reserve — the physical stock in my hold. The Kestrel-grade memories, forty canisters of other people's best days: weddings, first flights, children born in gravity gardens, a man's whole life of loving a woman who died too soon. I'd carried those through three customs sweeps and one near-miss with a Calloway interceptor, and they were worth more than my ship. I signed them over to Sefa's warehouse manifest with a thumbprint and a steady hand that surprised me.

The steady hand surprised me because inside, I wasn't steady at all. Inside, some voice kept saying: *this is everything, Wren. This is the whole escape route. After this, if the delivery fails, you own a ship with an empty hold and a name that both governments want. There is no quiet berth after this. There is no after.*

I transferred it anyway.

Because here's the arithmetic I'd learned from Tomas, posthumously, through a dead relay humming with forbidden voices: some things only have value if there's a world left to value them in. My fortune was a promise of a future. The message in my hold was the future itself. You don't haggle over the price of the ground you're standing on.

Mostly I believed that. The rest of me sat in the pilot's chair and felt the specific cold of having nothing left to lose, which is either freedom or a diagnosis, depending on the day.

* * *

Sefa met me at the market hall entrance at the fourth hour, and we walked the corridor together while her people made calls. I watched the corridor change around us — watched pickets look away, watch gate officers find sudden interest in their terminals, watch the machinery of obligation swing smoothly into motion. This was what I was buying, I realized. Not passage. Not silence. A web of small corruptions, each one a person deciding, for a price, not to see me.

It should have felt dirty. It mostly felt like relief, which was worse.

"You want to know something funny?" Sefa said, hands in her coat pockets, rings hidden. "Oyelaran used to sit right there — that alcove — and swear you were the only client he ever liked. Said you paid fair, lied little, and never once made him feel like a tool. He'd hate what this is costing you."

"He'd understand it."

"He would." She stopped walking. We stood under a light that buzzed at a frequency designed, I think, to discourage lingering conversations. "He's alive, by the way. Detained, but alive. Prisoner swap lists cycle through Verity's comm traffic, and his name came through last week. If you're doing what I think you're doing, he'd want you to know."

My chest did something complicated. Oyelaran, holding his tongue under interrogation, protecting my name with half-truths and wit, and now sitting in some detention block waiting for a war to shuffle him around like cargo. Like the manifests. Like Tomas's name on a ship that never flew.

"When this is done," I said, "if there's an after —"

"There's always an after, Wren. It's the during that kills people." She stuck out her hand. "Corridor's clean from now until your window closes. Nobody sees you. Nobody follows. And Wren —" her grip was strong, her voice lower, stripped of performance for exactly one sentence, "— whatever it is you're delivering? Deliver it. Some of us have been waiting a long time for someone to end this."

* * *

Back aboard, I ran pre-flight with the mechanical calm of someone who has already spent everything and therefore cannot be robbed. Fuel topped. Life support green. The message — Tomas's message, the buried accord, the names, the proof, the failsafe ticking down beneath the Static array — secured in the shielded hold where it had ridden through ambushes and burns and a tracker I hadn't detected until Ash tore it out of my hull plating with his own hands.

Two days. Thirty-one hours, if I was precise, and I was precise, because precision was the last currency I had left that nobody could take.

I keyed the departure request and waited for the gate.

That's when the priority burst came through on the diplomatic band — encrypted, high-priority, flagged with a cipher I knew better than my own heartbeat. Ash's cipher. The one he'd used for safe-passage codes, for midnight rendezvous coordinates, for *I miss you* compressed into operational brevity.

The message was short. It was also wrong in every way a message can be wrong: wrong routing, wrong authentication depth, sent in the clear-adjacent format of a man who expected his channel to be monitored and wanted whoever was monitoring it to read every word.

*WREN. SENNET KNOWS THE ARRAY IS THE TARGET. HE'S ORDERING ITS DESTRUCTION — NOT SEIZURE, DESTRUCTION. HE'LL LET THE REACTOR GO UP AND CALL IT PIRATE ARMAMENTS. YOUR WINDOW ISN'T THIRTY HOURS. IT'S WHATEVER'S LEFT BEFORE HIS STRIKE GROUP ARRIVES. I'M GOING TO TRY TO BUY YOU TIME. IF I CAN'T —*

The message cut there. No signature. No closing cipher-check. Just the absence where a man should have been, and the terrible arithmetic of what *if I can't* cost a person to type.

I sat in the dark of the cockpit with an empty hold, an empty account, and a full tank, and I did the only thing left that spending everything had bought me.

I flew.

The gate opened onto a corridor scrubbed clean of every eye that owed Sefa Maro a favor, and I pushed through it alone, toward the array, toward the countdown, toward whatever Ash Corven was about to do to buy me hours with a fleet officer's badge and a traitor's heart.

Behind me, Port Verity receded, and with it went eleven years of everything I'd been.

Ahead, the static was waiting.
