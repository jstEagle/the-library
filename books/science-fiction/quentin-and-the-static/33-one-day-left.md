## 33. One Day Left

There's a particular kind of silence that lives in an admiral's office aboard a flagship. It isn't the absence of sound — the *Vigilant* hums like a hive with a grudge — it's the absence of anything that might be held against you later. No ambient chatter. No music. Just the low thrum of a ship built for war and the soft tick of a chronometer counting down the last day of my career.

I'd rehearsed the conversation eleven times on the walk over. In three of those rehearsals I got shot.

"Commander Corven." Admiral Sennet didn't look up from his desk. He had the gift of making you feel like paperwork while he finished reading you. "You're early."

"You're expecting me, then."

"I'm expecting trouble in some form. It's usually you or Vale." He set down his stylus and finally gave me the full weight of his attention, which was considerable and entirely without warmth. Sennet was not a large man, but he occupied space the way gravity does — impersonally, absolutely. "Sit."

I sat. The chair was real wood, salvaged from something old and probably beloved. Everything in this room was older than the war except the war itself.

"I'll be direct, sir," I said.

"That will be novel."

"The strike order against the relay nodes. The targeting data came through your office with corrections appended after my own staff signed off on them. Corrections that moved the strike window up by thirty-six hours and shifted the primary node's coordinates by four-tenths of a degree into open debris field." I kept my voice level, the way you do when you're walking across ice and would prefer it to believe you're light. "Someone wanted that node hit before anyone could reach it. And someone else — me, as it happens — adjusted the firing solution so we'd miss."

The chronometer ticked. Sennet watched me with the expression of a man watching weather he'd already forecast.

"Four-tenths of a degree," he said. "You know what I find interesting about that number?"

"That it's small enough to deny and large enough to matter."

"That it's exactly the margin a competent officer uses when he wants deniability." He leaned back. "You've been sloppy lately, Ash. May I call you Ash? You've been calling yourself a great many things in fleet traffic lately — none of them Commander Corven."

My stomach did something professional and restrained, which is more than I can say for the rest of me. "Sir—"

"Don't." He raised one finger, and the finger alone had more authority than most captains. "Don't dig. Don't explain. Let me tell you what I know, and you can spend your energy deciding how much of it to confirm." He stood, walked to the viewport, hands clasped behind him. Outside, Calloway's blockade line stretched into the dark like a fence around a grave. "Eleven days ago, a decommissioned hull called the *Meridian Rose* turned up intact in a scrapyard on Halcyon Drift. Its records were scrubbed by someone with command-level access. Two days ago, an inspector named Vale — formerly customs, currently a problem — filed a request for the original crew manifest. Yesterday, a junior clerk in Fleet Records named Osei went missing along with six years of shipping archives. And this morning, my signals intelligence flagged a burst transmission in an obsolete cipher, addressed to no one, containing coordinates inside the Static band."

He turned back to me. "Your cipher, Commander. The one you use for personal correspondence. Shall I go on?"

"No," I said. "I think I see the shape of it."

"Do you? Because I don't think you do. I think you see a woman and a dead man and a conspiracy you'd very much like to be too late to stop. What you don't see is arithmetic." He came around the desk and perched on its edge, close enough that I could smell the stimulant gum he chewed when he hadn't slept, which was always. "This war costs Calloway nine thousand lives a year and earns us our independence from Meridian's grain cartels twice over. Every year it continues, we build ships instead of buying them. Every year, we become a nation instead of a colony with delusions. Do you know who ends wars, Ash? Losers. We are not losing."

"People are dying so we can keep winning."

"People have always died so that someone keeps winning. The only question worth asking is whether the dying buys anything." His eyes were pale and patient. "A peace accord exists. Yes. Signed eight years ago by negotiators both governments disavowed before the ink dried. Buried because the terms were generous to Meridian and humiliating to us, and because half the signatories were dead within the year under circumstances I did not arrange but certainly benefited from. Tomas Dunmore found a copy. He tried to move it through channels. Channels ate him."

The name landed where she lived in me, right under the sternum. I kept my face still. Eleven rehearsals, and still hearing her brother's name in this office felt like a round through the chest.

"So you admit it," I said.

"I admit nothing. I describe. There's a difference, and it's the difference between a court-martial and a conversation." He straightened. "Here is my offer, because I respect you and because killing useful officers is wasteful even by my standards. You will stand down. You will fly the strike tomorrow as ordered — the corrected coordinates, not your softened ones. The node goes, the reactor beneath it goes with it, and whatever message is buried in that band dies unspoken. In exchange, the investigation into your traffic irregularities closes. Your record stays clean. Your future stays yours."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then you're arrested within the hour, your access is revoked, and the strike proceeds anyway with a captain who doesn't ask about decimals." He smiled, and it was the worst thing in the room. "You cannot stop this, Ash. You can only decide whether you're standing in front of it or beside it."

I looked at him for a long moment. Then I said the thing I'd saved for all eleven rehearsals and never once used, because I'd hoped I wouldn't need it.

"There's a copy," I said. "Of everything. The accord, the manifest, Osei's archive extracts, the financial trails through the blockade contracts. Sealed, time-locked, and addressed to every news service on both sides of the line, plus the Meridian assembly, plus the Calloway civilian council. If I'm arrested, if I'm killed, if my ship suffers a tragic malfunction — it publishes automatically." I let that sit. "You taught me contingency planning, sir. Second-year tactics. You said a plan without a fallback is a wish."

Something flickered behind the pale eyes. Not fear. Recalculation. Sennet didn't fear things; he re-priced them.

"Where's the seal key?" he asked.

"With a person who doesn't exist, in a place I couldn't find under torture, and I mean that as a compliment to both of us." I stood. "Here's what I actually want, Admiral. Twenty-four hours. The strike window holds at its original time. Nobody flies at the node. Nobody touches Wren Dunmore. At the end of the day, either the accord broadcasts and the war ends with everyone's names attached to the truth — including mine, including what I did to help — or I hand myself over and you can have your court-martial with my compliments."

"And why would I grant you twenty-four hours?"

"Because in twenty-four hours, this stops being your secret and starts being history, and history is the one tribunal you can't stack." I picked up my cap. "Also because arresting me takes an hour and proves nothing, whereas letting me walk out of here takes no effort and costs you nothing you won't lose anyway."

The silence stretched. The chronometer ticked off seconds I could feel spending themselves like ammunition.

"Commander Corven," Sennet said at last, softly, "you are the finest officer I have mentored in thirty years of service, and I want you to understand that what happens next is not personal. It never was. That's rather the point of institutions — they let ordinary men do enormous damage without ever having to mean it."

"I know, sir. That's why I'm resigning from meaning nothing."

He touched the comm panel on his desk. "Security detail to my office."

And there it was. Eleven rehearsals, and in ten of them I'd gotten out the door before this moment. I put on my cap.

"For the record," I said, "the fallback copy is real. The rest — the timing, the contacts, the dead-drop schedule — I invented just now, and you know I did, because you taught me that a bluff only works when the bluffer believes it. So you have a choice: gamble that I'm lying, or gamble that I'm not. Either way, you've spent the last five minutes not ordering the strike moved up, and every minute counts now. That was the actual play. The arrest was always the price of admission."

For the first time in the eleven years I'd known him, Admiral Sennet looked genuinely surprised. It lasted perhaps a second and a half, and then it was gone, filed away somewhere with all the other things he'd never say aloud.

"Get him out of my sight," he told the marines in the doorway. "Disarm him. Log it as insubordination pending review."

"Pending review," I repeated, as they took my sidearm and my wrist terminal. "That's almost kind."

"It's accurate," Sennet said, returning to his desk, already reading something else, already elsewhere. "Reviews take time. Time is the one thing neither of us has enough of, Commander. I suggest you spend yours well."

They marched me through the corridors of the *Vigilant*, past officers who looked away, past bulkheads I'd helped certify, past the whole machinery of a life I'd been dismantling piece by piece for weeks without admitting it. The escort was polite. That frightened me more than hostility would have. Polite escorts meant orders written carefully, meant a cell with a functioning air supply, meant they intended for me to survive until someone decided otherwise.

In the brig — a comfortable one, officer-grade, which was its own kind of message — they left me alone with a bunk, a sink, and a wall screen displaying, with bureaucratic serenity, the words: *DETENTION PENDING REVIEW.*

I sat down and did the math. Sennet would verify the bluff within the hour, fail, and conclude that the real insurance was wherever Wren was. Which meant every asset he had would swing toward the Static band, toward the array, toward her. My arrest bought hours, maybe two, maybe three — the exact currency I'd promised, paid in the only coin I had left.

Which left the problem of being in a cell.

I pressed my palm flat against the wall screen and felt for the seam Vale had described when he'd briefed me two nights ago in a fuel depot smelling of ozone and bad coffee: *"Every brig on a Sennet-era hull has a maintenance override behind the display housing. He standardized the fleet to save money. The man's one genuine flaw is thrift."*

The housing popped on the second try.

Behind it: a manual release, a comm junction, and — because the universe occasionally tips its cap — a hardline data port that didn't route through the ship's monitored network.

I pulled the release. The door sighed open onto an empty corridor, because of course Sennet wouldn't waste bodies guarding a man whose arrest was theater. Somewhere above me, the strike fleet was spinning up its reactors ahead of schedule, and somewhere beyond the blockade line, Wren was flying toward an array rigged to kill her.

One day left. Less, now.

I stepped into the corridor and started running toward the hangar deck, composing as I went the message I'd send Vale — *they took the bait, I'm loose, get to her first or there's no one left to testify* — and understanding, with a clarity that felt almost like peace, that I had finally found the order I could follow with my whole heart:

The one nobody gave me.

* * *

The hangar bay was the problem, as hangar bays always are. Too much light, too many eyes, and my flight clearance revoked somewhere between the admiral's office and the brig. I needed a ship, a transponder that wouldn't scream, and ninety seconds of nobody looking at me.

What I had was a stolen maintenance jumpsuit from a laundry chute, a face that ranked high enough that most enlisted personnel hesitated to question it, and the specific desperation that makes people creative.

"Corven." The voice came from behind a loader mech, and for one absurd instant I thought the universe had simply given up on subtlety. But it wasn't security. It was Petty Officer Anselm, a tech I'd pulled out of a burning shuttle three years ago, who owed me a debt she'd mentioned exactly once and never let me forget she remembered. She looked at my jumpsuit, at the absence of escorts, at the general shape of the situation, and arrived at the correct conclusion with the speed of someone who'd survived the war by knowing when not to ask. "Bay nine. The survey skiff. Transponder's due for recalibration anyway — technically it reads as garbage until I fix it."

"How long until it reads as garbage?"

"Already does. Terrible shame." She tossed me a tool kit, which contained no tools and one ignition fob. "Bring it back dirty, Commander. Or don't bring it back."

"Anselm—"

"Go. You're making my shift complicated." She turned back to her mech, and that was the whole ceremony. Some loyalties don't survive being spoken aloud.

Bay nine's skiff was a stubby, ugly, honest little vessel, and I loved it immediately. I brought the reactor up quiet, slid out through the magnetic field on a garbage-disposal trajectory, and only lit the drives once Calloway's guns were behind me.

Then I opened a channel to the one frequency Vale and I had agreed on, the one buried so deep in the legacy band that monitoring it required believing in ghosts, and sent the shortest report of my career:

*Sennet knows. Strike moved up. Array is the target, not the node. I'm free, barely, heading for the band. Tell her the clock isn't what she thinks it is. It's worse.*

Vale's reply came back in eleven seconds, which meant he'd been sitting on the frequency waiting, which meant Vale had known how today would go before I did.

*Understood. One complication. She's not answering. Last confirmed position was the depot run twelve hours ago. Either she's dark on purpose—*

I finished the sentence for him, because someone had to, and saying it made it a fact I could act on instead of a fear I could drown in.

*—or Sennet's people got to her first.*

I pushed the throttle to a setting the manufacturer would have called irresponsible and I called necessary, and aimed the ugliest ship in the fleet at the place where the static lived.

One day left. Maybe less than that.

I'd spent my whole life learning to obey. It had taken a dead smuggler, a buried accord, and a woman I loved enough to lose everything for, to teach me the other skill — the one they never put in the manuals, the one Sennet had built his entire career on never needing:

When to disobey beautifully.

The stars smeared long and white, and I flew.
