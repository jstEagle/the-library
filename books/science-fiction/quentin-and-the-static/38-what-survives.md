## 38. What Survives

The wreckage field has a sound. I didn't expect that. I thought the aftermath of an explosion would be silence — the universe holding its breath, the way it does in the old war films Tomas loved. But the array's ruins creak and tick as they cool, kilometers of twisted lattice settling into itself like a dying animal rearranging its bones, and underneath all of it there's a hum I can't source, some residual charge in the metal that refuses to accept that the party is over.

I've been walking through it for six hours.

My suit radio is set to open channel, which is a stupid thing to do in a debris field because every stray carrier wave comes through as a whisper, a hiss, a half-word that might be a voice or might be my own signal bouncing back at me. I keep it on anyway. If Ash is out here — if he made it to a pod, if the pod made it clear of the blast radius, if any of the ifs I've been stacking like sandbags against a flood for the past day hold even one of them up — then he'll be listening on an open channel too, because that's how we found each other the first time. Encrypted bursts, back when encryption was all we had. Now I'd take anything. Static. Literally static.

"Wren." My own voice sounds wrong inside the helmet, flattened, like someone else doing an impression of me. "If you can hear this, key twice."

Nothing keys twice.

* * *

Here is what I know, laid out the way I lay out cargo manifests, because facts are the only containers I trust anymore:

The array detonated at 0347 station time. The broadcast went out at 0346 and eleven seconds. Forty-nine seconds of margin. I have replayed those forty-nine seconds so many times in my head that they've worn smooth, like a stone I carry in my pocket and rub with my thumb. Ash opened the jamming lattice from inside the array's control spine — Vale confirmed it, picked up the lattice telemetry going green across every band, shouted something over comms that was half triumph and half warning — and then the reactor hit zero and the array came apart in a bloom of light that every receiver in both systems caught, along with Tomas's voice reading the names of the men who buried the peace.

Forty-nine seconds is enough time to reach a pod bay. It is not enough time to reach a pod bay if you're running from the control spine, which sits deep in the array's core, which means Ash had maybe thirty seconds to get clear, and pods need twenty seconds to launch and spin up, and the math only works if everything worked, and nothing ever works, that's the first law of smuggling and probably the last law of everything.

Vale's ship pulled four pod beacons out of the debris cloud. Three were fleet personnel who'd been stationed on the array's outer ring — Sennet's guards, ironically, the men sent to make sure no signal ever left, saved by being posted farthest from the reason. The fourth beacon went dark forty seconds after activation. Dark could mean destroyed. Dark could mean battery failure. Dark could mean a pilot who powered down deliberately to drift quiet through the debris field rather than broadcast his position to whatever Sennet loyalists were still shooting.

Dark could mean a lot of things. That's the problem with dark. It doesn't testify.

* * *

I find the first pod at hour eight. It's one of the guards' — scorch-scored, hatch blown, empty, its occupant presumably recovered by the Meridian salvage crews working the far side of the field. I check anyway. Of course I check anyway. I've stopped pretending to myself that I'm conducting a systematic search. What I'm doing is walking through a graveyard asking it questions it can't answer.

The debris gets thicker toward the array's core footprint, which is where I shouldn't go, because the core sections are still hot in places, still shedding secondary fragments, and my suit's integrity readout has started giving me the amber eye. I go anyway. There's a specific kind of stubbornness that grief installs in you, and it doesn't respond to reason. It responds only to exhaustion, and I am not exhausted yet.

Tomas would have hated this about me. Or loved it. With Tomas it was always hard to tell the difference. He'd have stood in the airlock of some doomed idea and said, *Wren, statistically speaking,* and then he'd have walked in first, because my brother's statistics were always a form of prayer — numbers he recited so the universe would know he understood the odds and was choosing hope regardless.

He chose hope once, in a buried accord and a failsafe reactor and a message addressed to a nickname only I answered to. And the message got out. Both worlds heard it. Ceasefires are stuttering into place right now while I pick my way across cooling slag, and somewhere Admiral Sennet is in custody, and somewhere Idris Vale is publishing everything with the relish of a man who has waited his whole career to be right in public.

And none of it — not one second of it — feels like victory, because the man who opened the door isn't here to walk through it with me.

* * *

Hour eleven. I find the control spine.

Or what was the control spine. It's a cathedral now, if cathedrals were built by explosions — ribs of alloy arcing up out of the slag, hollowed, gutted, open to a sky full of slow-turning debris. I stand at its threshold and my suit lights play across the wreckage and I understand, with a clarity that feels like a physical blow, exactly how little thirty seconds is. How small a distance. How final.

"Key twice," I say again, to nobody, to everybody, to the hum in the metal. "Ash. Key twice."

The channel stays open and empty. Open channels are like that. They're an act of faith with no returns policy.

I climb into the ruin because stopping is not an option my body recognizes anymore. I climb over a collapsed conduit ring and down through what used to be a maintenance gallery, following the route I've reconstructed a hundred times in my head since the blast — the route he would have taken, spine to ring corridor, ring corridor to pod bay four, the nearest bay, the only bay with a chance. Halfway along the gallery there's a section where the hull simply isn't anymore, torn open to vacuum, and I have to clip across using the exposed framework, and hanging there over the drop with my gloves burning on the lines I let myself think the thought I've been outrunning for eleven hours:

Maybe he never left the spine. Maybe the lattice needed hands on it until the last second, and he knew it, and he stayed, because that's what duty looks like when it finally gets honest. He spent the whole war choosing duty over me, and then in the last forty-nine seconds he chose both, and the universe charged him full price.

I get myself across. I don't cry, mostly because in a suit crying is a logistics problem, and I am nothing if not a woman who respects logistics.

Pod bay four is gone. Not damaged — *gone*, a crater in the array's flank with the launch rails hanging like snapped ribs. Whatever was in it when the reactor blew is part of the light show now. I hover at the edge of the crater and run the scan anyway, third time, fourth time, sweeping for transponder fragments, for pod serials, for anything that would let me close the ledger. The scanner returns noise. Beautiful, useless noise.

There's a fifth bay. Pod bay five, on the opposite flank, reachable by an exterior crawl if the handholds survived. He'd have known that. He knew this array better than its own designers by the end — he'd spent two days learning it fast and dirty so he could open the lattice from inside. Maybe he ran for five instead of four when four became a fireball. Maybe.

Maybe is the currency I'm trading in now. I'm rich with it and it buys nothing.

* * *

I find the pod at hour fourteen, and for three full seconds I forget how to breathe.

It's wedged between two slabs of hull plating, nose-down, scorched, but whole — whole in the way that matters, pressure shell intact, and my heart does something acrobatic and illegal before I get close enough to read the situation properly. The beacon housing is dark. The launch telemetry stub shows it fired under power, clean separation, standard burn — and then the burn log just stops, mid-transit, like the pod decided halfway that it had somewhere else to be, or like something knocked it off its line and it fell back into the field.

The hatch is sealed. Sealed means pressurized or means locked-out dead. I put my palm flat against the hull, glove to metal, the way you'd check a fever, and I say his name out loud for the first time since the blast, and my voice cracks on the second syllable like cheap pottery.

"Ash."

No answer. No thump from inside, no cycling lock, no burst of that dry officer's voice telling me I'm standing in a restricted debris zone and should file a complaint with the appropriate authority. Just the hum of the ruins and my own breathing, loud and stupid in my ears.

I cycle the manual release. It fights me — of course it fights me, everything fights me today — and then it gives, and the hatch swings open on darkness, and my lights sweep the interior, and the pod is empty.

Empty. Couch harness cut, not unfastened — *cut*, with a blade, fast, the kind of cut you make when you're leaving in a hurry under your own power. An EVA kit missing from its rack. A suit bottle gone from the stores locker. And scratched into the inner face of the hatch, in the hurried gouge of someone writing without light, without time, with a piece of hull scrap:

Two words. I know the handwriting. I've seen it on a hundred encrypted bursts, in the margins of flight plans he wasn't supposed to send me, in the dust on the viewport of his quarters on the *Meridian's Wake* the one night we had more than an hour.

*Still here.*

Not a promise. Not quite. A status report. Officer Corven, even bleeding out of a pod mid-catastrophe, filed his paperwork: position unknown, condition functional, intentions ongoing. Still here. Which means he cut himself free, suited up, and went *somewhere* — away from the beacon, because a lit beacon in that debris cloud was a target for every Sennet loyalist still flying angry — and dark was the point. Dark was survival. Dark was a man drifting silent through a graveyard, waiting for the shooting to stop, trusting — with no evidence, with nothing but the same faith I've been burning for fourteen hours — that the person looking for him wouldn't stop either.

Or it means he cut himself free because the pod was failing, and he died in the dark between the slabs, and the words were the last thing he ever wrote, and I'm standing in his grave reading his headstone.

Both readings fit the evidence. That's the cruelty of it. Grief and hope are identical twins and they wear each other's clothes and neither one will tell me which is which.

* * *

I sit down on the pod's couch, in the empty pod, in the middle of the ruin of the thing that ended the war, and I let the fight go out of me for exactly sixty seconds. I watch the clock do it. Sixty seconds of letting my shoulders shake, of pressing Tomas's memory canister — my only keepsake, my whole inheritance — against the front of my suit like it could loan me some of his courage. Then the timer chimes and I stand up, because that's the deal I made with myself: mourn on schedule, search on schedule, and never let the mourning vote on the searching.

Because here's what I know that the evidence doesn't get a say in. Ash Corven committed treason in decimal places for me. He opened a jamming lattice with his own hands and stayed until the last second because he doesn't know how to leave a job half-done. A man like that doesn't write *still here* and then die politely nearby where I'd find him. A man like that writes *still here* and then makes it true — drifts, hides, limps toward the nearest rock with an atmosphere and a comm tower, working the problem, because working the problem is the only prayer he knows.

And I know how to find people who don't want to be found. It's the family business. It was always going to be the family business; Tomas smuggled truth and I smuggle memories and apparently love runs the same routes.

I climb out of the pod and seal the hatch behind me, gently, the way you'd close a door on a sleeping room. Then I turn my lights toward the field's edge, where the debris thins and the rocks begin, where a man on a stolen oxygen bottle would head if he wanted cover and distance and a chance.

"Open channel," I say, and my voice is steady now, steady the way cargo straps are steady, load-rated. "This is Wren Dunmore. I'm coming along the outer field, heading spinward. If you're listening, key twice. If you can't key, shoot a flare. If you can't shoot a flare—" I stop. Swallow. Start over, softer. "Just stay alive. I'm good at finding things. Ask anyone I've ever robbed."

The channel hisses. The ruins hum. Somewhere out in the dark between the slabs, either a man is drifting toward me or a word scratched in metal is all that survives him, and I won't know which until I've turned over every rock in this graveyard, and I intend to turn over every rock in this graveyard.

I thumb my thrusters and rise out of the crater, into the slow snowfall of debris, toward the rocks, toward the maybe.

Halfway up, on the open channel, out of the noise — faint, half-swallowed, almost certainly nothing, almost certainly my own signal ghosting back off the slag —

Something keys. Twice.
