## 3. Children Who Remember Tomorrow

They slept in the seed vault that first night, seven children curled among the cryo-cradles like kittens in a drawer, and Zephyrine sat awake among them counting heartbeats because she could not stop.

Seven. The Halcyon had launched with a crew of forty-two.

She had asked about the others, gently, the way you ask a child if they've lost something at school. A girl named Sefa had looked up at her with eyes too old for her face and said, *The ship ate them going backward. There wasn't enough future left to hold them all.* Then she'd rolled over and gone to sleep, and Zephyrine had stood in the dark of the vault for a long time, listening to the hum of the climate systems keeping three hundred thousand unborn plants alive, and understood that she was in the presence of something her training had no category for.

In the morning she made porridge from the protein stock—real stock, not the synthesized paste she lived on—and watched them eat. They ate like children. That was the horror of it, she thought. They chewed with their mouths open and fought over the honey ration and laughed, and between one laugh and the next their eyes would go somewhere else, somewhere far down a road they hadn't walked yet, and they would say things like:

*The eastern aquifer fails in the spring.*

*You burn your hand on the sterilizer in November. You always do.*

*There were nine hundred of us once. Before they wrote us out.*

That last one came from Ivo, the oldest-looking of them, maybe ten, maybe a hundred and six. He said it while staring into his bowl, and the other children went quiet the way people go quiet at a funeral, and Zephyrine felt the word *nine hundred* land in her chest like a dropped tool.

The corporate record said Meridian-9's terraforming initiative had been decommissioned for "sustained ecological nonviability." Population at time of decommissioning: zero. She knew this because she had read the file four hundred times over eleven years, looking for the flaw in it, the seam, the place where the story didn't hold. There was no population to evacuate because there had never been a population. The colony had failed before it began. She was a caretaker of an empty house.

But Ivo said there had been nine hundred.

* * *

—I remember the sirens but not the sound of them. Is that strange? I remember knowing they were sounding. I remember Mother putting her coat on me backwards because there wasn't time, and the collar scratching my chin, and I remember thinking: this is what the end smells like, ozone and my own hair.

They came at night. Not ships—ships come at day, ships want to be seen. These came quiet, under the aurora, and men in grey suits walked through the hab rings with tablets, and the tablets glowed blue on their faces, and wherever the blue light touched, people stopped being loud.

Not dead. Stopped being loud. My father shouted and then he was quiet and then he was a line in someone's column, and the column was labeled ASSETS REASSIGNED, and I was small enough that the light only got partway through me, which is why I am like this now, why I remember forward instead of back—

* * *

Zephyrine found him in the greenhouse at midday, standing at the edge of the irrigation channel, looking down at the water as if it were a window.

"Ivo," she said. "Come inside. It's cold."

"It's not cold." He didn't turn around. "It's never cold here anymore. That's wrong, you know. Meridian-9 is supposed to have winters. You fixed that too well. In the logs—the real ones, the ones you haven't written yet—you complain about it. No thermal cycling means weak root structures. You figure that out in year fourteen."

Her mouth went dry. "Ivo. How do you know about my logs?"

"Because I read them." Now he turned, and his face in the grow-lights was calm in a way no child's face should be. "All of them. When I was grown. I was the colony's records keeper, Zephyrine. I kept the archives. I catalogued every log you ever made, eleven thousand entries, and I read them twice, and then—" He stopped. His hands, small hands, chapped from the cold walk across the plain, opened and closed at his sides. "And then the Halcyon happened, and time folded, and now I'm eight years old and I've already died once."

"You haven't died," she said. "You're right here."

"I died *here*." He pointed at the floor. At the soil beneath the hydroponic trays, the engineered loam she turned every season. "Right about there. Under the third tray. During the erasure. I was twenty-nine. They couldn't finish the job on me so they finished it with a chemical agent in the water supply, and I went down in the irrigation channel, right there, and my body stayed in the cistern for six days before anyone—" His voice cracked, split into two ages at once, the child and the man fighting for the same throat. "Six days. And when they pulled me out I was already being forgotten. Everyone who remembered me was already a line in a column."

Zephyrine knelt down slowly, the way you kneel near a wounded animal. "Ivo. Listen to me. What you're describing—it didn't happen. I've been on this planet for thirty-one years. There has never been a colony here. There's never been nine hundred people. There's never been—"

"There's never been anything the record doesn't say there's been," he said. "That's the whole point. That's what erasure *means*. You think the absence of evidence is evidence of absence? You've been living inside the evidence of absence for thirty-one years. You've been watering its garden."

* * *

—The blue light comes for everyone eventually. Even the ones who hide. Especially the ones who hide, because hiding means you know, and knowing is the thing they're actually deleting.

My mother hid me in the seed vault. Isn't that funny? The same vault. She put me in a cryo-cradle meant for wheat embryos and told me to count backwards from a thousand, and I counted, and somewhere around six hundred the blue light swept through the cracks in the door, and everything after six hundred is missing. Everything after six hundred is a gap shaped exactly like my whole life.

When I woke up I was old enough to have grandchildren and I didn't have any, and the planet was empty except for one woman who talked to plants, and I applied for the records position because I wanted to find out what happened after six hundred.

I never found out. I found out what happened *before* six hundred instead, because the Halcyon's relay experiments broke the spine of time and we all fell through our own pages—

* * *

She spent the afternoon checking. That was her nature; grief made her methodical. She pulled the atmospheric records, the orbital survey data, the original colonial charter. And underneath the charter, in a directory she had never had clearance to open and suddenly—since the Halcyon's return, since some deep system had hiccupped back to life—did, she found a personnel manifest.

Nine hundred and twelve names.

Dated thirty-one years ago. Dated to the founding of Meridian-9.

She sat in front of the screen for a long time. Outside, the sun crawled toward the ridge and the children played in the yard of the hab module, playing a game with rules she couldn't follow, a game where one of them would shout a number and another would shout *not yet* and they'd all scatter. It sounded almost like laughter. It sounded almost like normal.

Sefa came in without knocking. She was the youngest, maybe five, and she climbed onto the chair beside Zephyrine and looked at the manifest with the flat competence of a customs officer.

"That's them," she said.

"Yes."

"You didn't know about them."

"No."

"They took them out of the record so the failure wouldn't be in the record either." Sefa swung her legs. "The terraforming worked, you know. That's the part nobody says. It worked too well, and the yield projections embarrassed somebody's quarterly report, so they closed it and deleted everyone and called it nonviable. Nine hundred people is a rounding error if they were never born." She looked up at Zephyrine, and her eyes were ancient and five years old. "You're the only one left who can prove they existed. Did you know that? We tried to tell you for years. You never listened. You just kept making your little logs."

Zephyrine's hands were shaking. "What do you mean, *we tried to tell you*?"

But Sefa had already slid off the chair, already running back to the game, already shouting *not yet* at the top of her lungs, and Zephyrine sat alone with nine hundred and twelve names glowing on the screen, and thought: *my logs*. Eleven years of daily entries, addressed to colonists who would never come. Filed, synced, archived to the relay network every single night.

Every single night.

If the children were telling the truth—if the record had been rewritten—then her logs were the only unedited copy of this place that existed anywhere. The only version of Meridian-9 that hadn't been touched by the blue light.

Which meant that somewhere, someone had decided those logs needed to stop existing too.

* * *

—Ivo again. Or the part of Ivo that is still twenty-nine, still in the cistern, still counting.

Here is what I know that I shouldn't: in three days, ships will come. Grey hulls, no markings, retrieval team, sanitization protocol. They will land on the plain by the old launch gantry. There will be eleven of them. Their commander drinks her coffee black and is afraid of open water.

Here is what else I know: last time, we ran too late. Last time, the archive cores burned in the greenhouse fire and everything after six hundred stayed missing forever.

This time I am eight years old and I remember everything, and I am going to stand in the irrigation channel where I drowned and I am going to say the numbers out loud until somebody listens.

* * *

He was waiting for her at the airlock when she finally came out, dusk pooling violet across the plain, and behind him the other six children stood in a line, silent, watching her with their impossible eyes.

"You checked the manifest," Ivo said. Not a question.

"Yes."

"Then you know we're not lying. And you know what comes next." He held up one small hand, fingers spread, and counted for her, the way children count, the way witnesses count. "Three days. Eleven soldiers. One fire. Unless we leave tomorrow."

Zephyrine looked past him at the horizon, at the darkening edge of the world where the dead-space relay sat invisible, three weeks' journey away, holding the only signal window that mattered.

"Tomorrow," she said.
