## 10. First Season After

The shuttle's descent window opened at dawn, and Ivo watched Meridian-9 come up to meet him through glass that was not the Halcyon's glass, in a body that was finally, blessedly, his own age.

Twenty-three years old. He had been twenty-three for eleven months now, and he still caught himself checking his hands each morning — the knuckles too big, the wrists scarred from a life he'd lived twice, once forward and once backward and once, somehow, sideways through the middle of it all. The doctors on Ceres Station said the reversal would stabilize as they aged into their remembered selves. They were right, mostly. But some mornings he woke up seven years old again inside, small and certain of his own death, and it took a full minute of counting ceiling tiles before the panic let go.

"Approach vector's clean," said Wren beside him. She was twenty now, tall where she had once been tiny, her hair cut short the way she'd always said she would wear it when she grew up. She'd grown up into her own prophecy. They all had.

Below them, Meridian-9 turned green.

Not the aggressive, engineered green of corporate terraforming brochures — not the flat monoculture carpets that Halcyon Industries had promised its investors forty years ago. This was a messier green. Patchwork. Stubborn. The ash plains had gone gray-green with lichen first, then moss, then grasses that no one had planted, seeds carried on wind from the seed vault's vents, from Zephyrine's garden, spreading outward year after year like a rumor that refused to die.

"There," Sefa said from the cockpit doorway, pointing. "There it is."

The settlement rose out of the plain like something grown rather than built: low domes of printed regolith, solar fields angled toward a sun that had spent decades shining on no one, and at the center — because the survey team had insisted, because the charter had required it, because some things are load-bearing even in architecture —

The greenhouse.

Ivo's throat tightened. He was twenty-three years old and his throat tightened like a boy's, and he let it.

* * *

They came down in the old landing field, the one Zephyrine had described in Log 4,412: *The pad is cracked but sound. If anyone ever comes, tell them to land slow. The ground remembers being broken, and it doesn't like to be surprised.*

The ground held. The crowd that met them was small — forty-one settlers, the entire founding population of New Meridian, plus a delegation from the Reconstruction Authority who kept their distance and their expressions carefully neutral. These were the people who had read the transmission. All of human space had read the transmission; it had played on every listening station for six days before the relay burned, and then it had played again from archives, and again, until *Meridian* became a word that meant something other than failure.

But these forty-one had done more than read. They had sold things. Left things. Crossed things. They had looked at a dead colony on the edge of dead space and seen a beginning.

A woman stepped forward from the settlers' line. She was older than Ivo expected — mid-fifties, silver threading her temples, hands stained the particular permanent green of someone who has gardened for decades.

"I'm Odile Vantrease," she said. "First Agronomist. We've been tending what we could reach of the vault." She stopped. Looked at him — really looked, the way you look at a person you have only ever known as text. "You're Ivo."

"Yes."

"You're taller than your logs."

He laughed, and it came out wet. "Everything about me is later than my logs."

She smiled, and then she did something none of the officials had done in three years of hearings and depositions and memorial services. She hugged him. Briefly, fiercely, the way you hug someone you've been waiting for.

"The greenhouse," she said against his shoulder. "We couldn't get the main bay open. Not properly. The locks are keyed to her biometrics and the backup systems died with the relay grid. We've grown everything else from vault stock — but that bay, the one she lived in—" She pulled back. "We thought you should be the one."

* * *

The greenhouse doors were fused at the seams, sealed from the inside by a woman who had known she was dying and had chosen, with typical Zephyrine, to make her last act an act of horticulture rather than farewell. It took two days to cut through without damaging what lay beyond. Ivo did the final meter himself, with a hand torch, alone, because some thresholds you cross by yourself or not at all.

The seal broke on the morning of the third day. Warm air breathed out against his face — warm, impossibly, after nine years — carrying the smell of living soil and water and something flowering.

He stepped inside.

The bay was a jungle. That was the only word. Nine years without a keeper, and the garden had not died; it had *gone feral*, vines climbing the support struts, canopy layers interlocking overhead, drip lines Zephyrine had rigged to gravity-fed cisterns still dripping with geological patience. And everywhere, everywhere, plants he knew by name before he knew them by sight, because he had heard her voice describe every one of them across a thousand logs, back when he was a child who remembered a future in which those logs existed:

*Log 2,207. The Meridian roses finally took. I told them about you. I told them all of you were coming.*

They had taken. They had spread across an entire wall, blooms the color of dawn seen through dust, and they were open — all of them, hundreds, in a bay that had been sealed and dark and unvisited since the year she died.

Odile stood behind him in the doorway, and he heard her breath catch. "That's not possible," she whispered. "No light cycle. No pollinators. Nine years—"

Ivo walked to the nearest bloom and touched it, and it was real, and it was soft, and it smelled like a memory he hadn't made yet.

"She planted them for us," he said. "And then the garden waited."

Later, much later, the botanists would find the explanation — a self-regulating photobioreactor in the subfloor, dormant-mode, running on geothermal trickle current, cycling light in slow pulses timed to a schedule Zephyrine had programmed with a terraformer's patience: *bloom in nineteen years, if no one comes sooner.* She had set the garden's clock to the date the children would be grown. She had calculated it. Of course she had. She had spent her whole life planting for people who hadn't arrived yet, and she was not going to stop just because she was dead.

But standing there in the green light with the roses opening around him like applause, Ivo didn't want the explanation. He wanted the truth underneath it, which was simpler and larger:

She had known they would come home.

* * *

They buried her properly that spring — or rather, they moved her, because she had never been buried at all. They found her where the children had left her nine years ago, at the edge of the relay site, wrapped in thermal sheeting against the cold she had chosen over the long walk home. Ivo had argued, at nineteen, at twenty, at every age between, that they should bring her back to Ceres, give her a name on a monument wall among the other names the Erasure Protocol had deleted and the transmission had restored.

Wren had ended the argument. "She stayed so the garden would have roots," she said. "You don't dig up roots."

So they buried her in the greenhouse annex, beneath the rose wall, under a stone Ivo carved himself with the only epitaph anyone needed:

**ZEPHYRINE STERLING — TERRAFORMER. SHE PLANTED US.**

The word *us* had been debated. Ivo had won.

* * *

The logs went into the settlement archive that same season — all four thousand six hundred and twelve of them, restored from the cores she had carried across the ash plains at terrible cost, transcribed, indexed, taught. Not like scripture, exactly, though some of the younger settlers read them that way, reverent and wide-eyed. More like letters. Like a grandmother's voice kept in a drawer, taken out when you needed to remember how to be brave, or how to be patient, or how to talk to a plant that was struggling.

*Log 3,891. Today I told the citrus about disappointment. I find they take it better than I do.*

*Log 1,006. If you are reading this, whoever you are: the colony did not fail. Remember that. Whatever they told you, whatever the record says — we were here. We loved this ground. Love is data too. It just doesn't fit in their columns.*

*Log 4,599. To the children, if it's the children: I am sorry for the cost. I am not sorry for the choice. Grow toward the light. It's the whole job. It was always the whole job.*

Ivo read one aloud every evening in the common dome. It had started as his private ritual and become the settlement's, the way things do — not by decision but by gravity. Forty-one settlers and however many children had been born since, sitting in lamplight while a dead woman's voice taught them how to live on a world that had tried to erase her.

Tonight's log was short. He saved it for last, the way he always did on this particular night, the anniversary of the transmission.

*Log 4,612. Final entry. The signal is away. The children are walking east, and they are alive, and they will grow up, and someday — not soon, but someday — someone will stand in my garden who is not me. When that happens, tell them the season turned. Tell them I got to see the first frost melt. Tell them—*

The log ended there. It always ended there. Nine years of scholarship, three formal reconstructions of the corrupted file, and no one had ever recovered the last words Zephyrine Sterling spoke into her recorder.

Ivo closed the reader. Outside the dome, Meridian-9's second spring was coming on — real seasons now, seeded clouds, rain that fell on ground that remembered being broken and had decided, like its first gardener, not to mind.

He was almost asleep when the comm chimed.

It was Odile, and her voice was strange — careful, controlled, the voice of a scientist holding something very delicate very far from her body.

"Ivo. You need to come to the greenhouse. Now."

"What is it?"

A pause. In the background, faintly, he could hear other voices — settlers waking, moving, the particular pitch of a crowd gathered around a thing it cannot explain.

"The annex," Odile said. "Under the rose wall. The soil sensors tripped an hour ago." Another pause, longer. "Something's growing there, Ivo. Something new. It's not in the vault catalog. It's not in any catalog. And it's blooming."

Ivo sat up in the dark, twenty-three years old, heart going like a boy's.

"It's blooming," Odile repeated, "and Ivo — the flowers are recording. Sound. On a loop." Her voice finally cracked, wonder and fear in the same breath. "It sounds like her. It sounds like she's finishing the log."
