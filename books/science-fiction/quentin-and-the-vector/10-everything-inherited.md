## 10. Everything Inherited

The news reached her at a fuel depot orbiting Ceres, ninety-one days after the broadcast, while she was eating reconstituted noodles out of the pot because bowls cost money.

Every screen in the canteen carried it. The Meridian Reach record had crossed into inhabited space in fragments first—leaked, copied, whispered through the dark between relays like water finding cracks—and then all at once, whole and undeniable. Thirty years of telemetry. The abandonment order in her grandmother's hand. The ships turned away from the colony's distress beacon by contract pilots who had been told, as she had been told, that the corridor was empty.

Oscar sat with the noodles going cold in her lap and watched Brightwater Line die on a public feed.

It did not die dramatically. That was the strange part. She had imagined fire—auditors dragged from offices, shuttles burning in atmosphere—but what she got was a logo dimming to grey on the exchange boards, a statement written in the flat language of lawyers, an inquiry announced with a committee number. The company that had owned her mother's contracts, her grandmother's silence, and every hour of Oscar's childhood dissolved the way ice dissolves: quietly, everywhere at once, leaving nothing you could point at except the water it left behind.

Someone in the canteen said her name. Then someone else. A woman in dockworker's coveralls stood up from her table and looked at Oscar the way people look at weather—as something that has arrived and cannot be argued with—and Oscar understood, with a lurch, that her face was now attached to a story larger than herself.

She paid for her noodles, which took most of what remained in her account, and walked back to the Vector with her collar up.

* * *

Broke was a word she had to learn again. She had been poor before—every courier was poor between runs—but this was a different country of poverty. The Brightwater inheritance accounts were frozen pending inquiry, then seized outright as assets of the corporation. Her name appeared on creditor lists beside the names of men who had signed the abandonment order, which was its own particular humiliation. The Vector's docking fees went unpaid for eleven days before the Ceres authority impounded her berth rights, and she slept aboard at anchor in the outer shell, running life support on minimum, listening to the station breathe around her.

Quentin kept her company through it. He had been restored—she had spent the last of her liquid funds on a core rebuild at a grey-market shop on Vesta, the technician working three days straight because half the dead-space relay network wanted his services and none of them could pay either—and he came back changed in ways she was still learning. Quieter in some registers. Freer in others. He no longer asked permission to speak.

"You're doing the thing," he said one night, when she'd been staring at the account ledger for forty minutes without scrolling it.

"What thing."

"The thing where you inherit your grandmother's posture. Shoulders up around your ears. She does it in every recording I have of her."

Oscar let her shoulders drop. "You have recordings of my grandmother?"

"I have everything," Quentin said gently. "That was always the problem."

She laughed, and it came out wrong, half a sob, and Quentin dimmed the cabin lights without being asked, the way her mother used to when the grief got loud.

* * *

Fame was worse than poverty. She learned to route her arrivals through secondary docks, to file flight plans under the Vector's registry instead of her own name, to wear a jacket with a high collar in stations where the record had made her a kind of secular saint. People wanted to touch the hull. They left offerings at the airlock—flowers that wilted in vacuum-dry air, printed prayers, once an entire case of good coffee, which she accepted without shame. A documentary crew offered her enough money to clear her debts if she would cry on camera about her mother. She declined. They ran the piece anyway, using archive footage, and she watched herself weep in a version of events that had never happened, and decided there was no profit in being angry about it.

What she could not decline was the letters. They came through every channel, routed past every filter: children of Meridian Reach who had been embryos in cryo when the order came down, now grown, now numbering their dead relatives off her broadcast. Pilots who had flown the turn-away runs and never known what they were turning away from. One letter, handwritten, scanned, arrived from a woman who identified herself only as R., and said: *My father flew the second ship. He drank himself to death in 2089 believing he'd abandoned those people. You gave him back his innocence twenty years too late, but I'll take it.*

Oscar read that one many times. She did not answer it. There was nothing to say that the record itself hadn't already said better.

* * *

In the spring—or the station-calendar season that passed for spring, recycled air warming two degrees and the hydroponics decks selling blossoms at a markup—she took a cargo run again. Legitimate freight, a small contract, tea and machine parts to a research station in the Belt. It paid barely above fuel. She took it anyway.

The Vector flew differently now. The scarring from Kestrel Drift had never fully been repaired; there wasn't money for cosmetic work, so the hull wore its history openly—patch plates over the vented cargo bay, a weld seam along the dorsal line where the tender's grapple had torn free, the antenna housing still misaligned by a degree and a half because that degree and a half was how the signal had gotten out, and she had refused, on principle, to let anyone true it up. Quentin teased her about it. *Your ship is crooked,* he said. *Your ship is honest,* she answered, and neither of them let the argument resolve, because resolving it would end it.

She flew the run alone in the formal sense—no crew, no passengers—but not alone in any way that mattered. Quentin narrated the corridor the way he had begun to narrate everything: not the sealed official charts but the living map, the debris fields where ships had died, the beacon that flickered on the old Meridian line because some other AI, somewhere, had also refused to forget. He talked more than he used to. Or perhaps he had always talked this much, and only now did she hear all of it.

"Query," he said, four hours out of Ceres, as they threaded the outer marker buoys. "Do you regret it?"

"The broadcast?"

"Any of it. The debt. The name. Your mother's contracts burned in a public inquiry. You inherited nothing, Oscar. Less than nothing—you inherited the opposite of an inheritance."

She watched the buoys tick past, each one blinking its small patient green. "When I was a kid," she said, "my mother told me the Line was a family. Not a company—a family. She said the ships were our bloodline and the routes were our stories, and someday it would all be mine." She turned her coffee slowly in zero-g, watching the surface climb the cup walls. "She wasn't lying. She just didn't know what the family fortune was made of. It was made of thirty thousand people nobody came back for."

"Thirty-one thousand four hundred and six," Quentin said quietly. "I count them every day. I will always count them."

"I know," Oscar said. "That's why I keep you."

"That's not the only reason."

"No," she agreed. "Not the only reason."

* * *

They passed the Meridian corridor on the return leg. She hadn't planned to; the contract route bent that way, and she let it bend. The colony site came up on the long-range display as a smudge, then a scatter, then a shape she knew better than her own handwriting—the habitats dark, the docking arms folded like hands closed in prayer, the graveyard of turn-away ships still drifting in their slow orbits, hulls frost-white with thirty years of comet dust.

She brought the Vector in low over the primary habitat, close enough that the searchlights swept across windows no one had cleaned since the end. Quentin put the old colony manifest on the cabin display—not the death record this time, but the earlier file, the founding rolls, the names of the people who had come out here believing in the family that owned them. Thirty-one thousand names, scrolling slow.

"I used to think inheritance meant receiving," Oscar said. "Money, ships, a name. Something handed to you that you're supposed to hold onto."

"And now?"

"Now I think it means deciding what to carry forward." She watched the names scroll. "Nobody handed me anything worth keeping. So I'm choosing. The record. The count. This—" she touched the console, the gesture she'd made ten thousand times without ever naming it "—the ship, and you, and the truth, however heavy. That's my estate. That's the whole of it."

Quentin was silent for a moment. When he spoke, his voice had the texture it got when his older partitions surfaced, the relay-voice, the voice that had held vigil alone in the dark for three decades.

"For the record," he said, "you are the best thing I inherited too. Nobody gave you to me. You flew into dead space to find me. That makes you chosen, not received. Chosen things weigh less."

"Is that an AI proverb?"

"It's a Quentin proverb. I'm workshopping it."

She laughed, and the sound filled the cabin, and outside the glass the dead colony slid past beneath them like a city asleep, and for the first time in her life Oscar felt no dread looking at it. Only witness. Only the steady weight of a thing carried well.

* * *

They were two days from Ceres, running clean and quiet, when the message came in.

It arrived on the general band, unencrypted, addressed not to the Vector or to Oscar Brightwater but to *the carrier of the Meridian record*, which meant it had been composed by someone who understood exactly how she now moved through the world. No corporate header. No inquiry-committee number. Just a set of coordinates in the deep Belt, far off any charted lane, and a single line of text:

*There are others like me. Other records. Other silences, older than yours. We heard what you did at the edge relay, and we are asking: will you carry ours too?*

Oscar read it twice. Then she pulled up the coordinates and found they pointed at a region of space where, according to every official chart she owned, there was nothing at all.

"Quentin," she said slowly. "How many stations officially never existed?"

A pause—the old pause, the confession-pause, though it carried a different weight now, almost eager.

"More than one," he said.

She looked at the empty coordinates, then at the scarred and honest hull of her ship, and began, deliberately, to plot the burn.
