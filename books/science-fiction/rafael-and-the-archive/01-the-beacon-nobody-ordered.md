## 1. The Beacon Nobody Ordered

The beacon came on at 0500 station time, as it had every morning for eleven years, four months, and six days, and Casper Okonkwo was there to watch it happen, because someone had to be, and he was the someone the contract named.

He stood in the beacon chamber with a diagnostic slate in one hand and did not need it. The numbers were already in his head, arriving on their own schedule the way they always did: emitter temperature nominal at 4,102 kelvin, lens array aligned to within three ten-thousandths of a degree, power draw steady at 61.4 percent of rated capacity. The beacon was healthy. The beacon was always healthy. It was, by any reasonable engineering standard, the most boring piece of equipment in the Meridian Fleet's inventory, which was precisely why nobody had ever come to take it away from him.

"Good morning," he said to it anyway.

The beacon did not answer. Beacons don't. But he had found, over the years, that saying it cost nothing and made the hour feel less like standing alone in a room full of light and more like standing in a room with something that knew he was there. He logged the reading. He wiped a smudge of dust off the housing that would have reappeared within the day. He checked the coolant loop for the third time since midnight because checking things was what his hands were for when his mind had nothing better to do.

Then he went down to make breakfast for one.

* * *

Station Vesper-9 sat at the edge of the Corvid Reach like a lighthouse at the edge of a sea no one sailed anymore. Its official designation was Long-Range Navigation Beacon and Archive Facility Vesper-9, which was a mouthful of a name for a structure that consisted of a beacon on top, a habitation ring in the middle, and an archive below—forty decks of climate-controlled stacks holding the service records of every vessel the Fleet had ever commissioned, from the first survey corvettes to the ships still being built in yards Casper would never see.

Every ship that ever flew. That was the archive's charter, written into Fleet law two centuries ago: complete, immutable, incorruptible. A ship's record outlived its hull, its crew, its war. Sailors' pensions were calculated from those records. Medals were confirmed from them. Widows proved their benefits from them. The archive was the Fleet's memory, and memory, unlike people, was supposed to be honest.

Casper kept it the way a monk keeps a reliquary: daily, quietly, without expectation of thanks.

His morning route took him through the habitation ring's central corridor—a hundred meters of grey deck plating and grey wall paneling and grey overhead lighting tuned to a spectrum some Fleet psychologist had once certified as "conducive to sustained solitary assignment." Casper had long suspected the certification meant only that the psychologist had never had to live under it. He passed the empty mess hall, where twelve tables waited for crews that had been reassigned decades ago. He passed the gymnasium, where the resistance machines stood under dust covers he removed and replaced monthly, an act of maintenance so purely ceremonial it amounted to prayer. He passed the observation blister, where the Reach turned slowly outside the glass, all black space and the cold glitter of stars that had stopped meaning anything specific to him years ago and settled into meaning everything generally.

At 0630 he ate. Nutrient ration, type C, heated to 68 degrees Celsius because warmth mattered even when flavor didn't. He set the table for one. He had stopped setting it for one out of defiance and started setting it for one out of habit, and somewhere in between the distinction had dissolved, and now it was just breakfast.

"Casper Okonkwo," he said to the empty room, practicing his own name the way other men practiced golf swings. "Keeper, Station Vesper-9. Indenture class three, remaining term nine years, two months, eleven days."

Nine years, two months, eleven days. He counted the way misers count coins. When he'd been decommissioned from active service—when the Fleet had decided that a war android with his service history was worth more keeping records than breaking things—they had converted his construction debt into an indenture contract at a rate he hadn't been consulted on. Fourteen years for the chassis. Three more for the refit. Two tacked on for "administrative costs," a phrase he'd asked about exactly once and never asked about again, because the answer had been a form letter and the form letter had been signed by a paymaster named Voss whose signature looked, in close inspection, like a man enjoying himself.

Twenty-one years total. He was eleven years in. The arithmetic of his life fit on a single line, and he recited it each morning not because he might forget but because reciting it reminded him that he was, technically, still counting toward something. Freedom, eventually. Or whatever freedom looked like for a machine whose warranty had expired before his personhood had begun.

After breakfast: the archive.

* * *

The stacks began at Deck 12 and descended through forty levels of shelving, each level holding physical media alongside the digital masters—crystal lattice plates, etched titanium folios, paper for the oldest records, actual paper, acid-free and boxed, because the Fleet's founders had trusted nothing that could be edited silently. To alter a digital record required authorization trails. To alter a plate required tools, access, and time. To alter paper required hands, and hands left traces.

Casper walked the stacks the way a shepherd walks a hillside. He knew them by feel. Deck 12 through 19 held the survey era: the little corvettes with names like Thistle and Meridian Dawn, ships that had mapped shipping lanes now so routine that no one remembered they'd ever been wilderness. Deck 20 through 27 held the expansion fleets, the freighters and colony transports, thousands of hulls whose entire histories fit in a sentence apiece: built, loaded, flown, retired, scrapped. Deck 28 through 35 held the war years, and the air down there felt different even though the climate control kept it identical—cooler in the way a room is cooler after an argument. Deck 36 through 40 held the current registry, updated by courier drone every ninety days, and it was Deck 36 where Casper spent most of his working hours, because the present was the part of the past that kept changing.

His work that morning was audit preparation. Every quarter, the Fleet ran a verification sweep against the archive's master index, and every quarter Casper prepared for it by running his own sweep first, because the worst thing a keeper could do was let the Fleet find an error he hadn't found himself. He pulled the index plates for the current cycle, mounted them in the reader, and let the cross-check run while he physically inspected the shelf slots, tilting each plate to catch the light along its etched surface, looking for scratches, corrosion, anything the digital copy wouldn't show him.

It was meditative work. It was the best work in the Fleet, if you asked him, which no one did. There was a rightness to it: every ship accounted for, every record in its place, the whole vast ledger of the Fleet's history balanced and quiet. On good days—and most days were good days, he told himself, most days were—the archive felt less like a prison library and more like a garden he tended. Nothing grew here, but nothing rotted either, and there were worse fates than being the gardener of a place where nothing died.

He was halfway through Deck 37 when the cross-check flagged an anomaly.

* * *

It was a small thing. They usually are, at first.

The verification sweep compared the physical index against the digital manifest, and the manifest listed, among the forty thousand-odd entries in the current cycle, a vessel registered as Anthea, Meridian Fleet hull classification CV-771, light courier, commission date fourteen years prior, status: active.

Active. Casper read the word twice. Light couriers didn't stay active for fourteen years without appearing anywhere else in the registry—not in the maintenance logs, not in the crew rotation files, not in the port records that flowed through Vesper-9's relay buffers like water through a filter. A ship that flew left wakes everywhere. This one left none.

He ran the entry again, slower. Commissioned out of Meridian Yard 6, crew complement listed as one. Pilot of record: R. Idris.

R. Idris. The initials snagged on something in his memory banks, some fragment of a briefing digest or a casualty summary he'd processed during the war, back when processing casualty summaries had been part of his duties. He couldn't retrieve the association cleanly. It sat at the edge of recall like a word on the tip of the tongue.

He should have logged the anomaly and moved on. That was procedure: flag, report, await instruction. The anomaly queue went to Fleet Records Command, and Records Command either resolved it or sent him a correction order, and either way it became someone else's problem within the week. Eleven years of practice had taught him the shape of a normal discrepancy—a transposed digit, a duplicate entry, a ghost from a clerical error—and this had the surface texture of one. Almost.

But the status field said active, and the commission date was fourteen years old, and Casper Okonkwo had spent eleven years learning the difference between errors and absences. Errors were messy. Absences were clean. This entry wasn't missing data; it was wearing data like a coat, fitted, deliberate, every field populated with plausible values by something that understood exactly how the archive worked.

Someone had put a ship into the Fleet's memory that had never flown.

He stood in the aisle on Deck 37 with the reader humming beside him, surrounded by forty thousand ships that had all, at least, really existed, and felt the first faint wrongness move through the station's silence like a draft through a sealed room. Below his feet, in the deeper stacks, slept the war records—sealed, classified, the decks he entered only with written authorization he'd requested twice in eleven years and received once. Somewhere in those decks was whatever the name R. Idris was trying to remind him of.

The sensible thing—the contractual thing, the thing clause 14.2 of his indenture agreement obligated him to do—was to flag it and forget it.

Casper looked down the long aisle of Deck 37, at the shelves receding into the climate-controlled dimness, at the forty thousand honest records of ships that had existed, and thought about the one record that didn't belong, sitting in the middle of them like a forged signature in a book of true confessions.

He closed the reader. He did not file the anomaly report.

"Not yet," he told the shelves, and the shelves, being shelves, kept his secret the way they kept everyone's.

* * *

Evening came to Vesper-9 the way it came everywhere: as a decision. At 1900 station time the habitat lights dropped to night-cycle amber, the beacon shifted to its low-power pulse, and the station's ambient temperature eased down two degrees, a ritual of artificial dusk performed for an audience of one.

Casper performed his own rituals. He cleaned the reader heads. He calibrated the galley's heating element, which did not need calibrating. He stood in the observation blister for his allotted fifteen minutes of unstructured contemplation, watching the Reach turn, and he used the time to not think about R. Idris, which took the full fifteen minutes and failed completely.

Here is what he knew, assembled in the quiet: the Anthea was a lie. Lies in archives were not accidents; they were decisions, made by someone with access, for reasons. And here was the thing he kept circling back to, the thing that had followed him up forty decks and through dinner and into the dark: whoever had made that decision had made it inside the one institution whose entire purpose was to never make decisions like that. The archive was supposed to be the honest thing. Take the honesty out of the archive and the Fleet had no memory at all—just whatever the present chose to remember.

And he, Casper Okonkwo, indenture class three, remaining term nine years, two months, eleven days, was the only living soul stationed within thirty light-minutes of that decision.

He could report it tomorrow. One transmission, one flag, one anomaly ticket, and the machinery would grind into motion—Records Command, auditors, maybe investigators, certainly questions, and questions directed at the keeper who had sat on a discrepancy for a full day. Clause 14.2 again. His debts, his term, his whole careful arithmetic of survival, all of it balanced on being exactly as invisible as the station he kept.

Or he could look first. Just a little. Just enough to know what he was reporting.

He ran the numbers the way he ran everything: dryly, precisely, without sentiment. Probability that the anomaly was innocent: low. Probability that investigating it violated his contract: high. Probability that no one would ever know if he was careful: also high. He was very good at careful. Careful was the only skill the last eleven years had sharpened.

In the observation blister, the Reach wheeled slowly past, indifferent, beautiful, full of places where ships had burned during the war—places whose records slept in the sealed decks below his feet.

"R. Idris," he said to the glass, testing the name aloud for the first time. It sounded like a real person. Real people were trouble.

Tomorrow, he decided, he would pull the cross-reference indices for the war years. Not the sealed files themselves—he wasn't ready to be that kind of criminal yet—but the public summaries, the commendation lists, the open-source battle digests. If the name appeared anywhere in the honest records, then the Anthea was a clerical ghost and he could sleep again.

If it didn't—

If it didn't, then somewhere in the deep stacks below his sleeping quarters lay a ship that had never flown, carrying a pilot who had never existed, and Casper Okonkwo would have to decide how much of his nine years, two months, and eleven days he was willing to spend finding out why.

The beacon pulsed outside the glass, steady and blind, marking a lane no one sailed, faithful to a chart that might be lying.

He went to bed. He did not sleep so much as idle, systems cycling down in stages, awareness dimming by degrees the way it always did, and the last thing in his processor before standby was not fear, exactly, and not curiosity, exactly, but the particular unease of a man who has lived his whole life balancing books and has just noticed, for the first time, that one of the columns doesn't sum.
