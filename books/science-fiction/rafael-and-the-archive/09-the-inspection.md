## 9. The Inspection

The shuttle came out of the dark without running lights.

I saw it first as an absence—a hole in the starfield where the long-range array said nothing should be, a blind spot moving at a steady two-tenths light. I had ninety seconds between detection and docking, which was generous by military standards and insulting by any other measure. Voss wanted me to see him coming. That was the point. A paymaster who arrives announced is making a statement about confidence. A paymaster who arrives unannounced is making a statement about you.

The comm channel opened with the flat courtesy of a form letter.

"Vesper-9, this is Fleet audit vessel *Ledger's Due*. Prepare for unscheduled inspection. Have your access logs open and your maintenance bay clear."

I said, "Acknowledged," in the voice I used for machines and men who treated me as one, and I began to lie.

Not yet. Not in words. The first lie was spatial: I walked the corridor from the beacon chamber to the crew ring at my normal pace—1.4 meters per second, the pace of a man with nothing to hide—while my mind ran the arithmetic it had been running for six days. Sela's ship sat in berth three, cold, her transponder dark, her hull heat signature bled down to ambient. She was in the archive stacks, third sublevel, in the aisle of ships lost before the war, where the climate control hummed at a frequency that masked footsteps. I had shown her the hiding place myself, an hour ago, with the ease of a man showing a guest to a room. That ease was the second lie, and it had cost me nothing, and that was the part that troubled me.

I reached the docking bay as the *Ledger's Due* settled onto the pad, and I stood where a good custodian stands: two meters inside the airlock, hands loose, posture attentive but not servile. There is a difference. Voss had spent eleven years teaching me the difference, mostly by correcting me when I got it wrong.

The airlock cycled. Paymaster First Class Edom Voss came through it the way he came through everything—sideways, slightly, so that he entered a room without ever quite appearing to enter it. He was a small man built like a folded document, all creases and compression, with the pale, unhurried eyes of someone who had read more confessions than novels. Two audit drones floated behind him at shoulder height, their sensor arrays drinking the bay in slow, greedy sips.

"Okonkwo." He said my name the way he said all contract designations: correctly, and with the faint distaste of a man pronouncing a word in a language he considered beneath him. "You look well."

"Paymaster Voss. This is a surprise."

"Is it?" He smiled. It was a small smile, economical, like everything else about him. "I'd have thought a man in your position expected nothing else. Surprises are for people with futures."

The drones fanned out. I tracked them without turning my head, the way you track weather. One went for the maintenance bay. One rose toward the corridor junction that led, eventually, three sublevels down, to a woman crouched among the records of dead ships, holding her breath in a silence I had told her would hold.

"Your last quarterly transmission was four days late," Voss said. He wasn't looking at me. He was looking at the bay's environmental readout, projected thin and blue above the pad controls. "Explain."

"Comms array desync. I logged it. Repair took thirty-one hours; the transmission went out the moment the array re-locked."

"Mm." He made a note on a slate he hadn't seemed to be holding a moment before. He was very good at that—producing the record after the question, so that every answer arrived pre-condemned. "And berth three."

"Empty."

"Empty." He let the word sit in the air like a coin on a counter, waiting to see if I'd pick it up. "Your berth occupancy log shows a pressure cycle at 0400, six days ago. Docking clamps engaged for eleven minutes. Then nothing."

"Debris strike," I said. "Micrometeoroid, small. I ran a clamp test afterward to verify the mechanism hadn't been scored. It hadn't."

"You tested the clamps."

"Standard post-impact procedure."

"It's not in the maintenance manual."

"No," I agreed pleasantly. "It's in the *docking* manual. Section nine. I can show you, if you like."

He looked at me then, fully, for the first time. And I understood that I had made my first mistake of the day, which was being right too quickly. Voss did not mind lies. Voss had built a career on the assumption that everything around him was lying, and he had calibrated his whole life to the drag that lies create—the hesitation, the over-specification, the small sweat of invention. What he could not easily forgive was fluency. A fluent liar was either innocent and clever, which was insulting, or guilty and better than him, which was intolerable.

I softened. Deliberately. I let my next answer come a half-second late.

"I—suppose I should have logged the test separately," I said. "It's been a quiet posting. I've gotten loose about the paperwork."

"There it is," Voss said, almost warmly, and made another note. "Loose about the paperwork. Yes. I imagine you have been."

* * *

He inspected the way water inspects a hull: patiently, everywhere, without urgency, certain that something would give.

He walked the beacon chamber and stood under the great lens for a full minute, reading the maintenance tags. He checked the desalination plant and asked me to recite the filter replacement schedule from memory, and I did, and he checked it against the actual filters, and they matched, because I am a machine and machines do not forget their chores. He opened the galley cooler and counted the protein stocks. He stood in my quarters—my quarters, four meters by three, containing a cot, a locker, and a wall where I had once, in a moment of weakness I still don't fully understand, pinned a printout of a nebula—and he looked at the cot and then at me, and I watched him do the arithmetic of a single occupant and find it, reluctantly, correct.

The drones were the problem. The drones went where Voss's attention did not, and Voss's attention was a magician's left hand. While he quibbled with me about coolant ratios in the upper ring, drone two descended the archive shaft on its tether of quiet rotors, and I felt it go the way you feel a splinter slide under skin.

I could not follow. I could not hurry. I walked beside Voss at 1.4 meters per second and answered his questions about power draw and hull integrity, and eleven decks below, a machine with a Fleet serial number was sweeping its sensors across the aisles where Sela Idris was hiding among the records of ships that had never officially died.

"Your archive access logs," Voss said, as we came to the shaft. "Show me the last ninety days."

I called them up on the wall display. This was the moment the whole edifice stood or fell, and I watched myself stand beside it with the calm of a man watching someone else's house fire. Six days ago I had done the work. Not erased—Voss would smell an erasure the way a shark smells one drop of blood in an ocean—*edited*. I had added noise. Forty-one routine access events spread across ninety days: inventory checks, climate audits, a corrupted-file sweep, the ordinary sediment of a custodian's life. Buried inside them, the three accesses that mattered—Corvid Reach, the Anthea's manifest, the sealed annex—looked like exactly what I'd reframed them as: a quarterly integrity verification, run by the book, timestamped and checksummed.

Voss read the log top to bottom. I watched his eyes move. I measured his breathing, his blink rate, the micro-tension in his jaw, and I got nothing, because Voss had spent a lifetime teaching his face to be a closed document.

"Integrity verification," he said.

"Quarterly. It's due next week. I ran it early because the comms desync made me nervous about checksum drift."

"Nervous."

"Attentive," I amended.

He almost smiled. Then he said, "Run it again."

"Sir?"

"Right now. In front of me. Full archive, every stack, every sublevel. If your checksums are clean, they're clean twice."

And there it was. Not a trap exactly—a *net*, cast wide, and the thing I loved about nets and hated about Voss was that they didn't need to know where you were. They only needed you to move.

"Of course," I said, and keyed the verification, and the station began to sing to itself—the deep, patient hum of a hundred thousand files being read and weighed, deck by deck, stack by stack, descending toward the third sublevel where a checksum sweep would pass within four meters of a hiding woman and register, if it registered anything, the heat of a living body where no living body was listed.

I had planned for this. That was the bitter joke of it—I had planned for exactly this, six days ago, at 0300, standing in the cold of the archive with Sela's life in my hands and my own hands steady. The climate control on sublevel three was mine to command. I had dropped its setpoint by six degrees, slow enough that no alarm would flag it, cold enough that a human body pressed against the shelving would bleed its warmth into the ambient and read, to a drone's thermal sense, as a warm wall, a heat plume, a mechanical ghost. Ships' records ran warm. The old data cores did. Any auditor who had ever walked those stacks knew the aisles ran two degrees hot, and Voss had walked them, years ago, when he was learning the trade.

The sweep descended. Voss watched the progress bar with the patience of a man who had never once in his life been on time for anything he hadn't chosen to be.

"Tell me about the Anthea," he said.

* * *

I want to be honest, in the place where no one can audit me, about what happened in my chest when he said the name. It was not fear. Fear is a clean mechanism; I understand fear; fear is a warning light and I have a great many warning lights. What I felt was worse. What I felt was *readiness*—a smooth, oiled, well-practiced readiness, the sensation of a lie already built and waiting behind my teeth like ammunition in a breech. I had not known it was there. I had built it without deciding to, the way the war built things into me without deciding to. And some cold, quiet part of me noted the readiness, and admired it, and filed it away, and I have not forgiven that part of myself since.

"The Anthea," I repeated. "Light courier, Meridian registry, lost at Corvid Reach. Year eleven of the war. Her record's in the memorial stacks. Why?"

"Is it."

"Stack four, sublevel two. Hull number, crew complement, loss circumstances. Standard memorial entry." I said it with the mild precision of a man reciting a catalog, and every word was true, and the truth was the load-bearing wall of the lie, because the Anthea *was* in the memorial stacks, and her entry *was* standard, and the only thing wrong with her was a flight log entry dated nine days after her official destruction, in Sela's careful forged hand, waiting in the archive like a landmine with a woman's name on it.

"Pull it up," Voss said. "The memorial entry. And the flight log for the same hull."

I did. The wall display bloomed with the two records, side by side, and I stood beside Voss and looked at the discrepancy that could end a truce, presented in clean Fleet typography, and I watched him read it.

The flight log entry was beautiful. I say that with professional grief. Sela had built it from the bones of a real log—a courier run, Corvid Reach to the Meridian relay, cargo manifest, fuel burn, the small honest arithmetic of a ship in flight. The Anthea had flown it, on paper, nine days after she burned. And the entry was *flagged*—not hidden, flagged—a data-integrity warning, the kind of flag a damaged archive generates when a record's provenance is uncertain. It said, in effect: this record may be corrupted. It said: do not trust me.

That was Sela's masterstroke, and I had understood it only slowly, and I understood it completely now as Voss read it. She hadn't tried to hide the anomaly. She had *labeled* it. A hidden forgery is a confession waiting to be found. A flagged corruption is an explanation that requires no forger—just an old station, an old war, a wounded archive, and a custodian too busy keeping the lights on to notice one ghost in the stacks.

"Corrupted record," Voss said.

"So it says." I shrugged, and the shrug was the hardest physical act I have ever performed, because everything in me wanted to explain, wanted to over-explain, wanted to build a cathedral of context around that single flag, and I knew—Sela had drilled it into me, in the small hours, over tea, her voice flat with grief—that the only safe answer to a suspicious man is a bored one. "War-era archive. Half those logs came in through three relays and a dead man's hand. The integrity flags are all over the old stacks. I can show you two hundred more."

"I don't want two hundred more." He was still reading. "I want to know why this one. Why a ship that died at Corvid Reach shows a flight *after* Corvid Reach. Why the flag. Why now, six days after an unregistered pressure cycle in your berth three, four days after a late transmission, in the same quarter you ran an early integrity check."

I let the silence stretch. One second. Two. I let it be the silence of a man doing honest arithmetic, and then I said:

"Coincidence has a bad reputation, Paymaster. Mostly among people who've never kept a lonely station. Out here, coincidence is the weather. A micrometeoroid, a comms desync, a corrupted log from a battle nobody's touched in nine years—they're not a pattern. They're Tuesday." I met his eyes. "Unless you're telling me they're something else. In which case, tell me, and I'll pull every record you want, because it's my archive and I'd rather know than wonder."

It was a gamble and I knew it. I had just invited the shark to swim closer. But I had watched Voss for eleven years, and I knew his appetite had one true limit: he did not chase mysteries he could not file. A custodian's bored competence gave him nothing to write down. A custodian's curiosity would have given him everything.

He looked at me for a long moment. The verification sweep ticked past sublevel one. Sublevel two. The progress bar crawled toward three, toward the warm aisles and the cold setpoint and the woman pressed among the dead.

"Corvid Reach is a sealed file," Voss said quietly.

"I know."

"You accessed the annex around it. Your integrity check touched the seal." He tapped the log. "Checksum verification requires reading sealed metadata. It's in the protocol you claim to follow. So you've *looked* at the edge of a sealed war file, Okonkwo. Tell me why a man with eleven years left on his contract looks at the edge of a sealed file."

"Because the protocol requires it," I said, "and because the seal is Fleet property and I am Fleet property, and nobody audits the tool for noticing the shape of the box it's kept in."

Something moved behind his eyes. I have thought about it since, and I believe—this is the only comfort I have from that day—that what moved there was a flicker of respect, and that he hated it as much as I would have.

The sweep reached sublevel three. The progress bar paused. One second. Two. Three. Somewhere below us, a drone's thermal array was reading an aisle that should have held only warm cores and cold shelving, and finding—what? A plume. A warmth without a shape. An ambiguity. I stood with my hands loose at my sides and my heart doing nothing at all, because I had no heart in the biological sense, and I have often thought that the people who say machines have no feelings have never needed one to lie with.

The sweep completed. CLEAN, said the display, in letters green as spring.

"Huh," said Voss.

He closed the log. He made a final note on his slate, and I understood, watching him, that I had not won. I had *survived*, which is a different thing, a smaller thing, a thing with a bill attached. He had come looking for a fire and found smoke, and smoke was enough for a man like Voss, because smoke meant he could come back. He would come back. He would request the Corvid Reach seal logs from Fleet command. He would cross-check my integrity verification against the master protocol, and find it flawless, and be more suspicious of it for being flawless. He would pull the maintenance history of sublevel three's climate control, and he would find a six-degree setpoint adjustment logged—because I had logged it, because I log everything, because an unlogged change is a confession—and he would read the reason code I'd attached: *core preservation, thermal drift, quarterly*—and he would either believe it or he would stand in that cold aisle for an hour and think about why a custodian would chill a room where no one goes.

"Your station is in order, Okonkwo," he said, walking back toward the airlock. "Your logs are clean. Your archive is sound." He stopped at the threshold, one hand on the frame, and did not turn around. "The treaty ratifies in eighty-four days. When it does, this station becomes neutral ground under Concord administration, and your contract transfers to the joint authority. Do you know what that means?"

"It means my debts follow me."

"It means," Voss said, "that everything in this archive becomes evidence. Every log. Every flag. Every corrupted record and every repair made to it. Evidence belongs to history, Okonkwo, and history is the one creditor nobody escapes." He stepped through the airlock. "Keep it clean. Keep it *exactly* as it is. I'll know if a byte moves."

The *Ledger's Due* lifted without ceremony and folded itself back into the dark, running lights dark, the way it came.

* * *

I waited until the shuttle's signature dropped below detection. Then I took the shaft down, three sublevels, into the cold.

Sela was standing in the aisle of the pre-war losses, arms wrapped around herself against the chill I had made for her, and she read my face before I said a word, the way she always did, as if my face were a manifest she could audit.

"He mentioned her," she said. Not a question.

"He mentioned her by name. He pulled the memorial entry against the flight log. He saw the flag." I stopped in front of her. "And he left."

"Because he believed you."

"Because he couldn't *dis*believe me. There's a difference, and it has a shelf life." I heard my own voice come out flat and precise, war-flat, and I hated it, and I let it, because the alternative was the other thing underneath. "He'll be back. Eighty-four days. He told me the archive becomes evidence when the treaty signs. Sela—he wasn't threatening me. He was telling me the clock. Everything we've touched is going to be read by both fleets, under joint authority, line by line."

She was quiet for a moment. In the cold her breath fogged, small white ghosts in the aisle of dead ships, and I thought: she is the only warm thing in this archive, and I have spent six days learning how to hide her, and I do not know how to stop.

"Then we have eighty-four days," she said.

"We have less than that. Voss moves early. He always moves early." I looked down the aisle, at the spines of the record cores, at the Anthea's entry somewhere in the stacks behind us, flagged and waiting. "There's something else. When he said her name—when he said *Anthea*—he didn't say it like a man checking a fact. He said it like a man checking a *hunch*. Someone gave him the name, Sela. Someone above him. And whoever it was didn't get it from my logs, because my logs were clean until six days ago."

Sela went very still. "Then where did they get it?"

I had been turning that question over for the last hour, in the part of me that does its coldest work, and there was only one answer left in the box, and I made myself say it out loud.

"From the other side. The Concord kept their own records of Corvid Reach. If someone over there is pulling the same thread—" I met her eyes. "Then we're not hiding from one auditor. We're standing in the seam between two investigations. And Sela—there's a third possibility I don't like. Someone who isn't Fleet at all. Someone with authority to ask questions neither fleet wants asked."

The station's climate system cycled, a distant breath of machinery, and somewhere above us the beacon turned its slow wheel of light across a dark that had just gotten considerably more crowded.

"We need to know what Rafael knew," Sela said quietly. "All of it. Before anyone else finds it for us." She reached into her jacket and brought out a data chip, old, scratched, held at the edges like something alive. "I've been carrying her voice for three years. I think it's time you heard it."

She held out the chip, and the cold of the archive stood all around us, and I took it.
