## 10. Rafael, In Fragments

The archive stacks smell like cold metal and old paper, which is a strange thing to say about a place that's mostly data. But Casper keeps physical copies too—printouts, microfilm, actual bound manifests in crates—and the smell of them is the smell of my sister's whole life, compressed into a room neither fleet wants anyone to enter.

I set the player down on a crate of Corvid Reach requisition forms and don't touch it for a full minute.

Casper stands at the end of the aisle, hands folded, waiting. He's good at waiting. He's an android; waiting is probably a feature. But there's something in the way he holds still that isn't machine patience. It's the stillness of someone who knows he's about to be handed something heavy and has decided, in advance, not to flinch.

"You don't have to watch," I say.

"I know," he says. "I'd like to."

I'd like to. Nobody says that. Nobody means it, either, usually. But he'd sat through my interrogation of his logs for six hours without blinking, and when I finally told him the truth—that I forged the manifest entry, that the Anthea never flew, that I put my dead sister's name on a flight log dated after her destruction just to see if anyone would notice—he hadn't called security, hadn't called the paymaster, hadn't done any of the ten things he was contractually obligated to do. He'd made tea. Bad tea. And he'd said, *Tell me about her.*

So here we are.

"Her name was Rafael," I say, and my voice comes out wrong, too formal, like I'm reading a eulogy. "Not Raffi, not Rafa—she'd break your arm. Rafael. Our mother won a bet with a cardinal."

I press play before I can talk myself out of it.

* * *

The first recording is dated eleven months before Corvid Reach. Rafael is in her bunk on the *Anthea*, helmet off, hair stuck to her forehead with sweat, still in her flight harness. She's talking to the camera like it's a person, which is how she talked to everything.

*"—and so Voss's office sends down this correction order, right? Casualty figures from the Kettle engagement, revised downward. Revised. Downward. We lost nine pilots at the Kettle. I carried two of them out myself. And the new official number is four."*

She laughs, but it's the wrong kind of laugh.

*"Four. Like they rounded down. Like you can round down human beings. I'm filing a discrepancy report. Sela, if you're watching this someday because I'm dead—hi. Sorry. I told you I'd be careful. I lied. I'm not going to be careful, because somebody has to count, and everyone else has stopped counting."*

I stop the recording.

Casper hasn't moved. "The Kettle engagement," he says quietly. "That's in the archive. Meridian losses listed at four."

"Nine," I say. "I know. She told me. I didn't believe her at first. That's the part that keeps me up at night—not what they did to her. That when she first told me, I said, *Raf, you're tired, you're seeing patterns.*" I press play again. "Keep going. It gets worse."

* * *

The second recording is three weeks later. She's in a maintenance corridor somewhere, camera handheld, the image shaking. She's whispering.

*"Okay. So the discrepancy report came back. You want to know what came back? A commendation. For my 'attention to administrative detail.' They gave me a medal for noticing and a direct order to stop noticing in the same week. And here's the thing—here's the thing—it's not just Meridian. I pulled a captured Concord flight recorder from the salvage bay, declassified, sitting right there like it's nothing. Concord lost eleven at the Kettle. Their official number is five."*

She looks directly into the camera. Rafael always looked directly into cameras. She believed in being seen.

*"Both sides are doing it. Same math, same method. They're shaving the dead off the books so the peace numbers work. Somebody did the arithmetic on what the truce public would tolerate and decided my friends were a rounding error. And the worst part—the genuinely worst part, Sela—is that they're right. If this comes out, the truce dies. If the truce dies, the war starts again. So what do you do? What's the right call when the lie is the only thing holding up the sky?"*

Her voice cracks on *sky*. She was twenty-six. She shouldn't have had to ask that question.

I don't look at Casper. I can't. In my peripheral vision he is very still, and I understand now that the stillness isn't neutral. He's a Meridian asset. He's indentured to the fleet that did this. Every word my sister recorded is a word against his owners, and he's standing in their archive, listening, choosing not to leave.

That's something. I'm not ready to say what.

* * *

The third recording is the last one. It's dated nine days before Corvid Reach.

She's calm. That's the thing that undoes me, every time. She's calm the way people are calm when they've stopped expecting to be rescued. She's sitting in the *Anthea*'s cockpit, pre-flight checks running behind her on the board, and she has a data chit in her hand, turning it over.

*"I found the source document. The master reconciliation ledger. Both commands sign off on the final casualty figures—joint signature, Meridian and Concord, same page. It's not a fleet doing this to the other fleet. It's both fleets doing it to their own people, together, so the treaty math works. Admiral Cheung signed for Meridian. I have the chit. I'm going to transmit it to the tribunal oversight office tonight, and then I'm flying the Corvid Reach patrol like nothing's changed, because if I act changed they'll know, and if they know I'll die before the transmission lands."*

A pause. She smiles, and it's her real smile, the crooked one, the one from the lake house.

*"Sela. Little sister. If you're watching this, then the transmission didn't save me, but the chit's in my flight bag's false bottom, and you're the only person alive who knows about the false bottom, so. You know what to do. Don't be careful either. Be smart. Careful is what they train into you. Smart is what you do anyway."*

*"I love you. Count everything."*

The recording ends.

The stacks are silent. Somewhere overhead, the beacon turns its slow circle, and I hate it a little, that sound, the patient heartbeat of a station that kept perfect records of every ship that ever flew while the fleet erased my sister from its books like a typo.

"False bottom," Casper says.

"Flight bag's in my ship," I say. "The chit's real. I've had it for two years. Do you know why I haven't sent it?" I laugh, and it comes out ugly. "Because I did the same math she did. If this comes out, the truce dies. I'm a coward with better information."

"You're not a coward."

"You don't know that. You've known me four days."

"I've known you four days," he says, "and I've watched you forge fleet documents, bluff a docking inspection, and sit in the archive of the people who erased your sister, reading their files page by page, alone, at night. Cowards don't do that. Cowards send anonymous tips and go home."

I want to hit him. I want to thank him. The two impulses sit in my chest like passengers who hate each other.

"The tribunal oversight office," he says. "The one she transmitted to."

"Never acknowledged receipt. I checked, after. Filed a records request under three different names. The transmission was received—there's a log stamp—and then it vanished. The office that was supposed to catch this was part of it. Or scared of it. Same thing, functionally."

"So the evidence exists. The chit. The recordings. The reconciliation ledger, if it still exists somewhere."

"It exists." I tap the crate of Corvid Reach forms. "You're sitting on top of a building full of it. That's the joke, Casper. The truth has been in your basement the whole time. Four hundred eleven people erased from two fleets' records, and the only complete set of the real numbers is in a beacon station nobody remembers commissioning, maintained by an android the fleet treats as furniture."

He doesn't answer right away. When he does, his voice is careful, the way his voice always is when he's about to say something that costs him.

"The reconciliation ledger is in the sealed stacks. I've seen the index entry. I haven't opened it—opening it would flag my access signature, and Voss audits those."

"Then we open it anyway."

"That's not a small thing, Sela. If Voss sees—"

"If Voss sees, we're dead. If Voss doesn't see, we're dead later, but we'll know the truth first. That's the trade. That's always been the trade." I stand up. My legs are shaky and I hate that too. "You asked me to tell you about her. This is about her. She didn't die in a battle. She died in a bookkeeping decision. And the book is *right here*."

* * *

We open the sealed stacks at 0300 station time, because if you're going to commit a crime, Casper says, you do it during the window when the relay lag to Fleet Command is longest and Voss is asleep in his orbital office. He's thought about this. He's thought about the mechanics of his own captivity so thoroughly that he knows its maintenance schedule, and something about that is so sad and so practical that I almost kiss him, which is a completely inappropriate thing to feel at three in the morning in a room full of erased people.

The ledger is a physical book. Of course it is. Anything that matters, both fleets keep on paper, because paper can't be hacked, only burned or stolen. It's bound in gray fleet-issue cover, and the joint signature page is exactly where Rafael said it would be.

Cheung. Meridian. And for Concord, a name I don't know—Osei, Deputy Director, Strategic Reconciliation. *Reconciliation.* They put the word *reconciliation* in the title of the office that deleted the dead. I have to put the book down for a moment and press my knuckles against my mouth.

Casper reads over my shoulder. He doesn't need to—he could probably recite the page from a single glance—but he reads it the way humans read, top to bottom, and I realize he's doing it for me. Keeping me company in the reading.

"The figures," he says. "Corvid Reach. Meridian official: thirty-one lost. Concord official: twenty-eight." He turns pages with a gentleness that seems almost reverent. "The reconciliation column. Meridian actual: fifty-seven. Concord actual: fifty-two."

One hundred nine people. In one battle. Fifty-eight of them subtracted, divided by two, laundered into silence so the treaty negotiators would have numbers they could sell.

"Rafael was one of the fifty-eight," I say.

"Yes."

"Say her name."

He looks at me. "Rafael Idris," he says. "Flight Officer, Meridian Fleet, killed in action at Corvid Reach, erased from the record by joint order." He says it like a report, but his voice does something at the edges, a roughness I haven't heard from him before, and I understand that for him, saying it aloud is also an act. Androids aren't supposed to have opinions about their owners' crimes. He's having one.

"Four hundred eleven," I say. "That's the number I've reconstructed from her recordings and the fragments she sent me over two years. Four hundred eleven across both fleets, every engagement since the truce talks started. I came here to find proof. I found a librarian instead."

"Archivist," he says.

"Librarian."

He almost smiles. It's a small thing, the almost, but I've spent four days learning his face and the almost is enormous.

Then his head tilts—just slightly, that machine tell I've learned to read—and his voice drops. "Incoming transmission. Fleet priority channel. It's—" A pause. "It's Voss. Addressed to me, marked urgent." He looks up at me, and the almost-smile is gone, and the cold comes back into the room like a tide. "Sela. The transmission is an inspection notice. Voss is coming to Vesper-9. His original inspection was scheduled for next quarter."

"When?"

"Three days."

Three days. Three days to hide a forged manifest entry, a stowaway investigator, an opened seal on a restricted stack, and a sister's ghost.

"He can't know I'm here," I say.

"He doesn't know yet," Casper says, and then, quietly, in the voice of a man deciding something: "And he's not going to."
