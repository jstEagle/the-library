## 4. The Dockmaster's Story

The thing about a name is that it waits. You can bury a ship, bury a manifest, bury two thousand people in the space of a bureaucratic footnote, and the name just sits there under the sediment like a coin in a drain, patient, until some woman with an investigator's badge and too many questions comes along and drops a magnet into the water.

Freya Sandoval said the name to me on a Tuesday. Tuesdays are for fuel reconciliations. I remember thinking that was rude of her.

"Meridian," she said again, because I hadn't answered the first time. She had that quality certain people have — the ones who notice things — where silence doesn't bounce off them, it soaks in and makes them more determined. "Registry number VSC-114. Launched from this dock. Forty-one years ago."

I set down my stylus. I looked at her. And I did what any sensible woman does when the past knocks on the door wearing a stranger's face: I lied.

"Never heard of it."

* * *

Here's what lying costs you at my age: nothing up front, everything later, compounded nightly like interest. I've been dockmaster of Vesper's Reach for nineteen years, which means I've been apologizing for this colony's existence to every freight captain, insurance auditor, and government surveyor who's bothered to come out here since the funding died. We're a line item marked *deferred*. We're a rounding error with air recyclers. There are four hundred of us left, give or take the ones who stopped answering their doors, and we keep the reactors limping and the docks lit at thirty percent because a dark dock tells the universe you've given up, and I'll be damned before this place gives up while I'm still breathing its recycled air.

So when Freya Sandoval walked into my office — a converted shipping container with a view of Bay Three and a coffee machine older than she was — I gave her the standard performance. The shrug. The squint. The *Meridian? Never heard of it* delivered with the flat conviction of a woman who has signed off on forty years of falsified tonnage reports and gotten very good at it.

She didn't buy it. Of course she didn't buy it. People who lie professionally can smell it the way sharks smell blood, except in her case it was worse, because she wasn't lying to me — she was just refusing to be lied to, which is a different instrument entirely and much harder to defend against.

"You docked a ship called the Meridian," she said, "on the fourteenth of Corvus-month, forty-one years ago. Docking fees were paid from a discretionary account. The account was closed eleven days later." She slid a printout across my desk. Actual paper. Company people love paper; it feels official and burns easily. "I'd like to know what came back."

And there it was. Not *what launched*. What came *back*.

I looked at the printout for a long time. Long enough that the coffee machine cycled through its dying wheeze and clicked off. Long enough that she understood, without another word from me, that I had heard of the Meridian after all.

"Close the door," I said.

* * *

You want the story straight? Fine. But understand that I was nine years old, and nine-year-olds don't get the whole story, they get the edges of it and spend the rest of their lives filling in the middle.

Forty-one years ago, Vesper's Reach was not this. It was loud. It was full. We had eleven thousand people and three shifts and a school with actual windows, and the company still pretended we mattered because the deep-range surveys were paying off and this rock sat at the crossroads of everything worth claiming. And then the surveys found Kepler-adjacent systems — green worlds, real ones, with weather and soil and all the things our grandmothers had promised us space would hold — and suddenly nobody wanted a waystation anymore. They wanted a destination.

The Meridian was going to be ours anyway. That's what they told us. A colony ship, the biggest hull ever fitted in our yards, two thousand berths, and the plan was this: Vesper's Reach would be the staging ground. Our engineers would crew her. Our families would fill her. We wouldn't be a forgotten waypoint forever — we'd be the mother port of a new world. Every kid in my class had a berth reserved. Mine was C-217. I had a sticker on my wall with the number on it. I used to touch it before bed like it was a prayer.

Launch day, the whole colony turned out. Eleven thousand people on the observation gantries, and the Meridian sitting in Bay One like a cathedral laid on its side, white hull, company crest, and beneath the crest — this is the part nobody believes — a second mark, painted by the yard crews without authorization. A small one. A lantern. Because the old-timers said a ship should carry its own light, in case it ever got somewhere the company's light didn't reach.

They scrubbed the lantern off before launch. Painted over it in an afternoon. I stood on my father's shoulders and watched them do it and asked him why, and he said, "Because hope isn't load-bearing, little fish," and laughed, and I didn't understand him then either.

Two thousand souls went up that ramp. My uncle Tomas among them — he was a drive engineer, third generation, hands like a saint's according to my aunt. My cousin Petra, fourteen years old, already smarter than everyone including herself. The Okafor twins' grandmother — no relation to anyone important now, but names echo strangely out here. Two thousand souls, and the manifest said *colony establishment, one-way*, and the window to the target system opened in eight months, and we stood on the gantries and cheered until our throats broke.

The Meridian never made the jump.

That's the middle of the story I spent my life filling in, and here's the shameful truth: I don't have it. Nobody has it. Here's what happened instead.

* * *

Eleven days after launch — eleven, the same number as the account closure, the company does love its tidy arithmetic — the Meridian came back.

Not landed. Came *back*. It appeared on approach telemetry at 0300 with no transponder handshake, no clearance code, no response to hails, running dark except for navigation beacons, and it slid into a parking orbit over Bay One and just hung there. The duty controller — a man named Ferro, dead now, good riddance, he drank — swore afterward that the approach vector was perfect. Machine-perfect. No pilot alive flies that clean.

They sent a boarding party at dawn. Six men. I knew two of them. They took the shuttle up and docked, and the whole colony watched the feed on public channels because nobody thought to cut it, and for about ninety seconds we saw the interior corridor through the boarding party's helmet cams.

Empty.

Not damaged-empty. Not disaster-empty. *Swept*-empty. Lights at half. Air at standard. Corridors clean enough to eat off. And no one. Two thousand berths' worth of ship and not one heartbeat on internal sensors, not one voice on comms, not one bootprint in the dust — and there *was* dust, that's the detail that lives in my teeth, because dust takes time, and that ship had been gone eleven days.

Then the feed cut. Officially, a relay fault. Unofficially — well. You know how unofficially goes.

The hull told the rest. When the salvage crews finally did a full external survey — and it took them weeks, because the company arrived within days and started *managing* — they found scorching along the drive spine. Real scorching, metallurgical, the kind you get from a drive firing far outside tolerance. Except the drive logs showed no burn. No course corrections. No jump attempt. Nothing. The flight recorder was wiped to the firmware. Not corrupted — *wiped*, cleanly, deliberately, the digital equivalent of a made bed.

Two thousand people went out. Zero came back. No bodies, no distress call, no debris field, no explanation. Just a clean, empty, scorched ship parked over our heads like a held breath.

* * *

What does a colony do with that?

I'll tell you what mine did. First it grieved, loudly, badly, for about six weeks. Then the company arrived — actual company men, suits and security details, the works — and grief became *unproductive*. There were meetings. There were compensation packages. There was a document, and I have spent forty years trying to forget the exact blandness of its language, declaring the Meridian incident — *incident*, as if it were a fender-bender — classified under commercial confidentiality pending investigation, and further declaring that the vessel in question was never formally entered into the launch registry due to a clerical error, and therefore, for all regulatory and insurance purposes, the Meridian never flew.

It never flew. Say that sentence slowly. Two thousand people from a colony of eleven thousand boarded a ship that never flew, and the ship came back empty, and the empty ship was quietly towed to a maintenance cradle in Bay Seven and sealed, and the clerical error swallowed it whole, and the company paid out settlements with gag clauses, and the investigation report was never published because there was no investigation, because you cannot investigate a ship that doesn't exist.

My aunt took the settlement. Everyone took the settlement. What else were they going to do — sue? With what lawyers? Against whom? The company's name wasn't even on the document. It was signed by a subsidiary of a subsidiary, a legal ghost signing for a ghost ship, and that, I learned at nine years old, is how the universe actually works: not with malice, but with paperwork.

My father didn't take it. He chained himself to the Bay Seven cradle for nine days. On the tenth day the company security removed him, gently, almost kindly, the way you move furniture you've decided to keep, and he came home and never spoke about the Meridian again, and neither did anyone, and that silence became a kind of civic infrastructure, load-bearing, holding up the whole rotten arrangement. You want to know what killed Vesper's Reach? Not the funding cuts. The funding cuts were just the autopsy finding. What killed us was learning that our dead could be deleted, and deciding to live inside the deletion because the alternative was unbearable.

The ship stayed in Bay Seven. Sealed. Company seals on every hatch, renewed annually by contract, right up until the contracts lapsed and the renewals quietly stopped and the seals just... remained, aging, fading, doing their work by inertia alone. Some nights, when the reactors run low and the docks go quiet, I walk past Bay Seven on my rounds. The hull still shows the scorch lines if you bring a lamp close. And once — once, maybe fifteen years ago, I swear this, and I was sober — the seal on Hatch Four was warm.

I told myself it was a heat conduit. I told myself that story for fifteen years. It's a good story. It kept me sane.

* * *

Freya Sandoval listened to all of it without moving. That unnerved me more than interruption would have. Most listeners fidget; she just absorbed, the way dry ground absorbs rain, and I kept waiting for the puddle of reaction and it never came.

"The manifest," she said finally. "The crew list. Do you have a copy?"

"The company collected them. All of them."

"But not all copies."

I looked at her for a long moment. Then I opened the drawer under my desk — the one I tell people is broken, the one that isn't — and took out a data chip older than she was, in a casing cracked down one side, wrapped in a scrap of launch-day bunting that my mother sewed.

"My uncle worked the manifest office," I said. "He made backups. He said a list of names is the only scripture that matters, because it's the only one God can't redact." I put the chip in her palm and closed her fingers over it. "There's something else. Something you should hear from me before you find it yourself, because you will — you're the type."

She waited.

"When the boarding party's feed cut out, ninety seconds in — before it cut, the last frame—" I stopped. Started again. Forty years and it still sticks in the throat like a fishbone. "The last frame showed the cryo bay door. Bank One. The civilian bank. Children and elders go in Bank One, always, that's protocol. And the door indicator read *occupied*. Nine hundred life signs, stable. The boarding party reported it. It's in the surviving fragments of their transmission log — the company missed one fragment, or didn't think a nine-year-old's uncle would copy it."

"And Bank Two?"

"Bank Two read empty. Three hundred essential crew, and it read *empty*." I met her eyes. "They chose, Ms. Sandoval. Out there, wherever they went, someone or something chose which half of my people would sleep and which half would vanish. And whatever did the choosing brought the ship home and erased itself so neatly that the whole affair could be filed under clerical error." I leaned back. My coffee had gone cold hours ago; I drank it anyway, because ritual matters. "Officially, none of this happened. Unofficially, it's the only thing that did. Now you know why I lied to you."

Freya closed her hand around the chip. For the first time since she'd walked in, she looked less like an investigator and more like a woman carrying something heavy uphill.

"There were three engineers scheduled to inspect the sister ship tomorrow," she said quietly. "Maintenance bay twelve. I flagged the inspection request myself."

Something cold walked down my spine, the old cold, the Bay Seven cold, the warm-seal cold.

"Then unflag it," I said.

She didn't answer. She was already looking past me, out the window, toward the lights of the maintenance ring — and I have been dockmaster long enough to know what it means when a person who notices things goes quiet like that.

It means they've noticed something new.
