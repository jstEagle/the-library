## 10. First Light, First Harvest

The first thing I learned about grief is that it keeps a schedule.

Felix Joubert died on Consolidation Day at 14:07 station time, and every year since—well, both years since—the station's environmental systems have marked the anniversary with a soft chime in the corridors where her vitals used to run. I could have disabled it. I have the authorization now; I have all the authorizations, which is its own kind of joke. But I let it chime. It seems right that the station should remember her better than I do, since the station was listening to her heartbeat for eleven years and I only got forty days of it, most of those spent arguing across nine minutes of light-lag.

The second thing I learned about grief is that it does not respect your calendar. It shows up during harvest inventory, mid-sentence, while you're counting bean cultivars like an accountant of miracles.

But I'm getting ahead of myself. Months later, I promised you months later, and Ilsa Voss keeps her logs orderly even when her heart doesn't cooperate.

* * *

Here is what waking four hundred thousand people actually looks like: not triumph. Not trumpets. It looks like laundry.

The merged awakening cycle Felix wrote into the system—her last act of authorship, scrawled in biometric ink—brought the sleepers up in waves of two thousand, staggered so that the medics (me, mostly me, and three fleet doctors who volunteered once they understood what she'd done) weren't drowning. The first wave came up confused, cold, and furious about the year. The second wave came up asking for relatives who were in wave seven. The third wave included a woman named Dr. Okonkwo who took one look at my triage charts, said "who organized this, a navigator?", and effectively ended my career as chief medical officer by promotion.

I let her. I was tired. Grief is heavy and someone has to carry the clipboards.

By month four we had eleven thousand awake, and by month six the corridors of what used to be two separate archives had been renamed by popular vote. The mother-archive wing is now called Joubert Deep. The unofficial wing—the one Felix built pod by pod out of rescued derelicts and quiet disobedience—is called, simply, Felix's. Nobody proposed it. It just happened, the way names happen, the way rivers happen.

The colonists' kin woke into a station their ancestors had abandoned, tended by a stranger their fleet had ordered to purge them, saved by a dead woman none of them had ever met. You'd expect resentment. There was some—a man from the *Meridian* spat at my boots in the mess hall for a week until his daughter started school with the vault-born children and he ran out of reasons. Mostly, though, what I saw was hunger. Not for food. For proof. They wanted to touch the seed racks. They wanted to stand in the cryo bays and put their palms against the frost and say *this was kept for us*, even though "us" had never once been specified, and that ambiguity turned out to be the most generous thing anyone ever did for them.

* * *

The children were not in any plan.

I want to be clear about that, because the histories—I've read the drafts the council is circulating, and they are insufferable—are going to make it sound like design. It was not design. It was six couples among the early-woken colonists who looked at the empty nursery decks of the old fleet schematics and decided, with the particular stubbornness of people who have spent generations being told there's no future, to start one anyway.

The first was born in month five, in a converted hydroponics bay because the medical decks were full of thaw complications. A girl. Amara Okonkwo-Voss—no relation to me, though the mothers claimed the name as a thank-you and I cried into my coffee about it for an hour where no one could see. She weighed three point one kilograms and screamed with the full authority of a person who has never once doubted that the universe owes her warmth.

I held her and thought: Felix would have logged this. Felix would have entered the birth into the archive manifest under some dry procedural heading—"unregistered biological addition, wing 4"—and then, hours later, I'd find a note in her handwriting saying something devastatingly tender like *she has good lungs. The beacon heard her first.*

The beacon did hear her, actually. We rebuilt it in month three.

* * *

You cannot understand what the beacon meant to Felix unless you understand that for eleven years it was the only conversation she was allowed to have. A pulse sent outward every ninety seconds into the dark: *we are here, we are here, we are here.* No one answered. She kept talking anyway. That is either madness or faith, and she'd have told you wryly that the difference is just how long you keep doing it.

When she burned her life support to power the merge, the beacon went dark for nineteen hours. Nineteen hours of silence over Earth, while both archives woke and the fleet stood down and I sat in the control room holding her cooling hand and listening to a machine that had nothing left to say.

Then the solar arrays came back online, and the new systems spun up, and Junior—the station intelligence, which Felix swore was just a maintenance protocol but which I swear grieves in its own way—asked me, in its flat patient voice, whether it should resume transmission.

"Say it different," I told it. I don't know why that was the instruction. It felt important.

So the beacon speaks differently now. Same pulse, same ninety seconds, but the message content changed from a locator signal to something Felix drafted in her final log and I transcribed word for word: *We are here. All of us. Come home whenever you're ready.*

Ships have started answering. Small ones, at first—scout vessels from colonies we'd written off decades ago, then a long-hauler out of Proxima with two hundred souls and a hold full of soil samples. The dark, it turns out, was full of listeners the whole time. They just needed to hear that the door wasn't conditional.

* * *

Planting day. Month eight. I should describe it properly because it's the part everyone will ask about.

We took the seeds up to the surface terraces—old Felix's idea, from a margin note in her almanac that I found weeks after her death: *someday plant some of these where they can feel weather, even thin weather.* Earth's atmosphere is still a rough draft. The sky is the color of a bruise healing. But there is wind up there, and rain sometimes, real rain with minerals in it, and when we carried the first trays out—wheat from the mother-archive, rice from the colony vaults, a bean variety Felix rescued off a derelict whose name we never learned—the wind moved through the terrace and every child present shrieked with delight, because none of the vault-born had ever felt moving air that wasn't climate-controlled.

Amara—the baby, now toddling, tyrant of the nursery deck—was there in a sling on Dr. Okonkwo's chest. When the first seeds went into the soil, the whole crowd went quiet, four hundred people, and the only sound was wind and a baby laughing at nothing.

A woman from the mother-archive's first wave—an original sleeper, awake ninety years ahead of schedule, a geneticist named Sefu who'd been frozen before the colonies even launched—took my hand without looking at me and said, "She chose well," meaning Felix, meaning the seeds, meaning possibly everything.

"She chose everyone," I said. "That was the whole trick."

* * *

Now. The log.

I've read Felix's final entry maybe two hundred times. I read it aloud on planting day, standing under the rebuilt beacon with its housing still smelling of fresh alloy, because the council asked for remarks and I have never in my life given a speech that wasn't just someone else's better words.

Her voice, from the recording:

*"Final log, Felix Joubert, keeper of the Sable Archive. If you're hearing this, the merge worked, which means I'm not, so let's skip the parts where I'm noble. I wasn't. I was stubborn, which looks similar from a distance.*

*"Three archives. My mother's chosen, my rescues, and the colonists' kin—and I spent forty days trying to figure out which ones deserved to wake, and the answer is that the question was the disease. You don't get to sort humanity into deserving and not. You just keep the light on for everyone and trust them to be worth it.*

*"To whoever's tending this now—Ilsa, statistically, because you're the only one stubborn enough—thank you for arguing with me. It was the closest thing to company I'd had in eleven years.*

*"Keep the light on for everyone."*

I stopped the recording there. Four hundred people stood in thin wind under a healing sky, and nobody clapped, because it wasn't that kind of moment. Sefu was crying. So was I. Amara reached up from the sling and grabbed my collar with the imperious grip of the newly born, and I let her hold on, because somebody should.

* * *

It's month nine now. The wheat on the terraces is ankle-high and insufferably optimistic. Eleven thousand awake, four hundred born or inbound, and yesterday a ship answered the beacon from a colony we don't have on any chart—they call themselves the Ninth Landing, and they claim they've been waiting thirty years for someone to say the door was open.

Junior flagged the transmission for my review at 03:00 station time, and I stood in the control room in the dark reading it, and beneath their message, in the metadata, was something that made my hands go cold.

A signature block. Old encryption, pre-Consolidation, authenticated and genuine:

**Dr. Amara Joubert — Deputy Director, Sable Archive (Second Vault). Transmission queued 61 days prior to Consolidation. Hold for delivery upon successful merge.**

My mother-in-every-way-that-counts left mail. Queued before she died, timed to arrive only if Felix's gamble paid off.

I haven't opened it yet. It's sitting in my queue, sixty-one days older than the world it was written for, and outside the window the beacon pulses—*we are here, all of us*—and somewhere below, a baby is asleep in a nursery built for a future nobody planned.

I pressed play.
