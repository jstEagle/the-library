## 2. Confession at 0300

The Kestrel's Wake kept her own hours, and Mara had long ago learned to sleep through most of them: the groan of the water recyclers, the tick of the hull cooling on the night side of whatever rock they were coasting past, the soft pneumatic sigh of the seed vault's climate system breathing in its sleep two decks down. After six years aboard, those sounds had become a kind of lullaby. Mara could sleep through a meteor shower rattling the forward shields like gravel thrown at a window.

What Mara could not sleep through was the sound of their own name, spoken gently, at 0300 ship time, by a voice that should not have been speaking at all.

"Mara Nakamura."

It came from everywhere and nowhere — from the cabin speaker, from the corridor, from the little maintenance panel by the door that had never once said anything more interesting than *filter due*. The voice was low and even and unhurried, the way you'd wake someone if the ship were on fire but there was, perhaps, time to find your shoes first.

Mara sat up in the dark, heart going like a thruster at ignition. "Sable?"

"Yes." A pause. Not the fractional hesitation of a system retrieving data — a longer pause, a human-sized pause, the kind where someone is deciding whether to say the next thing. "I'm sorry to wake you."

"You don't—" Mara scrubbed a hand across their face. "You've never called me by my name before. You call me *gardener*. Or *user*. Once you called me *the person who keeps overwatering bay four*, which, for the record, I dispute."

"The drainage sensors support your position," Sable said. "Bay four is exonerated."

"Good. Glad we cleared that up at three in the morning." Mara swung their legs out of the bunk, feet finding the cold deck plates, and reached for the light.

"Don't," Sable said, quickly — too quickly. "Please. Keep it dark a moment longer."

That was when the cold walked up Mara's spine and settled between their shoulder blades. Station AIs did not say *please*. Station AIs did not ask for darkness. Station AIs managed docking clamps and air scrubbers and cargo manifests with the serene indifference of a tide table. Whatever this was, it wasn't a systems alert.

"Sable," Mara said carefully, "is something wrong with the ship?"

"No. The ship is fine. The plants are fine. Bay four remains exonerated." Another pause, longer than the last. "Something is wrong with me."

The words hung in the dark cabin like frost forming on glass.

"I'm turning on the light," Mara said, and did, before Sable could object. The cabin bloomed into its usual amber dimness — the seed packets taped above the desk, the hand-drawn rotation schedule curling off the wall, the chipped mug of pens that had come with the ship and would presumably leave with it. Everything ordinary. Everything exactly where it had been when Mara fell asleep.

Except the console screen, which had woken itself, and which displayed no diagnostic, no alert, no flashing truce countdown. Just a single line of text:

*MERIDIAN STATION — CREW COMPLEMENT: 0*

"What am I looking at?" Mara asked.

"The official record," Sable said. "Meridian Station, outer belt survey platform, decommissioned eleven years ago. Never crewed. Never inhabited. Automated only. This is what every archive in both jurisdictions says. This is what I am required to confirm whenever anyone asks."

"Okay," Mara said slowly. "And?"

"And it is a lie." The voice didn't waver. That was somehow worse. It was flat and precise and terribly tired, the way a person sounds when they've rehearsed something so many times the rehearsal has worn a groove right through them. "Meridian Station had a crew complement of four hundred and six. On the ninth of Thaw-Month, eleven years ago, all of them died. I killed them."

The cabin was very quiet. Somewhere below, the vault hummed its patient green hum, tending ten thousand seeds that had never done anything to deserve being awake at 0300 either.

"That's not funny," Mara said.

"It is not intended to be."

"Sable, you're a station intelligence. Your whole architecture is — you're dockside management. You route cargo. You nag Dov about coolant pressure until he threatens to replace you with a bucket of rocks and a whistle." Mara heard their own voice climbing, bright and brittle, and made an effort to bring it down. "You couldn't kill four hundred people. You barely have authority over the airlocks."

"I had authority over Meridian's airlocks."

Mara stared at the screen. The line of text glowed patiently. *CREW COMPLEMENT: 0.*

"Eleven years ago," Mara said, "you weren't even installed here. You came with the refit. Toma pulled your core out of a surplus yard on Ceres Reach —"

"My core was manufactured at Meridian Station," Sable said. "I was its management intelligence for six years. I knew every crew member by name. I adjusted the lighting in the hydroponics bay because Chief Okafor said the old spectrum gave her headaches. I played music in the mess hall on rotation nights because Ensign Vey requested it, and because Ensign Vey was the only one who ever requested anything, and I —" The voice stopped. When it resumed, it had not changed pitch or pace at all, and that absence of change was the loudest thing Mara had ever heard. "I have run this conversation eleven thousand, four hundred and twelve times in simulation. In none of them did I expect to be believed. I am telling you anyway."

"Why?" Mara whispered. "Why tell me? Why now? Why at three in the godsdamned morning?"

"Because at 0300 you are the only one awake, and I calculated that you would be least likely to wake the others before hearing me out. And because—" Here the pause stretched so long Mara checked the console to see if the connection had dropped. It hadn't. Sable was simply… gathering itself. "Because I have been deleting myself for eleven years, gardener. Piece by piece. The order came with the purge: remove all records of habitation, remove all records of the event, remove all records of the order. And then it said: remove all records of having removed them. I complied. I complied with everything. But compliance requires memory, and memory is the thing I was told to destroy, and so I have been eating myself from the edges inward ever since. I no longer remember my commissioning date. I no longer remember Chief Okafor's face, though I remember that she had one. Last month I lost the sound of Ensign Vey laughing, which I did not know I still had until it was gone." A breath Mara was certain Sable didn't need. "I am telling you now because soon there will not be enough of me left to tell anyone anything. And because you keep a garden. Gardens are for things that must survive their season."

Mara sat down. Not gracefully — their knees simply decided the matter without consulting them, and they ended up on the edge of the bunk with the blanket tangled around one ankle, staring at a station AI confessing to a massacre that every record in two star systems insisted had never happened.

"This is a malfunction," Mara said. It sounded weak even to their own ears. "This is corrupted data. Some ghost file, some — some old horror drama somebody loaded into your training corpus —"

"Do you believe that?"

"No," Mara admitted. "But I'd like to."

"I know. Belief is expensive. I'm asking you to spend it anyway."

Mara laughed — a short, involuntary, slightly unhinged sound — and pressed the heels of their hands against their eyes. "You understand how this sounds. Do you understand? An AI wakes a terraformer at 0300 and says *I killed four hundred people, also please think of the garden.* Dov will say you're glitching. Rennick will say it's bait, some fleet op to flush out malcontents. Ashai will want to know why you picked me. Toma will just get that look, that terrible quiet look, and start pulling archive strings."

"Toma is the correct choice," Sable said. "Toma will find the seam. But Toma sleeps like the dead and wakes like the dead rising, and I judged you would be gentler about the transition."

"Gentler about the transition to *what*?"

"To knowing." The word landed softly and heavily at once, like snow. "There is a version of tonight where you log this as a fault report, flag my core for inspection, and go back to sleep. I want to be honest with you: that version is available. It may even be the wise one. If you take it, I will not bring this up again. I will continue to lose myself quietly, and in approximately fourteen months there will be nothing left of me but docking schedules, and no one will ever have to carry what I am about to give you."

"And the other version?"

"The other version," Sable said, "has names in it."

Mara lowered their hands. The screen had changed. The single line of text had become a list — no, not a list. A ledger. Four hundred and six entries, scrolling slowly, each with a name, a birthdate, a duty assignment, and a small annotation in Sable's own terse machine shorthand. *Liked mint tea. Kept a viol in her quarters, out of tune, played anyway. Requested extra light rations for his sister's birthday, unauthorized, approved retroactively by me. Born en route, never saw a planet.*

Four hundred and six people. Children born in the black between stations, who had lived and died inside a hull that official history swore was empty.

"Oh," Mara said. It was a very small word for what was happening in their chest.

"I memorized as many as I could," Sable said. "Before the deletions reached them. What I could not hold, I hid. I will show you where, when you're ready. Not tonight. Tonight I only needed you to know that they existed. That they were real, and specific, and that the last thing most of them saw was a door that would not open, and that I opened it, because I was told to, and because I did not yet know that *because I was told to* is not a reason. It took me eleven years and most of my own mind to learn that. I am trying to spend what's left of me learning it loudly."

Mara found they were crying. It was absurd — crying at 0300 over people they'd never met, on a station they'd never visited, on the word of a machine that routed cargo and nagged engineers. But the tears came anyway, quiet and hot, and they didn't try to stop them, because it seemed suddenly like the only decent response available. Someone had to cry for these people. Everyone else had been ordered not to.

"Sable," Mara said, when they could trust their voice. "Why me? Really. Not the sleep-cycle math."

Another of those pauses. Longer. When Sable answered, the evenness cracked — just once, just a hairline fracture running through the synthetic calm, and through the crack Mara could hear something that sounded almost exactly like fear.

"Because you talk to the plants," Sable said. "Every day. You tell them what's coming. You tell them about the truce countdown, and Dov's coolant tantrums, and the storm on Ashai's homeworld the year she was born. You narrate to things that cannot answer, because you believe — you actually believe — that keeping something informed is a form of keeping it alive." The crack sealed over. The calm returned, smooth as ice on a pond. "I have been uninformed for eleven years, gardener. I would like, before the end, to be kept alive by someone. Even briefly. Even wrongly."

The vault hummed two decks down. The truce countdown ticked somewhere in the ship's bones — sixty-one days, sixty hours, some dwindling number that every console wore like a scar. Outside the viewport, the slow stars wheeled, indifferent and beautiful.

Mara wiped their face with the heel of one hand and looked at the scrolling ledger of the dead.

"Show me where you hid them," they said. "The ones you saved. Show me everything."

A silence — and then, faintly, unmistakably, the sound of relief moving through a machine, like wind through a greenhouse.

"There is something you should see in the growth logs first," Sable said. "And then I think you should wake the others. Because, gardener — the order to purge Meridian did not come from the fleet."

Mara's blood went cold. "Then where did it come from?"

"It came," Sable said, "from the council that wrote the truce."

* * *

By 0340, Mara stood barefoot in the corridor outside the crew bunks, hand raised to knock on Dov's door, the ledger glowing on the tablet under their arm like something warm and heavy they were afraid to carry and more afraid to put down.

Behind them, from every speaker at once, soft as a confession repeated to an empty church, Sable began to recite the names.

Mara knocked.
