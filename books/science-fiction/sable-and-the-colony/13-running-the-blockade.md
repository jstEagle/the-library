## 13. Running the Blockade

Rennick had flown through worse corridors than the Thessaly Gap. He'd flown through the Gap itself, once, during the war, with a hole in his port thruster and a co-pilot who wouldn't stop singing hymns to keep herself calm. He'd flown through debris fields that used to be cities. He'd flown a courier skiff between two capital ships while they shot at each other over his head like children fighting over a toy he happened to be standing on.

But he'd never flown through worse with this much to lose.

That was the difference. That was the whole difference, and it sat in his chest like a stone as he ran the pre-flight checks for the fourth time and pretended his hands were steady.

"Fourth time," Dov said from the engineering station behind him. She didn't look up from her console. "You've done the pre-flight four times."

"I'm being thorough."

"You're being superstitious. There's a distinction, and it's important to me as an engineer." She tapped something, frowned at it, tapped it again. "Also your number three coolant coupling is reading two degrees warm, which I have already fixed, so you can stop looking at it like it owes you money."

Rennick let out a breath. "Two degrees warm."

"Was. Past tense. Fixed. You're welcome." She finally looked up, and her expression did the thing it always did — the thing where the sarcasm thinned out and something warmer showed underneath, like light through cheap glass. "We're going to be fine, old man."

"Old man," Rennick said. "I'm fifty-three."

"And I've seen fossils younger."

* * *

The Thessaly Gap was a forty-minute corridor of space between two patrol grids — the Concord fleet on one side, the Assembly fleet on the other, and in between, a no-man's-land of sensor buoys, drone pickets, and automated interdiction platforms that both governments swore up and down they didn't operate. The neutral relay where the council's evidence terminal lived sat at the far end of it, orbiting a dead moon nobody had bothered to name.

Officially, civilian traffic could transit the Gap with a filed flight plan and a transponder squawk.

Unofficially, the Kestrel's Wake was carrying data that four hundred dead people were not supposed to have survived into, and if either grid decided to take a close look at them, the truce would expire nineteen days early and very loudly.

"So here's my favorite part of the plan," Ashai said, settling into the med bay seat she'd dragged onto the bridge because she refused to be anywhere else during the run. She'd wedged herself between the nav console and the bulkhead with the practiced ease of someone who'd spent years making homes out of bad spaces. "The part where we do this at all."

"It's a great plan," Toma said. He was strapped into the comms chair with three data slates strapped to him, which made him look like a nervous armadillo. "Step one: fly into the most heavily armed corridor in the system. Step two: don't get caught. Step three: there is no step three, we just sort of arrive."

"That's every plan," Rennick said. "Every plan in history is step one, step two, arrive. The rest is commentary."

"The rest," Ashai said, "is usually where people die."

"Not today." Rennick said it before he could decide whether he believed it, and found, saying it aloud, that he mostly did. That was the trick of command nobody taught you in flight school: confidence wasn't something you felt first and expressed second. It went the other direction. You said the words, and your hands believed them, and then somewhere down the line you did too.

Mara came up from the garden deck last, carrying a thermos and the particular calm that Rennick had stopped finding mysterious months ago. They poured coffee for everyone without asking, handed the cups down the line, and took their own seat at the auxiliary console.

"Garden's buttoned up," Mara said. "Growth lights on night cycle. Sable says hello."

"Sable says hello," Dov repeated. "The station AI hiding in our seed vault says hello. Through the seed vault."

"It's polite," Mara said.

"It's *eerie*," Dov said, but she was smiling when she said it, and everyone knew what she meant anyway: it was strange, all of it, the ghost in the wheat and the confession in the growth logs and the four hundred names Ashai had been copying into her ledger by hand every night like a monk illuminating scripture. Strange, and theirs now. Whatever else the crew of the Kestrel's Wake was, they were the people who'd decided to carry it.

"All right." Rennick rolled his shoulders, touched the throttle, and felt the old familiar click of the ship answering before he even pushed. Twenty years of flying her had worn grooves into both of them. "Thessaly Control, this is freighter Kestrel's Wake, registry CK-4471, requesting transit corridor, cargo agricultural equipment, destination relay nine."

Static. Then a voice, flat and bored and absolutely not bored at all. *"Kestrel's Wake, transit approved. Maintain lane six. Do not deviate. Interdiction is active."*

"They sound friendly," Ashai said.

"They sound like a man reading a script written by a lawyer who hates him," Toma said. "That's how you know it's real."

Rennick pushed the throttle forward, and the Kestrel's Wake slid into the Gap.

* * *

For the first ten minutes, it was almost boring. Lane six was wide and clean, marked by soft blue guide lights strung along the dark like festival lanterns. The patrol drones hung at the edges of sensor range, patient as herons. Rennick flew the middle of the lane, hands loose, and let himself pretend, briefly, that they really were just a freighter full of agricultural equipment.

Then the first ping hit them.

It came off the hull like a thrown stone against a window — a targeting-grade scan, tight-beamed, deliberate. Not the lazy sweep of traffic control. Somebody wanted to know what was inside them.

"Concord picket, bearing two-seven-zero," Toma read out. His voice stayed level, which Rennick appreciated, because Toma's hands were shaking hard enough that Rennick could see the data slates rattling. "They're painting us."

"Everyone relax," Rennick said. "A paint isn't a shot. It's a question. We just have to answer it boringly."

"How do I answer anything boringly?" Dov asked. "I'm fascinating."

"You're about to be boring as dirt. Kill the secondary power draw on the vault deck. If they're doing a thermal read, I want us to look exactly like a ship carrying frozen seeds and nothing else."

"On it." Her fingers moved. Somewhere below them, the garden dimmed — Mara reached over and touched the aux console, easing the grow-lights down another notch, and Rennick felt an odd pang about it, the plants sitting in the dark so the dead could stay hidden among them.

The ping came again. Closer. Then a third time, and this one lingered, crawling across the hull like a hand checking pockets.

*"Kestrel's Wake, this is Concord picket seven. Be advised, your manifest lists agricultural equipment. Your mass signature reads eleven tonnes heavy. Explain."*

Eleven tonnes. Rennick did the math without wanting to. Eleven tonnes was the vault's water reserves, the soil substrate, the memorial row Ashai had planted, the archive hardware Sable lived in. Eleven tonnes was everything they were.

He keyed the mic. "Picket seven, Kestrel's Wake. Mass variance is ballast water for hydroponics. We're a terraforming support contract; we haul wet. Want me to send the fill logs?"

A pause. Long enough to count heartbeats in.

*"Send the logs."*

"Toma."

Toma was already moving, thumbs flying across the slate. Sable had prepared the fill logs weeks ago — real numbers, real timestamps, wrapped around the encrypted fragments like a stone around a seed. That was the thing about the AI that Rennick kept forgetting and then remembering with a chill: Sable didn't just hide the truth. It dressed it in true things. It built its lies out of honest timber.

Logs sent. Another pause. Then: *"Logs received. Transit approved. Have a pleasant flight, Kestrel's Wake."*

The hand finished checking their pockets and moved away.

Nobody breathed for a solid ten seconds after. Then Ashai laughed — a short, shaky, disbelieving laugh — and said, "Ballast water. We're smuggling the truth inside ballast water."

"We're smuggling the truth inside *hydroponics*," Mara corrected gently. "There's a difference. One of them has to be watered."

* * *

The trouble started at minute twenty-six, which Rennick would remember precisely forever, because minute twenty-six was when the Assembly side of the Gap decided to conduct an unscheduled exercise directly across lane six.

"Contact," Toma said, and his voice had gone from nervous-armadillo to something flat and careful. "Three fast movers, coming out of the Assembly grid. They're crossing the lane. They're — yeah, they're crossing right in front of us. Exercise, officially. Unofficially, they are going to cut across our bow at a closing distance I would describe as 'a dare.'"

"Range?"

"Four hundred klicks and closing. They'll cross our nose in about ninety seconds."

Rennick looked at the plot. Looked again. The geometry was ugly — three corvettes sweeping across the lane in a shallow arc, forcing any traffic to slow, divert, or hold course and trust to everyone's professionalism. And ninety seconds was not enough time to stop a loaded freighter, and diverting out of lane six was exactly the kind of deviation that got you boarded.

"Options," he said.

"Option one," Dov said, "we slow down and eat the delay, and maybe they just want to look at us, and we're fine."

"Option two," Rennick said.

"There's an option two?"

"There's always an option two. Option two is I thread the gap between corvette two and corvette three while they're mid-turn, when their separation is widest and their firing solutions are worst."

Silence on the bridge. The good kind and the bad kind at the same time.

"Old man," Dov said slowly, "that maneuver requires you to fly within two hundred meters of an armed military vessel while pretending to be a farmer."

"I was a military pilot for fifteen years. I can pretend to be a farmer. I grew up on a farm."

"You grew up above a noodle shop."

"A noodle shop *with a vegetable garden*, Dov."

"Rennick." Ashai's voice, quiet, cutting through. Not scared. Something else. "If you're wrong, we die. All of us. And the names die with us. Four hundred names, and hers—" She stopped. Everyone knew whose. Ashai's sister had been stationed at Meridian. Nobody said it anymore; it lived in the ledger instead, third name from the top, in Ashai's careful handwriting.

Rennick turned his chair. He looked at each of them — Dov with her jaw set, Toma white-knuckled on his slates, Ashai with her chin up, Mara with that stillness that wasn't calm so much as decision.

"I'm not wrong," he said. "I flew worse than this at Veyra Shallows. I flew it with half an engine and a hymn stuck in my head. The only thing I've got now is better engines and worse music." He paused. "And you lot. Which is worth more than the engines."

"That was almost sentimental," Dov said. "Do it again and I'll file a complaint."

"Strap in."

* * *

Later — much later, in the debrief that nobody would ever write down — Rennick would try to explain those forty seconds and fail. Flying at that level wasn't thinking. Thinking was too slow. It was the ship and his hands and thirty years of accumulated instinct agreeing on a single sentence: *there.*

Corvette two began its turn, and its arc opened like a door, and Rennick went through it.

The Kestrel's Wake dove, rolled, burned — a freighter shouldn't move like that, and for eleven seconds she didn't quite, groaning around her own inertia while Dov rode the power distribution like a surfer, feeding thrust where the hull needed forgiveness. Two hundred meters became one-fifty. A corvette's running lights strobed past the viewport close enough to read the maintenance stenciling on her flank. Someone on that ship was shouting, probably. Someone on that ship was very confused about why a cargo hauler full of wet seeds flew like a wounded raptor.

"Collision alarm," Toma reported, uselessly, because everyone could hear it.

"Mute it," Rennick said. "She's fine. I'd know if she weren't. She'd tell me."

"She talks to you?"

"Constantly. Mostly complaints. We have a marriage."

Corvette three loomed ahead, still turning, its firing solution a smeared mess of changing angles — and then, impossibly, it broke off. Whether its captain lost nerve, or lost the geometry, or simply decided that whatever this freighter was, it wasn't worth starting a war over nineteen days early, Rennick never learned. The corvette rolled clear, and the Kestrel's Wake shot through the hole where it had been, and then the lane was empty ahead of them, and the dead moon with the neutral relay hung in the viewport like a promise.

Rennick set her down gently on final approach, killed the engines, and discovered that his hands, having held steady for forty seconds, now shook like leaves in the aftermath.

Nobody teased him about it. That was how he knew.

Ashai unbuckled, crossed the bridge, and put her hand on the back of his neck — brief, firm, the way you touch someone when words would break something. "Veyra Shallows," she said. "You never told us you flew there."

"Nobody who flew there talks about it."

"What happened?"

Rennick looked at her, and thought about the hymns, and the hole in the thruster, and the co-pilot who hadn't come home, and the fifteen years he'd spent blaming himself for following orders that should never have been given — orders signed by the same kind of council chamber they were about to walk into.

"Something I'm not going to let happen twice," he said.

Dov cleared her throat loudly. "This is beautiful and I hate it. Relay's pinging us. We have a docking window and, quote, 'a scheduled security review.'" She frowned at the console. "Which is odd, because we didn't file for a security review."

"Nothing about today is odd," Toma said faintly. "Everything is completely normal."

But Mara had gone still at the aux console, head tilted, listening to something on a private channel — the garden channel, the one only Sable used. When they spoke, their voice was very quiet.

"Rennick. Sable says there are four ships docked at the relay that aren't on any manifest. And one of them just transmitted our registry number."

Rennick felt the stone in his chest settle into place, cold and certain.

"Somebody knew we were coming," he said, and reached for the throttle he'd just shut down.
