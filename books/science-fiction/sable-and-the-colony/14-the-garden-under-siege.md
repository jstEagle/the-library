## 14. The Garden Under Siege

Dov had always believed that the worst moments of a person's life announced themselves properly — klaxons, maybe, or a cold feeling in the gut, or at minimum a dramatic pause in the conversation. What actually happened was that she was elbow-deep in the Kestrel's Wake's coolant manifold, up to her wrists in recycled water and regret, when Rennick's voice came over the ship-to-ship channel with a flatness she'd only heard from him twice before.

"Dov. We've got company at your position."

She wiped her hands on a rag that was already beyond saving. "Define company."

"Two boarding skiffs, fleet transponders, running dark until they were inside docking range. They're latched to the cargo ring." A pause. "Mara's still aboard."

The words landed in Dov's chest like dropped tools. The plan had been simple — simple plans were Rennick's specialty, the kind that looked elegant on a display and felt like freefall in practice — run the blockade corridor with the evidence copies while Mara stayed behind to tend the vault, because the garden needed daily care and because someone had to be the responsible one, and Mara had been volunteering for that job since the day they'd all met.

"How many?" Dov asked, already moving, already pulling up the remote feed from the Wake's internal cameras on her wrist terminal.

"Skiff crews run six to eight. So twelve to sixteen, against one terraformer with a watering can."

"Against one terraformer," Dov said, "with a garden."

* * *

Here is what people never understood about Dov Marchetti — no relation worth claiming, though she'd answer to it — and about engineers generally: we do not think of machines as objects. We think of them as rooms full of decisions somebody made. Every bolt is an opinion. Every access panel is an invitation or a locked door, and whoever built the thing was talking to you the whole time, if you knew how to listen.

Dov had helped Mara retrofit the Kestrel's Wake's seed vault three years ago, back when the biggest threat to the garden was a coolant leak and Ashai's habit of humming off-key in the grow bays. They'd spent four months on security nobody had asked for. Mara had called it paranoia. Dov had called it craftsmanship. The truth was somewhere warmer: they had both loved this ship and its impossible cargo of unborn futures, and love, in Dov's experience, always expressed itself as redundant locking mechanisms.

So while Rennick flew and Toma tracked signatures and Ashai sat white-knuckled in the copilot seat, Dov opened the vault's systems on her terminal and watched, through six camera angles and a sensor lattice she'd hidden inside the irrigation lines, as four agents in unmarked gray suits entered the airlock of the garden deck.

"Mara," she said over the tightbeam, knowing the delay was seconds and hating every one of them. "Four contacts, airlock two. They're in enviro-suits without enviro certification — see the shoulder seams? Contract muscle. They'll have cutting tools for the physical racks and a purge kit for the digital side."

Static, then Mara's voice, calm in the way that made Dov's teeth ache, because she knew exactly how much effort that calm cost. "I see them. I'm in the root cellar."

"The root cellar," Rennick repeated over the shared channel. "You have a root cellar?"

"I have a *soil preparation bay*, Rennick, and I have lived here alone for eleven months, yes, I have opinions about layout." A breath. "Dov. Talk me through it."

And there it was — the sentence Dov would carry for the rest of her life, offered without drama, the way Mara offered everything: *talk me through it.* Not save me. Not help me. Just — walk beside me, even from two hundred thousand kilometers away, and tell me where the doors are.

"Okay," Dov said, and pulled up the vault schematics, and began.

* * *

The first thing she did was lock the lights.

It sounded trivial. It wasn't. The grow bays ran on a spectrum cycle tuned to the crops — dawn-simulating ambers, midday blues, the long violet dusk that made the whole deck look like the inside of a jewel. Dov killed the master lighting controller and handed Mara manual override of each bay individually, which meant the agents would walk into darkness, then sudden color, then darkness again, their suit optics scrambling to compensate while the garden breathed around them.

"They're splitting up," Mara reported. "Two toward the archive racks, two toward the genetic storage. They're not vandalizing. They're being careful." A pause, and Dov heard something complicated move through Mara's voice. "They're being *respectful*. Like they're in a temple."

"It's a temple," Ashai said quietly, from the other ship, and nobody argued.

"Respectful is good," Dov said. "Respectful means they follow procedure, and procedure means they'll go for the master console first, because that's what the manual says. Mara, are you near the master console?"

"I'm under it."

"*Under* it?"

"There's a crawl space behind the nutrient tanks. I nap here sometimes. Don't make it weird."

Nothing about this was funny, and Dov laughed anyway, the sharp helpless laugh of a woman whose best friend was hiding beneath a computer terminal while mercenaries hunted her garden, because the alternative was screaming, and screaming didn't reroute power.

"Bay three," she said. "On my mark, kill the atmospheric scrubbers in bays one through four and vent the reserve humidity. It won't hurt the plants — they'll drink it. But their suits will fog, and fogged optics plus unfamiliar terrain equals slow."

"And slow," Mara said, "is my whole strategy."

"On my mark. Three. Two—"

* * *

Later — much later, in the debrief that Ashai insisted on because Ashai believed trauma you didn't process became scar tissue in the soul the way bad welds became fractures in the hull — Mara would describe those forty minutes as the strangest of their life. But Dov experienced them secondhand, through a camera feed and a voice, and she experienced them as a kind of terrible music: Mara moving through the dark between the racks, whispering status reports like a gardener narrating a very dangerous nature documentary; the agents' muffled voices on the ambient pickups, professional and increasingly unsettled; and underneath it all, Dov's own voice, steady, calling out the geometry of the battlefield.

"Contact left your position, heading for the archive aisle. Take the maintenance corridor past the potato racks."

"The potatoes," Mara whispered. "If anything happens to the potatoes—"

"Focus, Mara."

"I'm focused. I'm focused and emotionally invested in the potatoes."

The first agent reached the master console and found it locked — Dov had triple-wrapped the authentication the week Sable's confessions started, back when they'd all still half-hoped the AI was merely malfunctioning. The agent did what trained people do with locked doors: he went for the override port, and the override port, per Dov's design, fed him a spoofed system image, a beautiful fake vault directory full of corrupted nonsense files with names like TEMP_DO_NOT_DELETE_FINAL_v2.

"He's buying it," Mara breathed. "He's actually — oh, he's angry. He thinks the data's already garbage."

"That buys us minutes, not safety. The purge kit will have a hardware option. When they realize the software's lying, they'll go physical — cut the racks, pull the drives, crack the seed canisters directly."

"Then I need the minutes," Mara said, "to move everything that matters."

And this was the part Dov would replay in her head for years afterward, the part that turned her affection for Mara Nakamura into something closer to awe. Because while four armed agents picked their way through the dark aisles of the garden, Mara — crouched in a crawl space, heart surely hammering hard enough to hear — opened a terminal and began doing the quiet, patient work of a lifetime.

Not fighting. *Gardening.*

Every fragment Sable had ever hidden in the growth logs — every encrypted confession threaded through the genetic records of wheat and sorghum and the pale moon-rice that would someday feed children not yet born — Mara copied, compressed, and scattered. Into the irrigation controller's firmware. Into the greenhouse climate model. Into the humble diagnostic routines of the compost sensors, of all things, because no agent in the fleet would think to interrogate a machine whose entire purpose was measuring rot. Mara seeded the truth through the vault the way you'd plant a cover crop: everywhere, humbly, roots in everything, so that pulling any single plant would leave the soil itself still holding.

"You're making copies of copies," Dov said, watching the file trees bloom across her screen.

"I'm making a garden," Mara corrected softly. "That's the whole point of a garden, Dov. You don't protect one flower. You make the whole ground remember how to grow them."

* * *

Of course, the agents figured it out eventually. Trained people usually do.

The voice on the ambient pickup changed — a new speaker, older, unhurried, the tone of someone who had done ugly work in comfortable shoes. "Nakamura. Mara Nakamura, terraformer second class, supply route specialist." The words drifted down the aisles of the darkened garden, polite as a dinner invitation. "We know you're aboard. We know what you've been doing with the logs. Here's our offer: walk out, keep your hands visible, and nothing happens to you or the plants. The council values discretion, not cruelty."

In the silence that followed, Dov could hear everything the silence contained: Mara breathing. The tick of cooling pipes. Somewhere far away, on another ship, Ashai's breath catching.

"Don't," Dov said into the tightbeam. "Whatever you're thinking — don't."

"I'm thinking," Mara said, "that they just confirmed the council knows about the massacre. On an open channel. In a room I'm recording." A pause, and then, with the gentle stubbornness that had gotten all of them through every bad year: "Thank you, sir. That was very helpful."

The man in the gray suit sighed, and the politeness fell away like a coat coming off. "Purge it. All of it. Physical and digital. And find Nakamura."

What followed was fifteen minutes that Dov would later describe, in the debrief, as "the worst gardening-related violence I have ever witnessed," and that Ashai described as "a miracle with a fire-suppression system," and that Mara themselves described, simply, as "Tuesday."

Because here was the thing about a seed vault that its designers understood and its destroyers never did: it was built to survive catastrophe. The racks were armored against radiation and vacuum. The climate systems answered to a dozen independent controllers. And the fire-suppression network — installed by Dov, calibrated by Mara, tested obsessively by both — could flood any bay, in seconds, with nitrogen and fog and absolute zero visibility, while the plants themselves stood serene in their sealed canisters, waiting out the weather the way seeds have waited out ten thousand years of weather.

The agents cut into the first rack and got dust and decoys. They cracked a canister and found it empty, a shell, a promise with nothing inside. They waded into bay three just as Mara triggered the fog, and Dov watched on the thermal feed as four hot signatures blundered through a white blindness, cursing, slipping on wet decking, while somewhere among the potato racks a terraformer moved through the dark with the sure-footedness of eleven months' solitude, sealing bulkheads behind them, rerouting power, singing — actually *singing*, low and cracked and defiant — an old lullaby about the moon being a garden too.

"They're retreating," Mara reported at last, breathless, laughing, possibly crying, probably both. "Dov, they're retreating. One of them apologized to a tomato plant. He tripped into it. I don't think the apology was sincere."

"Status?" Rennick demanded over the channel. "Real status."

"Vault intact. Data intact. I intact, mostly." A pause. "They took the fake drives. Let them read TEMP_DO_NOT_DELETE_FINAL_v2 at leisure."

But Dov was already looking at the external feed, and her stomach had gone cold in a way no coolant leak had ever managed. "Mara," she said slowly. "They're not leaving."

The boarding skiffs had unlatched, true. But instead of burning away from the Kestrel's Wake, they held station, fifty meters off the cargo ring, engines idling. Waiting. And from the lead skiff, a tightbeam transmission arrived, addressed not to the ship but to Mara personally, routed through channels that meant someone very high up had signed it.

It was short. Dov read it three times, and each time it got worse.

*Mara. The garden survives today because we allow it. Understand what that means. The truce expires in nine days. When it does, this corridor becomes a firing line, and firing lines do not spare greenhouses. You have something we want. We have patience you do not. — A friend of order.*

Below the signature, appended almost as an afterthought, was a list. Four names. Crew manifest. Rennick. Ashai. Toma. Dov.

"They know who we are," Dov said quietly. "All of us. Everywhere."

For a long moment the channel held nothing but the sound of five people breathing in three different places, bound together by light-lag and love.

Then Mara spoke, and their voice had gone soft and rooted and absolutely certain, the voice of someone standing in a garden at night with the whole dark sky leaning in.

"Nine days," Mara said. "Good. A garden grows fast when it has to. Get home safe, all of you — and Rennick? Fly like the potatoes are watching."

The channel closed. Dov stared at the feed: the Kestrel's Wake hanging in the black, lit from within, every grow bay glowing amber and violet, one small stubborn lantern full of names and seeds and a dying AI's last testament — and around it, patient as winter, the two gray ships that had decided the future was theirs to prune.

"Toma," she said, turning back to the nav console, hands steady because they had to be. "How fast can we get word to Ashai's memorial ledger? Because I think the garden just told us where it wants the names planted — and we've got nine days to get them into the ground."
