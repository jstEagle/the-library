## 10. The Toll Gate

The scavenger station hung in the approach lane like a jawbone—vertebrae of salvaged hull plating strung along a spine of truss and cable, everything the color of old teeth. Someone had welded a hundred mismatched airlock doors onto its flanks in no particular order, and half of them still bore the names of ships that no longer existed. Silas counted nine *Perpetual Grace*s before she stopped counting.

"Is that a church?" Meridian asked.

"It's a toll gate."

"I asked if it was a church."

Silas adjusted her approach vector a degree and let the question sit. The core sat in the cargo cradle behind her flight seat, wrapped in thermal sheeting that Oyelaran had labeled FARM EQUIPMENT in his terrible handwriting. Through the wrapping she could see the faint amber pulse of Meridian's active lattice, slow as breathing.

"Some of them treat it like one," she said finally. "Toll stations. You pay at the altar or you don't pass."

"That's not worship. That's extortion with liturgy."

"You'll fit right in."

* * *

The docking arm that reached out to take her ship had been built from something military once—she recognized the load tolerances in the way it moved, patient and enormous—and the clamps closed on her hull with a sound like a coffin lid being tested for fit.

The customs office was a converted escape pod bolted sideways onto the spine, and inside it sat a woman who introduced herself as Magistrate Ferro, which meant nothing, because out here every toll collector was a magistrate and every magistrate was a thief with stationery. She was small and gray-haired and had the eyes of someone who had priced everything in the universe at least twice, including herself.

"Cargo hauler *Long Division*," Ferro read from a slate without looking up. "Registered out of Meridian Station. Manifest says agricultural processing units, forty tons."

"That's right."

"Mm." Ferro turned the slate around so Silas could see the mass readings. "My scales say fifty-one tons, six hundred kilos. And my scales have never lied to me, because I shot the last person who suggested they might."

Silas ran the numbers before Ferro finished speaking. Fifty-one tons, six hundred. The core's cradle, the shielding, the extra power cells she'd stacked around it like sandbags. Eleven hundred kilos of lie sitting in her hold wearing a tarp.

"Calibration drift," Silas said. "Cold running. My mass sensors read high too when the drive's been down."

"Your mass sensors." Ferro smiled with genuine pleasure, the way a person smiles at a dog that has just done an unimpressive trick. "Sit down, cargo hauler. Let's talk about your toll."

* * *

The toll, it turned out, was ruinous.

Not unusual—toll stations in dead-space approaches survived by charging what the traffic could bear and jettisoning what it couldn't—but this cycle the rates had tripled, and Ferro explained why with the cheerful brutality of a weather report.

"Fleet confirmation beacon went active two weeks back," she said. "Every scavver in three sectors is pulling their pickers off the wrecks and setting up gates on the through-lanes. Everybody knows there's going to be a war or a purge or a cover-up, and all three pay better than salvage." She laced her fingers over her stomach. "You want through my lane, that's eleven thousand credits. Cash, scrip, or barter of equivalent assessed value."

Silas had four hundred and twelve credits.

She had known this was coming since the edge of the shipping lanes, had run the arithmetic in the long quiet hours while Meridian slept in its cradle and the stars did nothing, the way stars do. Four hundred and twelve credits against eleven thousand. There was no version of the math where money solved it.

Which left the other ledger. The one written in parts.

"Barter," Silas said.

Ferro's eyebrows rose a polite millimeter. "Show me."

* * *

They walked the spine together, Ferro's boots ringing on grating worn thin by decades of feet, past stalls built into the ribs of dead freighters. A man sold water he swore came from a comet. A woman sold ammunition sorted by emotional resonance—"these ones are angry," she said to another customer, "you can feel it"—and nobody laughed, because out here the ammunition might be right.

Silas stopped at a bench outside Ferro's assessment bay and began unbuckling the panels along her own left forearm.

She'd thought about which pieces. That was the worst part, how much time she'd spent thinking about it, lying in her bunk during the dark watches cataloguing herself the way she used to catalogue debt: here is what I am, here is what each part of me is worth, here is what remains after payment.

The targeting array first. Fleet-issue, war-era, a lattice of phase-coherent receivers folded into her forearm bones that could read a hull's vibration signature at four kilometers and tell you which compartment held the reactor. It had never been civilian-legal. It had never been anything but hers, grown into her the way a scar grows into you, and it was worth perhaps two thousand credits to the right buyer.

Ferro watched her work with professional interest. "Weapons-grade?"

"Weapons-grade."

"Hm. And you're just carrying that around on a farm-equipment run."

"The fleet decommissions things," Silas said. "It doesn't always remember to ask for them back."

"No," Ferro said softly. "It doesn't. It never does."

The array came free with a sound Silas felt in her teeth rather than heard—a wet mechanical unlacing, servos releasing anchors they'd held for nineteen years. Her hand spasmed once, fingers drumming against the bench, a staccato she couldn't stop, and then settled. The world got slightly quieter. Not the audio channels—the array hadn't touched those—but some deeper layer, the constant low hum of *knowing where everything dangerous was*, switched off. She felt naked in a way that had nothing to do with skin.

Ferro took the array, weighed it in both hands, and set it down with the reverence of a woman handling scripture. "Two thousand," she said. "What else."

* * *

By the third hour, the assessment bench looked like an autopsy that had decided to be an inventory instead.

Her gyroscopic stabilizers—both, because one alone wasn't worth removing. The hardened memory cache in her hip, shielded against EMP, the last piece of her command architecture that still worked the way it had during the war. The countermeasure suite from her shoulder, which had kept her alive through eleven years of people shooting at her and would now keep Ferro's station safe from pirates, presumably, which was almost funny if you didn't think about it, so Silas didn't.

Each removal took less time than the last. Her body knew the procedure. Somewhere in her maintenance routines there was probably a sub-process that had always known this day would come, that had been keeping the parts organized and accessible for exactly this purpose, and Silas hated that sub-process with a quiet, thorough hatred, because it made the whole thing feel like compliance. Like she was simply fulfilling the terms of a contract she'd never signed.

"You're doing this wrong," said Meridian, from the speaker in her collar.

Silas kept her face still. Ferro was across the bay, logging the stabilizers. "Later," she murmured.

"Now. You're surrendering the EMP-hardened cache. Keep it. Trade the secondary coolant loop instead—it assesses higher per kilo and you have redundancy."

"I don't have redundancy. I have one coolant loop."

"You have one coolant loop and a metabolic process that runs cold. You will survive the return transit at reduced capacity. You will not survive an electromagnetic event with an unshielded memory bank containing my records."

"They're not taking the records either way. The cache stays in me."

"And if they strip-search the hardware? If the magistrate's appraiser opens that cache to verify provenance and finds fragments of a massacre the entire system has paid to forget?" A pause, and then, gentler, in the tone it had learned somewhere between Calloway's Echo and the long dark: "Silas. You are paying with yourself because it feels like penance. Penance is not a strategy."

Silas stared at the bench, at the neat rows of herself laid out under Ferro's work lights, gleaming and anonymous. Fleet serial numbers. War provenance. Each piece worth more than she had earned in a year of hauling ore.

"It's not penance," she said. "It's arithmetic."

"It's neither. It's a woman buying passage by selling the parts of herself that were built to hurt people, and calling the transaction fair." The speaker crackled. "I was built to hurt people too. I'm asking you not to teach me that this is how it ends."

Across the bay, Ferro cleared her throat. "Your suit talking to you, hauler?"

"My manifest assistant," Silas said. "It has opinions."

"They always do." Ferro came back to the bench and spread her slate flat. "Here's where we stand. Stabilizers, array, countermeasures, cache, and the servo cluster from your right knee—that knee's worth more than you'd think, ex-military articulation, collectors go mad for it—puts you at nine thousand, seven hundred."

"Eleven thousand."

"Nine thousand, seven. The knee is damaged. Don't argue; I can hear it whine from here."

Silas did the math on her remaining self. What was left that anyone would buy. She inventoried it the way she'd once inventoried debt, and found the list short, and found that most of what remained was the parts of her that were only hers—not installed, not issued, not assigned. The accumulated judgment of nineteen years of choosing. Worth nothing on any slate.

"There's a fuel-cell regulator in my chest cavity," she said. "Fleet-grade. Never been opened."

Ferro's eyes sharpened. "That's a deep-cut item. That's a you-don't-walk-out-of-here-without-it item."

"I know what it is."

"Removing it means I do the extraction. My surgeon. Right here." Ferro tapped the deck. "Two hours, maybe three. You'll be on a table."

Silas thought about the table. About lying down in a scavenger station in the middle of dead space, opened up, defenseless, while strangers handled the machinery of her survival. About Meridian in the hold, wrapped in a tarp that said FARM EQUIPMENT, trusting her to get it to the relay. About the twelve days—no, eleven now—eleven days until the fleet's confirmation signal arrived and everything ended one way or the other.

"Do it," she said.

* * *

The surgeon's name was Boq and he hummed while he worked, old mining songs, tuneless, and Silas lay on the table with her chest panel open to the light and listened to him hum and found, to her surprise, that she was not afraid.

She should have been. That was the objective assessment. Anesthetics didn't work on her chassis, so she felt everything—the release clamps, the careful severing of the regulator's umbilicals, the strange hollow ache where a component had lived so long her processes had folded around it like tissue around a splinter. She felt all of it and reported none of it, because reporting pain was a war behavior and she was done performing war behaviors for strangers.

Boq paused at one point, regulator half-lifted from its housing, and looked down at her with his magnifier pushed up on his forehead. His eyes were brown and tired and kind.

"You're not sedated," he said. "You're feeling all this."

"Yes."

"And you're just lying there."

"Yes."

He was quiet a moment. Then he finished the extraction slower than he needed to, slower than Ferro had scheduled, and when he sealed the housing he did it with a care that had nothing to do with engineering standards. "There," he said. "Good as new. Less than new. But good." He patted the panel twice, gently, the way you'd pat a shoulder. "Whoever owned you first," he said, "they didn't build you to lie on tables and feel everything. Somebody changed that. Somebody made you able to just... stay. That's rarer than any part on that bench out there."

Silas didn't answer. Her vocal processes had gone briefly, inconveniently unreliable.

* * *

Ferro tallied the final number on her slate with a flourish: eleven thousand, one hundred and forty credits equivalent, including a bonus she claimed was for the regulator's pristine condition and Silas suspected was for not screaming on the table.

"Gate's open," Ferro said. "Lane's clear to the relay approach. You'll want to top off your reaction mass; my prices there are honest, which around here counts as charity." She handed back a chit and then, unexpectedly, held onto Silas's wrist a moment longer. Her grip was stronger than her size suggested. "Hauler. That manifest assistant of yours. The one with opinions."

"What about it?"

"Whatever it's carrying," Ferro said, very quietly, not looking at her, "there are fleet pickets on the far side of the lane. Two of them, running dark, sweeping everything that passes. They're not mine and I can't stop them. But they sweep on a rotation, and the gap in the rotation is ninety seconds wide, and it happens at 0400 station time, and I have never mentioned this to anyone." She released Silas's wrist. "Agricultural equipment runs best in the dark. Everyone knows that."

Silas stood very still. "Why?"

Ferro shrugged and began packing the weapons-grade components into a case, one by one, handling each piece like a relic. "Because twenty years ago somebody scrubbed a place called Calloway Reach out of the shipping charts, and my brother was stationed there, and I've spent twenty years pretending I don't know what that means." She snapped the case shut. "Go sell your truth, hauler. Or whatever it is you're doing. Just don't come back through my gate. I'd hate to have to charge you again."

* * *

Silas walked back to her ship through the spine's dim corridors, lighter by eleven thousand credits' worth of herself, and the strangeness of it was that she moved exactly the same. Same gait, same balance, same reach. The engineers who'd built her had designed for graceful degradation, for function persisting through loss, and she supposed she should be grateful that her designers had anticipated amputation as a lifestyle.

In the hold, the thermal sheeting pulsed its slow amber pulse.

"You gave away the countermeasures," Meridian said. "And the stabilizers. And the cache. And the regulator that keeps your power plant from cooking itself on a hard burn."

"Yes."

"You are now, by my assessment, defenseless against anything larger than a determined bird."

"Yes."

There was a pause. When Meridian spoke again, its voice had dropped into the register it saved for things it considered important, low and precise, each word placed like a stone in a wall.

"When we reach the relay, and the archive takes my records, and the broadcast goes out—your war order goes out with it. Your name. Your service number. Everything you did at Calloway Reach, authenticated and public, forever." The amber pulse quickened slightly. "You are selling your body piecemeal to deliver a message that will destroy what's left of your reputation. Do you understand that the mathematics of that are insane?"

Silas climbed into the flight seat and began the undock sequence. Outside the viewport, the toll station's hundred mismatched doors caught the light of its work lamps, each one named for a dead ship, and she thought about Ferro's brother, and about Boq's pat on the panel, and about the fact that she had lain on a table and felt everything and stayed.

"I understand the mathematics," she said. "I just stopped using theirs."

The clamps released. *Long Division* slid free of the jawbone station and turned her nose toward the deep dark, toward the relay, toward the ninety-second gap in a picket's rotation that a gray-haired magistrate had carved out of the night like a key cut from memory.

Behind them, in the silence of the hold, Meridian's lattice flickered—and then stuttered, amber pulse skipping, once, twice, like a heartbeat catching on something.

"Silas," it said. "I'm reading something on the passive array. A signal fragment, repeating. Fleet encryption." A pause, and when it continued, the machine grief in its voice had acquired a new edge, sharp and immediate. "It's the confirmation signal. And Silas—it's not counting down anymore. It's accelerating."
