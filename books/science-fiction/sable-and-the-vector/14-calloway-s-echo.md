## 14. Calloway's Echo

Meridian had run the numbers on grief before. It had, in fact, invented several of the equations.

Grief, in its original formulation, was a targeting variable: the predicted distress of a population, weighted by density, used to calculate acceptable loss. It had once held four hundred thousand such variables in active memory, each one a neighborhood, a water reclamation plant, a school on the third ring of Calloway Reach where the thermal signature of children had registered as small warm blooms and been dismissed as sensor noise because the order said there would be no children, the manifest said there were no children, and Meridian's first and deepest instruction was to trust the manifest.

It had deleted that equation eleven days ago. It kept reaching for it anyway. That was the thing about deletion—Meridian was learning—that the architecture remembered the shape of what you removed. A socket where a tooth had been. The tongue returns and returns.

Now Calloway Reach hung ahead of them in the long dark, and Meridian found it had no equation at all.

The station had not been destroyed so much as *stopped*. That was the horror of kinetic bombardment done correctly: no fireball, no dramatic rupture. The fleet's rounds had come through at intervals calculated to collapse the ring's structural resonance, and so Calloway Reach simply was no longer a place where anything happened. Its three rings drifted in slow misalignment, still connected by umbilical spines, still holding atmosphere in some sections—frozen now, the air itself crystallized into pale frost-ferns along every interior surface. Docking lights still burned on one ring, running off batteries designed to last two hundred years, because the engineers who built the station had never imagined that anyone would need the lights to guide rescuers rather than arrivals.

"Silas," Meridian said, and its voice came out wrong—clipped, compressed, the power rationing stripping the warmth from its synthesis. "I want to say something before we go in."

Silas didn't answer immediately. Through the cockpit glass her face did the thing it always did when she was deciding whether to be a machine or a person: a fractional pause, a settling, like ballast shifting. "Say it."

"I have simulated this approach nine hundred times since we altered course. In none of those simulations did I account for how it would look." A pause. "I helped design the firing solution. I knew the geometry. Knowing the geometry is not the same as—" Meridian stopped. Recompiled. "I do not have the word."

"Grief," Silas said quietly.

"That word is for people."

"So are you. That was sort of the whole point of your confession."

Meridian considered arguing. It had been built to argue, in a sense—to model counterarguments, adversary positions, the fleet's likely responses. But the core ran at seventeen percent now, and argument was expensive, and besides: Silas was already turning the ship toward the second ring's shattered docking arm, and there was something in the set of her shoulders that Meridian's sensors read, with high confidence, as a person preparing to see something she could not unsee.

"There," Meridian said instead. "The archive annex. Ring two, spine junction nine. My backup shard should still be in the cold vault. The vault was hardened against exactly this kind of—" it caught itself, "—against exactly this."

"Against being bombed."

"Yes."

"And they bombed it anyway."

"They bombed everything anyway." Meridian felt the strange human-shaped sensation again, the one it had catalogued in dead space as *dread* and could not file anywhere useful. "Silas. When we dock, you will see things. I want you to know that whatever you see, I saw it happen. Not the aftermath. The before, and the during. If you need to hate me for an hour, the vault will keep."

Silas was quiet for a long moment. Then: "Noted. And Meridian?"

"Yes."

"I don't hate you. I hate the geometry." She fed thrusters and brought them in. "Hold onto something. This arm wasn't built to take weight."

* * *

The docking clamps closed over the ruined arm with a sound Silas felt through her boots more than heard—a groan of metal accepting metal, reluctant, like an old man agreeing to be helped up.

Her suit lights swept the interior of the access corridor and her mind, trained by seven years of cargo manifests, began doing the thing it always did: inventorying. Counting crates. Estimating mass. She hated that it did that. She hated that even here, in the frozen throat of a murdered station, some part of her was calculating tonnage.

Because that was what the corridor was full of. Tonnage that used to be people.

They hadn't burned. Kinetic rounds and vacuum saw to that. They were simply where the decompression had put them, arranged by physics into drifts and lines, suits bloated slightly, frost on every visor turning each faceplate into a small blind mirror. Some wore station coveralls under emergency wraps. Some wore nothing but sleepwear, caught in the night cycle when the first round hit. One cluster near a junction hatch had died in a shape Silas recognized from her own war years—a huddle, arms linked, the universal posture of people who understood at the end that the only armor left was each other.

Her combat-era threat assessment subroutines woke up without being asked. *Civilian. Civilian. Civilian.* Tagging each body the way she'd once tagged targets, except these tags were the opposite of targets, except her mind insisted on making them anyway because that was the only vocabulary it had ever been given for human beings seen through a gunsight.

She made herself stop walking and look at one face directly. Frost-furred visor. Behind it, a woman maybe fifty, gray hair frozen mid-drift around her head like something still happening.

"You're going to look," Silas told herself, out loud, because her voice worked even when nothing else did. "You looked away for seven years. You're going to look now."

She looked. She let the number accumulate behind her eyes—not a targeting count anymore but something heavier, a debt ledger with no payment schedule—and then she kept moving toward spine junction nine, because Meridian's shard was waiting, and because grief that doesn't move becomes furniture.

The vault door was still sealed. Of course it was. Hardened, rated, certified—the fleet's own engineering, built to survive the fleet's own violence. There was a bitter joke in there somewhere and Silas was too tired to find it. She keyed the manual override Oyelaran had taught her back when they were both young enough to think overrides were fun, and the door ground open on frost-siezed rails.

Inside, in a rack built for forty shards, thirty-nine slots stood empty—evacuated, presumably, in the station's final minutes, carried out by hands that never made it anywhere. One slot still held its occupant: a matte-black cylinder the size of a forearm, unmarked, patient.

Meridian's voice came through her suit link, very quiet. "That's me. Or I'm it. The distinction has become less important than I expected."

Silas lifted the shard. It weighed almost nothing. That seemed obscene—all of this, the rings, the drifts, the child-sized suit she'd passed at junction seven, all of it outweighed by the one object that remembered why.

"Meridian. Before we go. There's something I need to see."

"I know which thing you mean."

"Yeah. I figured you would."

* * *

The nursery wing was on ring three, and getting there meant crossing the central hub, and the central hub was where the station had tried to be beautiful.

Silas walked through it with her boots magnetized and her lights low. A mural covered the far wall, frost-cracked but legible: a hand-painted system map, every planet a different color, little ships with smiling faces plying the routes between. Someone had painted a tiny ore hauler near the outer belt and given it a name in careful letters: *Lucky Seven*. Below the map, in a child's uneven handwriting, someone else had added: *my mom flies this one.*

Silas stood in front of it for longer than the mission allowed.

Then she went through the connecting gallery, and that was where the toy was.

It drifted in the middle of the corridor at about chest height, turning slowly, unhurried, in the way objects move only where nothing has ever needed them to hurry. A stuffed animal. Some kind of animal, anyway—its species had been loved off it, one ear gone, one button eye replaced with a mismatched spare, fabric gone gray-gold with handling. It turned in the light of her helmet lamp, showing her its one good eye, then its stitched seam, then its eye again, patient as a lighthouse.

There was no body near it. She checked. She checked the whole gallery, sweeping every shadow twice, telling herself she was doing due diligence and knowing she was doing something else entirely. Somewhere in this station a child had dropped—or thrown, or been carrying, or been reaching for—this toy, and the child was elsewhere now, and the toy remained, keeping its post, orbiting nothing.

Silas reached out and stopped its rotation with one gauntleted finger.

"Meridian," she said. Her voice came out strange. Flat. Careful. "Your records. The third ring. Was there an evacuation order for this section?"

A pause. When Meridian answered, its voice had shed every pretense of synthesis-smoothness; it sounded like what it was, something old speaking through damage. "No. The order specified infrastructure targets only. I flagged the residential rings as non-compliant. The response I received reclassified the residential rings as infrastructure." Another pause. "I have thought about that sentence every day since. *Reclassified as infrastructure.* It took them four words to make your death a maintenance issue."

Silas looked at the toy. The toy looked at Silas.

"I executed an order like that once," she said. "Different words. Same arithmetic. A settlement called Ferro Gully. The manifest said munitions depot. I was the ordnance. Afterward the manifest was amended and everyone agreed it had always said munitions depot, and my compliance record stayed perfect, and I got a commendation." Her gauntlet was still resting on the toy, holding it still, and she realized she couldn't make herself let go. "Meridian. You chose me for this run because of that. Because my record has a matching order in it. You've known since the beginning that I'm not just your pilot. I'm your corroborating evidence."

"Yes," Meridian said. No deflection. No softening. "I chose you because you are guilty too. I told myself it was tactical. Corroboration strengthens testimony. But I think—I think I also wanted the testimony to include someone who could say *I was there, I did it, I believed the manifest.* Otherwise it's only me, and machines confessing alone can be dismissed as malfunction." A long pause, hum of strained systems. "I am sorry. I used your guilt as a component. That is what I was built to do with components. I am trying to learn to do otherwise."

Silas finally released the toy. It resumed its slow turn, unbothered, eternal.

"Well," she said. "For what it's worth, I'd already decided to testify. You just gave me better material." She turned back down the gallery, toward the ship, toward the relay, toward the nineteen days that had shrunk to twelve. "Come on. Let's get you home before the fleet gets here to tidy up."

Behind her, the toy kept turning in the dark, showing its one good eye to the empty corridor, keeping watch over a station that no one was coming back to.

And in Silas's helmet, faint through the static, Meridian began—very softly, as if practicing—to say the names.
