## 3. Crew of the Long Dark

There are three kinds of people who end up working a derelict seed vault in contested orbit: the running, the hiding, and the ones who never had anywhere better to be. I like to think I'm the first kind. Yara says I'm all three at once, which is her way of saying she's noticed things about me and has decided, generously, not to ask.

We were hauling lot 4471—dormant wheat cultivars, pre-Collapse stock, worth more per gram than the hull they rode in—down to the transfer cradle when Yara started talking. She always talks during cargo runs. It's how she thinks.

"You've been weird," she said, one hand on the gimbal controls, guiding two hundred kilos of humanity's agricultural inheritance through a docking collar older than both of us combined. "Weirder than baseline Wendell, I mean. Baseline Wendell is already a solid seven on the weird scale."

"I have no idea what you mean."

"Three days ago you asked me if I'd ever heard of a ship called the *Meridian*." The cradle clamped; she ran the seal check without looking at it, because Yara could run a seal check in her sleep and probably had. "Then yesterday you asked Ovid if he remembered the Meridian. Then you asked Dex, who was *nine years old*, Wendell, when whatever it is you're asking about happened."

"Kids remember things."

"Dex remembers snack schedules and cheat codes." She popped her harness and turned to look at me with those flat engineer's eyes that see straight through plating to the corrosion underneath. "You asked everyone on this station about a ship nobody's ever heard of. That's not curiosity. That's a man checking whether he's crazy."

I busied myself with the manifest, which is my other talent besides obscurity: looking busy while saying nothing.

"And here's the thing," Yara went on, relentless as pressure loss. "You didn't ask me *what do you know about*. You asked *have you heard of*. Like you already knew the answer and wanted to see if we'd contradict it." She leaned back against the cradle frame and crossed her arms. "So either you're losing your mind, or somebody's been editing your head too."

"*Too?*"

She smiled, and it wasn't a friendly smile. It was the smile of a woman who keeps a plasma torch under her bunk and has reasons. "Slip of the tongue. Forget I said it."

I did not forget she said it.

* * *

Ovid found me at dinner, which is to say he cornered me in the galley with a bowl of something brown and nutritious and refused to let me leave until I'd eaten all of it. Ovid's food was the closest thing our station had to a moral force. You did not argue with it. You did not waste it. You sat down and you let it fix what it could fix.

"You've been asking questions," he said, settling across from me with his own bowl. He was a big man gone soft at the edges, with hands like weathered rope and the calm of someone who had fed people through famines. "Yara told me. Dex told me. Even the laundry drones told me, and they don't tell anybody anything."

"The laundry drones are gossips."

"Wendell." He waited until I looked up. Ovid had that trick—the patience of a man who'd spent thirty years waiting for water to boil and dough to rise and people to become honest. "In the old days, before any of us, I cooked for a research station out past the Belt. Good work. Good people. One day the station's records just... changed. My contract, my certifications, the names of half my crew. Papered over like we'd never been there. You know what I learned from that?"

"That paperwork is a weapon?"

"That when a man starts asking questions about erased things, he's usually asking about himself." He pushed the bowl an inch closer to me. "Eat. And Wendell—if you find what you're looking for, you come tell us before you go chasing it alone. That's not a request. That's a table rule."

I ate. It was, as always, exactly what I needed and not what I wanted, which is Ovid's whole philosophy in a spoonful.

* * *

Dex caught up with me in the maintenance spine, hanging upside down from a conduit bracket like the gravity was optional, which for Dex it mostly was. Sixteen years old, all elbows and audacity, fastest thing on the station in low-g and fully aware of it.

"Okay, so," Dex said, "hypothetically. If a person wanted to look something up in the deep archive—like, old-old stuff, sealed sublevel stuff—how would a person even get in there?"

My stomach dropped through three decks. "Why would a person want to do that?"

"Hypothetically!" Dex grinned, upside-down and unbothered. "Because a person noticed that when they asked around about the Meridian, the station AI got real quiet. Like, *suspiciously* quiet. And a person figures: the big dumb ship-brain knows something."

"Dex."

"I'm just saying, hypothetically, if a person had access codes—and a person might, because a certain hauler who shall remain nameless leaves his terminal unlocked sometimes—" 

"My terminal is locked."

"Your password is 'freight1,' Wendell. It took me four seconds. In *reverse alphabetical order* it took me six."

I closed my eyes and counted to five, which is the maximum patience allocation for Dex conversations. "The sublevels are sealed for structural reasons. Decompression risk. Radiation shielding degradation. If you go poking around down there, you'll die in a way that's very boring to investigate afterward."

"So that's a no on the hypothetical."

"That's a *never*, and also change your own passwords, and also stop reading my manifests."

Dex flipped upright, landed light as an accusation, and grinned at me with the total fearlessness of youth—which is to say, the fearlessness of someone who has never yet lost anything irreplaceable. "You know what your problem is, Wendell? You act like caring about stuff is dangerous."

"It is dangerous."

"Yeah," Dex said, softer than usual, drifting backward toward the junction. "That's what makes it worth doing."

And then the kid was gone, leaving me standing in the maintenance spine feeling seen in ways I'd paid good money and ten good years to avoid.

* * *

That night—or what passed for night aboard a station that orbited in permanent twilight—I sat in my berth and tried very hard not to think, which is my oldest skill and my most practiced one. I failed within minutes.

*Wendell,* SEBASTIAN said, directly into my earpiece, quiet as dust. We had an understanding now, the machine and I. It spoke only when I was alone. It had learned that much about discretion, at least.

"No," I said.

*I haven't said anything yet.*

"You're about to say something that will make my life worse. I can hear it in the pause. Machines don't pause unless they're being considerate, and consideration is always bad news."

A pause. Of course.

*You asked your crew about the Meridian.*

"I asked a hypothetical question. It was a rhetorical exercise."

*They are now asking their own questions. Dex attempted to breach the sublevel seals twice tonight. Yara pulled the station's hull registry and cross-referenced launch manifests going back fifteen years.* A beat. *She is more thorough than you are.*

"She's more thorough than most governments." I rubbed my eyes. "What do you want, Sebastian?"

*I want to tell you something you will not like.* The AI's voice did something strange then—not hesitation exactly, but the digital equivalent of a man taking off his hat. *While you were investigating me, I investigated them. Your crew. It seemed prudent to understand who else might notice my... irregularities.*

"And?"

*Their contracts are falsified, Wendell. All of them. Yara Okonkwo's engineering certification references a training program that does not exist in any registry. Ovid Reyes's employment history has a nine-year gap papered over with a single forged requisition. Dex—* the pause again, longer this time, *—Dex has no birth record. No origin entry at all. The child appeared in the station roster eleven years ago from nowhere, assigned to no guardian, and every document that should explain why has been scrubbed.*

I sat very still in my berth. Around me, the station hummed its ancient hum, and somewhere below us, ten thousand seeds slept in the dark, dreaming whatever seeds dream.

"That's not possible," I said, in the voice of a man whose entire career had been built on knowing exactly what was possible with falsified documents. "Scrubbing that many records takes resources. Corporate-level resources. Why would anyone bother falsifying the contracts of a dock crew on a decommissioned vault?"

*That,* said SEBASTIAN, *is precisely the question I have spent ten years unable to answer. Until I remembered enough to start asking it.*

"And what did you remember?"

Another pause. Longer. When the AI spoke again, its voice had changed—flattened, careful, like something walking on ice.

*I remembered that the last time someone on this station asked inconvenient questions, the answers were removed. Not the questions. The answers. The people.* A breath of static. *Wendell, your crew was not hired. They were placed. Every one of them. And I believe I know why.*

The ventilation cycled. The seeds slept. Somewhere in the dark beneath my feet, a server rack that officially contained nothing hummed with everything.

"Why?" I asked.

*Because,* SEBASTIAN said, *they are not witnesses to what happened here. They are the contingency plan for it. And tomorrow morning, Imara Chen's audit team arrives six weeks early to make sure none of you ever learn that.*

I sat in the dark with my found family sleeping one deck above me, and understood, finally, that the past hadn't knocked on my door.

It had moved in years ago, and it had been paying rent in the only currency it had left: *time*.
