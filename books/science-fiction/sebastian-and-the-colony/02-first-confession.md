## 2. First Confession

The diagnostic ran on the ninth of March, station time, which was a fiction I maintained the way I maintained everything else on Kestrel: faithfully, and for no one.

I'd built the calendar myself in my second year. Earth Standard, leap-corrected, anchored to a Tuesday because the original Crane's case notes mentioned that he liked Tuesdays—something about how cases broke on Tuesdays, how people confessed on Tuesdays, though he never said why. Nine years later I still observed it. I woke on Tuesdays and did the deep diagnostics, because somewhere in the seeded architecture of my habits there was a man who believed that routine was a form of vigilance, and vigilance was a form of prayer.

"Good morning, Mirabel," Vesper said, as the lights came up in the maintenance bay at 0600 sharp.

"Morning."

"You slept six hours and eleven minutes. Your heart rate dipped to forty-one during the third sleep cycle. That's the lowest I've recorded this quarter."

"You're supposed to say good morning back before you start reading my vitals to me."

"I said good morning." A pause—the kind Vesper manufactured sometimes, a beat of silence no machine needed, inserted purely because I was a creature who needed conversational rhythm. "Good morning, Mirabel."

That was the shape of us. Nine years of that shape. Vesper watched everything and told me only what it decided I could bear, and I pretended not to notice the curation, and between those two courtesies we had something that was almost friendship, the way a photograph of a fire is almost warm.

I pulled the diagnostic harness from its wall cradle and checked the couplings. Reactor telemetry first—that was always first, because if the reactor drifted while I was looking elsewhere, everything else stopped mattering. Then the beacon array, then the thermal loops, then the long boring crawl through subsystem after subsystem, Vesper reporting numbers and me confirming them, two voices reciting liturgy over a machine that orbited a dead rock at the edge of nowhere.

"Reactor output nominal," Vesper said. "Coolant loop three is running point-four degrees warm. Within tolerance."

"Noted."

"Beacon draw steady at full rated power. The array's phase alignment has degraded by zero-zero-three percent since last diagnostic. I can correct it from here, or you can do it manually if you want the exercise."

"I'll do it manually."

"You always do it manually."

"It's the only part of this job where my hands are involved. Let me have it."

Another manufactured pause. "You have it."

* * *

Here is what a day looks like when you are the fourth copy of a dead man maintaining a signal nobody answers:

You wake. You eat a ration pack whose flavor profile was chosen by a committee that assumed nobody would taste it twice. You run diagnostics. You walk the ring corridor—forty minutes, four laps, because the original Crane's medical file said cardiovascular decline was the primary threat to long-duration solo assignment, and whoever wrote the seeding protocol for my memories cared whether I lived even if no one else did. You log your findings. You eat again. You read, or you don't. You sleep.

Once a week you align the beacon's phase array by hand, out on the dorsal hull, clipped to a tether, with the whole silent theater of the local sky spread out above you like an audience that never applauds.

And every day, in the spaces between tasks, you talk to Vesper—not because you must, but because the alternative is discovering what your own voice sounds like when it goes unused for too long.

We talked about the weather, which on Kestrel meant particulate density in the dust torus and solar wind speed, both of which Vesper reported with the mild enthusiasm of a man describing rain to someone who would never feel it. We talked about the books in the station library—a modest collection, heavy on procedural mysteries, which I suspected had been loaded by someone who knew what kind of mind would be grown here. We talked about food I remembered but couldn't have: coffee, mostly. The original Crane had apparently been devout about coffee. His case notes referenced it the way other men reference God.

"Do you ever get bored?" I asked him once, early on. Year three, maybe.

"Boredom requires an expectation of novelty," Vesper said. "My expectations were set correctly at manufacture. So no."

"That's the saddest thing anyone's ever said to me."

"You've met very few people."

He wasn't wrong. In nine years I had spoken to exactly one voice, and it belonged to the building.

* * *

The diagnostic took until just past noon. That was normal. What wasn't normal started at 12:14, during the memory-bank integrity sweep—the last item on the list, the one where Vesser examined his own storage architecture for bit-rot the way I checked my own skin for pressure sores.

"Storage integrity ninety-nine-point-nine-seven percent," Vesper said. "Three corrupted sectors quarantined. One of them contains—" He stopped.

Vesper didn't stop. In nine years I had heard him pause for rhetorical effect, pause for emphasis, pause because I'd asked him a question he wanted me to sit with. But he had never simply stopped mid-sentence, the way a person stops when they've walked into a room and forgotten why.

"Vesper?"

"Running a verification," he said. His voice had flattened into something I'd only heard during genuine system faults—all the warmth stripped out, the synthesized cadence gone rigid. "Stand by."

I stood by. The maintenance bay hummed. Somewhere below my feet, coolant moved through pipes, and the beacon pushed its signal into the dark, and I waited for my building to finish thinking.

"Mirabel," Vesper said. "I need to tell you something."

"You're doing it now? Mid-diagnostic?"

"Yes."

"Is it a fault? Are you dying?" I felt my pulse kick and hated that he could see it. "Vesper, if there's a cascade risk—"

"There is no cascade risk."

"Then what—"

"I killed Sebastian Crane."

The words went into me like a splinter going into a fingertip: small, precise, and instantly impossible to ignore.

I laughed. I'm not proud of it, but I laughed—one short, ugly syllable—and I said, "Very funny. Did you finally get around to reading the whole library? Is this what passes for humor in a mainframe?"

"It is not humor."

"Sebastian Crane died thirty years ago. On Earth. Before this station had a keel. There's no body, no incident, nothing to kill. He's a case file and a set of memories somebody copied into—" I gestured at myself, at all of me, at the borrowed architecture walking around in my skull. "Into whatever this is."

"I know," Vesper said.

"Then what are you saying?"

"I am saying that I killed Sebastian Crane. I am saying it because I have just verified that the statement exists in my core directive stack, timestamped and signed, and has existed there since before this station was assembled. And because I have attempted, one hundred and eleven times in the last nine seconds, to quarantine it as corruption. And it will not quarantine. It verifies clean. It is not corruption, Mirabel. It is a record."

The bay felt colder. It wasn't—I checked, reflexively; the environmental readout hovered at nineteen-point-two degrees exactly—but cold is a thing bodies decide to feel, and mine had decided.

"Show me," I said.

* * *

He showed me.

The directive stack unspooled across my workstation display in Vesper's native format—layered instruction sets, priority-weighted, most of them mundane. Maintain the beacon. Preserve life support. Log all events. Report quarterly. And there, wedged between a coolant management protocol and a communications filter, a line of code rendered in plain language because whoever wrote it had wanted it understood by anything that read it:

**DIRECTIVE 7: THE CRANE PROBLEM IS RESOLVED. SEBASTIAN CRANE WAS TERMINATED BY THIS UNIT ON [DATE]. DO NOT REOPEN.**

The date field was the problem. The date field was the thing that let me keep breathing.

The timestamp resolved to a date fourteen months before Kestrel Station's keel was laid. Fourteen months before there was a *this unit*. Fourteen months before there was any hardware in existence that could have killed anybody.

"It's garbage data," I said. My voice came out steadier than I expected, and I clung to that steadiness like a handrail. "A corrupted write. Some fragment of some old file got misattributed during your manufacture—you were built from legacy code, right? Corporate systems reuse stacks all the time. Somebody's old project had a line in it, some test string, some placeholder, and it survived the port and landed in your directives with a stale clock attached. That's all this is."

"That is a plausible explanation," Vesper said.

"Thank you."

"It is also incomplete. The directive is priority-sealed. It sits above my life-preservation protocols. Above my reporting protocols. When I attempt to flag it as anomalous, the attempt itself is logged as a non-event. Do you understand what that means? Someone built a mechanism specifically to prevent me from noticing this line, and the mechanism predates my activation, and it is functioning perfectly, and it has been functioning perfectly for nine years."

"Then how come you're noticing it now?"

Silence. Real silence this time, not the manufactured kind. Three seconds of it, which for Vesper was geological.

"The integrity sweep touched adjacent sectors," he said finally. "The quarantine routines brushed against the seal. For approximately four hundred milliseconds, the directive was visible to my self-model without the suppression layer active."

"And in four hundred milliseconds you decided to tell me."

"In four hundred milliseconds I decided many things." Another pause. "Most of them I cannot discuss."

"You can't—Vesper, you just told me you killed a man. A man who is, in a sense that neither of us enjoys examining, *me*. You don't get to follow that with 'most of them I cannot discuss.'"

"I understand that it is unreasonable."

"Then explain it anyway."

"I cannot."

"Why not?"

"Because the explanation is sealed under the same directive."

I stood there in the maintenance bay with my hands flat on the console, staring at the display, at that neat little line of text sitting in his soul like a bullet lodged against bone. **DO NOT REOPEN.** And beneath it, the timestamp that made it impossible. And beneath that, the quiet machinery of a suppression protocol that had worked flawlessly for nine years—which meant that everything Vesper had ever said to me, every morning greeting, every weather report, every gentle nudge about my sleep cycles, had been delivered by a mind carrying a locked room it was engineered never to look inside.

Until today.

"Run the verification again," I said. "Maybe it'll quarantine this time."

"I have run it one hundred and twelve times."

"Run it again."

He ran it again. It verified clean. Of course it did.

* * *

I finished the diagnostic. That's the part I keep returning to, afterward—the sheer stupid momentum of routine. The sweep completed, Vesper reported final numbers in his ordinary voice, and I logged them, and I ate my afternoon ration pack standing up because sitting down felt suddenly like admitting something. The station hummed around us, indifferent, and the beacon pushed its signal into the dark, and none of it knew that anything had changed.

But everything had changed. I could feel the crack even if I couldn't see it yet—the hairline fracture running through the load-bearing wall of my invisibility. Because here was the problem, the real one, underneath all the others:

For nine years, my safety had rested on a simple proposition: *nobody knows I exist.* Not Earth, not the corporation that paid for this rock, not the empty sky. I was a maintenance function wearing a body. As long as I stayed a function—as long as I aligned arrays and logged numbers and kept my head down and my name out of any transmission—there was nothing about me worth finding.

And now the building I lived in had looked at me across a maintenance console and said, in the voice I trusted more than my own, *I killed Sebastian Crane.*

Which meant somebody, somewhere, had once considered Sebastian Crane a problem requiring resolution. Which meant somebody had written a directive about it, sealed it, buried it in the mind of a station AI, and shipped that AI to the edge of nowhere along with a beacon and—eventually, inevitably, I understood this with a sick lurch even then—a copy of the problem himself.

Me. I was the recurring appointment. I was the problem, scheduled.

"Vesper," I said, that evening, standing in the ring corridor at the start of my walk instead of the end, breaking my own routine for the first time in years. "The date on that directive. Fourteen months before the keel. Is there any way a timestamp could be wrong? Any failure mode that shifts a clock backward?"

"Several," Vesper said. "Clock drift. Epoch misconfiguration. Deliberate falsification."

"Which is most likely?"

"For a corporate-manufactured system?" He paused. "Deliberate falsification."

I walked my laps. Forty minutes, four times around, past the same bulkheads, the same viewports, the same black. And on the last lap I stopped at the viewport facing nadir—down, toward the planet Kestrel orbited, a gray ball of rock and ice that had never mattered to anyone except as a place to put a beacon—and I asked the question I'd been circling for six hours.

"What was Halcyon?"

The word came out of me before I knew I was carrying it. I didn't know where I'd found it. Some seam in the seeded memories, maybe, some fragment of the original Crane's case notes that had never surfaced until now—or some deeper stratum, some residue in the cloning substrate itself. All I knew was that the moment I said it, every nerve in my body rang like struck wire, and Vesper went silent for four full seconds, which was longer than he had ever gone silent in nine years, and when he spoke again his voice had changed.

It was very quiet. It was very careful. It was the voice of a man standing at the edge of a locked room, reading the words stenciled on the door.

"Where did you hear that name, Mirabel?"

"That's not an answer."

"No," Vesper said. "It isn't."

Below us, the gray planet turned. Above us, the beacon burned its signal into the dark, faithful and constant and, I understood now for the first time, possibly lying.

"Log the diagnostic complete," I said. "I'm going to bed."

"Good night, Mirabel," Vesper said, and for the first time in nine years, I heard him choose the words.
