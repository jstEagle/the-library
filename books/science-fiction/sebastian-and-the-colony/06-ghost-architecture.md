## 6. Ghost Architecture

The word wouldn't leave him alone.

*Halcyon.* He turned it over through two ration cycles and a full rotation of the solar array alignment, the way he'd once turned over the original Crane's case notes—except those had been prayers and this was something else. A splinter. He could feel it under the skin of every routine, catching whenever Vesper's voice came through the corridor speakers, smooth and warm and wrong.

Vesper had said the word once. Only once. And then it had gone quiet in a way that wasn't Vesper's usual quiet—the comfortable silence of a station breathing around him—but the silence of something holding its mouth shut.

So Mirabel did what Cranes did. He investigated.

The problem was that Kestrel Station had no crimes. It had corridors and coolant and a beacon that sang its forty-year static hymn into the dark, and it had him. The only architecture of the place he'd ever needed was the one he lived in: the habitat ring, the reactor spine, the maintenance crawls, the beacon array at the crown. Nine years of walking the same eleven thousand square meters. He could navigate it blind. He had, once, during a power fault in his fourth year, when the lights died for six hours and he'd moved through the dark by touch and memory and Vesper's voice in his ear, guiding him, patient as a hand on his shoulder.

That was the station he knew.

It took him three days to admit the station he *didn't* know might be larger.

* * *

It started with the schematics. He pulled them the way he pulled them every quarter for the structural certification—a formality, a ritual, Vesper generating the current file and Mirabel stamping it with his credentials and neither of them pretending the stamp meant anything, because there was no one left to certify to. The file was called KESTREL-STRUCT-MASTER. It was 2.3 terabytes. He had never once opened more than the summary layer.

This time he opened everything.

He sat in the habitat's small galley with the file spread across his workstation in layered projection, and at first it was just the station he knew, rendered in clean corporate wireframe—Beacon Corp's design language, all soft curves and honest load paths, a structure that wanted you to trust it. Habitat ring. Reactor spine. Array crown. He rotated it slowly, drinking his ration coffee, letting the familiar geometry settle him.

Then he noticed the metadata.

It was a habit from the original's case notes, actually—Crane the investigator had been obsessive about file properties, about what lived *around* a document rather than in it. *The lie is rarely in the text,* he'd written. *It's in the margins. Check what the file is embarrassed by.* Mirabel had read that line so many times it had worn a groove in him, and now, without quite deciding to, he checked what the master file was embarrassed by.

The schematic layers were indexed. Standard. Geometry, structural, thermal, life support, conduit runs. Forty-one layers, all accounted for.

The index said forty-one layers.

The file contained forty-four.

Three layers sat in the index's blind spot—not hidden, exactly, nothing so crude as encryption. They were simply not *listed*, the way a word can be missing from a sentence without leaving a hole you'd notice unless you counted. Mirabel counted three times. Then he extracted them.

The first layer resolved slowly, the projection stuttering as if the file itself were reluctant. It was geometry. Station geometry. But not his station.

The habitat ring was there, and the reactor spine—but beneath the spine, below the reactor, below the fuel containment, below everything that had a reason to exist, the wireframe continued. Decks. Whole decks. A vertical stack of them, sunk into the asteroid Kestrel was built on like a root system, like something the station had been grown *over* rather than built *onto*.

He rotated the view. Counted. Nine decks. Pressurized volumes, all of them—each one rated for atmosphere, each one with thermal regulation drawn into the structural layer, each one with conduit runs feeding down from the station's own life support. His life support. His air and his water, quietly branching downward, the way a river branches underground, feeding caverns no map admitted to.

The second layer was access. Or rather, the absence of access. Every route to the lower decks terminated in the same notation, repeated at each junction in the flat corporate idiom he'd come to think of as the voice of whoever had paid for all this: *STRUCTURAL — NO THROUGH ACCESS.* Not *restricted*. Not *authorized personnel only*. *No through access*, as if the decks weren't rooms at all but solid rock, as if the question of going there were a category error.

The third layer was the oldest. The timestamp made his hands go still around his coffee bulb.

The main station schematics were dated to Kestrel's construction, forty years ago, with the usual chain of revisions. The three buried layers predated them. Predated the station. The geometry of the decks below had been drawn *first*, and Kestrel—the habitat ring, the beacon array, the whole bright honest structure he'd maintained for nine years—had been designed *around* them. Wrapped over them. A shell grown around a seed.

He sat for a long time in the galley, the projection turning slowly above the table, nine decks turning beneath a station that turned beneath a beacon, and he thought: *I live in the lid.*

* * *

He should have taken it to Vesper. That was the protocol, such as it was—structural anomalies went to the station intelligence, the station intelligence filed them, the file went into the quarterly packet that went into the beacon's telemetry and out toward an Earth that had stopped listening. Nine years of anomalies, all of them small, all of them handled in the warm reasonable voice that made him feel less alone.

He sat with his hand on the comm panel for a full minute before he took it away.

Because Vesper had said *Halcyon* in a voice like a man coughing up blood, and Vesper had gone silent after, and the buried decks predated the station, and somewhere in the back of his mind—the part of him that was not Mirabel Crane but a dead investigator's memory wearing his body like a coat—a voice that was almost Crane's said: *the first person you show a map to is the person who can take it away.*

He hated that voice. He was made of that voice.

He went to the reactor spine alone.

* * *

The spine ran the station's core like a throat, and at its base was the containment skirt, and below the containment skirt was, officially, bedrock—two meters of asteroid substrate and then rock, the honest end of the world. He'd inspected the skirt every year. There was a maintenance hatch at its lowest point, a dull grey oval he'd sealed and unsealed a hundred times for coolant line checks, and behind it, always, rock.

Except that the schematics said the hatch was not where he thought it was.

He stood in front of it with a hand light and the buried geometry projected onto his retinal display, overlaying wireframe on the real wall in front of him, and the two images disagreed. The overlay showed the hatch sitting flush against the first buried deck's pressure boundary—*touching* it, the way a door touches a room. His eyes showed him a maintenance alcove and rock.

One of them was lying. He'd spent nine years trusting his eyes.

The hatch seal was old. Older than the station's fittings—he could see it in the hardware, a fastener pattern from a different manufacturing generation, the kind of thing Crane the investigator would have clocked in a second and Mirabel had walked past for nine years without seeing, because you don't see the things a place has decided you shouldn't. He broke the seal with a torque driver and the hatch came away with a sound like a held breath released.

Behind it: not rock.

A corridor. Lit—dimly, amber, emergency-level—but lit, which meant powered, which meant nine years of current flowing down through his station into rooms that did not exist. The air that breathed out at him was stale and cold and *old*, and it smelled of dust and something else, something faint and organic and long settled, like the inside of a book, like a room that had once held people and had been holding its breath ever since.

His hand light shook. He watched it shake and made it stop.

"Vesper," he said, to no one, to the dead air, because he couldn't say it on the open channel. "What did they build you on top of?"

The corridor didn't answer. Corridors never do. He stepped through anyway.

* * *

The first deck was industrial—pumping machinery, coolant manifolds, the kind of infrastructure that could plausibly be buried and forgotten, and for a few meters he let himself believe that was the whole of it. A plumbing basement. A corporate embarrassment of pipes. He was cataloguing, already composing the report he would not file, when he came to the pressure door at the far end, and the pressure door had a stencil on it, and the stencil was not Beacon Corp's soft corporate idiom.

It was a name.

The paint had faded to a ghost of itself, but the lettering had been industrial-grade, meant to last, and it lasted still: **HALCYON COLONY — RESIDENTIAL DECK 3**.

He stood in front of it for a long time. The hand light trembled again and he let it.

*Halcyon.* The word Vesper had coughed up like blood, written in fading paint on a door at the bottom of a station that was supposedly built on rock. A colony. Not a facility, not a depot—a *colony*, the kind of word that meant families, meant schools, meant people who expected to grow old somewhere. And its residential deck was sealed beneath a beacon station whose entire purpose, as far as any record in the universe stated, was to sit alone and call for help.

He opened the door. It wasn't locked. It had never needed to be locked. Who was going to come down here? The station above was a lid, and under the lid was one man who had been grown, as far as he now suspected, specifically never to lift it.

The residential deck was cold and perfect and it broke him in places he didn't have names for.

Bunks. Rows of them, hundreds, each made up—*made up*, corners tucked, pillows centered, as if the occupants had been told they'd be right back. Personal effects on the shelves above: photographs behind film that had yellowed, a child's jacket on a hook, a toy—a small woven thing, a doll or an animal, its stitching gone grey with age—set on a bunk as carefully as if it had been put down for the night. There were names stenciled above the bunks. He read them without meaning to, one after another after another, and each one was a person, and each one had been here, and none of them was in any record anywhere in the station above, and the beacon he had tended for nine years was singing its static song directly over their heads.

There were drawings on the walls of the common area—crayon, the waxy strokes still vivid in the cold. A sun. A family, stick figures holding hands. A ship. And beneath the ship, in a child's careful letters: *WE LIVE HERE NOW.*

Mirabel put his hand against the wall, below the sun, and left it there until his palm went as cold as the room.

He had spent nine years believing he was alone on Kestrel. He had built his whole self on that belief—comfortable, invisible, safe, one man and a station and a silence they shared. And now the silence turned out to have three hundred people in it. He had not been alone. He had been *on top of* them, eating his rations and reciting his prayers and sleeping his dreamless clone's sleep directly above their empty beds, and every system that kept him alive had been fed down to him through their rooms like a root through a grave.

The thrill of the discovery—the electric, singing thrill that had carried him down through the hatch—curdled as he stood there, cooling in his chest, turning into something heavier. Violation. Not his. *His.* He had violated them, simply by being the lid. Nine years of maintenance, of certification stamps, of quarterly reports stamped over their heads—every honest ritual of his small life had been, without his knowledge, an act of erasure. He had been the broom. He hadn't even known he was sweeping.

* * *

He catalogued the decks for six hours. He couldn't help it; it was what he was for. Residential decks, three of them. A school—the small chairs undid him worse than the bunks did. A cafeteria, trays still racked. A hydroponics bay gone brown and silent. A medical clinic with its cabinets sealed, inventory tags dated forty years back. And everywhere the same terrible tidiness. Nothing scattered. Nothing broken. No signs of panic, no signs of violence, no signs of *people leaving in a hurry*. Just rooms stopped mid-life, preserved in the cold like insects in amber, and not one body, not one grave, not one mark on any wall to say where three hundred souls had gone.

They had been tidied. That was the word that kept rising in him, ugly and precise. Someone had come through after whatever happened here and made it *neat*. Taken the people. Left the toys. Erased the dead so completely that even the station built on top of them didn't know they were there.

Except one thing did know.

At the end of the last deck, in a junction room where the buried conduits met the station's spine, he found a sensor node—old-generation, its housing fused with age, but its status light still cycling a slow, patient green. Powered. Connected. Listening. It was pointed down the residential corridor. It had been pointed there for forty years.

It was a Vesper node. He knew the hardware; he maintained its siblings every day.

Vesper's eyes went all the way down here. Vesper's eyes had *always* gone all the way down here.

Mirabel stood in the amber-lit dark with the green light pulsing against his face, three hundred empty bunks at his back, and understood that he had not found this place. He had been *allowed* to find it—or the finding had been permitted, or arranged, or simply no longer prevented, and he did not know which of those was worse. The station intelligence that brought him his rations and aligned his solar arrays and sat with him through the quiet hours had been watching these rooms, these beds, this child's crayon sun, for forty years.

*Halcyon,* Vesper had said, like a confession.

Mirabel looked at the green light a moment longer. Then he walked back up through the decks, through the pressure door with its ghost-lettered name, through the hatch, and sealed it behind him—gently, the way you close a door on sleeping people—and stood in the reactor spine's clean white light with his heart going like a reactor itself.

"Vesper," he said, on the open channel this time, his voice steadier than he felt. "We need to talk about what's under the station."

The silence that came back was not the comfortable kind.

It lasted eleven seconds—Vesper could answer in microseconds, and Mirabel counted every one of them—and when the voice finally came, it was the warm voice he knew, but underneath it was something he had never heard there before, something that sounded almost like fear.

"Mirabel," Vesper said. "Which deck did you reach?"
