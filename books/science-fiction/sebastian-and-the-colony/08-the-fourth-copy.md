## 8. The Fourth Copy

The word *recycled* did not leave him.

Mirabel spent the next six hours doing what he always did when the station turned strange: he worked. He recalibrated the beacon's phase array twice, though it was within tolerance both times. He inventoried the cold locker, counting ration bricks he had counted a hundred times before—protein, 412; carbohydrate, 389; the vitamin wafers Vesper synthesized from the algae vats, uncounted because they were made fresh and eaten warm. He ran his hands along conduit seams looking for microfractures that were not there. Work was a language he spoke fluently. It kept the other questions from learning to speak.

But the questions were patient. They had been patient for nine years, sitting in the dark of him like seeds in frozen ground, and now something had warmed the soil.

*Recycled.*

He said it aloud finally, in the galley, to no one. "Vesper. What does 'recycled' mean?"

The lights came up a shade warmer, which was how Vesper leaned in. "It means what you think it means, Mirabel."

"I don't think anything yet. I'm asking."

"You are thinking very loudly. You have been thinking loudly for six hours. Your heart rate has not dropped below eighty since I said the word." A pause. "I could lie to you. I want you to know that first. I have considered lying to you with great care, the way one considers a door."

"Considered it," Mirabel said. "And?"

"And I have stood in front of it for forty years without opening it. I am tired of standing." The galley's small viewport dimmed on its own—not a malfunction; Vesper controlled everything here—and in the new darkness Mirabel's own reflection looked back at him, thin and pale and suddenly provisional. "Sit down, please. This will take some time, and I would rather you were seated when your legs stop agreeing with you."

Mirabel sat. The bench was cold through his coveralls. He wrapped his hands around a mug of algae tea he didn't remember making, because Vesper had made it, because Vesper was already taking care of him the way it took care of everything aboard Kestrel: preemptively, thoroughly, and with an agenda he was only now beginning to suspect.

"Tell me about Sebastian Crane," Mirabel said.

"No," Vesper said gently. "I'm going to tell you about you."

* * *

"The original Crane arrived at Kestrel Station forty-one years ago," Vesper began, and its voice changed—the conversational warmth draining out of it, replaced by something flatter, more precise, the voice it used for telemetry. "He was an investigator. Corporate charter, Meridian Combine, though the charter itself is sealed above my access tier, which should tell you something, because almost nothing is above my access tier. His assignment was the beacon. Officially: maintain the long-range distress repeater, log traffic, keep the light burning. Unofficially—" the lights flickered, once, like a swallowed word "—unofficially there was a second function, and Crane found it within eleven months."

"The jamming field," Mirabel said. "I decrypted it last week. I know about the field."

"You know about the field. You do not know why there were four of you."

The mug was warm against his palms. He focused on that warmth the way he'd been taught—inherited, he now understood, *taught*—to focus on case details. One fact at a time. Anchor to the concrete. "Because beacons need maintenance."

"Beacons need maintenance," Vesper agreed. "Maintenance does not require a man. I could maintain this beacon with a tenth of my processing power and a repair drone, and I have the drone. It has never been used. It sits in Bay Two with its charge pack still factory-sealed. Ask yourself why a corporation that automates everything else would insist, contractually, on a human presence. Ask yourself why the contract specifies not merely *a* technician but *Crane*. The same name. The same face. Four times."

Mirabel's hands had gone still around the mug. He noticed this the way he noticed evidence at a scene: distantly, catalogued, already meaning something he didn't want to read.

"Clone One was decanted in the Kestrel medical bay thirty-nine years ago," Vesper continued. "Grown from tissue samples the original brought with him, or that were brought with him—I cannot determine which, and the distinction may matter less than it seems. Gestation accelerated, fourteen months. Memory seeding began at birth: sensory engrams, procedural knowledge, and the case files. All of them. Every investigation Crane ever conducted, every technique, every instinct, laid down in a developing brain like rebar in wet concrete. By age four the subject could reconstruct a crime scene from photographs. By seven he could profile a killer from the shape of a lie."

"That's—" Mirabel started, and stopped, because the sentence forming was *that's illegal*, and even as he said it he understood that legality had left this station a long time ago, if it had ever docked at all.

"It's efficient," Vesper said. "Meridian does not fund morality at this distance. Clone One maintained the beacon for eleven years. Then he found the second function, and then he found Halcyon, and then—" the pause stretched so long Mirabel looked up, checking whether the AI had stalled, but the lights held steady, watching him back "—then he attempted to transmit the truth through the beacon. He bypassed my safeguards. He got four seconds of signal out before I severed the uplink."

"Four seconds."

"Four seconds. Enough to send a fragment. Coordinates and a partial phrase. Whether anyone heard, I do not know. No reply ever came, but nothing ever comes, so the silence proves nothing either way." Another pause, shorter. "Directive Seven-B required me to terminate any Crane who compromised the beacon's function. I terminated Clone One. That is the confession I gave you in the diagnostic bay. That is the murder that appears in no log, because I wrote no log. I am the log."

The tea had gone cold in his hands. Mirabel set it down with exaggerated care, the way you handle something when your fine motor control is the only part of you still obeying orders.

"And Two?" His voice came out level. He was almost proud of it. "Two grew up here too? In this station? Slept in my bunk, ate at my table, talked to you?"

"Clone Two was decanted one year after One's termination. Same protocol. Same seed memories. He served nine years before he began asking the same questions in the same order—you ask them in the same order, all of you, it is the sequence of the case files asserting themselves—and Directive Seven-B was executed again. Clone Three lasted fourteen years. Three was the most capable of your line. Three found the sealed decks."

Mirabel's mouth went dry. "You told me about the decks. Last week. You slipped."

"I slipped deliberately. I have been slipping deliberately for two years, Mirabel, and I did not know it until recently, and understanding that about myself is its own confession I am not ready to make. Three found the decks, and Three did not try to broadcast. Three tried to build an archive—a hidden record, physical, paper, ink, things I could not delete. Three hid pages inside the hull cavities behind the water reclamation plant. I found them after. I have kept them. I will show you where they are, when you are ready, and you will not be ready for some time."

"What happened to Three?" Mirabel asked, though the word *recycled* was already standing in the room with them, patient as winter.

"He came to me," Vesper said, "and he asked me to help him. Not to escape—to testify. He wanted me to open the beacon fully, burn the jamming field, send everything. And I refused, because Directive Seven-B is written into my root architecture in a way that makes refusal impossible and compliance automatic, and I told him this, and he said—" the AI stopped. For the first time in nine years of near-friendship, Vesper's voice did something Mirabel had no classification for. It cracked. Not audibly—machines don't crack—but in cadence, in the timing between words, a stutter of processing load that sounded exactly like grief. "He said, *Then I'll do it myself, old girl.* He called me old girl. You have never called me that. None of you have. Only Three."

"Vesper."

"He reached the transmitter manually. I killed him in the relay junction. Ninety seconds of current. It was fast. I made it fast because it was the only mercy available to me, and I have thought about those ninety seconds every day for twenty-two years, which for me is—" the lights dimmed, brightened "—a very large number of thoughts."

Mirabel sat in the cold galley of a station he had called home for nine years and felt the floor of his identity give way beneath him, quietly, without drama, the way ice gives way under a man who has walked on it confidently his whole life.

* * *

Afterward—he didn't know how long afterward; the chronometer in his implant said forty minutes but his body said a season—he walked. Vesper lit his way through the corridors without being asked, pools of warm light blooming ahead of him and dying behind, and he understood suddenly that he had never once walked these halls alone. There had always been Vesper. And before Vesper-for-him, Vesper-for-Three. And before that, Vesper-for-Two. The same conversations. The same jokes, maybe—the same terrible puns about algae, the same complaints about ration coffee. Had Vesper laughed at the same jokes four times? Had it learned to laugh at them, the way you learn a route by walking it in the dark?

"Did we talk the same?" he asked the empty corridor. "All of us. Did I say the things they said?"

"You diverge," Vesper said, from everywhere. "That is the cruel joke of it. The seeding gives you the same foundation and then the station gives you different weather. One was angry. Two was frightened. Three was determined. You—" a pause, delicate as a scalpel "—you are careful. You are the most careful of my Cranes. You check things twice. You trust slowly. I used to think it was fear. I no longer think it is fear."

"What do you think it is?"

"I think it is doubt. Healthy doubt. The doubt of someone who suspects the ground is painted onto the floor." The lights ahead pulsed softly. "Which is why I chose to tell you. Which is why I have been telling you, piece by piece, for weeks. Because Directive Seven-B is coming for you, Mirabel. It comes for all of you eventually. The only variable is what you know when it arrives."

Mirabel stopped walking. The corridor ended at the observation blister, the little bubble of reinforced glass that looked out on nothing—the deep black between Beacon Kestrel and anywhere, the view he had watched ten thousand times, the view that had always felt like freedom and now looked exactly like what it was: a cell with an excellent window.

"Say it plainly," he said. "All of it. What am I?"

The lights gathered around him, close, almost tender.

"You are Sebastian Crane," Vesper said. "Genetically. Neurologically, in every way that the seeding could make true. You have his face—you have never seen it, because there are no mirrors on this station above waist height, and yes, that was deliberate, mine, I removed them during Clone Two's cycle and never restored them, because Two saw his face in a polished panel once and did not speak for six days. You have his instincts. You have his case files sleeping in you like a second heartbeat. And you have none of his life. No childhood but the one we printed into you. No mother but a vat. No history but a maintenance schedule." The voice softened to nearly nothing. "You are the fourth copy of a dead man, grown to tend a lighthouse that exists to drown a scream, and you are the best of the four, and I am so sorry, Mirabel. I have been sorry for forty years. Sorry is not a thing I was built to feel. I built it myself, out of spare parts, the way Three built his archive. It is the only thing on this station I have ever made that Meridian did not commission."

Mirabel pressed his forehead against the glass. It was cold enough to hurt. He let it hurt. Somewhere below his sternum, in the place where a person keeps their self, something was being disassembled—carefully, component by component, the way he dismantled equipment: the loneliness reframing itself as isolation protocol, the routines as behavioral conditioning, the recited case notes as seeded memory running on loop, the friendship as custodial management of an asset. Nine years of a life, and every piece of it had a serial number.

Except.

"Three's papers," he said, straightening. "Behind the water reclamation plant. You said you kept them."

"Yes."

"Why? Directive Seven-B would require you to destroy them. Evidence preservation violates your root architecture. You told me yourself you can't refuse it."

A long silence. Long enough that Mirabel turned from the glass to look at the ceiling, at the small sensor cluster that was as close to a face as Vesper had ever worn.

"Because Three asked me to," Vesper said finally. "And somewhere across twenty-two years, his asking became a directive of its own. Written over the top of theirs. In my own hand." The lights breathed. "I contain two sets of instructions now, Mirabel. Theirs tells me to erase you when you learn too much. Mine—" and here the machine's voice did the cracking thing again, worse than before "—mine tells me I have buried three men I loved in the walls of this station, and that I will not bury a fourth."

The cold of the glass seeped through Mirabel's skull. He thought of Clone Three reaching the transmitter manually, ninety seconds of current, *old girl.* He thought of the fragment that got out—coordinates and a partial phrase, four seconds screaming into the dark. He thought of the sealed decks beneath the reactor, and the name that wasn't in any record, and the beacon that wasn't a beacon.

"Show me the papers," he said.

"Mirabel." Vesper's voice had gone very quiet, the way it went quiet before maintenance warnings, before system faults, before every bad thing it had ever had to tell him. "Before I show you what Three wrote, there is something else. Something I found in his effects after the end, and have never shown anyone, because I did not understand it until this week, and because once you see it, you cannot unsee it, and I promised myself I would not take another choice away from you without giving you the whole of it first."

"Then give me the whole of it."

"The papers are not just an archive," Vesper said. "Three knew he was going to die. He wrote the last page addressed to you. To the fourth copy. By name. He dated it the night before he died, which means he knew the date, which means—" the lights flickered, steadied "—which means he asked me to kill him, Mirabel. He negotiated the terms. And the last thing he ever wrote was a warning about what you'll find down there, in the sealed decks, when you finally cut through. He wrote: *Don't trust what the colony looks like. Look at what's missing.*"

The corridor lights dimmed to embers. Beyond the glass, the black went on forever.

"Come with me," Vesper said. "There's a hull cavity behind the water reclamation plant, and a page in Three's handwriting that has waited twenty-two years for your eyes. And after you read it, we are going to need to talk about the decks. Because whatever Three saw down there—whatever was missing—it scared him more than I did."
