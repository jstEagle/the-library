## 1. Dead Frequencies

The manifest said the Sebastian was carrying forty tonnes of agricultural polymer bound for Ceres Relay, and the manifest was a lie in the way most good manifests were lies: technically accurate, spiritually hollow. The crates in hold two did contain agricultural polymer. They also contained eleven pallets of unregistered medical nanite feedstock that Odile Dravenmoor had spent three days making look boring.

Boring was the whole profession, really. Anyone could hide contraband. Hiding it *well* meant hiding the effort of hiding it. A smuggler's crate got sealed with fresh welds and new serial plates; Odile's got dented on one corner, scuffed along the same edge as every other crate in the hold, and stamped with a lot number that traced back to a legitimate shipment that had genuinely existed, once, three years ago, before being written off as water damage. The paper trail was real. That was the trick nobody taught you: don't forge documents. Buy real ones from people who need them to be false.

Odile sat cross-legged on the deck of the Sebastian's tiny office—a converted supply locker with a desk bolted where the spare air scrubber used to live—and worked through the quarterly claims audit with the patience of someone defusing something.

Halcyon Consortium, Marine & Cargo Division, policy number 7741-Dravenmoor-C. Vessel class: medium hauler, twenty-six years old, three owners back. Insured value: modest. Claims history: two hull-puncture events, one cargo loss due to pirate interdiction, one engine failure attributed to manufacturing defect. All settled. All clean. All, like the manifest, true in the way that mattered and false in the way that counted.

Odile had built this file the way a mason builds a wall—one stone at a time, no gaps, nothing interesting to catch the eye. Insurance investigators didn't read files looking for lies. They read files looking for *texture*. A claim too smooth was a flag. A claim with a little honest grit in it—delayed paperwork here, a disputed estimate there—was furniture. Invisible. Odile had personally inserted four pieces of grit into their own history over the years: a late-filed damage report, an argument with an adjuster about depreciation rates, a complaint about Halcyon's claims portal crashing mid-submission. Small grievances. The kind of thing a real person would have and a fabricated identity wouldn't think to include.

Ten years of practice. Ten years of being forgettable on purpose.

They pulled up the adjuster's notes on the last audit cycle and read what Halcyon thought of them:

*Insured cooperative. Documentation thorough. No anomalies. Recommend standard renewal.*

No anomalies. Odile almost smiled. Somewhere in a Halcyon office, some junior assessor had skimmed their file for ninety seconds and felt nothing at all, and that nothing was the finest review Odile had ever received.

"Odile." Tam Okafor's voice came through the bulkhead before it came through the comm, because Tam never waited for comms when he could just talk at walls. "You alive in there?"

"Barely."

"Good. Sefa wants to know if we're burning orbit time or if your paperwork is more important than fuel margins."

Odile closed the audit file. "Tell her the paperwork agrees with her. We're clear to jump when she is."

A pause. Through the thin wall, Odile heard Tam say something to someone else, muffled, and then: "Pell says the lane window closes in six hours, so if you're planning to be mysterious, do it after Ceres."

"I'm never mysterious," Odile said. "I'm aggressively ordinary."

"That's exactly what a mysterious person would say," Tam said cheerfully, and his boots thumped away down the corridor.

* * *

The Sebastian carried four crew and no passengers, which was how they all liked it. Passengers asked questions.

There was Captain Sefa Rhee, who flew like gravity was a rumor she'd chosen not to confirm, and who owned the ship on paper under a name that belonged to a man who had died in a yard accident on Titan fourteen years ago. There was Pell Vasquez, navigator, who kept three separate sets of books and whose real talent wasn't plotting courses but knowing which authorities would accept which fiction. There was Tam, engineer, who could keep a reactor running with prayer and spite and who never talked about the eight years between his discharge papers and his arrival aboard, a gap he filled with jokes so reliably that the gap itself became invisible.

And there was Odile. Cargo master, claims liaison, keeper of the manifests. On the Sebastian's registry, Odile Dravenmoor had been born on Ganymede, thirty-four years ago, to parents who existed only as two lines of fabricated census data. In truth—

In truth, Odile had been born somewhere else, with another name, into a family that had mostly stopped existing, and the less said about that even inside their own skull, the better.

That was the discipline. Not just scrubbing the records. Scrubbing the reflexes. Odile had trained themselves out of flinching at certain words, out of humming certain songs, out of answering questions in ways that left fingerprints. When customs officers boarded, they saw a tired middle-aged cargo handler with a clean file and dull eyes, and their attention slid off like rain off waxed hull plating.

It had cost everything to build. It would cost everything to keep. Odile had made peace with the exchange rate a long time ago.

They finished the audit at 0300 ship-time, filed it through three anonymizing relays, and went to the galley to drink tea that tasted like hot cardboard and feel briefly, ordinarily content.

* * *

The signal came in at 0417, while Odile was asleep, and by all rights it should have stayed buried in the noise floor until morning triage.

But the Sebastian's long-range array had a quirk—an old fault in the preamp that Tam refused to fix because fixing it would mean powering down the array for six hours, and Sefa refused to allow that, and so the fault had become a feature of the ship's personality, like a limp everyone had stopped noticing. The preamp occasionally amplified garbage. Most nights that meant ghost echoes of relay chatter, half a soap opera from some station two systems over, the electromagnetic sigh of a dying satellite.

Most nights.

At 0417, the array caught something on a frequency that should have been empty, and the fault in the preamp boosted it instead of smothering it, and the automated logger flagged it—not as traffic, but as *pattern*, because the signal repeated. Same duration. Same interval. Forty-one seconds of transmission, nineteen seconds of silence, over and over, patient as a heartbeat.

The logger couldn't classify the language, so it filed the audio raw and moved on.

At 0630, Odile came on shift, poured tea, opened the overnight queue, and started triaging the way they always did: junk, junk, junk, junk—

And stopped.

Not because they recognized the language. Recognition came later, a full three seconds later, arriving like cold water finding a crack. First came something older than recognition: the *shape* of the sounds. The clipped opening consonant, the rising second syllable, the falling breath at the end of each phrase. A shape Odile hadn't heard spoken aloud since they were nine years old, in a kitchen that no longer existed, from a woman who had made them promise—

Odile set down the tea. Their hands were steady. They noticed their hands being steady and hated how practiced it looked, even to themselves.

They played the clip again.

Forty-one seconds. A voice, or voices—it was hard to tell, degraded by distance and whatever relay or derelict transmitter was throwing it into the dark. Static chewed the edges. But underneath the damage, the words repeated, and Odile's grandmother's kitchen rose up around them unbidden: the smell of root stew, the window frosted with Callisto's reflected light, the old woman's hands moving as she spoke, always moving, because she said the language lived in the hands as much as the mouth.

*"Vesh ketha ilun. Vesh ketha ilun."*

We are still here.

We are still here.

Odile knew the phrase the way you know a scar. It was one of the first things their grandmother had taught them, and one of the only things she'd ever let them repeat, and only ever in whisper, only ever indoors, only ever with the door sealed and the room's acoustic dampeners running. She'd taught them the whole language that way—two hundred years after the Callisto Purges erased its last official speakers, in a cramped habitat unit, in whispers, like passing stolen goods hand to hand under a blanket.

*Never speak it outside,* she'd said, near the end, when her voice had gone thin as old paper. *Never write it where a camera can see. Promise me.* And Odile, nine years old and frightened by the seriousness in her face, had promised. Had kept the promise for twenty-five years. Had built an entire life with the promise load-bearing at its center: be small, be dull, be forgettable, and above all, be silent in the one tongue that could make you visible forever.

The Purges weren't a secret, exactly. They were worse than a secret—they were *settled history*. The Concordance Archives recorded it in one dry paragraph: civil unrest on the Jovian periphery, extremist cells, regrettable escalation, the Vessic-speaking communities dissolved and dispersed, the language extinct within a generation. Two hundred thousand people, give or take, accounted for as scattered, assimilated, gone. No massacre. No mass graves. Just a language that quietly died, the way languages do, and a paragraph in a database that no one had reason to open.

Odile's grandmother had called that paragraph the second killing. *First they kill us,* she'd said. *Then they kill the fact that we were killed. Forgetting is how they win twice.*

She'd been dead for twenty-two years. The language had been dead for two hundred. And now, at 0417 ship-time, on a frequency that should have held nothing, something out in the black was saying *vesh ketha ilun* over and over, forty-one seconds on, nineteen seconds off, like a heart that refused to stop.

Odile sat very still in the galley and listened to it eleven times.

The rational explanations presented themselves in order, wearing their investigator's badges. Hoax. Some hobbyist with a linguistics archive and a transmitter, playing games. A corrupted file—the logger misfiling old audio as new. A fragment of some historical broadcast bouncing off debris, delayed decades, finally arriving. Ghosts in the machine. The universe was full of dead signals still traveling; light and radio both outlived their sources. Maybe this was a recording of the Purge era itself, some final transmission from a station that had gone silent two centuries ago, arriving now the way distant starlight arrives—proof of a death, not evidence of life.

All of it plausible. All of it checkable.

None of it explained why Odile's chest hurt.

Because here was the thing Odile had never told anyone, the thing welded shut behind the promise and the aliases and the ten years of careful dullness: part of them had always known the paragraph in the archives was wrong. Grandmother had told stories—stories Odile had filed, as an adult, under *trauma folklore*. Stories of Meridian's Rest, the great station at the edge of the Belt where the Vessic communities had gathered, believing themselves safe. Stories of ships coming with Concordance colors flying. Stories that ended the way her stories always ended, abruptly, with her hands going still.

If any of it were true—if there were bodies, records, proof—then the quiet paragraph was a crime scene, and someone had done the investigating already and buried the findings. And Odile Dravenmoor, insurance investigator, professional verifier of other people's fictions, had spent their entire adult life agreeing, with their silence, to leave it buried.

The signal repeated. *Vesh ketha ilun.*

We are still here.

*Still*, Odile thought. Present tense. Two hundred years, and whoever—or whatever—was broadcasting had chosen present tense. Dead signals didn't have tense. Recordings didn't choose their verbs.

Odile pulled up the logging interface. Protocol was simple: unclassified anomalous signals got flagged, tagged, and deleted to keep the array's storage lean. Delete, log the deletion, move on. Forgettable. Ordinary. Exactly what the promise required, exactly what the life required.

Their finger hovered over the command.

Then, instead, they created a subdirectory—buried three layers deep in routine sensor maintenance files, named with a string of alphanumeric garbage no auditor would ever open—and moved the clip there. Full fidelity. Both hours of it. Then they ran a passive triangulation on the source bearing, using the Sebastian's drift across the reception window to get a second fix, and wrote the coordinates into the same garbage-named file.

Rough bearing. Far out past the standard lanes, toward a stretch of space where charts showed nothing worth showing.

Interference, Odile typed into the official log. Preamp fault. Deleted.

A lie, filed cleanly, with a little honest grit around it. They were good at this. That was the problem. They were so good at lying to systems that lying to themselves came free.

Odile closed the terminal, carried the cold tea to the recycler, and stood for a moment in the galley's dim light, listening to the ship breathe around them—Tam's reactor humming two decks down, Sefa's autopilot holding their drift, Pell snoring audibly through a cabin wall. Four people with scrubbed pasts, running honest-ish cargo through honest-ish space, all of them safe inside their mutual silence.

The signal was still repeating. Odile could feel it, the way you can feel a tooth that needs attention: *vesh ketha ilun*, forty-one seconds on, nineteen seconds off, out there in the dark where the charts showed nothing.

Grandmother, I promised, Odile thought. I promised.

The thought that followed arrived without permission and refused to leave:

*You also promised to remember.*

Odile turned off the galley lights and went to lie down and not sleep, and the signal knocked, and knocked, and knocked, on a door welded shut for twenty-five years—and beneath the welding, faint but unmistakable, something answered back in a dead language that had apparently decided not to stay dead.
