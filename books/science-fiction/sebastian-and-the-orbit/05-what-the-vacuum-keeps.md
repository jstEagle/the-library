## 5. What the Vacuum Keeps

The airlock cycled with a sound like a held breath finally released.

Odile stood in the inner vestibule of Meridian's Rest and let the station decide whether to kill them. The suit's seals checked green. The atmosphere readout climbed: nitrogen, oxygen, trace argon—breathable, cold, dead still. Two hundred years of vacuum on the other side of that hull, and here, in the heart of the station, air.

That was wrong. Odile knew it was wrong before they could say why. Stations don't keep their air when they die. Whatever killed this place, it hadn't been a hull breach. The hull was scarred outside—weapons fire, unmistakably—but inside, the pressure had held. Someone had sealed the airlocks from within, exactly as the exterior readouts suggested, and then—

Then what?

"Sebastian to Dravenmoor." Sefa's voice in their ear, thin through the relay. "You're reading atmosphere in there?"

"I am."

"That's not possible for an abandoned station. Life support doesn't run for two centuries on nothing."

"It's not running." Odile watched the readout tick. "It's preserved. Sealed and preserved." They paused. "Somebody wanted this place kept."

A silence on the channel long enough to feel. Then Tam: "Odile. You can come back. We log the coordinates, we file it as a hazard, somebody official deals with it."

"Somebody official," Odile said, "is why there's no station here on any chart." They keyed the vestibule door. "I'm going in. Keep the lock warm."

The corridor beyond opened like a throat.

*

Gravity was off—or nearly; the station's spin had died with its power, and what remained was a faint drift, residual momentum in the structure itself. Odile moved through it in slow bounds, mag-boots clanking against deck plating every few meters, helmet lamp carving a cone out of the dark. Frost glittered everywhere. Not the chaotic frost of a freezing hull—the frost here lay in patterns, in sheets, in places where moisture had gathered and frozen with geometric patience. Condensation from two hundred years ago, flash-frozen mid-fall, hanging in the corners of doorways like cobwebs made of glass.

The station had been residential. That registered slowly, in pieces. A corridor lined with apartment doors, each one marked with family designations in three scripts—one of them Vessic. Odile's stomach tightened at the sight of it, the angular script their grandmother had traced on fogged windows during winters on Ceres, the letters she'd made them practice in condensation because paper records could be seized but breath on glass evaporated.

*Never speak it where machines can hear,* she'd said. *But never forget how it looks. A language you can't see is a language you've already lost.*

The doors were sealed. Most of them locked from inside. Odile passed a thousand small domestic details drifting in the low gravity: a child's shoe turning slowly near the ceiling. A dried plant in a wall planter, brown and brittle and perfectly preserved, like something pressed between the pages of a book. A jacket hung on a hook by a door, sleeves relaxed, waiting for shoulders that were never coming back.

No bodies here. That was the first mercy, and Odile distrusted it immediately. Two hundred thousand people didn't vanish from their own apartments. They'd been moved. Gathered. Herded somewhere central, the way you move anything you intend to count.

The corridor bent toward the axis of the station, toward the central hall every habitat ring fed into. Odile felt the pull of it—not gravity, something older than gravity. The way water finds a drain. The way grief finds its lowest point.

Their breathing sounded loud in the helmet. Too loud. Like someone else was in the dark ahead of them, matching their rhythm.

*

The central hall of Meridian's Rest had been, according to the signage still legible above the great doors, a market and assembly space. THE COMMON ROOM OF ALL PEOPLES, the Vessic script said beneath the Concordance standard, and Odile translated it without meaning to, the way you translate your own name.

They went through the doors and stopped moving.

The hall was vast—a cylinder four hundred meters across, tiered balconies rising toward the axis, the kind of space built to hold ten thousand people comfortably for a festival. It was lit faintly by Odile's own lamp and by emergency strips along the floor that still glowed a dying amber, some chemical persistence in them outlasting everything else, outlasting even memory.

And arranged across the entire floor, in rows, in ranks, in a pattern so orderly it took Odile's mind several full seconds to refuse it—

People.

Thousands of them. Tens of thousands. Preserved by the cold and the vacuum into a state that was not decay and not sleep and not anything the living had a word for. They lay in rows that stretched back past the reach of Odile's lamp, row after row after row, aligned head to foot like words on a page, like entries in a ledger, like data. Adults holding adults. Adults holding children. Some in civilian clothes, some in station uniforms, a few in what looked like formal dress—as if some of them had been taken from a celebration.

Odile's knees hit the deck before they decided to kneel. The impact rang up through their bones.

They had prepared for this. They'd told themselves they had, in the six hours between deciding to board and actually boarding—they'd run the scenarios, decompression casualties, radiation deaths, the mummified quiet of vacuum-preserved dead. They were an insurance investigator. They had seen bodies. They had catalogued bodies, priced bodies, written reports in which human beings became line items, and they had told themselves that skill would hold.

It did not hold.

Because these people had not died of catastrophe. Catastrophe is chaos; catastrophe scatters. These people lay in rows. Someone had arranged them. Someone had walked these floors after it was done and placed each body with care—or with procedure—and the arrangement was the message. Not a massacre scene. An exhibition. A filing system. Here are the residents of Meridian's Rest, the rows said, in a voice that required no language. Here they all are. Count us if you dare.

Odile's breath fogged the inside of their visor and they realized they were making a sound, low and continuous, something between a moan and a prayer, and they shut it off with an act of will that felt like breaking a bone.

*Get up. Get up. You're an investigator. Investigate.*

They got up.

*

The suit's medical suite pinged them twice—elevated heart rate, cortisol spike—and Odile dismissed both alerts with a gesture that shook only slightly. They began to walk the rows. Mag-boots. One step. Another. The amber strips lit their path like runway markers, guiding them down aisles between the dead.

Close up, the preservation was terrible in its detail. Faces intact. Expressions intact. Vacuum does strange things to a body but it does not erase it, and these faces were not peaceful. Odile saw fear. Saw confusion. Saw, on a woman maybe sixty years old, lying with her hands folded over her chest in a posture someone else had given her, an expression of such specific, personal outrage that Odile had to stop and stand beside her for a full minute, breathing, until they could move again.

She hadn't accepted it. Whoever she was—whatever her name had been—she had gone to her death furious, and two hundred years of silence hadn't cooled it, and Odile thought: *good*. Thought it like a vow.

There were no wounds they could see at first. No burns, no breaches, no signs of the violence the hull scars promised. The people of Meridian's Rest had been executed—Odile understood it now with a professional clarity that sat in their chest like swallowed ice—executed cleanly, systematically, probably in this hall or elsewhere and then arranged here afterward. Executed and then *displayed*. Because whoever did this wanted the next visitors to understand. Wanted any survivor, any rescuer, any auditor from history to walk in and comprehend instantly, completely, that this had been done on purpose, by people, to people, and that it had been tidy.

Tidy. Odile almost laughed. Ten years of writing the word in claims reports. *Cause of loss: tidy.* There should be a category for it. There should be a form.

They walked for what their suit clock said was forty minutes and what their body insisted was a year. The rows continued. Somewhere around the two-hundredth meter, Odile stopped counting individuals and started counting meters, and then stopped counting entirely, because numbers had become obscene.

*

It was near the far side of the hall, where the tiers of balconies threw deeper shadow, that Odile found the child.

Not a child's body—there were children in the rows, too many, and Odile had trained themselves to look at the deck plating between them, a survival tactic that was itself a kind of wound. This was different. This was on the wall.

The interior surface of the hall's eastern bulkhead was paneled in composite sheeting, and across a wide section of it, the frost had formed and re-formed over the years into a pale rime. And pressed into that rime, frozen there, preserved there, was a handprint. Small. A child's hand, fingers spread, pressed flat against the panel the way children press hands against windows.

Below the handprint, drawn with the same small finger, in the condensation before it froze:

Five characters. Angular. Deliberate. A child's handwriting, uneven, the strokes wobbling where a small hand had tired—but complete, correct, each glyph formed the way Grandmother Ilsa had formed them, the way she'd guided Odile's finger through the same shapes on a frosted viewport while the haulers burned hydrogen behind them and the Purge's second anniversary came and went unmarked by anyone official anywhere.

Odile knew the word before they finished reading it. Knew it in their body, in the place where their grandmother's voice still lived.

*Zhyremeth.*

Remember.

The sound of it went through them like current. They heard it in her voice—heard the low, cracked alto of a woman who had crossed the black with a language hidden in her mouth like contraband, singing it soft over a child's bed because songs were the only place it was safe. *Zhyremeth, little star. Whatever they take, they cannot take it if you carry it. Memory isn't stored, it's carried. You're the vessel now.*

Odile put their gauntleted hand against the panel, beside the print. Their glove dwarfed it. Two hundred years apart, palm to palm through a centimeter of composite and a century of silence, and Odile stood there in the amber light among the ordered dead and felt grief arrive the way the plan said a hull breach arrives—not gradually, not with warning, but all at once, the whole atmosphere of them rushing out into a void they hadn't known was there.

They sank down the wall until they were sitting. They let it come.

It came for a long time. It came as sobs first, ugly and mechanical behind the visor, and then as something worse: images. Grandmother Ilsa's hands, knotted like tree roots, folding a napkin into the shape of the Meridian spire—"that's where our music lived," she'd said once, once only, and never again. The way she'd gone silent at news broadcasts about Concordance resettlement initiatives. The way she'd died asking for a word in Vessic that the hospice staff thought was delirium, and Odile, nineteen and frightened and eager to be assimilated, to be *safe*, had pretended not to know it.

They had pretended not to know it. And she had closed her mouth and turned her face to the wall and waited to die in a language no one would speak to her in, because her own grandchild had chosen comfort over her.

*I'm sorry.* Odile said it aloud, in Vessic, into the dark of the hall, and the words came out ragged and rusted and real, the first time in twenty years they had spoken the language where anything living might hear. *I'm sorry. I'm sorry. Zhyremeth. I remember. I'm here.*

The hall did not answer. The hall held ten thousand kinds of silence, and Odile sat in all of them.

*

Time passed. The suit clock said fifty-one minutes since entry when Odile finally stood, legs stiff, face raw inside the helmet, eyes swollen. Grief had not left. It had simply, like all heavy things, settled—down into the bones, into the load-bearing structures, where it would stay and be carried.

They looked out over the rows one more time. The amber strips glowed. The dead kept their counsel.

"You're going in the record," Odile said quietly. Their voice sounded strange to them—stripped, scraped, but steady. "All of you. Every single one. I don't know how yet. But I have spent ten years learning how to make paperwork out of tragedy, and I swear to you on my grandmother's grave and on every alias I've ever buried—I will write yours so precisely that no one will ever be able to call it interference again."

It wasn't a prayer. Prayers asked for things from powers that might decline. This was a scope of work. This was a claim filed against the universe, itemized, documented, non-negotiable.

Odile turned back toward the wall, back toward the small handprint, and raised their lamp to study it properly—because grief had done its work, and underneath the grief, patient as sediment, the investigator was already moving. Timestamp the frost formation. Photograph the glyphs. Sample the condensation chemistry for age verification. Establish chain of custody for evidence two hundred years orphaned.

The lamp beam swept across the panel beside the handprint, and Odile stopped.

There, lower on the wall, half-hidden behind a conduit housing, scratched into the composite itself—not frost, not finger-paint, but cut deep with something sharp, by someone with time and privacy and desperate intent—was more Vessic. Adult handwriting. Small, cramped, hurried, a whole paragraph of it, the letters of someone writing fast because they did not expect to finish.

Odile leaned close. The suit lamp steadied. Their breath did not.

The scratched lines weren't a message to the future. They were a record—a list, or the start of one, names in Vessic transliteration, dozens of them, ending mid-entry. And beneath the last unfinished name, one final line, gouged so hard the composite had split:

*They came in Concordance colors. The logs will lie. Find the maintenance levels. Ryn saved the truth.*

Odile read it three times. Then they photographed it, forty frames, every angle, hands shaking so badly they had to brace the camera against the wall.

*Ryn saved the truth.*

Somewhere in this station, someone named Ryn had hidden something worth killing for—and worth finding.

Odile straightened, wiped their visor with a sleeve that accomplished nothing, and turned toward the maintenance access hatches at the hall's far end, their lamp beam cutting forward into the dark like a promise.
