## 7. The Consortium's Long Arm

The flag arrived at 04:12 station time, which was the hour Corvel had learned to love best.

He had been with Halcyon for thirty-one years and he had never once requested a different shift. The night cycle was when the machine spoke most honestly. During the day, the anomaly queues filled with noise—smugglers' decoys, sensor ghosts, clerical errors, the ordinary static of ten thousand vessels lying about who they were. Daytime flags required judgment. Nighttime flags were usually true.

This one was true. He could tell from its shape.

*Unregistered vessel. Stationary. Duration of loiter: 61 hours. Proximity to coordinate 774-Black-9: 0.3 light-minutes.*

Corvel read it twice, then set down his tea and pulled up the coordinate file, because 774-Black-9 was not a coordinate that generated flags. It was a coordinate that had been *retired*. He had personally signed the retirement order eleven years ago, one of four hundred and six such orders in his career, each one a small funeral for a place that had never officially existed.

The file opened the way it always did: a single line of text, no imagery, no history, no attachments.

*Coordinate 774-Black-9: Status — Non-site. No records exist.*

No records exist. It was his favorite sentence in the entire Concordance legal corpus, and he had helped draft the template. Not "records destroyed." Not "records sealed." Those phrases invited questions—*destroyed by whom? sealed why?* No records exist was a statement about the universe, not about anyone's actions. It accused no one. It simply described an absence so complete that even curiosity bounced off it.

And now something was parked next to the absence, loitering, for sixty-one hours.

Corvel took a sip of tea. It had gone lukewarm, as it always did on nights like this, because he made it before opening the queue and the queue always held him longer than he planned. He did not mind. Patience was not merely a professional virtue in his line of work; it was the entire methodology. Halcyon Compliance did not chase. Chasing implied urgency, and urgency implied that something mattered enough to be lost. Nothing was ever lost. Everything was eventually filed.

He began, as he always began, with the vessel.

*Unregistered* covered a wide territory. Most unregistered ships were poor—fishers running expired licenses, couriers dodging tariffs, the small dishonest economy that kept the margins of inhabited space warm. Such vessels were beneath his attention; they were handled by regional offices, fined, impounded, released, forgotten. But a poor ship did not hold station for sixty-one hours beside a retired coordinate. Poor ships moved. They had cargo to deliver, debts to flee, engines too expensive to idle.

A ship that *lingered* near a non-site was a ship that had found something. Or gone looking for something. And since there was nothing there to find—

Well. That was the interesting part, wasn't it.

Corvel opened the sensor packet. The tender's long-range array had caught the vessel at extreme resolution, which meant the image was grainy in the way that only very good instruments are grainy—a hauler-class hull, mid-century frame, drive signature consistent with a civilian fusion torch run at low output. Running quiet. Running dark, actually: no transponder ping, no registry handshake, no insurance beacon.

No insurance beacon. Corvel noted that with something adjacent to pleasure. Every legitimate ship in Concordance space carried one; it was legally mandatory and practically unavoidable, since no port would berth an uninsured hull. A ship without a beacon was either stolen or hiding, and stolen ships ran *fast*, not stationary. This one was hiding. Hiding in plain vacuum, beside a hole in the record.

He flagged the file for priority review and drafted the initial assessment. He wrote assessments the way other men wrote letters home—carefully, in full sentences, with nothing wasted.

*Anomaly 4471-C. Unregistered hauler-class vessel holding position proximate to retired coordinate 774-Black-9 for 61+ hours. No transponder activity. No beacon. Behavior consistent with deliberate site investigation. Recommend classification: Tier Two inquiry. Recommend asset: compliance tender Vantablack, currently stationed Ceres relay, transit time nine days. Recommend no escalation pending first observation report.*

Tier Two. Not One. A Tier One inquiry summoned armed interdiction, Concordance marshals, the whole loud machinery of law—and law asked questions in public. Tier Two was quieter. Tier Two was a tender with a polite crew and a deep database, sent to drift nearby and watch, and if the vessel departed, well, then it was nothing, a sensor echo, a fisher napping, and the file would close itself with a little sigh of paperwork. And if the vessel did not depart—if it went where it should not be able to go, saw what should not exist—then Tier Two would deepen, patiently, tier by tier, until whatever had crawled out of the dark was surrounded by documents instead of guns.

Documents were better than guns. Guns created martyrs. Documents created *nothing*, and nothing was the product Halcyon Compliance had been manufacturing, profitably, for two centuries.

He signed the assessment. The system timestamped it, routed it, and forgot it, exactly as designed. Somewhere aboard the Vantablack, a duty officer would receive the tasking in the morning and feel the mild dread of a nine-day transit to a coordinate with no name, and would say nothing, because duty officers said nothing. That was the culture. Corvel had built much of the culture himself, over three decades, one training seminar at a time.

He finished the tea. Outside his office window—the office was real, on Ceres, with real glass and a view of the yards, because Corvel believed in being visible; invisible men made people nervous, and nervous people made mistakes—the yard lights burned in their patient rows, tugs nudging hulls into slipways, the whole economy breathing in and out.

Sixty-one hours, he thought. Someone out there was spending their life's savings in fuel cells to sit beside a grave they weren't supposed to know existed.

He found he was almost curious. Almost. Curiosity was permitted, in small doses, so long as it never reached the point of sympathy.

* * *

The morning brought the second flag, and this one changed the temperature of the room.

Corvel arrived at 08:00 sharp—he kept day hours too; the night queue was a private ritual, but discipline was discipline—and found Anomaly 4471-C updated with a supplemental packet. The Vantablack hadn't even cleared Ceres yet; this data came from the passive listening grid, the constellation of cheap omnidirectional ears scattered through the Belt that mostly heard garbage traffic and the complaints of lonely tug pilots.

The grid had caught a transmission burst from the unregistered vessel. Narrow-beam, tightly focused, aimed at the non-site itself. Duration: 2.7 seconds. Content: encrypted, or possibly not encrypted at all—possibly just noise shaped like language, the kind of thing old hulls emitted when their comms arrays decayed.

But the frequency band was the problem.

Corvel sat very still for a moment. Then he opened the restricted band registry, entered his credentials—Level Nine, one of only fourteen Level Nine credentials in the entire Compliance division—and looked up the band designation attached to the burst.

Band 88-Vessic. Reserved status: historical. Licensed users: none. Notes field: *Spectral allocation retained per Concordance Cultural Heritage Act, Section 12. No active transmissions authorized or anticipated.*

No active transmissions authorized or *anticipated*. Another beautiful sentence. Anticipation was a form of memory, and memory of that particular band had been managed very carefully for two hundred years.

Vessic. Even thinking the word felt faintly transgressive, though Corvel was too disciplined for superstition. It was just a dead language. Languages died all the time; the Concordance had buried dozens, some by neglect and some by policy, and the difference between those two categories was precisely the difference between Halcyon's public face and its private ledger. Vessic belonged to the second category. The Purges had been before his time, before his father's time, but the *methodology* of the Purges—that was his inheritance, refined and maintained like any other corporate asset. You did not kill a people twice. You killed them once, and then you killed the evidence that they had ever been a people, and then you waited. Waiting was free. Waiting compounded.

And here was an unregistered, uninsured, transponder-dark hauler, broadcasting into a dead band, at a dead coordinate, for going on three days.

Corvel revised the assessment. Tier Two became Tier Three. Tier Three authorized content analysis, pattern matching against the historical linguistics archive, and—this was the clause he had fought to have written into the manual twenty years ago, over the objections of the legal department who thought it excessive—Tier Three authorized a full identity workup on the vessel's registered owners, former owners, insurers, creditors, and crew manifests, current and historical, regardless of registration status.

Because ships were anonymous, but people were not. People left trails. People had mothers who filed claims, sisters who held licenses, debts that surfaced in civil courts, medical records, pension accounts, the vast soft tissue of modern life that no one thought of as a record until someone like Corvel came reading.

He initiated the workup himself rather than delegating it. This was unusual—delegation was the whole architecture of Compliance—but the Vessic band changed things. Band transmissions near Purge-era sites were a known pattern class. There had been eleven incidents in the last fifty years. Eleven. He had reviewed every one during his tenure on the Historical Integrity Committee, back when he still served on committees. Survivors' descendants, mostly. Grief-driven hobbyists with inherited fragments of dead languages, sailing out to stand in the vacuum where their grandparents had died, broadcasting lullabies at holes in space.

Those cases resolved themselves, usually. The descendants ran out of money, or died, or were quietly bankrupted by administrative fees until the obsession became unaffordable, which was the gentlest instrument in the whole toolkit and Corvel's personal favorite. Nobody suffered. Nobody vanished. They simply discovered, over five or six years of escalating bureaucratic friction, that the past was a country they could not afford to visit. The files closed. The bands went silent again.

But those were individuals. Lone ships, lone mourners, lone fanatics. This was a hauler—a working vessel, which meant a crew, which meant provisioning, which meant *resources*, and resources meant organization, and organization was a different animal entirely.

Corvel pulled the eleven prior incident files and laid them side by side, looking for the shape of the thing. Six solo mourners. Three small family groups. One cult, absurdly harmless, dissolved by tax audit. And one more—incident seven, forty years ago—a chartered survey vessel whose captain had claimed to be mapping debris fields. That one had required a Tier Four response. Corvel had not been assigned to it; he had merely studied it, the way a young cleric studies scripture. The survey vessel had been met at a neutral port by three separate agencies presenting three separate regulatory violations, its charter revoked under four jurisdictions simultaneously, its captain ruined with such thoroughness and legality that he spent his remaining years writing letters to newspapers that declined to print them. The vessel's data core was seized as collateral for unpaid docking fees. Its findings were never reviewed, because there was nothing to review. No records existed.

That was the template. That was the art. Not violence—violence was what you used when you had already failed, and Halcyon Compliance did not fail, because failure was a records problem too.

Corvel drafted the supplemental directive to the Vantablack:

*Upon arrival, establish passive observation at minimum 0.5 light-minute separation. Do not hail. Do not approach. Do not activate active sensors. Log all emissions. If vessel departs prior to arrival, log vector and stand down to monitoring posture. If vessel remains, hold position and await further instruction. Under no circumstances initiate contact without direct authorization from this office.*

The last sentence was the important one. Contact created events, and events created witnesses, and witnesses created stories, and stories were how non-sites stopped being non-sites. The Vantablack's job was to be a pair of eyes with no mouth.

He sent it. Then he did something he rarely did: he opened the personnel file.

Not the vessel's workup—that was still grinding through the databases, cross-referencing hull frames against salvage registries, drive signatures against fleet sales, the slow mechanical patience of pattern matching. No. Corvel opened a different file, one that lived outside the anomaly system, in the drawer where Compliance kept its institutional memories.

It was labeled simply *Purge-Site Protocols*, and it was less a document than a liturgy. It contained everything two centuries of practice had taught them about the shape of these events: the typical timeline (grief discovery, verification attempt, evidence gathering, contact attempt, exposure attempt), the typical actors (descendants, historians, journalists, the occasional honest archivist), the typical failure points of the cover (never the archive itself—the archive was airtight—but the *periphery*: shipping manifests, insurance claims, the accidental witness, the bribed dockhand who talked). And it contained the countermeasures, arranged in escalating order like a pharmacopoeia: financial pressure, credential revocation, reputational contamination, legal entanglement, and, at the bottom of the list, in a section marked only *Last Resort*, protocols that Corvel had read exactly once and hoped never to authorize.

He closed the file. His tea had gone cold again. He noticed he had not touched it in forty minutes.

On his desk terminal, the identity workup chimed: preliminary results available. Corvel opened them with the mild anticipation of a man opening a bill he already knows the size of.

The results were thin, which was itself informative. Hull frame matched a mid-century Meridian Yards construction run—no surprise, half the Belt flew Meridian frames. Registry history: clean, transferred twice, lapsed eight years ago. Current owner of record: a shell holding company, standard privacy structure, nothing alarming. Insurance history—

Corvel paused.

Insurance history: the hull had carried continuous coverage for twelve years. Underwriter: Halcyon Risk Partners. Policy status: *active*.

He sat back. Well. That was not alarming either—not yet, not necessarily. Halcyon insured a third of the Belt; the odds of any random ghost ship carrying their paper were better than even. But it meant the vessel was inside the house. Whatever this was—mourners, fanatics, treasure hunters—it was a Halcyon-insured hull poking at a Halcyon-buried site, and that transformed the matter from an external nuisance into an internal hygiene problem. Internal problems were handled differently. More carefully. More completely.

He drafted a third note, this one to the Risk Partners data office, requesting a full claims and behavior profile on the insured hull: ports called, claims filed, crew changes, premium payment patterns. Payment patterns especially. Desperate people paid late, or paid in lump sums drawn from strange sources, or let policies lapse and scramble to reinstate. The money told you the shape of the fear.

Then he added a line he had added to perhaps a dozen such requests in his career, a line that would raise no eyebrows at the data office because it raised eyebrows nowhere, being pure boilerplate:

*Flag any connection, however indirect, between insured parties and persons listed in Schedule R.*

Schedule R. The roster of Purge-descended family lines, compiled across generations, updated quietly, consulted quietly. Not a watchlist—watchlists were illegal, and Halcyon Compliance had never done anything illegal in its entire existence, a fact attested by the complete absence of records proving otherwise. Schedule R was a *genealogical resource*. For actuarial purposes. Longevity modeling. Perfectly routine.

If the ship's crew touched Schedule R—if even one name, one grandmother, one inherited vowel of that dead language connected a living person to the Purges—then this stopped being an anomaly and became a continuity event. And continuity events got Assessor-level attention.

Corvel looked at his reflection in the darkened window: a gray man in a gray office above gray yards, holding cold tea, caring intensely about a dead language. He smiled at himself, briefly, without warmth. Sentiment would say he was a monster. Sentiment had never balanced a ledger. Two hundred thousand people had died at the Purges—or had they? He genuinely did not know; the numbers themselves were classified into meaninglessness, another elegant solution. What Corvel knew was this: the Concordance stood on the settlement of that account, and reopening it would cost trillions, topple charters, unravel treaties, and change absolutely nothing for the dead. Stability was not evil. Stability was arithmetic. Someone had to do the arithmetic, and do it without flinching, and that someone had been Corvel for thirty-one years, and he had never once flinched.

The workup would take days. The Vantablack would take nine. The vessel at the non-site might leave tomorrow, and the file would close with a small sigh, and Corvel would forget it by quarter-end.

Or it wouldn't leave. And then the machinery would begin to turn, gear by patient gear, and somewhere far out in the dark a handful of people who thought they were alone with a secret would discover, slowly and legally and irreversibly, that they had never been alone at all.

Corvel rinsed his cup, set it to dry, and turned to the rest of the morning queue.

At 11:47, the Risk Partners data office returned its first preliminary: the insured hull's most recent claim filing, submitted fourteen months ago, routine hail damage, processed and closed. Attached to the claim, as regulations required, was the signature of the filing party—the named operator on the policy.

Corvel read the name. It meant nothing to him. It matched nothing in Schedule R. It was a small, forgettable name, the kind of name that had been designed, whether its owner knew it or not, to pass through systems without leaving a mark.

He flagged it anyway. Names that tried to be forgettable were, in his long experience, the ones worth remembering.

Somewhere out past the Belt, a ship hung motionless beside a grave, speaking to the dead in a forbidden tongue. Nine days, give or take, and the Vantablack would be watching.

Corvel opened the next file in the queue. There was always a next file. That was the whole point of him.
