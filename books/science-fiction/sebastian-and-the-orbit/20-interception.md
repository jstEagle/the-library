## 20. Interception

### Sefa

Sefa saw the tender before the depot's traffic control did, which was either a compliment to her instincts or an indictment of the depot's payroll.

She was running a manual scan of approach vectors out of habit—the kind of habit you build when you've spent eleven years flying other people's secrets through other people's airspace—and there it was: a hard return sliding up the ecliptic on a burn profile too clean to be anything but military surplus wearing a corporate paint job. Halcyon livery, matte grey with the consortium's sunburst stenciled small and polite on the flank, like a signature on a bill.

"Compliance tender," she said over the ship-wide. "Class-four hull, escort-grade thrusters. It's not here for fuel."

The *Sebastian* hung in the queue outside Vasko's Depot, a fueling station strung across three captured asteroids like laundry between poles. Forty-one ships ahead of them in the queue. The depot's docking arms were busy, its controllers bored, its security cameras—Sefa had checked on approach—running firmware two generations old.

The tender wasn't slowing for the queue.

"Pell." She keyed the nav station. "Tell me I'm wrong."

Pell's voice came back flat and fast, all the drawl burned off it. "You're not wrong. It's matching our transponder echo. It pinged us twice already—we answered with the standard handshake, but it's holding the query open. That's not verification. That's a leash."

"How long?"

"If it keeps this burn? Twelve minutes to intercept range. Fifteen if they want to be gentle about it."

Sefa did the math she didn't want to do. The *Sebastian* was a cargo hauler with a gutted hold full of nothing sellable and one encrypted archive worth more than the ship. Her engines were honest workhorses, not racers. A class-four tender could run her down in open space like a dog taking a walk.

But this wasn't open space. This was a depot—a neutral port, technically, though neutrality these days was a price tag, and Halcyon carried a very large wallet.

"They're going to try to make the depot do their work for them," she said. "Deny our docking clearance, box us in the queue, and board us 'in compliance with port safety protocols.' Clean hands. We'll be sitting in a parking orbit while they walk aboard."

"Then we don't sit," Pell said.

She could hear him thinking. She'd flown with Pell long enough to know his silences had shapes, and this one had the shape of a man calculating exactly how much trouble he was willing to be worth.

"Sefa." Odile's voice now, quiet, coming from the comms alcove where they'd been living for days among the decrypted files like a spider in a web of dead people's words. "Options."

"One option." Sefa watched the tender's burn curve tighten. Ten minutes now, maybe less. "Vasko's has a gravity assist corridor off the big rock—every hauler uses it to slingshot outbound without burning fuel. It threads between the second and third asteroids. Clearance is tight. Tighter than regulation likes."

"How tight?" Odile asked.

"Tight enough that nobody does it loaded. Tight enough that if I'm off by a degree we become a new asteroid field." She exhaled. "And tight enough that a class-four tender can't follow without tearing its own fins off. They'll have to go around. That buys us maybe ninety seconds of lead."

"Ninety seconds isn't escape velocity."

"No," Sefa agreed. "It's a head start. Whether it's enough depends on what we weigh when we hit the corridor."

She heard Odile go still on the other end of the line. Then, softly, almost to themselves: "What we weigh."

### Tam

Tam was already in the cargo bay when Odile came down the ladder two steps at a time, and he'd already figured out what they were going to ask, because there was only one thing in the bay that weighed anything.

The manifest said the *Sebastian* was carrying forty tons of agricultural polymer feedstock, legitimate, boring, insured. The manifest lied, as manifests did. The crates were real—Halcyon shipping containers, real mass, real ballast—but the contents had been sold off leg by leg across six ports until what remained was mostly packaging and pride. Forty tons of empty boxes riding a ship that needed to look heavy to look honest.

"You want to vent it," Tam said. Not a question.

Odile landed on the deck plating. Their face had the particular blankness Tam had learned to read as terror under management. "The tender's tracking our mass signature. If we shed forty tons mid-burn, we change everything—acceleration profile, drift pattern, the works. Their firing solution goes stale."

"And every sensor in the depot sees a hauler dumping unmanifested cargo inside a neutral port's traffic envelope." Tam wiped his hands on a rag he hadn't needed for an hour. He kept it for moments like this, moments when his hands wanted something to do besides shake. "That's a violation fine, a black mark, and probably a permanent ban from Vasko's. Every port shares those lists, Odile. You know what happens after that."

"I know." Odile's voice was steady. Steadier than it had any right to be. "I know exactly what happens after that. I've spent ten years learning what happens after things."

Tam looked at the crates. Forty tons of nothing. Forty tons of the lie they'd all been living together, stacked in neat rows and strapped down tight.

He thought about the draft notice that had never borne his name because he'd made sure it couldn't find one. He thought about the engineer's certification he'd bought from a man who owed him, the one with someone else's exam scores laminated behind someone else's face. He thought about how every single person on this ship had built themselves out of paperwork that would not survive a strong light.

If they vented the cargo, the *Sebastian* became a liar in front of witnesses. There was no version of this where they went back to being forgettable.

"We'll need to cut the restraints manually," he said. "The auto-release is tied into the manifest system, and the moment we touch it, the system logs why. I'll blow the clamps from the bay panel—it'll look like a mechanical fault. Depressurize the bay, open the doors, let the venting thrust do half the work. You'll get a kick, so tell your pilot to brace for a push to starboard."

Odile blinked. "You've done this before."

"I've done worse before." He was already pulling his suit gloves from the wall rack. "Get Sefa her numbers. And Odile—" he caught them at the ladder, one hand on the rail, "—whatever's in that archive, whatever's making a warship chase a freighter, you're going to tell me eventually. Not today. But eventually."

Odile held his eyes. Something moved behind their face, some door opening a crack onto a room lit by fires.

"Eventually," they said. "I promise."

Tam nodded once and sealed his helmet. Promises on this ship were currency, and everyone spent carefully.

### Pell

Pell fed Sefa the numbers and hated every keystroke.

This was the part of himself he'd sworn he'd buried—the part that ran scenarios in the dark, the part that always kept a door marked EXIT in the back of his mind. For six years he'd been the navigator who never flinched, the man who'd found a family that asked no questions because he couldn't have survived the answers. And now a Halcyon tender was falling toward them out of a clean sky, and the old arithmetic started up on its own, unbidden:

*One archive against four lives. One truth against four sets of lungs.*

He caught himself pricing it. That was the worst part—not the temptation itself but the fluency of it, how easily his mind slid back into the language of transactions. He knew what consortium money looked like. He'd taken it once, years ago, a quiet payment for a quiet name, and he'd told himself it was survival, and the family he'd priced that day had stopped answering their comm within the month. He'd never confirmed what happened. Confirmation would have required admitting he'd helped load the gun.

"Course is laid," he said aloud, because saying things aloud made them real and real things could be fought. "Slingshot corridor, threading the gap. Sefa, you'll have a window of about forty seconds where the rock walls are closer than regulation allows me to type without my hands shaking."

"My hands are steady," Sefa said.

"Your hands are insane. That's different." He pulled up the tender's projected track. "They'll break off at the corridor mouth—they have to. Then they'll do one of two things. Either they go around the long way, which costs them four minutes, or they call ahead and put a second asset on our exit vector."

Nobody said the third thing, the thing everyone was thinking: *or they shoot us in the gap and call it debris.*

"The depot has defensive batteries," Sefa said. "Neutral ports protect their traffic. Even Halcyon doesn't shoot inside Vasko's."

"Even Halcyon especially shoots inside Vasko's," Pell said. "They just do it with lawyers afterward instead of missiles. But the batteries are real. If we're inside the traffic envelope when something happens, the depot has to respond. So we stay inside the envelope as long as possible. Every meter counts."

He looked at the plot. The tender's burn was patient, unhurried, the confidence of an organization that had never once been in a hurry because history always bent toward it eventually. Pell knew that patience intimately. He'd been patient once himself, right up until the day patience cost him everything he'd pretended he didn't love.

"Odile," he said. "Whatever happens in the next twenty minutes—I need you to know I'm not selling anybody out today."

A pause on the line. Then Odile's voice, careful: "Today?"

"Today." Pell closed the plot and locked it in. "Ask me again tomorrow. I'm trying to build a streak."

* * *

### Odile

Odile stood in the cockpit doorway with the archive drive strapped to their chest like a child, watching the tender grow on the display from a smudge to a shape to a fact.

It was beautiful, in the way that well-made instruments of coercion are beautiful. Sleek lines, efficient radiators, no wasted tonnage. Somewhere aboard it, a compliance officer was probably filling out a form with a field labeled REASON FOR INTERDICTION, and the form had a dropdown menu, and none of the options included *because you know what we did.*

"Bay's rigged," Tam reported. "On your word."

"Course is hot," Pell said. "Window opens in ninety seconds."

"Depot control just denied our docking request," Sefa added, conversational, as if reporting the weather. "Cited a 'documentation discrepancy.' That was fast. Somebody made a call."

Of course somebody had. Odile felt the whole machine of it closing around them—the tender, the denied clearance, the queue that was suddenly a trap. This was how Halcyon worked; they'd seen it in the decrypted fleet orders, in the tidy bureaucratic grammar of extermination. Never a mob. Always a process.

"Vent the cargo," Odile said.

Through the hull they felt it: the deep thud of the bay clamps blowing, then a shudder as forty tons of hollow lies tumbled into vacuum, scattering across the depot's approach lane like a dropped hand of cards. The *Sebastian* jumped sideways, lighter by a heartbeat, and Sefa rode the kick with a low curse and a correction so smooth it barely registered.

"Mass signature just changed," Pell announced. "Tender's solution is stale. They're recalculating. They look... annoyed. Can ships look annoyed? That one looks annoyed."

"Corridor in thirty seconds," Sefa said. "Everyone strap in and pray to whatever you've got. Mine's a dead grandmother who flew worse rocks than this."

Odile strapped in beside her and gripped the archive drive through their jacket. On the display, the gap between the second and third asteroids opened up ahead of them—a slot of darkness narrower than the *Sebastian* was long, walled in stone older than any record anyone had ever falsified.

The tender broke off pursuit at the mouth of the corridor, exactly as predicted, peeling away with the unhurried certainty of something that believed time was on its side.

For one moment, one whole breath, Odile let themselves believe it might work.

Then the first shot came from behind—not from the tender but from somewhere else entirely, a second contact Pell hadn't even had time to name, a lance of light that missed the *Sebastian*'s stern by thirty meters and struck the corridor wall instead, and the asteroid shrugged off a boulder the size of a shuttlecraft directly into their path.

"Evasive!" Sefa was already moving, hauling the stick, and the *Sebastian* rolled onto its side and dove through the gap with meters to spare on either side, stone screaming past the hull, the gravity of the big rock grabbing them and slinging them outward like a drunk being thrown out of a bar.

They came out the far side tumbling, engines flaring, alive.

"Battery fire from the tender—one glancing hit, we lost the dorsal array," Pell shouted over alarms. "We're flagged, we're painted, and there's a second contact shadowing our exit vector. I don't have a clever route. I have bad options and worse ones."

Odile unbuckled, floated free for a half-second before the spin stabilized, and looked at the display—at the tender falling astern, patient, unhurried, certain—and understood, finally, completely, what the last chapter of their life was going to look like.

There was no port left that would take them. No queue, no neutral ground, no forged manifest clean enough. They had traded invisibility for a name, and names could be hunted.

"Bad options," Odile said, and heard their own voice come out level, almost calm, the calm of someone standing at last on solid ground. "Good. I've got one of my own. Set course for Kepler Deep."

Pell turned around slowly in his chair. "The relay station? The *Halcyon* relay station?"

"Yes," Odile said. "Exactly that one."
