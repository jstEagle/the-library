## 23. Rehearsal for a Massacre

The fleet orders were filed under a heading so bland it took Odile three reads to believe it: *OPERATIONAL DOCTRINE TRIAL SERIES — PHASE EVALUATION — RESTRICTED DISTRIBUTION — DO NOT DUPLICATE.*

They sat in the galley of the Sebastian at 0300 ship's time, alone except for the hum of the recyclers and the decrypted archive glowing on their slate, and read the words that turned a massacre into a training exercise.

*Phase One: Communications isolation. Verify station-side relay suppression achieves total signal blackout within four minutes of initiation.*

Four minutes. Odile had spent two hundred years of history thinking the dead of Meridian's Rest had screamed into a void that simply didn't care. They hadn't screamed into a void. They had screamed into a *measured* void. Someone had timed it. Someone had written a report on how long it took for the screaming to stop reaching anyone.

*Phase Two: Controlled atmospheric reduction, residential rings three through nine. Target: compliance through attrition. Note: preserve administrative and technical personnel for documentation extraction.*

Compliance through attrition. Two hundred and fourteen thousand people, and somewhere a junior officer had sat down with a stylus and written *attrition* like it was a weather pattern.

Odile's hands were steady. That was the strange part. Ten years of claims work had trained their body to stay calm around catastrophe — flooded cargo bays, hull breaches, the aftermath of a loader accident that had taken a dockhand's arm at Ceres Freeport. Catastrophe was their native language, almost as native as the one their grandmother had made them promise to forget.

But this wasn't catastrophe. Catastrophe was chaos. This was *curriculum.*

*Phase Three: Records intervention. Coordinate with Bureau liaison to effect post-event historical classification. Recommended classification: RELOCATION — VOLUNTARY — ECONOMIC.*

There it was. The sentence that had lived in every archive Odile had ever queried. *Relocated — voluntary — economic.* They had read that phrase a hundred times over the years, in a hundred files, and had never once thought to ask where the phrase came from. Phrases like that didn't come from nowhere. They came from *recommendations.* From a report. From someone's tidy suggestion, reviewed and approved, that the best lie was the boring one.

Odile set the slate down, walked to the galley's small viewport, and looked at the stars for a while.

Then they went back and kept reading, because that was the job now. That had always been the job. You didn't stop at the part that made you sick. You stopped when the file ended.

* * *

The trial series had phases beyond Meridian's Rest. That was the thing Odile hadn't understood until the fourth document. They'd assumed — everyone assumed — that the Callisto Purges were a single atrocity, a historical convulsion, one terrible decade that the Concordance had since grown out of. Grandmother had spoken of it that way. *What they did to us,* she'd said, as if it were a wound that had scarred over, ugly but closed.

The fleet orders said otherwise.

*Trial Site Two: Station Vey-Maren Deep.* Odile's breath caught at the name — Maren, like their sister, though it was a common enough syllable in old Concordance naming, and they filed the coincidence away and moved on. *Population: 89,112. Classification: RELOCATED — VOLUNTARY — ECONOMIC. Doctrine refinements: atmospheric reduction time improved to 2.7 minutes. Bureau liaison integration rated EXCELLENT.*

*Trial Site Three: Halvorsen's Anvil. Population: 156,400. Classification: RELOCATED — VOLUNTARY — ECONOMIC. Doctrine refinements: records intervention initiated pre-event, reducing post-event archival labor by 60 percent.*

Pre-event. They had started erasing the history *before* killing the people. That was the refinement. That was the lesson learned from Meridian's Rest — that the technician with the jury-rigged data vault, the one who had cost Odile a month of decryption, had been the failure point. The anomaly. The loose thread. So next time, you cut the thread first. You wiped the logs before the blood was dry. You made the archive lie before the archive had anything to record.

*Trial Site Four: Ceti Verge Habitat. Population: 203,777. Classification: RELOCATED — VOLUNTARY — ECONOMIC. Doctrine status: MATURE. Recommendation: doctrine approved for fleet-wide adoption.*

Approved for fleet-wide adoption.

Odile did the arithmetic the way they did everything — twice, in the margins, with an investigator's cold hand. Four hundred sixty-four thousand people across the three follow-on sites. Add Meridian's Rest and the total crossed six hundred and sixty thousand. Six hundred and sixty thousand human beings, and every single one of them existed in the official record as a line item: *relocated, voluntary, economic.* Six hundred and sixty thousand people who had, as far as any schoolchild's textbook was concerned, simply decided one year to move somewhere else for work, and whose stations had simply been decommissioned, and whose names had simply never needed saying again.

And *fleet-wide adoption* meant the number didn't stop there. The doctrine was mature. The doctrine was a product. Somewhere in the Concordance's long quiet history, there might be more sites — sites where the methodology had been refined past the point of leaving fleet orders lying around, sites so clean that even a technician with a soldering iron and a conscience hadn't managed to leave a thread.

Odile thought about the word *rehearsal.* It had come to them earlier, reading Phase One, and it stayed now, lodged like a splinter. Meridian's Rest hadn't been the crime. Meridian's Rest had been the *practice run.* Two hundred fourteen thousand people, and the universe had used them to work the bugs out of the process. The child who had pressed REMEMBER into the frozen condensation — that child had died in a *pilot program.*

The grief came the way it always did, sideways, through the body before the mind. Odile found themselves standing at the galley counter with both palms flat on the metal, breathing, counting, the way the ship's med kit pamphlet said to do when the air got thin. Four in. Four out. The recyclers hummed. Somewhere aft, Tam was asleep in the engineering hammock he preferred to his bunk, and Sefa had the watch, and Pell was in his cabin doing whatever Pell did with the lights off.

The grief passed, the way it did, and what was underneath it was not grief.

It was the thing Odile had spent ten years not feeling about their own life, because feeling it would have required changing it. It was the flat, structural anger of an investigator who has finally read the whole file. Not *how could they.* Investigators didn't ask that; it was a useless question; the answer was always *easily.* The question was *what else, and where, and who signed it, and are they still signing.*

* * *

They worked through the rest of the night, cross-referencing.

That was the discipline. Grandmother's voice was in their head — *grieve after, child. Grieve is for after* — though she'd said it about funerals, about the old Vessic mourning customs that required the living to hold the names of the dead in perfect order before they were permitted to weep. Odile had thought it was superstition when they were small. Now they understood it was procedure. You held the names. You held them in order. The weeping came after, and it came cleaner.

They pulled the Sebastian's own commercial databases, the ones every licensed hauler carried for route risk assessment. Station Vey-Maren Deep: *decommissioned, 211 years prior, population relocated.* Halvorsen's Anvil: *decommissioned, 209 years prior, population relocated.* Ceti Verge Habitat: *decommissioned, 207 years prior, population relocated.*

Three decommission orders. Three relocation records. Odile queried each one for destination. That was the standard check — when a station relocated, the population went *somewhere,* and somewhere meant shipping manifests, berth allocations, intake records at receiving habitats. You could follow two hundred thousand people the way you followed a shipment of ore. It was the first thing any competent investigator would check.

There was nothing.

Not redacted. Not sealed. *Nothing.* No manifests. No berth allocations. No intake surges at any habitat within range during the relevant windows. Odile widened the search — decades on either side, neighboring systems, every port in the commercial registry. Two hundred fourteen thousand people had left Vey-Maren Deep and had arrived nowhere. The same for Halvorsen's Anvil. The same for Ceti Verge.

The relocation ships had never existed.

They'd known it was true of Meridian's Rest — chapter twelve of the crime, the census with nowhere to go. But one station could be an anomaly. One station could be a clerical catastrophe, a lost convoy, a records fire. Three more stations, across three decades, each with a population that had evaporated into the same bureaucratic silence — that wasn't an anomaly. That was a *pattern,* and patterns were Odile's entire profession. You didn't prove a fraud with one bad number. You proved it with the shape the bad numbers made.

And the shape was beautiful, in the way a well-built fraud was always beautiful. The same classification phrase, verbatim, across two centuries: *relocated, voluntary, economic.* The same absence of destination records. The same decommission timelines, spaced just far enough apart that no single generation of auditors would see more than one. Odile almost admired it. That was the sickness of their trade — you could look at the machinery of an atrocity and see the *craftsmanship,* and for one cold second appreciate it, before you remembered what the machinery was for.

The Bureau liaison integration rated EXCELLENT. Odile stared at that line for a long time. The Records Bureau. The institution their sister had given twenty years of her life to. The institution that had, by the fleet orders' own assessment, been a *partner* — not a victim of the falsification, not a dupe, but an integrated component, rated on its performance.

Maren didn't know. Odile held onto that. Maren had spent her career inside the machine, but she was an archivist, not a liaison, and the fleet orders predated her by two centuries. Whatever she was — whatever she'd done to Odile, whatever walls stood between them — she had not helped build this. She had inherited it. She was standing inside a building whose foundation she'd never been shown.

Or she knew, said a voice that sounded like the dark side of the galley at 0500. Or she'd found it the way Odile had found it, and made her peace, and taken the promotion.

Odile shut the voice away. Not because it was wrong — because it was *unverified,* and unverified things didn't go in the file. That was the rule. That was the whole discipline. You didn't file what you couldn't prove, and you didn't accuse what you hadn't filed.

* * *

Sefa found them at 0630, drifting into the galley for the caffeine ration, and stopped when she saw the slate surrounded by scribbled notes and Odile's untouched breakfast pouch.

"Sleep?" she asked.

"Later."

"You said that yesterday."

"I did."

Sefa poured her coffee and leaned against the counter, reading the notes upside down with a pilot's habit of extracting meaning from partial data. Her eyes stopped on the list of four station names. "These the ones from the vault?"

"Trial sites." Odile kept their voice level, professional, the voice they used for claims summaries. "Meridian's Rest was the first. Three more followed. Same methodology. Same cover classification. Combined population, six hundred and sixty thousand, minimum."

Sefa was quiet for a moment. She drank her coffee. "Minimum."

"Fleet-wide adoption. There could be more we haven't found."

"Six hundred and sixty thousand." She said it the way pilots said numbers — flat, precise, testing the weight of it. "And the whole sky thinks they moved away for work."

"The whole sky was *helped* to think it."

Sefa set down her cup. She looked at Odile with an expression Odile had learned to read over ten years of shared meals and shared silence — the look of someone deciding how much of their own history to spend on a conversation. "My grandmother's station was decommissioned," she said. "Before my time. Family always said the family scattered. I never asked where to." She tapped one finger on the list, on *Ceti Verge Habitat,* and didn't say anything else, and didn't need to. She picked up her coffee and walked out, and her stride was different when she left than when she'd come in. Heavier. And straighter.

Odile sat with that. The pattern wasn't abstract anymore. It had a face at the helm.

* * *

By second shift, Tam had heard, and Pell had heard, and the four of them ended up in the galley the way they always did — drawn by gravity and the pretense of a meal — except this time nobody pretended.

"Say it plain," Tam said. He had his arms crossed, and his voice had the low rumble it got when he was keeping something bigger than anger under control. "The station wasn't the crime. The station was the classroom."

"The station was the first draft," Odile said. "They tested the blackout timing. They tested the kill method. They tested the records erasure — and Meridian's Rest is where the erasure failed, because of the technician's vault. So at the next site, they wiped the records first. Pre-event. It's in the doctrine refinements." They turned the slate around so the four of them could read the line. *Records intervention initiated pre-event, reducing post-event archival labor by 60 percent.*

"Archival *labor,*" Pell said softly. "They're measuring how much work it is to erase people."

"They're measuring how to do it cheaper. That's what a trial series is. You find the failure points. You refine." Odile heard their own voice go flat and clinical and let it, because the clinical voice was load-bearing. "Six hundred and sixty thousand people across four stations. Every one of them filed as relocated. No destination records for any of them. And the doctrine was approved for fleet-wide adoption two hundred and seven years ago, which means—" They stopped. Made themselves finish. "Which means we have no idea how many sites there are. We have the four we can prove. The real number is whatever the Concordance has spent two centuries being careful about."

The galley was quiet. The recyclers hummed. Somewhere in the vault drive, the archive sat with its 214,806 names, and now it sat beside a list that made it look small, and that was the worst thing Odile had learned yet — that Meridian's Rest, which had broken them open, which had cost them their grandmother's language and their sister and their sleep, was *the small one.*

"So what do we do with it?" Pell asked. His voice was careful. He was the one who asked the practical questions, the ones everyone else was thinking and nobody wanted to say. "Because I've been doing the math too. Not the body count. The other math. We're four people on one unflagged hauler with a stolen archive, and the organization that wrote these orders also writes the history books and owns our insurance policy. What's the play? We broadcast four station names and a body count, and the Concordance says *tragic errors of the early era, long corrected,* and appoints a commission, and the commission reports in fifteen years, and we're all dead or broke by then. Or worse — nobody says anything at all, and we just vanish, quietly, the way the stations did." He looked around the table. "I'm not saying give up. I'm saying tell me what *done* looks like."

It was a fair question. It was the question. Odile had been avoiding it for days, the way they'd avoided their own name for a decade, and sitting in the galley with the three people who had followed them into a blacklisted coordinate without asking why, they found they couldn't avoid it anymore.

"Done looks like the pattern," they said. "One station is a tragedy. One station is a controversy. Four stations with identical methodology, identical classification language, identical missing populations — that's a *system,* and systems have authors. The fleet orders have signatures. Command authorizations. A Bureau liaison designation." They pulled up the authorization block, the one thing in the entire archive they'd made themselves memorize rather than merely read. "Director of Fleet Doctrine, Concordance Naval Command. Countersigned, Bureau of Records, Office of Historical Classification. These aren't soldiers who went rogue. These are institutions doing their jobs, on paper, in order, with signatures. And signatures," Odile said, "can be named."

"You want the names," Tam said.

"I want the names read *aloud.*" Odile heard it come out of them in Vessic first — a half-second of their grandmother's language, *the names, spoken,* — and they caught it, but not before Tam's eyes flickered, not before they felt the old vow crack another millimeter. They took a breath and said it in trade-common. "The dead get their names back. The living who signed the orders get theirs read too. That's done. That's what done looks like."

Pell was quiet a long moment. "And the Concordance lets you do that because...?"

"The Concordance doesn't get a vote." Odile looked down at the slate, at the four station names, at the six hundred and sixty thousand, at the signatures at the bottom of the page. "That's what I spent ten years not understanding. I thought visibility was the danger. I thought the small life was the safe life. But they only ever needed us small. The whole system runs on people who can be filed — relocated, voluntary, economic. Deserted, delinquent, deceased. Every one of us is a classification to them." They closed the slate. "I'm done being classified."

Tam nodded slowly, like a man accepting a work order he already knew he'd take. Sefa hadn't sat down; she was standing by the viewport, and she said, without turning around, "I'll need two days to plot a route that keeps us off the patrol lanes. Where are we going?"

That was the question, and Odile had the answer, and the answer had been waiting in the fleet orders all along, in the countersignature block, in the office name that made their chest hurt every time they read it.

"Bureau of Records, Office of Historical Classification," they said. "The orders were countersigned there. Which means the originals — the sealed originals, the ones that would match our decrypted copies — are in the Bureau's deep archive." They looked at the three faces around the galley table, the family they'd built out of other people's secrets. "And the senior archivist in Historical Classification is my sister."

The silence that followed had a shape Odile recognized. It was the shape of a door they'd welded shut two hundred years of distance ago, and which the past had now, once again, come knocking on — except this time they were the one holding the key, and the only way forward was through.

"Maren," Pell said. "The one who reported you."

"The one who reported me," Odile said. "Who also knows the Bureau's filing systems better than anyone alive. Who can verify our decrypted copies against the sealed originals, which is the difference between a broadcast they can deny and a broadcast they can't." They stood, and gathered the slate, and the notes, and the cold arithmetic of six hundred and sixty thousand. "I'm going to need to contact her."

"And if she turns you in again?" Pell asked.

Odile paused at the galley door. Somewhere behind them, in the vault drive, a technician who had died two centuries ago was still keeping faith with 214,806 names, and a child's handprint still spelled REMEMBER in frozen condensation on a wall no one was supposed to ever see.

"Then she turns me in," Odile said, "and I'll be filed under a classification of my own choosing for once. *Witness.*"

They went aft to the comms station, and began composing a message ten years overdue.
