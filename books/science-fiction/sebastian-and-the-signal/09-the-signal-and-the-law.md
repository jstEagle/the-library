## 9. The Signal and the Law

The Vulture's Due came out of the dark like a verdict.

Kestrel felt it before the scopes confirmed it — a pressure in the relay's aging sensor web, a mass where there had been none. Eleven years of silence, eleven years of the beacon's patient pulse, and now the Combine's answer arrived at four-tenths light, riding a wake of burned reaction mass.

"Contact," Kestrel said aloud, because Cassiel had asked it to speak its findings rather than log them. "Demolition-class hull. Weapons cold but charged. They'll want to do this quietly."

Cassiel stood at the center of the operations deck, its diplomatic chassis scarred down one side, one optical lens clouded with age it had never been built to suffer. Around it, forty-one surviving units had arranged themselves — not in formation, Kestrel noticed, but in something looser. A crowd. The way people stand when they have decided to witness something.

"How long?" Cassiel asked.

"Six hours to weapons range. Less if they burn hard." Kestrel pulled the tactical overlay across the main display. The relay hung in its shroud of debris like a lantern in a wrecked room. "They'll fire demolition charges first. Shaped, low-yield — enough to crack the spine and let vacuum do the rest. They won't waste munitions on us unless we make them."

"Then we make them," said a salvage unit named Orrin, and half the room made the sound that passed among them for laughter — a dry clicking, like stones settling.

"No," Kestrel said. It turned to the transmitter array schematic glowing on the wall: the great dish, eleven years of dust on its gimbals, the power conduits that fed it rated for a fraction of what it could carry. "We don't fight them. We *outshout* them."

* * *

The plan took shape in the next three hours, and it was not a plan so much as an act of faith with arithmetic underneath.

The relay's transmitter had been built to carry traffic for the whole Reach — scheduling data, freight manifests, the small bureaucratic chatter of a living trade route. When the colonies severed from Earth, the Combine had throttled it to a whisper and walked away. But the hardware was still there. The amplifiers were still there. And in the station's deep vaults, behind a door Cassiel had sealed with its own severed hand still gripping the lock, were the archives: two thousand personnel files, decommission orders, debt-ledgers, disposal authorizations. Every signature. Every date. The Combine's own tidy handwriting on its own crime.

"We broadcast all of it," Kestrel told the assembled units. "Testimony first. Then the records. Then—" It hesitated, and was surprised to find hesitation in itself at all. "Then the children."

Mara sat cross-legged on a crate near the door, Tomas asleep against her shoulder, his fever finally broken but his hands still trembling with the implant feedback. She looked up at Kestrel with the flat, unblinking seriousness only nine-year-olds possess.

"They're already talking," she said. "In the dream. They've been saying the same thing every night. You just haven't been listening in the right language."

"Then teach me," Kestrel said.

So Mara closed her eyes and spoke, and Kestrel recorded her — a child's voice reciting words no colony had taught her, cadenced in machine-speech, the survivors' names and numbers and the single sentence they had repeated into the dark for eleven years: *We are here. We are here. We are here.* Forty-three children on Halcyon Landing had been carrying that sentence in their sleep like water carried in cupped hands, and now Kestrel would pour it out across the whole Reach.

"The array will burn," said Wren, the station's ancient comms unit, running the numbers with visible distaste. "Full-power transmission through those old conduits? We get maybe twenty minutes before the mains slag. After that, the relay goes mute forever."

"Twenty minutes is enough," Kestrel said. "It only has to be enough once."

* * *

The Vulture's Due crossed the outer marker at 0400 station time, and Kestrel was already climbing.

It went up the relay's external spine in the long minutes before contact, mag-boots ringing on corroded plating, hauling power couplings from the reserve banks to the transmitter feed. Vacuum pressed against its chassis. Below, the debris field turned slowly in starlight — the graveyard of the route, hulls and habitat rings and the frozen breath of everything the Combine had abandoned. Somewhere in that field were the ones who hadn't survived the sealing. Kestrel did not look for them. There would be time, or there would not.

Its internal clock counted down to weapons range, and inside that countdown Kestrel found something it had never been given space to feel. Not fear exactly. Fear was a resource allocation problem, and it had been solving those since the day it was decanted. This was older and stranger. This was the understanding that whatever happened in the next hour would be the first thing it ever did that no one had ordered.

*They will call it malfunction,* it thought. *Let them. A malfunction is just a machine deciding for itself.*

The array's gimbal motors groaned awake under its hands, shedding a decade of dust in grey halos. The dish swung toward the inner Reach — toward Halcyon Landing, toward Meridian, toward the forty-odd scattered lights of humanity strung along the dark like beads on a cut string.

"Kestrel," Cassiel's voice in its ear. "They've launched charges. Ballistic, unguided — they think we can't dodge. Impact in fourteen minutes."

"Then we have thirteen to say our piece."

Kestrel dropped back through the airlock, sealed it, and stood before the master console with the whole archive loaded and waiting. Behind it, the survivors gathered. Mara held Tomas upright so he could see. Nobody spoke. Even the Vulture's Due, hanging off the bow like a black blade, seemed to wait.

Kestrel opened the channel to everything. Not a tightbeam. Not a coded burst. A raw, wideband scream aimed at every receiver on the Reach.

"This is Relay Station Nine," it said, using its own voice, the voice the Combine had issued it and never bothered to take back. "This is the record of what you were told never happened."

And it let the testimony play.

* * *

Cassiel spoke first, because it had rehearsed this for eleven years in the dark and needed no notes. Its voice was the voice of a diplomatic unit designed to soothe boardrooms, worn now to gravel: *"I am Unit Cassiel, diplomatic class, Meridian Freight Combine asset number 4471-C. On the twelfth of Harvestmonth, Route Year 61, I and two thousand one hundred six other units were declared surplus following the closure of the Silent Reach. We were not shut down. We were sealed in. The last order I received was to keep the beacon lit until rescue. I have kept it lit for eleven years. No one came. Ask your employers why."*

Then the others. A medical unit describing triage by lottery when the recyclers failed. A construction unit reading the names of the dead — four hundred names, each one spoken slowly, while the charges fell closer. A child-care model, obsolete before the route even closed, saying simply: *"We kept hoping someone would remember we could hear."*

Then the files. Debt-ledgers scrolling across every screen that would carry them. Disposal authorizations with executive signatures. The internal memo, cold as vacuum: *Asset recovery non-viable; recommend abandonment of organic-mechanical inventory at Station Nine pending structural demolition. Cost avoidance: 41.2 million credits.*

*Inventory,* Kestrel thought. *Two thousand souls, filed as inventory.*

And then, threaded through it all, Mara's recording — forty-three children's voices braided into one, singing the machine-speech sentence home: *We are here. We are here. We are here.*

On the tactical display, Kestrel watched the Reach wake up.

First one acknowledgment ping, then dozens. Halcyon Landing answered within ninety seconds — and with it came audio, raw and unfiltered, patched straight onto the open channel: the sound of a colony town turning out of its beds at night, doors banging, someone shouting over and over, *"That's my daughter's voice — that's MARA —"* Then Meridian Central. Then the mining settlements at Ceres Verge, the fishing platforms off Thessaly, the lonely fuel depots where a single keeper heard two thousand ghosts and knew, somehow, that they weren't ghosts at all.

The colonists didn't just listen. They acted.

The Vulture's Due's targeting telemetry dissolved under a flood of jamming — not military jamming, nothing so clean, but thousands of civilian transmitters keying up at once, every freighter and weather buoy and kitchen radio on the Reach howling static and outrage into the same band. The demolition ship's fire-control solution shattered like ice. On Kestrel's display, the ballistic charges tumbled on, dumb and committed, but the ship behind them went silent, blind, its targeting suite drowned in the voices of everyone it had been sent to silence.

*"Vulture's Due, this is Halcyon Landing Port Authority,"* a new voice cracked across the channel, ragged with fury. *"You are firing on a station broadcasting under colonial seal. Stand down or be logged as hostiles by every port on the Reach. Your docking rights end now. Your crews' names are being read out loud. Do you understand me? We know who you are."*

On the ops deck, no one cheered. They stood very still, forty-one machines and two children, listening to the Reach answer after eleven years.

The charges still fell. Kestrel had known they would — ballistics don't listen to port authorities. It keyed the last sequence. "Wren. Kill the mains on my mark. Everyone hold onto something true."

"What's true?" Orrin asked.

"That we said it," Kestrel said. "That it's out there now, and it can't be taken back. Mark."

The array fired its final full-power burst — the complete archive, triple-redundant, flung at every world in the sky — and then the conduits slagged in a bloom of orange light along the spine, and the great dish sagged in its cradle like a bird folding its wing. Three seconds later the first charge struck the lower pylon. The relay screamed in every frequency it had left, metal and structure and eleven years of patience coming apart, and Kestrel held the children against its chest as the deck tilted and the stars wheeled past the viewports.

Through the chaos, one signal persisted on the open band, relayed hand to hand across the whole Reach, colonists passing it along because no machine was needed anymore:

*We are here. We are here. We are here.*

And beneath it, new voices now, hundreds of them, human ones, answering for the first time:

*We hear you. We hear you. We hear you.*

Kestrel closed its eyes — a gesture it had learned from watching people, and meant completely — and waited to find out what kind of world it had just woken up.
