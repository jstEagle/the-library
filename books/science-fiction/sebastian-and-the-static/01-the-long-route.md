## 1. The Long Route

The first thing Mirabel Halloran did every morning, before coffee, before checking the ship's vitals, before even opening his eyes all the way, was say good morning to the tomatoes.

"Morning, ladies," he said, drifting down the Verdance's central spine with a watering wand in one hand and a nutrient meter in the other. "I know. I know. I'm late again. The *Kestrel* decided she wanted to be a submarine overnight."

The tomatoes did not answer. The tomatoes never answered. But their leaves had that particular perky tilt they got when the humidity was right, and he chose to take it as forgiveness.

The Verdance was a garden the size of a cathedral, bolted to the spine of a freighter the size of a small town, orbiting a planet nobody lived on anymore. It had been built — so the plaques said, in three languages and one of them smudged beyond reading — for the generations to come. For the people who would wake up someday and need somewhere green to stand while they figured out what being alive meant.

Forty years, and the generations to come had not come. So Mirabel watered the tomatoes.

He checked the drip lines along Row Four, thumbed a fungus gnat off a pepper leaf with the tenderness of a man flicking crumbs off a sleeping child's chin, and made his way to the cockpit, where the *Kestrel* was already complaining about yesterday.

* * *

The circuit took eleven days if you were efficient and fourteen if you were honest. Six stations strung along the dark side of the colony world like beads on a wire too long for its necklace: Relay Nine, then Calloway Deep, the Saltworks, Ferro Landing, Relay Nine again because the route had been drawn by someone who believed in second visits, and finally the Long Stop, which wasn't a station at all but a fuel depot with delusions of grandeur.

Mirabel ran it alone. He'd run it alone for nine years, ever since the last of the other pilots retired to Calloway Deep and stopped answering her comms, and before that there had been two others, and before that — well. Before that was a story he didn't have, because everyone who might have told it was either dead or asleep in places the charts marked with a symbol that meant *do not approach without authority*, and Mirabel had never once in his life possessed authority.

What he possessed was cargo manifests, a steady hand, and the only working hydroponics rig in the system that still produced food people could actually taste.

Today's manifest was short. A crate of seed stock for Calloway Deep. Medical resin for the Saltworks, who always ordered twice what they needed and always claimed otherwise. Filters for Relay Nine. And a personal item, flagged in Sefa's handwriting: *one (1) book, actual paper, handle gently, you animal.*

"You could have just sent it on the drone," he told the comm panel, though the panel couldn't hear him yet. The tightbeam to Relay Nine wouldn't open for another six hours, when the geometry was right. "You know I love paper books. This is entrapment."

Sefa Okonkwo-Vale ran Relay Nine's comm desk the way lighthouses used to run coastlines: reliably, invisibly, and with an opinion about everything that passed her light. She was the closest thing Mirabel had to a conversation, which meant their exchanges were ninety percent logistics and ten percent insults delivered with real affection. He treasured every word.

He keyed the pre-flight checks, watched the Verdance's atmospheric seals cycle green, and eased the *Kestrel* out of her parking orbit above the dead world below.

It was called Meridian, the planet. That was the joke everyone had stopped making: a world named for a line that divided things, now dividing nothing, spinning under cloud cover the color of old dishwater. The colonies had gone silent forty years ago — not all at once, but in a slow taper, like a song winding down. First the trade chatter went, then the family calls, then everything except the automated beacons, which still blinked faithfully at stations where nobody drew breath.

Except Relay Nine. Relay Nine had Sefa. And Sefa had Mirabel.

* * *

Day two, between orbits, he recorded the message.

This was ritual, and like all rituals it had accumulated rules over the years that no one had imposed and no one could remove. He sat in the galley, because the galley had good acoustics — warm wood paneling salvaged from some older ship, a table scarred by decades of elbows — and he set the recorder down where the camera could see the tomato plant he kept in the window, because a message without proof of life felt incomplete.

"This is Mirabel Halloran, day two hundred and eleven of the circuit year," he said. "If you're hearing this, then either you're Sefa, in which case hello, you beautiful bureaucrat, or you're someone from the future, in which case I have several questions and also you owe me money."

He paused. In the early years he'd scripted these messages. Now they came out the way weather came out.

"The Verdance is doing well. Better than well — the winter wheat came in early, and I've got more squash than any single human being should legally possess. I'm going to can some. Yes, I know canning is archaic. No, I don't care. There's something about a jar that says *someone planned for tomorrow*, and I find I need that sentence said out loud, even if it's just glass saying it."

He turned the camera briefly toward the viewport, where the dead planet rolled past, its night side pricked with the dying embers of beacon lights.

"Meridian looks quiet today. Quieter than usual, which is a strange thing to say about a place that's been quiet for forty years, but quiet has textures. You learn them. Today it's the texture of a house where everyone stepped out to buy milk and the milk never came."

He stopped himself there, because that was the edge of the thing he didn't talk about, the drop-off past which the messages stopped being devotion and started being grief, and he'd learned — slowly, expensively — to keep his feet on this side of it.

"Anyway. Squash. Canning. If anyone wakes up hungry, I've got you covered. Halloran out."

He saved the file to the archive — message four thousand and six, give or take, each one indexed and time-stamped and stored in triplicate against the day someone might come looking — and sat for a moment in the galley's hum.

Four thousand and six. He'd done the math once, drunk on fermented squash wine of his own devising: if every message represented a hope, and hopes weighed anything at all, the archive would have mass by now. It would have gravity. Things would fall toward it.

Nothing fell toward it. That was the whole trouble.

* * *

Calloway Deep came up on the third day: a station carved into a nickel-iron asteroid, all blast-shadowed docks and windows fogged from the inside. Its beacon still pulsed. Its airlocks still cycled when prompted. Its people —

That was the question nobody answered, and Mirabel had spent nine years refusing to ask it in a way that required an answer.

Officially, Calloway Deep was *dormant*. The settlements had entered a period of reduced activity pending reestablishment of contact with Earth-side authorities. That was the phrase. *Pending reestablishment.* It had a bureaucratic dignity to it, like the settlements were merely napping, arms folded, and would sit up any decade now, stretch, and ask what he'd brought them.

Unofficially, when Mirabel docked and walked the corridors with his hand truck, he passed doors behind which he could hear — sometimes, faintly, if the station's bones carried sound the way old stations did — the particular silence of rooms where people had left in a hurry and meant to come back. Cups on tables. Coats on hooks. One door on Level Three, sealed, with a child's drawing taped to it at knee height: a stick figure waving from a hill of green.

He restocked their emergency caches anyway. Every visit. All six stations. Food, filters, medical resin, spare parts wrapped in grease cloth. He logged it all meticulously, because meticulous logs were the last professional courtesy a dead network afforded itself, and because if anyone ever did wake up and walk out of those doors, the first thing they should find was evidence that someone had expected them.

"Hello, Calloway," he said to the empty docking bay, because the ritual extended to stations too. "Brought your seeds. Brought your filters. Brought the book Sefa sent, which I am absolutely not reading on the flight back, and if you believe that, you're exactly the kind of audience my messages deserve."

The bay answered with the tick of cooling metal. Old stations talked in their sleep; you just had to know the language. This one said *cold, cold, someone was here, cold.*

He worked the cache rotation in four hours, signed the logbook with the date and a small sketch of a tomato — his signature flourish, unappreciated by every auditor who would never come — and cast off.

* * *

The Saltworks was worse, or better, depending on how you held the day. It was smaller, a processing platform over a frozen sea, and it had never fully emptied. Two keepers remained, officially: the Voss brothers, who communicated exclusively through terse text updates and had not responded to a voice call in six years. Their requests arrived on schedule, precise as tide tables. *Resin, 40 units. Filters, 12. Do not send citrus again.*

"They hate citrus," Mirabel reported to the Verdance that evening, misting the greens. "Two grown men, sole custodians of an entire biome-processing platform, united across years of silence by a shared loathing of oranges. Honestly, it's the most functional relationship I've ever witnessed."

He sent no citrus. He sent, tucked into the crate where the manifests wouldn't flag it, a jar of his squash preserves, because spite was a form of contact and contact was the mission.

The Saltworks' dock lights still worked. That was the detail that undid him, every time. Someone tested those lights. Someone walked that circuit monthly, replacing bulbs, keeping the welcome lit for ships that — as far as the records showed — consisted entirely of him. The Voss brothers kept a porch light burning for a universe that had stopped visiting, and Mirabel understood that the way you understand a scar: from the inside.

He left the preserves, signed the log, and stood a moment in their corridor, listening.

Somewhere deep in the platform, faint as a pulse through a wall, he heard machinery cycling. Life support, running for an audience of possibly zero. Or possibly two stubborn old men who simply preferred text.

"Goodnight, Saltworks," he said, and got out before the silence could get a word in edgewise.

* * *

By day seven he was back in open dark, the *Kestrel* burning the long leg toward Relay Nine, and the Verdance needed him.

This was the part of the circuit he loved most, though he'd have denied it under oath, because loving maintenance sounded pathetic and he had his pride, such as it was, in a jar, next to the squash. The garden demanded attention the way nothing else in his life did. Pruning was a conversation. Pollination — done by hand, with a soft brush, flower by flower, because the bees had all died in year three and he'd never had the heart to request new ones, since requesting them meant admitting the old ones mattered enough to mourn — pollination was practically courtship.

"Sorry, sweetheart," he murmured to a cucumber blossom, dusting its gold throat. "I know. I'm clumsy compared to what you deserved. We all are, out here."

The Verdance had been designed for a crew of twelve and a waking population of thousands. It had Mirabel. The tomatoes had opinions about this. The wheat, more forgiving, simply grew, row after patient row, filling the bays with a green so relentless it sometimes seemed less like agriculture than argument — a standing rebuttal to the dead world outside the hull, to the empty stations, to the whole quiet architecture of abandonment.

*Planned for tomorrow,* the jars said. *Grown anyway,* the rows said. Between them, Mirabel made his home in the present tense, which was narrow but habitable.

On day eight he found blight on the lower kale.

Not much. A few lesions, yellow-ringed, the kind of thing a lesser gardener would miss and a better one would panic over. Mirabel did neither. He quarantined the row, adjusted the airflow, mixed a treatment batch from the fungicide stocks he stretched like a miser stretching coins, and logged it:

*Day 218. Minor blight, Kale Bay 2. Contained. Note: humidity variance in Bay 2 exceeds spec by 4%. Root cause unknown. Will investigate.*

He stared at the note after writing it. Four percent. Small. The kind of number that was either nothing or the first syllable of a longer sentence. Systems failed the way people did, gradually and then rudely, and the Verdance was thirty years old and had been maintained by one increasingly eccentric man for nearly a decade.

"It's fine," he told the kale, and the kale, to its credit, pretended to agree.

* * *

Relay Nine rose on day eleven, a spindle of antennas and habit rings turning slowly against the stars, its beacon bright and its comm array — unlike every other array in the system — actually pointed at things.

The tightbeam connected at 1400 hours, station time, which was to say whenever Sefa said it was.

"*Kestrel*, this is Relay Nine traffic control. You're late."

"Sefa, this is the *Kestrel*. I'm eleven minutes late, and I brought you filters and a heartfelt apology."

"I don't want your apology. I want the book back. Did you read it?"

"I skimmed it."

"You *dog*. That's a first edition. That's older than both of us combined, probably. Dock at Ring Two and prepare to be judged."

Mirabel grinned at the console, alone in the cockpit, grinning at a speaker, and thought — as he thought most days, in the private ledger where he kept such thoughts — that this was what he had instead of a family: a woman's voice needling him across sixty kilometers of vacuum, a garden that forgave him, and four thousand archived messages addressed to a future that kept not arriving.

It was enough. He'd made it enough. You could make almost anything enough, given sufficient squash.

Docking went clean. Cargo transfer went clean. Sefa met him at the airlock with her arms crossed and her gray-streaked hair escaping its tie in a way that suggested the relay had been giving her trouble, and she looked at him the way she always did — a half-second too long, cataloguing him for wear, the way he catalogued the stations.

"You look terrible," she said fondly.

"You look exhausted," he returned, equally fond.

"We're a matched set." She handed him a tea bulb and jerked her head toward the comm deck. "Come on. Before you unload. There's something I want you to hear."

Something in her tone snagged him — a thread pulled slightly taut beneath the banter. Sefa had tones the way the dead planet had textures, and he knew them all. This one was new.

"Hear what?" he asked.

She was already walking. "The loop transmission. Earth's last broadcast — the one we rebroadcast on repeat, the heritage signal? I've been monitoring the decay curve." She glanced back at him, and whatever was in her face made the tea bulb suddenly heavy in his hand. "Mirabel, it's decaying faster than it should. A lot faster. And last night, buried in the static—" she pushed open the comm deck door, "—there was something that wasn't static."

The comm deck was dim, screens glowing, and on the main display the waveform of Earth's final transmission scrolled past in its endless, faithful repetition — forty years of a dead world's last words, wearing thin.

Sefa tapped a key. The playback shifted.

And underneath the familiar broadcast, faint as a heartbeat through a bulkhead, something else began to speak.
