## 7. Passengers

The distress call came in on a frequency that hadn't been used for anything but automated weather pings since before Mirabel was born.

He was three hours out from the first coordinate, the Halloran archive still sitting in his chest like a swallowed stone, when the Verdance's long-range array caught it: a repeating pattern, old protocol, the kind of mayday that assumed someone would answer. *Vessel adrift. Life support nominal for six days. Please acknowledge.*

Please acknowledge. As if acknowledgment were a courtesy anyone still extended out here.

Mirabel sat with his thumb hovering over the transmit key for a full minute. Sefa had told him — twice now, with increasing sharpness — to stop answering things. Every unanswered signal he'd ever logged had taught him the same lesson: silence was the local language. But a lifeboat was not an archive. A lifeboat was a person, or had been, and whatever else Odile Halloran had done to him, she had raised him to check the locks on empty houses and leave them warmer than he found them.

"Verdance actual to drifting vessel," he said, and his voice came out rustier than he liked. "I read you. State your position."

The reply took eleven minutes. Light-lag, he told himself, though the geometry didn't quite work out to light-lag, and he knew it.

*Position transmitted. We see you, Verdance actual. We've been watching you for two days.*

That was the first wrong thing. The second wrong thing was the voice itself: male, middle-aged by its grain, exhausted in a way that sounded less like fear and more like a man who had run out of places to put his fear down. And the third wrong thing — the one that made Mirabel's hands go cold on the yoke — was the name attached to the transmission header.

RENK, I. ENGINEERING DIVISION, LOOP AUTHORITY (FORMER).

Mirabel had spent four days learning what the loop authority was. He had learned it from ledgers written in his grandmother's hand, in a dead habitat orbiting a coordinate she'd chosen, and every page of it had smelled of dust and deliberate concealment. Now a man with *former* after his division was floating in a tin can along Mirabel's own flight path, asking to be picked up.

Either the universe had a sense of humor, or his grandmother did, and Mirabel no longer believed there was much difference between the two.

"I'm coming to get you," he said anyway. Because he was, because that was the kind of man he apparently was regardless of what his family tree turned out to be growing.

* * *

The lifeboat was a Corvid-class, forty years obsolete, its hull scorched along one flank in a pattern Mirabel recognized from the manuals he'd memorized as a boy: atmospheric interface damage. Someone had ridden this thing down through a sky recently and hard. It tumbled with the lazy, wounded grace of a bird with a broken wing, and when Mirabel matched velocities with it — a maneuver that took him ninety minutes and cost him most of his reaction mass reserve — he saw the face in the observation port.

Ivo Renk looked like a man assembled from spare parts by someone in a hurry. Tall, stooped even in null-g, gray stubble over hollow cheeks, eyes so red-rimmed they seemed lit from behind. When the airlock cycled and Mirabel pulled him through into the Verdance's tender cabin, the man grabbed Mirabel's forearms with both hands and held on like a drowning sailor, and for a moment neither of them said anything at all.

"You're real," Renk croaked finally. "Halloran. You're actually real."

"That depends who's asking," Mirabel said. "And how they know my name."

Renk laughed — a short, cracked sound with no humor in it, the laugh of a man discovering that the universe's jokes were all on him. He let go of Mirabel's arms and floated back against the crash webbing, and up close Mirabel could see the details that made the picture worse rather than better: burn scars on the backs of his hands, a pressure bruise across his ribs where a harness had done its job too well, and under his collar, half-hidden, the faded ink of an employee tattoo. Loop Authority. Engineering Division. The same mark, Mirabel realized with a small cold shock, that appeared on the spine of every ledger in his grandmother's archive.

"You're the engineer," Mirabel said. "From the trial recordings."

"I'm the engineer who wasn't called to testify." Renk closed his eyes. "There were eleven of us who understood the selection architecture. Ten testified. I ran."

"Why?"

"Because ten testified and the settlement enforcers shot six of them anyway." He opened his eyes, and they were steady now, which was somehow more frightening than the exhaustion. "Officially it was a transport accident. Unofficially, Captain Hale does not tolerate witnesses to arithmetic. I did the math on my odds and chose the atmosphere. This boat was built for reentry. I wasn't sure I was built for it, but the alternative was staying planetside and finding out."

Mirabel got him water. Watched him drink it the way thirsty men drink — greedily, then apologetically, then greedily again. Outside the port, the Verdance turned slowly on its axis, gardens catching the distant sun, and Renk stared at it with an expression Mirabel couldn't parse until the man spoke.

"It's beautiful," Renk said. "Do you know what it is? Do you know what you're keeping alive up here?"

"A garden," Mirabel said. "For whoever comes next."

Renk looked at him for a long time. Whatever he saw seemed to settle something in him, because his shoulders dropped a centimeter, which in null-g meant nothing physically and everything otherwise.

"She never told you," he said. "Not any of it. She just left you the keys and let you find the doors yourself."

"My grandmother," Mirabel said carefully, "communicated exclusively through chore lists. So no. She never told me anything. She made me *find* things. There's a difference, and I've spent four days hating her for it."

"Good," said Renk. "Hold onto that. You'll need it where we're going."

* * *

They talked for six hours while Mirabel nursed the tender back toward the Meridian's last known drift vector, and by the end of it Mirabel understood three things clearly and a fourth thing only in outline, the way you understand a shape through frosted glass.

First: the sabotage was real, and it was old. The decay eating Earth's final transmission loop — the static that had been chewing through the archive of humanity's last years, the noise Sefa had been charting with rising alarm — was not entropy. It was design. A throttling routine buried in the loop's maintenance layer, set to degrade the authority structure on a schedule. Someone had built a countdown into the foundation of the thing, and then hidden the countdown inside forty years of ordinary-looking wear.

"Your grandmother's work?" Mirabel asked.

"Her specification. My implementation." Renk said it flatly, without pride, without shame. "She came to me twenty-two years ago with a request that looked like routine hardening. Redundancy pathways, failover logic. I built what she asked for. It took me eight months to understand what I'd actually built, and by then she was dead and the code was live and there was no version of reporting it that ended with me alive. So I did what engineers do. I documented everything and waited for someone competent to come looking."

"And you think that's me."

"I think you're the only person alive whose name opens the doors," Renk said. "Which is a different thing, and worse."

Second: the override codes existed. Real codes, physical tokens, sealed in a way that couldn't be spoofed remotely — Renk carried them in a lead-lined case strapped to his chest, and when he showed Mirabel, the case was dented from the reentry burn and the seals inside were intact. Three codes. One for Vault A. One for Vault B. One, Renk said, tapping the third slot without opening it, for the transmitter heart itself.

"The vaults can be woken separately, locally, if you have physical access and years you don't have. The heart can wake both at once. That was always the design argument — the families wanted the heart capable of single-vault authorization so they could choose. Your grandmother wanted it capable of both, and she lost that argument, officially. Unofficially, she had me build the capability into the hardware and then bury the command path so deep in the maintenance layer that nobody auditing the system would ever find it." He smiled, and it was a terrible smile. "I'm very good at burying things. It's why I'm alive. Burying myself was just one more job."

Third: the price. Renk laid it out without ceremony, the way men do when they've rehearsed a sentence so many times it has worn smooth.

"There's a girl asleep in the Meridian's cradle bay. Nell. My daughter. Fourteen years old when I put her under — she's fourteen still, biologically, because cryo doesn't care about our schedules. Her mother died in the purges. I had exactly one asset worth anything to anyone, and I spent it buying her a cradle position through intermediaries I don't fully trust, and then I ran, because the only thing more dangerous than knowing what I know is being the father of a child positioned where she is." His voice caught on the last word and he pushed through it. "When you wake the vaults — whichever way you wake them, however many — she wakes first. Before the families' chosen. Before the protesters. Before your grandmother's grand design resolves into whatever it resolves into. She wakes first, or I take these codes somewhere else, and I promise you, Halloran, there are people who would pay me very badly for them and hurt me very thoroughly after."

Mirabel was quiet for a while. The Verdance hummed around them, hydroponics cycling, water moving through pipes in the walls like a slow heartbeat.

"You understand," he said finally, "that 'she wakes first' is exactly the kind of sentence that started all of this. The whole rot in the loop started with someone saying *my people go in the good line.*"

"I know." Renk didn't flinch. "I've thought about nothing else for two years. And here's what I decided: the crime isn't loving someone enough to spend yourself on them. The crime is building a world where love has to be spent like currency because fairness was never on the menu. I'm not fixing that world tonight. I'm saving my daughter in it. If those are incompatible, then the world was already broken and I'm just the man holding the receipt." He met Mirabel's eyes. "You can hate me for it. I'd respect you more if you did, honestly. But hate me *while* you fly me there."

And the fourth thing — the thing Mirabel could only see through frosted glass — arrived quietly, sometime during the sixth hour, when Renk had finally fallen asleep in the crash webbing with the code case still strapped to his chest, one hand resting on it even in sleep, the way a child holds a blanket.

Mirabel sat alone in the cockpit doing the navigation math and realized what the fourth thing was: he wasn't being recruited. He was being *collected*. His grandmother had left him coordinates instead of instructions, an inheritance trap instead of a letter, and now the pieces were arriving one at a time — the manifest, the archive, the engineer — each one delivered into his hands by coincidence so precise it stopped being coincidence somewhere around the second act. Odile Halloran had been dead for forty years and she was still running his life, and the worst part, the part that made Mirabel laugh out loud in the empty cockpit, startling himself, was that she'd known him well enough to know he'd say yes. To the stranger. To the mission. To all of it. She'd raised him remotely, all instruction and no affection, and apparently instruction was enough. Apparently instruction was the whole recipe.

"You manipulative old woman," he said to the dark beyond the port, conversationally, the way he spoke to the tomatoes. "You couldn't have just written *dear Mirabel, please commit treason, love, Gran.*"

The tomatoes, as always, declined to comment.

* * *

They found the Meridian on the ninth day, exactly where the manifest's drift solution said it would be, hanging in the shadow of a rogue planetesimal like a secret kept in a coat pocket.

Renk stood beside him at the viewport while Mirabel brought the tender in, and neither of them spoke, because there was nothing to say that the ship wasn't already saying. She was beautiful the way old promises are beautiful — unweathered, unfaded, waiting so patiently it hurt. Her hull bore the launch crest of a program that had never launched. Her running lights were off. But as Mirabel closed the last kilometer, the docking beacon woke and began to pulse, slow and green and unmistakably deliberate, and Renk made a sound low in his throat.

"She sees us," he whispered. "Forty years asleep and she sees us."

"Grandmother's automation," Mirabel said. "Or grandmother's ghost. At this point I've stopped distinguishing."

The airlock accepted his hand — his palm, specifically, reading something in the whorls and heat signature that made the inner door cycle open without a code, and Renk watched this happen with an expression Mirabel chose not to examine too closely. Inside, the Meridian smelled of clean plastic and forty years of nothing, and their footsteps rang in corridors built for hundreds and occupied by two.

The cradle bay was aft, past the common quarters, past the mess hall with its tables still bolted down and its galley still stocked with food that had become geology. Mirabel counted cradles as they passed. Two hundred and six of them, ranked in rows, each with a nameplate and a status glyph glowing soft amber. Sleeping. All of them sleeping. He made himself look at the nameplates as he walked — Okafor, Lindqvist, Adeyemi, Brandt, Sousa, Chen — ordinary names, human names, names that belonged to people who had once argued about weather and children and the price of fuel, and something in his chest tightened with every row.

Then the last row. Two cradles, set slightly apart from the others, the way important things are always set slightly apart.

The first held a girl. Small for fourteen, or maybe not small, maybe just young in the particular way that makes size irrelevant — dark hair floating in the cradle's suspension field, one hand curled near her chin, an expression on her sleeping face that Mirabel recognized with a jolt because he wore it himself every morning: the expression of someone mid-dream about being late. NELL RENK, said the plate. STATUS: STABLE. PRIORITY FLAG: RENK, I. — CONDITIONAL.

The second cradle was empty.

It was identical to the other two hundred and seven — same frame, same suspension field humming gently around nothing, same amber status light — except for the nameplate, which was new, which was bright, which was engraved in the same precise machine lettering as every other plate on the ship:

HALLORAN, M.

STATUS: AWAITING OCCUPANT.

Beside him, Renk exhaled slowly through his teeth. "I wondered," he murmured. "When I heard your name on the manifest. I wondered if you knew what the manifest was *for*."

"I thought it was a passenger list," Mirabel said. His voice sounded far away to him, like it was coming from the other end of the corridor. "Passenger lists tell you who's on the ship."

"This one tells the ship who's on the ship," Renk said softly. "There's a difference, and your grandmother spent her whole career making sure nobody noticed the difference until it mattered."

Mirabel stood in front of the empty cradle for a long time. The suspension field hummed. The amber light pulsed, patient as a held breath. Somewhere in the ship's deep systems, he understood suddenly, completely, the way you understand a fall only after it starts — somewhere in those systems, a process was waiting on his biometrics, keyed to a Halloran, holding open a door that had been holding itself open for forty years.

The comm panel beside the cradle blinked. Once. Twice. Then it spoke, in a voice Mirabel had heard only in recordings, dry and clipped and infuriatingly composed, a voice forty years dead:

*"Hello, Mirabel. Take your time. But not too much."*

Behind him, Ivo Renk put a hand briefly on his shoulder — the first unprompted kindness anyone had shown him since Sefa — and said, "So. Which door do we open first?"

The amber light pulsed. The ship waited. And Mirabel Halloran, keeper of gardens, pilot of supply runs, heir to a conspiracy, reached out and touched the nameplate that bore his own name, and felt it warm beneath his fingers like something freshly alive.
