## 9. What the Garden Is For

The Verdance turned slowly beneath him, a green eye in a black socket, and Mirabel Halloran watched it through the observation blister with the particular attention of a man who has just realized he has been lied to by something he loves.

He had come up to the blister for the morning survey, the same ritual he'd performed eleven thousand times or thereabouts. Coffee bulb in one hand. Tablet in the other. The garden spread out below in its great spiral terraces — wheat gold on the outer rings, the orchard rows dark and orderly, the wetlands steaming where the misters cycled at dawn. It was beautiful. He had thought so every single day for nineteen years, and he had never once, until this moment, asked himself the question that now sat in his chest like a swallowed stone:

*Why does a supply pilot need a garden this size?*

* * *

The memory came the way memories do when you're tired — uninvited, vivid, smelling faintly of soil.

He was twenty-two, newly certified, standing in the Verdance's first module before it was anything but a steel drum full of imported dirt. Odile's voice came through the comm panel, thin and precise as always, relayed from wherever she lived inside her own schedule.

*"The seed order is wrong. You've put the barley before the mycorrhizae."*

"It's barley, Grandmother. It grows in gravel."

*"Nothing grows in gravel. Things survive in gravel. There is a difference, and the difference is the entire point of your job."*

He'd rolled his eyes at the empty module, twenty-two and immortal, and re-ordered the crates. He hadn't understood then why she cared so much about the difference between growing and surviving. He wasn't sure he understood it now. But he was beginning to suspect that she had been looking past him the whole time — past his shoulder, down the long decades, at someone who hadn't been born yet.

* * *

Back in the present, he pulled the garden's health reports, because that was what you did when you were worried: you looked for numbers to comfort you.

The reports were fine. That was the first thing wrong with them.

Mirabel had written enough of these reports himself to know their grammar. The Verdance reported to the settlement authority on Relay Nine, which reported to the family consortium, which reported to no one, because there was no one above the families except the dead. And somewhere in that chain of custody, the reports had developed the smooth, reassuring texture of documents written by people who had already decided what they wanted to be true.

Nitrogen fixation: nominal. He stood in the soybean terrace an hour later and dug his hands into the soil, and the soil smelled *tired*. Not sick — tired. Like bread dough that hadn't risen. The fungal networks that Odile had made him lay down crate by crate, season by season, were thinning. He could feel it in the crumble of it, the way it held together less than it used to, the way the earthworms had gone scarce and shy.

Canopy density: stable. Except he walked the orchard rows and counted gaps. Forty-one trees missing in the east block, logged as "rotational removal," which was a real practice, except rotation meant replanting, and the replanting logs stopped fourteen months ago. Fourteen months ago was, he noted with a cold feeling in his stomach, roughly when the transmission loop's decay had started accelerating. Roughly when someone, somewhere, had decided the future was a line item that could wait.

Water reclamation: ninety-four percent efficiency. The number was true. What it didn't say was that efficiency was the wrong metric. Ninety-four percent of what? Of a system designed for two hundred people. The Verdance had been built — he checked the original specifications now, really read them for the first time, instead of skimming them the way you skim instructions from a grandmother who never asked how you felt — the Verdance had been built to support four thousand.

Four thousand waking mouths. Four thousand bodies coming out of cryosleep with muscles like wet paper and immune systems that had forgotten what dirt was. Four thousand people who would need this garden not to be beautiful but to be *enough*, in their first year, while they learned again how to eat food that hadn't been printed.

It was not decoration. It had never been decoration. It was a lifeboat's hull, disguised as a park, and he had spent nineteen years polishing the railings.

* * *

Another memory, unbidden. This one warmer, which somehow made it worse.

His thirtieth birthday. He'd spent it alone in the Verdance, as he spent everything alone, seeding the rice paddies on terrace six. And Sefa had patched herself through from Relay Nine without being asked, her voice tinny through the garden speakers, and said, "You know what today is, right?"

"Tuesday."

"Mirabel."

"Planting Tuesday. Very important agricultural holiday."

"I put a candle in a nutrient bar and I'm going to eat it in your honor. This is the loneliest birthday party in human history and I want you to appreciate that I'm attending it."

"You're attending it from forty thousand kilometers away."

"Love travels at the speed of light, Halloran. That's practically intimate."

He had laughed, actually laughed, mud to his elbows, and something in him had thought: *this counts. Someone remembered.* And then, months later, he'd found the paddy yield report and noticed that terrace six had been flagged in the specs as a calibration terrace — its yields were meant to be benchmarked against the others, a control group, so that when the woken arrived, the agronomists among them would have honest data about what this soil could do.

A control group. Even his birthday rice had been part of the proof.

And yet — and this was the thing he kept snagging on, the splinter in the thought — she had *designed* it that way. Odile. Every warm thing in his life, examined closely, turned out to have a function stamped on its underside. The garden. The messages. The manifest with his name on it. She had built him a childhood out of assignments and called it love, and maybe it was love, the only kind she knew how to manufacture, but standing here among the tired soil he couldn't tell anymore whether he'd been raised or *grown*.

* * *

He spent three days doing what he did best, which was work nobody had asked him to do carefully.

He ran the surveys himself, by hand, sensor by sensor, and compared them against what got transmitted to Relay Nine. The divergence was small at first glance and enormous once you knew how to read it. Soil biomass: reported down two percent, actual down nine. Pollinator colony strength: reported stable, actual down by a third since the last true audit. The seed vault's genetic diversity index — the number that mattered most, the number that determined whether four thousand people could eat in year three, after the first harvests failed the way first harvests do — reported at 0.91. Actual: 0.74.

Below 0.70, the old manuals said, a closed ecosystem entered decline it could not reverse without outside genetic input. And there was no outside. There was Earth, forty years silent, and there was nothing.

Someone knew. The reports didn't lie by accident; they lied by *editing*, and editing took hands. Which meant somewhere in the chain between his sensors and the consortium's comfortable screens, a person — or a policy — had decided that the garden's decline was information the families didn't need to act on. Or worse: information they needed *not* to act on, because acting on it would mean admitting the timeline, admitting the loop was decaying, admitting that the window for choosing which half of humanity woke up was closing faster than anyone official wanted to say aloud.

The garden wasn't failing because it was neglected. The garden was failing because it was *scheduled to fail* — quietly, deniably, after the decision had been made, so that whichever vault opened would inherit a world too fragile to argue with its makers.

He sat down in the dirt of terrace six, right in the middle of his birthday rice, and let that settle over him.

They had even planned the famine. Probably. Somewhere in a ledger he hadn't found yet, there was likely a projection showing exactly how many of the woken the diminished garden could carry, and it was probably a very tidy number, and it probably matched some other tidy number in a ledger about vault populations.

"You absolute bastards," he told the soybeans, quietly. They didn't disagree.

* * *

That evening he recorded his daily message, the ritual he'd kept for nineteen years, the messages no one answered. But tonight he pointed the recorder not at the settlements' general relay but at the loop itself — at the dying archive of Earth's final years, the static that Sefa swore was eating the last voices of the dead world.

"This is Mirabel Halloran, tending the Verdance. Day six thousand nine hundred and—" he paused, did the math, hated the math "—four. Report follows."

He gave the real numbers. All of them. The nine percent, the third of the pollinators, the 0.74. He spoke them plainly into the static, knowing the loop would degrade them, knowing that maybe no one would ever hear them, and finding that he didn't care. If the loop was going to die anyway, it could die carrying the truth for once.

Then he sat with the recorder a moment longer, and said the part he hadn't planned.

"You knew, didn't you. You knew what this place was for, and you knew they'd let it rot the moment it stopped being convenient, and you left it to me anyway. Twenty-two years old and a seed order. That was my inheritance. Dirt and arithmetic."

The static said nothing, in the way static says nothing, which is to say it said everything.

"But I'm still here," he said. "So I guess that's the answer you wanted."

* * *

He slept badly and woke at shipboard dawn with the answer sitting fully formed in his head, the way answers do after a night of the mind chewing on its own tail.

The garden was proof-of-concept. Fine. Understood. But proof-of-concept cut both ways: if the Verdance failed, the case for waking *anyone* failed. A dying garden was not just a tragedy; it was ammunition. Every point of decline in that diversity index was a argument Hale's authority could use — *the colonies can't sustain the woken, the risk is unacceptable, better to keep the vaults sealed until conditions improve,* and conditions would never improve, because the same people defunding the garden were writing the reports saying it was fine.

Which meant the garden wasn't just his responsibility. It was the *evidence*. If he could stabilize it — really stabilize it, honestly, with the numbers broadcast wide — then the claim that humanity couldn't afford to wake both halves of itself collapsed. The law said choose one vault. The land said there was room for everyone, if someone fought for the land.

He was out in the east orchard before first shift, grafting stock from the seed vault's oldest lines onto the rootstocks of the missing trees, working fast and dirty, when the garden's external array pinged him with an incoming transmission.

Not Sefa's usual channel. Not the authority's encrypted band either.

An open frequency. Unregistered origin. And threaded through the carrier wave, unmistakable even before he decrypted it, the sound of engines running hot and someone breathing hard, and then a voice he didn't know, ragged and urgent:

"—Halloran, if you're receiving, I don't have much time. My name is Renk. I worked the loop. They're coming for me, and I have something you need, and your grandmother told me—"

The transmission dissolved into static.

Mirabel stood in the orchard with grafting wax on his fingers, heart hammering, staring at the receiver.

*Your grandmother told me.*

He keyed the channel open. "Renk. Say again. Renk, I'm here—"

Static. Then, faintly, from somewhere off his port bow, a distress beacon began to burn its way across the dark.
