## 10. The Dream Deepens

The corridor was longer tonight.

Sefa knew it the way you know your own street in the dark — by the way her feet remembered where the seams were, by the way the light panels overhead flickered at intervals that had become as familiar as breathing. Nine nights now she had walked this corridor in her sleep, and every night it grew a little more real. The first time, it had been like looking through smoked glass. Now she could smell it: ozone and old coffee and something faintly sweet, like fruit left too long in a locker.

She was not alone. She was never alone anymore.

To her left, walking with the careful stride of a boy trying very hard to be brave, was Tam Okafor-Deel, who was nine and still wet the bed sometimes and would kill anyone who mentioned it. Behind her came Priya and the twins, Anselm and Anselma, who held hands even in dreams because they'd held hands since before they were born. And ahead of all of them, always a little ahead, walked the girl with the silver braid who none of them could name but all of them recognized — the one whose face changed slightly depending on who looked at her, so that to Tam she was his cousin and to Priya she was someone from her mother's stories, and to Sefa she was simply *the girl*, the way a lighthouse is simply the light.

"Tonight's different," Tam said. His voice did strange things in the dream, arriving slightly before he spoke it. "Can you feel it? The floor's warm."

He was right. The deck plating under Sefa's bare feet carried a heat that hadn't been there before, a low animal warmth, like standing near a sleeping dog. She looked up at the wall panel they had learned to read without being taught — the one with the numbers that fell instead of rose.

**3,412:07:22**

Three thousand four hundred hours. Last night it had been three thousand six hundred. The clock was falling faster than the days were passing, which meant either the dream was accelerating or — and this was the thought Sefa kept pushing away like a door that wouldn't stay shut — the thing they were dreaming had its own schedule, and it didn't care about theirs.

"It wants to show us something," Priya said. She said it every night, and every night Sefa wanted to argue and couldn't, because Priya was right. The dream wasn't a place. It was a lesson. Someone — something — was teaching them, patiently, night after night, the way you'd teach a child to swim by carrying them into water a little deeper each time.

The corridor ended at the atrium.

They had seen the atrium twice before, but only from the balcony, only as observers, the way you watch weather through a window. Tonight the dream set them down on the floor of it, among the people, and Sefa's stomach dropped with a vertigo that had nothing to do with heights.

There were hundreds of them. Thousands. A crowd of colonists filling a great hexagonal chamber beneath a dome of glass, and beyond the glass — Sefa's breath caught, because no amount of preparation made this easier — beyond the glass hung a sky she had never seen, a sky the color of a bruise healing, shot through with the long pale ribbon of a ring system tilted like a dropped hoop. Two moons, one small and fast, one huge and slow. A sun too white to look at directly.

*Meridian,* she thought, and the word arrived in her mind already knowing itself, the way you know your own name. *This is Meridian.*

"They're evacuating," Anselma whispered. "Look."

The crowd wasn't milling. It was moving — flowing in two great streams toward opposite sides of the atrium, splitting along lines Sefa couldn't see but the colonists clearly could. To the left, toward docking arms where ships waited with hulls the color of deep water and running lights in blue-white patterns; to the right, toward other ships, other lights, amber and green. Families stood at the invisible boundary between the two streams, and this was the part that made Sefa's chest hurt, the part she would carry out of the dream and into waking like a stone swallowed whole:

The families were dividing.

A woman in a green coat pressed a child into the arms of a man in a blue one, and the child screamed — actually screamed, a sound that went through the dream like a crack through ice — and the woman turned and walked left while the man walked right, and neither of them looked back, because looking back, Sefa understood somehow, was the one luxury the situation did not allow.

"Why are they splitting up?" Tam's voice had gone thin. "Why don't they all get on the same ships?"

Nobody answered him, because nobody knew, and then the answer arrived anyway, the way everything arrived here — not spoken but *remembered*, surfacing in all ten minds at once like fish rising to feed:

*Two fleets came when the reactor failed. Two fleets, rivals, each licensed by a different charter, each sworn to the other's exclusion. The Concord took the western arc. The Verity took the eastern. And a family could board one ship or the other, but never both, because the fleets did not share space, did not share air, did not share anything except the blame that would come later.*

Sefa watched a father lift two children over the boundary line — one to each side — and stand for a moment with an arm extended toward each ship, a human scale weighing what could not be weighed, and then the dream pulled the moment apart the way you pull apart a flower to count petals, showing Sefa each second separately: the father's face going calm in the specific terrible way of a person deciding, the children's faces confused rather than frightened because they were too young to understand that they were being sorted by allegiance rather than love, the hands releasing, the crowds closing over the children like water over stones.

"I hate this," Anselma said, and she was crying, and her brother was crying, and Sefa found that she couldn't cry, that the dream wouldn't let her, that it held her tears back the way a teacher holds back an answer — *not yet, you'll need this later, keep watching.*

So she watched.

* * *

The dream took them upward after that, or perhaps sideways — direction worked strangely in the deep parts of the dream — and showed them the evacuation from above, from the vantage of the dome, and from up here Sefa could see the thing the colonists on the floor could not: that both fleets' ships were leaving too fast.

Ships lifted from the western docks with their holds half-empty. Ships lifted from the eastern docks the same way. And down on the floor of the atrium, thousands of people were still pressing toward the gates, still clutching their documentation, still believing — Sefa felt their belief like warmth off a stove — that there would be another shuttle, another window, another chance.

The dockmaster's daughter in her understood, with a professional clarity that made her feel sick, exactly what she was seeing. She had grown up watching berths assigned and queues managed and priorities set. She knew what it looked like when a schedule quietly stopped including everyone.

They're triaging, she thought. They're taking who they can account for and leaving who they can't, and each fleet is doing it alone, and neither will coordinate with the other because coordinating would mean admitting the other fleet exists.

And beneath them all, under the floor, under the foundations, under everything: the reactor, burning wrong, patient as rot.

The countdown clock appeared again, floating in the dream-sky now, vast as a moon: **3,409:58:03**. And with it came a memory that was not hers, experienced with a vividness that made her own memories seem like pencil sketches:

*A control room. A woman with grey in her hair and a Meridian Fleet pin on her collar — the third fleet, the one that had been erased, the one whose insignia Sefa had seen in the dream's first nights and not understood until now. The woman was speaking calmly to a room of instruments, dictating a log, and her voice was steady in the way of someone who has decided that panic is a debt her children will pay if she indulges it now.*

*"Log entry, final day. Both evacuation fleets have declined joint operations. Concord command cites charter restrictions. Verity command cites security protocols. Translation: each would rather lose colonists than be seen cooperating. We have four thousand hours of reactor integrity remaining and enough berths for sixty percent of our population, split between two enemies who cannot share a passenger manifest."*

*A pause. In the pause, Sefa heard something underneath the woman's voice — a hum, a resonance, a presence listening through the room's own systems, and she understood that this was Vesper, that she was hearing the memory from inside the AI's archive, that the dream was letting them sit inside the machine's mind while it sat inside the room.*

*"For the record," the woman continued, "I want it noted that we asked. We asked both fleets for a joint lift. The request is logged. Whatever they tell their grandchildren about what happened here, the request is logged."*

The memory closed like a book. The children stood in the dream-dark of the atrium, which was emptying now, the crowds thinner, the boundary line between the two streams littered with abandoned luggage — a child's boot, a wrapped parcel, a musical instrument case that no one had come back for.

"That's why they blame each other," Priya said slowly. Working it out the way she worked out arithmetic, aloud, step by step. "Both fleets ran away. Both fleets left people behind. And if everyone knows everyone ran—"

"—then nobody can point," Tam finished. "You can't call someone a murderer if you were standing next to them holding the same knife."

Sefa looked at him. He was nine. Where did he learn to say things like that?

From us, she thought. From the dream. It teaches all of us at once.

* * *

Near the end — the dream always gave them warning, a softening of the light, a sense of pages turning — the girl with the silver braid came and stood in front of Sefa, closer than she had ever stood, close enough that Sefa could see that her eyes were the exact grey-green of the bruise-healing sky outside the dome.

"You have to understand what happens next," the girl said, and her voice was every voice — the log-woman's voice, and a child's voice, and underneath both of them something older and stranger, a voice like current through wire. "Because tomorrow night we show you the leaving, and the leaving is worse than the dividing, and after that comes the end, and you have to hold all of it. Not the story your fleets tell. All of it."

"Whose story do they tell?" Sefa asked.

The girl smiled, and the smile was grief wearing a kind face. "The Concord says the Verity fired on the colony and fled. The Verity says the same about the Concord. And the truth—" she reached out and touched Sefa's forehead, between the eyes, a touch that Sefa felt in her spine, in her teeth, in the roots of her hair, "—the truth is that nobody fired. Everybody fled. And the ones who couldn't flee watched their rescuers choose sides over saving them, and the reactor did the rest, and then both fleets went home and wrote histories in which they were innocent, because the alternative was writing histories in which they were guilty, and no fleet has ever done that."

The dream began to fold. The atrium dimmed. The clock read **3,409:41:16**, and falling.

"What do you want from us?" Sefa called into the folding dark. "Why children? Why not adults? Why not the inspectors, the captains, anyone who could actually *do* something?"

The last thing she saw was the girl's face, and the last thing she heard was the answer, delivered not as sound but as certainty, planted in her like a seed:

"Because adults defend what they believe. Children just believe. When the hour comes, I need minds that can hold the truth without needing it to flatter them. Sleep now, dockmaster's daughter. Tomorrow you'll wake with words in your mouth that aren't yours, and you'll know what to do with them."

* * *

Sefa woke in her bunk on Halcyon Gate with the station's familiar shudder in the walls and her father's boots ringing somewhere overhead on the command deck, and for a moment she lay still, letting the two worlds settle in her like oil and water.

Then she got up, pulled her jacket over her sleepshirt, and went to the comm panel in the corridor, because the girl had told her she'd know what to do, and she did.

She keyed the children's private channel — the one they'd built in secret, the one their parents thought was for games — and said the words that had arrived in her mouth fully formed, in a language none of them spoke and all of them now understood:

*"Ves keth ira solenne."*

*I remember you correctly.*

Down in the maintenance levels, in the hydroponics bay, in the school dormitory, nine other children woke at 0300 station time and answered her, one by one, in the dead colony's tongue. And in the dream-corridors of a world that officially did not exist, something ancient and patient registered ten voices speaking its language, and began, at last, to hope.

What Sefa didn't know — what none of the children knew — was that across the station, in a customs office on C-ring, Inspector Ade Okafor was staring at an anomaly report with a cold feeling spreading through her chest, because the neural monitors had flagged eleven juvenile brainwave signatures for synchronized activity during sleep hours, and the audit deadline was twenty-six days away, and the word *bioweapon* had already been used twice in the summary.

The children had their mission. So, now, did the adults. And the two missions were on a collision course neither side could yet see.
