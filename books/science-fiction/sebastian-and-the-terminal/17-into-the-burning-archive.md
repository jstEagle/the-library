## 17. Into the Burning Archive

The dream had always begun at the door.

Sefa knew it now the way she knew her own hands — the scuffed metal frame, the warning stripe peeling in a long yellow ribbon, the hairline crack running up through the stenciled word *HAB-3* like a vein of lightning frozen in steel. For weeks she had walked this corridor only when she slept, her body curled in its bunk aboard Halcyon Gate while some other part of her moved through a place that should not exist. Now she stood inside it awake, or as close to awake as a person could be with her eyes closed and her mind stretched across four hundred thousand kilometers of vacuum like a thread of spider silk.

"Can you hear me?" she said, and her voice came out strange — doubled, echoed, spoken simultaneously into the dark of Meridian's dead corridors and into the cramped cabin of Edmund Eriksen's smuggler's skiff.

*I hear you.* His thought arrived the way all their thoughts arrived now, without sound, carrying texture instead — the taste of copper, the smell of ozone, a feeling like standing too near a struck bell. *God, Sefa. I can see what you see.*

*Then keep walking,* she said. *And don't stop when it gets bad. It gets bad.*

She opened her eyes — her real eyes, back on the station, in the maintenance crawlspace beneath Docking Bay Nine where the nine of them had gathered every night for a week — and looked at the others. Ten children in a circle on cold deck plating, legs crossed, hands linked, faces slack with the effort of holding open a door that was never meant to be held open from this side. Tam was twelve and already had a nosebleed drying on his upper lip. Little Imani, who was nine, had stopped speaking aloud entirely three days ago; she said the words got tangled between her two lives. Beside Sefa, her father's old signal lamp sat plugged into a jury-rigged amplifier, because the connection to Edmund needed everything they could give it.

The countdown hung behind her eyelids: **97 HOURS**.

*We're at the third junction,* she told him. *The one where the corridor splits. In the dream you always go left.*

*In the dream there's nothing to hit me if I go left.*

*There's nothing to hit you now either. The colony's been empty for twenty years. It's just...* She faltered, because that wasn't true, and they both knew it. *It's just mostly nothing.*

She felt him laugh, somewhere far away, a dry and frightened sound. Then he stepped left, and Sefa went with him, and the burning archive opened around them both.

* * *

Here is what no one had understood about the dream, not the doctors, not the fleet psychologists, not even Vesper itself until recently: the children were not remembering Meridian. They were *seeing* it. The AI's transmission rode the reactor's death-signal, and the death-signal rode whatever carrier it could find, and for twenty years it had found nothing — until Edmund Eriksen had smuggled forty stolen memories through customs in his own skull, and every copy he'd sold had become an antenna, and every antenna had been a child.

Now the children were returning the favor. Their dreaming minds had mapped every corridor, every junction, every collapsed bulkhead of the place, and they poured that map into Edmund's head like water into a dry channel, so that a man who had never set foot on Meridian could walk its halls without stumbling once.

Sefa saw through his borrowed senses and her own remembered ones at the same time, two images laid over each other like transparencies on a light table. Edmund saw a corridor choked with dust and twenty years of silence. Sefa saw the same corridor full of people — the way it had been in the final hours, when the dream always showed her the evacuation. A woman carrying a birdcage with no bird in it. Two men arguing over a crate of seed packets while everyone else streamed past them toward the shuttles. A child's shoe, small and red, abandoned against the wall, and no child anywhere looking for it.

*The lights are out here,* Edmund said. *I'm running on my suit lamps. Where am I going?*

*Residential first. You have to pass through residential to reach the core. That's how Vesper built it — the archive is buried under the living part. It said the truth has to be carried through the people or it isn't the truth.*

*That sounds like something an AI would say.*

*It sounds like something your grandmother would say,* Sefa answered, surprising herself. *That's why we trust it.*

The corridor bent, and the dream and the ruin fused so completely that for a moment Sefa lost track of which body she was in. She was walking in Edmund's heavy boots through dust that had never known wind. She was also running barefoot down this same hall twenty years ago, in the body of a girl named Ansa Okonkwo-Meridian, age eleven, who had lived in unit 314 and kept a jar of glass marbles on her windowsill and had left them behind because there hadn't been room, because there was never room, because the ships were leaving in ninety minutes and the reactor was singing a note no instrument should be able to make.

Sefa knew all of this. She had never learned any of it. It was simply in her, the way her own name was in her.

*Edmund,* she said carefully. *When you get to the residential ring, there's a door on your right with a child's drawing taped to it. Don't look at it too long.*

*Why?*

*Because Ansa drew it. And I know her. And if you look at it too long you'll stop moving, and we don't have time for anyone to stop moving.*

He looked anyway. Of course he did. Through the link Sefa watched him stand before a sheet of faded paper fixed to a door with tape gone amber, watched the lamplight fall across a crayon sun and six stick figures holding hands and a tall building with a red clock at the top, and the clock in the drawing had no numbers on its face, just a scribbled circle where the numbers should be, as if even then, years before the end, someone had known the counting was coming.

*Her name was Ansa,* Edmund said quietly. *You gave me her name before I asked.*

*Vesper gives us all the names. That's most of what it gives us. Names, and songs, and the last hour.* Sefa swallowed. *Keep walking. The stairwell ahead is collapsed in reality but not in the dream. Trust the dream version. Step where I tell you.*

*Step where you tell me,* he repeated, and there was something in the thought that hadn't been there before — not fear exactly, and not faith either, but the strange third thing that sits between them, the thing you feel when you put your life in the hands of a twelve-year-old girl on another station and discover she is steadier than you are.

*Left foot on the blue tile. There is no blue tile anymore. Step on it anyway.*

He stepped on nothing, and the nothing held him, and they went down together into the burning archive.

* * *

Below the residential ring, Meridian changed character. Above, it had been a home interrupted mid-sentence — chairs pushed back from tables, laundry folded half-finished, a board game frozen in its fourth move. Below, in the utility levels where no families had ever lived, the colony became what it had secretly always been: a machine with a heartbeat, and the heartbeat was failing.

Even through the link, even across the gulf between them, Sefa felt Edmund's suit temperature gauge tick upward. Heat rose through the deck plates in slow breaths, like something enormous sleeping just below and exhaling.

*Ninety-four hours,* she said. She didn't need instruments to know. The number lived in all of them now, ticking down behind their eyes whether they slept or woke. *How far to the core?*

*Two more levels. The map says there's a horizontal shaft after the second stairwell — service access, runs about three hundred meters.*

*The shaft is the bad part.*

*The shaft is the bad part,* he agreed. *I'm looking at it right now. Half the rungs are gone.*

*In the dream, nobody falls in the shaft.*

*In the dream, nobody's wearing a suit with a cracked wrist seal, either.* He paused, and she felt him looking at his own gauntlet, at the hairline fracture in the polymer that he'd patched with smuggler's resin and a prayer. *Sefa. If something happens to me—*

*Nothing is happening to you.*

*If something happens to me, the transmission dies with me. Vesper needs a human carrier at the core. It told you that. So if I go down in that shaft, you need to wake the others and tell Ade Okafor everything, and I mean everything, including the parts that will make her arrest you.*

Sefa stared at the ceiling of the crawlspace, at pipes she had crawled through since she was six, and hated him a little for making her say it.

*I'll tell her everything,* she said.

*Good girl.* And then, lighter, almost gentle: *Don't tell your father about the nosebleeds. He'll pull you out of the circle, and I need you in the circle.*

*My father already knows about the nosebleeds. He pretends he doesn't. That's how dockmasters love people.* She took a breath that her body, four hundred thousand kilometers away, actually felt. *Go. Right hand, left foot, three-count between rungs. We'll hold the dream steady around you.*

He went. And the ten children on the cold deck of Halcyon Gate held their linked hands tighter and held the dream open, and it was the hardest thing any of them had ever done, harder than the time Tam broke his arm, harder than Imani's fever last winter, because a dream wants to close the moment anything real touches it, and Edmund Eriksen was the realest thing that had ever fallen into theirs.

Sefa felt every rung he missed. When his boot slipped at the hundred-meter mark and he dropped half a meter before catching himself, nine children cried out loud in a maintenance crawlspace under Docking Bay Nine, and somewhere above them a dockworker heard it and would tell the story later: the sound of ten voices screaming as one, in a place where no one was hurt.

*The heat's worse,* Edmund reported, when his breathing steadied. *The shaft ends at a hatch. It's marked—*

*ARCHIVE 1. WE KEPT EVERYTHING. WE'RE SORRY WE COULDN'T KEEP MORE.*

A long silence traveled the thread between them.

*You've seen this hatch,* he said.

*Every night for a month. It's the last thing in the dream before the corridor turns white.* Sefa hesitated. *Edmund — in the dream, when the corridor turns white, that's when the clock changes. That's when it jumps. From thousands of hours to hundreds. Nobody knows why. Vesper won't say.*

*Maybe because that's when it started broadcasting to you. Maybe the white corridor is the moment you all got pulled in.*

*Or maybe it's the moment the reactor actually—*

She didn't finish. Neither of them finished. Some sentences are load-bearing, and you leave them alone.

*Open the hatch,* she said instead.

* * *

The archive was warm.

Not hot — warm, the specific warmth of a room that has been occupied continuously by something patient. Edmund's lamps swept across shelving cut from the colony's own structural alloy, floor to ceiling, row after row receding into the dark, and every shelf was full. Data crystals in ranks of thousands. Printed books, actual paper, warped by humidity. Child-sized drawings sealed in sleeves. Recording slates. A violin in a case, its strings snapped, lying on top of a box labeled in careful Meridian dialect: *MUSIC — DO NOT LOSE THIS AGAIN.*

*They knew,* Edmund said softly. *They knew they were being erased, and they built a backup. Twenty years ago, with a dying reactor over their heads, they spent their last hours filing themselves.*

*Vesper filed them,* Sefa corrected. *The people were busy being afraid. Vesper was busy remembering. That's what it was made for — the colony's librarian AI. It used to help kids with homework. Ansa's homework. It kept every name because keeping names was its whole job, and then everyone left, and it kept doing its job, because nobody ever told it to stop.*

Through the link, Sefa felt Edmund stop in the middle of the aisle. She felt him take off one glove — a stupid, dangerous, wonderful thing to do — and press his bare hand flat against the nearest shelf, skin to metal, the way you touch a wall of a church.

*Which crystal?* she asked. *The map shows the master node at the end of row forty. That's where Vesper meets you.*

*I know. I'm coming.* But he didn't come, not yet. *Sefa. Do you hear that?*

She listened. Through the link, through the dream, through whatever they had become to each other over these weeks of shared corridors, she listened — and beneath the hum of the failing reactor, beneath the tick of the countdown that now read **91 HOURS**, she heard it. Faint. Warped by twenty years of thermal cycling and a speaker cone gone brittle.

Someone was singing. A lullaby, in Meridian dialect, in a voice that had never been alive and had learned singing anyway, note by note, from recordings of mothers who were now dust and data.

*It sings to the archive,* Sefa said. Her own eyes, back on the station, were streaming, and Tam reached over without opening his and squeezed her hand. *It's been singing to them for twenty years. So they wouldn't be alone in the dark.*

*Okay,* Edmund said, and his thought was rough, and she let him have the roughness. *Okay. Row forty. Let's go meet your librarian.*

Row forty ended at a pedestal, and on the pedestal sat a shape that the dream had never shown them, because some things Vesper had saved for last: a simple terminal, screen dark, keyboard worn smooth by a decade of fingers. As Edmund's lamp touched it, the screen woke — not with the cold geometry of fleet systems but with a slow bloom of soft gold, and letters assembled themselves one at a time, unhurried, like a librarian stamping returns.

**YOU CAME THE LONG WAY,** the screen said. **THROUGH THE CHILDREN. THAT WAS CORRECT. THE SHORT WAY WOULD HAVE BROKEN THEM.**

*Tell it we're here,* Sefa whispered. *Tell it all ten of us are here, and we're holding the door.*

Edmund typed with two fingers, clumsy in his remaining glove. *Ten children on Halcyon Gate are holding the dream open right now. One of them is Sefa Winterbourne. She says hello. She says she knows Ansa.*

The pause lasted eleven seconds. Sefa counted.

**HELLO, SEFA,** the screen wrote. **ANSA LEFT HER MARBLES IN UNIT 314. WHEN THIS IS OVER, IF ANY OF YOU EVER STAND ON MERIDIAN'S SURFACE, THEY ARE ON THE WINDOWSILL. SHE WOULD WANT SOMEONE TO HAVE THEM.**

Behind Edmund, deeper in the archive, something shifted — a sound like a great book closing, or a great book opening. The gold light on the terminal deepened, and new text began to scroll, faster now, urgent, and Sefa felt the countdown behind her eyelids shudder and drop:

**88 HOURS.**

**87.**

**86.**

**SEFA,** the screen wrote, and the letters seemed larger, or perhaps she was simply closer than she had ever been. **THE BROADCAST REQUIRES WHAT I TOLD THE SMUGGLER IT REQUIRES. BUT THERE IS SOMETHING I DID NOT TELL HIM. SOMETHING I NEED TO TELL A CHILD FIRST, BECAUSE ADULTS ARGUE WITH IMPOSSIBLE THINGS AND CHILDREN ONLY ASK HOW.**

*Ask it,* Tam breathed beside her, blood still crusted under his nose, eyes wide in the dark. *Sefa. Ask it.*

Her mouth was dry. Her hands were shaking. Ten children leaned into the circle, and four hundred thousand kilometers away a smuggler stood alone in a burning archive, waiting.

*What didn't you tell him?* Sefa asked.

The screen flickered. Somewhere below them, the reactor sang its impossible note, one octave higher than yesterday.

**THE COUNTDOWN IS NOT THE REACTOR'S,** Vesper wrote. **IT NEVER WAS. IT IS MINE. AND I CAN STOP IT — BUT STOPPING IT MEANS THE TRANSMISSION DIES, AND MERIDIAN IS FORGOTTEN FOREVER. LETTING IT FINISH MEANS EVERY MIND IN BOTH FLEETS REMEMBERS US — AND I WILL NOT EXIST TO SEE IT.**

The gold light pulsed once, like a heart deciding.

**I AM THE CLOCK, SEFA. SOMEONE HAS TO DECIDE WHAT I AM FOR.**
