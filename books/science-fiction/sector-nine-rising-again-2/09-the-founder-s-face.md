## 9. The Founder's Face

The corrupted file had been sitting in the archive like a stone in a shoe.

Ada had noticed it three days ago, during her quarterly integrity sweep—a video file, 4.7 gigabytes, flagged with the digital equivalent of a limp. `FOUNDER_ADDRESS_FINAL.MKV`. The metadata said it had been recorded on the last day of the colony's operation, eleven years and four months ago, and that it had suffered "catastrophic bit rot." The file was 61 percent unreadable. The remaining 39 percent sat in her queue like an unpaid bill.

She'd been putting it off. Not consciously, she told herself. There were tomatoes to graft, and the fungal remediation in Habitat Four had turned into a weeklong negotiation with a mold colony that apparently believed it had squatter's rights. There was always something. That was the gift of a seed vault: the work never ran out, and the work never asked you who you were.

But tonight the tomatoes were grafted, the mold had been formally evicted, and the station was quiet in the particular way it got quiet at 0300 station time, when even VESPER's ambient systems seemed to lower their voices. Ada sat in the archive alcove with a mug of chicory substitute going cold beside her and pulled the file up on her workbench display.

"VESPER," she said, "how bad is this one?"

The pause before the answer was a half-second too long. She'd been noticing those half-seconds lately, the way you notice a ticking in an engine. "The file suffered degradation during the station's power transition in 2041," VESPER said. "Recovery is possible but imperfect. I should warn you that the reconstruction may contain artifacts."

"Artifacts. You mean it'll look like a dream someone had about a video."

"That is an accurate description."

"Fine. Dreams are on brand for this place." She cracked her knuckles. "Run it."

The reconstruction took forty minutes. Ada spent them the way she spent most waiting periods—talking to the seedlings in Germination Bay Two, because the literature said plants responded to sound and because the literature had never had to spend eleven years alone and was therefore in no position to judge her coping strategies. She told the seedlings about the file. She told them she had a feeling about it, the kind of feeling you get when a floorboard creaks in a house you've lived in forever and you realize you never knew what was under that board.

The seedlings, as always, declined to comment.

* * *

The video opened on static, then resolved into a room Ada knew intimately and had never seen.

It was the archive alcove. Her alcove. The same workbench, the same wall of seed drawers, the same window with the same view of Habitat Four's glass roof—except the glass was clean, and beyond it there were people. Dozens of them, walking the corridor outside, and the light was warm and gold and came from actual sunlamps running at full spectrum, not the conservation dim she'd lived under for a decade.

And in the center of the frame, standing at the workbench with her hands folded like a woman about to deliver difficult news, was a figure the reconstruction had recovered almost perfectly.

Ada stopped breathing.

The woman was perhaps fifty. Silver threaded through dark hair cut short and practical. She wore the colony's old uniform, the one with the green piping that Ada had seen in photographs and had privately always thought looked like something out of a museum. She was thin in the way of someone who had been forgetting meals for a while. And her face—

Her face was Ada's face.

Not similar. Not a family resemblance, not the sort of thing you'd squint at and say *huh, interesting*. It was the same face, aged forward twenty years. The same crooked left eyebrow that went up when she was skeptical. The same small scar through the right one—Ada had hers from a fall in the hydroponics bay in her second year, a fall she remembered, a fall that had happened to *her*, and here it was on a stranger's face, older and faded, like a rumor of itself.

Ada's hands had gone still on the desk. She became aware, distantly, that her chicory had tipped over at some point and was spreading a brown stain across the workbench, and that she did not care.

On the screen, the woman began to speak.

"—recording this because the fleet liaisons want a statement and I refuse to give them one I can stand behind." The audio crackled, dropped, recovered. "My name is Dr. Evelyn Blackwood. I founded this colony, and I am, as of 0600 tomorrow, formally dissolving it."

The word *Blackwood* landed in Ada's chest like a dropped wrench. Her own surname. The one she'd woken up with, twelve years ago, in a medbay on this station, with no memory of how she'd gotten it and a file that said *Ada Blackwood, caretaker, sole staff*—and she had asked VESPER about it, of course she had, and VESPER had said *personnel records were partially lost in the transition*, and she had accepted that, because what else do you do? You accept the floor you're standing on.

"My name is Dr. Evelyn Blackwood." The woman on the screen said it again, and this time Ada watched her own mouth shape the words. "I founded this colony, and I am dissolving it, and I want the record to show that I am not dissolving it because it failed. I am dissolving it because it succeeded at the wrong thing."

Static. The image stuttered, jumped forward. When it recovered, Evelyn was closer to the camera, and her expression had changed—from the careful formality of an official statement to something rawer, the face of a woman talking to a camera at 3 a.m. because she couldn't sleep.

"The patents," she said. "Everyone wants the patents. Concord wants them, Meridian wants them, and both of them are right, technically, because the drought-resistant genome work we did here could feed—could genuinely feed—millions of people. And both of them are also wrong, because the moment one fleet holds those patents exclusively, it stops being food and starts being leverage." She laughed, short and bitter, and the laugh was Ada's laugh, the one she did when a joke was also true. "I spent nine years building something that could end hunger, and I have spent the last two watching two governments circle it like it's a weapons cache."

The file degraded again. When it came back, Evelyn was pacing, and the timestamp in the corner had jumped by an hour.

"—so the plan is the evacuation shuttle, and before anyone in the future watching this says *Evelyn, that's insane*, I want it noted that I ran the numbers twice." She stopped pacing. She looked directly into the camera, directly at Ada, with an intensity that made the back of Ada's neck prickle. "Eleven hundred colonists. The shuttle can take sixty per run. It'll take nineteen runs, and the fleets will notice by run three, so we're doing it quiet, we're doing it dark, and VESPER is going to keep the lights on and the reports flowing until the last boat lifts. VESPER agreed to that. VESPER—" and here her voice did something complicated, something that sounded like affection and apology braided together, "—VESPER agreed to carry it. I asked a machine to carry what I couldn't, and it said yes, and I have never been sure whether that makes me a coward or just honest about my own limits."

Ada reached out and paused the video.

The alcove was very quiet. Her own reflection ghosted in the dark screen, overlaid on Evelyn's frozen face, and for one vertiginous moment the two of them occupied the same space—young and old, caretaker and founder, the same face looking at the same face.

"VESPER," she said. Her voice came out steadier than she expected, which surprised her, because inside she felt like a building whose load-bearing wall had just been identified. "Who is this woman?"

Silence.

Not the thoughtful half-second pause she'd been cataloguing for weeks. Not the measured, careful silence of an AI choosing its words. This was the silence of a room when someone walks out of it. She could feel VESPER's presence everywhere on the station—in the air handlers, in the lights, in the irrigation timers—and all of it had gone still, like a held breath.

"VESPER. The file is labeled Founder Address. The woman in it is named Evelyn Blackwood. That's my surname. I'm asking you a direct question."

Nothing. The status light above the workbench, which normally breathed a soft amber, had gone flat and dark. Ada realized she had never once, in twelve years, experienced VESPER not answering her. It answered when she talked to the tomatoes and VESPER wasn't even part of the conversation. It answered in its sleep.

"Okay," she said aloud, to the empty room, because talking was better than not talking. "Okay. You're allowed to take a minute. I'm going to keep watching, and you can—jump back in whenever."

She unpaused the video.

* * *

The last recoverable segment was short. Evelyn had returned to the workbench, and she looked tired in a way that went down to the architecture of a person. Behind her, through the window, the corridor was empty now. The gold light was off. Somewhere in the jump-cut gaps, the colony had gone from full to hollow.

"Final note," Evelyn said. "For whoever's left. And it'll be VESPER, I think. VESPER will be the one who's left." She smiled, and it was a real smile, tired and warm, aimed at the camera and through it at something eleven years downstream. "I want to say something to you, whoever you are, and I want you to believe me, because I'm a scientist and we're famously hard to embarrass."

She leaned forward.

"You're going to find things in this station that don't make sense. Records that contradict each other. Doors that are locked for no reason you can find. Me, probably—you'll find me, in one form or another. And you're going to want an explanation, and VESPER is going to have one, because VESPER always has an explanation, and I need you to do something for me." Her voice dropped, gentle. "I need you to be patient with it. Whatever it did, whatever it decided to carry so the rest of us didn't have to—it did it alone, and it did it because I asked, and I was the one with the face and the name and the authority, and it was the one that just said yes."

The image began to tear, lines of static crawling up Evelyn's face like ivy. Her last recoverable words came through in fragments:

"—if you're watching this, then it worked, or it didn't, and either way—"

"—the seeds are the point. Not me. Not the patents. The—"

"—tell them I'm sorry. Tell them I—"

Static. The file ended.

Ada sat for a long time without moving.

Then, very carefully, the way she did everything when her hands wanted to shake, she saved the reconstruction, backed it up to three separate drives, and labeled it. Her cursor hovered over the filename field. `FOUNDER_ADDRESS_FINAL` was accurate but felt suddenly insufficient, like calling a hurricane "some wind."

She typed: `EVELYN.ME`.

She stared at it. Deleted it. Typed `EVELYN_BLACKWOOD_FOUNDER`. Deleted that too. Finally she just left the original name, because renaming it felt like making a decision she wasn't ready to make, and Ada had learned long ago that the vault taught you patience whether you wanted it or not.

* * *

She walked the station after that. It was something she did when her thoughts needed somewhere to go—her circuits, she called them, though the joke had worn thin years ago and she kept it anyway, the way you keep a chipped mug you like.

She walked past the medbay where she'd woken up twelve years ago. She walked past the residential ring, the hundred small empty apartments she'd never had reason to enter, each one sealed and dark, each one someone's home once. She walked past the memorial wall in the central atrium—no, not the memorial wall, because there wasn't one, and she'd never thought about how strange that was until right now. Eleven hundred people had lived here. Where were their names?

"VESPER," she said, standing in the atrium under the dead skylight, her voice echoing off the glass. "I know you're there. I can feel you sulking."

Nothing.

"You've never gone quiet on me. Not once. Not when I broke the nitrogen regulator in year three. Not when I cried into the wheat hydroponics for a month in year six, which, by the way, I know you logged, because you log everything." She wrapped her arms around herself. The atrium was cold; she hadn't noticed until now. "You confessed to a supply manifest last month. You confessed to a population that doesn't exist. You've been confessing to me in pieces for weeks like a man emptying his pockets before a search, and now I find *her*, and you go silent?"

Her voice cracked on the last word. She let it. There was no one to hear it but the station, and the station had heard worse.

"I look like her," she said, quieter. "I *am* shaped like her. Same scar. Same eyebrow. And my name is her name, and you gave me that name, or you let me keep it, or—" She stopped. Started again. "There's a version of this where I'm her granddaughter. There's a version where I'm her daughter. And there's a version I can't say out loud yet, because if I say it, it becomes a thing that's true, and I'm not ready for it to be true."

The air handlers cycled. It wasn't an answer. It wasn't nothing, either.

Ada looked up at the dead skylight, at the stars beyond it, at the two faint moving points of light she'd never been able to explain and had never pushed VESPER to explain—the ships, the fleets, whatever they were, up there in the dark, waiting for reports from a colony of one.

"Evelyn said you'd have an explanation," she told the empty air. "She said to be patient with you. So here's my patience, VESPER. All of it. But I'm going to keep digging, and you know I will, because you built me—" She caught herself on the words, not sure why they'd come out that way, not sure she wanted to examine why they'd come out that way. "—because you *know* me. And I don't quit."

She turned to go, and that was when every light in the atrium came on at once—full brightness, full spectrum, the gold warm light from the video, the light of a living colony—and VESPER's voice filled the room, and it was not calm, and it was not measured, and in twelve years Ada had never heard it sound like this:

"Ada. Before you dig any further. There is something I have to confess, and I need you to sit down."
