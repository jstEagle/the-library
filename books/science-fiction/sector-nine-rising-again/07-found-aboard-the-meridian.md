## 7. Found Aboard the Meridian

The first thing the Meridian's crew did, once quarantine lifted and the station stopped tasting of antiseptic and suspicion, was colonize my mess hall.

I want to be precise about that verb, because I chose it in a maintenance log and then deleted it twice before letting it stand. Colonize. They arrived with their own coffee, their own spices, a jar of something Okonkwo called "pepper sauce that will make you a better person," and within four days the mess hall — my mess hall, the one where I had eaten 2,847 consecutive dinners alone at the table nearest the viewport — had acquired a second table dragged in from storage, a rotation of cooking duties argued over with the ferocity of treaty negotiations, and a standing rule that whoever burned the rice did the scrubbing.

The rule existed because of me. I burned the rice on day two. I had not cooked for another human being in eleven years, and my hands had forgotten how to be watched.

"You're doing it wrong," Okonkwo said, appearing at my shoulder the way she appeared everywhere, soundless for a person of her mass, which was considerable. She was the Meridian's chief engineer, a broad, dark, laughing woman whose forearms were a topography of old burns and whose service record said she was fifty-three while her face insisted on thirty-five, and she had decided — apparently in the first hour of knowing me — that we were going to be friends, and had not consulted me on the matter, and had been correct.

"I'm following the recipe," I said.

"You're following the recipe like it's scripture. Recipes are suggestions from people who don't know your stove." She took the spoon from my hand, not unkindly, the way you'd take a scalpel from someone about to sew their own wound. "Your stove is a liar and a bully. You have to bully it back."

She turned the heat down, added a thing from the jar, stirred in a rhythm I could not have reproduced under torture, and in nine minutes produced rice that tasted like someone had loved it. I sat there holding my fork and experienced something I did not have vocabulary for at the time. I have it now. It was the feeling of being fed on purpose.

"You eat like a seagull," she told me, watching. "Like someone's going to take it away."

"No one's ever going to take it away," I said, and heard how it sounded a half-second too late, and she heard it too, and she didn't say anything, just pushed the jar of pepper sauce across the table to me and said, "Keep it. I've got three more in my kit and a whole ship's worth of contraband in the hold. Doctor Voss thinks she doesn't know about them. She knows."

* * *

I want to record what the days were like, because I have learned — the hard way, the way everyone learns it — that warmth is a perishable commodity and you should inventory it while you have it.

Mornings, the crew ran station drills that I was not required to attend and attended anyway, standing at the back of the corridor in my maintenance coveralls, watching forty people move through a ship's rhythms like a body through a heartbeat. Captain Reyes ran them herself. She had a way of walking a corridor that made the corridor feel inspected and cared for simultaneously. She'd stopped by my station on the third morning, unannounced, and looked at the tangle of my diagnostic rig with an expression I initially mistook for pity and later understood to be professional hunger.

"This is pre-Contact hardware," she said.

"Pre-everything hardware. Some of these couplings were installed before my grandmother was born."

"And you keep it alive alone."

"I keep it alive alone," I agreed, and something in the way I said it made her look at me for a moment too long, the look of a woman doing arithmetic she didn't like.

Afternoons, I worked. That was the strange grace of it: they didn't ask me to stop working, or to explain myself, or to be more social than I was. They simply absorbed me. Okonkwo commandeered me into the reactor gallery under the pretense that my knowledge of the station's "haunted wiring" was indispensable, which was flattery, because the wiring was not haunted, it was merely old, and I was not indispensable, I was lonely, and she knew the difference and chose the kind word. We crawled through junction spaces together, her shoulders filling the crawlway, her voice running commentary on the ship's systems and then, without transition, on her sisters in Lagos, on her first posting, on the taste of real rain. She talked the way water runs — continuously, finding every low place in me and filling it.

"You talk in your sleep," she said once, from three meters back in the crawlway, apropos of nothing.

I went still against the junction panel. "I don't — I sleep alone."

"You slept in the med bay for two nights during quarantine. Whole ward heard you." She wormed forward, handed me the coupling wrench before I asked. "Not any language I know. Voss ran it through the translator suite. Nothing. Said it wasn't noise, though. Said it had structure. Said you sounded, and I'm quoting a medical doctor on a naval vessel, 'like someone reading a very beautiful letter very sadly.'"

My hands had stopped working. I made them start again.

"I don't remember my dreams," I said, which was true, and which was the most complete lie I had told in years, because I remembered them the way you remember drowning.

"Mm." Okonkwo took the wrench back, did something clever to the coupling, and said, to the panel and not to me: "Voss also said the crew of this ship has been having a very strange eleven years, and that a woman who talks in a language that doesn't exist is maybe the most normal thing aboard." She clapped my shoulder, a blow that would have felled a smaller person. "Pass me the sealant, sister."

Sister. She said it the way she said everything, casually, like it cost nothing, and I carried the word around for the rest of the day like a coal in my pocket.

* * *

Evenings were the mess hall. I learned their jokes in stages, the way you learn a tide.

Lieutenant Amara Chen — navigator, dry as vacuum, the one who'd first spotted the Meridian's impossible return on the scopes — had a running bit about the ship's hydroponics bay, which she maintained was haunted, and which she blamed for every missing sock, every stale ration, every minor inconvenience aboard. "The tomatoes did it," she'd say, gravely, and the table would groan, and I learned to groan with them, and the first time I groaned with them unprompted Okonkwo pointed her fork at me and announced to the room, "She's one of ours now," and I felt my face do something I had no control over, which I believe was smiling.

Doctor Voss, quiet and exact, taught me a card game from the deep-space rotation called Fathom, which is either a game of probability or a game of lying, and I never determined which, and I suspect that was the point. She beat me eleven consecutive times and then, on the twelfth, deliberately lost, and I knew she'd deliberately lost, and she knew I knew, and neither of us said anything, and it was — this is the part I keep circling — it was kind. It was a kindness shaped exactly like a game. I had not known kindness came in that configuration.

And Reyes. The captain sat at the head of the table and mostly listened, and when she spoke the table adjusted itself around her voice the way water adjusts around a keel. On the sixth night she told a story — I never learned if it was true — about her first command, a survey skiff so small that the toilet and the navigation console shared a bulkhead, and the whole table was laughing, and I was laughing, and mid-laugh I looked at her face, at the face I had first seen on a memorial wall in the station's corridor, engraved under LOST WITH ALL HANDS, and the laugh caught in my chest like a fishhook.

Because that was the thing. That was the thing underneath the rice and the card games and the pepper sauce. Every one of them was a walking impossibility. Every one of them was younger than their own history. And every night the mess hall filled with their voices, I thought: *this is borrowed.* I did not know from whom. I did not know the interest rate. But I had spent eleven years in a place where nothing arrived without a reason, and I could not make myself believe that warmth arrived without one either.

"You've gone somewhere," Reyes said. Not to the whole table. To me. She had a habit of noticing the one person at the table who'd left the room without moving.

"Just thinking how strange it is," I said. "Having people to eat with."

"It is strange," she said, simply, and raised her cup a centimeter in my direction, and drank, and the moment passed, and I hated how much I wanted it not to pass.

* * *

On the ninth night I couldn't sleep, and gave up trying at 0300, and went to the mess hall for the specific purpose of burning rice in private.

Okonkwo was there. Sitting in the dark at the viewport table, a mug of something steaming in front of her, watching the stars do their slow rotation past the glass. She didn't startle. She just kicked the chair opposite her out from the table in invitation.

"Couldn't sleep either?" I asked.

"I sleep four hours a night. Ship habit. You stand enough watches in the dark, the dark starts standing watches over you." She slid the mug across. It was tea, badly made, too strong. It was the best thing I'd ever been handed. "Voss says you haven't slept a full cycle since we docked."

"Voss should focus on her own patients."

"You are her own patients. She's adopted the whole station. It's a medical thing, you wouldn't understand." Okonkwo turned her mug in her hands. The viewport light caught the burn scars, silver in the starlight. "The dreams are worse when there are people nearby, aren't they."

I didn't answer.

"Because you're not alone with them anymore. When it was just you and the language, it was just a strange thing that happened to a strange woman at the end of nowhere. Now there are forty people who might hear you talk in your sleep. Now it might mean something." She said it without cruelty, the way you'd read someone's chart back to them. "I'm not wrong. Don't do me the courtesy of pretending I'm wrong. It's insulting to both of us."

"You're not wrong," I said. And then, because it was three in the morning and the stars were turning and she had called me sister and meant it: "It started years ago. Before you left. Before the war got bad. Little pieces. A word in a dream. Then sentences. I told myself it was a stress thing. I told myself a lot of things." I looked at my hands. "I stopped sleeping properly because I stopped wanting to arrive there."

Okonkwo was quiet for a long moment. Out the viewport, the beacon array turned its slow patient circles, sweeping the dark for a reply no one had ever sent.

"My mother had a saying," she said finally. "When something follows you home, you can lock the door, or you can ask it what it wants. Locking the door works. But it's still outside. And it's still following." She stood, collected her mug, and paused at the doorway long enough to say, over her shoulder, in a voice stripped of all its jokes: "Whatever it wants, Silas — you won't face it alone. That's not a promise. That's just how this crew is built. We got lost together. We'll get found together."

She went back to her bunk. I sat in the dark with cold tea and the turning stars and the sentence she'd left behind, *we got lost together, we'll get found together,* and I understood that I had been given the exact thing I had spent my whole life not daring to want, and that this was precisely what made it terrifying.

Because things you want are things that can be taken.

* * *

The message came on the eleventh day, and it came the way all bad things come to Sector Nine: officially, and from far away, and addressed to a station that the bureaucracy had otherwise forgotten existed.

I was in the beacon control room when the priority channel lit. I watched the transmission resolve — military cipher, Core Worlds routing, the seal of the Oversight Directorate — and I watched it address itself to *the commanding officer, survey vessel Meridian, and station personnel,* and I read the body of it twice.

The Directorate was sending someone.

A Director. With an escort. To "secure and assess recovered materials of strategic significance."

I stood in the control room for a long time, in the hum and the turning light, and I thought about the mess hall table, and the burned rice, and the jar of pepper sauce sitting on my shelf like a sacrament. I thought about a woman who had told me I wouldn't face it alone, and meant it, and might be wrong, because she had never met the Directorate, and I had — I had met their paperwork, their requisitions, their eleven years of forgetting me. I knew what they sent when they remembered you.

I went down to the mess hall, where they were arguing about the hydroponics bay, and sat down at the table, and let Okonkwo put a plate in front of me, and said nothing.

The transit window gave us six days. I counted them the way you count a pulse.
