## 13. Interrogation by Kindness

They brought her tea.

That was the first thing Ilsa Reyes noticed, and the thing that frightened her most. In thirty years of war she had been shot at, boarded, court-martialed once on a technicality, and threatened with everything from demotion to decompression. No one had ever brought her tea.

The room they'd given her — *given*, not thrown her into; there was a difference and Hale wanted her to feel it — had once been Sector Nine's meteorological office. She recognized the wall mounts where the atmospheric charts used to hang, the scuffed rectangle of brighter paint where a duty roster had lived for decades. Now there was a table, two chairs, a carafe of water beaded with condensation, and a single cup of tea steaming in front of the empty seat across from hers. Chamomile. She could smell it from across the room, soft and honeyed, absurd against the hum of a station that was counting down to its own death somewhere beneath their feet.

She had been sitting alone for forty minutes when the door opened and Director Hale came in without his escort.

He was not what the dossiers promised. The files made him sound like a blade: Oversight's quiet knife, the man who arrived after disasters to decide who had caused them. But in person he moved like a tired physician at the end of a long shift. He hung his coat on the back of the chair. He sat down. He wrapped both hands around the second cup — he'd brought one for himself — and sighed as if the weight of an entire war had settled into his shoulders and he'd simply gotten used to carrying it.

"Captain Reyes." His voice was low, unhurried, the voice of a man who had never once needed to raise it. "Thank you for seeing me."

"You didn't leave me much choice, Director."

"I know." He smiled, and the smile was genuinely warm, which was somehow worse than a cold one would have been. "I find that honesty early saves time later. You were given no choice about this conversation. But everything inside it is yours. Your answers are yours. Your refusals are yours. I'm not here to take anything from you, Captain. I'm here because I think you're the only person on this station who understands what we're actually holding."

Ilsa looked at the tea. Steam curled off it in slow spirals, catching the light like something alive.

"Is it drugged?"

Hale laughed — a real laugh, startled out of him. "You've spent eleven years around Vessh. Of course you'd ask." He lifted his own cup, drank, set it down. "Chamomile, honey, a splash of something the Meridian's medical officer synthesized from station stores. If I wanted to compromise you, Captain, I have considerably more elegant tools than sedatives in a teacup."

"That's exactly what a man with elegant tools would say."

"True." He turned his cup slowly on the table, a small orbit. "Drink or don't. I'll talk either way. Talking is mostly what I do these days. It's remarkable how little else the job requires."

She picked up the cup. Not because she believed him — she didn't, not entirely, maybe not at all — but because refusing it would tell him something too, would hand him a measurement of her fear, and Ilsa Reyes had spent three decades learning never to hand an enemy a measurement. The tea was good. That annoyed her.

* * *

For a while he didn't ask her anything at all. He talked about the Meridian.

"You know what they said when your ship went dark," he said. "Eleven years ago. I read the transcripts. There was a memorial service at Command Spire. They played that awful recording of the reactor harmonics — someone thought it would be moving. It sounded like a dying animal. Half the families walked out." He shook his head. "And then your ship comes home with a crew whose service photographs don't match their faces. Younger. Every biometric scan confirms it. Cellular age reversed by roughly the duration of the voyage plus some margin we can't explain." He looked up at her, and his eyes were kind, and that kindness was the sharpest instrument in the room. "What did that feel like, Captain? Coming back to your own body's past?"

Ilsa held the cup and let the warmth soak into her palms.

Her hands had been sixty-one years old when the Meridian left dock. They were forty-nine now. She knew them intimately, both versions — the knuckles that had swollen with arthritis during the long years out, and these, smooth again, the joints loose and painless, hands that belonged to a woman who hadn't yet buried her mother, hadn't yet signed the order that burned the *Corvane*'s survivors rather than let them drift into Vessh space. She woke every morning in a body that remembered a future it had already lived, and every morning for eleven days she had lain in the quarantine bunk and grieved for the old woman she had earned the right to become.

"It felt like theft," she said finally. "Not a gift. Theft. Someone took the years I paid for."

"And you want them back."

It wasn't a question. It was a door, held open, and behind it she could see the whole architecture of what he intended. She made herself look at the door instead of walking through it.

"Everyone wants something impossible, Director. Wanting it doesn't make me stupid."

"No," Hale agreed softly. "It makes you human. Which is precisely why the Vessh chose you."

There it was. He laid it out the way a jeweler lays out stones — patiently, letting each one catch the light. The lattice. The message. A communication, offered for the first time in thirty years of war, encoded in a structure so perfectly compatible with human cognition that it seemed less like a message than an invitation. And delivered, of all places, to a ship whose captain had been present at First Contact. Whose testimony had shaped the entire official record of how the war began.

"They didn't choose us," Ilsa said. "We found them. Or they let us find them. There's a difference."

"Is there?" Hale leaned forward, elbows on the table, and for a moment the physician fell away and she saw the other thing underneath, the thing the dossiers had tried to describe and failed: not a blade, not a knife. A scale. Something that weighed. "Eleven years drifting through territory our maps call dead. A crew that comes back younger. A message that arrives speaking fluent humanity. Captain, I've spent my career studying how wars start, and I will tell you what I've learned: nothing is more dangerous than an enemy that suddenly learns your language. Aggression is honest. Translation is a weapon with the safety off."

"The message hasn't even been translated. Blackwood is still working—"

"Blackwood." He said the name gently, tasting it. "The beacon keeper. The xenolinguist who dreams in a language no one taught her." He watched Ilsa's face very carefully as he said it, and she understood that her expression was data to him, that every flicker was being weighed on those invisible scales. "A remarkable coincidence, wouldn't you say? Thirty years of war, and the one woman stationed at the one receiver built to hear this transmission just happens to already speak it in her sleep."

"It's not a coincidence."

"Oh?"

"The beacon isn't ours." Ilsa set down the cup. Her heart was beating hard, and she let it beat hard; hiding it would cost more than showing it. "Okonkwo pulled the fabrication stamps out of the primary array housing. The keel structure predates the colony charter. Director, your own archives must know this — Sector Nine wasn't built by us. We found it. We put a flag on it and called it ours, and we've been feeding it power for thirty years without ever asking what it was built to listen for."

Something moved behind Hale's eyes. Not surprise — she would have bet her restored rank that he had known all of it before his shuttle ever docked. Recognition, maybe. The look of a man watching a board resolve into the position he'd predicted six moves ago.

"Yes," he said quietly. "We know."

*We.* The pronoun landed in the room like a dropped tool.

"Then you know the countdown isn't sabotage," she pressed. "The reactor isn't failing. It's *responding*. It's doing whatever it was built to do, and it started doing it the moment the lattice came aboard. This station is finishing a sentence that someone started writing generations ago, and every hour we spend treating it like a bomb is an hour we—"

"Captain." He raised one hand, and the gentleness of the gesture stopped her more effectively than a shout. "May I tell you a story? It's not classified. It's older than classification."

He turned his cup again. One full orbit.

"When I was a young analyst — younger than you are now, which I imagine amuses you — I worked on the Corvane incident. The first contact. Everyone works on the Corvane eventually; it's the wound at the center of everything. And in the archives there's a recording. Forty seconds of it. The moment before the firing began. Have you ever heard it?"

Ilsa's mouth went dry. "I was there."

"I know," Hale said, with terrible softness. "I've read your testimony. All nine hours of it. You described the light patterns, the harmonic pulses, the way the Vessh formation folded inward like a closing hand. And then the *Meridian*'s predecessor opened fire, and thirty years of war began, and everyone from the Fleet Assembly down to schoolchildren knows the story: the aliens attacked, and we defended ourselves." He paused. "But here's what struck me, sitting in that archive at twenty-six years old. The Vessh signal in those forty seconds — the one nobody could decode — its structure matches the lattice in your cargo hold. Nearly note for note. Whatever they were trying to say to us at First Contact, Captain, is the same thing they're trying to say to us now. We've been shooting at a sentence for thirty years because no one would stop long enough to finish reading it."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere below them, deep in the station's bones, the reactor hummed its rising count, and Ilsa felt it through the floor, through the chair, through the soles of her feet, like a pulse in a wrist she was holding.

"Then help us read it," she said.

"I intend to." He leaned back. "Here is my offer, and I give you my word it is the whole offer, with nothing hidden in the hinges."

*Nothing hidden in the hinges.* She almost laughed. Almost.

"The Fleet Medical Directorate has been studying your crew since docking. The reversal phenomenon is real, it's stable, and — this is the part that matters — it appears to be tied to prolonged proximity to the lattice's resonance field. Eleven years in the hold, and your bodies walked backward up their own timelines." He let that sit between them. "If the lattice remains aboard this station, and if the resonance continues to build as the reactor counts toward whatever it's counting toward, exposure becomes uncontrolled. Uncontrolled means unpredictable. Some of your people might continue to reverse. Some might not survive the process. I have seen the projections, Captain. They are not kind."

"You're threatening my crew."

"I'm describing physics," Hale said, and his voice never rose, never sharpened, and that was the most frightening thing about him. "Threats require intent. Physics requires only time. Now — the offer. Cooperate with me. Help me move the lattice to a controlled facility where the resonance can be studied safely, where your crew can be treated, where the translation can proceed under proper supervision rather than in the hands of a sleepwalking linguist and a mutinous engineer. Do that, and I will personally sponsor every member of your crew for restorative treatment. Full stabilization at their recorded ages. Your arthritic hands, Captain. Your sixty-one years, honestly earned, returned to you. Your rank, retroactively continuous — eleven years of seniority, back pay, command priority. You would walk out of this war as one of the most decorated officers in Fleet history: the lost captain who came home and helped end it."

He spread his hands, palms up. An open scale, empty, waiting for her to place something on it.

"And all I ask in return is patience. Order. Process. Not surrender of the message — supervision of it. There is a difference, and I think a woman of your experience can appreciate the difference."

* * *

Ilsa looked at the tea going cold in her cup, and at the man across the table who had brought it warm, and she understood that she was looking at the most dangerous weapon the war had ever produced: not a ship, not a fleet, but a reasonable voice offering a tired woman her life back.

Because God help her, she wanted it. She wanted it with a hunger that scared her. She wanted her hands to ache again with the honest weight of her years. She wanted to be old, to be finished, to have paid. She wanted to stand at Command Spire and watch them strike her name off the memorial wall with full honors. She wanted her mother's grave to be older than she was, the way it was supposed to be.

And underneath the wanting, quieter, colder, she saw the shape of the trap. Because Hale had told her the truth about the Corvane recording — she was certain of that, certain the way you're certain of a load-bearing wall — and if he knew the truth, then the truth had been in the archives for thirty years, available to any analyst willing to sit with forty seconds of silence and listen. He had known, and he had done nothing, and now he was offering to trade the ending of the oldest lie in human history for her cooperation in burying it a little deeper, a little slower, under proper supervision.

Restored age. Restored rank. Restored ignorance.

"Director," she said, "may I ask you something?"

"Anything."

"The treatment you're offering. The restorative stabilization. Has it been tested?"

His pause lasted half a second. To anyone else it would have been invisible. Ilsa had spent eleven years reading the pauses of creatures who communicated in light.

"Not at this scale," he admitted. "But the mechanism is well understood—"

"So my crew would be the trial." She nodded slowly. "You'd take the only eleven human beings who ever survived the thing we're negotiating about, and you'd run them through an untested procedure to reverse the one proof we have that the Vessh can give gifts instead of wounds. And if it works, we testify that cooperation with Oversight was worth it. And if it doesn't—" she met his eyes, and kept meeting them, and did not blink, "—then the evidence dies in the same facility as the patients. Isn't that right?"

"Captain, that is an uncharitable—"

"It's arithmetic." She stood up. Her legs were steady, and she was grateful to this young treacherous body for that, at least. "You want me to sell the sentence that ends the war in exchange for a few borrowed years and a pension. You dressed it in chamomile and called it patience. But here's what thirty years taught me that your archives couldn't, Director: I watched the Vessh fold their formation inward at First Contact, and everyone said it was a threat display. It wasn't. I've seen the same fold since, twice, in ceasefire lulls. It's what they do when they're bracing to be hit. They've been bracing for thirty years." She walked to the door, then stopped, hand on the frame, and gave him the only gift she had left to give: the truth, unpaid. "You're not afraid the message is a weapon. You're afraid it's true. Because if it's true, then everything you've built stands on a grave you helped dig."

She heard him set down his cup. Carefully. Precisely. The sound of a scale being leveled.

"Where are you going, Captain?"

"To check on my crew." She turned. "You should know that Okonkwo rerouted the lattice core's telemetry through the beacon array four hours ago. Whatever your monitors are reading from the cargo bay, it's a ghost. The real signal has been moving through this station's bones since before you docked." She allowed herself the smallest smile she had managed in eleven days. "You said nothing was hidden in the hinges. You were right. It was hidden in the walls."

Behind her, for the first time since he'd entered the room, Director Hale said nothing at all.

She walked the corridor toward the quarantine ward, past the sealed bulkheads, past the guards who did not quite meet her eyes, and with every step she counted them — guard, junction, camera, junction — mapping the rhythm of her captivity the way she'd once mapped the rhythms of the Vessh pulse-language, looking for the rests between beats, the spaces where a signal could slip through. Somewhere above her, Silas Blackwood was sleeping, or dreaming, or becoming something neither of them yet had words for. Somewhere below, the reactor counted.

*Hold on,* she thought, at the dreamer, at the engineer, at the whole improbable family assembled at the edge of everything. *Hold on. I bought you the evening. Buy me the night.*

In her quarters, the lights timed out on schedule, and Ilsa Reyes lay down in her stolen-young body, closed her eyes, and for the first time in eleven days, dreamed — not of the Corvane, not of the fire, but of a language she didn't know, arriving in her sleep like light through a closing hand.
