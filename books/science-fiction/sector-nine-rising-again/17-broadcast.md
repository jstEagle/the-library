## 17. Broadcast

The reactor's countdown had a voice.

I hadn't understood that until now — that the deep subsonic groan threading through Sector Nine's bones was not machinery failing but language being spoken, syllable by syllable, in a grammar I had been dreaming for six years. The station was reciting itself to death, and I was the only one who could answer.

Two hours and eleven minutes. That was what Okonkwo's readout said when she pressed her palm flat against my shoulder on her way out of the beacon chamber — a touch that meant *go*, that meant *we're holding the corridor*, that meant everything a person can say with five fingers and no time. Then the blast door sealed behind her, and it was just me and the lattice core humming in its cradle of salvaged cable, and the beacon array above me like a great metal flower waiting for someone to make it bloom.

I sat down at the transmission console. My hands were shaking. I noticed them shaking the way you notice weather in another country — from a distance, without much investment. There was a version of me, the one who'd written ten thousand maintenance logs to nobody, who would have found this funny. *Log 4,417: Station experiencing total reactor cascade. Broadcasted first contact between civilizations. Replaced air filter in lavatory two.* The dark humor was still in there somewhere. It would keep. Everything else was about to be spent.

I keyed the open channel. Not the military bands, not the encrypted fleet frequencies Hale would have wanted me to whisper into — all of it. Every frequency the old array could reach, wideband, unencrypted, naked signal flung out into the dark. If this was going to be said, it was going to be said to everyone. The Vessh armada hanging off the system's edge like a bruise. The human fleets burning hard toward us with their weapons hot and their orders sealed. The listening posts, the mining rigs, the lonely ships like the Meridian had once been, carrying people who deserved to hear the truth before they died not knowing it.

"This is Silas Blackwood," I said, because someone should say a name. "Sector Nine relay. I have a message. It isn't mine. I'm only the mouth it came through."

My voice sounded strange to me — steady, almost calm, a stranger's voice. Six years of talking to myself in empty corridors had taught me how a voice carries when there's no one to bounce off of. Now there were forty thousand ships' worth of ears. Maybe more. Maybe everyone.

I put my hand on the lattice core. It was warm. Of course it was warm; it had been warm since the Meridian brought it home, warm as a sleeping animal, and I had spent weeks telling myself that was residual energy from crystallization. It wasn't. It had been waiting for me to stop being afraid of it.

*Begin,* it said, in the tongue under my skin.

And I began.

---

The Vessh language doesn't have a word-for-word shape the way human languages do. I had learned that in dreams before I could articulate it awake — that meaning moved through it the way water moves through roots, branching, doubling back, arriving everywhere at once. When I spoke it aloud for the first time, alone in the beacon chamber with the reactor counting down beneath my feet, it didn't sound alien. That was the thing I had never been able to say to anyone, the thing that had made me feel insane for six years: it sounded like something I'd forgotten. Like a song from a house I couldn't remember living in.

"*Vesh-kara ilun. Vesh-kara tera.*" One seed. One seed. The same phrase twice, and the second time it meant *you*, and the first time it meant *us*, and the grammar held them together so tightly that to translate one without the other was to lie.

I translated anyway. That was the job. That had always been the job.

"One seed," I said in Standard, my voice raw against the console pickup. "One seed sown in two soils. You are our reflection across the dark. We are yours. The war between us is a war between hands that grew from the same root."

The words went out. I felt them go — not metaphorically, not mystically, but physically, the array drawing power in surges that made the floor shudder, the lights browning and recovering, browning and recovering, the whole station breathing around me in ragged gasps. Somewhere below, coolant screamed through pipes that had been built before I was born by engineers who had never been told what they were really building. A receiver. An olive branch buried in a weapons platform, waiting generations for a throat to speak through.

Ninety-one minutes.

The first response came in under four minutes, which told me everything about how scared they were. Human fleet command, automated priority channel, a voice like gravel poured into a uniform: *"Unidentified broadcast, Sector Nine relay, cease transmission immediately. This is a controlled communications environment during active fleet operations. Cease or be—"*

They cut themselves off. Or someone above them did. I kept speaking.

---

Here is what I know about the message, now that I am finally its keeper instead of its hostage. I'll set it down here, in the order I gave it, because if the reactor takes me with it I want the sequence to exist somewhere outside my skull:

First: origin. Two worlds, seeded from one source — not gods, not accident, but intention, an ancient dispersal neither species remembered because memory doesn't survive a hundred thousand years of separate weather. The Vessh had found the record in their own deep places, in ice cores and gene-shadows, the way we might have if we had ever thought to look. They had known longer than us. They had carried the knowledge like a wound.

Second: the mistranslation. Thirty years ago, over a young survey captain's shoulder, a first-contact greeting had been read as a claim of ownership — *your world is our soil* — when the actual root-words meant *your world is our kin*. Ilsa Reyes had watched the wrong translation become doctrine. So had I, in a way; every textbook I'd ever studied had been built on that single bent sentence, and I had never once thought to check its foundation.

Third: the request. Not surrender. Not conversion. Recognition. *Look at your enemy and see the family resemblance.* And beneath it, threaded through every clause like gold wire: grief. Thirty years of fratricide, mourned in advance by the ones who started the war by trying to end it too gently, too quietly, and failed.

That was the part that undid me. Not the cosmology — I could hold the cosmology; I'd been holding it since chapter fourteen of my own life. It was the grief. The Vessh had composed this message the way you write to a sibling you've been estranged from for decades, rehearsing apology after apology and never sending any of them, until finally you send the worst, clumsiest draft because the alternative is dying unreconciled.

I was crying while I translated. I want that on the record, in whatever log survives me. Not beautifully. Ugly crying, nose running, voice cracking on the third declension of the word for *shared*. The beacon didn't care. The beacon took my wrecked voice and amplified it across eleven light-minutes of killing space, and somewhere out there, forty thousand human gunners heard a woman sobbing in an alien tongue, and somewhere beyond them, the Vessh heard their own grief come back to them in a human throat.

Seventy-eight minutes.

---

The weapons locked at sixty-two.

I knew it was happening before anyone told me, because the lattice core changed pitch — a thin rising tone, like a wineglass feeling the note that will break it. Targeting solutions, incoming from both sides. The human fleets had resolved their hesitation into procedure; procedure is what fear wears to work. And the Vessh ships, those vast dark shapes at the system's edge, had gone still in the way that I now understood was their version of holding breath.

*"Blackwood."* Hale's voice, cutting through on a direct line he should not have had access to anymore, since Okonkwo's people had stripped his override authority from the station net an hour ago. He sounded unbothered. He always sounded unbothered. That was his genius and his horror — a man who could watch the world end and critique its posture. "You're broadcasting classified material on open channels. Do you understand what you've done? Every faction, every syndicate, every opportunist in three sectors has that message now."

"Yes," I said, and kept translating.

"You think truth ends wars? Truth is ammunition. Whoever fires it first wins, and you've just handed it to everyone." A pause, and then softer, the register he'd used on me in his office with the immunity offer gleaming on the table between us: "Silas. Come down from there. Let the reactor blow. The fleets will stand down rather than lose the station — it's leverage either way. You don't have to die for a sentence."

"It's not a sentence," I said. "It's a name. Their name for us. Ours for them. The same name."

He made a sound that might have been pity. "Sixty seconds of carrier left before the array burns out. Make them count."

He was right about the array. I could smell it — hot polymer and ozone, the sweetish stink of insulation giving up, the rack-mounted amplifiers cycling their warning fans from whine to shriek. The old flower above me was blooming itself to death. Fine. Flowers are allowed.

Fifty-five minutes on the reactor. Forty-something on the array. I did the arithmetic the way you do it in nightmares, the numbers sliding past each other, and decided not to decide. The math would resolve itself. My only job was the words.

So I gave them everything I had left.

---

I stopped translating and started speaking both tongues at once — interleaved, the Vessh line and its Standard shadow, call and response, my own voice duetting with itself. It shouldn't work, anatomically. Human mouths aren't built for the Vessh consonants, those sounds like wind deciding to be stone. But I had practiced them for six years in my sleep, in the country under the skin, where the body learns things the waking mind refuses. My throat ached. I let it ache.

And the dreams — God, the dreams — they weren't dreams anymore. They were here. The language had finished itself, the way a river finishes itself at the sea, and I was standing in the delta with my arms open.

"*Terash vell. Terash vell im-kara.*"

"We looked into the dark and called it enemy," I said, "because we had forgotten it was mirror."

"*Sella-vosh. Sella-vosh tera.*"

"The seed does not apologize to the soil. The seed remembers."

I don't know how long I spoke. Time had gone strange, thickened, each minute a room I walked through slowly. At some point I became aware that the channel was full — not just fleet command, not just the automated squawk of threat protocols, but voices. Hundreds of them. Thousands. Ships relaying the broadcast onward because their crews demanded it. Mining platforms piping it into mess halls. A destroyer's comms officer, a kid by her voice, sobbing openly on an open mic three systems away because her brother had died at Calloway Reach and she had just heard the word for *kin* applied to the thing that killed him, and she didn't know yet whether to be destroyed or saved by it.

Both, I wanted to tell her. Both at once. That's what reconciliation is.

Forty-one minutes. The array's carrier wave stuttered, recovered, stuttered again. In the gaps, the silence rang.

And then — I will write this plainly, because plainness is the only honest container for it — the Vessh answered.

Not with weapons. With the same broadcast, returned. Their own voice, layered over mine, speaking the message back in perfect unison, and then continuing it — verses I had never received, coda after coda, the parts of the letter that had been withheld pending proof of receipt. They had been listening the whole time. They had been waiting, for thirty years and maybe a hundred thousand, to find out whether anyone on our side would ever say the words back.

Their voice came through my array and it was beautiful. It sounded like glaciers learning to sing. It sounded like the dreams, finally, fully, awake.

The targeting locks wavered. I felt it in the lattice — the breaking-glass tone softening, bending, resolving downward into something almost like relief. On the tactical feeds flickering across my console, icons that had been fixed on firing solutions began to drift, to hesitate, to gray out as commands up and down both chains of authority shouted questions that procedures had no answers for. Weapons are built to fire at enemies. Nobody had ever defined the target for them as *family*. The machines waited, patient and terrible, for someone to tell them what they were pointing at.

Twenty-nine minutes of reactor. Eleven, maybe less, of array.

I kept speaking. My voice was gone to gravel and I spoke anyway, whispering into the pickup now, trusting the old machine to carry even a whisper the last distance it needed to go. Out there, the human flagship *Aegis Doctrine* had gone silent on all channels. Out there, the largest Vessh ship had begun to move — slowly, majestically, not toward us but alongside, presenting its long flank to the human fleets in a maneuver every military academy in two civilizations would spend the next decade arguing about, and which I understood instantly, because I dreamed in their grammar: it was turning its side to them the way you turn your cheek. *Here. If you must. Here.*

Eighteen minutes.

Then the carrier wave died.

The array's main amplifier blew with a sound like a cathedral clearing its throat — a boom I felt in my teeth, in the fluid of my eyes — and the console went dark except for emergency strips, and the silence that fell over the beacon chamber was so total I could hear my own heart stumbling. I sat in the sudden quiet with smoke curling off the racks and the reactor's countdown still tolling beneath me, seventeen minutes and falling, and I thought: *it's done, whatever happens now, it's done, they heard it, both of them heard it—*

Which is when the blast door unsealed.

It shouldn't have. Okonkwo had welded the controls from the outside; I'd watched her do it, laughing, swearing, sparks in her hair. But the door ground open anyway, slow and reluctant, and standing in the corridor's red emergency light, flanked by four marines whose loyalty I suddenly could not guess, was Director Hale. He had a portable override unit under one arm, connected by a fat cable to something behind him, and he was looking at the dead array the way a man looks at a horse that died mid-race.

"That was quite a performance," he said. "Now let's talk about what happens when the reactor hits zero — and who gets to tell the story afterward."

Behind him, far down the corridor, I heard Okonkwo shout my name.
