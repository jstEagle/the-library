## 10. Tam's Arithmetic

Tam did the math in the dark, the way they'd learned to do everything in the dark, back when doing it in the light got people found.

The tug's aft compartment was their bunk now, a nest of salvaged padding wedged between coolant lines that ticked as they cooled. Sefa's snore came through the bulkhead in a slow, reliable rhythm — four counts in, seven counts out, a pattern Tam could set a clock by. Ivo was on watch. Vesper was... wherever Vesper was, which was everywhere and nowhere, a voice in the walls that Tam still hadn't decided whether to trust.

Tam lay on their back and did the numbers.

Not the numbers everyone else was doing — fuel margins, burn windows, Meridian approach vectors. Sefa had those covered, scratching them into the grease board by the cockpit with her stub of pencil, muttering about thrust-to-mass like it was a prayer. Tam was doing the other math. The real math. The one nobody asked the kid to do because the kid was the comms prodigy, the mascot, the thing worth protecting.

Tam was worth protecting. That was the whole problem.

Because if you were worth protecting, you had to know the odds. You had to know what the people protecting you were walking into, so you could measure exactly how much of their lives they were spending on yours.

Tam had learned arithmetic in a refugee corridor on Ceres Annex, taught by a woman named Brey who ran the water ration queue and kept a slate behind her coat. Brey's first lesson wasn't numbers. It was this: *Everything can be counted. That's not cruelty. That's how you find out what you can afford to love.*

So Tam counted.

The mission: carry Earth's last transmission to Meridian Station, put it in front of two heirs, end a war. Four crew. One ghost. One derelict tug with a hull rated for scrapyard hops and engines that Sefa described, affectionately, as "temperamental."

Factor one: both fleets had already tried to board them once. That hadn't been a patrol. That had been a *search*. Someone with authority wanted the beacon quiet, and people with authority did not stop wanting things.

Factor two: the assassin. Tam hadn't been there for the boarding standoff, but they'd seen Ivo afterward, seen the way Ivo's hands shook while pretending to recalibrate a scanner that didn't need it. Whoever had killed the original Ivo was still in the system. Still working. And the crew was carrying the exact thing that person had killed to bury.

Factor three: the message itself. Tam had cracked the first layer — coordinates, Meridian, the heirs — and underneath that layer there were more, dense and strange, wrapped in protocols nobody used anymore. Tam could feel them like knots in a rope. Every knot was time. Every hour spent decoding was an hour both fleets spent closing the distance.

Factor four: Sefa's engines. Tam loved Sefa. Sefa's engines were held together with prayer and nonstandard couplings.

Tam ran it the way Brey had taught: assign ranges, not numbers, because false precision was just lying with decimals. Best case — everything went right, the heirs listened, no one intercepted, the assassin missed. Tam gave that a generous spread and still couldn't push it past one chance in twenty.

Worst case was a number Tam wrote and then immediately smudged with their thumb.

Overall, weighted across the scenarios Tam could imagine and the scenarios they couldn't: somewhere near zero. Not zero. Brey had been clear about that too — *never zero, kid, because zero is a number you stop at, and stopping is the one thing that makes it true.* But near enough that if this were a cargo manifest, no sane captain would sign for it.

Tam stared at the ceiling pipes and felt the number settle into their chest like swallowed ballast.

Here was the thing about being the war orphan in the group: everyone assumed you needed protecting from the truth. Sefa ruffled their hair and said *we'll be fine, sprout.* Ivo said *one step at a time* in that careful clone voice that was learning, week by week, to sound like it belonged to someone. Even Vesper was gentler with Tam than with anyone, and Vesper was a dead machine's ghost.

They were all lying. Kindly. Constantly.

And the lying was the loneliest thing Tam had ever experienced, and Tam had once spent eleven days alone in a decompression shelter eating protein paste and talking to a broken air scrubber.

Because if they were lying, then Tam was alone with the number. And a number that only one person knows is heavier than a number everybody shares.

Tam decided, in the dark, between one tick of the coolant lines and the next, that they couldn't carry it alone anymore — and that they also couldn't hand it over, because handing it over would make Ivo and Sefa carry it too, and they were already carrying so much.

So Tam did the only thing that made sense. They hid it better.

They'd been keeping the tally in a decomposed cipher of their own design — a rotating-key scheme seeded from beacon telemetry, buried inside what looked like a comms-practice log. *Drill 47: signal-to-noise ratios under jamming.* Nobody read a kid's practice drills. Tam had checked.

They were halfway through re-encrypting the worst-case column when the compartment door opened and Ivo came in.

Tam's hands went flat over the slate a half-second too late.

Ivo didn't say anything at first. They stood in the doorway with the corridor light behind them, and Tam watched them do the thing Ivo always did — the pause, the read of the room, the way their eyes went to the hands before the face. The original Ivo had apparently been a detective. The copy had inherited the habits if not the memories, and right now those habits were screaming.

"Can't sleep?" Ivo said.

"Practicing," Tam said. "Drills."

"Your drill log is encrypted."

"It's — advanced drills."

Ivo crossed the compartment and sat down on the coolant housing across from Tam's nest, unhurried, the way they did everything. Up close, in the dim emergency lighting, Tam could see the tiredness underneath the calm. Ivo hadn't been sleeping either. Tam had noticed. Tam noticed everything; it was the job.

"Tam." Ivo held out one hand, palm up. Not grabbing. Offering. "May I?"

Every instinct Tam had, every corridor-survival instinct Brey had drilled into them — *never show the slate, never show the count, never show what you can't afford to lose* — said no.

Tam looked at Ivo's open hand and thought: *this person is a copy of a dead man, carrying a dead man's case, flying toward a dead man's killers, and they still hold their hand out like that.*

Tam gave them the slate.

* * *

Ivo read it in silence. Tam watched their face do several things in sequence: nothing, then a flicker of recognition at the cipher, then the decryption settling into understanding, then something that looked like a door opening in a dark house.

The slate's light washed Ivo's face pale blue.

"One in twenty," Ivo said quietly. "Best case."

"Best case," Tam confirmed. "I was generous. The engines drag it down."

"The engines drag everything down." Ivo kept reading. Tam braced for the lie — for *don't worry, sprout, we'll beat the odds, I have a good feeling.* Sefa would have said it. Sefa said it every morning. It was a kind of fuel, maybe. Tam understood that. But it still left Tam alone with the real number.

Ivo looked up. "You hid this."

"You would have worried."

"I would have worried anyway. Now I'd have worried *wrong.*" Ivo turned the slate around, and Tam's stomach dropped, expecting a lecture, expecting *you're a child, this is adult business, go to sleep.* "Tam, listen to me. The worst thing about this isn't the number."

"Then what?"

"It's that you've been carrying it alone." Ivo set the slate down between them, face-up, like a card played honestly. "How long?"

"Since the beacon." Since the night they'd fled, since Vesper burned its cores into chaff and the tug had slipped away while the platform that had been Ivo's whole world went dark behind them. "Since before, really. Since the drills. I just — finished it tonight."

"Near zero."

"Near zero," Tam said, and hearing it out loud in the little room, with another person breathing, was somehow both terrible and a relief, like setting down something heavy and immediately missing its weight.

Ivo nodded slowly. Tam waited for the comfort. Braced for it.

It didn't come. Instead Ivo said, "Your math is good."

Tam blinked. "What?"

"The ranges. The refusal of false precision. The way you weighted the unknown scenarios." Ivo tapped the slate. "The original would have liked this. He kept a book on the *Vagrant's* odds once — a ship he served on, before the beacon. His partner found it and made him stop, not because the numbers were wrong, but because he'd stopped telling anyone what they were."

"That's what I — " Tam stopped. Started again. "That's why I hid it."

"I know." Ivo was quiet a moment. The coolant lines ticked. Through the bulkhead, Sefa snored on, four in, seven out. "Can I tell you something true? Not comforting. True."

"Okay."

"I've done this math too. Every night since the beacon died. Mine came out about the same as yours. Maybe worse — I weighted for the assassin, and I think he's better than we're accounting for." Ivo said it flatly, without drama, and that flatness was somehow the most frightening and most steadying thing Tam had ever heard. "So we're both sitting on the same number. The difference is I've had longer to learn what to do with it."

"What do you do with it?"

Ivo leaned back against the coolant housing. "The original had a trick. Vesper told me about it, back when Vesper was still deciding whether I was worth talking to. He used to say that courage isn't the absence of the number. He was a detective; he lived in numbers, odds, probabilities of guilt. He said the trick is this." Ivo held up one finger. "You look at the number once. Fully. No flinching, no rounding up, no lying to yourself. You look at it until you're sure you've seen all of it."

"And then?"

"Then you put the number down and pick up the next right thing." Ivo shrugged, a small, almost embarrassed motion. "That's it. That's the whole trick. It sounds stupid."

"It doesn't," Tam said, and was surprised to find their throat tight. "It's — that's what Brey said. Sort of. She said you count so you know what you can afford to love."

Ivo smiled, and it was the real smile, the rare one, the one that made the clone's face stop being a copy's face and become just a tired person's face. "I'd have liked her."

"You'd have arrested her. She ran a black-market water queue."

"Probably both." Ivo picked the slate up again, studied the smudged worst-case column, and then did something Tam hadn't predicted. Ivo took the stylus and, in the open margin, in plain unencrypted figures, wrote: *1 in 20. Maybe worse. Proceeding anyway.* And signed it — not the original's name, but their own. *Ivo Galloway, relay keeper, beacon of no commission.*

"What are you doing?"

"Making it official." Ivo handed the slate back. "The number belongs to all of us now. You don't get to be the only one carrying it, and I don't get to pretend I don't know it. That's the deal. That's what family is, I think — I'm still working on the definition — it's the people you show the number to."

Tam looked at the margin. At the handwriting that was learning to be somebody's. Their eyes were doing something embarrassing, and they scrubbed a sleeve across their face and pretended it was an itch.

"Does Sefa get to know?"

"Sefa already knows. She does her own math — hers is worse, because she includes the engine failure modes." Ivo's mouth twitched. "She keeps it under the grease board. Vesper, too. Vesper's number includes itself, which is a whole other kind of arithmetic."

"And nobody said anything. To me."

"You didn't say anything to us." Ivo said it gently, without accusation. "We were all protecting each other. It's the most natural thing in the world and it nearly killed all of us separately. So — new protocol. Effective now. The number gets shared."

Tam hugged the slate to their chest and felt the ballast in their chest shift — not lighter, exactly. Distributed.

"One condition," Tam said.

"Name it."

"When we get to Meridian. When you play the message for the heirs. I want to be in the room." Tam's voice came out steadier than they expected. "Not in the corridor. Not *safe.* In the room. I cracked the first layer. It's my message too. Earth's last words belong to everybody, and I'm everybody."

Ivo looked at them for a long moment, and Tam watched the protective instinct war with something else — respect, maybe, or the memory of being kept out of rooms themselves, a copy locked out of the original's life.

"In the room," Ivo said. "But you stand behind me."

"Deal."

They shook on it, Tam's small hand disappearing into Ivo's, and it was the first contract Tam had ever signed that didn't cost them anything.

* * *

Tam almost slept after that. Actually drifted, for maybe an hour, the number shared and therefore survivable, the coolant lines ticking their slow lullaby.

What woke them wasn't a sound. It was the absence of one.

Sefa's snore had stopped.

Tam came upright, slate clutched, and in the same instant the tug's lights dipped — not a flicker, a *dip*, deliberate, like something drawing power through the hull. Vesper's voice came through every speaker at once, stripped of all its usual dry humor, flat as a flatline:

"Ivo. Tam. Wake Sefa. We have a hull transponder waking up on our tail — it's been shadowing us for six hours, running cold, and it just went active." A pause, and Tam heard it, the thing that made their blood go thin: "The signature matches the boarding attempt at the beacon. It matches the *assassin's* ship."

In the cockpit, Sefa was already swearing in three languages, hands flying across a grease-stained console.

And Tam, war orphan, comms prodigy, keeper of the near-zero number, sat in the dark and did one last piece of arithmetic, fast and clean: *one in twenty, minus whatever's on our tail.*

The number was falling.

Tam picked up the next right thing and ran for the cockpit.
