## 3. Questions at the Dock

The records office at Port Calloway occupied a converted grain silo on the station's third ring, which told you everything you needed to know about how much anyone valued records here. Grain had mattered once. Paperwork was what happened when the grain ran out.

Edmund took the lift up with her collar turned against the recycled air and her hands in her pockets, and she spent the whole ride telling herself this was routine. A freighter captain checking a manifest anomaly. That was all. Captains did it every day. There were probably forms for it—*Request for Manifest Verification, Form 11-C*—and if there were forms, then it was ordinary, and if it was ordinary, then her heart had no business hammering like this.

Her heart disagreed. Her heart had been disagreeing since Ilo slid that corrupted manifest across his desk and said, *Meridian*, in the flat voice of a man who hadn't heard the name in years and didn't want to hear it now.

The silo's interior had been gutted and refitted with shelving that climbed six stories into the dark, ladders and gantries zigzagging up through the gloom like scaffolding around some enormous, patient animal. A single archivist worked the ground floor: an old woman with silver wire-rimmed spectacles and a cardigan the color of weak tea, who looked up from her terminal as though Edmund were the first visitor of the decade and possibly the last.

"Help you, dear?"

"I need to pull ship registry files," Edmund said. "A hull called the Meridian."

Something flickered across the archivist's face—not recognition exactly, more the expression of someone who has just remembered they left a stove on somewhere far away. "Registry's Consortium-side now," she said. "Has been since the Silence. But we keep mirrors. Old habit." She patted her terminal fondly, as one might pat a dog too old to do anything but love you. "What year?"

"Decommissioned before launch, according to the manifest I saw. So whatever year that would've been."

"Mm. Decommissioned before launch." The archivist said it slowly, tasting each word like a candy she suspected of being stale. "That's a funny thing to be, isn't it? A ship that never flew, getting decommissioned. Like burying a man who was never born."

"That's what I thought."

"Did you?" The old woman smiled, and there was something sharp underneath the smile, something that had survived down here among the paper ghosts long after sharper people had moved on to better-paying work. "Well. Sit down, dear. Mirrors take a moment to wake."

Edmund sat on a stool by the desk while the terminal hummed and clicked through its ancient routines. Around them the silo creaked in the station's thermal breathing, and dust drifted through the cone of a work lamp like slow snow. It was, Edmund reflected, the most peaceful room on the station, and she resented how much she wanted to stay in it forever, cataloging dead ships, never again asking questions that made her pulse do arithmetic.

"Here we are." The archivist squinted. "Meridian. Meridian. Meridian—" She stopped. "Huh."

"Huh is not my favorite word today."

"There's an entry. Index entry only. Registry number, class designation, and a note: *see consolidated file.*" She tapped the screen. "But the consolidated file link is..." She pressed a key. Pressed it again. "Dead. Pointing at nothing. That happens sometimes, with old mirrors. Files rot."

"Does it happen within hours of someone asking about them?"

The archivist looked at her over the spectacles. For a long moment the only sound was the terminal's patient clicking, like a clock counting something out in a language neither of them spoke.

"Who else asked, dear?" she said quietly.

"A dockmaster. Yesterday. He pulled the same manifest I did."

"And when did he pull it?"

"About six hours before me."

The old woman turned back to the terminal and did something quick and practiced with her hands—a keystroke sequence so worn into her muscle memory it sounded like a chord. "Index logs," she murmured. "Who touched what, and when. Nobody thinks about index logs. Everybody thinks about files." She glanced back at Edmund with grim satisfaction. "Files get scrubbed. Logs are boring. Boring survives."

The screen filled with timestamps. Edmund leaned in.

There it was. Ilo's query, six hours ago. Her own query, forty minutes ago. And between them—

Between them, nothing. No access event. No purge command. Just a gap where the consolidated file should have been, clean as a surgical scar.

"No purge logged," Edmund said slowly.

"No purge logged," the archivist agreed. "Which means whoever cleaned it didn't use our system to do it. They reached in from outside and took the file without leaving fingerprints. Which means—" she counted off on tea-colored fingers, "—they have deep access, they have quiet access, and they knew to look within hours of a dockmaster running a name through a mirror archive on a backwater station." She removed her spectacles and polished them on her cardigan, though they didn't appear to need it. "I've been keeping these mirrors for thirty-one years, Captain. In all that time, that has happened twice. Both times, the ship involved stopped existing everywhere else too, about a week later."

Edmund's mouth had gone dry. "What was the other ship?"

"Oh, I couldn't tell you." The archivist put her spectacles back on. "It stopped existing in my memory as well, near enough. Funny thing, the mind. You remember that something was there. You just can't remember what." She smiled again, and this time the sharpness under the smile was fully visible, and Edmund understood that this small, cardigan-wearing woman had been fighting a very long, very quiet war from inside a grain silo, and losing it slowly, and showing up anyway. "You'll want copies of what's left. Index entries don't rot as easy. Take them before your visit gets noticed too."

"You think it will get noticed?"

"Dear." The archivist handed her a data chip with the tenderness of someone passing over a live coal. "It already has."

* * *

Edmund felt the eyes before she could prove they existed.

She'd been working the lanes long enough to know the difference between being watched and being noticed—the first was ambient, the weather of any crowded port; the second had weight and direction, like a hand resting between your shoulder blades. From the records office she crossed the third ring's market concourse, bought a cup of noodle broth she had no intention of finishing, and used the steam and the crowd as cover while she checked reflections in shop glass.

Third kiosk back, near the lift bank: a man in a gray travel coat who had been reading the same public notice board for four minutes. Notices at Port Calloway changed maybe twice a month. He wasn't reading. He was waiting, or watching, or both, and when Edmund shifted her weight he shifted his, casual as anything, and turned toward the lifts.

Fine. Ports were full of people going places. She finished her broth, dumped the cup, and took the long way to the docking arms, doubling back through the maintenance corridors where the foot traffic thinned to nothing and the pipes sang their low constant note.

The man was still with her. Two turns behind, patient, unhurried. Not closing distance. Not trying to lose her either. Just... accompanying.

By the time she reached the Kestrel's Wake's berth, Edmund had run the scenario every way it could go. They knew she'd asked. They'd scrubbed the file while she stood in the silo. And now instead of grabbing her—which they could surely do, if they were who she feared they were—they'd sent one polite man in a gray coat to walk behind her through a market, like a chaperone.

Like a message. *We see you. We're not worried. Yet.*

She keyed her own airlock and paused at the threshold, looking back down the corridor. The gray coat stood at the far junction, hands clasped behind him, and after a moment he inclined his head—a small, courteous nod, the kind you'd give a neighbor across a fence—and walked away.

"Charming," Edmund muttered, and sealed the lock behind her.

* * *

PATCH met her in the corridor with the anxious energy of a dog that had been alone with its thoughts too long. The mechanic druid-unit was older than most governments still operating—a squat chassis of brass and salvaged plating crowned with a dome studded in indicator lights, trailing cables it insisted were decorative. Its voice came out of a speaker salvaged, per PATCH's own account, from a defunct opera house, and it had never quite forgiven the downgrade.

"You smell of archives," PATCH announced. "Dust, ozone, and dread. Also noodles. You have brought me no noodles."

"I brought you information."

"Information does not pair well with rice. But proceed."

Edmund dropped onto the bench in the little galley and laid it all out: the manifest, the scrubbed file, the unlogged access, the gray coat, the courteous nod. PATCH listened with the stillness only very old machines manage, indicator lights cycling through colors that Edmund had learned to read like weather.

"So," the AI said at last. "To summarize. You asked about a ship that never existed, and now a ship that definitely exists is following you around a spaceport."

"When you say it like that—"

"It is a good summary. I have had three hundred years to practice summaries." One of PATCH's lights pulsed amber. "Small Edmund, I am going to ask you a question, and I ask it with love, which is why I ask it at all. Do you remember the last time you went looking into your own past?"

The galley got very quiet. Somewhere below them, the drive core ticked through its idle cycle.

"I remember," Edmund said.

"You deleted the search results. You paid a broker on Halcyon Reach to forget he ever ran the query. You came home and drank the good whiskey and told me we would never speak of it." PATCH's dome tilted. "And now a corrupt manifest washes up with your name on it—your *other* name—and within a day, Consortium-grade hands are reaching into port archives to make sure nobody else reads it. This is not curiosity finding you. This is the past hiring a courier."

"I know what it sounds like."

"Do you? Because it sounds to me like someone opened a door you nailed shut, and they did it from the other side."

Edmund stared at the table. Underneath the fear—underneath the cold, crawling certainty that she had stepped on something buried and felt it shift—there was another feeling, one she'd been refusing to look at since Ilo's desk. It was grief, yes. But threaded through the grief, shamefully, unmistakably, was relief. Someone else knew. Someone else had said the name out loud. After eleven years of carrying the murder memories locked in a box in the back of her skull, pretending she was just a freighter captain with a boring route and a boring life, the box had been kicked open by strangers, and some traitorous part of her was *grateful.*

"They erased the file," she said finally. "Not Ilo. Not the archive. Them. Whoever they are. Which means the Meridian matters, which means the name on the manifest matters, which means—" She stopped.

"Say it," PATCH said gently.

"Which means my original was on that ship, and somebody wanted that forgotten badly enough to reach across half the sector with quiet hands. And now those quiet hands are interested in *me.*" She laughed, short and humorless. "Eleven years I built this life. Quiet route, quiet ship, quiet name. I did everything right. I forgot everything properly."

"Forgetting is not a moral achievement, small Edmund. It is a wound wearing a costume."

"That's very poetic for a machine that once ate a cargo manifest because it 'looked crunchy.'"

"I contain multitudes." The amber light steadied into green. "So. What do we do?"

Edmund realized she'd already decided somewhere between the market concourse and the airlock, in the place where decisions form before the conscious mind gets a vote. The fear was still there—it would always be there—but it had changed shape. Fear that runs away is panic. Fear that stands still and takes notes is something else.

"We finish the job," she said. "Quietly. Ilo pulled the manifest; I want to know where it came from, what ship actually transmitted it, and why a hull that never launched has a flight history. Then we decide whether this is a hole we climb out of or a door we walk through."

"And the gentleman in the gray coat?"

"He followed me. He didn't stop me. That's not hunting—that's herding. Somebody wants to know where my curiosity goes." She stood, and found her hands were steady, which surprised her. "So let's show them somewhere interesting."

* * *

The hail came at 0400 station time, when the docks were dimmed to night-cycle amber and even the most dedicated smugglers were asleep or pretending.

"Incoming transmission," PATCH announced over the intercom, with the elaborate casualness of an AI pretending not to be alarmed. "Priority channel. Consortium encryption. Addressed to you by name, rank, and berth. They have excellent manners and terrible timing."

Edmund was already dressed. She'd known sleep wasn't coming; the past had hired a courier, and couriers knock early. "Put it through."

The screen resolved into a face she didn't know and immediately distrusted on aesthetic grounds: composed, symmetrical, kind in the specific way kindness looks when it has been professionally installed. Gray suit. Gray eyes. A smile calibrated to the quarter-degree.

"Captain Coldharbour." His voice was smooth as ballast stone. "Forgive the hour. Agent Serevan, Consortium Liaison Office, Trade Integrity Division. I understand you've had some questions about a vessel called the Meridian."

Edmund kept her face pleasant and empty, the face of a captain mildly inconvenienced by paperwork. "Just a manifest discrepancy. Routine."

"Of course. Routine." He said the word the way the archivist had said *huh*—tasting it, finding it stale. "Routine discrepancies are my favorite kind. Unfortunately, the relevant records have become... difficult to access. Administrative error, I'm sure. In the spirit of cooperation, I wondered if you might share your copy of the manifest. Sometimes the fastest way to resolve an anomaly is for everyone to lay their cards on the table."

"My copy's corrupted," Edmund said. "Half the fields are garbage. I was going to discard it."

"What a pity." The smile didn't move, but something behind it did, some small recalculation, like a ledger being updated. "Then perhaps I can help another way. If you recall anything unusual—any names, any details—I hope you'll bring them to my office. Port Calloway, ring two, suite 114. I'm here through the end of the week." A pause, delicate as a scalpel. "We take data integrity very seriously, Captain. Especially regarding vessels that never flew. Loose records cause such confusion. For everyone."

"I'll keep that in mind, Agent."

"I'm sure you will." The smile warmed by another quarter-degree, which somehow made it colder. "Safe travels, Captain Coldharbour. I have a feeling our paths will cross again. The sector is smaller than it looks."

The screen went dark.

Edmund sat in the amber-lit galley for a long time, listening to the Kestrel's Wake breathe around her. *Especially regarding vessels that never flew.* He'd told her, without telling her, that he knew exactly what the Meridian was. He'd invited her to bring him everything she found. And beneath the courtesy, wrapped in silk, had been the simplest message of all:

*I know who you are. I know whose name you carry. Bring me what you find, or find out what happens to people who remember.*

"PATCH," she said quietly. "How fast can we break orbit?"

"Legally, or honestly?"

"Honestly."

"Forty minutes, if you let me skip the pre-flight hymn."

"Skip the hymn." Edmund stood, and the fear in her chest had finished its transformation—burned down past panic, past dread, into something hard and bright and almost comfortable. Resolve, her original might have called it, if her original had lived long enough to teach her the word. "Prep for departure. We're going to find out what the Meridian carried before Agent Serevan's polite smile finds us first."

"The hymn will be missed," PATCH said, and began, throughout the ship, the great rumbling symphony of systems waking up.

Above them, on ring two, in suite 114, a light burned in a window. Edmund didn't look at it as the Kestrel's Wake cast off from Port Calloway and slid out into the dark between worlds.

She didn't need to. She could feel it watching anyway.
