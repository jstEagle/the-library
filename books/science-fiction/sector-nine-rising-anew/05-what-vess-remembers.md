## 5. What Vess Remembers

Vess had been a prisoner of the Kestrel's Wake for six hours before anyone offered her tea.

It wasn't good tea. It was the kind of tea that tasted like it had been brewed from the sweepings of a cargo hold floor and then apologized to, but it came in a real cup, in a real galley, poured by a clone who watched her over the rim of her own mug like Vess might bolt for the airlock at any moment.

"Drink," Edmund said. "You look like you're about to faint, and I don't want to fill out the paperwork."

"Do freighters have paperwork for stowaways?"

"This one does. It's one line long." She sat down across the little table, and the magnetic latch on her chair caught with a soft clunk that seemed, to Vess, unnecessarily final. "Start talking. The Meridian. How do you know her name?"

*Her* name. Not *its*. Vess wrapped both hands around the cup because they needed something honest to do.

"I served on her."

"The Meridian never flew. Decommissioned on the slips at Calloway before her keel cooled. That's the record."

"The record's wrong." Vess took a sip and immediately regretted it. "The record is *designed* to be wrong. That's rather the whole point of it."

Edmund didn't move. That was the thing about her that kept catching Vess off guard—the stillness. Rhea Coldharbour had been all motion, all gesture, hands always half-raised toward some problem only she could see. This woman sat like a held breath. Like someone who had learned early that moving too fast got you noticed.

"You were crew," Edmund said.

"For eleven months." Vess set the cup down. "Junior systems engineer. Which meant I did what the senior engineer told me, and the senior engineer did what the captain told her, and the captain—" she stopped, and found she had to start again from further back than she'd planned. "The captain was Rhea Coldharbour. Your original."

The galley hummed. Somewhere aft, a coolant pump cycled with a sound like a patient sigh.

"And you recognized my name on a corrupted manifest," Edmund said slowly, "and your response was to hide in my cargo bay among the agricultural drones."

"In fairness, the alternative was staying on Calloway, where a Consortium liaison had just started asking after me by name."

"That tracks," Edmund admitted, and there—just for a second—a flicker of something dry and familiar crossed her face, and Vess's chest hurt with how much it looked like Rhea.

*Don't do that,* she wanted to say. *Don't be her when you don't mean to be.*

* * *

"So tell me about the last voyage," Edmund said. "All of it. And Vess—if I catch you shaping the story to make yourself look better, I'll know."

"How?"

"Because I'll remember it differently."

Vess laughed before she could stop herself, a short, startled sound. "Right. Yes. The inheritance." She turned her mug in a slow circle. "You have to understand, none of us knew what you were. What any of you were. Seven crew, and we thought we were seven people."

Edmund went very still. "Seven."

"The full complement. Captain Coldharbour; first mate Okonkwo-Dai; navigator Sefa; engineer Brenner—that was my boss; comms officer Lark; medic Ansel; and cargo master Pell." She recited them the way you recite a prayer you no longer believe in but can't bear to drop. "Eleven months together. You get to know people in eleven months. You learn how they take their coffee. You learn which ones snore. You learn—" Her voice snagged. She pushed through it. "You learn who they are. Or you think you do."

"And then?"

"And then the last run." Vess stared into the terrible tea as if it might show her the past. "We were hauling out of the Verge, deep lanes, the kind of route nobody takes unless the pay is triple or the questions are few. Ours was both. Sealed cargo, sealed manifests, the whole contract routed through three shell companies so tangled that even Brenner—who could untangle anything—gave up and called it 'the octopus.'"

"What was in it?"

"That's the thing. We never knew. Pell logged the mass, and the mass was wrong—too dense for its declared volume, and it drew power. Passive draw, tiny, constant, like something aboard was breathing slow." Vess looked up. "Brenner complained about the power draw for two weeks. Then she stopped complaining, and started being careful, and then she stopped talking about the cargo entirely, and I was young enough to think that was relief."

"It wasn't."

"No. It was instructions. Somebody had gotten to her, or to all of us, quietly, one at a time. Carry the box. Don't ask about the box. And the destination—" Vess exhaled. "Sector Nine. The relay."

Edmund's hand had stopped moving entirely. "Nobody goes to Nine. Nine's been dead since the Silence."

"We knew. That was the job. Drop the cargo at the relay, let it integrate with the relay's systems, and come home rich enough to retire." Vess's mouth twisted. "Rhea hated it. Not the danger—I think she'd have flown into a star if the pay was right and the crew voted yes. She hated not knowing. She pulled every string she had, spent half our bonus money on information brokers, and what she got back scared her badly enough that she changed course without telling anyone."

"Changed course where?"

"Nowhere. That's what I mean. She altered our heading by four degrees for six hours, long enough to pass close to a dead listening post, and she downloaded everything that post had ever recorded. Forty years of traffic data from before the Silence. Including—" Vess hesitated. Here it was. The part she'd rehearsed in the dark of the cargo bay, and it still wouldn't come out clean. "Including records of clone transports. Registry numbers, manifests, destinations. Dozens of ships. Hundreds of names. All of them flagged with the same internal code."

"What code?"

"One word. *Redundant.*"

The word dropped into the galley and lay there between them. Edmund's face did something complicated and controlled, the way a hull does under stress—no visible failure, but you could hear it holding.

"Go on," Edmund said.

"After the download, Rhea changed. She stopped sleeping. She ran the crew through emergency drills nobody had ordered. She started copying things—ship logs, her own personal files, the post's data—onto physical storage, the old kind, crystal wafer, nothing networked. And one night in the mess, after Brenner had gone forward, she sat down next to me and said—" Vess closed her eyes to hold the memory steady. "She said, *'Vess, if anything happens to me, find whoever comes after. They always send someone after.'* And I said, *after what?* And she smiled at me, this awful tired smile, and she said: *'They erase what remembers. So remember harder.'*"

Silence. The coolant pump sighed again.

"She knew," Edmund said at last, very quietly. "She knew what she was. Before the end."

"She suspected. And whatever she found at that listening post confirmed it hard enough that she started treating her own crew like witnesses to protect instead of family to trust." Vess opened her eyes. "That's the part that shames me. Not that I missed it. That when she got strange, I was relieved. I thought, good, the captain's finally taking things seriously. I didn't understand she was saying goodbye."

* * *

They'd moved to the cockpit without either of them quite deciding to. Edmund stood at the viewport with her arms folded, looking out at jumpspace's grey nothing, and Vess sat in the second seat feeling like a ghost giving testimony at her own haunting.

"The relay," Edmund said. "You actually docked with it."

"Twelve hours. Rhea and Lark went inside with the cargo. The rest of us stayed aboard. Comms were jammed—not broken, *jammed*, deliberate, tight-beam interference from somewhere we couldn't source. When they came back, Rhea was different again. Calmer. Worse than the not-sleeping. Calm like a woman who'd finished a sentence." Vess rubbed her arms. "Three days later we made port at Halloway Station, and Rhea ordered everyone ashore for mandatory leave. First time in eleven months. She stayed with the ship alone."

"And?"

"And I don't know." Vess's voice cracked and she hated it. "I was planetside forty hours when the Meridian's transponder went dark. By the time I got back to the berth, the ship was gone—towed, impounded, *decommissioned*, depending on which clerk you asked. No wreckage. No bodies. Six crew unaccounted for, and the seventh—" she gestured helplessly at Edmund, "—the seventh apparently walking around with her memories in a new body, minus the parts that would explain anything."

Edmund turned from the viewport. "My memories end at Halloway. Shore leave. A bar called the Tin Lantern. Then nothing until I woke up in a growth vat on Ceres with a life already installed in my head like furniture." She paused. "Except that's not true, is it? There are edges. Things I remember that don't fit the installed life. A corridor humming at a frequency I've never heard anywhere else. The smell of ozone and old paper. Someone saying *'remember harder'* in a voice I now realize is yours."

Vess's breath left her in a rush. "She wiped you. Before they came. She cut the dangerous parts out of your inheritance and left the rest standing, like stripping wiring out of a wall before the inspectors arrive."

"Or like hiding a key," Edmund said, "where only a locksmith would think to look."

For a moment neither of them spoke. Vess became aware that she was crying, quietly, without drama, the way you cry when grief is old and has learned manners. Edmund watched her with an expression Vess couldn't read—and then crossed the cockpit and, awkwardly, gently, put a hand on her shoulder.

"You loved her," Edmund said.

"Everyone loved her. That was the problem. It made her easy to follow and impossible to save." Vess laughed wetly. "And now I'm sitting here talking to her face on a stranger, and I don't know whether I'm comforting you or grieving her or just—"

"Both," Edmund said. "Probably both. I get that too, sometimes. Looking in mirrors."

* * *

Later—much later, ship's-night, the corridors dimmed to their amber standby glow—Vess lay in the guest bunk she'd been assigned rather than locked up in, staring at the ceiling, and heard footsteps in the passage. She tensed out of habit. But it was only Edmund, stopping outside the open door, silhouetted against the amber light.

"I tested your story," Edmund said. "Against what I carry."

Vess sat up. "And?"

"The octopus cargo. The listening post. The jamming at the relay. The shore leave at Halloway." A pause. "It's all there. In fragments. Shredded, but the pieces match the shape of what you told me, which means you didn't invent it, because you couldn't have invented the pieces." Another pause, longer. "There's one thing you didn't mention."

"Tell me."

"A name. In the fragments, there's a name attached to the man who came aboard the Meridian at Halloway while the crew was ashore. My memory keeps it the way a tongue keeps a broken tooth." Edmund's voice was flat and careful, a person handling something live. "Serevan."

Vess felt the temperature of the room change. "The Consortium liaison. From Calloway. The one asking after me."

"The same."

"Oh," Vess said, very softly. "Oh, that's not a coincidence. That's a leash."

Before Edmund could answer, the ship's ancient AI woke throughout the hull—not the alarm tone, but the other one, the rare one, the one PATCH used exactly twice in Vess's time aboard, both times for weather. Its voice filled the corridors, mild and apologetic as ever:

*"Beg pardon, Captain. Long-range array's caught something repeating out of the deep dark. Bearing Sector Nine. It's fragmented, degraded, and I may be imagining things in my old age—but I do believe it's a voice. And Captain, I've cross-checked the voiceprint against the ship's oldest crew registry."*

A pause, delicately theatrical, the way only a machine older than both of them could manage.

*"It's hers."*
