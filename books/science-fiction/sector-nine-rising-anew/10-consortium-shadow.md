## 10. Consortium Shadow

Edmund felt the shadow before she saw it.

That was the thing about the dark lanes—the stretches between beacons where nothing moved but you and your own engine noise. Out there, silence had a texture. She'd spent six years learning it the way a musician learns a room: where it echoed, where it swallowed sound, where a wrong note would hang in the air like a smell.

The silence was wrong tonight.

She sat in the Kestrel's Wake's cockpit with her boots up on the console, a mug of reconstituted tea going cold beside her elbow, pretending to read a freight contract she'd already read twice. Behind her, in the galley, Vess was doing something to a protein printer that involved a wrench and increasingly creative swearing. The ship hummed its old familiar hum around them both.

And underneath the hum, so faint she might have imagined it, something else. A pressure at the edge of perception. Like being watched through frosted glass.

"Vess," she said, without raising her voice. "How attached are you to that printer?"

"Deeply. It's the only thing on this ship that respects me." A clank. "Why?"

"Because in about four minutes I'm going to ask you to stop making noise."

The swearing stopped. Footsteps, then Vess's head appeared in the cockpit doorway—dark hair tied back with what looked suspiciously like a length of coolant line, grease on one cheekbone, eyes already sharpening into the question she hadn't asked yet. She'd been aboard eleven days now, long enough that Edmund had stopped flinching when she heard someone else breathing in the corridors. Long enough that the flinching's absence frightened her more than the presence ever had.

"You've got that face," Vess said.

"What face?"

"The one where you look calm but your jaw is doing arithmetic."

Edmund almost smiled. Almost. Instead she keyed the passive array and let the feed bloom across the main display: a starfield rendered in cold false-color, the debris shoal of the Calloway Verge sliding past off their port bow—a graveyard of hulls from some war nobody alive had fought, tumbling slow and patient in the dark.

"There," she said, and touched the edge of the display where, if you weren't looking for it, there was simply nothing.

Vess leaned closer. Squinted. "Nothing."

"Nothing that occludes." Edmund traced the outline with one finger. A patch of stars, subtly too few. A smudge of infrared that could have been a sensor artifact, except sensor artifacts didn't hold formation with your own course corrections across forty minutes. "Something's back there running cold. No transponder, no drive flare, riding our wake exactly. If I adjust, it adjusts. If I drift, it drifts."

"How long?"

"Since we cleared Threnody. Maybe longer. I only caught it because it got greedy coming out of the station's shadow—it wanted to keep us in view and the geometry wouldn't let it do both."

Vess straightened up slowly. "Consortium?"

"Serevan let us go too easily at the boarding inspection. People like him don't let go. They hand you slack and wait to see which direction you run." Edmund took a slow sip of the cold tea and grimaced. "We ran toward Sector Nine. So now they follow."

"And they'll keep following until—"

"Until we jump somewhere they can pin down, or until they decide following is boring and start shooting." She set the mug down. "I'd rather not wait for their decision."

* * *

They killed the lights first. All of them—cabin strips, galley, the soft amber glow over the nav station. The Kestrel's Wake went dark as a closed eye, running on battery bleed and the faint blue ghost-light of the displays dimmed to embers. In the sudden black, the ship felt older and smaller, a wooden boat on an ink sea.

"Talk to me about the drives," Edmund said.

Vess's voice came out of the darkness near the engineering hatch, low and steady. "Main drive's healthy. Thrusters are thrusters—they're thirty years old and they complain, but they answer. Jump core needs ninety minutes to cycle after the last burn, so whatever we do, we do it inside normal space."

"Ninety minutes." Edmund pulled up the local chart and studied it until the lines burned themselves into the backs of her eyes. The Calloway Verge spread out ahead of them like a frozen explosion: eleven thousand registered wrecks, and probably three times that unregistered, drifting in slow ellipses across a span of empty space half a light-hour wide. Somewhere beyond it, the lane bent toward the Sector Nine boundary—and toward the relay, and toward everything waiting there.

A conventional pilot would go around. Around cost fuel and time, and time was the one thing the shadow behind them was spending generously.

A conventional pilot also wasn't carrying a dead woman's memories and a stowaway the Consortium wanted badly enough to send hunters.

"We're going through," Edmund said.

Silence from the engineering hatch. Then: "Through the Verge."

"Through the Verge."

"That's not a route, Edmund. That's a dare."

"It's better than a dare. It's math." She rotated the chart, and the debris field turned like a slow carousel. "Hunter vessels are built for open-lane pursuit. Big sensor suites, big engines, crews trained to chase things that run in straight lines. But all that sensitivity works against them in clutter—they can't burn hard without lighting up every rock between here and the horizon, and they can't thread tight debris without risking a hull they can't afford to explain losing." She tapped the densest knot of wreckage, deep in the field's heart, where the old war had been at its most enthusiastic. "We're small. We're ugly. We were built to blunder through places like this hauling scrap. If I fly it right, they either lose us or break themselves trying not to."

"And if you fly it wrong?"

Edmund was quiet for a moment. Inherited memory stirred—not the murder, not tonight, but smaller things: hands on a yoke that had been hers before they were hers, muscle memory written into cloned nerves by a woman who'd flown these lanes first. Sometimes those fragments felt like theft. Tonight they felt like tools.

"If I fly it wrong," she said, "we find out very quickly whether the insurance company honors claims filed posthumously."

"That's not funny."

"It's a little funny."

"It's really not." But Edmund heard Vess moving—strapping in, checking seals, doing the quiet competent things that said *I'm scared and I'm staying anyway*. Eleven days ago this woman had been a stranger hiding in her cargo bay. Now her voice in the dark was the steadiest thing on the ship. Edmund decided not to examine what that meant. There would be time for examining later, if there was a later.

"Hold onto something," Edmund said, and lit the drive.

* * *

The first hour was patience.

She took them in shallow, along the Verge's ragged skirt, letting the wreck density build gradually—shuttles first, then freighter spines snapped like celery, then the true leviathans: capital hulls the size of towns, split open and gutted, their interiors exposed to vacuum like anatomical diagrams. The Wake slipped through their shadows one by one, and Edmund flew with a delicacy that would have surprised anyone who'd only seen her dock a freighter with two tons of attitude and a sarcastic wave.

Behind them, the shadow held. Patient. Professional. Matching every turn at a distance of nine thousand kilometers, never closing, never falling back.

"They're good," Vess reported from engineering, watching her own repeater displays. "Whoever's flying that thing isn't a hired thug. That's Consortium fleet training."

"I know." Edmund's hands rested light on the controls, barely touching. "Good is a problem. Good means they won't make mistakes."

"So what's the plan when good doesn't make mistakes?"

"The plan is to make the environment make mistakes for them." She checked the jump core readout. Sixty-one minutes to cycle. Her stomach did a small cold calculation and filed it away. "There's a wreck ahead. Registry says it was called the Anvil of Meru. Battlecruiser. Two kilometers of dead ship, broken into three major sections, tumbling in a slow rotation. On the far side of it, the debris gets thick enough to hide a fleet."

"You want to use it as cover."

"I want to use it as a curtain. And then—" she pulled up the maneuver she'd been building in her head for the last hour, the one she'd been telling herself wasn't insane, "—then I want to slingshot."

Vess appeared in the cockpit doorway again, strapped into a harness she'd clearly rigged herself from cargo webbing. "Define slingshot."

Edmund rotated the plot. "The Anvil's largest section has a rotational period of about fourteen minutes. Its mass is enough to matter, if you come in close and fast. I take us down its shadow side, skim inside its rotation, and let its gravity whip us around the far edge while I burn the whole way. We come out the other side going significantly faster than anything our size should be going, in a direction the hunter can't predict, directly into the deep debris. By the time they round the wreck, we're a smear of noise buried in ten thousand contacts."

"And the ship?" Vess's voice was carefully neutral. "What happens to the ship during 'significantly faster than anything our size should be going'?"

"She complains."

"Edmund."

"She *complains*, Vess. Frame stress, probably. Something rattles loose, maybe. I'll keep the burn under structural tolerance—mostly under. Mostly is a word I'm choosing to believe in right now."

Vess stared at her for a long moment. The ember-glow of the displays caught the grease on her cheek and the white around her knuckles where she gripped the doorframe. "You know what my last captain used to say?"

"What?"

"'Any maneuver you can't brief in under a minute is a maneuver you shouldn't fly.'"

"He sounds wise."

"He died in a docking accident, so maybe not that wise." Vess exhaled, long and slow, and Edmund watched her make the decision the way you watch someone step off a ledge and trust the air. "Do it. But I'm bracing in engineering, next to the drive housing, because if something lets go I want to be the one who hears it first."

"Deal."

"Oh—and Edmund?" Vess paused in the corridor, half-turned. "For what it's worth. I've flown with a lot of pilots. None of them would try this. Most of them couldn't."

Edmund kept her eyes on the plot. "Most of them aren't me."

"No," Vess said softly, and was gone. "They're not."

* * *

The Anvil of Meru grew in the viewport like a mountain range lying on its side.

Up close, the wreck stopped being a shape on a chart and became a place—a country of torn metal and frozen atmosphere vents, of blast-scars the size of cities, of a command tower still standing proud from the forward section like a lighthouse for a coast where no ships would ever again come home. Edmund brought the Kestrel's Wake in along its shadowed flank, close enough that the wreck filled half the sky, close enough to see individual hull plates the size of playing fields peeling away in the slow wind of vacuum.

Eight thousand meters. Six. Four.

"Rotation sync in twenty seconds," she murmured. "Drive standing by at full. Nobody breathe anything expensive."

In the engineering channel, Vess's voice, tight and bright: "Ready when you are, captain."

Captain. Not *you clone freak*, not *the original*, not any of the names people used when they wanted to remind her what she was. Just captain. Edmund tucked the word somewhere safe and put both hands on the controls.

The wreck's rotation swept its broken stern section across their path like the arm of a gate. Edmund waited—one heartbeat, two, feeling the timing in her body the way she felt weather—and then dropped the nose and burned.

The Kestrel's Wake dove.

Gravity took hold of them, gentle at first and then not gentle at all, the dead battlecruiser's mass reaching out with invisible hands. The viewport became a blur of hurtling metal. Alarms woke across the console and Edmund silenced them one by one, calmly, the way you'd shoo cats off a table. The frame groaned—a long, deep complaint from the keel, the sound of a ship being asked a question it had never been asked before.

"Fifty percent over rated thrust," Vess shouted over the comm. "Frame stress climbing! You're at yellow, Edmund, we are solidly at yellow!"

"Yellow's a color," Edmund said through her teeth, "not a wall."

She rode the curve. The wreck's rotation whipped past beneath them—close enough that the drive wash scorched frost off a gun turret older than both their grandmothers—and then the curve bent, and bent, and broke, and the Kestrel's Wake came around the Anvil's shattered flank like a stone leaving a sling.

Edmund cut the burn, flipped them end over end in a shriek of thrusters, and burned again—hard, flat, straight into the deepest throat of the debris field, engines screaming against the mass of the wreck behind them, velocity stacking up in numbers the little freighter had no business knowing.

Behind them, in the displays, the hunter vessel rounded the Anvil of Meru forty seconds later.

Forty seconds is a long time, in a fight. It's a lifetime, in a slingshot. By the time the hunter's sensors cleared the wreck's interference, the Kestrel's Wake was gone—swallowed by the Verge, one more flicker of reflected light among ten thousand, decelerating hard behind a tumbling colony barge, then drifting silent, dark, and cold in the lee of a shattered fuel depot, while somewhere behind them the pursuit's drive flare bloomed and hunted empty space.

* * *

It was, Edmund reflected afterward, mostly a victory.

The *mostly* arrived about ninety seconds after they went dark, when something in the aft frame let go with a bang that sounded like the ship biting through a steel cable. Vess's voice came over the comm immediately, breathless and strange: "Coolant line's ruptured in bay two. And, uh. Edmund? The number-three thrust vector assembly is... loose."

"Loose how?"

"Loose in the sense that it's currently held on by two bolts and its own stubbornness. Loose in the sense that I would not recommend using that thruster for anything more aggressive than apologizing to it."

Edmund floated in the dark cockpit, strapped into her chair, adrenaline draining out of her blood in slow cold tides. Her hands, she noticed distantly, were shaking—not from fear, she realized, but from the effort of having refused to shake for three hours. Fierce competence, it turned out, ran on a fuel that had to be paid back later, with interest.

"Status," she said, when she trusted her voice.

"Drives functional. Life support fine. We're hidden, we're alive, and we're limping." A pause, and then Vess laughed—a short, disbelieving, giddy sound. "We actually lost them. You threw a freighter around a battlecruiser and *lost them*. Who taught you to fly like that?"

Nobody, Edmund thought. Somebody. Me. A dead woman whose hands mine are copies of.

"Some lessons you inherit," she said instead.

She unstrapped and drifted to the viewport. Beyond the glass, the debris of the Calloway Verge turned in its ancient slow dance, and beyond that, invisible, the dark lanes stretched toward Sector Nine—toward the relay, the signal, the voice that was somehow her own. The hunter was out there still, somewhere, licking its wounds and reporting to Serevan. They hadn't escaped. They'd bought time. Time was a currency, and Edmund knew exactly how poor she was.

But in the viewport's reflection she saw Vess appear in the cockpit doorway, bruised, filthy, grinning like a woman who'd bet everything on a bad idea and watched it fly.

And behind Vess, faintly, the ship's ancient intercom crackled with static that almost sounded like words.

"Vess," Edmund said slowly. "Was the intercom always doing that?"

Vess's grin faded. She cocked her head, listening.

The static shaped itself, thin and dry and unmistakably deliberate, into a single word:

"*Ouch.*"
