## 7. Eleven Days of Evidence

**MARGUERITE**

Day 401. The first day of my life in which I committed a crime before breakfast.

I want it noted—I want *something* noted, even if only this log—that the crime was small and administrative in nature. I falsified a maintenance requisition. I wrote that the reactor vault's secondary access corridor required inspection for "seismic microfracturing," which gave me, on paper, a legitimate reason to be down there with a sample kit and a very good excuse.

The original Marguerite would have been proud. Or horrified. Her memories offer both opinions simultaneously, like two sisters arguing over one shoulder.

Ilsa was waiting for me at the vault door at 0500, breath fogging in the cold, holding a thermocanister of tea like a weapon she intended to use on anyone who objected to her presence.

"You didn't have to come," I said.

"I've been transmitting into that vault for six years," she said. "You don't get to excavate my grandmother's tomb without me."

There was no argument I could make against that, so I made tea instead.

The bone fragments were where the original's memories said they would be—beneath the coolant runoff channel, under forty centimeters of slagcrete poured in a hurry by people who wanted the floor flat and nothing else. We pried up a section near the third support strut, and there they were: small, porous, unmistakably human. Ilsa knelt over them for a long time without touching them, and when she finally did, she handled each fragment the way you handle a sleeping child.

"There are more," she said. It wasn't a question. The original's memory supplied the answer: a second channel, a fourth strut, a cavity behind the heat exchanger that the corporate survey had marked, in the original's own handwriting, as "void—no significance." She had signed that lie herself. I carry her signature in my head. Some mornings I can feel the pen.

We worked until the shift bell. By then we had eleven bone fragments, three teeth, and—wrapped in oilcloth with a care that stopped us both—a child's shoe. Leather, hand-stitched, sized for someone perhaps five years old. Someone had buried it deliberately, separately, the way you bury a thing you cannot bear to throw away but cannot bear to look at either.

Ilsa held it in both hands and said, in the old language, something soft. I only understood half of it. The half I understood was *remember*.

**ILSA**

Here is what nobody tells you about digging up your family's massacre: it's boring most of the time. Sifting. Labeling. Arguing about chain-of-custody protocols with a man who has an entire dead woman's memories and still can't remember which crate the evidence bags are in.

"Third shelf," I told him. "Left of the radiation dosimeters. You looked right past them twice."

"I was distracted," Marguerite said, with dignity, "by the dosimeters."

That's the thing about him nobody would believe from Earth: he's funny. Not on purpose, mostly. He has the humor of a person who learned all his jokes from bureaucratic manuals and inherited grief, and somehow it lands. On day four of the excavation, when he mislabeled a bag of ash as "organic residue (probable)" and I pointed out that probable was doing a lot of work in that sentence, he laughed—an actual laugh, startled out of him—and I thought: oh. Oh no. This is inconvenient.

Because we were running out of days. Nine left when we found the shoe. Eight when we found the data core.

The core was the original Marguerite's masterpiece. She'd hidden it inside a decommissioned atmospheric scrubber in the vault's maintenance alcove, behind a panel she'd sealed with a weld pattern only her muscle memory could reproduce—which meant only *his* muscle memory. I stood back and watched him work, his hands moving without hesitation, and it was the strangest thing I'd ever seen: a dead woman's ghost guiding a living man's fingers, both of them wearing the same face I'd fallen—

Well. Both of them wearing the same face.

The core held everything. Survey records from the Founding year. Medical logs of workers registered as "equipment" on the manifests. A list of four hundred and six names, and beside each name, in the original's careful hand, a cause of death that the official record had replaced with *relocated*. And audio. She'd recorded the order coming down from Earth: *Purge non-registered personnel prior to charter ratification. Method discretionary. Documentation prohibited.*

Documentation prohibited. So she documented. That was her whole crime. That was why there were bones under the reactor and a clone wearing her face.

I cried, and he held the light steady for me because his hands, being partly hers, knew how to be useful while mine weren't. Then we got back to work. There were eight days left, and four hundred and six names, and every one of them needed to be verifiable by someone who wasn't us.

* * *

**MARGUERITE**

The auditors arrived on day seven. Two days early.

Their shuttle came down through the dust haze at 0630, unannounced, and my terminal lit with their credentials before their boots hit the tarmac: Voss and Adeyemi, Corporate Oversight Division, Sector Nine Decommissioning Verification. Their photographs showed two people with the specific freshness of individuals who have never missed a meal or questioned an instruction.

"Administrator Marchetti," Voss said, shaking my hand with a grip calibrated to communicate something. "You've been busy down here. Unusual diagnostic activity on the reactor systems."

"Pre-decommissioning due diligence," I said. My voice did not shake. I checked—it did not shake. Somewhere in my inherited reflexes was a woman who had lied to these same people for years, and apparently some of her steadiness had survived the copying. "Standard protocol."

"Of course." He smiled. "We'll need full access to the vault."

"Radiation protocols require a forty-eight-hour clearance window after any internal survey," I said. This was true. I had verified it was true specifically so I could say it. "I can schedule your access for Thursday."

Adeyemi frowned at her tablet. "Our orders say immediate."

"Our orders," I said pleasantly, "say compliance with safety regulation 9-Vault-114, which supersedes scheduling preferences. Unless Oversight has formally exempted itself from its own safety code? I can file that request tonight, if you like. It makes for interesting reading in tribunal archives."

I had never said the word *tribunal* out loud before. Voss's smile stayed exactly where it was, which told me he'd heard it.

They took the guest quarters. They ate in the mess hall, politely, asking settlers cheerful questions about hydroponics yields. And that evening Ilsa found me in the admin office, closed the door, and said, "You lied beautifully."

"It's the first lie I've ever told them."

"No." She sat on the edge of my desk, close enough that I could see the exhaustion under her eyes. "You've been lying since the day you were decanted. Today was just the first time you picked your own side."

I didn't have an answer for that, so I gave her tea instead. It's becoming a habit. I'm not going to examine it.

* * *

**ILSA**

On day five we started teaching the names to say themselves.

Marguerite's idea, though he credited the original. Four hundred and six names, read aloud in the old language, recorded—one voice per name, so that no single transmission carried the whole list and no single seizure could take it from us. The settlers came in shifts to the hydroponics bay, where the plants hid us from casual observation and the humidity covered the sound. Old Tomás Reyes, who is ninety-one and pretends he only speaks Standard, recited his great-aunt's name in flawless pidgin and then pretended he hadn't. The teenagers treated it like a game until the third shift, when little Ama Osei read the name of the shoe's owner—recovered from the data core, a girl named Bette Okonkwo-Ferrante, age five—and nobody treated anything like a game after that.

Marguerite read names too. His voice fits the language strangely, like a coat cut for someone else, but he reads them anyway, every evening, and he's getting better. Last night he stumbled through a blessing formula and I corrected his vowels, standing close enough to feel the warmth off him, and he looked at me and forgot what he was saying mid-sentence, and I confess I enjoyed it enormously. There's not much room for enjoyment in an excavation of genocide. You take it where you find it.

Two days ago he asked me, quietly, what happens to him after. After the broadcast, after the tribunal, after. Whether a clone gets to be a witness or just evidence.

"You're a witness," I told him. "Evidence doesn't make tea."

He smiled at his hands. I'm keeping count of those smiles. Somebody should; he certainly won't.

Tonight the auditors requested, again, vault access. Tomorrow, Marguerite says, he runs out of regulations to hide behind. Tonight he sits across from me translating the last of the medical logs, lamplight on the face I love in both its versions, and neither of us says the obvious thing: that we might not finish. That the countdown doesn't care about our vowels.

Then his terminal chimed. He read it, and went very still, and turned the screen toward me.

Message from Voss, timestamped nine minutes ago:

*Administrator — Oversight has completed an independent review of your diagnostic query history. Please report to the guest quarters at 0800 to discuss anomalies in your behavioral baseline. Bring nothing.*

*Bring nothing,* I said aloud.

"Yes," Marguerite said. He was looking at the message the way the original must have looked at her last sunrise—with full knowledge, and no intention whatsoever of obeying. "I believe," he said quietly, "that tomorrow morning, I finally meet the person who killed me."
