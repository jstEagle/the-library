## 2. Customs at Ceres Gate

Ceres Gate hung in the dark like a wedding cake built by a paranoid architect: three rings of docking bays, scanning arrays bristling from every surface, and a spine of administrative towers running down its axis where the inspectors lived and worked and, if the rumors were true, occasionally slept. The station rotated slow enough that its lights never seemed to move, which gave the whole structure the quality of something frozen mid-fall. Xanthe had been coming through it for eleven years, and every time he saw it he thought the same thought: *they built all this just to look inside other people's pockets.*

He brought the Lark Ascending in on a cargo lane, transponder squawking honest and dull. *Independent hauler, registered Thetis Shipyards, manifest: agricultural polymer feedstock, forty tonnes, origin Kestrel Belt.* The polymer was real. It sat in his forward hold in honest grey drums, smelling faintly of solvent, boring as rain. Boring was armor. You didn't get boarded for boring.

The contraband rode beneath it.

Twelve memory-crystals, sealed in lead-lined cases under the deck plating of the aft compartment, each one holding a pressed slice of somebody's life from the war years — the war whose name nobody used, the war the archives had sanded down to a paragraph about "regional instability." Some of them were probably nothing. A soldier's leave. A farmer watching smoke on a horizon. But one of them, the one his contact on Kestrel had handled like it was made of eggshell, had come out of a Sector Records vault with the Authority's own seal broken across its casing, and crystals like that had a way of making people disappear. Including the people carrying them.

Xanthe's rules were simple, and he recited them to himself the way other men said prayers. Never open the cargo. Never ask whose memories. Never carry anything you can't afford to lose — including yourself. He'd added that last one himself, year six, after a close call at Vesta Deep that still showed up sometimes in his dreams wearing different faces.

The Gate's traffic control assigned him Bay 214, outer ring, and a voice like warm gravel told him to stand by for inspection. Standard. Everything here was standard until it wasn't.

* * *

He dressed for the part while the clamps took hold. Coveralls, patched at both knees because honest haulers patched their knees. A toolbag with real tools in it, worn handles, grease worked into the seams. His ship's papers in a folio soft enough at the corners to look carried rather than printed. And the manifest — his masterpiece, this run — stamped with a Kestrel Belt authority chop he'd bought from a dying forger who'd spent forty years perfecting the wobble in the ink roller. Machines could be fooled. It was people who needed art.

The airlock cycled, and Inspector Odile Ferran came aboard.

He knew her by reputation before he knew her face. Ferran, Ceres Gate, four years without a single missed shipment on her watch — which meant either she was the best inspector in the Nine Sectors or she was taking a cut, and the betting pools in the wrecker bars gave even odds. She was younger than her file suggested, mid-thirties maybe, dark hair cut short and practical, a scanner unit slung across her back like a rifle she'd grown tired of carrying. She stopped three meters inside his airlock and looked at him the way a surgeon looks at an X-ray.

"Captain Vane." She consulted a slate without really reading it. "Thetis-registered. Eleven transits through this Gate in six years. No violations. No detentions. Not so much as a late filing."

"I'm very boring," Xanthe said. "It's my best quality."

Something moved at the corner of her mouth — not quite a smile, more like the place a smile would live if it ever visited. "We'll see. Walk me through your load."

He walked her through it. Forward hold, polymer feedstock, forty drums, batch numbers matching the manifest. He talked the way he always talked during inspections: steady, helpful, a little too fond of the technical details of polymer logistics, the kind of man who genuinely loved his cargo because his cargo was the only interesting thing about him. Boredom was a language, and he was fluent.

Ferran listened with her head tilted, and he realized after a minute that she wasn't listening to the words. She was listening to the rhythm. Looking for the hitch, the half-second pause where a lie gets dressed.

"You've done this before," she said.

"Eleven times through your Gate, Inspector. You said so yourself."

"That's not what I mean." She unslung the scanner and thumbed it awake. "Most haulers hate inspections. They tolerate them. You *perform* yours. Like a man who's rehearsed being innocent so long he's gotten good at it."

The words landed with a precision that made his spine hum. He kept his face doing what it was supposed to do — mild affront shading into professional amusement. "Inspector, I perform everything. It's how I stay likable. Ask anyone."

"No one aboard to ask?"

"Crew costs money. Polymer doesn't complain."

"Mm." She turned toward the forward hold, and he followed, and behind them the airlock irised shut with a sound like a held breath.

* * *

She scanned the drums first, of course. Of course she did. The reader chirped along the batch codes and found them clean, because they were clean, because the drums were exactly what they claimed to be. Then she did the thing that separated her from every other inspector he'd ever stood in front of: she stopped scanning the cargo and started scanning the ship.

Her wand drifted along bulkhead seams. Along conduit runs. Along the deck plating of the aft compartment, where it paused — one second, two — over the exact spot where twelve crystals lay sleeping under eight centimeters of hull steel and a false floor he'd welded himself.

Xanthe breathed. In through the nose, out slow, the way you do when the alternative is letting your pulse climb into a range a good inspector can hear in your voice.

"Lead shielding," Ferran said. Her tone hadn't changed at all. Conversational. Almost idle. "In the deck plating."

"Radiation hardening," Xanthe said. "Kestrel route runs close to the Belt's inner edge. Thetis ships are spec'd for it. You can check the yard records."

"I have checked the yard records." She straightened and looked at him, and up close her eyes were a brown so dark it read as black under the hold lights, and they were not unkind eyes, which somehow made them worse. "Your ship's radiation hardening is standard Thetis issue. Standard Thetis issue uses borated composite. Borated composite doesn't show up as lead on a density scan." She tapped the deck once with her boot heel. "Somebody re-plated this section. Recently. The welds are better than factory work, which means it wasn't the factory."

The silence stretched. Somewhere in the ship's guts, a coolant pump cycled, absurdly loud.

Here was the moment, Xanthe knew, where careers ended. Where men reached for blasters or lies too big to carry, and inspectors called security, and freighters got torn down to their ribs in search bays while their owners learned what Sector detention smelled like. He felt the old thrill rise in him anyway — the same electric lift he got every time the net tightened, the part of himself he liked least, the part that had chosen this life precisely because it made his heart beat like this. That was the cost of invisibility nobody warned you about: eventually you couldn't feel alive without almost being caught.

He decided to tell her a smaller truth.

"You're right," he said. "I re-plated it myself. Two years ago."

Ferran's eyebrows rose a fraction. Encouraged, he kept going, laying the story out flat and unhurried, the way you hand someone a knife handle-first.

"After the war — after the *instability* — there was a stretch when nobody inspected independent haulers. Nobody cared. And things moved through the lanes that shouldn't have. Weapons. Worse." He let his gaze drop to the deck, and here he gave her something real, mined from actual memory, because the best forgery is ninety percent true. "I bought this ship from a man who'd been running that kind of cargo. When I found the compartment, I didn't report it. Reporting it meant questions, and questions meant delays, and delays meant I don't eat. So I welded it shut and put lead under the floor, and I've hauled polymer through nine sectors ever since, and I have never once opened that compartment. Not to use it. Just never got around to cutting it out, because cutting it out means explaining why I waited two years." He shrugged, and made the shrug honest. "You're the first person I've ever told. Congratulations. Now you can arrest me for ship modification without a permit, which carries, what, a fine?"

Ferran studied him for a long time. The scanner hummed against her hip. Out in the bay, a tug groaned past the viewport, dragging a container the size of a chapel.

"The compartment's empty," she said. It wasn't quite a question.

"Scan it and see."

"I intend to." She knelt, produced a slim probe from her belt kit, and ran it along the seam where his weld met the original plate. The probe chirped. She ran it again, slower. Behind her composed face, Xanthe watched her mind working — he could see it, the way you see weather coming — and he understood that she already knew the compartment was empty, had known since the density scan, and that the entire conversation had been something else entirely. An audition. She'd wanted to see what he did when cornered.

He'd given her a confession instead of a fight. He hoped that counted for something. With some inspectors it counted against you; only liars cooperated that smoothly.

"The welds are good," she said finally, standing. "Better than good. You wasted a career on polymer feedstock, Captain Vane."

"My mother wanted me to be a doctor. I disappointed everyone equally."

That got the almost-smile again, fuller this time, there and gone. She clipped the probe back onto her belt and looked around the hold one last time — at the honest drums, the patched coveralls, the toolbag with its worn handles — and Xanthe watched her assemble a verdict out of all of it, and he understood, with the strange clarity that comes in the last second before a fall, that she was going to let him go. And that she knew. Not what was under the floor — the lead had eaten the scan, the cases were shielded, the crystals were ghosts. But that there was something. That the boring man with the beautiful welds was exactly the kind of quiet the Gate existed to catch, and she was letting him walk anyway, and he would probably never know why.

"Manifest checks out," she said, stamping his folio. "Batch codes match. Compartment's clean. You're cleared for transit, Captain." She handed the folio back, and her fingers held onto it one beat longer than necessary. "A word of free advice. That deck section? Cut it out. Whatever a man keeps hidden, even unused, even forgotten — it has a way of finding him. I've seen it happen. The cargo always comes home."

"I'll take it under advisement, Inspector."

"Do." She stepped toward the airlock, then paused, half-turned. "Odile Ferran," she said, as if completing a form. "Since we're being honest with each other now. Next time you come through, I'll remember your welds."

"And I'll remember yours," Xanthe said, before he could stop himself.

She looked at him for a moment — a long, level look that contained, somewhere deep in it, a warning wrapped in something that might have been respect — and then she was through the lock and gone, boots ringing on the bay gantry, leaving behind the faint smell of scanner ozone and the sensation of having survived something by a margin he couldn't measure.

* * *

He didn't move for ten minutes. He sat in the pilot's chair with his hands flat on his thighs and listened to the Lark Ascending breathe, and he made himself name what had just happened, because naming things defanged them. An inspector had found his false floor. An inspector had found his false floor and stamped him clean. Either Odile Ferran was corrupt, or she was curious, or — worst possibility, the one that made his stomach tighten — she was patient. The dangerous ones were always patient. They let the fish swim, and they remembered the shape of it.

He undogged the aft panel and lifted the deck plate, and there they were: twelve cases in the dark, cold and mute, each one a life folded small enough to fit in a palm. He didn't open them. Rule one. He never opened them. But tonight, for the first time in eleven years, he crouched over the compartment longer than it took to count them, and he wondered — actually wondered, with a feeling like a hairline crack running through old plaster — whose memories he was carrying, and why a Records vault seal had been broken to make them, and what it was about the war that made people press it into crystal and pay smugglers to keep it moving through the silence between worlds.

The comm chimed. Departure clearance. Ceres Gate releasing him back into the long dark.

"Acknowledged, Gate Control," he said, and his voice came out steady, and he hated how easy it still was.

The Lark Ascending pushed off from Bay 214 and fell away from the frozen wedding cake of the Gate, out past the scanning arrays, out past the traffic buoys, into the black between sectors where nobody looked inside anybody's pockets. Xanthe set his course for home port and told himself the run was over, the danger passed, the invisible man invisible again.

He was three hours out, watching the stars hold still, when the general band lit up with an emergency bulletin from Sector Nine Traffic Command, and the voice reading it was warm gravel, and the words were:

*All vessels divert attention. Derelict freighter Halcyon Wake entering controlled space. Repeat: the Halcyon Wake. Thirty years missing. Requesting all available—*

Xanthe's hands went still on the controls.

He knew that ship. Everyone above a certain age knew that ship. And whatever was coming home aboard it, thirty years late, out of the deep dark where no ship came back from — he already understood, with the certainty of a man who has spent his whole life learning what people hide, that it was going to crack the silence wide open.
