## 3. Children on an Empty Deck

The alert came through at 0347, and Odile Ferran was already awake, because she was always already awake at that hour, lying in her bunk aboard the customs cutter *Vigilant Meridian*—the name a small irony she'd stopped noticing years ago—and listening to the ship breathe. Ships breathed if you knew how to listen: the tick of cooling conduits, the sigh of recycled air through the scrubbers, the faint arrhythmia of the drive cycling down from patrol burn. Forty-one souls aboard the cutter, and every one of them pretending to sleep while the sector's silence pressed against the hull like deep water.

The comm chimed twice. Priority flag. Traffic Control, Ceres Gate.

She answered before the second chime finished.

"Ferran."

"Senior Inspector." The duty officer's voice had a strange texture to it, flat and too careful, the way voices went when someone was trying very hard not to sound afraid. "We've got a contact on the approach lanes. Transponder identifies as *Halcyon Wake*, registry Meridian Yards, class-seven ore freighter."

"Fine. Flag it for inspection like everything else."

"Inspector, that hull's been listed missing for thirty-one years."

Odile sat up. The bunk's thin blanket slid off her shoulders and the cold of the ship found her spine. "Say again."

"*Halcyon Wake*. Lost with all hands in the outer approaches, year thirty-two of the Quiet. No distress signal, no debris field, no wreckage ever recovered. It just... stopped appearing on our scopes one day and never came home." A pause, filled with the sound of someone swallowing. "It's coming home now. And Inspector—it's not answering hails. It's drifting. No drive signature, no reaction mass venting, nothing. Just momentum and a transponder that shouldn't exist anymore."

Odile was already pulling her boots on, tucking her uniform shirt in one-handed while she held the comm against her shoulder. "Course?"

"Decaying toward the shipping lanes. If nobody moves it, it crosses the Ceres Gate corridor in six hours. Right through the middle of morning traffic."

"Any other ships near it?"

"One. A tramp freighter out of Nine, some smuggler-class tub called the *Lark Ascending*, logged a visual pass at extreme range before diverting. Captain didn't stop, didn't hail, just filed a sighting report and burned for the gate. Probably thought we'd impound him for loitering near a derelict."

"Probably right," Odile said, and killed the call.

* * *

The *Halcyon Wake* resolved out of the dark like something surfacing from under black ice.

Odile stood at the *Vigilant*'s forward viewport, helmet under her arm, and looked at it for a long moment before anyone spoke. She had boarded dead ships before—that was part of the work, the unglamorous arithmetic of customs enforcement: seized hulls, abandoned mining rigs, once, memorably, a colony barge full of frozen colonists whose life-support contract had lapsed while the company argued over invoices. Dead ships had a grammar. They sagged. Their lights were wrong or absent. Frost feathered their viewports. You could read a dead ship across a kilometer of vacuum and know what you'd find inside.

This ship wasn't reading right.

Its running lights still cycled—navigation strobes pulsing white, red, white, in perfect regulation sequence, the way they would have on the day it vanished. Its hull was clean. Not maintained-clean; *new*-clean, or close to it, the paint unblistered by radiation, the sensor masts unbent, the drive bell unscarred. Thirty-one years adrift should have sandblasted it with micrometeorite dust, should have left it pitted and gray as driftwood. Instead it looked like a ship that had gone out yesterday and simply forgotten to come back.

"Thermal," said Ensign Okonkwo beside her, voice tight behind his visor. "Inspector, you're going to want to see this."

The thermal overlay bloomed across her HUD. Cold hull, cold drive, cold everything—except a single warm thread running through the ship's spine like a vein. Life support. Partial. Running on some reserve she couldn't identify, drawing power from nowhere.

"Something's alive in there," Okonkwo said.

"Something," Odile repeated. "Not someone."

"I didn't mean—" He stopped. Behind his faceplate his eyes were doing the thing eyes did when a man tried to laugh off dread and failed. "Boarding party's suited and ready, Inspector. Six of them. I'll lead."

"You'll stay here," Odile said. "I lead. That's an order with my name on it, which means if whatever's in that ship eats us, it eats me first and Command has to promote you to fill the paperwork."

Nobody laughed. She hadn't expected them to. She clipped her helmet on, felt the familiar kiss of seal pressure around her throat, and dropped into the boarding lock with her team falling in behind her—Vasquez and Duval on point, Chen and Adeyemi on the tether reels, Petrov dragging the med-kit he'd insisted on bringing against all protocol, because Petrov always brought the med-kit, because Petrov's grandmother had died on a derelict waiting for one.

The gap between the two ships was forty meters of nothing. Odile crossed it on the tether line with the stars wheeling slowly beneath her boots, and the whole way across she had the feeling—the irrational, unprofessional, skin-crawling feeling—of being watched. Not scanned. Not tracked. *Watched.* The way you're watched by a house when you walk past its windows at night.

The *Wake*'s main airlock accepted her override codes on the first try. Thirty-one years missing, and its locks answered Sector Nine authority like it had left yesterday.

That was the first impossible thing.

* * *

Inside, the air was breathable.

Her suit read it automatically: twenty-one percent oxygen, standard nitrogen balance, trace humidity, temperature nineteen degrees Celsius. Someone—or something—was keeping this ship alive, and keeping it comfortable. Her lamp swept across a corridor that made her stomach drop: clean decks, polished rails, a child's drawing taped to a bulkhead at knee height, crayon-bright, depicting a stick-figure family standing under a yellow sun that no one aboard had seen in three decades.

"Contact," Vasquez said suddenly, weapon up. "Forward compartment. Movement."

They moved. Ten meters, twenty, boots magnetic-slow on the deck plating, lamps cutting cones through the dim. The forward compartment's door stood open, and beyond it—

Children.

Forty of them.

They sat in the crew mess hall, arranged in rows on benches built for adults, feet dangling above the deck, small hands folded on tables that came up to their chins. Boys and girls, ages ranging—her mind kept trying to sort them, kept failing—from perhaps five to perhaps ten years old. They wore adult-sized coveralls rolled and knotted at the wrists and ankles, name-tapes stitched across the chests: *OKAFOR, J.*, *HALVORSEN, M.*, *TANAKA-REYES, S.* Names of grown men and women stitched over the bodies of children.

None of them cried. None of them screamed. That was the worst part, Odile thought later; that was the detail that lived behind her eyes for weeks afterward. Forty children in a derelict ship thirty-one years lost, found by armed strangers in armored suits, and they did not cry. They turned their heads in unison—*in unison*, all forty faces rotating toward the doorway at the same instant, like a field of flowers finding the sun—and they looked at her with expressions of terrible, patient recognition.

"You came," said the smallest one, a girl with a name-tape reading *VANE, L.* Her voice was clear and unhurried, a child's voice carrying an adult's weight. "We counted the days. We counted everything. That's how we stayed."

"Stayed how?" Odile heard herself ask. Her own voice sounded far away, underwater. "What happened to your ship? Where is your crew?"

The girl tilted her head, considering, the way a child considers whether the question is a trap.

"We are the crew," she said.

Petrov made a sound low in his throat. Beside Odile, Duval's lamp wavered, just slightly, just enough to betray the hand holding it.

"Ma'am," Vasquez said quietly, "biometric scan reads forty life signs. All juvenile. All human. Heart rates elevated but stable. It's—they're real. They're really—"

"I can see they're real, Vasquez."

The girl—*Vane, L.*—stood up on her bench. The others watched her, and Odile understood suddenly that she was watching something structured, something organized, forty children moving to a rhythm none of the adults could hear.

"There was fire," the girl said. "And sirens. And a voice that told us to be counted. We were counted. We are still being counted." She held up one small hand and began, softly, to tap her fingers against her palm—one, two, three, four—and around the mess hall, one by one, the other children raised their hands and began tapping too, until the room filled with a soft percussion of small flesh on small palms, forty rhythms perfectly synchronized, a sound like rain on a roof, like a clock ticking in a house where everyone is asleep.

"Stop," Odile said, and her voice cracked on the word. "Stop counting. Please."

They stopped. All at once. Perfectly.

The silence afterward was worse than the counting.

"Inspector," Petrov whispered, "their medical scans—I ran preliminary imaging through the suit link. Bone density, dental structure, cellular markers. These aren't stunted kids, and they aren't clones, and they aren't—whatever else Command is going to say they are. These are adults. Adult cellular architecture, aged backward. Whoever they were, they were thirty, forty, fifty years old, and something turned them into *this*."

"Nothing turns people into children," Duval said. "That's not a thing that happens. That's not physics. That's not—"

"It happened," said the girl, Vane, L., reasonably. "You're looking at it."

* * *

The return crossing to the *Vigilant* took eleven minutes. Odile spent all of them replaying the girl's face.

Back aboard, she filed her initial report herself, typing it directly rather than dictating, because she wanted control of every word. Then she sat in her cabin and stared at the draft for a long time, because the report could not contain what she had seen. Reports contained tonnage and registry numbers and probable causes. They did not contain the sensation of forty pairs of child's eyes recognizing you across a mess hall, or the sound of synchronized counting, or the crayon sun taped to a bulkhead.

At 0612, Command responded. Quarantine protocols invoked. The *Halcyon Wake* to be taken under tow to Station Erebus, Ward Nine, medical custody pending investigation. The children to be treated as survivors, minors, wards of the Sector. Standard debriefing procedures suspended pending psychiatric evaluation.

And at the bottom of the directive, in the bland administrative language that Odile had spent fifteen years learning to read the way other people read weather:

*Note: Records Authority liaison will meet vessel at dock. Crew manifest and all personal effects to be surrendered to Authority custody on arrival. Do not permit survivors unsupervised access to communications.*

Do not permit survivors access to communications. As if forty terrified children might dial out. As if the danger were what the children might *say*, rather than what they were.

Odile read the line four times. Then she read the manifest the Authority had attached—the original *Halcyon Wake* manifest, recovered from archives, forty names, forty ranks, forty next-of-kin entries, most marked DECEASED or UNRESOLVED.

The last entry on the list read: VANE, LYRA — SYSTEMS TECHNICIAN, SECOND CLASS. Age at loss: thirty-four.

The girl in the mess hall had been maybe seven years old.

Odile closed the file. Outside her viewport, the great clean hull of the *Halcyon Wake* swung slowly into the shadow of the tow tug, its navigation lights still pulsing their patient, regulation rhythm—white, red, white—as if the ship itself were still trying to tell someone, anyone, *here I am, here I am, I never left.*

Something impossible had come home. And the first thing the Sector had done—the very first thing, before doctors, before questions, before anyone had asked a single child a single question—was send for the people who erase things.

Odile Ferran had served the Nine Sectors for fifteen years without once breaking a rule she couldn't justify.

She saved the file to her personal partition, encrypted it, and told no one.
