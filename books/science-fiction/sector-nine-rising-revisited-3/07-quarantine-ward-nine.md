## 7. Quarantine Ward Nine

The quarantine ward smelled of antiseptic and something older, something Odile Ferran couldn't name until she stood at the observation glass and understood: it smelled like a school. Like chalk dust and warm milk and the particular sweetness of children kept indoors too long. The scent had no business being aboard a naval medical station orbiting Ceres Gate, three days after a ghost ship had drifted into traffic lanes that hadn't carried ghosts in thirty years.

Forty of them. That was the number that kept snagging in her mind like a burr in fabric. Forty crew members of the *Halcyon Wake*, registered manifest average age forty-one years, recovered from a derelict freighter with no power, no logs, and no explanation—and every single one of them now under four feet tall.

She watched them through the reinforced glass of Observation Bay Two while the station's chief medical officer, Dr. Halloway, stood beside her with a tablet he consulted more often than he looked up.

"Cellular regression," he said, for the third time. "Not de-aging in any mystical sense. Every cell in their bodies reads as juvenile tissue. Bone density, neural development, endocrine profiles—all consistent with children between six and nine years old. It's as if someone took their bodies back to a prior draft."

"And their minds?"

Halloway's jaw worked. "That's where it gets complicated."

"Everything about this is complicated, Doctor. I've spent three days reading reports that describe an impossible ship in increasingly defensive language. Give me the uncomplicated version."

He almost smiled. She'd noticed that about him—Halloway wanted to be honest but kept checking whether honesty was permitted. She recognized the reflex. She wore it herself some mornings.

"Their memories are intact," he said. "Adult memories, mostly fragmented, stored in what appears to be fully functional juvenile brains. They remember their names. Their ranks. Their families. One of them—the former navigator, a woman who is now approximately seven—asked me to contact her husband on Meridian's sister colony." He paused. "She asked if her husband would still recognize her voice."

"What did you tell her?"

"That we were still working on communications."

Odile turned back to the glass. Beyond it, the ward stretched in a long crescent of soft-lit bays, each one scaled down—small beds, small chairs, small everything, requisitioned from the station's family annex in a scramble that had taken twelve hours and revealed, she suspected, how rarely anyone aboard thought about children at all. The forty sat or lay or wandered in loose clusters, and even from this distance Odile could see the wrongness of it: adult gestures performed by child hands. A man rolling his shoulders like a dockworker before a shift, except the shoulders belonged to a boy of eight. A woman rubbing her temples with the weary precision of someone who'd done it ten thousand times, on a face that had never known a headache.

"They're frightened," Odile said.

"They're composed," Halloway corrected. "Which frightens me more. Children should be crying. These ones are waiting."

* * *

She conducted the first formal interview at 0900 station time, in a room built for pediatric counseling, which meant the table came up to her ribs and the chairs forced her knees nearly to her chest. She'd considered standing. Then she'd considered what it would look like—an inspector looming over a child in a recorded interview—and sat down like everyone else, folding her tall frame into furniture made for smaller lives.

Her first subject was the one the medical staff had designated Child One, though his intake bracelet said HALCYON WAKE / CREW / VANE-REGISTRY 0044. He was perhaps eight years old, with a crew-cut grown shaggy and the posture of a man who'd spent decades sitting watch. His name, when he gave it, was Tomas Reyes, Chief Engineer, twenty-two years of service.

"You understand why you're here?" Odile began.

"I'm here because my ship came home wrong," Reyes said. His voice was a child's voice, high and clear, delivering words in an adult cadence. "And because nobody upstairs believes us."

"Believes you about what?"

He looked at her then—a long, level look that made her feel like the junior officer in the room. "About what happened before we went dark. About the fire. About the counting."

"The counting."

"You'll hear it," he said. "Everyone asks eventually. And when enough of us say it together, maybe someone will write it down properly instead of calling it confabulation." He pronounced the word carefully, like a man handling evidence. "I know what your psych people are putting in their reports, Inspector. Trauma-induced shared delusion. Suggestibility. Mass false memory. I wrote performance reviews for twenty years; I know bureaucratic language when I hear it. But there are forty of us, and we didn't talk to each other before they separated us into different bays. Check your own logs. We've been isolated since recovery. So explain to me how forty people in separate rooms tell the same story."

Odile had already checked. She'd pulled the isolation order herself, actually—one of the few procedural decisions in this entire affair that had been made correctly, by accident or instinct. The children had been separated within two hours of boarding, before any of them could compare notes.

"We'll get to that," she said. "Start at the beginning. When did the *Halcyon Wake* leave port?"

"Eleven years ago. Standard deep-haul contract, out of Sector Nine, cargo sealed, destination classified above our clearance. We knew the route was old—pre-War lanes, the quiet ones. Captain Osei didn't like it but the pay was triple rate and half the crew had debts." He recited it flatly, a man reading his own service record. "We were nineteen months out when we found the beacon."

"What beacon?"

"A distress signal. Old encoding—War-era, the kind nobody's used since the sectors cut the lines. It was coming from a station that isn't on any chart we had. The captain logged it as MERIDIAN-ANOMALY and we argued about whether to answer it for six hours." Something moved behind the child's eyes, something far too old. "We answered it. That was our mistake. Or our orders. Depends which version of the log you believe, and both versions exist, Inspector. I checked."

Odile's stylus had stopped moving. "Go on."

"We docked. The station was dead—cold hull, no power, atmosphere holding by some miracle of old engineering. And inside—" He stopped. For the first time, the adult composure cracked, and underneath it was just a boy, eight years old, sitting in a chair too big for him, trying not to cry. "Inside there were names. Thousands of names scratched into the bulkheads. And dates. And a number painted over the main hatch in letters taller than I am now."

"What number?"

"1,204." He said it without hesitation, without stumbling, the way a person says their own birthday. "And beneath it, the word COUNTED."

The room's environmental system cycled, a soft mechanical breath. Odile realized her hands were cold.

"Tomas. What happened next?"

"Sirens," he said simply. "Fire. And the counting."

* * *

By the fourth interview, Odile had stopped taking notes in sequence and started building a grid. She interviewed each child alone, in separate sessions, asking identical questions in identical order, and afterward she laid the transcripts side by side across her quarters' floor like a cartographer assembling a coastline from fragments reported by different ships.

The details matched. Not loosely—not in the way trauma accounts usually rhymed, all smoke and shouting and unverifiable terror—but precisely. Forty independent interviews, and thirty-seven of them described the same sequence: the beacon, the docking, the cold corridors, the scratched names, the number over the hatch. Thirty-one described the sirens beginning at what they insisted was exactly 0400 station time. Twenty-nine described the intercom voice.

It was the intercom voice that made her doubt everything she worked for.

"It was calm," said Child Twelve, formerly Lieutenant Amara Diallo, now a solemn girl of seven with her hair braided too tightly by a nurse who hadn't known what else to do with it. "That's what I remember most. Not angry. Not scared. Calm, like someone reading a shipping manifest. It said: 'This is a scheduled count. All personnel proceed to your assigned sections. Remain in your sections until the count concludes.' And people went. That's the part I can't make sense of, Inspector. They *went*. Grown adults, engineers and medics and spacers who'd survived things—they heard that voice and they walked into those sections and the doors closed behind them."

"Why would they do that?"

"Because the voice used the right words." Diallo's small hands folded on the small table. "It used emergency protocol language. Section integrity verification. Count confirmation. Words drilled into every spacer since before the War. You obey the count. Everyone knows you obey the count—it's how you find out who's alive after a breach. The voice made it sound like procedure. And procedure is the thing spacers trust more than their own mothers." Her chin trembled once, and steadied. "Whoever did it knew that. Knew us better than we knew ourselves."

"Did you see who was speaking?"

"No one saw anything. The sections had no windows. That was the design flaw—or the design feature. We only heard the voice, and the doors, and after a while..." She stopped. Started again, quieter. "After a while, the sounds stopped being voices."

Odile waited.

"I was in Hydroponics section," Diallo said. "There were eleven of us. We banged on the door for six hours. Then the air started going wrong—thin, then sweet, then nothing. The last thing I remember is everyone sitting down, because sitting uses less oxygen, and someone saying, 'They're counting us,' and someone else saying, 'No. They're finishing the count.'" She looked up. "I have dreamed that sentence every night for eleven years, Inspector. In an adult body and now in this one. Tell your psychologists which category of confabulation that falls under."

* * *

The debriefing session—officials called it a "consensus review"—was held on the fifth day, in the ward's common area, with Odile, Dr. Halloway, Station Commander Brekke, and a Records Authority liaison named Voss who had arrived unannounced aboard the morning shuttle and whose credentials, when Odile ran them, came back stamped with a priority flag she'd only seen twice in fourteen years of customs work.

Voss was smooth and gray and smiled exactly as much as required. "The Authority appreciates the thoroughness of your interviews, Inspector Ferran. However, I'm instructed to remind all parties that testimony from subjects in compromised neurological states carries limited evidentiary weight."

"They're not neurologically compromised," Halloway said. "Their brains are juvenile but healthy. Scans are clean. If anything, memory formation in these subjects should be *enhanced*—"

"Doctor." Voss's smile didn't move. "The official position is under development. Until it's finalized, I'd ask that raw testimony not be circulated beyond this station."

Brekke cleared his throat. "Perhaps the consensus session will clarify matters. The children have requested—together, formally, in writing—to give a joint account. Their counselor thinks it may be therapeutic."

"It's suggestibility," Voss said. "Put forty traumatized subjects in a room and they'll harmonize their stories within the hour. Any competent defense counsel would shred it."

"Then let's shred it ourselves," Odile said. "Let's hear the harmonies and check them against the solo performances. That's how corroboration works, Liaison. You test the story against itself."

A flicker crossed Voss's face—there and gone, fast, but Odile had spent her career reading faces across customs tables, faces belonging to people with something in their luggage. What crossed Voss's face was not boredom or bureaucratic patience.

It was calculation.

"Proceed," Voss said.

* * *

They arranged the forty in the common area in a wide semicircle, small figures on small chairs, and Odile stood before them with a recorder running and the strange sensation of addressing a classroom that had outlived its teacher. Halloway hovered near the medical station. Voss stood by the door with his arms folded. Brekke watched from the gallery above with the expression of a man hoping the floor would hold.

Odile asked her standard opening question. "Can you tell me what happened to the *Halcyon Wake*?"

For a moment, silence. Forty children looked at her, and she saw them glance at one another—not anxiously, but the way musicians glance at each other before a piece begins, finding the downbeat.

Then Tomas Reyes, at the center, spoke: "We found the beacon."

And a girl beside him continued: "We docked with the dead station."

Another: "Meridian."

Another: "The hull was cold but the names were warm."

They spoke in relay, passing the account hand to hand around the semicircle, each voice picking up the thread mid-sentence as if the story were a single organism distributed across forty bodies:

"The corridors were scratched with names—"

"—thousands, floor to ceiling—"

"—and dates going back thirty years—"

"—before the Quiet, before the lines went silent—"

"—and over the main hatch, the number—"

And then, without any visible cue, without a leader, all forty of them said it together, in a unison so perfect it raised the hair on Odile's arms:

"*One thousand two hundred and four.*"

The recorder's level indicator jumped. Above, in the gallery, Brekke had gone pale. Beside the door, Voss had not moved at all, but his stillness had changed quality—from the stillness of a bureaucrat to the stillness of a man watching a structure he'd built begin, very quietly, to crack.

"The sirens came at 0400," the children said together, and then broke apart again into relay: "—the intercom woke us—"

"—the voice was calm—"

"—it called it a scheduled count—"

"—we went to our sections—"

"—the doors sealed—"

"—the air went thin—"

"—then sweet—"

"—then nothing—"

And then the unison again, softer now, forty small voices braided into one, and Odile understood with terrible clarity that she was not listening to children performing a story. She was listening to a crew. A crew that had rehearsed this account in the dark places of their minds for eleven years, that had carried it through whatever transformation had been done to them, that had held it intact the way you hold a flame through a storm—with your hands cupped and your body curled around it and no thought except *don't let it go out.*

"When we woke," they said together, "we were small. And the ship was empty. And the counting wasn't finished."

Silence filled the common area like water filling a hull.

Odile became aware, distantly, that her hands were shaking. She pressed them flat against her thighs and raised her voice to keep it level. "One question. Just one more. The voice on the intercom—the one that ordered the count. Did anyone ever learn its name?"

The children looked at one another. Some shook their heads. But in the second row, a small boy—Child Nineteen, formerly a comms officer named Pell—raised his hand like a student, waited to be acknowledged, and said:

"We didn't know his name. But we knew his voice signature. Comms officers log everything." He reached into the pocket of his oversized ward smock and held up something small and dark: a data chip, scuffed, salvaged, impossibly retained through whatever hell had remade them. "Every broadcast on a registered channel leaves a signature. We kept the recording of the count. All forty of us kept copies, in case—" His voice wavered, the child surfacing through the officer. "In case nobody believed us. In case somebody tried to say it never happened."

He held the chip out toward her, arm straight, the way you surrender a weapon or offer a gift.

"And Inspector," he added, as she crossed the room to take it, "the signature has a name attached. It's in the header. It was always in the header. We just couldn't reach anyone who'd listen."

Odile closed her hand around the chip. It was warm from the boy's pocket, and light, absurdly light, for the weight it carried.

Behind her, Voss unfolded his arms and stepped forward, smile reassembled, voice pleasant as a locked door. "Inspector Ferran. A word about chain of custody."

* * *

That night, in her quarters, Odile sat with the chip connected to an isolated terminal—her own terminal, her own encryption, nothing routed through station systems—and opened the file header the boy had told her about.

The recording was eleven years old. The metadata confirmed it: timestamped, signed, authenticated by protocols so old they predated the Quiet. Whatever else had happened aboard the *Halcyon Wake*, this file had not been fabricated recently. Fabrication left fingerprints. This had fingerprints, but they were the right ones, the aged ones, worn into the data like footsteps worn into stone.

She played the first four seconds. A voice, calm as a shipping manifest, filled her dark quarters:

*"This is a scheduled count. All personnel proceed to your assigned sections."*

She stopped playback. Sat very still.

Then she opened the authentication block and read the signature line, the name attached to the voice, the name that had been sitting in the header all along, waiting for someone willing to listen.

She read it twice. Three times. Then she pulled up the public registry of the Sector Records Authority and found the same name at the top of the organizational chart, smiling out at her from an official portrait, gray and smooth and exactly as remembered.

Director Cassian Roe.

Outside her porthole, Ceres Gate turned slowly against the stars, and somewhere below in the ward, forty children were sleeping—or not sleeping—in their small beds, keeping their count, waiting to see whether anyone would finally finish it.

Odile reached for her comm, to file a report, and stopped with her thumb above the send key.

Because she had just realized the question that would define everything that came next: a report filed through official channels would pass through the Records Authority. Through him. Through the voice on the intercom.

Her thumb hovered in the dark, and the long quiet of the nine sectors seemed suddenly very close, pressing against the glass, waiting to see what she would do.
