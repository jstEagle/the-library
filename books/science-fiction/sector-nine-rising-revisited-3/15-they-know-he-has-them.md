## 15. They Know He Has Them

The warning came as a smell.

Xanthe had spent thirty years learning the language of Ceres Gate's docking ring—the way it breathed through its ancient ventilation, the particular sourness of recycled air when a ship too large was berthed nearby, the mineral tang of ice-haulers sweating their cargo. So when the Lark Ascending's berth began to taste of ozone and cold polymer, he knew before his console confirmed it: someone had flooded the adjacent corridor with stasis foam. Emergency protocol. The kind they used when they didn't want anything in a compartment to burn, or run, or be destroyed.

They were coming for him.

He'd felt it building for two days, the way you feel weather behind a hull. After Odile Ferran's offer—*immunity for the Meridian crystals, Mr. Vane, and we never speak of this again*—he'd gone dark, rerouted his transponder, paid three separate dockhands to lose track of his comings and goings. But Odile had looked at him across that cramped customs office with eyes that missed nothing, and he understood now what he'd refused to understand then: she hadn't been offering him a deal. She'd been confirming inventory. She'd needed to know how much he carried before someone else came to take it.

The console chimed. *Berth 44-K: security lockdown initiated.*

"Right," Xanthe said aloud, to no one, because there was no one. That had always been the arrangement. "Right."

He moved through the Lark Ascending's narrow spine on muscle memory alone, killing lights as he went—not to slow the agents, who would have full-spectrum imaging anyway, but out of habit, the smuggler's instinct to leave darkness behind him like a signature. In the cargo hold, his remaining crystals sat in their padded cases, forty-one of them, each one a sealed life pressed into lattice. Meridian crystals among them. He could feel them the way you feel a bruise.

He couldn't take them all. The escape pod in the ventral hull held one passenger, one survival kit, and precious little else. Every kilogram was a second of thrust he might not have.

So he did the thing he had sworn never to do, the thing that had kept him alive through nine years of silent lanes: he opened the cargo.

His hands shook. Not from fear—from the sheer transgression of it, like cutting open his own chest. The case seals broke with a sound like a held breath released. He worked fast, reading manifest tags by penlight, pulling crystals into the light one by one. War-era memories from the border worlds. A wedding from Thessaly Reach. A child's first steps on some gravity-poor colony where the sky was green. Forty-one lives, and he had carried them all without ever asking whose they were, because asking made you a witness and witnesses got erased.

Three of them bore the origin stamp he'd only just learned to see: a seven-pointed star inside a circle. Meridian Station.

He took those three. Then, after a heartbeat that cost him more than he wanted to count, he took a fourth—a crystal tagged only *HALCYON WAKE / PERSONAL / DO NOT SELL*, which he'd hauled for a broker two years ago and never delivered because the broker's account had gone dead mid-route. It had sat in his hold ever since, an orphaned memory waiting for a port that would never open.

Four crystals into his jacket's lining pockets. Four small weights against his ribs, heavier than lead.

Then he pulled the thermite charge from beneath the deck plating near the reactor housing—the one he'd installed the day he bought the Lark Ascending, back when he still told himself a man should always have a way to end things cleanly—and armed it with a six-minute delay.

"I'm sorry," he said to the ship. To the whole rusted, patched, stubborn length of her. Nine years she'd been his silence, his armor, his moving grave of other people's memories. He laid his palm flat against the bulkhead and felt her hum, faint and faithful, like something sleeping.

Then he ran.

* * *

The maintenance crawlway behind Berth 44-K was a smuggler's artery, older than the Gate itself, and Xanthe knew its twists the way other men knew their own signatures. He moved low and fast, elbows and knees on cold metal, the four crystals pressing against him with every contraction of his body like a second heartbeat.

Behind him, muffled through three decks, he heard the breach team hit the Lark Ascending's airlock. No announcement. No warrant read over comms. They'd cut the berth's external cameras first—he'd watched the feeds die ninety seconds ago, which was how he'd known the countdown had truly begun. This wasn't customs. Customs knocked. This was Records Authority, and Records Authority preferred its subjects surprised, contained, and unrecorded.

A voice crackled through the crawlway's ancient intercom grid, distorted but legible—the berth master's automated system, overridden by someone with real authority:

*"All personnel in Ring Seven evacuate to designated shelters. This is not a drill."*

Not a drill. And not, he noticed, an accusation. No mention of smuggling, no charges named. When they finally announced it—and they would announce it, loudly, everywhere—it would be something clean. Tax fraud, maybe. Weapons trafficking. Something that explained the thermite, explained the man running, explained everything except the truth.

He wondered if Odile Ferran was down there in the boarding party. He decided he hoped not. He wasn't sure why. Maybe because she'd looked at him with something almost like respect, and he'd have hated to learn it was theater. Maybe because if she was there, then she'd sold him, and he wasn't ready to carry that weight along with everything else.

The crawlway spat him into a service junction above the hydroponics ring, and here he made his mistake—or rather, here his luck, which had been running thin for thirty years, finally called in its debts.

Two agents stood at the junction's lower level, backs to him, watching a ladder well. Full tactical kit, matte black, no insignia—which itself was an answer, because legitimate authority wore its name like armor. He froze in the shadow of a coolant conduit, four meters up, and began easing backward.

His boot found a loose panel.

The sound was small—a soft metallic click—but in the dead air of the junction it rang like a struck bell. Both heads turned up. For one suspended instant nobody moved, the agents reading shadows and Xanthe reading their posture, calculating angles, distances, whether the crawlway behind him was wide enough for a burst of fire to miss—

"Contact, upper gallery," one said, calm as a man reporting the time. "Single target."

Xanthe ran.

Stun fire lit the junction blue-white behind him. A bolt kissed the conduit a hand's width from his skull and the metal screamed and spat molten flecks that seared his neck. He threw himself through the gallery hatch and slammed the manual lock, knowing it would buy him ten seconds, maybe twelve, and spent them sprinting down the hydroponics access spine past walls of engineered wheat glowing under grow-lamps, past a startled agri-tech who flattened against a planter and wisely said nothing, past the whole quiet machinery of ordinary life that had no idea it was brushing against a man being unwritten.

Twelve seconds later the hatch blew its lock and the pursuit resumed, and now he could hear them talking to each other on short-range, unencrypted because they didn't care who heard:

*"Target is mobile, Ring Six, heading spinward—"*

*"Seal the transit tubes. Nobody docks, nobody leaves. Director wants him breathing."*

Director. Not Ferran. Roe. Cassian Roe, whose name sat on the erasure orders like a signature on a death warrant, whose name sat—Xanthe's stomach turned even mid-sprint—on his parents' old employment contracts. The man who signed the past shut had come personally interested in the last man carrying it open.

*Breathing.* That word followed Xanthe down the ring like a debt collector. They wanted him alive. Which meant they wanted something from him besides silence—testimony, perhaps, deniable and coerced. Or the crystals. Or simply the pleasure of making an example that walked and talked and confessed to crimes everyone could pretend were the real ones.

Ring Six ended at the outer hull maintenance locks, and beyond them, the void.

* * *

There was a place on Ceres Gate where the station's skin grew thin—a section of old hull near the debris-shield pylons where the original builders had left inspection blisters, and where, over decades, the scrap-riggers and decommissioned spacers who lived in the station's legal shadows had quietly bolted together a graveyard of derelict pods and gutted tugs. The riggers called it the Molt. Station security called it a navigational hazard and pretended it was empty, because pretending was cheaper than clearing it, and because half the security officers' cousins lived out there.

Xanthe had traded with the Molt for years. Never stayed. Never let them see his face twice in the same month. His rules again: never open the cargo, never ask questions, never belong anywhere. Belonging was a beacon. Belonging got people burned.

But rules were for men with ships.

The EVA suit he stole from the hydroponics emergency locker fit badly and smelled of someone else's sweat, and the helmet seal gave a wheeze he chose to find reassuring rather than fatal. He cycled through the maintenance blister into hard vacuum with the stars wheeling slow and indifferent overhead, Ceres Gate's vast turning bulk below him, and clipped his tether line to the guide cable the riggers had strung between the pylons like a clothesline across infinity.

Below and spinward, in Berth 44-K, the Lark Ascending waited for her six minutes to end.

He tried not to look. He looked anyway.

From outside, through the berth's force-field shimmer, he could see her silhouette—the freighter that had been his whole world, his coffin of silence, his home. He thought about the thermite crawling toward her reactor housing. He thought about forty-one crystal cases he'd left behind, and whether the Authority would bother cataloguing them or simply vaporize them, forty-one lives deleted twice over. He thought about the day he'd bought her, eleven years ago now, signing false papers with a false name, becoming no one on purpose, and how it had felt like freedom.

It had been freedom. He could admit that now. It had also been a kind of death, self-administered, painless, and he'd called it safety for so long he'd forgotten it was a choice.

The charge went while he was still fifty meters along the tether line.

No sound—vacuum keeps its secrets—but the berth's force-field flared white, and the Lark Ascending came apart from the inside like a flower blooming in fast-forward, hull plates peeling outward, atmosphere flashing to frost-plume, and for one impossible moment she hung there as a expanding sphere of fire and debris, beautiful and obscene, and Xanthe stopped moving and hung on his line and watched his own name die.

Because that was what it was. Everyone who tracked such things—the brokers, the fences, the customs officers, the Authority—now had their answer. Xanthe Vane, smuggler, dead in a cargo explosion of his own making. Case closed. The false names he'd worn would scatter like ash; the routes he'd run would go cold; the face that had never been photographed would stay unphotographed. He had burned his identity with his ship, exactly as designed, and the design had worked perfectly.

He felt nothing. Then he felt everything, all at once, in the silence where the engine hum had lived for eleven years: grief like pressure, grief like vacuum pulling the air out of his chest. He'd killed the last thing on any world that knew him. He was, officially and actually, no one at all.

Somewhere below, in the wreckage of Berth 44-K, agents were finding slagged crystal cases and drawing their conclusions. Somewhere above, in an office with a good view, Cassian Roe was learning that his quarry had chosen fire over capture—and either believing it, or not.

Xanthe put his helmet against the tether cable and let himself shake for exactly sixty seconds. He counted them. It was a trick he'd learned young, after Meridian, after Lyra, after the archives went blank: grief in measured doses, sealed and scheduled, because unmeasured it would take everything.

Then he unclipped, fired the suit's thrusters in three short bursts, and fell toward the Molt.

* * *

They were waiting for him. Of course they were; the Molt saw everything that moved along the pylons. When his boots mag-locked to the scarred deck of a gutted ore-tug, four figures stood in the tug's cavernous hold, backlit by work-lamps, faces shadowed under welding visors pushed up on their foreheads. Old spacers, most of them—bodies reshaped by decades of wrong gravity, hands like root systems. One of them, a woman with a voice like gravel in a drum, spoke first.

"Heard your ship died."

"My ship died," Xanthe agreed.

"Heard you died with it."

"You heard wrong."

The woman studied him for a long moment. Around her, the others watched with the flat patience of people who had survived by never being surprised. Finally she jerked her chin toward the tug's interior depths, where heat and light and the smell of real food leaked through a bulkhead curtain.

"Old Iyo Sen's been asking after you," she said. "Says a man who burns his own ship has finally got something worth saying." She turned away, then paused. "You carrying anything worth saying?"

Xanthe's hand rose, without permission, to his jacket—to the four small weights against his ribs. Three crystals stamped with a seven-pointed star. One orphaned memory from a ship full of children.

"Yeah," he said. "I think I am."

She nodded slowly, and something in her face shifted—not warmth, exactly, but recognition. The look of someone seeing a ghost decide to keep living.

"Then get inside," she said. "And welcome to the Molt, whoever you are."

That was the phrase that undid him, though he didn't let it show. *Whoever you are.* An hour ago it would have been his proudest achievement. Now it landed like a sentence of exile. He had made himself invisible for thirty years, and invisibility had saved his life tonight—but standing in the lamplight of the scrapfields, stripped of ship, name, and route, he understood with sudden clarity that the next part of his life would not be survived by disappearing. The Authority knew the crystals existed. Roe knew someone had them. The hunt wouldn't end because one smuggler burned; it would end when the truth did its work, or when he did.

He had three Meridian crystals, one impossible orphan, a dead sister who was somehow alive and eight years old, and nowhere left to hide.

Which meant, for the first time in thirty years, hiding was off the table.

Inside the tug, past the curtain, an old man sat by a stove built from a coolant coil, and when he looked up at Xanthe his eyes went straight to the jacket, straight to the shape of what was hidden there, and he smiled like a man greeting a long-delayed delivery.

"Sit down, boy," Iyo Sen said. "You look like a man who's ready to hear about Meridian."

Xanthe's blood went cold. "How do you know that name?"

The old wrecker fed another scrap of fuel into the stove, and the firelight made his face flicker like a faulty recording.

"Son," he said, "I was there."
