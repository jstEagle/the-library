## 16. Refuge Among Wreckers

The Lark Ascending was dead in the water, and Xanthe Vane had never felt so alive.

He'd burned her three days ago—her registry, her transponder codes, the forged manifests that had been his face to every customs gate in nine sectors for eleven years. He'd done it in a scrapyard furnace on the edge of the Perseus Drift, feeding her identity into the fire one data-slate at a time while the man who called himself Brannock watched with the patient amusement of someone watching a funeral he'd seen a thousand times.

"Ship's not dead," Brannock had said, when Xanthe fed in the last slate and watched it curl. "Just her name. Names are cheap out here."

Xanthe hadn't understood what he meant until now.

The wrecker settlement hung in the shadow of a dead moonlet that the charts called K-77 and the wreckers called the Anvil—a half-melted lump of nickel-iron where some pre-war mining concern had tried to crack the core and only succeeded in cracking itself. Around it, tethered by cables and habit, drifted the carcasses of forty ships: freighters too old to certify, tugs with their spines broken, a passenger liner whose hull still bore scorch patterns no one talked about. The wreckers lived inside them like hermit crabs, welding corridors between hulks until the whole cluster had become one sprawling organism of salvaged steel and stolen air.

No beacon. No registry. No customs gate. The Sector Authority charts marked the whole cluster as navigational hazard, debris field, uninhabited.

Which was exactly how the wreckers liked it.

Xanthe drifted the Lark's battered shuttle through the outer cable-web, following hand-painted markers that were less signage than graffiti: a stenciled skull with a smile, a warning in six scripts, and finally a simple arrow and the word IN, which struck him as either very welcoming or very ominous.

"They'll want your guns," Brannock had told him. "Give them up. They'll want your story. Give them most of it. And they'll want to know why the Authority is offering two million credits for your head." A pause. "Don't lie about that part. They already know."

The shuttle dock clamped onto a collar welded to the flank of what had once been a grain hauler named *Prosperity of Ceres*, according to the ghost-lettering on her hull. The airlock cycled without being asked, which meant someone had been watching him since the Drift, and when the inner door opened, Xanthe stepped into warmth, noise, and the smell of frying onions—the most disorienting thing he'd encountered in weeks.

* * *

The mess hall occupied the grain hauler's old cargo bay, and it held perhaps two hundred people eating dinner under strings of work-lamps. Not a military mess, not a station cantina—something older. Long tables built from deck plating. Children doing homework at the end of one bench, supervised by a woman with a welding rig slung over her shoulder like a rifle. An old man playing a stringed instrument with a body made from a coolant manifold, the music thin and strange and utterly unselfconscious.

Nobody looked up when he entered. Then everybody looked up at once, the way a body does when its immune system notices something new in the blood.

The conversations didn't stop so much as lower, continuing beneath the surface. Xanthe walked to the serving line as if he belonged there, because Brannock had told him that was the first rule: walk like you belong, and let them correct you.

A woman behind the counter—sixty, maybe, forearms like dock cable, a burn scar running from jaw to collarbone in the shape of a lightning strike—looked him over and jerked her chin at the tray.

"You eat like a spacer or like a tourist?"

"Spacer," Xanthe said. "But I haven't eaten like one in a long time."

She loaded the tray anyway. Rice, onions, something dark and savory that might have started life as protein stock and had certainly ended it as something better. "Brannock vouched for you. That buys you dinner and a bunk. It doesn't buy you anything else. Iyo will see you after."

"Iyo Sen."

"You know the name?"

"I know Brannock said Iyo Sen would decide whether I stay."

The woman snorted. "Iyo decides whether the sun comes up, around here. Eat. Bay twelve, aft corridor, follow the smell of solder." She leaned closer, voice dropping. "And smuggler—whatever you're carrying in that bag against your ribs, stop holding it like that. Everyone here has already noticed, and nobody cares. We're not thieves." A beat. "We're *wreckers*. There's a difference, and it matters to us even if it doesn't to you."

* * *

Bay twelve was a workshop carved from the hauler's old pump room, lit by a single high-intensity lamp and cluttered with the archaeology of a hundred ships: valve assemblies sorted into bins by touch rather than sight, a wall of hand-drawn schematics, and at its center, a woman in her seventies working on a fuel cell with hands that moved with the certainty of someone who had stopped needing to think about it fifty years ago.

Iyo Sen was small. That was the first thing. Small and round-shouldered and silver-haired, with reading glasses pushed up into her hair and eyes that went through Xanthe like a scanner through cargo.

"You're the one they're paying two million for," she said. No greeting. She set down her tool. "Sit."

There was only one chair, an ejection seat from a fighter two generations obsolete, its upholstery patched with tape that had itself been patched. Xanthe sat. Iyo Sen did not.

"Brannock says you ran memory-crystal along the Quiet Route for eleven years and never opened a crate. Says you never asked whose memories. Says that's why you're good." She folded her arms. "Is that true?"

"It's true."

"And now?"

"Now I've opened them."

Something shifted in her face—not softening, exactly. Recognition. "And what did you find?"

"A party," Xanthe said. "On Meridian Station. Four hundred people dancing at a festival the same year the archives say the station was unmanned. And then another crystal. An evacuation order that wasn't an evacuation. A man sealing sections of the station with people inside them and calling it a count." His voice stayed level. He'd practiced it staying level. "Twelve hundred and four people, Director Cassian Roe wrote out of existence, and my mother signed the erasure."

The lamp hummed. Somewhere down the corridor, the manifold-instrument player had moved on to a different tune.

"Say the number again," Iyo said quietly.

"Twelve hundred and four."

"Twelve hundred and five." Her voice didn't rise. It didn't need to. "They missed one. Or they decided one wasn't worth the arithmetic." She pulled her glasses down onto her nose and looked at him over the rims. "Me."

* * *

She told it plainly, the way people tell things they have told themselves ten thousand times in the dark. She had been a junior reactor tech on Meridian Station, twenty-six years old, when the seal order came through. She'd been in Section G, the maintenance ring, checking coolant lines, when the bulkheads dropped—and she'd survived because she was small enough to fit through a crawl duct the designers had forgotten existed, and stubborn enough to try it when the air started tasting wrong.

"I came out in the docking ring. There were maybe thirty of us who got clear before they locked the whole spine down. Thirty out of twelve hundred and five." She turned a valve fitting over in her fingers, not looking at it. "The rescue tugs never came. The Authority ships came instead. They took us aboard, debriefed us for six days, and then put us on a transport to Ceres with severance pay and a document we all signed. It said Meridian Station was decommissioned. Unmanned. Peaceful. It said we had never worked there." Her mouth did something that was almost a smile. "It also said that if we ever said otherwise, our families would be audited. Do you know what an audit means to people who live on sector wages? It means everything. It means your housing assignment, your children's school permits, your medical clearance. They didn't threaten to kill us. They threatened to make us invisible. And for spacers, that's worse."

"What happened to the other twenty-nine?"

"Scattered. Some died quiet deaths—accidents, illness, bad luck. One drowned himself at Ceres Gate in '61. Two just... stopped talking, ever, about anything. I kept moving. Kept working salvage. Kept away from Records Authority personnel." She looked up. "Kept remembering. That's the one thing they couldn't requisition. For sixty years, the entire official record of Meridian Station has been a lie, and the entire true record has been one old woman's head. You understand what that does to a person? Carrying twelve hundred and five names alone?"

Xanthe thought of Lyra humming a lullaby in a quarantine ward, carrying a massacre inside a child's body. He thought of his mother's authorization chip hidden in a box of linens. He thought of the crystals in his hold, which he had carried for years without asking, the way everyone involved had carried their pieces without asking, the truth distributed across a hundred hiding places like seed grain against a famine no one would admit was coming.

"I understand," he said.

"Do you?" Iyo set the valve fitting down. "You came here because you're running. That's fine. Running is honest work. But listen to me, boy, because I'm going to say this once." She stepped closer, and the lamplight caught the burn scar, and for a moment Xanthe saw the twenty-six-year-old reactor tech standing inside the old woman like a figure inside a statue. "You didn't come here to hide. You came here because hiding stopped working, and you needed somewhere to stand. There's a difference. Hiding is what you do alone. Standing—" she tapped his chest once, hard, with one finger, "—that takes other people."

* * *

They gave him a bunk in the hull of a dead tug called *Charity*, in a compartment he shared with a retired deep-range pilot named Osk who snored in a minor key and a young rigger named Tess who talked in her sleep about torque specifications. The first night, Xanthe lay awake waiting for the fear to arrive—the old companionable fear of the invisible man, the vigilance that had kept him alive through a decade of customs gates and double-crosses.

It didn't come. That frightened him more than anything else had.

On the third morning, he woke to the sound of children running down the tug's corridor, and found that someone had shown them the stranger's door and they had decided, with the merciless democracy of children, that he was now theirs. A girl of about seven presented him with a drawing of the Lark Ascending—or possibly a potato; the medium was ambiguous—and demanded to know if he'd really outrun a customs cutter.

"Once," he admitted.

"Was it scary?"

"Yes."

"Good," she said with satisfaction, and ran off.

He ate breakfast in the mess hall and discovered that people saved him a seat without being asked. He fixed a recycler that had been complaining for months, and three people thanked him by name by evening. He stood watch on the cable-web rotation because everyone stood watch, and during the long silent hours drifting above the Anvil, an old hull-scavenger named Petros pointed out constellations of wreckage and told him what each one had been.

"That one there—the liner? *Cassiopeia Rose*. Evacuation ship, last war. Carried eleven thousand off Meridian's sister colony when the sectors cut the lanes." He said *last war* the way people say *the flood* or *the fire*—a shared catastrophe that needed no date, no name, no explanation. "Authority charts say she broke up in a navigation accident. She broke up because they ordered her crew to scuttle her rather than let the refugees land anywhere with a broadcast antenna. Eleven thousand souls, and the report calls it *navigational accident*." Petros spat, delicately, into a recycling vent. "Out here, we keep the real charts. In our heads. That's the only archive they can't edit."

"Why do you stay?" Xanthe asked. "People with your skills could live anywhere. Legally. Comfortably."

Petros looked at him for a long moment, genuinely puzzled. "Because out there, I'd have to agree that my brother doesn't exist. He's on that ship's manifest. Out there, he's a rounding error. In here, we say his name at dinner every year." He shrugged. "You get to choose what's true, if you're willing to pay for it. Most people aren't willing. We paid."

* * *

On the fifth night, Iyo Sen summoned him back to bay twelve. She had cleared a space on her workbench, and on it sat a device Xanthe recognized with a jolt: a crystal reader, jury-rigged but sound, its playback array rebuilt from parts of three different manufacturers.

"You said you opened them," she said. "How many do you have left?"

"Three. The rest burned with my berth when the Authority raided it." He heard his own voice go flat. "Eleven years of cargo. Some of it irreplaceable. Gone in nine minutes."

"Grief later. Inventory now. What are the three?"

"The festival. The seal order. And one more I haven't played. It's labeled in my mother's handwriting." He hesitated. "I've been afraid of it."

Iyo nodded slowly, unsurprised. "Of course you have." She gestured at the reader. "You don't have to play it tonight. But I want you to understand something about this camp before you decide anything. There are two hundred and fourteen people on this rock, and every one of them lost something to the Quiet. Ships, families, names, homeworlds. When the sectors sealed the borders after the war, they didn't just close the lanes—they closed the *records*. Whole colonies delisted. Crews written off. Wars renamed as weather events. Everyone out here is walking around with a piece of the true record in their head, and for sixty years we've had nowhere to put it down." She laid her hand flat on the jury-rigged reader. "So when Brannock told me a smuggler was coming who'd spent eleven years carrying other people's memories and never once looked at them—I told him to bring you in. Because a man like that is either the best courier the truth ever had, or the worst kind of coward. And I wanted to look in your eyes and find out which."

"And?"

"I'm still deciding." Almost a smile. "But you fixed the recycler in bay four without being asked, and you gave your seat to Amara Okonkwo at dinner, and you didn't flinch when the girl asked about the cutter. So the odds are improving." She turned back to her workbench. "Get some sleep, Xanthe Vane. Tomorrow, we talk about what you're going to do with those three crystals. Because I promise you—the Authority isn't spending two million credits to recover contraband. They're spending it to make sure it never plays."

He was halfway to the door when she spoke again, quieter.

"One more thing. Your mother's name. The one on the authorization chip."

"Maren Vane."

Iyo Sen was silent for a long moment. Then: "Maren Vane was the records officer who processed our severance documents. She cried while she stamped them. All thirty of us watched her do it, and none of us understood why." She looked up at him over her glasses. "Now I think maybe we did."

Xanthe stood in the doorway of bay twelve, holding that, the way he'd once held crystals he refused to open.

Down the aft corridor, the manifold-instrument player had begun again—a slow tune, older than any of them, and from the galley came the sound of two hundred people eating together under work-lamps, keeping the real charts, saying the names.

In his bunk aboard the dead tug *Charity*, Xanthe took out the third crystal and set it on the shelf beside his bunk, label up, his mother's handwriting facing the ceiling.

Tomorrow, he thought. Tomorrow he would play it.

He fell asleep before he could be afraid, and for the first time in eleven years, no one in the dark was looking for him—which lasted exactly until morning, when Tess shook him awake with news from the cable-watch: a survey drone, Authority-marked, had passed the outer web at 0400.

It hadn't stopped. It hadn't scanned.

It had just looked.
