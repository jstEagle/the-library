## 25. Stealing the Tower

The broadcast spire of Sector Nine rose out of the Ceres shelf like a needle someone had driven through the crust of the world and forgotten to pull back out. From the scrapfields it was a hairline of light against the permanent dusk, forty kilometers tall, its transmission crown blazing white where it caught the orbital mirrors. Every screen on every world in the sector drank from that tower. Everything anyone was allowed to know came down that needle.

Xanthe had spent thirty years learning how not to be seen. He knew the sightlines of customs gates, the blind angles of cargo cradles, the exact shade of boredom that made an inspector's eyes slide off a patched freighter. Evasion had been his craft, his religion, his armor.

Tonight he was going to walk straight at the most watched structure in nine sectors, and he found—to his own surprise—that his hands were steady.

"You're smiling," Odile said beside him. She crouched at the lip of a drainage culvert at the edge of the wreckers' territory, her ex-inspector's coat swapped for a rig-scrapper's thermal shell, her hair bound under a salvage hood. "Why are you smiling?"

"Because for once I'm not hiding from something." He nodded at the spire. "I'm walking toward it."

"That's usually called a mistake."

"It's usually called a plan," said Iyo Sen's voice behind them, dry as vacuum-packed grain.

The old wrecker came up the culvert with the rolling gait of someone who'd spent sixty years in spin gravity that never quite matched her inner ear. Her left arm ended above the elbow in a polished brass cap she'd machined herself, etched with the names of ships she'd broken—and, if you looked closely enough, one name she hadn't: *Meridian*. She'd told them about it around the burn-barrel three nights ago, in the flat voice of a woman reciting cargo manifests: twelve hundred and four souls, sealed sections, a voice on the intercom calling it a count. The wreckers had listened the way people listen to weather reports about storms they survived long ago.

Now she unfolded a grease-stiffened chart across a crate of stripped reactor shielding and weighted the corners with bolts.

"The spire," she said. "Built seventy years ago, before the Quiet, when the sectors still pretended they liked each other. Which means it was built by engineers who assumed inspectors would someday have to inspect it. They left maintenance ways. The Authority has spent seven decades welding doors and posting guards, but you can't weld physics."

She tapped the base of the tower. "Ground level: four checkpoints, biometric, rotating every six hours. Above that, service elevators—sealed since the Meridian era, because somebody upstairs got nervous about who could ride up unannounced." A thin smile. "Nervous men weld doors. Nervous men also forget what's behind them."

Her finger climbed the spire's shaft, past the habitation bands, past the relay decks, to a cluster of heat-exchange nodes two-thirds of the way up.

"Cooling vents. Primary loop runs coolant down from the transmission crown to heat sinks buried in the shelf rock. The ducts are big enough to crawl—barely. They were sized for maintenance drones, and drones don't complain about elbows." She looked up at Xanthe. "You'll complain about elbows."

"I've smuggled myself inside a cryo-pod for eleven hours," he said. "I can complain quietly."

"There are three access mouths to the vent system. Two are monitored—thermal sensors, camera coverage, the works." She tapped a point on the chart's western edge, where the spire's root met a tangle of old industrial infrastructure half-buried under slag. "The third is here. Condensate outflow, number nine. It drains into a runoff channel that my people have been quietly salvaging around for twenty years. The Authority thinks it's silted shut. It isn't. We've kept it clear—not because we planned this, but because a clear drain is a door, and wreckers keep doors."

Odile studied the chart with the particular intensity Xanthe was learning to read: the inspector's habit of looking for the flaw in any story, turned now toward helping instead of catching. "Thermal sensors on the other mouths will flag anomalous heat the moment we open this one. If they cross-reference—"

"They won't tonight," Iyo said. "Because tonight they'll be looking somewhere else."

* * *

The somewhere else was a decommissioned ore hauler named *Charity's Tithe*, beached on the northern scrapfields like the skeleton of a leviathan, and it was going to be on fire in approximately four hours.

Iyo laid it out over supper—reclaimed protein, algae flatbread, tea brewed from pine needles someone had hoarded since before the Quiet. Around the burn-barrel, the wreckers leaned in. There were maybe forty of them in the camp proper: hull-breakers, cable-riggers, a woman who could recite the mass tolerances of every drive core ever licensed in Sector Nine. People the sectors had used up and written off. People who remembered.

"Authority patrol pattern shifts tomorrow at 0400," Iyo said. "Rotation change, fresh shift, all eyes reset. So we give them something to look at tonight." She jerked her chin at a broad-shouldered wrecker named Doss Kell, whose family had been breaking ships since the war. "The *Tithe*'s reactor housing is already cracked—we've been bleeding her slow for weeks. Tomorrow night she goes up. Tonight, we make sure everyone with a badge knows she's going to go up."

"A distress call?" Odile asked.

"A *fire*. Real smoke, real alarms, a beacon squawking emergency codes on a registered hulk inside Authority jurisdiction. They can't ignore it—protocol says hazardous materials response, full perimeter, air support. Every drone within two hundred kilometers pivots to watch a dead ship burn." Iyo's smile showed too much gum and no warmth at all. "The Authority has rules. Rules are wonderful things, in the right hands."

"And while they're watching the fire," Xanthe said slowly, "we climb into their tower."

"While they're watching the fire," Iyo agreed, "you crawl up a drainpipe into the throat of everything they've built. And then—" she paused, and for a moment the dry irony fell away and something older looked out of her face, something that had counted names around fires like this one for thirty years, "—you say what needs saying."

Nobody spoke for a moment. The fire popped. Somewhere out in the dark, a hull groaned as it cooled.

It was Odile who broke it, quietly. "You understand what happens to this camp if we fail. Roe won't send inspectors next time. He'll send the kind of people who make records disappear."

"We've been disappeared for thirty years," said Doss Kell. His voice wasn't angry; that was the worst part. It was tired, the way bedrock is tired. "Being noticed again—even as targets—is more than we've been allowed since the war. You're not endangering us, inspector. You're reminding the sector we exist."

Xanthe looked around the fire—at the faces lit orange, scarred and wind-burned and ordinary—and felt something shift in his chest that had no clean name. For thirty years his rule had been simple: never belong anywhere, because belonging could be taken away, and he'd already lost everyone belonging had ever given him. Lyra. His parents, in the way that mattered. Even himself, folded small and carried far.

But these people had lost everything too, and they had answered it not by vanishing but by staying—staying near the worlds that discarded them, keeping their doors clear, keeping their count.

Purpose, he thought, is just grief that finally found somewhere to put itself down.

* * *

They spent the next day preparing, and the preparation had the rhythm of a smuggling run—which meant it felt, absurdly, like coming home.

Iyo's people stripped the Lark Ascending's hold of everything identifiable and re-registered her transponder under a wrecker salvage code, one of a dozen dead hulls' identities the camp maintained like heirlooms. Xanthe watched a sixteen-year-old named Wren—a girl born after the Quiet, who had never seen a customs gate—solder a bypass chip into his own ship's transponder housing, and felt the strange vertigo of his life being handled by strangers who treated his secrets as common tools.

"Three crystals," Odile said, laying them out on the Lark's mess table in a line. They caught the cabin light like drops of frozen water. "The party. The order. Your mother's hand letting go. That's all we take."

"I know the plan."

"Say it anyway. Saying it is how we find the holes."

He made himself look at them. He'd played the first two alone in the dark of his berth, and once with Odile silent beside him, and each time the crystals had done what memory-crystals do: poured someone else's experienced hours directly into his skull until he couldn't tell where the dead ended and he began. The party—laughter, music, a woman raising a glass to a station full of living people the archives insisted had never existed. The order—the intercom voice, smooth and reasonable, Director Cassian Roe explaining that a count must be conducted, that sections must be sealed for accuracy, that this was for everyone's safety. Bureaucracy with a knife in its sleeve.

And the third crystal he still hadn't finished. He'd gotten as far as his mother's face—younger than he remembered her, harried, resolute—and the crowd of evacuees she was escorting, and then he'd pulled the crystal out because his hands were shaking too badly to hold it steady. Some cargoes you carry your whole life without opening. He was going to have to open this one in front of nine sectors.

"Party first," he said aloud. "Let them see the faces. Twelve hundred people laughing, working, dancing—make the sector fall in love with the dead before we tell them how the dead died. Then the order. Then—" his jaw tightened, "—then the third. And threaded through all of it, the children's testimony. Odile's recordings. Forty voices finishing each other's memories, corroborating details no single edited archive could fake."

"Timing?"

"The Halcyon Wake countdown gives us a window. When the reactor goes critical—or looks like it might—every screen in nine sectors locks onto crisis coverage. Roe will want to control that narrative; he's already broadcasting his soothing lies about radiation delusion. But crisis means open channels. Emergency bandwidth gets prioritized, authentication protocols loosen, and the spire's systems accept priority overrides from..." Odile tapped the crystal case, "...sources carrying valid Records Authority credentials."

"Your credentials."

"My former credentials." Her mouth twisted. "They haven't revoked them yet. Officially I'm on medical leave pending review of my conduct during the quarantine interviews. In reality, I'm standing in a scrapfield planning treason with smugglers." She said it lightly, but Xanthe saw her flex her right hand—the hand that had held a service weapon for fifteen years, that had signed seizure orders, that had once nearly caught him at Ceres Gate and let him go for reasons she still hadn't fully explained. Defection, he was learning, wasn't a single decision. It was a hundred small ones, made daily, each one costing a piece of the person you'd been.

"You could still turn back," he said. "Walk into any Authority post, tell them it was coercion. They'd believe you. I'm the criminal here."

"I spent fifteen years enforcing a version of the truth," Odile said. "I interviewed children who remember a massacre and wrote reports calling it confabulation because the record said so. The record said so because people like me didn't check." She closed the crystal case. "No. I'm done being part of the erasure. Whatever this costs."

* * *

They went over the route until Xanthe could walk it blindfolded, which was the point.

Iyo's chart, annotated in three generations of wrecker handwriting, broke the infiltration into legs. Leg one: approach through the runoff channel under the cover of the *Tithe*'s fire, two kilometers of culvert on foot, low ceiling, standing water, cold enough to make teeth ache. Leg two: condensate outflow nine—a welded grate that Doss Kell's cutting torch would open at 0300 and reseal behind them, leaving no trace but a bead of fresh weld that wouldn't be inspected for weeks. Leg three: the vent system itself, four kilometers of ductwork climbing the spire's interior shaft, marked at intervals with Iyo's notes—*drone junction, avoid, thermal bridge*; *fan cycle 90 sec, move between cycles*; *here the duct narrows, breathe in*.

Leg four was the transmission deck itself, and that was where the chart ran out of certainty.

"The vents discharge into the heat-exchange gallery one level below the transmission floor," Iyo said. "From there you need to cross an open maintenance bay, forty meters, exposed, covered by two cameras and a roving guard. No wrecker has ever been past that point. This is where my map ends and your problem begins."

"So we solve it the way I've always solved problems," Xanthe said. "With paperwork."

Odile raised an eyebrow.

"Every facility in the sector runs on work orders. Nobody questions a technician with a valid chit and a bored expression—I've walked into restricted yards for twenty years on forged manifests. Give me an hour with the camp's fabrication bench and I'll have maintenance credentials for a coolant-loop inspection, scheduled and logged through the Authority's own queue system." He smiled thinly. "The beautiful thing about bureaucracies is that they trust their own forms more than their own eyes. Roe taught the whole sector that lesson. We're just going to grade him on it."

"And the guard rotation?" Odile asked.

Iyo produced a battered slate. "My people have been counting. Three months of counts." She flicked it on: columns of timestamps, guard movements, camera sweeps, elevator use. The wreckers had been watching the tower the way they watched everything—patiently, obsessively, without ever quite knowing why, the way you keep a door clear. "Night rotation changes at 0400. Between 0340 and 0410, the maintenance bay runs on a skeleton watch—one guard, cameras cycling on a ninety-second overlap gap. Ninety seconds to cross forty meters. It's tight."

"Tight is fine," Xanthe said. "Tight is Tuesday."

Odile almost laughed. Almost. "You realize if we're caught, there's no forged manifest charming enough for this. This is terrorism in their courts. Sedition."

"It's testimony," Xanthe said. "We're going to testify. The only difference is the size of the courtroom."

* * *

The last evening before the run, Iyo Sen took him aside, out past the burn-barrel, to the edge of the scrapfields where the wreckers kept their own memorial: a row of hull plates driven into the ground like grave markers, each stamped with a name. Most were wrecker names. At the far end, newer, brighter metal: a plate reading MERIDIAN — 1,204 — WE COUNTED.

"When I die," Iyo said, "put me under one of those. Not the fancy end. The plain part, with the others." She looked up at the spire, a needle of light stabbing the sky. "Thirty years I've watched that thing talk for them. Telling the sector what to think, what to remember, what never happened. Tomorrow you're going to make it say something true." She turned to him, and her one hand gripped his forearm with surprising strength. "I'm too old to crawl up a drainpipe, Vane. So you carry me up there. Understand? Every name you speak through that tower, I'm in it. Don't leave any out."

"One thousand two hundred and four," he said. "I have the manifest memorized."

"Good boy." She released him and shuffled back toward the fire, then paused. "One more thing. The informant question."

He stiffened. "What about it?"

"Roe's offering a fortune for your location. Fortunes melt loyalties faster than torches melt hull plate. Someone in this camp, or someone who passes through it, may already have sold your name." She said it without apology—that was Iyo; she dealt in facts the way other people dealt in kindness. "Doss swears his people are solid, and mostly they are. But mostly isn't certain, and tomorrow you'll be inside the one place in the sector where being sold means dying. So: trust the plan, trust the fire, trust the vents. Trust people exactly as far as you can see them, and no farther."

"You could have told me this gently."

"Gentleness is for people with time." She walked back toward the light of the fire, brass arm catching sparks. "You have a tower to steal."

* * *

At 0240, Xanthe stood in the mouth of the runoff channel with Odile, both of them in scavenged thermal gear, packs sealed, the three crystals riding in a shielded case against his chest where he could feel them like a second heartbeat. Two kilometers away, the *Charity's Tithe* was beginning to smolder on schedule—first thin threads of smoke, then the alarm beacons waking, then the distant stutter of Authority drones lifting from their roosts like startled birds.

The fire worked exactly as promised. They watched the searchlights swing north, heard the emergency channels saturate, felt the whole attention of the sector pivot toward a burning hulk full of nothing.

"Leg one," Odile murmured.

They waded into the cold.

The channel swallowed them to the waist, black water smelling of rust and old industry, the ceiling close enough to touch. Xanthe moved by feel and by the faint chemlight glow Odile carried cupped in her palm, counting paces, checking landmarks against Iyo's annotations. Purpose had replaced evasion so completely that he barely recognized the sensation: for thirty years every step had been a step away from something. Now every step was a step toward. Toward the tower. Toward the broadcast. Toward Lyra, waiting in her quarantine ward with the other children, insisting the count wasn't finished. Toward his mother's unfinished confession, and the ending he intended to write for it in front of nine worlds.

Outflow nine appeared as a disc of deeper darkness, and then Doss Kell's torch flared blue-white, precise and quick, and the grate swung free.

The vent mouth breathed warm air at them—the tower's exhalation, the heat of ten thousand transmissions rising from the crown above. Xanthe looked up into the duct: a vertical throat climbing forty kilometers into the dark, laddered rungs every few meters, fan housings thrumming somewhere overhead on their patient ninety-second cycles.

"Ninety seconds per cycle," Odile whispered. "Move between them."

They climbed.

It was brutal in exactly the way Iyo had promised—elbows barking on duct seams, shoulders wedged, breath loud in the confined dark—but Xanthe had spent a lifetime in tight places with his heart hammering, and this time the hammering felt different. Cleaner. Each rung was a sentence in the testimony. Each fan cycle they waited out was a pause before a word that needed saying precisely.

At the drone junction they flattened against the wall and held their breath while a maintenance unit whirred past close enough to feel its wash. At the narrowing, they breathed in, exactly as instructed. Four kilometers became three, became two, became one, and the hum of the tower grew around them until it felt less like machinery and more like a held note, the whole structure waiting to speak.

Then the duct opened, and they dropped down into the heat-exchange gallery—one level below the transmission deck, steam-warmed, dimly lit, and utterly silent except for the distant footfalls of a single guard making his rounds forty meters of open bay away.

Xanthe pressed against the vent housing, pulled up his forged inspection chit, and checked the time.

0338. Twenty-two minutes until rotation change. Ninety-second gaps between camera sweeps. One crossing. One chance.

Beside him, Odile exhaled slowly, and he realized she was trembling—not with fear, he thought, but with the sheer strangeness of arriving, after a lifetime of enforcing silence, at the threshold of the room where silence was manufactured.

"Ready?" he whispered.

She nodded. "Say the names, Xanthe. All of them."

He stepped out of the shadows—

—and froze, because far above them, muffled by decks of steel, a sound was beginning to bleed down through the tower's bones: alarms. Not the *Tithe*'s fire, not the rotation change. Something else, closer, rising fast—and with it, on the maintenance band of his smuggler's earpiece, a voice he didn't know saying words that turned his blood to ice water:

*"All units—all units—be advised, priority target located. Repeat: the smuggler Vane is inside the perimeter. The spire is compromised. Lock it down."*

Someone had sold them. And the net was already closing.
