## 27. Ambush in the Scrapfields

The camp woke him the way it always did: with the smell of solder and recycled coffee, the groan of hull-plate wind chimes strung between the ribs of a dead hauler, and Iyo Sen's voice cracking across the scrapfields like a man who had never learned to whisper.

"Vane! Your girl's got your engine apart again!"

Xanthe rolled out of his bunk in the gutted shuttle they'd given him—a coffin-shaped thing called the *Patient Mercy* that some wrecker had once flown out of a war nobody named—and pulled on his jacket over the three crystals strapped against his ribs. He'd slept with them there for six nights now. He would sleep with them there for the rest of his life, however long that turned out to be.

He ducked through the hatch into gray morning light. The scrapfields stretched to every horizon: the carcasses of freighters and cutters and one ancient colony barge stacked in dunes of stripped alloy, their bones picked clean by generations of people the sectors had decided not to see. Smoke rose from cook-fires. Children—real children, born here, never de-aged by anything stranger than hunger—chased a dog made mostly of scar tissue between the hulks.

Odile stood ankle-deep in the *Lark Ascending*'s open engine cowling, sleeves rolled, hair tied back with a length of wire, cursing at his fuel recycler with the fluency of someone who had grown up around dockhands.

"It's not my fault," she said without turning around. "Your recycler is held together with prayer."

"Prayer holds better than Authority parts," Xanthe said. "They stopped making good ones after the Quiet started."

Iyo Sen sat on an ammo crate near the fire pit, sharpening a knife that was older than the nine sectors' silence. She was small and folded like a closed hand, seventy-some years packed tight, her forearms mapped with burns from a lifetime of cutting ships open. She'd been the first to believe him. The first to say the word *Meridian* out loud where others only mouthed it. She'd told him about the count—1,204—in a voice so flat it had taken him a full day to understand she wasn't reciting a rumor. She was reciting a roll call she'd kept in her head for thirty years because no record anywhere else would hold it.

"You look terrible," she told him now, not looking up from the blade.

"I slept fine."

"You slept like a man counting exits." She tested the edge against her thumb. "Good. Keep doing that."

He crouched by the fire and poured himself the camp's coffee, which tasted like hot rust and was the best thing he'd drunk in years. Around them the camp went about its morning: riggers hauling plate on magnetic sleds, a woman teaching signal-lamp code to a circle of kids, the old colony barge's bridge converted into a mess hall where someone was frying something that smelled almost legal.

Three days ago he'd stood in front of these people and told them everything. The crystals. Meridian. His mother's seal. The plan forming in Odile's head like frost spreading across glass—the broadcast spire, the crisis coverage, every screen in Sector Nine tuned to the dying *Halcyon Wake*. He'd expected skepticism. He'd gotten instead a wiry old woman saying, "We've been waiting thirty years for somebody to carry this," and forty pairs of hands starting to work.

The wreckers were mapping security rotations off salvaged Authority transponders. A former spire technician named Kesh—who'd been decommissioned with prejudice and a falsified psych file—was drawing cooling-vent schematics from memory onto a sheet of hull plating. Odile drilled the approach runs. And Xanthe sat with the three crystals against his ribs and tried to learn how to belong to people again, which was harder than any smuggling run he'd ever flown.

"You're thinking about the spire," Iyo said.

"I'm thinking about how many ways it goes wrong."

"List them."

"Incoming fire on the ascent. Vent seals locked down. Roe moving the broadcast schedule. Odile's face on every wanted board by tomorrow. Me dead in a vent shaft with the truth still in my jacket."

"And?"

"And the crystals getting destroyed with me, and 1,204 people staying dead forever."

Iyo finally looked up. Her eyes were pale as slag-glass and missed nothing. "Then we don't put all the eggs in one jacket, do we. Kesh copied the manifests last night. Two crystals stay in the camp, sealed in the *Mercy*'s black box. You carry one. If you die, we finish it."

"That's not—" He stopped. Swallowed. "That's not your fight."

Iyo Sen laughed, a dry rattle like gravel in a hopper. "Boy. I watched the rescue trawlers get turned away from Meridian's debris field. I watched the burn clouds from eleven kilometers out and then I watched the archives say there was never anything there to burn. I have been fighting this since before you could walk. You're just the first courier who brought me ammunition." She went back to her knife. "Now stop thanking me and drink your coffee before it gets worse."

He drank the coffee. It got worse. It was, he thought, the closest thing to family breakfast he'd had in thirty years, and he was so busy holding that feeling carefully, like a crystal he was afraid to crack, that he didn't notice the sky until the dog started screaming.

*

The dog heard them first. Then the sentry on the colony barge's spine rang the camp bell—not the two-stroke weather warning, but the fast triple-stroke that meant *skimmers, low, coming fast*, and the whole scrapfield changed shape in under ten seconds.

Xanthe had seen camps scatter before. He'd scattered them himself, early in his career, when a rival crew paid him to buzz a settlement and drive them off a salvage claim. Nothing prepared him for watching this one move. Nobody panicked. Nobody ran blind. The children vanished into pre-drilled bolt-holes inside the colony barge's water tanks. The sleds swung to cover the lanes. Riggers who'd spent the morning stripping plate suddenly stood in doorways with cutting torches set to beam and shotguns older than the Quiet, and the whole camp became what it had always secretly been: a garrison of people who had already survived one erasure and refused to be erased twice.

"They're not even hiding it," Odile said, appearing at his shoulder with a rifle she must have pulled from under the *Lark*'s floor plates. "No transponder spoofing. No stealth approach. They want us to see them coming."

"Intimidation," Xanthe said.

"Announcement." Her jaw was tight. "Roe wants witnesses. He wants the sector to know what happens to people who remember."

Six skimmers came over the eastern dune-line in wedge formation, gunmetal and matte-black, running lights dead, Authority crest stenciled white on their flanks like a brand on livestock. Behind them, higher up, hung a drop-cutter—the kind of ship used to breach station hulls during the war, repurposed now for breaching communities.

The loudspeaker voice was calm, professional, almost gentle. *"Residents of unauthorized settlement. This is a Records Authority enforcement action pursuant to Statute Nine-Seven. Surrender the fugitive Xanthe Vane and all contraband data media. Comply and no charges will be filed against resident personnel. You have four minutes."*

Four minutes. Xanthe felt something cold slide down his spine. Not fear—recognition. Four minutes was exactly long enough for the camp to comply or die, and exactly short enough that no one could plausibly claim they hadn't been warned. It was bureaucratic mercy. It was the same voice, he realized, that had ordered the count on Meridian Station. Calm. Professional. Gentle. The sound of a door sealing while it tells you it's for your safety.

"We should give them me," he said.

"No," said Odile.

"It buys you all—"

"*No.*" She grabbed his collar. "Listen to me. They are not leaving witnesses either way. That's why the drop-cutter's here. You surrender, they take you, they take the crystals, they level this camp for 'sanitation,' and the report says armed smugglers resisted. I've written those reports, Xanthe. I know how they read." Her grip tightened. "So we don't surrender. We scatter, we run, and you live to climb that spire."

The four minutes ran out the way four minutes always do—too fast.

*

The first shell took the old *Lark Ascending*.

Xanthe was twenty meters away when the drop-cutter's railgun spoke, one flat crack of displaced air, and thirty years of his life came apart into burning confetti. The patched freighter that had carried him past customs a hundred times, that had carried the Meridian crystals unknowing for a decade, that had been the only home he'd allowed himself—he watched its spine fold and its fuel cells bloom orange and its name peel off the hull in a sheet of flame.

He didn't feel grief yet. Grief would come later, billed afterward, the way it always was. Right now there was only motion.

"Black box!" Odile shouted over the second impact. "Kesh has it—go, go!"

The camp's answer to the bombardment was beautiful and doomed and utterly characteristic: they fought back with what they had. Cutting torches lanced out from the hulks, slicing a skimmer's sensor mast clean off. Someone had mounted a decommissioned point-defense cannon on the colony barge and it walked tracer fire across the skimmers' formation, forcing them wide. Signal lamps flashed coded warnings down the salvage lanes. The wreckers didn't stand and die; they melted, dispersed, folded into the dunes of dead ships they knew better than anyone alive, drawing the Authority forces into a maze where every corridor was a trap made of razor-edged alloy.

But there were six skimmers and a drop-cutter, and there was one camp.

Xanthe ran the route Kesh had drilled into them—between the severed bow of a container ship and the reactor housing of a tug, down a lane floored with shredded insulation, toward the secondary pad where the *Patient Mercy* waited with its engines already warm because Iyo Sen had insisted, three days ago, that they be warm *every morning* from now on.

Kesh met him at the pad with the black box under one arm and blood running from a cut above her eye. "Box is intact. Manifests, copies of both testimony recordings, the vent schematics." She shoved it into the shuttle's cargo cradle. "Two crystals inside, sealed. One stays with you."

"Get in," Xanthe said. "Both of you. We can take—"

"Shuttle flies better light and you know it." Kesh grinned through the blood. "Besides, somebody's got to keep their guns busy while you lift. That's the fun part."

That was when the third skimmer came around the tug's reactor housing at rooftop height, guns already tracking the pad, and Iyo Sen stepped out from behind the *Mercy*'s landing strut with the camp's oldest shotgun in her hands and absolutely no expression at all.

"Iyo—" Xanthe started.

"Board your ship, boy."

The skimmer's chin-gun spun up. Iyo raised the shotgun and fired at the cockpit canopy—not to kill, Xanthe understood later, not even really to hit, but to make the pilot flinch, to drag the gun's arc off the shuttle, to buy three seconds. The skimmer jinked. Its return fire chewed across the pad, stitching holes through the *Mercy*'s wing, sparking off the deck a meter from Xanthe's boots.

And Iyo Sen, seventy-some years old, survivor of the burn clouds, keeper of the count, stood her ground in the open and reloaded.

"BOARD YOUR SHIP," she screamed at him, and it was the first time he'd ever heard her raise her voice, and it sounded like every name she'd ever carried in her head being shouted at a universe that refused to write them down.

He boarded. He hated himself for boarding. The canopy sealed on Kesh shoving him into the pilot's seat and Odile slapping the harness closed across his chest, and through the viewport he saw the skimmer swing back around, and he saw Iyo Sen look straight at it, and he saw her mouth move—one word, two syllables, and he would spend the rest of his life believing he knew which two—and then the chin-gun fired and the oldest wrecker in the scrapfields came apart in the act of standing between the truth and the men sent to bury it.

The *Patient Mercy* lifted through smoke.

*

He flew badly for the first hundred kilometers. His hands knew the controls; the rest of him was back on that pad, replaying it frame by frame, searching for the version where he'd moved faster, where he'd tackled her behind the strut, where he'd surrendered himself four minutes earlier and none of it happened. There was no such version. There was only the one where an old woman had chosen, deliberately, with open eyes, to spend herself buying his escape—and had done it without hesitation, without drama, the way she'd done everything: flatly, practically, as if it were simply the next task on a list thirty years long.

"She knew," Odile said quietly from the copilot's seat. She had a plasma scorch along her forearm she was dressing one-handed and she didn't look at him, giving him the mercy of pretending the cockpit wasn't small enough to share grief in. "Xanthe. Look at me. She *knew*. She told us to keep the crystals split between the box and your jacket. She warmed your engines every morning. She built her own exit from this the way she built everything—on purpose." Her voice cracked on the last word and she let it, and kept going. "You don't get to steal her choice and turn it into your guilt. That's just another way of making it about you."

He flew. The scrapfields fell away beneath them, the smoke column shrinking to a smudge, then a scar, then nothing. Somewhere behind them the Authority skimmers were picking through a camp of empty bolt-holes and burned hulks, finding no Vane, no crystals, no witnesses who would talk—and finding, too, that the wreckers had already begun filtering back in, dragging their wounded, burying their dead, and starting, before the smoke cleared, to rebuild. People who remembered the war the sectors denied did not scare easily. But Iyo Sen's body lay on that pad, and no amount of rebuilding would change it.

At the two-hundred-kilometer mark, Kesh's voice came through on the wrecker band, ragged but steady. "Camp's standing. Eleven hurt, none critical but us. They torched the barge mess hall and left. Roe's people don't occupy—they make examples and go." A pause, static filling it. "We put her in the ground at the north ridge, facing the debris fields. Old custom. So she can keep watch on what's left." Another pause, longer. "She left something for you. Was in her kit, marked with your name. Picking up the relay at Grid Seven-Nine in ninety seconds. Receiving now."

The *Mercy*'s comm unit chattered, buffered, and printed.

It was a single sheet, actual paper, soft with handling—the kind of thing a person writes and keeps and does not send. In a cramped, iron-hard hand:

*Vane — if you're reading this I'm dead and you're not, which was the arrangement. Don't waste it. Three things. One: the count is 1,204 and you will say every name, no shortcuts, no summaries. Names are the whole weapon. Two: the girl on the Halcyon Wake is your sister whether she knows it or not, and knowing it or not, she'll need you in both directions. Three: when it's done, come drink the bad coffee somewhere and tell me how it ends. I'll hear you. Dead isn't deaf out here.*

Below that, in smaller letters, as if added later, almost shyly:

*You belonged here from the first night. Took you three days to notice. Slow for a smuggler.*

Xanthe read it twice and then folded it and put it inside his jacket, against the last crystal, against his heartbeat, and flew.

Behind them the smoke column stood up straight in the windless gray, a thin dark line drawn between the ground and the sky, like a signature on a document the sectors would soon be forced to read.

Ahead, on the horizon, catching the weak morning sun, the Sector Nine broadcast spire rose out of the city haze—two kilometers of lattice and light, the tallest structure human hands had built, wired to every screen on every world.

Odile checked her sidearm, then her watch, then looked over at him with eyes that had gone hard and clear as slag-glass.

"Security rotation changes in fourteen hours," she said. "The vents are open. Let's go finish her list."
