## 30. Broadcast: The First Thousand

The transmission deck of the Sector Nine broadcast spire smelled of ozone and old dust, a cathedral built for a voice no one had used in thirty years. Xanthe Vane stood before the master console with three memory-crystals in his jacket pocket and blood on his sleeve that was not his own.

Behind him, Odile Ferran sat propped against the base of the relay column, her left side dark where the Authority round had torn through the padding of her jacket and the flesh beneath it. She had pressed a sealant patch to the wound with the same brisk efficiency she'd once used on his forged manifests, and now she held the uplink tablet in both hands like a woman holding a door shut against a flood.

"Signal's ours," she said. Her voice was steady. Her hands were not. "Every screen in Nine. And if the old trunk lines still handshake the way they did before the Quiet—" she tapped through three menus, "—they do—we can push it outward. All nine."

"They'll cut us in minutes," Xanthe said.

"Then we'd better be worth watching." She looked up at him, gray-faced, gray-eyed, and for the first time since Ceres Gate she smiled without any irony in it at all. "Go on, smuggler. Break your last rule."

He set the first crystal into the reader.

* * *

It began, as all lies begin, with something ordinary.

A party.

The crystal bloomed onto every public screen in Sector Nine—every transit concourse, every hab-dome commons wall, every bar terminal and customs kiosk and hospital waiting room—and what nine sectors saw was a room full of people dancing.

Meridian Station, four months before its official decommissioning date. Meridian Station, which the archives described as an unmanned research platform, sealed and shuttered in an orderly administrative close. Meridian Station, population: zero.

Population: one thousand two hundred and four.

The camera moved through the crowd the way memory moves—unsteady, warm-edged, greedy for detail. A woman in a green engineering smock laughing so hard she had to grip a railing. Two men attempting a dance from a world none of them had been born on, getting it wrong, not caring. Children running between legs with paper streamers. A band assembled from salvaged instruments, a cello with a cracked body played by someone who loved it anyway. Someone off-camera shouted *Meridian forever!* and a hundred voices roared it back, and the sound was so alive, so unbearably alive, that in a transit concourse on Ceres Gate a customs inspector's assistant stopped mid-scan and put her hand over her mouth.

Xanthe watched it from behind the console and felt his anger arrive exactly as he had known it would—not as fire but as arithmetic. *They were real. They laughed. They had favorite songs.* Every face that crossed the frame was a name the archives had never carried. He began reading them aloud as they appeared, quietly, into the deck's open channel, because someone had to say them and there was no one else.

"Hollis Anwar. Devi Marchetti-Osei. Tam and Ruth Ilves. Petra—"

"You don't know their names," Odile said softly.

"The manifest does. It's in my head. I've been reading it for six hours." His voice was flat as ballast. "Someone should hear them out loud."

Across Sector Nine, screens showed the unwritten dead being born back into the world one laugh at a time.

* * *

In Quarantine Ward Nine, forty children sat on the floor in a circle around a med-tech's portable display, because Nurse Adeyemi had made a decision ten seconds after the broadcast began and would answer for it later or not at all.

The children did not gasp. That was the thing the ward staff would describe afterward, the thing that would follow them home: the children did not react with surprise. They reacted with recognition. They leaned toward the screen the way the cold lean toward heat.

"That's the solstice party," said a boy named Callum Reeve, who had been fifty-one years old eleven weeks ago.

"I danced with Auntie Sefa," said a girl of about seven who had been a navigation officer.

"It rained confetti because the recyclers glitched," said another, "and nobody fixed it for two days because everyone kept saying just one more hour."

And then, without signal or cue, without looking at one another, the forty of them began to speak in unison—a low, layered chorus, each child a half-beat offset from the next, the way a congregation says amen:

*"Hollis Anwar. Devi Marchetti-Osei. Tam and Ruth Ilves—"*

Nurse Adeyemi stood very still in the doorway and let them finish the first hundred names before she reached for the ward recorder.

* * *

On the Halcyon Wake, drifting dead-still above the slow red pulse of its failing reactor, Lyra Vane watched the party on a cracked maintenance panel and hummed.

She was eight years old, or she had been made eight again, and she remembered two childhoods the way you remember two different houses you once lived in. In one of them, a boy taught her a lullaby on a station balcony. In the other, she had grown up, married, buried a husband, crewed a freighter for thirty years, and come home young. Both memories were hers. Both were true. She had decided, with the terrible clarity of children and the very old, that this was allowed.

"They're showing the party," she told the others. "The first part."

"Then the sirens come," said Captain Okafor, who wore a child's body and a captain's patience like an ill-fitting coat. "After the party, the sirens."

"After the sirens, the counting," said forty voices, gently, together.

Lyra touched the screen, over the face of a laughing woman in green.

"That's my mother," she said. "Not Xanthe's mother. My other mother. The one who let go."

No one asked her to explain. On the Halcyon Wake, no one ever had to explain anything twice; the count lived in all of them equally.

* * *

The second crystal went in ninety seconds later, when the party's warmth had done its work—when the sectors had seen the faces, learned to love them a little, learned that these had been *people*—and Xanthe gave the console the nod.

The sirens came.

The memory shifted hard, the way trauma shifts memory: colors draining, sound going thin and metallic. The same grand hall, now a muster point. The same thousand-odd people, now afraid. And over everything, everywhere, from every speaker in every section of the station, a voice—calm, measured, professionally reassuring, the voice of a man reading from prepared remarks:

*"This is Director Cassian Roe, speaking for the Sector Records Authority oversight office. Please remain calm. This is not an evacuation. This is a routine census procedure. You will proceed in an orderly fashion to your designated sections, where you will be counted. When the count is complete, the sections will be reopened. There is nothing to fear. This is only a counting."*

In the memory, a man near the camera called out: "Counted for what? Counted by whom?" And the voice replied, with infinite patience, *"Everyone will be counted. No one will be lost."*

The camera turned. Sections were closing—not doors slamming, but bulkheads descending with hydraulic slowness, the unhurried motion of machinery following orders. People pressed against the lowering barriers. A mother lifted her daughter up so she could see over the crowd. Somewhere a child was crying and an adult was lying to it in a bright, breaking voice: *it's just a game, we're playing stations, we'll all be found at the end—*

*"This is only a counting,"* said Cassian Roe's voice, one more time, to one more section, and the bulkhead met the deck with a sound like a full stop.

In a government housing block on Sector Four, an archivist named Bem Oduya—who had spent nineteen years maintaining the Meridian file, who had personally entered the phrase *unmanned research platform, decommissioned peacefully* into the record under orders he had told himself were probably fine—stood up from his dinner, walked to his terminal, opened the file, and looked at his own entry for a long time.

Then he began writing a letter of testimony. He did not finish it that night. But he started it.

* * *

Odile Ferran had stopped monitoring the intrusion counter twenty minutes ago. It didn't matter anymore how many Authority trace-teams were climbing toward them; the numbers had passed the point where knowing helped. What mattered was uptime. Seconds of uptime. Each second the broadcast lived, it was being recorded—pirated, mirrored, copied onto private drives and black-market relays and wrecker scrap-rigs across nine sectors. You could kill a broadcast. You could not kill a copy.

"Third crystal," she said. "Whatever's on it—now. Before they take the trunk line."

Xanthe hesitated. One breath. She saw him hesitate, and understood: the third crystal was the worst one. The third crystal had his mother in it.

"Now," she said again, gentler.

He fed it in.

* * *

The third memory was shot from a corridor camera's angle, handheld, urgent—the perspective of someone running. Evacuees filled the passage, moving toward the docking ring, and threading through them came a woman in a Records Authority field coat, directing traffic with her arms, her voice hoarse: *this way, keep moving, children first, this way.*

Xanthe knew the face. Half the sector knew it now; the broadcast had already aired the authorization chip, the contracts, the signature. Maren Vane, Records Compliance Officer, Meridian Station. His mother. Younger than he had ever known her, thirty years old and drowning.

The evacuees reached the docking ring. The shuttle bay doors stood open. And then the intercom spoke, and the whole corridor flinched:

*"All personnel be advised. Docking ring access is now restricted to authorized census staff. Civilian embarkation is suspended pending completion of the count. Repeat: civilian embarkation is suspended. This is for your safety. Everyone will be counted. No one will be lost."*

The crowd's hope made a sound Xanthe would carry to his grave—a single collective exhale, a thousand people deflating at once, turning back into the corridors they'd come from because the voice had told them to and the voice was the Authority and the Authority was the sky itself.

Maren Vane stood in the middle of it, radio in her hand, and the memory's owner—running, filming, desperate—caught her arm.

"Maren. The shuttle. We can fit forty more. Take the children, at least—"

Her face, in the memory, is the reason this chapter exists. Not monstrous. Not cold. *Torn.* A woman doing arithmetic with her own soul, and losing.

"If I open the ring, they'll seal my family file," she said. "They have my file. They have—" her voice dropped, broke, "—they have my children's names."

"Then let me take them. Give me your codes and stay. Stay with them, Maren. If you walk away from this ring, you know what the count means."

For three seconds—one, two, three, the longest three seconds in nine sectors' living memory—Maren Vane held out the code key.

Then the intercom crackled her name. Then it recited her children's names. Then it said, kindly, *"Officer Vane. Your count."*

She closed her hand. She turned away from the open shuttle. She walked back into the corridors, toward the sections, toward the counting, and the memory's owner filmed her going until the recording ends in static and, underneath the static, faintly, the beginning of a scream that belongs to no one and everyone.

One thousand two hundred and four people heard *no one will be lost*, and believed it, because believing it was easier than the alternative, and the voice kept its promise in the cruelest possible grammar: no one was lost. Everyone was counted.

* * *

The montage broke across the nine worlds like weather.

On Sector Two, in a mining cantina, men who had fought in the war no one names stood up from their drinks without a word and faced the screen, the way you face a burial.

On Sector Seven, a schoolteacher turned the broadcast toward her students and said, "This is why we ask who writes the books."

On Sector Five, an entire orbital dock went silent shift-wide; foremen passed the word down the gantries by hand, mouth to ear, *watch the screens, watch the screens,* because some of the dockers were wreckers, and some of the wreckers had known.

On Sector One—the capital, the gilded heart, where the Quiet had been policy before it was practice—an aide burst into a deputy minister's office and said, "You need to see this," and the deputy minister watched the third crystal to its end and then said, very quietly, "Get me everything we have on Meridian. Real everything. Off-book everything." Because power survives by reading the wind, and the wind had changed.

And in the scrapfields outside Sector Nine's patrol envelope, the wreckers gathered at Iyo Sen's fire ring—the ring where Iyo Sen had told the truth for forty years to anyone who'd sit down—and an old woman named Ferro laid Iyo's battered tin cup in the center of the ashes, and they listened to the names, and when the count reached the ones Iyo had always recited from memory, Ferro said them along with the broadcast, word for word, pitch perfect, forty years of oral record matching the recovered manifest name for name.

Oral memory versus official record. Oral memory won.

* * *

By the eleventh minute, the transmission deck was shaking.

Not metaphorically—the spire's old trunk amplifiers were cycling hot, pushing a signal volume they hadn't carried since before the Quiet, and the whole upper structure hummed like a struck bell. Dust sifted from the ceiling seams. Odile had gone pale past gray into something translucent, her sealant patch blooming red at the edges, and she tracked the feed-mirrors on her tablet with the focus of the dying and the determined, which are often the same focus.

"One hundred and eighty mirrors," she reported. "Public, private, pirate. They can't pull it now. Even if they cut our uplink, the copies are loose. It's out of the bottle, Xanthe. It's out of every bottle."

He wasn't listening. He was reading names. He had been reading names for eleven minutes, into the open channel, his voice roughened to gravel, and he had reached the seven hundreds, and somewhere around the seven hundreds he had stopped sounding like a smuggler reading a manifest and started sounding like a man at a graveside, and the difference was audible on every screen in nine sectors, and no one who heard it forgot it.

"—Sefa Okonkwo-Bell. Jun Park. Amara, Tobias, and little Ruth Ilves, counted together, the whole family, one entry—"

His voice caught on that one. He let it catch. He had spent thirty years believing silence was safety, invisibility was skill, and the long quiet between the worlds was just how the universe worked. Eleven minutes of reading names had burned all of it down. He was visible now. He was legible now. Every trace-team in the sector knew his name and his heading, and he found—to his own astonishment—that he did not care, that some load-bearing wall inside him had come out and the building was still standing, lighter than it had ever been.

"Xanthe." Odile's hand closed on his wrist. "Look at the mirror count."

He looked. The number had jumped: four hundred, six hundred, nine hundred. And interleaved with the mirror addresses were messages—raw text flooding the spire's ancient public return channel, a channel so old most administrators didn't know it existed, now lighting up line after line after line:

*Sector Three acknowledges. We remember the war. We remember why we stopped speaking. Who did this?*

*Sector Six: medical teams standing by. Is there anyone alive to help?*

*Sector One: this transmission is unauthorized. All citizens are advised—* followed, thirty seconds later, from a different address, also Sector One: *ignore the advisory. We are advised. Keep broadcasting.*

*Sector Four: we are sorry. We are sorry. We are sorry.*

Nine sectors. Nine voices. For the first time in thirty years, the Quiet had broken—not with treaties, not with fleets, but with grief, crossing borders that had been sealed since before Xanthe was born, pouring through a hole his sister's massacre had finally torn wide enough.

Odile read the scrolling messages and pressed her uninjured hand flat against the relay column as if steadying herself against a moving ship. "It's working," she whispered. "God help me, it's actually—"

The console shrieked.

Every status light on the master board went amber at once. A new priority flag slammed across the uplink display in letters half a meter high:

**INCOMING OVERRIDE — SECTOR RECORDS AUTHORITY — DIRECTOR'S AUTHORITY — AUTHENTICATION PENDING**

"They found the root channel," Odile breathed, dragging herself upright against the column, one hand clamped over her wound. "That's a director-level override. That's *him*. He's coming in on his own authentication—he's going to cut the feed and put his version on top of ours, live, while everyone's watching—"

Xanthe stared at the flag, at the authentication field blinking empty, waiting for Director Cassian Roe to enter the codes that would silence a thousand voices for the second time.

And then he saw it—the thing Odile saw a half-second later, the thing that made them look at each other in the humming, shuddering dark of the transmission deck with identical expressions of disbelieving, dangerous hope.

The override required director-level authentication.

And the seal order—the original seal order, the one that had closed the sections on Meridian Station, the one whose signature block Xanthe had aired to nine sectors twelve minutes ago—had been signed with a director-level code.

Roe was about to type his own confession into the authentication field, live, in front of everyone.

"He doesn't know," Odile whispered. "Thirty years ago the codes were different, they've rotated them, but the *authority tier*—oh, you arrogant, careful, meticulous *bastard*, you kept the same tier, you'd never lower yourself—"

The authentication field began to fill. Character by character. The override was coming through.

"Keep reading names," Odile said, hauling herself up the relay column, leaving a smear of red on the metal, her eyes blazing with something beyond pain. "Whatever happens in the next sixty seconds—keep reading names. Don't you dare stop reading names."

Xanthe turned back to the open channel, drew breath, and continued.

"—entry eight hundred and eleven. Yusra Haddad. Station teacher, age thirty-four. *No one will be lost.*"

Somewhere below them, in the roots of the spire, Cassian Roe finished typing.
