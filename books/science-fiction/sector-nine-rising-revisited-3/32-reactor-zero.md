## 32. Reactor Zero

The transmission deck was still humming with the ghost of the broadcast when the first klaxon reached them—thin, distant, rising through the spire's bones like a scream through water.

Xanthe stood at the console with his hands flat on the glass, watching the last of the names scroll across every screen in nine sectors. *Meridian Station. Crew manifest. 1,204 souls.* The feed was live everywhere now. He could see the confirmation lights stacking up in columns: Sector One receiving. Sector Four receiving. Sector Seven, silent for thirty years, *receiving*. Somewhere below him, a world that had spent three decades pretending to be asleep was sitting bolt upright in its bed.

"We did it," Odile said beside him. Her voice was hoarse. There was blood on her sleeve where she'd taken shrapnel on the ascent, and she hadn't mentioned it once. "It's out. It can't be pulled back."

He should have felt triumphant. Instead he felt the way he'd felt standing at airlocks his whole life—that moment before the seal breaks and everything you've been holding in comes rushing out at once.

Then the klaxon again. Closer. And beneath it, a new sound: the deep, subsonic groan of a structure changing its mind about existing.

The spire's emergency channel crackled open on the console. A dispatcher's voice, tight with controlled panic: *"All units—all units—reactor event aboard the Halcyon Wake. Core containment failure is confirmed. This is not a drill. Meltdown sequence initiated, estimated time to criticality fourteen minutes. All evacuation assets to Sector Nine approach corridor. I say again—"*

Fourteen minutes.

Xanthe didn't remember deciding to move. His body decided for him—one heartbeat he was at the console, the next he was running for the maintenance lift, and Odile was shouting his name behind him and then her boots were hitting the deck beside his because of course they were.

"The shuttle dock," he said. "There's a courier berth on level twelve."

"Xanthe." She grabbed his arm and spun him. Her eyes were fierce and wet at the same time. "The Halcyon Wake is going to come apart like a flare. You cannot be inside that corridor when it goes."

"Lyra's on it."

"She's *evacuating*—"

"They're not evacuating." The words came out of him like something torn. "You told me yourself. They won't leave. 'The count isn't finished.' Odile—they're going to stand there. All forty of them. My sister is going to stand in front of that reactor and wait for it, because some part of her believes that if no one witnesses it, it all happens again. It all gets erased again. And she'd be right, wouldn't she? That's exactly what happened last time. Everyone looked away and 1,204 people stopped existing."

Odile's jaw worked. He watched her weigh it—the officer's arithmetic, lives against lives, the cold clean math she'd been trained to do—and watched her throw the math away.

"Level twelve," she said. "Go. I'll get you a corridor."

"You're coming with me?"

"I'm getting the trawlers moving," she said, already turning, already talking into her wrist-comm. "Every scrap-hauler and salvage tug in the outer fields owes me a favor or fears me enough to fake one. If we can't stop the blast we can at least make sure nobody else dies catching it. Go, Xanthe. *Fly.*"

He ran.

* * *

The Lark Ascending was gone. He'd known that since the raid—burned identity, stripped registry, her hull probably sitting in an Authority evidence bay being picked apart by technicians looking for crystals that weren't there anymore. But the courier berth on level twelve had been part of the wrecker network's escape plan, and Iyo Sen's people kept their promises even with Iyo Sen dead. The little ship waiting in the cradle was a scow called the *Second Chance*, which was either a joke or a prayer, and Xanthe didn't have time to find out which.

He dropped into the pilot's seat and his hands knew the pre-flight without him. Power to the drive. Cradle clamps releasing. The berth master's voice in his ear, a stranger's voice with a wrecker's cadence: *"You're cleared hot, Vane. Corridor's yours as far as the buoy line. After that you're on your own and probably dead."*

"Noted," Xanthe said, and throttled up.

The Halcyon Wake hung in the approach corridor like a wound in the sky.

He'd seen her before, in leaked footage and from a distance—a great scarred leviathan of a freighter, older than the war, her hull plated over so many times she looked like a history written in metal. Now she was lit from within. Not navigation lights. Not emergency strobes. A sick amber glow bleeding out through every seam and viewport, pulsing slow as a dying heart, and around her the vacuum shimmered where the reactor's failing containment field bled radiation into space in visible sheets.

Evacuation trawlers clustered at a cautious distance—six, eight, a dozen of them, their floodlights sweeping the hull, finding nothing to take aboard. Because the children would not come.

*"Halcyon Wake, this is evacuation command,"* the band chatter said, one voice after another, pleading. *"You must disembark. Repeat, you must disembark. We have capacity for all hands—"*

And then a child's voice, small and clear and absolutely unafraid, cutting through the traffic channel like a bell through fog.

*"The count isn't finished."*

Lyra.

Xanthe's throat closed. He keyed his own channel and put every ounce of calm he had ever faked in his life into his voice. "Wake, this is Xanthe Vane, inbound in a courier, four minutes out. Lyra—if you can hear me—it's your brother. It's Xanthe. Don't finish anything. Just stay where you are. I'm coming to you."

Static. Long static. Then, faintly, a sound he hadn't heard since he was seven years old: humming. The lullaby. Three notes of it, off-key, the way she'd always sung it, and then gone.

He pushed the throttle past the red line.

* * *

The buoy line flashed under him and the radiation wash hit the *Second Chance*'s hull like weather. Alarms woke across the board—dosimetry screaming, shielding margins shrinking—and Xanthe silenced them one by one because he did not need a computer to tell him he was flying into something that killed. He needed the computer to tell him one thing only.

"Time to criticality," he said.

*"Nine minutes, forty seconds,"* the ship answered, in a pleasant synthetic voice that made it worse somehow.

Nine minutes to cross the last two kilometers, dock with a ship whose docking systems were probably dead, find one child among forty in a maze of decks, and get out. Every smuggler instinct he owned—the ones that had kept him alive through Ceres Gate and customs sweeps and thirty years of silence between worlds—told him the numbers didn't work. You couldn't outrun a meltdown. Nobody outran a meltdown.

But he had spent his whole life carrying other people's memories in sealed crystal, never opening them, never asking whose they were. He had made invisibility into a religion because feeling things had hurt too much. And now, with nine minutes on the clock, he understood with total clarity that he would trade every year of that careful, competent, empty life for four more minutes with his sister.

The Halcyon Wake grew in the canopy until she was all there was.

He found a docking collar on the cargo ring—a freight lock, old pattern, the kind he'd been slipping through since he was younger than Lyra looked now. The Wake's systems were half-dead but the lock's manual override still turned, and the moment the pressure equalized he was through, into air that tasted of ozone and hot metal and something sweeter underneath, like flowers burning.

The corridors were amber-lit and empty. Evacuation alarms rang in a rhythm that had already given up hope. And taped to the bulkhead at the first junction, in crayon, in a child's careful block letters:

**CORE DECK. ALL OF US. COME AND SEE.**

They had left him signs. Forty children who remembered a massacre, walking calmly toward a reactor, leaving directions like breadcrumbs for anyone brave or foolish enough to follow.

Xanthe followed.

* * *

His wrist-comm crackled. Odile. *"Trawlers are moving. I've got eleven hulls forming a screen on the far side of the Wake—wreckers, mostly, plus two customs cutters whose captains suddenly developed hearing problems. If you get her out, we catch you. But Xanthe—you have six minutes. Maybe less. The core telemetry just went nonlinear."*

"I'm in the cargo ring. Heading coreward."

*"The central spine elevator's dead. Take the maintenance shafts—frame forty, there's a ladder run all the way down. And Xanthe?"* A breath. *"I'm glad it was you on the spire. Come back from this one too."*

He took the shafts. Down and down through the ship's skeleton, hand over hand on rungs warm as fresh bread, the amber glow strengthening below him until it wasn't light anymore but a presence, a pressure, something vast and patient waiting at the bottom of the world. The heat rose to meet him. Somewhere beneath his feet, matter itself was forgetting how to hold together.

Frame forty opened onto the reactor gallery, and Xanthe stopped.

The children were there. All forty of them, arranged in a wide semicircle before the containment housing, small figures in oversized emergency coats, utterly still. They weren't crying. They weren't praying. They were *watching*—faces lifted toward the pulsing core with the solemn attention of an audience waiting for a curtain. And they were counting. Softly, in unison, voices overlapping like waves:

"One. Two. Three. Four..."

Not seconds. Names. He realized it with a jolt—they were reciting the manifest. The 1,204. The broadcast had given them the list and they had taken it into themselves, and now they were saying the dead aloud, one by one, in a room that was about to become a pyre.

"Lyra!"

She turned. Small, dark-haired, thirty years old behind eight-year-old eyes, and when she saw him her whole face changed—not surprise, not joy, but recognition so complete it looked like relief. As if she'd known he would come. As if she'd left the crayon signs for exactly one person.

"You read them all," she said. "On the screens. Every name. So they're real now. They can't be erased if everyone heard them."

"That's right." He crossed the gallery fast, dropping to his knees in front of her, taking her small shoulders in his hands. Her skin was cool. The room was furnace-hot and her skin was cool, and that frightened him more than the countdown. "So we're done here. The count is finished, Lyra. Everyone heard it. Now we go home."

"It's not finished." She said it gently, the way you correct someone you love. "We're almost at four hundred. Some of them don't have names anymore, so we give them our own. I gave one Mama's name. Is that wrong?"

His heart broke cleanly, like crystal under a hammer. "No," he said. "That's not wrong. That's the most right thing I ever heard. But sweetheart—listen to me—the ship is going to explode. Very soon. And the dead don't need us to die with them. They need us to *live* and keep saying the names. That's how it works. That's the whole trick. Witnessing isn't standing in the fire. Witnessing is walking out and telling everyone what you saw."

Behind her, the other children had stopped counting. Forty faces turned toward him, waiting, and he understood that he wasn't arguing with one little girl—he was arguing with a consensus, a chorus, a collective memory that had decided its purpose. He thought of Odile's reports: they complete each other's memories. No single edited record could fake that. They were not trauma victims. They were *archives*. Living ones. And archives do not burn themselves for the sake of the data they hold.

"Come with me," he said to all of them, standing, spreading his arms like a man trying to embrace a crowd. "All of you. There are ships outside—eleven ships—waiting to carry you. You've done the hardest thing anyone has ever done. You carried the truth through thirty years of dark and you delivered it. Let us carry you now. Please."

A boy near the center—Xanthe recognized him from the quarantine footage, the eldest, maybe ten—looked at Lyra. Lyra looked at the core. The core pulsed. And somewhere in the structure beneath them came a sound like a mountain clearing its throat.

*"Five minutes,"* his wrist-comm said, Odile's voice frayed to threads. *"Xanthe. Five minutes. Tell me you have them."*

Lyra took his hand.

"Okay," she said. "But we say the rest of the names on the ships. All of them. Out loud. Even the ones we made up. Especially those."

"Yes," Xanthe said, and his voice failed on the second word, and he didn't care. "All of them. Especially those."

* * *

Getting forty children up a maintenance shaft in four minutes was impossible, so he stopped doing arithmetic and started doing the next thing, and then the next thing after that. He sent the eldest up first with instructions shouted in smuggler's shorthand—*hand over hand, don't look down, count the rungs, you're good at counting*—and the joke landed, God help him, it landed, and forty children began climbing while counting rungs in their soft unified voices, and the sound of it followed him up the shaft like a rope thrown back down into a well.

The heat climbed with them. By frame twenty the rungs were too hot to grip bare-handed and he tore the lining out of his coat and wrapped the children's palms in strips as they passed him, working the line like a man hauling nets, touching each small hand, memorizing each face, refusing to let a single one be anonymous even for a second.

*"Three minutes."* Odile again. *"Trawlers are holding position off the cargo ring. Eleven hulls, floodlights on, hatches open. It's beautiful, Xanthe. It looks like a harbor."*

"Hold it," he gasped. "Two minutes. We're at the cargo ring."

The freight lock was chaos—small bodies funneling through, the eldest children passing the youngest hand to hand down the boarding tube into the floodlit dark where the trawlers waited. Xanthe stood at the collar and counted them through the way they'd taught him: aloud, by name where he knew it, by description where he didn't. *Girl, red coat, age six. Boy, missing tooth, age nine.* The count as rescue instead of execution. The same act, turned over, made clean.

Lyra went third from last. At the threshold she stopped and looked back down the amber corridor, toward the core, toward the heat, and Xanthe knelt and put his forehead against hers.

"You don't have to watch it," he whispered. "You already witnessed everything that mattered."

"I know," she said. "I wanted to see the stars instead. Can we open a window?"

"Yeah," he said, lying completely, meaning it entirely. "All the windows."

He shoved her gently through the lock and dove in after her, and the collar sealed behind them with a sound like a held breath finally released.

* * *

The *Second Chance* tore free of the Halcyon Wake's hull with ninety seconds on the clock, and Xanthe flew the way he had never flown—flat-out, shields screaming, dosimetry howling, threading between trawlers loaded with children while the amber glow behind them swelled from a pulse to a blaze to a second sun being born inside a broken ship.

Odile's voice on the band, steady as bedrock: *"All vessels, hard burn, break orbit line, NOW. Xanthe—your port side is clear, take the corridor and don't look back."*

He looked back anyway. Of course he looked back. In the canopy, growing smaller and brighter, the Halcyon Wake blazed like a lantern being carried down into the sea—thirty years lost, forty children carried home, 1,204 names spoken at last into every ear in nine sectors. Whatever happened next, whatever the tribunal did or didn't do, whatever Roe's lawyers and the sector governments tried to bury or rebrand—no one could unhear it now. The count was finished. It was finished in the only place records couldn't reach: inside people.

"Criticality," the ship said pleasantly. "Brace."

The shockwave caught them at the edge of the corridor and slapped the *Second Chance* sideways like a hand. Xanthe rode it, white-knuckled, engines flaring, and behind them the light bloomed and bloomed and slowly, slowly dimmed into a spreading cloud of sparks and ash and cooling metal, falling forever toward the world below.

In the cabin behind him, forty children pressed against the viewports, watching. And softly, in unison, they began again:

"One. Two. Three..."

Saying the names. Keeping the count. Carrying it home.

Xanthe set a course for the trawler screen, and his hands were shaking, and he was smiling, and he did not stop smiling until he saw the customs cutter *Vigilant* swing alongside with Odile Ferran standing at its viewport, and saw her raise one bloody hand—not in salute, not quite—in something newer than either of them had words for yet.

Then the comm band lit up with traffic from every direction at once, nine sectors' worth of voices crowding into a single frequency for the first time in thirty years, and the first message to resolve out of the noise was not from any government or fleet.

It was from Sector Four. From strangers. Reading names.
