## 37. Growing Up Twice

The ward they moved us to was not a ward at all. It had been a hydroponics bay once—Lyra could tell by the irrigation channels still scored into the deck plating, the ghost-rings of old grow-lights stamped into the ceiling—and someone in Sector Nine's vast kindness had filled it with beds and soft walls and machines that hummed at frequencies meant to feel like mothers.

They called it Restorative Care Unit Four. The children called it the Garden, because children name things honestly, and because forty beds arranged among the dead channels of an old farm did look, if you squinted, like seeds planted in rows.

Lyra Vane lay in bed eleven and did not sleep.

The ceiling above her was doing its nightly work: a slow projection of a sky that belonged to no world any of them had ever lived on, all violet clouds and patient stars. It was supposed to be calming. Dr. Amara Osei had told her so that first morning, kneeling beside the bed with her hands folded like a woman in prayer, saying, *This is your sky now, Lyra. You can watch it as long as you like.*

*My sky,* Lyra had thought, *is the inside of a reactor housing, and it is full of names.*

But she watched it anyway. She was learning to do two things at once. That was what growing up twice meant, she was beginning to understand—not being big and small at the same time, exactly, though her body was eleven years old and her memory held thirty years of fire and silence and a counting that would not stop until it was finished. No. It meant holding two skies. Two childhoods. Two versions of every door she walked through, one remembered and one being made.

She pressed her palm flat against the mattress and felt for the tremor. It was always there at night, faint as a heartbeat under floorboards: the ship's shiver, the reactor's dying note, still ringing in her bones even though the Halcyon Wake had been ash for months. The doctors said it was somatic. The doctors said many things.

Across the aisle, Tam Wexler whimpered in his sleep and called out a number. "Four hundred and six." Then he rolled over and was quiet.

That was how it went, some nights. The count leaked out of them in their sleep, number by number, like water finding cracks. In the morning none of them would remember saying it. But Lyra heard. Lyra kept a tally on the inside of her forearm in washable ink, because paper got confiscated and screens got monitored, but skin was hers. She was up to three hundred and nineteen numbers spoken aloud by the forty of them since the broadcast. She had checked each one against the manifest Xanthe carried in his coat pocket, the real one, the recovered one, and every single number matched a name.

*We are not broken,* she told herself, watching the false stars. *We are leaking truth. There is a difference.*

* * *

Dr. Osei came at 0700 with the light-therapy panel and the tray of nutrient wafers, and behind her came the machine.

Everyone hated the machine. It was called a Mnemonic Resequencer, which was a long word for a thing that reached into your head with cold fingers and tried to put the years back where they belonged. It looked like a crown of thin silver antennae mounted on a halo frame, and when it worked you saw things—flashes, whole minutes sometimes, of the life that had been taken from you. When it didn't work, you saw nothing, or worse, you saw something that wasn't yours.

"Good morning, garden," Dr. Osei said, because she had stopped correcting the name weeks ago and had started using it, which Lyra decided was the single most honest thing any adult in this facility had done.

Forty voices murmured back. Some of the younger ones—the ones who had been de-aged furthest, who were five and six now in body and had almost nothing left of their crew-years—murmured brightest. They were the lucky ones, everyone agreed. Less to lose.

Lyra sat up for her session. She always sat up. She had learned early that lying down during resequencing made the memories arrive sideways, and sideways memories were harder to hold onto.

"Bed eleven," said the technician, a round-faced man named Pell who chewed mint gum constantly and smelled like a pharmacy. "Lyra Vane. Session thirty-eight."

"Session thirty-seven," Lyra corrected. "You skipped Tuesday when the coolant line burst."

Pell blinked. Dr. Osei's mouth did the small twitch it did when Lyra caught the institution in an error, which happened more than anyone liked. "Session thirty-eight," Pell amended, entering it.

The crown came down over her head. Cold fingers. Antennae whispering against her scalp like grass.

"Think of something warm," Dr. Osei said, as she always did. "Anchor to something warm before we begin."

Lyra thought of the lullaby. Not the version she'd hummed in quarantine, half-remembered, a melody with holes in it—but the version that had come back last month, whole, when Xanthe had sung it to her in the visiting room and his voice had cracked on the third line and she had suddenly, violently, remembered their mother singing it while peeling fruit at a window that looked out on Meridian's ring, the knife going *shk, shk, shk*, the song going up where the knife went down. Warmth with a blade under it. That was the only kind of warmth Lyra trusted anymore.

The machine breathed. The world went silver.

* * *

What came back this time:

A corridor. Her own hands, adult hands, scarred across both knuckles from cargo work, gripping a rail as the ship shuddered. Someone shouting deck numbers. The smell of burnt insulation and, underneath it, absurdly, coffee—someone had been brewing coffee when the whatever-it-was had taken them, and the smell of it had soaked into everything, and for years afterward, in the long dark between then and now, the smell of coffee had been the smell of the end of the world.

Her own voice, older, saying: *"Seal is holding on Deck Six. Seal is holding."*

And then—new, this had never come back before—a face. A man at the far end of the corridor, young, laughing, waving her toward him through the smoke-haze, and he was wearing a Lark Ascending patch on his jacket, and he was saying something she couldn't hear, his mouth shaping words the memory wouldn't give her sound for—

The machine pulled her out. Silver became white. The Garden swam back into focus, Dr. Osei's face above hers, careful and kind.

"What did you see?" Dr. Osei asked. They always asked. They wrote it all down, every fragment, building the record back up out of forty heads the way you'd rebuild a shattered window one shard at a time.

"A corridor," Lyra said. "Coffee burning. And—" She hesitated. The face felt private, somehow, in a way the corridors and the fires did not. "Someone I know."

"Can you describe him?"

"He has my brother's chin," Lyra said, and watched Dr. Osei write that down, and understood, with the strange double vision that was becoming her native way of seeing, that somewhere in a file this sentence would sit next to photographs and genetic scans and the official account of the Halcyon Wake, and that no file in the nine sectors could hold what it actually meant: *the machine gave me back my brother before I ever met him.*

Because here was the thing nobody at Restorative Care Unit Four quite knew what to do with: Lyra had two sets of memories of Xanthe Vane, and they did not match, and she loved him in both of them anyway.

In the old set—the deep set, the one the resequencer kept dredging up in pieces—he was a boy of nine, all elbows and schemes, teaching her to pick the lock on the observation dome so they could watch freighters come in. He was brave in the way of boys who have never yet lost anything. He smelled like engine grease because he hung around the maintenance bays stealing knowledge. He promised her, once, solemnly, hand on heart, that he would never let anything happen to her, and then Meridian Station burned, and something happened to her, and he wasn't there, and for thirty years that promise had been the loudest silence in her head.

In the new set—the shallow set, four months old—he was a gaunt man with smuggler's eyes who had walked into Quarantine Ward Nine looking like a man walking into his own funeral, and she hadn't known him, and he had known her, and the grief coming off him had been so strong she'd tasted it, metallic, like blood in the water. And then she had hummed the lullaby without deciding to, and he had gone to his knees right there on the ward floor in front of the med-techs and the cameras, and she had thought: *oh. Oh, I know you. Not from my life. From somewhere underneath my life.*

Two brothers. One face. She was growing up twice, and both times, she was growing up with him.

* * *

The sessions were not kind to everyone.

Three doors down, Maren Kesh—who had been the Wake's chief engineer, who was now seven years old and would be seven for another year of accelerated growth at least—had begun refusing treatment. She sat in the corner of her bed with her arms wrapped around her knees and told anyone who approached, in a flat little voice far too old for her mouth, "I don't want the years back. The years are where the fire is."

The staff had protocols for this. Counseling schedules. Family liaison officers. A laminated card about trauma responses that Dr. Osei kept in her breast pocket like a talisman.

Lyra had another theory. She waited until rest period, slipped out of bed eleven—she could be very quiet; thirty years of hiding in vents taught you things that survived de-aging—and padded down to Maren's bed and sat on the edge of it without asking.

Maren glared at her. Seven-year-old glare, engineer's weight.

"You think we should take them back," Maren said. "The memories. You go to every session. You never miss."

"I go to every session," Lyra agreed.

"So you think we should."

"I think," Lyra said, "that the fire isn't in the memories. The fire is in the forgetting."

Maren's glare flickered. "That doesn't make sense."

"It does, though." Lyra pulled her knees up too, mirroring the smaller girl, so they were two identical shapes sitting in a rectangle of projected starlight. "Before the broadcast, we were the only ones who remembered, and everyone said we were sick. Delusional. Radiation dreams. Remember? They were going to take us away for *protective study.*" Both of them flinched at the phrase; it had become a curse among the forty. "It wasn't the remembering that hurt, Mare. It was being the only one. Now the whole nine sectors read the names every anniversary. Now when I say *four hundred and six*, there's a name attached, and a face, and a mother on Ceres Gate who lights a candle. The memories aren't lighter. But I'm not carrying them alone anymore. That's the difference. That's the whole difference."

Maren was quiet for a long time. Out in the false sky, a satellite that existed nowhere crossed the false moon.

"The machine hurts," she said finally. Smaller.

"Yeah," Lyra said. "So did the reactor. We sat with that too."

She stayed until Maren's breathing evened out, and then she went back to bed eleven and added a mark to her arm-tally, not a number this time but a small symbol she'd invented: a circle with a seed inside it. She had eleven of those now. Eleven of the forty who had chosen, in one way or another, to keep both lives.

The others worried her more than she let anyone see. The ones who woke up some mornings as pure children, laughing, wanting games, their crew-years washed away entirely by the treatments or by whatever had de-aged them in the first place—and who would look at Lyra across the breakfast table with bright, empty eyes, no recognition at all, and ask her why the tall sad man with the freighter jacket kept bringing her books about stars.

Those days, Lyra ate her breakfast and answered pleasantly and grieved quietly, because healing without erasure was a choice, and not everyone could make it, and she had promised herself she would not love the ones who forgot any less.

* * *

Xanthe came on Thursdays.

Visiting rules said family, twice weekly, two hours. The Records Authority tribunal had expedited his guardianship petition—there had been a moment, Lyra gathered from overheard conversations, when the Authority had wanted to place all forty children in institutional care, and Odile Ferran had stood up in a hearing room with copied testimony in her hands and used a word like *protective study* in a very dangerous tone, and the petition had died quietly in committee. So: Thursdays, Xanthe, two hours, a room with a table and a window overlooking the actual sky, which was grey and industrial and which Lyra preferred to the violet lie on her ceiling precisely because it didn't pretend to be anything.

He always brought things. This week: a paper book of star charts from before the Quiet, real ink, pages that turned; a tin of candied ginger from a wrecker recipe Iyo Sen had left behind, which made Xanthe's jaw tighten when he handed it over, grief moving through him like weather; and a data chip, plain grey, which he set on the table between them without explanation.

"What's that?" Lyra asked.

"Your mother's log," he said. "The private one. The tribunal released copies to next of kin last week." His thumb moved over the chip's edge, back and forth, a smuggler's tell she'd learned to read. "I've read it maybe fifty times. I keep thinking I'll find something different in it. I don't. She hid the crystals hoping someone would finish it. She signed the seal order because Roe had us—had you and me—in a holding cell on Ceres Gate, and he showed her the footage." His voice did the thing it did, the flattening, the careful leveling, when the feeling underneath was too big to let through all at once. "She chose us. Over twelve hundred strangers. And then she spent the rest of her life trying to unchoose it, and she couldn't, so she hid the truth in her son's cargo hold and hoped."

Lyra turned the chip over in her small hands. Adult calluses lived on these hands somewhere under the new skin; the resequencer had shown her loading manifests, welding seams, a wedding ring she'd worn for six years to a woman whose face hadn't come back yet.

"She let go of my hand," Lyra said. "In the third crystal. I've seen it. Everyone's seen it now—it's in the memorial archive, the school modules, everywhere. My hand letting go of her hand." She looked up. "Do you want to know what I remember that the crystal doesn't show?"

Xanthe went very still. "Tell me."

"After she let go—after the door sealed—she put her palm flat against the glass. Right here." Lyra touched her own chest, sternum-height. "I remember the sound of it. The crystal doesn't have sound on that part, the recording's damaged. But I remember. *Thump.* Once. Like a heartbeat through a wall. And I spent thirty years thinking it was goodbye." She set the chip down between them, aligned with the table's edge, precise. "But I was wrong, Xanthe. I figured it out. It wasn't goodbye. It was the same motion she used on us when we were babies—when she'd check we were breathing. Palm flat, one press. *Are you alive? Are you alive?* She couldn't save me and she couldn't say sorry and she couldn't break protocol with Roe watching, so she did the only thing her hands knew how to do. She asked if I was alive. Through the glass. And I was. And I am."

Xanthe made a sound she had never heard a grown man make—half laugh, half breaking—and put his face in his hands, and his shoulders shook, and Lyra climbed onto the table, which was against visiting rules, and knelt in front of him and pressed her palm flat against his chest. Once. *Are you alive?*

"I'm alive," he said into his hands.

"Then we're done grieving the wrong thing," she said. "Both of us. Today. I'm declaring it."

He laughed then, really laughed, wet and startled, and pulled her into a hug that smelled like engine grease, because some things survived everything, even thirty years and a war and a machine that rearranged your years like furniture.

* * *

Later—much later, near the end of the two hours, when the light in the visiting room had gone amber—Lyra asked him the question she had been building for weeks, carrying it around the way she carried the tally on her arm.

"When the care program ends," she said. "When they decide we're restored, or as restored as we're going to get. What happens to us? To me?"

Xanthe leaned back. Outside the window, a hauler rose from the port on a thread of blue fire, climbing toward the reopened lanes. Traffic every day now. The whole sky busy again, after thirty years of silence. Sometimes Lyra just watched the traffic and marveled that the worlds were speaking.

"There's a house," Xanthe said. "On Thessaly Ring. Odile found it—it used to belong to a wrecker family, and the community vouched for the transfer, and there's a berth for the Lark Ascending and a school two stops down the tramline that teaches real history now, Meridian and all, names and faces." He was talking fast, the way he did when he was nervous, laying out a route the way he'd lay out a smuggling run. "There's a room in it. It's not much. The ceiling's low and the previous owners painted it orange, of all the crimes. But it's got a window that faces east, and I'm told the sunrises are—"

"Xanthe."

"—adequate. Mediocre sunrises, honestly, I've seen better, there was this moon off Sector Four—"

"*Xanthe.*"

He stopped. Looked at her.

"I'm eleven," Lyra said. "And I'm forty-one. And I'm going to be both for the rest of my life, and I've made peace with that, mostly, on good days. I remember a brother who promised to protect me and failed. I remember a brother who crossed nine sectors and burned his whole invisible life down to finish a count. I don't want to choose between them. Choosing feels like erasing, and I'm done with erasing—all of us are done with erasing, that's the entire point of everything we did." She took a breath. Her hands were steady. Somewhere under the new skin, welder's calluses. "So here's what I want. I want you to be my brother in both. The one who owes me thirty years and the one who paid them. The one from before and the one from after. Can you do that? Can you be both? Because I can't keep one childhood and throw the other away, and I won't let you do it either. You don't get to only be the man who saved me. You also have to be the boy who picked locks with me. Deal?"

For a moment he didn't answer, and she watched him try to, watched the words jam in him like crystals in a smuggler's hold.

Then he reached across the table and pressed his palm flat against her chest. Once. *Are you alive?*

"Deal," he said. "Both. All of it. Every year of you."

"Even the bad ones?"

"Especially the bad ones. Those are the ones that made you able to sit with Maren Kesh in the dark and tell her the fire is in the forgetting." He smiled, crooked, wrecked, real. "Orange ceiling and all."

"Orange ceiling and all," Lyra agreed, and shook on it, small hand in large hand, eleven years old and forty-one years old, sealing a contract no authority in nine sectors had forms for.

* * *

That night, back in the Garden, bed eleven, the false stars wheeling overhead, Lyra took out the washable pen and did something new with her arm.

Below the tally of spoken numbers, below the eleven seed-circles, she drew a line. And below the line, she began a second list—not numbers this time, but names, starting with her own, written twice: *Lyra Vane, aged nine. Lyra Vane, aged forty-one.* Then *Tam Wexler, twice.* Then *Maren Kesh, twice,* because Maren had gone to her session that afternoon, sat under the silver crown, and come out crying and said, "I saw the engine room. I saw my tools. I want them back. All of it, even the fire."

Twelve seed-circles now.

Lyra was capping the pen when the ward's comm chimed—an unusual hour for announcements—and Dr. Osei's voice came through, carefully neutral in the way that meant the news was either wonderful or terrible and the institution hadn't decided which.

"Attention, Unit Four. If everyone could remain in bed. We have... an incoming transmission. Sector-wide priority. It appears the tribunal findings have been ratified early, and—" a pause, a rustle of papers, and then, briefly, the professional mask slipping into something almost human— "and Director Cassian Roe has been taken into custody. Effective immediately. All charges. All of them."

The Garden went silent. Forty beds. Forty children with two lifetimes apiece.

Then Tam Wexler, four hundred and six, sat straight up in the dark and said, in a voice that was seven years old and sixty years old at once:

"Read us the names tomorrow. All of them. Out loud. I want to hear it with my ears that are new."

And forty voices answered him as one, the way they had answered the intercom on the worst day of their lives, except this time it was a yes:

*"All of them."*

Lyra lay back against her pillow, palm flat over her own heartbeat, and began, silently, to practice.
