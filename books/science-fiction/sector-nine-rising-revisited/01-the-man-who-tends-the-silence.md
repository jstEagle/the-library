## 1. The Man Who Tends the Silence

There were one hundred and forty-two channels assigned to Beacon Nine, and Quentin Brightwater had spent the better part of four years learning that every single one of them was a lie.

Not a malicious lie. More the way a house is a lie — the way a front door promises visitors that never come, the way a second bedroom promises children that were never born. The channels existed in the registry. They had frequencies, designations, bandwidth allocations. Somewhere in the Consolidated Directorate's vast and indifferent filing systems, each one was listed as *active*, which was the Directorate's way of saying *someone is legally responsible for this*, which was, in turn, the Directorate's way of saying *no one is responsible for this at all*.

Quentin cataloged them anyway. All one hundred and forty-two. Every morning, coffee in hand, he walked the ring corridor of the beacon with his slate and listened to the silence on each channel the way a doctor listens to a chest — not because he expected to hear anything, but because the not-hearing was itself a kind of information, and someone ought to know it, even if that someone was only him.

*Channel 7, dead. Channel 8, dead. Channel 9, dead but humming at a frequency it shouldn't have, which was new, which was interesting, which he'd flagged twice and been ignored twice.*

He had given the channels names. Not official ones — he wasn't a fool, whatever the Directorate's personnel algorithms might have concluded about him — but private ones, the way you name a one-eyed cat. Channel 12 was *Old Grumpy* because its carrier wave sagged in the middle like a tired back. Channel 31 was *The Chorister* because of a harmonic overtone that made it sing, faintly, when the solar wind came through. Channel 88 he called *Marta*, for no reason he could defend, except that it felt like a Marta: steady, unglamorous, there.

"You're all dead," he told them each morning, in the same tone he might have used to break the news gently. "I know. You know. But we keep showing up, don't we? That's something."

The channels did not answer. They never answered.

That was the job, or what passed for one. Quentin Brightwater, xenolinguist, second class, unassigned, custodian of Beacon Nine — a relay station orbiting a gas giant at the ragged edge of Sector Nine, so far out on the charts that the charts themselves seemed embarrassed about it. He had been posted here six years ago. The posting orders had been signed by a directorate sub-bureau he'd never been able to find again, no matter how carefully he searched the archives during his quarterly reports. When he'd queried the assignment — politely, twice, then less politely once — the responses had come back with the warm, frictionless tone of automated correspondence: *Your service is valued. Continue current operations.*

Continue current operations. As if current operations were a thing. As if tending a hundred and forty-two dead channels in the dark were a career rather than a sentence with the punctuation removed.

He'd stopped asking after the second year. Not because he'd given up — Quentin was constitutionally incapable of giving up on a question; it was the one flaw in him he'd never managed to regret — but because he'd begun to suspect that the question itself was wrong. The right question wasn't *why am I here?* The right question, he was slowly coming to understand, was *why does the beacon need me here?* And that question had teeth.

* * *

The beacon was old. That was the other thing nobody talked about, mostly because nobody talked to Quentin at all.

Officially, Beacon Nine was forty years old, a standard Directorate relay of the Kestrel-class line, one of thousands strung along the trade corridors like beads on a wire. But Quentin was a linguist before he was anything else, and linguists develop an ear — not just for words, but for the shape of things that make words. And the beacon's shape was wrong. The Kestrel-class schematics he'd pulled from the archive showed a clean geometry, right angles and standardized couplings, the bureaucratic architecture of a civilization that had stopped improvising. The beacon he actually lived inside had curves where there should have been angles. Its corridors bent in ways that served no structural purpose he could identify, unless the purpose was aesthetic, and the aesthetic was one he didn't recognize — an organic, almost calligraphic sweep, as if the station had been written rather than built.

He'd measured it once, obsessively, over three weeks, and the discrepancy between the schematics and the reality ran to eleven percent of total volume. Eleven percent of a space station is a lot of somewhere. He'd crawled through maintenance shafts looking for the missing space and found instead a chamber he had no authorization to enter, no record of existing, and no key for — except that the door had opened for him on the first try, smoothly, as if it had been waiting.

Inside: a core of dark, non-reflective material, threaded with conduits that pulsed, very faintly, with something that wasn't quite light and wasn't quite heat. He'd stood there for a long time with his slate recording nothing, his instruments reading nothing, and the hair on his arms standing up in a way that had nothing to do with temperature.

He'd named that chamber *the Study*, because it felt like a room where someone had once read something important, and because naming things was how Quentin kept the universe at a manageable size.

He went to the Study most evenings now. He didn't touch the core. He wasn't a fool. But he sat in the curved silence of it, in the faint pulse of the not-light, and he worked on the problem that had quietly become the center of his life — the problem he had told no one, because there was no one to tell, and because he was fairly sure that telling someone would be the beginning of the end of something.

The problem was this: Quentin Brightwater dreamed in a language no human being spoke.

* * *

It had started in his second year on the beacon — the same year, he'd later realize, that the beacon's power draw had begun its slow, inexplicable climb. He hadn't noticed at first. Dreams are weather; you don't file a report on weather. But the dreams had a consistency that weather doesn't. They had *grammar*.

He would be somewhere green — Meridian-green, though he had no way of knowing that word yet, no way of knowing that anywhere called Meridian had ever existed — and someone would be speaking to him. Not in images, not in the dream-logic of ordinary sleep, but in actual language: structured, inflected, agglutinative, with verb forms that stacked meaning like sediment. He could never see the speaker's face. He could hear the voice the way you hear your own thoughts, from inside.

And in the morning, the words stayed.

That was the impossible part. Dreams evaporate; that's their contract with us. But these left residue — syllables he could write down, phonemes he could transcribe into his slate before the coffee finished brewing. Within a month he had a notebook. Within six months he had a corpus. Within a year he had, God help him, a *conjugation table*, because he was who he was, and there was apparently no version of Quentin Brightwater that could be handed a dead language in his sleep without building it a grammar.

He ran the corpus through every linguistic database the Directorate maintained. Zero matches. He ran it through the historical archives, the colonial records, the pre-Expansion linguistic surveys. Zero matches. He ran it, in a moment of desperation, through the xenobiology division's contact protocols — the formal records of every first contact humanity had ever made. Zero matches.

The language was not human. The language was not alien. The language was not *anything*, according to every record the species had ever kept, and yet Quentin Brightwater spoke it in his sleep, fluently, nightly, with an ease he had never achieved in any of his six acquired languages while awake.

He should have reported it. He knew that. A xenolinguist experiencing spontaneous acquisition of an unknown language aboard an unregistered station core was — well, it was a report. It was several reports. It was probably a medevac and a psych evaluation and a very long conversation with people whose job titles contained the word *compliance*.

But he'd been alone for four years, and the dreams were the only conversation he had that went both ways, and there is a kind of loneliness that will make you protect the thing that visits you. So he wrote the words in his notebooks, and he said them aloud in the Study where no one could hear, and he let the language live in him like a tenant he'd decided not to report to the landlord.

Some nights, in the dreams, the voice seemed to be trying to tell him something. Not words exactly — urgency. The feeling of a message being repeated by someone who has said it many times and been heard by no one. He'd wake with the shape of it in his chest: *listen, listen, listen*, translated into a grammar that had never existed on paper, in a tongue that had never existed anywhere he could find.

*Listen to what?* he'd asked, aloud, in the dark of the Study, in the language itself, his accent terrible, his grammar worse, his sincerity total.

The core would pulse. Faintly. The way a sleeping thing breathes.

He'd decided, in his more rational moments, that it was coincidence. In his less rational moments — the ones at three in the morning, the ones that were increasingly becoming all the moments — he'd decided that the beacon was dreaming, and that he was the dream it was having, and that somewhere in the eleven percent of missing space, something was very patiently teaching him to listen.

* * *

On the morning this story properly begins — though Quentin, of course, didn't know it, because no one ever knows it, that's the whole trick of beginnings — he was on his usual rounds with his usual coffee, and he was talking to Channel 31, The Chorister, about the solar wind.

"You're showing off," he said. "I know it's a good wind. You don't have to harmonize at me."

The Chorister harmonized at him. Quentin smiled, made his note — *Channel 31: dead, singing, unrepentant* — and turned to Channel 32.

Channel 32 was dead. Channel 32 was always dead. Channel 32 had been dead for six years, and Quentin had named it *The Patient* because it had the quality of something waiting rather than something gone — a held breath rather than an empty room.

The Patient was not dead.

The slate in his hand registered it before his ears did: a carrier wave, rising out of the noise floor like a fin breaking water. Not static. Not drift. A *signal* — structured, repeating, deliberate. Quentin stood very still in the corridor, coffee cooling in his hand, and felt six years of silence rearrange themselves around a single sound.

He set the slate down. Picked it up. Checked it against the raw feed. Checked the feed against the antenna logs. Checked the logs against the star charts, in case something — a pulsar, a reflection, a ghost of physics — was playing a joke that only he could hear.

The signal repeated. And this time, because he was who he was, because four years of notebooks had built a vocabulary inside him the way coral builds a reef, Quentin Brightwater heard it — not as noise, not as pattern, but as *words*.

His own mouth translated before his mind could stop it, a whisper in the curved corridor of a station no one claimed:

*"Beacon, hear me. Beacon, answer. I know you remember."*

The language was the one from his dreams.

The signal was addressed to him.
