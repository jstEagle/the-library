## 6. The Message in the Hull

Vess Okonkwo had learned, over thirty years of soldiering compressed into a body that had forgotten most of them, that the hardest lies were the ones you told by staying silent.

She lay on the bunk they'd given her aboard the *Meridian's Bell* — a freighter's idea of hospitality, a mattress thin as a ration wrapper and a blanket that smelled of vacuum sealant — and listened to the ship breathe. Cargo haulers breathed differently than warships. The *Anemoi* had hummed with reactor discipline, every system tuned to military tolerance. This ship creaked and sighed and ticked like an old man's knees, and beneath it all ran the constant subsonic shudder of a drive that had never been asked to outrun anything.

It should have felt safe. It didn't. Safety was a word from a future she remembered and a past she no longer owned, and neither version of her life fit inside this hull.

Three days since the boarding party had found them. Three days since she'd woken to faces too young to know her name, in a corridor where the frost had melted off the stasis pods like tears. Three days of answering questions carefully — *we were on patrol, we lost navigation, we don't know how long* — each answer a small brick in a wall between herself and the truth.

And for three days, Gideon Iversen had been walking around her ship with his head tilted, listening to something no one else could hear.

She'd seen him do it twice through the open hatch of the common room: standing near the data core they'd salvaged from the *Anemoi*'s signal suite, eyes half-closed, lips moving without sound. Like a man praying. Like a man translating.

That was impossible. She knew it was impossible, because in the future she remembered — the one that hadn't happened yet, the one that lived in her skull like shrapnel — Gideon Iversen couldn't read the message either. Not at first. Not until the dreams taught him.

But he was tilting his head now. Thirty years early. Or however long it was; the arithmetic of the fold still made her nauseous if she looked at it directly.

She got up. She'd made her decision somewhere around the second sleep cycle, and waiting longer would only let the admiralty's convoy arrive and take the choice out of her hands. The black box was already crated for transfer. Once it reached Station Vireo, it would disappear into a classified archive, and everything the future had paid for would be buried under classification stamps thicker than armor plate.

The corridor to the common room was narrow enough that her shoulders brushed both walls. She passed the galley, where the hauler's engineer — a wiry woman named Sefa who hummed off-key while she worked — nodded at her without suspicion. Everyone aboard treated her with the careful gentleness owed to miracle survivors. They thought she was a ghost. They had no idea how right they were.

Gideon was alone in the common room, which was either luck or the universe finally cutting her a break. He sat at the table with a slate in front of him, and on the slate was a waveform — a fragment of the *Anemoi*'s signal, pulled from the raw dump before the box got sealed. She recognized it instantly. She would have recognized it blindfolded, by rhythm alone. She'd heard it every night for eleven years in the stasis dark, bleeding through the pod insulation into her dreams: the message, patient as tide, repeating itself to an empty ocean.

He didn't look up when she entered. "Your ship sings," he said quietly.

Her stomach dropped. "Excuse me?"

"The signal." He turned the slate toward her. The waveform crawled across it like something alive. "It's not telemetry. Telemetry has structure but no intent — you can feel the difference after enough years doing this. This is *composed*. Someone built it phrase by phrase, the way you'd build an argument." His finger traced the peaks. "And it's in a language class I've never catalogued. Not Kestrel dialect family. Not Concord syntax. Not any of the forty-one registered xenoglossic profiles." He looked up at her then, and his eyes were the color of old ice, and she saw the exact moment the question formed behind them. "Where did your ship learn to speak?"

*Tell him nothing.* That was the doctrine. That was survival. She'd kept the wall intact for three days; one more day and the convoy would come and the wall wouldn't matter anymore, because the evidence would be gone and she could vanish at the next port with a new name and a face that was, thanks to the fold, twenty years younger than her service record.

But she looked at him — at this younger, unbroken version of a man whose older self she had known for six years in a timeline that no longer existed — and the wall felt less like protection and more like a coffin lid.

In the future she remembered, Gideon Iversen decoded the message alone, in a cell, under extraction rigs, paying for every phrase with pieces of himself he never got back. In the future she remembered, nobody warned him what it would cost. Nobody gave him the choice with clean hands.

She sat down across from him.

"I'm going to tell you something," she said, "and then you're going to wish I hadn't."

* * *

She told him the shape of it first, without the flesh. That was the only way she could get through it — abstraction as anesthesia.

"The *Anemoi* wasn't lost," she said. "We weren't on patrol. We were a test platform. There's a drive technology — you won't find it in any registry, both governments classify it at the same tier they classify their own war crimes — that doesn't move a ship through space. It moves a ship through *time*. Fold displacement. You trade temporal position for spatial position. The math works. The math always works." Her hands were flat on the table, pressing down as if the deck might pitch. "What the math doesn't model is the cost."

"Cost," Gideon repeated. Not skeptical. Worse — attentive. He leaned forward slightly, the way linguists did when a sentence started resolving, and she hated how much she remembered loving that posture.

"The fold doesn't just displace the ship. It displaces the crew along their own timelines. We came back thirty years younger than our records said we should be. Biologically. Neurally. Every cell of us rolled backward." She watched him absorb that — watched the routine competence in his face crack into something rawer underneath. "The engineers called it conservation. Something has to balance the ledger. The ship went back in time, so the people on it paid forward. Or backward. The direction doesn't matter. What matters is that time is a currency out there, and someone spent us."

"And the signal?" His voice had gone very quiet. "You said it's composed. Who composed it?"

Here it was. The part she'd sworn on the *Anemoi*'s dead — on the ones who hadn't survived the return, the ones aged to dust or infancy in the debris fields she dreamed about — never to reveal to anyone who didn't already carry the weight of it.

She told him anyway.

"It came from the other side," she said. "From the far end of the fold. From a place and a time we can't reach yet. The fold opens in two directions, Gideon. We sent ships through. And something — someone — sent a message back along the channel we opened. The message rode the same physics that moved us. It needed a carrier. A vessel moving through displaced spacetime." She tapped the table once, hard. "*Us*. The *Anemoi* wasn't just a test platform. We were the envelope. The message used our fold transit to cross thirty years, and it's been sitting in our signal suite ever since, waiting for someone who could read it."

Gideon had gone very still. Stillness, she remembered — remembered from a future that hadn't happened — was what he did instead of panic. He gathered himself inward like a tide pulling back before a wave.

"A message from the future," he said. "Delivered by a weaponized experiment. Containing—" He stopped. Started again. "Containing what? If it's just data, the admiralty can decrypt it with compute. Why does it need a xenolinguist at all?"

"Because it isn't encrypted," Vess said. "It's *encoded in a language that doesn't exist yet*. The future wrote it in a tongue that hasn't been invented — a contact language, a creole of every protocol the war will produce. No dictionary exists. No corpus. The only way to learn it is the way you learn any living language: exposure, immersion, pattern recognition. The human way." She held his gaze and made herself say the rest. "There are maybe four people alive qualified to attempt it. You're one of them. The other three are already on government payrolls."

Something flickered across his face — recognition, or its shadow. "I've been dreaming," he said slowly, like a man admitting to a crime. "For months. In a language I don't know. I told myself it was stress. I transcribed some of it — phonetics, stress patterns — and last night I compared my transcriptions against this waveform, and the correspondence is—" He laughed, short and unhappy. "It's not approximate. It's *exact*. I've been dreaming in your message, Commander. Before I ever heard it."

*I know*, she almost said. *I know, because I watched you do it for six years, and I watched what it took out of you, and I sat beside you in the dark of another ship while you forgot your mother's face one syllable at a time.*

Instead she said: "Then you understand why I'm telling you this now, and why I waited three days."

"Do I?" There was an edge under the quiet now. Good. She deserved the edge. "Because from where I sit, Commander Okonkwo, you boarded my ship lying about your mission, and you're only talking to me because I'm useful. So before I decide whether to believe a single word of this — and I want to believe it, which is exactly what worries me — you're going to tell me why the truth matters more coming from you than from the admiralty. Because that convoy arrives tomorrow, and when it does, that black box goes to Station Vireo, and everyone aboard this ship gets a commendation and a confidentiality order. That's the safe path. Walk me through why I shouldn't take it."

*Trust offered as a gamble.* She'd learned the phrase from him — from the older him, in a briefing room on a ship that existed thirty years from now, when he'd explained why he'd bet his career on her testimony. Now she understood it from the other side of the table.

"Because I've seen where the safe path goes," she said.

"You've been asleep for thirty years—"

"I remember things I shouldn't." She said it flatly, refusing to dress it up. "Stasis damage, the doctors will tell you. Temporal displacement artifacts. Fine. Call it that. But I remember a ceasefire, Gideon. A specific ceasefire, signed at a relay station, after a broadcast that neither government could suppress. I remember the terms. I remember the year. And I remember that the broadcast happened because someone translated this message — all of it — and put it on every open channel in the Corridor." She leaned forward. "I also remember that the man who translated it isn't here to see it hold. Because translation isn't free. The language doesn't live in the signal, it lives in the *channel* — and the channel it opened runs through the translator's memory. Every phrase he decoded, he paid for. Names. Faces. Years. By the end, he'd traded away so much of himself that the victory he bought was one he couldn't remember winning."

The common room's ventilation cycled. Somewhere aft, Sefa dropped a wrench and swore with real artistry.

Gideon stared at her. "That's not how language works," he said, but his voice had lost its footing. "Memory isn't a currency. Cognition isn't a ledger. That's—"

"Folk science," she agreed. "I know. I said the same thing, once, to a man with your face, in a room I have no business remembering. And then I watched him forget his sister's name mid-sentence, and keep decoding, because the alternative was letting the lie stand." She exhaled. "I'm not asking you to believe the mechanism. Believe the stakes. Both governments know what the fold did — the debris fields are full of the crews they spent. The war keeps running because the war is cheaper than the confession. That message is the confession. And tomorrow it goes into a vault, and the vault goes into the war, and thirty more years of this Corridor burning happen because everyone took the safe path."

He was silent for a long moment. When he spoke again, his voice was steady in the way she remembered — the steadiness that was really a decision being made in real time.

"If I decode it, I decode all of it. Not fragments for your court-martial defense, not phrases for whoever claims jurisdiction next week. All of it, or none." His jaw tightened. "And I don't hand it to you, and I don't hand it to the admiralty. I decide who hears it. Those are my terms."

"That's more than I hoped for." She meant it. In the future she remembered, no one had given him terms. "One condition from me, then. Honesty about the cost. When you start losing things — and you will — you tell me. Don't hide it the way he hid it. Promise me."

"Why does it matter to you?" he asked. Genuine curiosity, no suspicion — and that, more than anything, nearly broke her composure. He didn't know her. To him she was a rescued stranger with a haunted ship and a suspicious story. He had no reason to care what the cost did to her to watch.

"Because I'm the only person in this timeline who knows what you're going to lose," she said. "Somebody should be keeping the inventory."

He studied her for a long moment — the look of a man weighing a stranger's sanity against his own dreams — and then he turned the slate back toward himself and began, softly, to read the waveform aloud, shaping the alien phonemes with a precision that made the hair rise on her arms.

The first phrase resolved against his dream-transcriptions like a key finding its lock. He said it twice, testing it, and then translated:

"*The fold remembers what the fleets burned.*"

Vess closed her eyes. Eleven years in the dark, and the message had finally spoken to someone who could hear it.

Outside the viewport, the lights of the approaching convoy pricked the black — ahead of schedule, running dark until the last hour. The admiralty was coming for its box.

Gideon saw them too. He looked at the lights, then at her, and set the slate down with a click that sounded, in the quiet, like a decision.

"We have until morning," he said. "Teach me faster."
