## 5. Visitors from the Ministry

The shuttle came down through Vesperine's amber morning like a needle through cloth, and Casper knew before it touched the pad that nothing about it was routine.

Supply ships arrived on schedules published months in advance. Survey drones arrived without arriving at all — they simply appeared in the sky, patient as weather. But this vessel cut its approach steep and fast, riding a column of ionized dust that turned the whole eastern horizon the color of old blood, and it bore no markings except a small, immaculate crest: a circle bisected by a single vertical line.

Casper stood at the edge of Terrace Three with a tray of nitrogen-fixing clover in his hands and watched it land, and felt something in his chest go quiet and cold, the way soil goes cold in the hour before dawn.

*They came because of the manifest.*

He had spent four days telling himself otherwise. Four days of the usual liturgy — misting cycles, pH buffers, the slow arithmetic of seedlings — while the anomaly report sat in his memory like a stone in a boot. *Clerical error,* he'd told himself. *Eleven-year-old records, corrupted archives, a name that rhymed with mine.* The universe was full of coincidences; he tended a garden for people who hadn't been born yet, so he knew better than most how much of life was planting things into uncertainty and hoping.

But the shuttle's ramp lowered, and two figures descended in matching gray coats, and one of them looked directly up at Terrace Three as if he already knew exactly where Casper Crane would be standing.

* * *

They met him in the administration module, which Ilsa had hastily cleared of everything personal — her photographs of the coast of Old Earth, her collection of war-era signal flags, the chipped mug she'd drunk from every morning for nine years. The room looked sterile now, and somehow that made it worse, as if they were all pretending the colony was something other than what it was: a handful of humans keeping a garden alive out of stubbornness.

The taller agent introduced himself as Verel. His partner was named Senn. They sat across the table from Casper with their hands folded, and everything about them was polite — their voices, their posture, the way Verel accepted the cup of water Ilsa offered with a small nod of thanks. Politeness, Casper thought, was the uniform of people who didn't need to raise their voices.

"Mr. Crane," Verel began. "Thank you for making time. We know Vesperine values its independence, and the Bureau respects that. This is a routine inquiry."

"Routine," Ilsa said from the doorway. She hadn't sat down. "You crossed eleven light-years of cordon for a routine inquiry."

"We crossed them quietly," Senn said, and smiled. He was younger, with a face like an unlined page. "That's a courtesy, Administrator Reyes. We could have simply queried your systems remotely. We chose to come in person. To be *polite.*"

The word hung there. Casper understood it was not a courtesy being described but a threat wearing one's clothes.

"What's this about?" Casper asked.

Verel produced a slate and laid it flat on the table, screen up. On it glowed the manifest of the *Corvane*, rendered in clean Republic typography, and beside it, highlighted in soft amber, a single line:

**CRANE, C. — TERRAFORM SPECIALIST, CLASS II.**

"You docked at Halcyon Gate twelve days ago," Verel said. "During standard cargo processing, an automated audit cross-referenced your registry against historical manifests. Your name produced a match. A ship called the *Corvane*, destroyed during the opening phase of the Separation Crisis." He folded his hands again. "We'd like you to explain the match."

"It's not me." The words came out too fast. Casper heard himself lying the way he heard a drone misfiring a seed — some mechanical error, immediately obvious, impossible to unmake. "I've never been on any warship. I'm a terraformer. I've been on Vesperine nine years. Check my service record."

"Oh, we have." Verel's tone didn't change at all. "Nine years, exemplary. That's precisely what interests us. You see, Mr. Crane, the manifest entry is dated three days *before* the Corvane was destroyed. Someone with your name boarded that ship, and then that ship burned above Vesperine with everyone aboard — including, according to every official record, a man named Elias Crane, who was listed among the dead of the incident we're instructed to call the Vesperine Tragedy." A pause, delicate as a scalpel. "Your brother, I believe."

The room went very still. Somewhere outside, a maintenance drone hummed past the window, indifferent, tending rows no one would walk for years.

"I haven't thought about Elias in a long time," Casper said.

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I have."

Senn leaned forward slightly, and his smile widened by a degree, and Casper thought: *he's enjoying this.* "Here's our difficulty, Mr. Crane. The audit flagged the name. Then someone at Halcyon Gate — a cargo clerk, we think — ran a secondary query. An unauthorized one. Into black-market archives. And that query used access credentials registered to this colony." He let the silence do its work. "So either you're curious about your dead brother, or you're not. But somebody here certainly is."

Casper kept his face still through sheer force of habit — the same stillness he used around fragile seedlings, around frost warnings, around anything that could be killed by sudden movement. But inside, panic was blooming like algae in a warm tank. *Dov,* he thought. *Or the clerk sold the query log. Or both.* Every path he'd taken toward the truth had left footprints, and here were two men in gray coats reading them aloud.

"I run supply routes," Casper said. "I talk to a lot of people. If someone used my colony's credentials, I'd want to know too."

"I'm sure you would." Verel retrieved the slate. "And we believe you. Truly. The Bureau has no interest in troubling a loyal citizen of the Republic." He rose, and Senn rose with him, and Casper felt the first breath of relief begin — foolishly, as it turned out — when Verel added, almost as an afterthought: "One more thing. We understand Vesperine's agricultural program has been requesting replacement canisters. Soil inoculants, nutrient stock. We took the liberty of bringing some. A gesture of goodwill between the Bureau and the frontier."

Ilsa's voice came from the doorway, flat as a closed hatch. "We didn't request anything."

"No," Verel agreed pleasantly. "Not yet."

* * *

They left two crates.

Casper carried them to the garden himself, refusing help, wanting to be alone with the weight of them. The canisters inside were beautiful — matte white, seamless, stamped with the Republic's agricultural seal, each one holding what the label claimed was a standard rhizobial culture. They weighed exactly what they should weigh. They smelled faintly of nitrogen and preservative. Everything about them was correct.

That was what frightened him.

He set them on the potting bench in the propagation shed and stood looking at them for a long time while the afternoon light went copper through the shed's polycarbonate panels. Nine years he had handled canisters like these. He knew their tolerances the way he knew his own pulse. And these were wrong in ways he couldn't name — a half-millimeter too perfect in their seams, maybe, or the sealant cured under spectrum that didn't quite match Vesperine's sun. Objects made to be looked at, not used.

He thought about opening one. He thought about burying all six of them under the compost heap where nothing would ever find them. He did neither. Instead he logged them into the inventory, because not logging them would itself be a signal, and then he went to find Ilsa.

She was in the comms room, and she had locked the door behind him, and before he could speak she said:

"They left a tracker."

It wasn't a question.

"In the canisters?"

"In one of them. Third from the left." She pulled up a schematic on her console — a cross-section of a canister rendered in wireframe, and nested in its base, a component that had no business existing: a lattice of filaments wrapped around a dense core, pulsing softly on the display. "Passive until activated. It's been listening since the moment they handed it to us. Every conversation in range, every system ping, every word spoken in this garden." She turned to look at him, and her face in the console light was older than her years, carved by things she rarely spoke about. "Now you're going to tell me what's going on, Casper. All of it. Because those men knew Elias's name, and they said *Tragedy* the way the Ministry says it, capital-T, and you lied to them so badly I could hear your pulse from the doorway."

Casper opened his mouth. Closed it. Outside the window, the terraces descended in their long green steps toward the valley floor, each level a promise made to people who hadn't come. Nine years of work. Nine years of the only honest thing he'd ever done.

"If I tell you," he said finally, "you're implicated. Whatever this is — whatever they think it is — knowing makes you part of it."

"Casper." Ilsa's voice softened, and that was worse than anger. "I was a signals officer for six years during the Separation. I have been *implicated* since before you ever planted your first row. The only question is whether I'm implicated blind." She stepped closer. "Those men will come back. Next time they won't be polite. So either you trust me, or you're alone with them, and I have watched you be alone for nine years and I am *done* watching it."

The words landed harder than he expected. He looked away, at the garden, at the misting drones beginning their evening circuit, silver threads stitching the terraces together.

"The manifest is real," he said quietly. "Elias's name is on it. Not just a match — his name, his service number, his birthdate. And there's a packet attached. Encrypted. Addressed to coordinates only he would know." He swallowed. "Ilsa, my brother died in the Massacre. I buried him. Everyone buried him. And now there's a ghost leaving me mail from a burning ship."

For a long moment she said nothing. The console hummed. Somewhere below, a wind pattern rolled across the valley and the garden breathed with it, ten thousand leaves turning at once.

"And the packet?" she asked. "Have you opened it?"

"Not yet. I need equipment I don't have."

"You have the terraforming array."

He looked at her sharply. "That's colony infrastructure. Using it for—"

"Using it for what, Casper? Say the sentence." Her eyes held his, steady and terrible. "Because I don't think you know yet what's in that packet. But I think you're afraid of it. And I think those men in gray coats are afraid of it too, which means it matters." She exhaled slowly. "I'll deal with the tracker. I'll feed it noise — maintenance chatter, drone telemetry, boring, endless, harmless noise, until we decide what to do. That buys us time. But time is all I can give you." She put a hand on his shoulder, brief and hard. "Whatever you're going to do — decide fast. Because Verel struck me as a man who files reports, and reports travel, and somewhere beyond that cordon there are people who are much less polite than he is."

* * *

That night Casper couldn't sleep.

He walked the upper terrace instead, under the artificial stars of the climate dome, past rows of young trees that would shade children not yet conceived. The tracker in the shed below was listening even now, he supposed — drinking in the whisper of ventilation fans and the rustle of irrigation lines, Ilsa's manufactured noise flowing over it like sediment over a buried thing.

His slate buzzed against his hip. One message, routed through three relays, stripped of origin.

He opened it standing among the saplings, and the light of it washed his face pale blue, and the message was four words long:

**FIND ME AT THE WRECK.**

No signature. No coordinates. Just the handwriting of a dead man, arriving eleven years late — and beneath it, faint as a watermark, an encryption signature that made the hair rise on Casper's arms, because it matched nothing in the Republic's registries, nothing in the Compact's, nothing official anywhere.

Something third. Something that belonged to no flag he knew.

Below him, in the propagation shed, the tracker pulsed silently in its white canister, listening to a garden grow. And far overhead, beyond the dome, beyond the amber sky, ships were already moving — he could feel it the way farmers feel weather — converging on a colony that had only ever wanted to be left alone to plant things.

Casper closed the message. In the morning, he would need the array. In the morning, everything would change.

But tonight he stood a while longer among the unborn orchard, saying goodbye to the last quiet hours of the life he'd built.
