## 10. The Other Side of Silence

Dov Maro had crossed the Line eleven times in his life, and every crossing had cost him something he couldn't put on a manifest.

The first time, he'd been nineteen and stupid, running medical gel to a Compact mining station that the Republic blockade had written off as acceptable losses. He'd made three times his usual fee and spent it all on a funeral for his cousin Petra, who hadn't been so lucky with her own run. The second through tenth crossings had taught him the rest of the curriculum: how to read a patrol net's breathing patterns, how to make a ship look boring, how to lie with paperwork the way other men lied with their faces. The eleventh crossing had taught him what fear actually was.

This would be the twelfth.

He sat in the Meridian's copilot seat — Casper's seat, technically, though the terraformer flew like a man who'd learned from manuals and prayed during turbulence — and watched the border come up on the long-range display like a scar across the dark. The Republic called it the Demarcation Veil. The Compact called it the Shield of Sovereignty. Dov called it what it was: a line drawn through the middle of a family, because two governments had decided that grief was easier to govern than guilt.

"You're doing the thing again," Casper said from the pilot's chair.

"What thing."

"The thing where you stare at nothing and your jaw does that." Casper nodded at him without looking over. "Ilsa does it too. Colony people. You all learned to worry in silence."

"Colony people learned it because talking about worry doesn't grow crops." Dov shifted in the seat. The Meridian smelled like soil and nutrient solution and the faint green breath of the seed vaults — a smell he'd never gotten used to in thirty years of hauling cargo that mostly smelled like fuel and regret. "Your brother's papers are good for another six hours. After that, we find out how good."

Casper didn't answer right away. Through the viewport, Vesperine was already a memory behind them, a smudge of engineered weather and terraced light, shrinking toward irrelevance. Dov had watched the man say goodbye to it through a dust storm, no clearance filed, no farewell transmitted, and he'd seen what that cost. It was the same face Petra's mother had worn when they sealed her daughter's coffin. The face of someone leaving a piece of themselves on the wrong side of a door.

"He'll hold," Dov said, because Casper needed to hear it out loud even if it wasn't entirely true. "Elias. Eleven years alone will hollow a man, but it doesn't kill the part that waits. I've seen it."

"You've seen men who waited eleven years?"

"I've seen men who waited four." He let that sit. "The waiting is the same. Only the arithmetic changes."

* * *

The Line itself was nothing and everything. On sensors it appeared as a lattice of listening posts strung along a curve of empty space like beads on a wire, each one blind to the other side but deaf to everything between. The Concordance had built most of them, back when there was still one government to betray, and both factions had kept them running afterward because surveillance was cheaper than trust.

Dov took the Meridian down into the debris shoals — the graveyard of the war's opening months, where hulls drifted in slow orbits around a dead gas giant's outer moons. It was an old smuggler's trick: hide your signature inside the signature of ten thousand wrecks. But it meant flying through the wreckage itself, and the wreckage meant remembering.

There. A Republic corvette, split lengthwise, its reactor scar still glowing faintly after eleven years like a coal that refused to die. There, a Compact freighter with its cargo doors blown open, spilling a frozen river of grain across the void — wheat, someone had told him once, actual wheat, meant for famine relief, destroyed by the very war its shipment was supposed to prevent. And everywhere the small things, the things nobody memorialized: escape pods with their hatches open, tool kits drifting in lazy constellations, a child's jacket, maybe, or just a piece of insulation shaped like one. Dov had stopped looking closely years ago. Looking closely was how you found names.

"They were all somebody's brother," Casper said quietly. He was pressed to the viewport, watching the shoals scroll past.

"Yes."

"That's what the recording said. The partial one. That the massacre was staged." His voice had the flatness of a man reciting something he still didn't believe. "Eleven thousand people, Dov. They killed eleven thousand people to draw a line on a map."

"Worse than that." Dov adjusted their drift, nudging the hauler deeper into the shoal shadow. "They killed eleven thousand people so that nobody would ever have to count the rest. You understand? One atrocity, named and numbered and mourned forever, and behind it —" he gestured at the debris scrolling past, the corvettes and freighters and pods, the whole silent census of the war — "behind it, forty years of this, and everyone on both sides gets to say *at least we're not them*. At least we didn't do the massacre. The massacre is the wall they hide behind."

"And the Concordance built the wall."

"The Concordance sold the wall. Built it, mortgaged it, and collects rent on both sides." Dov checked the passive array. Clean, for now. "You don't end a business model like that with a broadcast. You end it with a fire. And fires, little gardener, do not care whose house they started in."

* * *

They ran dark for nine hours through the shoals, engines cold, life support on recycle, the two of them sitting in a silence that grew teeth. Dov used the time the way he always did: cataloguing exits, rehearsing lies, remembering the dead. Casper used it tending the seed vaults, moving through the hauler's spine with a hand-light, checking moisture seals and germination trays as if the fate of humanity weren't folded into a data chip in his breast pocket. Dov watched him do it and understood, finally, why the man had survived nine years of gardening for ghosts. It wasn't denial. It was discipline. You kept the living things alive because the alternative was to stop, and stopping was death with better posture.

At hour ten, the patrol net found them anyway.

Not by sensor — by pattern. A Compact interdiction frigate slid out of the shoal shadow two klicks off their bow, running lights blazing, so close and so casual that it could only mean they'd been tracked since the border. The hail came in clipped, accented Standard:

*Unregistered hauler, this is Compact Customs Vessel Sternwatch. Maintain course and velocity. Prepare for boarding inspection. Resistance will be logged.*

"Logged," Dov repeated. "That's new. Used to be *punished*."

"Is that better or worse?"

"Better. Bureaucracies that log you intend to let you go. Bureaucracies that punish you intend to make an example." He was already moving, pulling the forged documentation from the hidden compartment beneath the nav console — papers he'd paid half a year's earnings for, built around Casper's real name because the forger had sworn that a false name was the first thing customs software sniffed for. *Casper Crane, independent agricultural contractor, licensed through the Halcyon free registry, carrying seed stock and soil substrate under Compact trade exemption 44-C.* Every word true except the ones that mattered.

"Casper." He caught the man's arm. "When they board, you say nothing unless asked. When asked, you answer short. Terraformers are supposed to be dull. Be dull. Think about compost."

"I know about compost."

"Then think about it *out loud*, slowly, and we live."

* * *

The boarding party came across in two shuttles, six inspectors in grey-and-crimson Compact livery, their sidearms holstered but present the way weather is present. Their officer was a woman in her fifties with a face like a topographical map of some hard country, and she walked the Meridian's corridors reading them the way Dov read shipping lanes — for what had been changed, what had been hidden, what had been grieved over.

She stopped in the seed vault.

Dov's stomach dropped. Of all the rooms. The vault was full of Vesperine, full of the colony's whole tender argument with the future — trays of embryonic wheat, cryo-dormant orchard stock, the fungal cultures Casper babied like children. Nothing contraband. Everything incriminating, in the way that hope is incriminating to people who've been ordered to stop hoping.

"Agricultural contractor," she said. Not a question.

"Yes, ma'am," Casper said. Dull. Perfectly dull. "Colony development work. Pre-settlement phase."

"Pre-settlement." She lifted a tray of dormant seedlings, turned it in the light. "How long has your colony been pre-settlement?"

"Nine years."

Something moved behind her eyes — not suspicion. Recognition. She set the tray down with more gentleness than she'd handled anything else on the ship. "We had a program like that. Before the Accords. Meridian-class colonies, both sides of the Line. Twelve of them, total. Do you know how many ever received settlers?"

"No, ma'am."

"One." She looked at the tray a moment longer. "Ours. The other eleven are still waiting. Still staffed. Still growing food for people who exist only in planning documents." She turned to Dov, and the softness shuttered closed. "Your papers, contractor."

Dov handed them over and prepared to die by degrees while she read.

It took four minutes. He counted. Four minutes of the inspector's thumb moving down the document, of Casper standing rigid beside the seed trays radiating compost thoughts, of the young inspector by the door shifting his weight in a way that meant he'd done boarding actions that went badly. Four minutes, and then the woman looked up.

"Exemption 44-C covers substrate and viable germplasm." Her voice was flat as a ledger. "It does not cover decryption hardware. Your aft compartment contains a signal analysis rig with military-grade filtering. Care to explain?"

Dov's mouth opened. Closed. Every lie he owned lined up behind his teeth and none of them stepped forward.

"It's mine," Casper said.

The officer turned.

"I use it to analyze soil spectrography returns. Remote sensing. The military-grade filtering is surplus — I bought it at Halcyon Gate, third-hand, from a decommissioning yard. I can show you the purchase record." He could, too; Dov had built it into the papers for exactly this contingency, and hearing Casper deploy it without a flicker made him reassess everything he thought he knew about gardeners. "Terraforming is signals work, Inspector. You spend nine years listening to a planet before you dare plant anything in it."

Silence. The vault's climate system breathed. Somewhere in the stacks, a moisture seal cycled with a sound like a sigh.

"Listening to a planet," she repeated. She looked at Casper for a long moment — and Dov saw it happen, saw the exact instant the inspection stopped being an inspection and became something older and stranger. She was looking at him the way you look at a man standing in a field you used to own. "My brother was a signals officer," she said. "Kestrel Station, year two of the war. Your Republic's fleet burned it. Or our own command did — the records disagree, and the records are allowed to disagree, which tells you everything." She handed the papers back to Dov. "Your filtration rig is improperly documented. Fine of eight hundred credits, payable at any Compact port. Fix your paperwork, Contractor Crane. The Line is not kind to men who carry the wrong kind of listening equipment."

"Yes, ma'am."

She turned to go, then paused at the vault door. Without looking back she said, "Twelve colonies. One settled. Whoever ends up counting the graves from the last war should also count the gardens." Then she was gone, and her inspectors with her, and the airlock cycled, and the Meridian was alone again in the dark.

* * *

They didn't speak for an hour after the frigate fell away. Dov flew them out of the shoals on a quiet burn, threading the wreck-field's last fingers, and only when Compact space opened up around them — vast, hostile, and for the moment indifferent — did he let out a breath he felt he'd been holding since the border.

"Eight hundred credits," he said. "She fined us eight hundred credits."

"She believed us."

"She *pitied* us. Belief is cheaper." He rubbed his face with both hands. "That woman lost a brother to your side's guns, or her side's lies, or both, and she let us go because you reminded her of a garden. Do you understand what that means? It means the enemy is not what the files say it is. It has never been what the files say it is. Both governments have spent eleven years selling us a picture of each other, and the picture is worth less than the paper it's printed on."

Casper was quiet for a while. Out the viewport, Compact space unrolled — different stars, same darkness.

"When Elias sends his message," he said finally, "'find me at the wreck' — he's been out here. In this. Eleven years on the other side of the Line, inside the territory my government taught me to hate." His voice caught on something. "What if he's not — what if eleven years made him into someone I—"

"Then you'll meet him anyway," Dov said. "That's what brothers do. They meet each other anyway." He fed new coordinates into the helm — the Corvane's last recorded position, deep in the Shoalwater Drift, past two more patrol nets and a Compact naval reserve anchorage. "We're twenty hours out. Twenty hours, and then whatever's left of your brother, you collect him yourself."

He said it lightly. It didn't land lightly. Nothing landed lightly anymore; the whole sky had gone heavy with what they carried.

Dov settled back into the copilot seat and watched the enemy's stars turn slowly overhead, and thought about Petra, and about the inspector's brother, and about twelve colonies growing food for the unborn on both sides of a line that had never once kept anyone safe. Somewhere ahead, in the frozen throat of a murdered ship, a man who should have been dead eleven years was keeping a secret hot enough to burn down two governments.

Dov had crossed the Line eleven times, and every crossing had cost him something.

He was beginning to suspect the twelfth would cost him everything — and that, for the first time in his life, he might actually get his money's worth.

Ahead, the Shoalwater Drift opened its mouth.
