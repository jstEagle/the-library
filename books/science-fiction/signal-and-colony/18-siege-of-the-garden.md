## 18. Siege of the Garden

Vesperine rose out of the dark like a held breath.

Casper saw it first as a smudge of reflected light on the Meridian's forward glass — the colony's unfinished arcologies catching the system's pale sun, the terraced garden a green scar across the ochre face of the southern plateau. Nine years he had flown this approach. Nine years of watching that green spread one terrace at a time, patient as lichen, certain in its smallness. He had never once come home to find warships above it.

There were three fleets.

The Republic's Bureau vessels hung in a tight wedge at high orbit, hulls the grey of gunmetal and bureaucracy, their running lights strobing the regulation pattern that meant *we are authorized*. Farther out, arranged in a loose crescent like a hand half-opened, the Compact's flotilla — darker ships, older lines, engines idling hot enough that Casper could read their impatience in the infrared bloom. And between them, closer to the planet than either dared to be, moved the Concordance hunters: sleek, unmarked, the kind of ships that officially did not exist, flying in formations no navy would admit to teaching.

They had not fired. Not yet. The standoff had the terrible stillness of a held knife — everyone waiting to see which throat would open first, everyone aware that whoever struck would be striking in front of witnesses.

"They see us," Elias said from the copilot's seat. His voice was gravel dragged over gravel. He had not slept in thirty hours; the wound in his side had soaked through two dressings, and his skin had gone the color of the dust storms that scoured Vesperine's northern hemisphere. But his eyes were clear. They had been clear since the relay station, since he'd pressed the decryption key into Casper's palm and said *you decide*, and whatever clarity was keeping him upright now, Casper intended to borrow every gram of it. "Bureau's pinging us. Compact too. Everyone wants to know who we are."

"Tell them nothing."

"They'll shoot us down as unknowns."

"No." Casper brought the Meridian around, threading toward the night side, letting the planet's bulk swallow them from the fleets' sightlines. "Nobody shoots. Not here. This is the whole point of everything they've done for eleven years — keep it quiet, keep it clean, keep it *deniable*. A hauler full of smoke falling on a civilian colony is not deniable. They're all waiting for someone else to be the monster first."

Elias made a sound that might have been a laugh if laughter hadn't forgotten how to live in him. "And you're going to make them watch anyway."

"I'm going to make everyone watch."

Below them, the terminator line crawled westward, dragging dawn across the plateau. Casper had watched that dawn a thousand times from the garden's upper terraces, coffee cooling in his hands, checking the moisture wicks while the light came up amber through the climate membranes. He knew every contour of that land the way you know your own heartbeat — the dry wash where the first soil samples had come back alive against every prediction, the windbreak of engineered poplars Ilsa had insisted on planting before there was anyone to sit under them, the long shallow bowl of the broadcast array, half-buried, humming its terraforming frequencies into rock and sky.

He was going to land in the middle of it. In the garden. In the only place on two worlds' worth of territory that had never belonged to a flag.

*Home transformed into battlefield.* The phrase arrived in his mind uninvited, cold and precise, and he pushed it away because there was no time to grieve what was about to happen to the most beautiful thing he had ever helped build.

"Dov's transponder died an hour ago," Elias said quietly. It wasn't a question. They had both felt it — the sudden hole in the traffic chatter where Dov Maro's ship should have been taunting the Concordance pickets, buying them their corridor with noise and nerve. Somewhere behind them, in the black between the border and here, Dov had spent himself the way he'd promised he would: loudly, gloriously, and without any intention of coming back. Casper hadn't let himself think about it yet. He filed it in the place where grief went to wait its turn. There would be a turn. Just not now.

"He bought us the window," Casper said. "We use all of it."

He dropped the Meridian low, riding the storm front.

* * *

The dust storm had been building for two days — Ilsa's last message had mentioned it, her voice stripped down to compressed syllables over the covert channel: *Storm front off the northern massif. Bureau has two shuttles groundside at the spaceport. Concordance team inserted near the array sometime overnight — we found their breach kit. Defense corps is twelve people with hunting rifles and a lot of stubbornness. Come home, Casper. Land in the garden. We'll hold it.*

*Come home.* As if home were a door left unlocked. As if nine years of tending had been leading somewhere after all.

The storm swallowed them at four kilometers. Dust turned the world brown-gold and screaming; the Meridian shuddered like something alive being carried. Casper flew by memory and instrument, hands steady in the way they only ever were when the work was impossible — the same calm that came over him during seed vault failures, during pressure breaches, during every crisis that asked him to be exactly as careful as the situation deserved and not one degree more frightened than useful.

"There," Elias said, pointing through the grit-smeared glass.

The garden emerged from the storm like a vision — the terraces glowing under their emergency floodlights, the climate membranes billowing, the great ribs of the array dome rising out of the dust with their sensor masts bent sideways by the wind. Small figures moved between the terraces. Too few. Carrying weapons that looked obscene among the planter beds.

Casper brought the hauler down through the gap between the third and fourth terraces — a landing corridor he'd measured a hundred times for supply drones, never for a forty-meter ship — and set the Meridian down in the wide basin of the old irrigation reservoir, thrusters flattening a decade of carefully composted topsoil into a blast ring. The ship settled with a groan. Dust closed over them like water.

For a moment, in the sudden quiet, Casper could hear the garden itself: the tick of the irrigation lines, the whisper of the membranes, the deep subsonic hum of the terraforming array beneath everything, patient as a heart.

"We're down," he said. "Stay with the ship until—"

Gunfire cracked somewhere above them. Muffled by the storm, but unmistakable — the flat stutter of kinetic rounds against composite, answered by two slower, heavier reports. Hunting rifles. Ilsa's twelve.

Elias was already unbuckling, face white, one hand pressed to his side. "The array," he said. "If they take the core before you're in—"

"I know."

"Casper." His brother caught his sleeve. Eleven years of silence were in his grip, and underneath the blood-loss pallor his eyes were wet. "Whatever happens in there. You were the one who kept something growing. All this time, while I was—" He stopped. Swallowed. "Go."

Casper gripped his brother's shoulder once, hard, and went.

* * *

The storm was a weapon and a shield. It shredded visibility to meters and swallowed sound, so that Casper climbed the service ladder from the reservoir basin into a chaos of orange light and blowing grit, muzzle flashes blooming and dying like heat lightning, and could not tell friend from enemy until he was close enough to touch them.

He nearly shot Ilsa.

She materialized out of the dust at the mouth of the fourth-terrace access tunnel, rifle braced, and only years of knowing the way she stood — weight forward, chin tucked, the posture of a woman who had learned to aim at things she loved — stopped his finger on the trigger.

"You're late," she said.

"Traffic."

Her face was streaked with dust and something darker at the temple, a graze crusted over. Behind her, in the flickering light, Casper saw the shape of the battle: the defense corps had fallen back to the array dome itself, fighting from the terrace walls, using the planter beds as cover. Two Concordance operatives lay in the dirt of the herb beds — Casper recognized the mint, absurdly, thought *that was going to be for the settlers' kitchens* — and three more were working up the switchback path in armored pairs, methodical as machines. Above them all, the dome's floodlights swept the storm, and inside the dome, visible through the shattered observation panel, the broadcast core waited: a column of old terraforming hardware wrapped in cable and light, built to sing to stone and seed, about to be asked to sing to two civilizations instead.

"They've got a breaching charge on the maintenance hatch," Ilsa said, shouting over the wind now. "Thirty seconds, maybe less. We can't get to it. Can you?"

"The core room connects to the nutrient line access. Sub-level." He had crawled those tunnels a hundred times, hauling fertilizer, repairing pumps, alone with his headphones and his quiet contentment. Every lonely hour of nine years had been reconnaissance for this. "Keep them off the dome. Thirty seconds, Ilsa. Then get everyone behind the north berm, because when this thing fires at full power, the membranes are going to blow."

She looked at him — really looked, the way she had that first day nine years ago when he'd shown her a tray of seedlings and she'd said *you understand this isn't for us, right? Nobody's coming for years* — and whatever she saw in his face now, she nodded once and turned to her people and started shouting coordinates.

Casper ran.

* * *

The nutrient tunnels were dim and smelled of earth and mineral solution, and for eleven seconds — the length of the sprint from the access hatch to the sub-level junction — Casper was briefly, unbearably homesick inside his own home. Here was the pump housing he'd rebuilt twice. Here was the hairline crack he'd sealed with polymer and prayer. Here was the wall where he'd scratched a tally mark for every month the garden survived without a single settler to walk it, a habit he'd told no one, one hundred and eight marks climbing the concrete like ivy.

He passed them at a dead run.

The core room door was sealed from the inside — Ilsa's doing, or the defense corps'. Casper keyed his override, the one he'd installed himself because he'd never trusted anyone else with the garden's heart, and slipped through into heat and light and the enormous rising hum of the array spinning up.

The core stood in its cradle like a tree. That was always how he'd thought of it — the engineers had called it a broadcast lattice, but to Casper it had always been a tree, trunk of conduit, branches of antenna filament, roots sunk into the bedrock of a world that didn't exist yet except in soil samples and stubbornness. Its status board glowed amber: standby. Waiting, as it had waited for nine years, to be given a reason.

Behind him, muffled through meters of rock and soil, the breaching charge detonated. The floor jumped. Dust sifted from the ceiling in slow gold curtains. Shouting followed — Ilsa's voice, sharp and commanding, and the crack of rifles, and then the deeper percussion of Concordance weapons answering, and Casper understood with terrible clarity that the sounds above him were people spending themselves, minute by minute, to buy him seconds at a keyboard.

He jacked in.

His hands knew the interface the way they knew seed trays. Authentication, bypassed — he'd built the garden's systems; the garden trusted him. Power routing: he took the array off its terraforming cycle and fed it everything, every reserve cell, every trickle charger, the whole accumulated patience of nine years of solar collection poured into one moment. The hum climbed. The filaments began to glow along their lengths like branches budding light.

The key sat in his pocket where Elias had pressed it, warm from his brother's body. Casper slotted it home.

The archive opened like a wound.

Forged orders. Staged atrocity footage, timestamped and annotated by the hands that staged it. Shipping manifests of arms sold to both sides by the same brokers. The Silence Accords draft memos, written *before* the massacre they claimed to answer. Names — hundreds of names, on every world, in every faction, the whole architecture of a lie built to keep humanity divided and profitable. It flooded the console, terabytes of guilt, and the array drank it and asked for more.

Above him, the gunfire changed character. Closer. Someone had reached the dome.

"Casper!" Ilsa's voice, raw, over the local channel. "They're inside the north gallery — we can't—"

"Hold the tunnel door. Sixty seconds."

"We don't *have*—"

"*Sixty seconds.*"

He threw the switches.

The array had been designed to speak gently — to coax microbes awake in frozen regolith, to murmur encouragement to root systems, to seed clouds with a whisper. It had never been asked to shout. Casper took every governor off it, every limiter, and felt the deck plates begin to vibrate under his boots like the whole garden drawing one enormous breath.

On his display, the targeting solution assembled itself: both faction networks, both homeworlds, every relay, every receiver, the entire nervous system of a divided species. Unencrypted. Unstoppable. Undeniable.

The dome above him rang like a struck bell — the Concordance operatives hitting the outer shell, cutting, charging. Through the observation panel Casper saw torchlight, armored shapes, and beyond them, silhouetted against the floodlights, Ilsa and her twelve holding the inner gallery with their bodies.

The power readout climbed past redline. The filaments blazed. The garden — his garden, the one he'd kept for people not yet born — stood ready to speak to everyone at once.

Casper put his hand flat on the console, over the tally marks he couldn't see but knew were there, one hundred and eight months of waiting, and reached for the transmit key.

The dome split open above him like an egg, and the first Concordance operative dropped into the core room with a weapon leveled at his chest — and Casper, looking down the barrel of the gun that had chased him across two borders and a wreck field, smiled, and pressed transmit.

The world filled with light.

* * *

Every screen on two worlds lit with the same truth at the same instant. In fleet command bunkers and mining camp mess halls, in the Bureau's grey towers and the Compact's deep-orbit habitats, the forged orders scrolled and the staged footage played and the names came, name after name after name, and above Vesperine three armadas went silent mid-maneuver, thousands of crews watching their own complicity bloom across their instruments.

In the core room, the operative's weapon wavered. His comms were screaming. He looked at Casper — at the tired man in the dusty coveralls standing in a garden that had just ended a war — and did not fire.

Casper looked back, and behind the operative, through the ruined dome, through the dying storm, he saw the fleets hanging motionless in the dawn-lit sky like words caught in a throat.

Then the operative's earpiece crackled, and his face changed, and he raised the weapon again — because some orders, Casper understood, outlive even the truth — and from the tunnel doorway behind them both, Ilsa Reyes stepped into the light with her rifle already aimed, and said, very quietly, in the voice of a woman who had buried one war already:

"Not in my garden."
