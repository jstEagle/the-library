## 5. Crew of the Long Route

The *Long Route* had a crew complement of one for eleven years, and I want to be clear about something before I tell you how that changed: I liked it that way.

A ship is a conversation. Every hull plate, every coolant line, every flickering console has an opinion, and if you're alone long enough you learn to hear them all. The port thruster complained in a dialect of low groans. The galley recycler whistled when it was lying about its filter status. I spoke fluent ship. What I had never learned to speak — not in forty years of study, not across two hundred dead languages — was *people*, plural, at close range, on purpose.

So when Ilsa Voss looked at me over the ruins of Meridian-9's archive and said, "You can't run this operation alone," my first instinct was to list the ways she was wrong. My second instinct was to notice that I couldn't think of any.

* * *

Sefu Adeyemi found us before we found her, which should have been our first warning.

We'd docked at Ceres Verge, a refueling station that smelled like burnt ozone and regret, and I was standing at the cargo manifest terminal trying to remember which forms declared "one disgraced archivist, one xenolinguist, one forged apocalypse" when a woman dropped out of the ventilation shaft above me.

Not climbed. Dropped. Landed in a crouch, straightened up, and said, "You're Halloran. The relay pilot."

"I'm nobody," I said, which was true and also a lie, and I was beginning to appreciate how often those two things travel together.

"You're the man who's been transmitting in Vessic." She said it quietly, but the word Vessic still landed like a dropped wrench in the middle of the concourse. She glanced around, then grabbed my sleeve. "Not here. Your ship. Now."

In the galley, over tea that tasted like hot cardboard, she laid it out. Sefu Adeyemi, formerly of the Corvane Fleet Auxiliary, honorably discharged in the way that means dishonorably discharged but with paperwork. She'd spent six years flying courier runs through contested space, and six months ago she'd started noticing something odd: traffic patterns that only made sense if both fleets were avoiding a corridor no one had ordered them to avoid.

"Someone's scaring them off," she said. "And whoever it is, they're using old signal architecture. Pre-Collapse handshake protocols. I ran the numbers for a year before I realized what I was looking at." She leaned forward. "Then three weeks ago, a distress burst in a language I couldn't ID, and every database in the Verge came back empty. Except one forum post, buried, from a linguistics hobbyist who swore it matched Vessic." She looked at me. "Vessic died two hundred years ago. So either I'm crazy, or you're a ghost with excellent diction."

"Both fleets are watching this corridor because of me," Ilsa said. "Or rather, because of what I made them believe is watching."

I expected Sefu to laugh. Instead she went very still, the way pilots go still, processing three seconds of telemetry that changes everything about the next thirty years.

"That's insane," she said finally.

"Yes," Ilsa agreed.

"It's also the most elegant thing I've ever heard." Sefu drained her tea. "I want in."

"Why?" I asked. It seemed important to ask. It would remain important for exactly as long as it took me to stop asking it.

She shrugged, and for a moment the competence fell away and I saw the thing underneath it — a woman who'd been very good at a job that stopped wanting her. "Because I've spent six years flying between two fleets that hate each other, waiting for the day they stop being careful. You're the reason they're still careful. That's the closest thing to a mission worth having that anyone's offered me since the Auxiliary cut me loose." She stuck out her hand. "Also, your ship handles like a drunk ox and I find that offensive. I want to fix it."

That's how we got Sefu. I mention the handshake because it was the last straightforward transaction of the entire recruitment.

* * *

Tam Okafor-Reyes did not want to be recruited. Tam Okafor-Reyes wanted, more than anything in the occupied systems, to be left alone, and I respected that so much that I immediately ruined it.

We needed an engineer because Meridian-9's transmitter — the forged heart of the whole peace — was dying, and neither Ilsa nor I could tell a capacitor from a coffee maker. Ilsa had a name: a former Ashen Fleet engineer, drummed out after refusing to certify a weapons array she called, in her official report, "a war crime with a serial number." Last seen working salvage out of Pallas Drift.

Pallas Drift turned out to be a scrapyard pretending to be a station, and Tam Okafor-Reyes turned out to be a person pretending to be a scrapyard. We found her elbow-deep in the guts of a decommissioned freighter, surrounded by parts organized according to a system comprehensible only to her, wearing goggles pushed up into gray-streaked hair and an expression that suggested we were the fourth-worst thing to happen to her that day.

"No," she said, before Ilsa finished the first sentence.

"You haven't heard—"

"Forged third-power transmissions holding two fleets apart. Heard. No." She pulled a coupling free with a crack that sounded personal. "Do you know what happens when a lie that big needs maintenance? The maintenance becomes the lie. Somebody has to keep the machine running forever, and forever is a long time to be the person whose hands are on it."

"Weeks, not forever," Ilsa said. "Until we can stabilize the transmitter."

Tam snorted. "Everything is 'until.' I certified 'untils' for nine years. The until always arrives." But she'd stopped working, and I noticed — I notice things, it's my entire pathology — that she hadn't told us to leave.

It was Juno who closed the deal, though none of us knew Juno existed yet.

Because while Ilsa argued engineering ethics with a woman holding a plasma torch, a small figure in an oversized coat slipped aboard the *Long Route* through the cargo hatch, hid behind a crate of water reclamation filters, and waited. We discovered this four hours later, lifting out of Pallas Drift, when the crate sneezed.

I want to state for the record that I handled it well. I did not yell. I did not reach for the emergency protocols. I stood in the cargo bay looking down at a kid — sixteen, maybe seventeen, sharp-faced, dark curls escaping a knit cap, eyes doing rapid threat assessment — and said, with tremendous calm:

"You're stowing away on a ship with no crew, no cargo, and no destination I'm willing to disclose."

"Yeah," the kid said. "I know. That's why I picked it."

"That's the opposite of how picking works."

"Worked, didn't it?"

I looked at Ilsa. Ilsa looked at me. And I understood, in that moment, that we were going to keep the kid, and that neither of us was going to say so out loud, because saying so would make it a decision, and decisions can be revoked.

"Name," I said.

"Juno."

"Last name?"

"Just Juno. Like the planet. Or the goddess. Whichever's less disappointing."

* * *

Here is what the first week looked like, and I report it honestly because the honest version is funnier than any polished version could be.

Sefu rewrote the flight plan twice and referred to my eleven years of solo piloting as "surviving, technically." Tam refused to come aboard at all until Sefu flew back to Pallas Drift, landed, and said, "Your 'until' is coming either way. At least be there when it does." Even then Tam boarded carrying her own tools and a sleeping bag, and announced she was "consulting, temporarily, under protest," a phrase she repeated for nine days until Sefu started answering it with "noted, permanently, shut up."

Juno appeared at meals as if by diffusion — never entering a room, simply becoming present in it — and ate like someone who had recently been hungry for a long time and expected it to resume. Nobody asked. Everybody noticed. Tam started leaving extra ration bars on the counter near where Juno tended to materialize, and if either of them acknowledged this, I didn't see it, and I suspect that was the entire design.

Me, I did what I do. I translated. Not languages — those I'm fine at. I translated *between*, the small collisions: explaining to Tam that Sefu's silence during approach wasn't disapproval but concentration; explaining to Sefu that Tam's refusal to eat in the cockpit wasn't distrust but a superstition about crumbs and gravity; explaining to Juno, when they finally asked, why Ilsa kept the archive drives wrapped in cloth.

"They were her mother's," I said. "The wrapping, I mean. The drives are just drives."

Juno considered this. "You wrap things people give you?"

"When they're heavy enough," I said, and the kid nodded like I'd said something profound instead of merely true.

The friction didn't vanish. Friction doesn't vanish; it gets load-bearing. On day five, Tam found unauthorized modifications to the transmitter housing and tore a strip off Sefu in front of everyone, and Sefu took it, and then that night I found them both in the engine room at 0300, not speaking, handing each other tools, fixing together the thing they'd fought about. On day seven, Juno got caught rerouting power to a hidden terminal — some private project, fiercely defended — and instead of blowing up, I sat down next to them and said, "Show me when you're ready. Not now. When." They didn't show me for another month. But they stopped hiding the terminal's glow.

On day eight, Sefu cooked. Actual food, from actual ingredients she'd traded half her back pay for at Ceres Verge without telling anyone. She called it "crew rations, upgraded," and deflected all thanks with military precision, but I watched her watch us eat, and I watched her face do the thing faces do when a person realizes a table can hold more than plates.

"This isn't a crew," I said to Ilsa that night, on the observation deck, watching the corridor stars crawl past. "It's a collection of people with nowhere better to be."

"Describe the difference," Ilsa said.

I opened my mouth. Closed it. Somewhere below us, Tam was arguing with the recycler, Sefu was arguing with Tam, and Juno was almost certainly listening to both arguments through the vents and taking notes for purposes unknown.

"There isn't one," I admitted. "That's the horrifying part."

* * *

On day ten, everything went wrong at once, which is how I knew it was starting to work.

An Ashen patrol pinged us at the corridor boundary — routine, supposedly, but routine had a new edge since Meridian-9. Sefu flew the response vector so smooth the patrol officer actually complimented us. Tam kept our transponder signature inside parameters by performing what she later described as "minor surgery on a lying machine, which, given this ship's mission, felt thematic." And Juno —

Juno sat in the cockpit the whole time, uninvited, unhidden, strapped into the second seat like they'd always belonged there, calling out readings in a clear steady voice before Sefu asked. Afterward, Sefu didn't order them out of the seat. She just said, "Good calls," and Juno glowed for six hours, and I pretended not to see, and failed.

That night I stood in the galley making tea — real tea, Sefu's contraband — and listened to the ship. The port thruster still groaned. The recycler still lied. But underneath it there were new voices now: Tam humming in the engine room, off-key, in what might have been Yoruba or might have been pure defiance. Sefu running simulation drills aloud, narrating to herself, and to whoever might be listening. Juno's footsteps, light and deliberate, learning the ship's rhythms the way I'd once learned dead tongues — by living inside them.

Eleven years I'd flown this route fluent in everything except belonging. Ten days, and the *Long Route* already sounded like a sentence with too many speakers, overlapping, interrupting, disagreeing about grammar.

I wouldn't have changed a word.

Which is, of course, exactly when the message came in — priority-flagged, fleet-encoded, addressed to the *Long Route* by name, though no one outside this crew should have known the name. I read it twice. Then I carried it to Ilsa, and watched the color leave her face.

"Rafael," she said quietly. "This isn't a fleet transmission. This is a reply. Someone answered us — in Vessic."
