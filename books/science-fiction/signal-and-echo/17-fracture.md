## 17. Fracture

**Ilsa**

I have been keeping secrets since before I could spell the word *classified*. My mother taught me that a lie, maintained properly, is a load-bearing wall. You don't tear it out to see what happens. You reinforce it. You paint over the cracks. You move into the house and you live there, and if the house is holding up two fleets' worth of peace, then the house is holy, whatever it's made of.

So when Rafael stood in the galley of the *Long Route* and said, "It's over. We end it," my answer came out before my conscience could file an objection.

"No."

He looked at me the way archivists look at a corrupted file. Like something precious had failed checksum.

"Ilsa—"

"Meridian-9 is ash because the Ashen thought we were close to exposure. Halvard knows the third power is fiction and wants it kept alive so he can inherit it. If we broadcast the truth now, on our schedule, with no plan, no protection—" I spread my hands. "We're not ending the lie. We're just choosing which version of the massacre gets our names on it."

Tam, at the engineering table, stopped pretending to fix a coolant coupling he'd already fixed twice. Sefu leaned against the bulkhead with his arms crossed, doing his statue impression. Juno sat in the corner with their knees up, watching us like it was a match they'd bet on.

"Two hundred thousand people," Rafael said quietly. "That's the current estimate for open war between Corvane and Ashen. First year alone."

"And how many die when both fleets learn simultaneously that everything they've feared for forty years was one woman's typing?" I heard my mother in my voice and hated it and used it anyway. "Panic isn't peace, Rafael. Humiliation isn't peace. You don't get to detonate a myth under two armed camps and call the rubble honesty."

"It's not rubble," he said. "It's ground."

"Ground is what you call rubble after enough time passes."

* * *

**Rafael**

Here is what nobody tells you about being fluent in dead languages: every one of them died the same way. Not all at once. Not in fire. They died the way Meridian-9's transmitter died — a component here, a speaker there, until one morning the signal goes out and nothing answers, and everyone agrees afterward that it had probably been over for years.

Vessic didn't die. That's what I understood in the dream, the dream I still haven't told them about fully. Its last speakers went silent *on purpose*. They refused to be weapons. They folded the language closed like a hand around something too valuable to be stolen, and they let the galaxy believe it was extinct rather than let it be conscripted.

And I have spent eleven months doing exactly what they died refusing to do. Composing threats in a grammar built for mourning. Writing ultimatums in a tongue whose word for *surrender* literally translates as *to set down your grief*. Every transmission I've sent as the Watcher — the phantom third power — has been a small desecration, and I told myself it was arithmetic. Two hundred thousand lives against the dignity of the dead. Any decent person does that math.

But the math changed at Meridian-9. The Ashen bombarded a station full of ghosts to erase evidence of a forgery, and Ilsa came out of it bleeding, and Tam hasn't slept right since, and somewhere in me a door I'd propped shut swung open: *the dead were right.* Silence was their answer. It should have been mine.

So I said it in the galley, with all of them listening, because a thing like this can't be said privately. A fracture announced in private is a secret. Announced in front of your family, it's a choice.

"We end it," I said. "The truth, in every language I know, broadcast wide. Both fleets. At once. No warning."

"You'll start the war," Ilsa said.

"No. Halvard will try to start the war, and he'll fail, because half the people in both fleets are already lying." I looked at Sefu. "You've seen it. The Corvane crews who cross themselves in Old Meridi when they think no one's listening. The Ashen gunners who hum lullabies in dialects their officers banned."

Sefu's jaw worked. He didn't deny it. That was its own kind of answer.

"The lie only works," I said, "because the truth is already underneath it, alive, waiting. I'm not detonating anything. I'm turning the lights on in a house full of people who were told they were the last ones awake."

* * *

**Sefu**

I have flown for three navies and zero flags, which means I have developed an extremely refined sense for when a room is about to come apart. This room was coming apart. You could hear it in the way Rafael and Ilsa had stopped hearing each other — both of them talking past the other like two transmitters on the same frequency, guaranteed interference.

What surprised me was where I landed.

"I'm with Rafael."

Ilsa turned on me. "Sefu—"

"Hear me out before you archive me." I held up a hand. "I boarded ships for Corvane for six years. I've seen what these people do when they believe someone bigger is watching. They behave. And I've also seen what happens the one time a patrol found out the watcher wasn't real — deck officer named Vashti, broke down in the corridor, started telling everyone the war was coming back. You know what her own crew did? Reported her. Not because she was wrong. Because they couldn't afford for her to be right."

"That proves my point," Ilsa said.

"It proves my point," I said. "They couldn't afford it. That's not peace, that's a held breath. And I've been holding this one for six years and my chest hurts." I looked at Rafael. "But I want it on record that I think you're wrong about how this goes. I think when the lights come on, some people shoot first and grieve later. I'm with you because somebody has to fly the getaway route either way, and I'd rather fly it for the truth than for the lie."

"Charming," Ilsa said. "The truth, with a flight plan."

"Everything human comes with a flight plan. That's what makes it different from a principle."

* * *

**Tam**

I fixed the coolant coupling again while they argued, because my hands need work the way other people's mouths need words, and because if I stopped moving I was going to say something unforgivable.

I said it anyway.

"My sister was stationed at Meridian-9."

The galley went quiet the way a reactor goes quiet — not calm, just holding everything.

"She rotated off six weeks before the bombardment. Six weeks. I did that math every day since, and I keep getting the same answer, which is that she lived because of scheduling and died-in-advance anyway, because forty-one of her crewmates didn't rotate off." I set down the wrench. "Ilsa, I understand load-bearing walls. I'm an engineer. But I also know what happens when you keep reinforcing a structure past its rated tolerance. It doesn't hold forever. It holds until it fails catastrophically, and it takes everyone standing inside it with it."

"So you want to bring it down on purpose."

"I want to evacuate before it comes down on its own. Because it will. The transmitter's dying — I told you that, I showed you the decay curves. Weeks, not months. We don't get to choose whether the lie ends. We only get to choose whether it ends as truth or as collapse." I looked around the table. "Halvard already knows. The Ashen already shelled a station. The failure has begun. Rafael's not proposing to break the peace. He's proposing to admit the peace is already broken and stop pretending otherwise."

Ilsa sat very still. She had the archivist's gift for stillness — you could hide whole decades inside it.

"And if you're wrong," she said finally, "and the fleets open fire the moment the myth dies?"

"Then at least they'll be shooting at each other instead of at ghosts," Tam said, and heard his own voice crack, and hated it. "At least they'll know who they're grieving for."

* * *

**Juno**

Everyone forgot I was in the corner, which is standard, and honestly it's the best seat in any argument. You get the whole play and none of the blame.

Except this time I didn't want the seat. This time I wanted to be in it.

Because here's the thing nobody else knew, the thing I'd been carrying since the night I played back Rafael's transmissions and found the name — *Selu*, child, daughter — threaded through threats like a hair through cloth. Rafael had been speaking to his dead daughter in every message he sent as the Watcher. Grief dressed up as strategy. And I'd said nothing, because it was his, because some things you overhear are yours to carry quietly.

But now the whole family was splitting along a line, and I realized the line wasn't really about fleets or myths or load-bearing walls. The line was about what you do with a grief you've been feeding instead of burying. Ilsa fed hers by maintaining it — her mother's lie, her mother's silence, kept alive like a shrine. Rafael wanted to bury his the only way left: by saying it out loud, in every tongue, to everyone, until it belonged to the living again.

"I'm with Rafael," I said.

Four heads turned. Rude of me, honestly, to exist so loudly.

"Kid," Sefu started.

"I recorded his transmissions. Months ago. I know about Selu." I watched Rafael go still — the archivist stillness, he'd caught it from Ilsa, they infect each other. "I'm sorry. I should have said. But I'm saying it now because it matters: he's not trying to save the galaxy. He's trying to stop hiding. And I know what that's worth, because I hid in a cargo pod for nine days and it nearly killed me, and walking out was the bravest thing I ever did, and nobody shot me." I shrugged, aware suddenly that I was fourteen and my voice was doing something embarrassing. "Most people, when the lights come on, they just... blink. And then they look around. And then they find each other. That's what happened to us, isn't it? None of us were supposed to find each other. We were all stowaways in our own lives."

Silence. The good kind, mostly.

Then Ilsa stood, and the good kind ended.

* * *

**Ilsa**

A child lectured me about courage in my own galley — well, Rafael's galley, nothing aboard this ship is mine except the guilt — and the worst part is that the child was right, and I knew it, and knowing it made me dig in harder, because that is the family inheritance too. My mother dug in. Her mother dug in. Forty years of women planting their feet in front of a collapsing wall and calling it duty.

"Fine," I said. "Count the votes. Rafael, Sefu, Tam, Juno. Four against one." I laughed, and it came out wrong, brittle as old tape. "You know what my mother would say? She'd say: majority rule is how archives burn. One custodian, one conscience, that's the design. That's why the lie survived — because it never needed permission."

"Maybe that's why it has to die," Rafael said softly. "Nothing that needs one person's silence to survive deserves to."

I looked at him — this man I blackmailed into partnership, who became the closest thing to a colleague I've had since my mother died, who dreams in languages I can barely catalog — and I felt the fracture go all the way through, clean as a break in a transmission. Love doesn't prevent this. Love is what makes it hurt, which is different, which is worse.

"If you do this," I said, "without a plan, without protection, without me — you won't just lose the lie. You'll lose the timing. Halvard will control the narrative. He'll spin the truth into proof of conspiracy, his conspiracy, and he'll be *right*, because you handed him the microphone."

"Then help us time it," Rafael said. "Help us do it right. You know the fleets' listening schedules better than anyone alive. You built the lie — you're the only one who knows exactly where its seams are."

And there it was. The offer. The trap. The door.

I stood in it for a long moment, four faces looking at me — the pilot who'd chosen truth with a flight plan, the engineer grieving a station, the kid who'd confessed to spying because honesty mattered more than safety, and Rafael, who had learned to speak to the dead and was now asking me to help him speak for them.

My mother kept the lie for forty years and died alone in an archive with no one to tell.

"No," I said, and picked up my coat. "Not 'no, I'll help.' No. I'm going to Corvane space. Halvard wants the lie kept alive — fine. I'll keep it alive myself, my way, with a custodian who actually understands what she's carrying. And when your lights-on moment comes, Rafael, and it burns the way I think it will — I will not be standing next to you. I'll be standing over there, in the dark, where at least I'll be able to say I saw it coming."

Nobody moved to stop me. That was the cruelest part. They loved me enough to let me go, which is either the best thing this ragged little family ever did or the worst, and I genuinely could not tell you which.

At the airlock, Juno caught up with me. I braced for a speech.

Instead the kid pressed something into my hand: a data chip. "Backup of everything. All of Rafael's transmissions, the decay curves, the whole archive. In case you're right," Juno said. "Somebody should keep the receipts either way."

I looked at the chip. Forty years of my family's sin, duplicated by a teenager in a borrowed jacket.

"You're going to be dangerous someday," I told them.

"I'm already dangerous," Juno said. "I'm just polite about it."

The airlock cycled. The *Long Route* fell away behind me, and I watched it shrink toward the deep relay lanes, carrying my family toward a broadcast I believed would kill them, carrying my mother's ghost away from me at last — and I sat down in the shuttle and did the only thing Ashgroves and Vosses have ever known how to do.

I started planning.

Three days later, in a Corvane listening post disguised as a freight office, I received a burst transmission on an old diplomatic band. Unencrypted. Deliberately, extravagantly unencrypted, because it was meant to be intercepted by everyone.

It was in Vessic. My Vessic parsers were good enough to catch the shape of it even where the meaning slipped: a date, a frequency, and a single repeated phrase that Rafael must have known I'd run through every dictionary I owned before I understood it.

*Come stand in the light with us. There is room.*

He wasn't threatening me. He wasn't begging. He was inviting me to the funeral of the only thing I'd ever been faithful to — and he'd timed the send so that Halvard's people would intercept it too.

Which meant the countdown hadn't started when we voted.

It had started the moment I walked out the airlock.
