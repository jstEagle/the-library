## 18. The Broadcast

They came for us at the edge of the Kestrel Drift, where the relay buoys thin out and even honest ships start to feel like rumors of themselves.

I was asleep when it happened — actually asleep, which should have been my first warning that the universe had decided to be cruel in an efficient way. I woke to Tam's voice on the intercom saying "Rafael, you need to see this" in the particular flat tone he reserves for hull breaches and other inconveniences that are technically survivable.

The *Long Answer* had company. Six Corvane warships in a formation so tight they looked like a single animal with six opinions. And riding our comm channel, uninvited and unmistakable, was the voice of Admiral Renn Halvard.

"Captain Halloran," he said, though I'd never been a captain of anything except my own bad decisions. "You've been a difficult man to find. I admire that. It suggests you understand what finding means."

Sefu was already in the pilot's seat with her hands hovering over the controls like a surgeon deciding whether the patient deserved saving. Juno stood in the corridor behind me, and I could feel them not asking questions, which is a skill most adults never learn.

"He's got tractor lock," Sefu said. "Has had it for — I don't know. Maybe an hour. He let us fly into it."

"Why?" I asked, though I already knew. You always know why, afterward. The why is just the story you tell yourself about the trap you walked into with your eyes open because the alternative was worse.

"Because he doesn't want us dead," Sefu said. "He wants us *useful*. There's a difference, and it's the kind of difference that gets people killed slower."

Halvard's voice came back, patient as erosion. "I'm going to board you now, Captain. Please don't make my marines feel unwelcome. They're very sensitive about hospitality."

* * *

They came aboard at 0400 ship time, boots ringing on our deck plates like a metronome counting down something. Twelve marines who moved like they'd rehearsed this boarding on a schematic of our exact ship, which meant someone had sold them the schematics, which meant I'd have to have a conversation with Tam later about which of his black-market suppliers hated us.

Halvard came last. In person he was smaller than his voice, which is true of most powerful men and all of their empires. Silver at the temples, a face built for official portraits, and eyes that did the arithmetic of every room he entered and always arrived at himself.

"You're shorter than your transmissions," he told me.

"You're exactly as tall as your reputation," I said. "Which is disappointing. I expected ambition to add a few centimeters."

Something flickered across his face — amusement, maybe, or the memory of it. "Ilsa Voss is in my brig on the *Vigilant*. She's fine. Comfortable, even. She asked me to tell you that she's sorry, and that she told you so, and that if you're reading anything into the order of those two statements, you're overthinking it."

"That sounds like Ilsa."

"She also told me you'd never cooperate." He walked our corridor slowly, trailing marines, looking at things — Juno's contraband shelf of salvaged language-cards, Tam's wall of hand-labeled fuses, the galley table where we'd eaten together maybe four hundred times without once calling it a family. "She said you'd rather burn the whole fiction down than keep it running under new management. Was she right?"

I thought about lying to him. It occurred to me, with a clarity that felt almost like grace, that I had become genuinely good at lying — that eight months of composing threats in a language built for mourning had honed something in me that I wasn't sure I wanted to look at directly. But there was no percentage in lying to a man who already owned the answer.

"Yes," I said.

"Good." He smiled, and it was the worst thing I'd seen all year, because it was sincere. "Then we understand each other. I don't need your cooperation, Captain. I need your *voice*. The forgery continues tonight, from this ship, under my authority. You'll transmit exactly what I give you, in exactly the Vessic you alone can produce. The Corvane fleet will believe the third power has sanctioned my command. The Ashen will believe the same. And in eighteen months, when both fleets have bled each other gray, I'll stand up the peace on the ruins, and no one will ever know the god they feared was a dead language wearing a uniform."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I put a bullet through your transmitter's primary coupling, your engineer's kneecap, and your pilot's career, in whatever order amuses me, and I find another way. There are only eleven hundred fluent Vessic speakers left in the sector, Captain, and I've spent two years locating them. Do you know how many refused?"

"How many?"

"All of them." He said it lightly, like a man describing weather. "Farmers, monks, one very stubborn archivist's grandmother. Every single one chose silence over service. It's the great mystery of dead languages — the people who keep them alive are always the people least willing to use them." He turned to face me fully. "You're the last variable. So. Will you transmit?"

I looked past him, down the corridor. Juno stood at the junction, arms crossed, watching me with an expression I couldn't parse — fear, yes, but underneath it something else. Trust, maybe. Or the specific terror of a kid who has finally found people worth being afraid for.

And I thought about Vessic. Not the forgery — the language itself. The way its grammar curved toward grief the way water curves toward a drain. The way its word for *speak* and its word for *mourn* shared a root, because to the Vessic, telling someone something true was itself a small act of mourning: you gave up a piece of your silence and it didn't grow back.

Its last speakers had gone silent on purpose. Refused to be weapons. I'd understood that in a dream, months ago, and I'd been carrying it around like a stone in my shoe ever since, waiting to find out what it was for.

Now I knew.

"I'll transmit," I said.

Halvard's smile widened. "Wise."

"Not for the reason you think," I said, and walked toward the comm room, and nobody stopped me, because I was doing exactly what they wanted, and that is the oldest camouflage in the universe.

* * *

The comm room smelled like burnt dust and old coffee, which is to say it smelled like home. Tam was already strapped into the engineering couch, a marine's rifle barrel resting against the armrest near his shoulder, and he looked up at me with a question in his face he was too smart to ask out loud.

I sat down at the transmitter console. My console. Eight months of forged threats had been born here, in flawless Vessic, addressed always to a name that meant *child*, because grief was the only register the language trusted and I had grief to spare.

Halvard stood behind me with a slate in his hand. On it: the transmission. Sanction of the third power. Confirmation of his authority. A threat dressed in mourning clothes, aimed at forty thousand ships.

"Read it," he said. "In your voice. Make them believe."

I pulled up the transmitter. Opened the channel wide — not fleet-band, not Corvane encryption, but everything. Every open frequency in the sector, every relay buoy still listening, every silent colony that had stopped transmitting years ago and left its receivers running anyway, the way you leave a porch light on for someone who isn't coming home.

"Channel's open," I said. "Wide-open. Is that a problem?"

"It's efficient," Halvard said. "Both fleets will hear it at once. Begin."

I looked at the words on his slate. *By sanction of the Watchers, the Corvane armada is granted dominion.* Good Vessic. My Vessic. I'd taught myself to write it so well that sometimes, late at night, I forgot I was the author and flinched at my own sentences.

I began to speak.

But not his words.

I spoke the opening phrase he'd scripted — the invocation, the one every forged transmission had started with, the formal address to the powers that watched — and then, instead of continuing, I let it fall. Let the silence sit in the channel for three full seconds, which in broadcast terms is a geological age, long enough for every listener in the sector to lean forward.

And then I said, in Vessic, the truth:

*"There is no third power. There never was. What watches you is a lie kept by tired people, and the lie is dying, and what comes after it is yours to choose."*

Behind me, Halvard went very still. "Halloran—"

I kept talking. Faster now, because I could hear him moving, because I had perhaps thirty seconds before someone decided bullets were cheaper than patience, and I had a lot of languages to get through.

I switched to Ashen Old Tongue — the liturgical register, the one the Ashen fleets still used for funerals without understanding half of it. *"Your ancestors made peace once, with words instead of ships. The words are still here. I am speaking them."*

A marine grabbed my shoulder. I shrugged him off with my left hand and kept my right on the transmit key, and switched again — to Meridian Trade Cant, dead sixty years, the pidgin that had once stitched a hundred colonies together before the borders hardened. *"You were one people once. This is what it sounded like."*

Someone cut power to the console. The screen died. But Tam — bless him, brilliant, impossible Tam — had spent three months rewiring this room against exactly this day, because Tam believed in redundancy the way other men believed in gods, and the transmitter ran on its own buried line, and it stayed hot.

I couldn't see the frequencies anymore. Didn't matter. I knew them by heart.

Kessarin next, the whistled trade-language of the outer belt, extinct since the Blockade. Then Old Corvane, the founding dialect, the one their own admirals needed scholars to read on their own monuments. Then Vessic again, because Vessic was mine, because I owed it this.

*"Listen. I am Rafael Halloran. I have spent my life learning languages no one speaks anymore, and I am telling you what they all say, which is the same thing: silence was a choice, and you can choose differently."*

The door banged open. Boots. Halvard's voice, no longer smooth, no longer reasonable: "Shut him UP—"

Juno got there first.

I heard it before I saw it — the kid wedging themself into the doorway, arms spread, fifteen years old and ninety pounds of absolute refusal, screaming at four armed marines that they'd have to go through a minor, which would look *terrible* in the report. It bought me nine seconds. It might have been the bravest nine seconds I ever witnessed, and I have listened to the black-box recordings of doomed ships for a living.

In those nine seconds, I did the last thing. The thing I'd been building toward without admitting it, the transmission I'd been drafting in my head for months, addressed always to a name that meant *child*.

I said her name. Mira. My daughter's name, in Vessic, on an open channel, in front of two fleets and an admiral and a boy holding a doorway with his body.

*"Mira. If anyone hearing this has lost someone — a child, a parent, a whole language — then you know what I'm saying isn't strategy. It's just what's left when everything else is taken. I lied to keep a peace. The lie is over. The peace doesn't have to be. Speak. Whoever you are, whatever tongue you kept alive in secret, in cellars and kitchens and funeral rites — speak now. I'll hear you. We'll hear each other. That's all a civilization ever was."*

Hands pulled me out of the chair. The channel stayed open — I'd locked the key, and Tam's wiring laughed at their cutoff attempts, and somewhere in the guts of the ship a little buried line kept singing into the dark.

They got me against the wall. Halvard stood over me, breathing hard, and for one moment the portrait-face cracked and I saw underneath it, which was just a man watching his life's architecture come down in real time.

"You've killed the peace," he said.

"No," I said. "I've killed the lie. Whether there's a peace is up to them now. It always was. That's what you never understood — you thought the silence between the fleets was something you could own. It was never yours. It was theirs. All along, it was theirs."

"They won't answer," he said. "Dead languages have no speakers. That's what dead *means*."

And I looked at him — really looked, the way you look at someone you're about to watch be wrong about the most important thing in the world — and I said:

"Admiral. I have spent thirty years listening to the dark. Do you know what I've learned? Nobody ever goes truly silent. They just stop using the frequency you're listening on."

The ship shuddered — tractor lock releasing, or weapons arming, I couldn't tell. Sefu's voice came over the intercom, ragged and fast: "Rafael, whatever you did, something's happening out here. The Corvane picket is — they're transmitting. Someone on the lead destroyer is transmitting, and it's not fleet code, it's—"

She stopped. And then, over the open channel, crackling through our own speakers, faint and distorted and absolutely unmistakable, came a voice.

Whistled. Kessarin. The outer-belt trade-tongue, dead sixty years, answering.

Then another voice, deeper, in Old Corvane. Then a woman's voice in Meridian Cant. Then another, and another, a chorus assembling itself out of the static like a city turning its lights on one window at a time — farmers and monks and archivists' grandmothers, everyone who had ever chosen silence over service, choosing, all at once, to speak.

Halvard stared at the speakers. His slate slipped from his hand and hit the deck.

"What did you do?" he whispered.

"Nothing," I said, and I was crying, and I didn't care who saw. "I just asked. Turns out that was the one weapon nobody thought to forge."
