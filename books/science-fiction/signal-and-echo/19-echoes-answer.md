## 19. Echoes Answer

### Juno

I want it on the record that when Rafael started broadcasting, my first thought was not *history is being made*. My first thought was *oh no, we are all going to die, and my last words to Tam were about the state of the galley*.

Which, in fairness, was a fair thing to have last words about. The galley looked like a crime scene where the victim was a protein rehydrator.

Here's what happened, as best as I can piece together from the bridge log and from the way everyone's faces looked afterward, which is its own kind of record:

Halvard had Rafael in a transmission chair with two marines behind him and a gun that didn't need to be fired to be persuasive. Halvard wanted the forgery. The lie, sustained, polished, deployed under his signature instead of ours. And Rafael — this man who hadn't spoken more than ten consecutive human words to me in the whole time I'd known him, who said goodnight in Vessic like other people say prayers — Rafael opened his mouth and gave him something else entirely.

He started with Vessic. Of course he did. It's his first language now, whether he admits it or not. But then he did something I've only seen described in archive fragments: he shifted registers mid-sentence, the way you'd change keys in a song, and came out the other side speaking Ashkari. Then Kel-Thenne. Then a language so old the file headers just called it *pre-contact coastal*, which is archivist-speak for "we don't know and we're embarrassed."

The truth, in every dead tongue at once.

And here's the part nobody expected, least of all me: the dead tongues answered back.

* * *

### Sefu

I was on the comm board when the first reply came through, so technically I heard history before anyone else, which I intend to mention at every dinner party for the rest of my life.

It wasn't much, at first. A burst of static with structure in it — and I don't mean signal structure, I mean *grammar*. You spend enough years around Rafael, you learn to hear grammar the way sailors hear weather. This had the cadence of someone choosing their words carefully. Someone awake. Someone scared.

"Corvane destroyer *Vigilant*, channel nine," I said, because my hands knew the protocol even while my brain was screaming. "You're receiving a broadcast on a restricted—"

"It's not restricted," said a voice, and it took me a second to place the language, and then another second to believe I'd placed it, because the voice was speaking Kel-Thenne, and Kel-Thenne has been extinct since before my grandmother's grandmother, and this woman spoke it the way you speak your mother's cooking — casually, lovingly, without thinking.

"We kept it," she said, in accented Standard now, as if translating for my benefit. "My family. Four generations of pretending we couldn't. Do you know what that's like? To hold a whole language in your mouth and never let it out?"

I looked across the bridge at Rafael, still in the chair, marines forgotten, eyes closed, listening the way other people pray.

"Yeah," I said. "I think I'm starting to."

* * *

### Tam

Engineering perspective, since apparently I'm the one who notices when the ship itself has opinions.

When Rafael went off-script, the transmitter load spiked past anything the forgery rig was rated for. I watched the thermal numbers climb and did the math on how long we had before we cooked the array, and the math said eleven minutes, and I said a word Tam Okafor-Reyes is not supposed to say on an open channel, and then I did something stupid.

I rerouted auxiliary power. All of it. Life support margins down to minimums, gravity in the aft sections flickering, everything into keeping that broadcast alive. Because here's what I understood before anyone said it out loud: whatever Rafael was doing, it wasn't a transmission anymore. It was a *roll call*. And you do not cut power during roll call. Not when people are answering.

And they were answering. By minute four we had responses on six channels. By minute eight, fourteen. Corvane ships, Ashen ships, ships whose registry codes predated both fleets. Little pockets of the silenced, coming up out of the dark one by one, each of them clearly terrified, each of them doing it anyway.

My grandmother used to hum songs in a language she never taught my mother, saying there was no point, nobody left to talk to. I found the recording of her humming after all this was over. I ran it through the translator core just to hear it rendered.

She was singing in Ashkari. She'd been singing in Ashkari my whole childhood, right there in the kitchen, and I never knew.

I don't cry at machinery. But I sat in the engineering bay for a long time after that, holding a recording of a dead language that turned out to be alive the entire time, and the ship hummed around me in frequencies I chose to interpret as sympathy.

* * *

### Ilsa

Forty years. That's how long my mother maintained the forgery before she handed it to me, and four more years on top of that for me, and in all that time I told myself the same story she told herself: *the lie is a wall, and walls keep people safe.*

Rafael's broadcast knocked that wall down in nineteen minutes, and what I learned watching it fall is that walls also keep people apart. Which is, I suppose, the point of them.

The fleets' command channels lit up like a festival. Not with panic — with something worse, from Halvard's perspective. With *recognition*. Ship after ship acknowledging the broadcast in languages their own crews had been forbidden to log. There was an Ashen corvette whose navigator answered in Vessic — actual Vessic, fluent, unbroken — and I watched the fleet's internal traffic stutter around her transmission like water around a stone, because half the officers listening understood her and the other half suddenly realized they'd been serving alongside people they'd never truly heard.

That's the thing about a myth like the third power. It doesn't just enforce peace. It enforces silence. Everyone who knew the truth had to be quiet; everyone who suspected had to pretend; and the pretense spread outward until entire cultures went underground inside their own militaries. Halvard thought he was inheriting a weapon. He was inheriting a graveyard, and graveyards, it turns out, are full of people who can still talk.

By the time the broadcast hit twenty minutes, three Corvane captains had formally requested verification of the third-power mandate. That sounds bureaucratic. It was a mutiny with paperwork. When no verification came — because there was nothing to verify, there never had been — the requests became declarations, and the declarations became a council, and the council convened on an open channel that Halvard could listen to but could not control.

I stood next to Rafael's chair while it happened. At some point I realized I was gripping his shoulder hard enough to bruise, and neither of us mentioned it, and I am mentioning it now only because it was the first time in nine years I had touched another person without calculating what it would cost.

* * *

### Halvard

They will write that I raged. Let the record show otherwise. I listened.

I had built my career on the premise that people are governed by fear, and fear requires an object, and I had intended to be the object's custodian. What I failed to model — what none of us modeled — was that the fear had been hollow for decades. The third power was a scarecrow, and scarecrows work only as long as the crows don't land on them. Rafael Halloran landed a fleet of crows on mine, in languages I could not parse, and the crows turned out to number in the thousands, embedded in my own chain of command, patient as seeds.

Admiral Osei called for the vote at 0400 fleet time. Verification of the third-power mandate: denied. Continuation of hostilities posture: suspended. My authority, pending investigation: revoked, pending investigation, which is the politest way anyone has ever taken something from me.

I gave the order to seize the *Long Route*'s crew one last time, and my own communications officer looked at me with an expression I recognized, because I had worn it myself for thirty years, and said, in Kel-Thenne, "No, sir," and then repeated it in Standard so there would be no ambiguity.

There is a particular solitude in being the last man to believe a lie. I recommend avoiding it.

* * *

### Juno

Afterward — "afterward" meaning about forty minutes later, because history moves fast when it finally gets moving — the replies started including names.

Not ship designations. Names. People signing their transmissions the way you sign a letter. *This is Maret, of the third watch, my grandmother was born on the coast before the Silence.* *This is Deniz-Kai, I teach my daughter in secret, she is seven.* One transmission was just a lullaby, sung badly, by someone who was clearly crying, and nobody on any channel asked them to identify themselves because everybody already understood.

Rafael answered every single one. In their own language, every time. His voice got rougher with each reply but he didn't stop, and Tam kept the power flowing, and Sefu kept the channels open, and Ilsa stood there like a woman watching a debt get repaid that she'd spent her whole life believing was hers alone to carry.

I recorded all of it. Obviously. Old habits.

But near the end, when the channels were starting to quiet down — not empty, never empty again, just resting — Rafael turned in the chair and looked directly at the recorder in my hand, and instead of being angry, he held it up closer to his mouth and said something in Vessic, slow, deliberate, addressed to the recording itself.

Isla translated for me afterward, her voice not entirely steady: *"To whoever keeps the records: thank you for hearing us. We are not gone. Write that down."*

So I wrote it down. I'm writing it down right now.

* * *

### Sefu

The armistice request came in at dawn, ship-time, from both fleets simultaneously, which I'm told has literally never happened before, and they asked — formally, officially, with the full weight of two governments behind it — for the translator aboard the vessel *Signal and Echo* to mediate preliminary talks.

Rafael read the message twice. Then he looked around the bridge at the four of us: the disgraced archivist, the washed-out pilot, the engineer with burnt fingers and wet eyes, the teenage stowaway holding a recorder like a torch.

"They're asking me to sit in a room with both fleets," he said, "and translate."

"You're the only one who speaks enough of the languages," Ilsa said.

"That's not why they're asking." He almost smiled. "They're asking because I lied to them beautifully for months, and then told them the truth in front of everyone, and survived. Apparently that's the job description now."

"So do we?" Juno asked. "Go?"

Rafael looked at the comm board, where dozens of channels still glowed softly, each one a small fire somebody had kept burning in secret for generations. Then he looked at us, longer, the way he does when he's translating something that doesn't have words yet.

"We go together," he said. "All of us. If I'm walking into that room, I'm bringing my family."

And Juno — who has never once followed an instruction in their life — said "yes sir" in perfect Vessic, and the whole bridge laughed, and somewhere out in the dark, on channels we would keep open from that day forward, people began answering.
