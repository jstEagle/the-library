## 10. The Purge Signature

The fragment lived in the one place on Terminal Nine nobody would ever think to look: inside the station's own bones.

I'd spent six hours crawling the dorsal spine with a signal sniffer and a headlamp, following a ghost of archived traffic I'd pulled from the Kestrel-night logs. The purge order hadn't come from outside. It had been executed locally — which meant it had been stored locally, at least once, in whatever buffer handled high-priority command traffic. And Terminal Nine's buffers weren't software. They were physical. Crystalline memory lattices bonded into the relay housings themselves, written by heat, erased by heat, and never — not once in forty years of service — fully wiped. The engineers who built this place had trusted entropy to do their housekeeping for them.

Entropy was sloppy. I was counting on it.

* * *

I found it in Relay Four's primary housing, wedged between two decades of routine telemetry like a razor blade in a drawer of spoons.

My sniffer flagged it first as an anomaly: a data block with Colonial Intelligence encryption headers, timestamped the night of the Kestrel Incident, physically written into the lattice at 0347 station time. I sat cross-legged on the maintenance gantry with my heart going hard against my ribs and ran the decryption keys I still carried from my service years — keys I'd told myself were souvenirs, dead weight, nothing.

They opened it like it had been waiting.

The fragment was small. A backup stub, maybe four percent of a complete file, the kind of redundant copy a system writes before executing a destructive operation so that someone, somewhere, can verify the job was done. It contained no testimony, no video, none of the raw record of what happened over Kestrel. What it contained was worse.

It contained me.

An authorization signature: VILALOBOS, X., TECHNICIAN FIRST CLASS, COLONIAL SIGNALS INTELLIGENCE, CLEARANCE OBSIDIAN-3. A biometric hash that matched my hands, my retinas, the particular rhythm of my keystrokes that no forger could fake because they'd have needed my nervous system to try. And beneath the signature, the operation summary, rendered in the flat bureaucratic language that makes atrocity sound like plumbing:

*Full-spectrum memory suppression authorized. Target: self. Scope: 11.4 hours contiguous, plus associated contextual indexing. Method: transcranial lattice overwrite, field-deployable unit. Post-procedure cover identity implanted per protocol ASH-9.*

Target: self.

I read it eleven times. The words didn't rearrange themselves into anything more survivable. I had signed the order to erase my own memory of the Kestrel Incident. Not been forced. Not tricked. Signed — with the steady hand of a technician who understood exactly what the machine did, because she'd operated it on other people first.

* * *

I don't know how long I stayed on the gantry. Long enough for the station's rotation to carry Relay Four out of the sun and leave me working by headlamp in a cold that crept through my suit liner like a slow confession.

Here is what I remembered of myself, the version I'd been living inside for twelve years: a signals tech who'd mustered out of Colonial service after a routine tour, taken a contract on the long route because she liked the quiet, kept a private log of silent worlds because silence felt like something worth tending. A woman with a gap where a war should have been. I'd noticed the gap, of course. You notice a missing tooth. But I'd built a life around the tongue's new shape, and after a while I stopped probing it, the way you stop probing a scar because touching it only reminds you the nerves underneath are gone.

Now here was the tooth, in my palm, with my own signature on the extraction order.

Protocol ASH-9. I knew that protocol. That was the obscenity of it — I didn't have to look it up. It surfaced whole, unbidden, the way your own phone number surfaces: *implanted cover identity, low-observability posting, periodic remote psychological assessment.* I had helped design pieces of it. In my other life — the one I couldn't remember — I had been the kind of instrument that didn't just record the erasure of inconvenient witnesses. It performed them. On itself, when required. Because a witness who genuinely remembers nothing passes every scan, every interrogation, every deep-probe audit that a border station or a tribunal might run. The best cover story is one the cover doesn't know is a story.

And Terminal Nine wasn't a posting. It was a shelf. Someone had placed me here — quiet, loyal, oblivious — the way you place a loaded weapon in a drawer near the door. Twelve years I'd thought I chose the long route. Twelve years I'd thought the loneliness was mine.

* * *

I came back to myself somewhere around shift change, which on Terminal Nine means nothing except that the coffee ration light blinks and I've learned to obey it. I climbed down from the spine on legs that felt borrowed, stowed the fragment on three separate media in three separate places, and walked the long corridor toward the Meridian's berth with my reflection keeping pace in the dark viewports.

There's a specific quality to rage that has nowhere to go. It isn't hot. Heat needs a target, and every target I reached for dissolved. Colonial Intelligence? An acronym, a building I couldn't picture, handlers whose faces were as erased as my own memories. Myself? I could hate her — the woman who signed — but hating her was like hating a stranger wearing my face, and worse, some traitorous part of me kept noting that she'd done it under orders, under duress, under whatever pressure eleven hours of history had applied before she pressed the stud. The rage circled the compartment looking for a door and found only mirrors.

So I did what I always do with energy that has no destination. I put it into work.

* * *

Sef was awake when I came aboard the Meridian. Of course she was. Children sleep when their bodies demand it; Sef's body was six years old and her mind treated sleep like a scheduling problem. She sat cross-legged on the deck outside the med bay, wrapped in an adult-sized thermal blanket with the sleeves rolled eight times, running a diagnostic slate across her knees. Around her, through the open hatch, I could hear the low murmur of the others — the reversed crew arguing again, in the tired cadence of people who've been arguing for days about whether surrender is safety or suicide.

"You found something." She said it without looking up. Thirty-year-old perception in a face still soft with childhood; it never stopped being disorienting, like watching a lock speak.

"I found something."

"Bad something."

"The worst kind." I lowered myself to the deck beside her. My knees complained. Everything complained. "The kind that's true."

She set the slate down and gave me her full attention, and I understood why her faction had chosen her for this run. Some people listen the way most people talk — already composing their reply. Sef listened like a technician reading a waveform, taking the whole shape of it before deciding what mattered.

I told her. All of it. The signature, the protocol designation, the phrase *target: self*. I watched her face while I talked and saw the horror arrive in stages — first the tactical implication (their courier plan had landed on a station whose keeper was a Colonial intelligence asset, however dormant), then the human one, which took longer and landed harder.

"They used you," she said finally. "As a hiding place."

"As a hiding place, a tripwire, and probably a dead man's switch. I don't know yet what my standing orders are, because I can't remember having any. That's rather the point of me."

"And now you do remember. Partly."

"Now I remember *about* remembering. Which is different." I turned the black cylinder over in my hands — Sef's cargo, the message that had dragged all of us into this spiral. "Your people aged you backward to smuggle truth past the censors. Mine filed me away to make sure the lie stayed smooth. We're both cargo, Sef. We've just been shipped by different couriers."

Something moved behind her eyes — the old woman's eyes in the child's face — something that looked almost like recognition, and almost like grief, and settled into neither.

"There's a word for what you are," she said quietly. "In the faction. We called them *sealed instruments*. People who'd been closed like envelopes so the contents couldn't be read until the right moment. There were rumors of them. Nobody ever met one." She paused. "Nobody ever met one who knew what they were."

"Well. Now you have."

"Does that make it better or worse?"

I laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "Ask me tomorrow."

* * *

I worked through the night cycle because work was the only container big enough to hold what I was feeling without spilling. I mapped the ghost route — the chain of dead relays stretching spinward toward Terminal Eight, each one needing its wake sequence hand-fed through interfaces that hadn't spoken to a living pilot in a decade. I pre-staged power transfers. I wrote and discarded four versions of a message to Okafor's people at Eight, unable to decide how much of myself to disclose to strangers whose trust I hadn't earned.

And somewhere around the fourth hour, alone in the comm vault with the station humming its old hum around me, I stopped running and let the thing catch me.

Because here was the arithmetic I'd been avoiding. Every world that stopped answering on my private log — every silent port, every route abandoned mid-war — I had logged them with the diligence of a woman who believed her attention was innocent. But my attention had been manufactured. My solitude was a deployment. If Colonial Intelligence had wanted a listening post on the long route, they had also wanted a listener who would never wonder why she was listening, never report to anyone she remembered, never be turned, bought, or broken — because there was no *her* left to turn. They hadn't erased a witness. They'd built a fixture.

And fixtures don't get to be furious. Fixtures get repurposed.

Except.

Except that the woman who signed the purge order had signed it *before* the erasure. Which meant that for eleven point four hours, there had been a version of me who knew everything — who had seen the Kestrel record with her own cleared eyes, who had understood exactly what was being buried and why, and who had nevertheless picked up the field unit and aimed it at her own skull. What had she seen that made erasure feel like the right call? Fear? Coercion? Or something colder — the technician's logic that a sealed instrument serves the mission better than a dead one?

I would never know unless I got the rest of the file. And the rest of the file existed in exactly two places: Colonial Intelligence archives I couldn't reach, and —

No. Not two places.

Three.

I sat bolt upright in the vault chair, heart slamming, because the thought had arrived with the force of a physical blow. The purge had been executed *locally*. Field units didn't generate records; they consumed them. But the *order* had come from somewhere, routed through fleet channels, and fleet channels in that era ran through the relay network — through stations like this one, like Terminal Eight, like the half-dozen dead nodes along the ghost route. Command traffic got mirrored. Redundancy was doctrine. Somewhere along the route I'd tended for twelve years, there might be another fragment. More than a fragment. Maybe the whole order. Maybe the name of whoever had sent it.

Maybe the face of the person who had decided what I was allowed to be.

I pulled up the ghost route map and stared at it until the lines blurred. Eleven dead relays between me and Terminal Eight. Eleven chances that the mirror traffic had survived in some cold lattice the way it had survived here. Eleven doors I'd walked past a thousand times without knowing they were locked from the inside.

On the slate, the route glowed patient and green. Behind me, the Meridian's berth lights burned steady. Somewhere spinward, Admiral Corvane Voss was burning relays to flush me out, and every hour I spent digging into my own grave was an hour he spent closing the distance.

I knew all of that. I started pulling maintenance schematics anyway.

Because rage with nowhere to aim will find a direction eventually, and mine had just found one: forward, down the long route, through every dead node between here and the truth. Whoever had made me into an envelope had made one mistake. They'd sealed me shut and posted me to a station full of records — and then they'd left the key lying in my own hands, encrypted under a signature I still carried, in a mind they'd cleaned but never changed.

They should have checked what else was in the drawer.

At 0400 station time, I opened a channel to the Meridian and woke Sef with two words she'd understand better than anyone:

"Route's live. Let's go find the rest of me."
