## 11. Terminal Eight

Terminal Eight announced itself the way the dead announce themselves: by absence. No running lights. No beacon pulse. Just a dark mass against the starfield, a splinter of architecture tumbling in a slow, patient rotation that had gone uncorrected for years.

Xanthe watched it grow on the Meridian's forward display and felt something in her chest tighten — not fear exactly, but recognition. She had spent eleven years tending stations like this one, coaxing warmth back into cold hulls, and she knew what she was looking at. A body that still breathed, barely. A station on the edge of the long sleep.

"Life support is cycling," Sef said from the copilot's seat, her small hands moving across the console with the certainty of someone who had done this ten thousand times in a life her body hadn't lived yet. "Irregular. Someone's rationing power. That means people."

"People or ghosts," Xanthe said.

"Ghosts don't ration." Sef glanced up at her, and in the girl's old eyes there was something Xanthe was learning to read: the particular weariness of a person who had already buried more than most. "Ghosts don't hide, either. Whoever's in there killed their own transponder. They don't want to be found."

"Neither do we."

"Then we should get along fine."

The Meridian drifted in on thrusters alone, silent as a held breath. Xanthe brought them alongside the station's primary docking arm — a corroded spine studded with airlocks, one of which still showed a faint amber telltale behind its grime. She matched rotation with the patience of a decade's practice, felt the magnetic collar kiss the hull, and waited.

Nothing happened.

For three full minutes they sat clamped to a dead station, and nothing happened, and Xanthe understood that this too was an answer. *We see you. We're deciding.*

Then the airlock cycled.

* * *

They came through with weapons up — two figures in patched fleet exosuits with the insignia stripped away, and behind them a third who moved differently, unhurried, hands empty and visible. The suit seals retracted. The face beneath was broad, gray-streaked at the temples, marked along the jaw by an old burn scar that had been closed with field sutures by someone in a hurry.

"You're not fleet," the woman said. It wasn't a question. Her eyes went past Xanthe to Sef, and something flickered across her face — recognition, or its ghost. "And you're not what your ship says you are. The Meridian's registry expired four years ago. Officially, you're all dead."

"We've heard," Xanthe said. "Officially, so is everyone out here."

The woman studied her for a long moment. Then she lowered the hand that had been resting near her sidearm, and the two guards did the same, though they didn't lower the weapons entirely. Trust, Xanthe thought, came in degrees here, measured in millimeters.

"I'm Okafor. Adaeze Okafor. Fleet Medical Corps, twelve years — until six weeks ago, when I stopped being that." She looked at Sef again, longer this time. "I know you. Not your face. Your file. Sef Almáriz, courier-grade, Meridian deep-range run, 2219. You were thirty-one when your file closed."

"I'm six now," Sef said flatly. "It's a long story."

"No," Okafor said quietly. "It isn't. I've seen the others." She turned, gesturing them deeper into the station. "Come inside before the cold finds a seam. We have heat, water, and about nine hours of oxygen margin if we're careful. And we have questions. I suspect you have answers. Let's trade."

* * *

Terminal Eight's interior was a museum of slow decay. Frost ferned across bulkheads where condensation had frozen and refrozen through hundreds of failed thermal cycles. Emergency strips glowed at quarter power, turning the corridors the color of old honey. Xanthe counted the signs as they walked — patched conduits, jury-rigged scrubbers, a hydroponics bay converted into a bunkroom with seed trays growing pale lettuce under salvaged grow-lights. People had been living here a while. Living carefully.

There were seventeen of them. She counted twice to be sure. Most were civilians — colonists, spacers, a family with two actual children who stared at Sef with the frank curiosity of the young meeting someone their own size who moved like a stranger. Three wore the stripped uniforms of fleet deserters. One, an older man with tremoring hands, sat apart from the others and would not look at anyone.

"They came off the *Kestrel's Wake*," Okafor said, following Xanthe's gaze. "Colony transport, three hundred sleepers bound for Halcyon Deep. Voss burned their relay out from under them during transit. They made it to the pods; the station didn't." Her voice didn't waver, but it flattened, the way voices flatten when grief has been carried too long to be fresh. "He didn't even hail them first. He just fired."

"Why?" Xanthe asked, though she already knew.

"Because we were listening. Because we'd started asking what happened at Kestrel Station nineteen years ago, and asking out loud, on channels he could hear." Okafor led them into what had been the station's medbay — the one room that looked maintained, loved almost, its equipment kept clean and calibrated. "I was fleet medical intelligence. I processed casualty reports from the war's first year. I noticed things. Numbers that didn't add. Atrocity reports filed and then unfiled. And I noticed that every report touching the Kestrel Incident passed through the same sanitization chain on both sides — Colonial *and* Earth. Two governments that supposedly hate each other, using the same redaction protocols." She smiled without any humor in it. "That kind of symmetry doesn't happen by accident. It happens by agreement."

Sef had gone very still. Xanthe saw the girl's small hands close around each other in her lap, knuckles whitening, and she understood: Okafor was saying aloud what the cylinder in Xanthe's pack already whispered. The war had architects. Plural. On both sides.

"How many survived the Wake?" Xanthe asked.

"Eleven made it to pods. Nine reached us. One died of his burns." Okafor's eyes were steady. "Which brings me to why you're really here, Technician Villalobos. Yes — I know your name. Terminal Nine's maintenance logs pinged our passive array when you crossed the ghost route. You're the only human being alive who can walk that corridor of dead relays, and you just walked it carrying a ship that's been dead for eleven years, crewed by children with adult service records." She leaned forward across the medbay table. "I'm not fleet anymore. I'm not going to demand anything. But I'd very much like to know what you're carrying."

* * *

Xanthe looked at Sef. Sef looked back, and between them passed the whole argument they'd already had twice aboard the Meridian — trust as currency, spend it or hoard it, and either way you might be robbed.

It was Sef who decided, because it was Sef's truth to spend.

"The cylinder holds testimony," the girl said. "Recorded testimony, encrypted, courier-sealed. From people who were at Kestrel Station the night it burned. It names the officers who gave the order — on both sides. It names the protocol they used to bury it. It names the intelligence programs that wiped everyone who might remember." Her voice stayed level, but Xanthe heard the effort holding it level, a cable under load. "My faction built the reversal drive to carry it. Age the crew backward, cross the border scanners, and no one stops a ship full of children. Children aren't threats. Children get waved through."

Okafor sat motionless for a long moment. When she finally spoke, her voice was rough. "Nineteen years. Nineteen years of war. Four hundred thousand dead by the last honest count, and both governments have known since the beginning that the war is a lie they wrote together." She pressed both palms flat against the table, as if steadying herself against a deck that had tilted. "And you're telling me the proof has been sitting in a black cylinder the size of my forearm."

"It's sitting right here," Xanthe said, and set the cylinder on the medbay table between them.

The sound it made — a soft, dense click of metal on metal — seemed louder than it was. Everyone in the room looked at it. One of the guards actually took a step back, as though proximity to the thing might be dangerous. In a way, Xanthe thought, it was the most dangerous object within forty light-years.

Okafor didn't reach for it. That, more than anything, decided Xanthe. A thief would have taken it. A zealot would have seized it. Okafor just looked at it the way people look at a wound they're afraid to clean.

"Here's my offer," Okafor said at last. "Shelter. Air, water, heat, whatever medical care my skills cover — and I'm told you need it, because those children out there are aging forward through eleven years of accumulated cellular debt, and some of them are going to crash if nobody intervenes. I can hold maybe half of them stable. The transmitter array on the comms tower is intact; it's rated for colony-band broadcast, enough power to reach the independent networks — Ceres Free, the Belt cooperatives, the outer cluster relays that neither government controls." She paused. "In exchange, when you decide what to do with that cylinder — and you will have to decide, soon, because Voss knows you're in this volume and he's hunting you relay by relay — you share it. Not with me. With everyone. All of it, unredacted, to every network that will carry it. Justice doesn't go through channels anymore. Channels are how we got here."

"And if we refuse?" Xanthe asked. "If we take your shelter and fly on?"

Okafor met her eyes without flinching. "Then you fly on, and I'll seal the dock behind you and wish you luck, because you'll need it. I'm not going to steal the truth from a child." A tired half-smile. "But I'll be honest with you, technician: I hope you don't refuse. Because I've spent six weeks hiding in the dark with seventeen frightened people, and I am so very tired of surviving. I'd like, just once, to *do* something."

* * *

Later — after the Meridian's crew had been distributed among bunks, after Okafor had drawn blood from three of the reversed and muttered grimly over the results, after Xanthe had crawled halfway into the station's power grid with a fusion torch and come out smelling of ozone — she found Sef in the observation blister at the station's crown.

The girl sat with her knees pulled up, watching the stars turn slowly past the cracked viewport. Beyond them, faint as breath on glass, hung the direction of the Kestrel debris field. Of home, for none of them anymore.

"She wants us to broadcast everything," Sef said, without turning. "No conditions. No negotiation. Just — open the box in front of the whole galaxy."

"That frightens you."

"It should frighten you too." Sef's voice was quiet. "You haven't read what's in the cylinder. I have. Parts of it. There are things in there that don't just end a war, Xanthe. There are things in there that could start a worse one. Names. Orders. Retaliations that never got reported. If it goes out raw, half the colonies will want vengeance before they finish reading the first page."

"And if it stays hidden?"

Sef was silent for a moment. Out in the dark, a piece of orbital debris tumbled through the viewport's frame, catching starlight, vanishing again.

"Then Voss wins, and the lie keeps killing people for another nineteen years," she said finally. "I know. I've run the arithmetic a hundred times. It always comes out the same. It just comes out ugly." She looked up at Xanthen then, and her voice dropped to almost nothing. "There's something else. Something I haven't told you. About the recording. About what my faction did to make sure it would land the way it needed to land."

Xanthe felt the words arrive like a change in pressure, the way a hull announces a breach before the alarm does.

"Sef," she said carefully. "What did you do to it?"

Before the girl could answer, the station's comm panel crackled to life — Okafor's voice, tight and fast, stripped of everything it had carried in the medbay:

"Xanthe — long-range contact. Single signature, military profile, coming up the ghost route behind you. It's burning hard." A pause, one heartbeat long. "It's Voss. He found the door you left open."
