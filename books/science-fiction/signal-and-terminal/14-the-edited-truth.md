## 14. The Edited Truth

The cylinder sat on the workbench like a held breath.

Xanthe had not touched it since Sef's confession. She had made herself tea she didn't drink, walked the length of Terminal Eight's spine twice, and stood for a long time at the viewport watching the dead relay chain glitter against the dark like a rosary someone had dropped and never picked up. Then she came back to the bench, because fury was a fuel and she had an engine that needed turning.

*The courier faction falsified part of the record.*

Sef had said it the way a surgeon says *there's nothing more I can do* — flat, final, watching for the flinch. *We weighted it. Earth's share of the guilt had to outweigh the Colonies' or the message would die in committee. Nobody reads a balanced indictment, Xanthe. They read a villain.*

Xanthe had thrown her out of the compartment. Not violently — Sef was six years old and thirty-one years deep, and you didn't throw a child violently — but she had put her hand flat against the little shoulder and turned her, and said, *Out,* in a voice she didn't recognize, and Sef had gone, and the door had sealed, and now it was Xanthe and the cylinder and the truth, whatever shape the truth actually was.

She jacked her maintenance deck into the cylinder's read port.

The encryption unfolded for her like it had been waiting — Sef's faction had keyed it to her, or to her station, or to some signature buried in her that she couldn't name. Files cascaded up her display: sensor logs, fleet traffic intercepts, medical scans, testimony recorded in voices she didn't know. And at the center, the Kestrel Incident, the atrocity that had lit thirty years of war, rendered in cold administrative detail.

She opened her recovered purge fragment alongside it. Her own signature, pulled from Terminal Nine's deep archive — the authorization she had no memory of giving, the data purge on the night everything burned. She laid the two records over each other the way she'd lay two star charts over each other, looking for the seam, the place where the mapmaker had lied.

The seam was everywhere.

* * *

The official story — the story both governments had told for thirty years, the story she'd grown up on in the Colonial orbital schools, the story that had made her enlist — was simple. Earth's Third Fleet had fired on the Kestrel habitat, a civilian research station in the neutral band, killing four thousand people. The Colonies had answered with the embargo, then the blockade, then the war. Earth denied it. The denial was a lie. Everyone knew the denial was a lie. That was the whole architecture of the conflict: one crime, one liar, one long punishment.

The cylinder told a different story, and Xanthe had believed it, because the cylinder came with timestamps and telemetry and the raw voice of a weapons officer weeping into a log he thought would never be read.

But the cylinder was weighted. Sef had admitted it. So Xanthe stopped reading the cylinder's narrative and started reading only its raw data — the telemetry, the drive signatures, the comm intercepts that Sef's faction had gathered but not curated. And she cross-referenced every one against her purge fragment, which was not a narrative at all. It was a work order. Her hands, her authorization code, her station, deleting something on the orders of someone whose name had been redacted even from her own memory.

The raw telemetry showed two fleets at Kestrel that night.

Earth's Third Fleet, yes — weapons hot, firing solution confirmed, the crime the cylinder led with. But forty minutes *before* the Third Fleet opened fire, a Colonial strike wing had entered the neutral band with transponders dark, running a spoofed Earth signature, and had vented the Kestrel habitat's atmosphere through a maintenance uplink that someone on the inside had unlocked.

Someone on the inside. Xanthe scrolled the access log with a feeling like descending in an elevator that never stops. The uplink authorization had come from a relay technician. A relay technician with her clearance level. A relay technician whose signature matched, digit for digit, the signature on the purge order she'd recovered from her own archive.

She read the timestamp again. And again. The numbers didn't change. They never change. That's the thing about numbers.

The sequence assembled itself in her head with the horrible inevitability of an orbital decay calculation. The Colonies had committed the atrocity — had murdered four thousand of their own researchers, disguised as Earth, to give the war its martyr and its moral engine. Earth's Third Fleet, arriving to find a burning habitat and Colonial warships in the neutral band, had fired in what its own logs called *retaliatory confusion* — and then Earth's command, discovering within days that the kill shot hadn't come from their side, had made the calculation that a war fought over a lie they could pin on themselves was cheaper than a war fought over a lie they'd have to expose. So Earth confessed to a crime it hadn't committed, and the Colonies built a church on a grave they'd dug themselves, and thirty years of burning followed, and both governments had spent those thirty years quietly cooperating to keep the lie pressurized.

Her purge hadn't deleted evidence of Earth's guilt.

Her purge had deleted evidence of the Colonies' guilt — the strike wing's true telemetry, the spoofed signatures, the inside access. She had been the broom. Colonial intelligence had swept up after their own atrocity and used her hands to do it, and then they had swept her, wiped the night out of her head and posted her to the long route where nothing ever happened and no one would ever ask her a question.

She sat back from the display. The compartment hummed. Somewhere down the spine, Okafor's survivors were sleeping in shifts, and Sef was — where? Awake, probably. Children's bodies didn't seem to need as much sleep as they pretended.

* * *

She ran it a third time, because twice could be error and she wanted the third pass to be grief.

It wasn't error. The cylinder's doctored sections were elegant — she could see the craft in them now, the way you can see the brushstrokes once someone tells you it's a forgery. Sef's faction had taken the true telemetry and shifted one wing's drive signature by a factor small enough to survive casual audit. They had reordered two intercepts so that the Colonial strike wing appeared to arrive *after* the Third Fleet's first shots rather than before. They had amplified Earth's role and buried the Colonial strike under a layer of plausible noise. The result was not a lie, exactly. It was the truth with one load-bearing wall moved.

And it would have worked. It *had* worked — on her. She had read the cylinder in the first hours after the Meridian docked and she had believed it completely, had felt the old righteous fury come up in her like water into a bilge, and she had been ready to broadcast it to forty worlds and call it justice.

A clean broadcast would start the war again. Not might. Would. Because the Colonies' founding grievance was real in their blood — Kestrel was their martyrdom, their reason, the wound they'd built an identity around. Broadcast a version that said *you did it to yourselves and framed Earth*, unverified, arriving from a faction that had already doctored it once, and every Colonial world would call it Earth's latest atrocity: a forged confession designed to break them. And Earth, handed a record that said *you confessed to a crime you didn't commit and let thirty million of your people die for it*, would tear itself apart looking for who knew — and the ones who knew, the ones on both sides who had signed the joint cover-up, would burn the evidence and the messengers before any tribunal could convene.

Both sides guilty. Both sides liars. Both sides holding a document that proved the other side's worst crime and buried their own.

She thought of Voss's face on the comm, that terrible patient cruelty, and understood him a little better than she wanted to. He wasn't defending the lie because he loved it. He was defending it because he'd seen the math — because he knew that the truth about Kestrel wasn't a weapon you could aim at one enemy. It was a bomb with no fuse you could cut. It went off under everyone.

And she had been carrying it through the dark, planning to light it.

* * *

She found Sef in the observation blister, folded into a chair built for adults, her small legs straight out in front of her like a doll's, her old eyes on the relay chain.

"You ran it," Sef said. Not a question.

"I ran it." Xanthe leaned against the frame. "Your edit was good. I want you to know that. If I hadn't had my own fragment — if I were anyone else, anywhere else — I'd have broadcast it and died believing it."

"It wasn't my edit." Sef's voice was flat. "I was eleven when they briefed the courier team. They told us the weighting was necessary. They told us the truth, unweighted, was a luxury the dead couldn't afford." She turned her head. "I believed them. That's the part I can't — I was an adult, Xanthe. I'd buried a husband. I'd voted in four elections. And I sat in that briefing room and I nodded."

"Who ran the weighting?"

"A committee." Sef smiled, and it was the ugliest thing Xanthe had seen in years, because it was a child's face doing an old woman's contempt. "There's always a committee. That's how you know a lie is official."

Xanthe pulled the second chair around and sat, and for a while neither of them spoke, and the dead relays ticked past the viewport one by one, each one a light someone like her had once kept lit.

"Here's what my fragment says," Xanthe said finally. "And I need you to listen without deciding anything, because I've spent the last six hours deciding things and it nearly made me do something stupid." She laid it out: the Colonial strike wing, the spoofed signatures, the inside access with her signature on it, the joint cover-up, the purge. She watched Sef's face while she talked, and watched the child's features do what no child's face should do — track, compute, grieve.

When she finished, Sef was quiet for a long time.

"The strike wing," she said at last. "The telemetry you recovered. Is it in the cylinder?"

"No. Your faction had pieces of it — that's how they built the edit. But the full sequence, the inside access, my signature — that was in the purge. That's the part they deleted. That's the part nobody has."

"Then nobody can prove it."

"Nobody can prove any of it. That's the point. That's always been the point." Xanthe pressed her palms against her knees. "Sef. If we broadcast your version, the Colonies fracture and Earth burns its own archives. If we broadcast the true version — my version — we're one technician and a shipload of children claiming the founding atrocity of the war was a false flag, and the first thing every analyst on both sides will ask is *where's the primary telemetry*, and the answer is *deleted, by me, thirty years ago, and I don't remember doing it*. We have no case. We have a story. And stories start wars faster than evidence does."

"Then what do we do?" Sef's voice cracked on the last word, and for one moment she sounded exactly her size, and it was unbearable. "We didn't survive eleven years in a can and get shot at and lose Okafor's people and — and everything — to arrive at *we have no case*."

Xanthe looked at the cylinder on the deck between them. Black, sealed, patient. A vessel, like Sef. Like her.

"We don't choose between your version and my version," she said slowly, because the idea was assembling itself as she spoke, the way her best repairs always had — not designed but discovered. "Both versions are evidence now. Your edit proves your faction thought the truth needed shaping — that's a confession in itself. My fragment proves Colonial intelligence ran a wipe on its own technician — that's a confession too. The cover-up isn't the thing that happened after the crime. The cover-up *is* the crime, the living one, the one still breathing. And it has two parents."

Sef was watching her with those old eyes. "You want to broadcast the forgery alongside the truth."

"I want to broadcast the *whole* wound. Both governments, both lies, both sets of hands — including mine. Let forty worlds see the edit and the correction and the purge signature and all of it, raw, unweighted, uncurated, and let them do what no committee on either side will ever allow: judge it together." She heard her own voice steady, and was surprised by it. "Voss wants the cylinder because a single message can be killed. A single truth can be buried. But a whole archive — the lie *and* the seam in the lie — that has to be read, all of it, or none of it makes sense. We make them read all of it."

"They'll call it chaos."

"They'll call it a lot of things." Xanthe stood. Through the viewport, far down the relay chain, a new light had kindled — a drive flare, small and hard, moving with the deliberate economy of a warship that believed it had all the time in the galaxy. Her stomach dropped before her mind caught up. She checked the bearing. Checked it again.

It was coming up the ghost route. Their route. Toward Terminal Eight.

"Sef," she said quietly, "go wake the others."
