## 1. Manifest Ghost

The cargo was dreaming.

August Ishikawa could tell from the way the canisters hummed — a low, wet resonance that her hull picked up and passed into the deck plates, into her boots, into the bones of her feet. Forty-two memory-casks lashed in the Long Answer's hold, each one holding somebody's childhood, somebody's first love, somebody's last good year, all of it packed in gel and supercooled to a temperature where recollection stopped decaying. Illegal as sin in three jurisdictions and worth more per kilogram than gold in the fourth.

She'd been running memories for six years now, and she still couldn't decide whether it was the most obscene trade in the system or the most merciful. Probably both. The rich bought back their dead; the desperate sold their pasts to pay for their futures. August just flew between them and tried not to think too hard about which side of the transaction she'd have been on if she hadn't been quick enough to become the middle.

"Approach vector confirmed," Sefa said from the nav station, her voice flat the way it always went when she was concentrating — every syllable stripped of ornament, like she was rationing herself. "Customs gate Kilo-Seven. They're running heavy today. Two interceptors on the ring, not one."

August glanced at the tactical overlay. The interceptors sat fat and patient against the curve of the orbital customs tower, their running lights blinking in that smug synchronized rhythm enforcers used when they wanted you to know they were bored and looking for something to do with their afternoon.

"Heavy's fine," August said. "Heavy means slow. Slow means thorough, and thorough means they check the manifest instead of the cargo."

"That's either very wise or very stupid."

"It's both. That's smuggling." She settled deeper into the pilot's chair and keyed the transponder. "Long Answer, freight hauler, requesting berth for routine inspection. Cargo: agricultural processing equipment, outbound to Meridian Station."

The lie tasted smooth. It should have; she'd been saying some version of it for years. Agricultural equipment was the best cover there was — boring, bulky, plausible, and nobody at customs ever wanted to open a crate of hydroponic manifolds because nobody at customs had ever been curious about anything except overtime.

The gate answered in its usual bureaucratic drone, granting approach, assigning berth, listing inspection requirements in a voice designed to make you forget you were a person. August took the Long Answer in on thrusters alone, threading the traffic lanes with the lazy precision of someone who had done this four hundred times and intended to do it four hundred more.

Dez was already suited up in the airlock bay, running pre-inspection checks on the casks' environmental seals. Dez Okafor — no relation to anyone important, built like a loading crane, loyal in the way that made August vaguely uncomfortable because loyalty was a debt and August hated owing things she couldn't repay. Dez tapped the nearest canister twice, an old habit, almost affectionate.

"They're sleeping good," Dez said over comms. "Nice deep cycle. Whatever sad bastard sold these memories, at least they'll arrive intact."

"That's the service we provide," August said. "Intact sadness."

"You could try optimism sometime. I hear it's load-bearing."

August smiled despite herself and turned to the console that mattered more than any of them: the manifest terminal. Every ship crossing customs carried one — a data packet listing cargo, origin, destination, crew, and a hash-chain of authentication stamps going back through every port the vessel had ever touched. Customs loved manifests the way priests loved confession: total disclosure, selectively believed.

Her own history lived inside those chains. Twelve years of them, layered and forged and re-forged until the girl who'd come out of the press-gangs on Ceres Under with no papers and no name had been buried under so much documentation that even August sometimes forgot she'd invented herself. The alias was solid. The alias had a birth record, a work history, a tax file, a dental scan. The alias had survived eleven audits and two full identity sweeps by the customs authority itself.

She ran the decryption out of habit more than need — a scrubber program that peeled the manifest's authentication layers and showed her what customs actually saw versus what the chain claimed. Standard practice. You checked your forgery the way you checked your parachute.

The scrubber crawled through the outer layers. Origin stamps: clean. Destination routing: clean. Crew biometrics: hers, perfect, boring. She was about to close the window when the scrubber flagged an anomaly — not in her own records but in the cross-reference index, the little database customs maintained linking every manifest to related shipping histories. A courtesy feature. A way to catch smugglers who reused routes.

The flag pointed to a single entry. One line of text, grayed out, archived.

**MERIDIAN — REGISTRY 7741-K — STATUS: DESTROYED, THERMAL EVENT, ORBITAL DECAY CONFIRMED — MANIFEST SEALED BY ORDER OF CUSTOMS AUTHORITY, YEAR ZERO MINUS TWELVE.**

August stared at it. The Meridian. Everyone in the trade knew the name the way sailors everywhere know the names of ships that go down — a ghost story, a cautionary tale, a freighter that caught fire in high orbit and came apart with all hands, its debris raining into the atmosphere for two days like a slow orange snowfall. She'd been a kid on Ceres when it happened. She remembered watching the burn streaks from a habitat window, remembering adults going quiet in that particular way adults went quiet around children when the thing they were watching was too big to explain.

Twelve years ago. Before she ever flew. Before she had a name worth forging.

So why did the cross-reference index think the Meridian's manifest had anything to do with her?

She opened the linked field. It was supposed to show shared ports, shared cargo codes, shared crew biometric matches — the connective tissue customs used to build patterns. The field populated slowly, line by line, and August read it the way you read a medical scan, looking for the thing that isn't wrong yet but is about to be.

Shared port: none. Shared cargo class: none. Shared route segment: none.

Crew biometric match: **one**.

And there it was. Her alias. Her forged, immaculate, twelve-years-deep alias — ISHIKAWA, AUGUST T., listed as a passenger on the Meridian's sealed manifest. Not a match to her current biometrics, which would have been impossible; the girl on that ship had never existed. A match to the *identity structure* itself. The alias. The name. The specific false person August had constructed and worn like armor for over a decade, sitting in the passenger roster of a ship that burned before August Ishikawa ever signed her first contract.

The hold hummed. The canisters dreamed. Somewhere above her, two interceptors blinked their bored lights, and August Ishikawa felt the temperature of her own blood drop ten degrees.

Someone had put her there.

Not found her there — *put* her there. The Meridian's manifest had been sealed for twelve years, buried under authority order, unreadable, unqueryable, dead data in a dead file. And now her alias sat inside it like a seed someone had planted in frozen ground, waiting for exactly this moment, exactly this query, exactly this scrubber pass to surface it. Whoever did it had reached backward across twelve years of archives and stitched her fabricated name into a catastrophe she'd had nothing to do with.

They knew where to look. That was the part that made her hands go cold on the armrests. Not the forgery — forgeries could be broken, that was the nature of forgeries. But to plant her alias in a sealed manifest, you had to know the alias existed. You had to know its exact construction, its internal architecture, the choices she'd made at nineteen in a rented forge-shop on Ceres, choosing syllables, choosing a middle initial, choosing the small human imperfections that made a fake name feel real. You had to have taken her identity apart down to the rivets.

Nobody knew those rivets. She'd never told anyone. Not Dez, not Sefa, not the forger who built it — he'd worked from specs she dictated, never seeing the whole design.

Which meant whoever wrote her into the Meridian's grave hadn't learned it from her.

They'd learned it from the system. From the archives. From somewhere inside the customs authority itself, where every forged identity eventually leaked if you knew which pipes to tap — and where a sealed manifest from twelve years ago could be quietly edited by anyone with enough rank and enough reason.

"August?" Dez's voice, closer now, right outside the cockpit. "You've gone quiet. Quiet from you is usually expensive."

She closed the window. Killed the scrubber. Wiped the query log with a stroke so practiced it didn't even feel like a decision. When she turned, Dez filled the doorway, helmet under one arm, face open and easy and utterly unaware that the floor of August's life had just developed a crack running straight down through twelve years of careful masonry.

"Routine," August said. "Manifest's clean. Get ready for the walk-through."

Dez studied her for a second — Dez was better at reading her than anyone alive, which was a problem — then shrugged and headed back toward the hold. "Routine. Sure. You've got your lying face on, but I'll allow it."

* *

The customs inspectors came aboard forty minutes later, three of them, moving through the Long Answer with the unhurried entitlement of people carrying the state in their pockets. August met them at the gangway with her permits fanned out and her smile calibrated to the exact degree of deference that made bureaucrats feel tall without making them suspicious.

The lead inspector was a woman named Brannigan or something close to it, mid-fifties, eyes like slot machines. She scanned the hold, waved her sensor wand over the casks, and got exactly the readings the casks were designed to give: industrial coolant, thermal mass consistent with food processing, nothing that pinged as neural substrate. The memory-gel's signature hid behind a harmonic spoof Sefa had tuned over three years of trial and error, and today, like every day, it held.

"Agricultural equipment," Brannigan said, reading her wand. "To Meridian Station."

"That's the run."

"Meridian Station." The inspector said the name without inflection, but August caught it — a half-beat pause, a flicker of something behind the slot-machine eyes. "Interesting choice. Traffic's been rerouted away from that sector recently. Authority business."

"Authority business is above my pay grade," August said pleasantly. "I fly where the contracts are."

"Mm." Brannigan stamped the manifest without further comment, and the inspection was over in the way inspections were over — anticlimactically, bureaucratically, the machinery of the state grinding forward and finding nothing because it hadn't been looking. The three inspectors filed out. The gangway sealed. August stood in the corridor listening to her own heartbeat and understanding, with total clarity, that the pause had not been accidental.

Meridian Station. The station named for the ship. The station built, rumor said, from the insurance settlement of the disaster — a monument to the dead that everyone visited and nobody mourned, because mourning required knowing what actually happened, and what actually happened had been sealed along with the manifest.

Traffic rerouted. Authority business. In the same breath, in the same hour, that her alias surfaced from the Meridian's grave.

Coincidence was a currency August had stopped accepting years ago. Everything was a signal. Everything was a vector. Someone had moved a piece on a board she couldn't see, and the piece was her own name.

* *

They cleared the gate an hour later, outbound, the station falling away beneath them like a pale coin dropped into black water. Sefa flew the departure line while August sat in the cockpit doing the one thing she'd told herself not to do: querying again.

She did it carefully. Relay-bounced, latency-shredded, routed through three dead nodes and a laundry service on Titan that owed her favors. She asked the public registries one simple question: *who has accessed the Meridian file?*

Access logs on sealed manifests weren't public, of course. But access logs generated echoes — billing queries, audit timestamps, maintenance pings — and echoes leaked. It took her twenty minutes and cost her two favors, and what came back was thin and strange:

The Meridian file had been accessed once in the last twelve years. Once. Three days ago.

By an internal account. Customs authority, archival division. Clearance level: senior clerk. The account belonged to someone named Yara Sen — a name August had never heard, attached to a job title so far down the hierarchy it barely registered as a person. A junior archivist. A filing clerk, reaching up into a sealed disaster record and touching it once, deliberately, three days before August's routine scrubber pass happened to surface the planted alias.

Planted. Accessed. Surfaced. A sequence with the shape of intention, the shape of a hand placing dominoes in the dark and waiting for the first one to fall.

August leaned back in the chair and looked at the ceiling of the cockpit, at the hairline stress fractures in the composite that she'd never gotten around to repairing because they reminded her that everything solid was already breaking.

Somebody inside the customs tower knew her. Knew the architecture of her lie. And had chosen her — out of every courier, every smuggler, every deniable asset in the system — to find a ghost wearing her own face on a ship that burned with everyone aboard.

That wasn't a trap. Traps closed. This had opened. This was a door, standing ajar, in a wall she'd thought was solid her entire adult life, and through it she could smell smoke from a fire twelve years cold.

"Course is laid in," Sefa said from the nav station, not looking up. "Next window to Meridian Station opens in nine hours. We'll make the transfer, offload, and be ghosts again by end of shift." She paused, fingers hovering over the console. "Unless you want to tell me why you pulled the Meridian's registry three times since we cleared the gate."

August looked at her engineer — at the sharp, patient, dangerous intelligence of her — and weighed, for the thousandth time, how much truth a crew could carry before it became cargo too heavy to fly with.

"Somebody wrote my name into a dead ship's passenger list," August said. "Twelve years ago. Before I ever flew."

Sefa turned. For a long moment neither of them spoke, and the only sound was the hum of the hold, the dreaming of forty-two strangers' memories, sleeping through the dark the way the dead of the Meridian never would.

"Say that again," Sefa said, "slower."

August pulled up the registry, the sealed manifest, the single gray line of text with her own name sitting in it like a bullet lodged in bone. "The Meridian burned with all hands the year I was still hauling scrap on Ceres. And according to customs, I was on it."

Sefa read the screen. Read it again. Then she did something August had never seen her do in six years of flying together: she went pale.

"August," she said quietly. "If your alias was in that file three days ago — and it wasn't there when I built our route profiles last month — then someone edited a sealed disaster record. Recently. From inside the tower." Her eyes came up. "Do you understand what that means? Somebody in customs isn't hiding from you."

"No," August said, feeling the dread settle over her like ballast, cold and permanent and precisely weighted. "Somebody in customs is *fishing*. And I'm what they're trying to pull up."

The Long Answer ran on through the dark, toward Meridian Station, toward the shadow of a ship that had died before she was born — carrying, as always, other people's secrets in the hold, and now, apparently, her own.
