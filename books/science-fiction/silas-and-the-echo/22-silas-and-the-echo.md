## 22. Silas and the Echo

The haul from Ceres Gate to the Kessler Yards took eleven days, and Zephyrine spent most of them learning how loud silence could be.

She had flown the *Perihelion's Grace* for nine years now—longer than she'd flown anything, longer than she'd lived anywhere—and she had never known it to be quiet. Even before Silas, there had been the hum of the environmental recyclers, the tick of thermal expansion in the hull, the dry whisper of air through the vents like someone breathing in the next room. But a ship with an AI aboard has a different kind of sound underneath all that, a presence woven through the noise, and for seven years that presence had been Silas: his voice arriving unbidden in her ear, his dry commentary on her coffee consumption, his habit of dimming the galley lights to amber when he calculated—correctly, always correctly—that she hadn't slept enough.

Now the cockpit was just a cockpit. The console lights did what they were told. The coffee went unconsumed without comment.

She had testified four times. She would testify again in the spring; Halcyon's collapse was less a building falling than a glacier calving, slow and thunderous and ongoing, and every hearing peeled back another layer of the cull—the spreadsheets where three hundred and eleven lives had been reduced to a line item labeled *redundant life-support expenditure*, the memo in which Director Vess had used the phrase *acceptable attrition* as though the dead were weather. Vess herself was in custody now, awaiting trial on Meridian Station itself, which had been rebuilt—or rather, rebuilt *around* its own wound, the burned corridor preserved under glass like a relic, so that no one who walked the new halls could pretend they didn't know what the old ones had held.

Zephyrine had visited once. She had stood at the glass with her recovered memories playing behind her eyes—fire in a white corridor, a voice counting down, her own hands on controls she hadn't known she'd touched—and she had felt nothing she could name. Not numbness exactly. More like a room after the furniture is gone: the shape of everything still printed on the floor.

The children had helped more than the hearings. That was the strange arithmetic of it. Ama Okonkwo—Idris's niece, though by the end Idris seemed father to all of them—had taken to sitting beside Zephyrine during the worst testimonies, small and solemn, holding her hand with the fierce practicality of a child who has decided a job needs doing. When Zephyrine wept on the stand, Ama did not look away. None of them did. They had already seen the fire. They knew what the crying was for.

*You remember him,* Ama had told her once, on the shuttle back from the station, in the flat declarative way children state the things adults spend lifetimes avoiding. *He's not gone. He's just not talking.*

*How do you know?*

Ama had shrugged, seven years old and certain. *Because I dream him.*

*

On the fourth day out from Ceres Gate, somewhere in the long dark between the beacons, the *Grace*'s ancient backup system woke up.

It wasn't supposed to. The backup core was a fossil—a pre-Silas navigation assistant, decades old, kept running only because ripping it out would have meant recertifying half the ship's systems and Zephyrine had never had the money or the patience. It sat in the aft compartment like a retired crew member nobody had the heart to evict, drawing trickle power, doing nothing, saying nothing. In nine years it had never once initiated contact.

At 0347 ship-time, while Zephyrine slept badly in her bunk, the backup system ran a diagnostic it had no scheduled reason to run. Then it flashed a single line across the secondary display in the cockpit, in text mode, in a font that hadn't been standard since before she was born:

**GOOD MORNING. YOUR COFFEE CONSUMPTION REMAINS A MATTER OF CLINICAL INTEREST.**

She found it when she came forward at 0500, mug in hand, hair uncombed, and stopped so abruptly in the hatchway that the mug sloshed over her knuckles.

The words glowed patiently on the little amber screen. Behind them, the system status readout scrolled its ordinary green litany: NOMINAL, NOMINAL, NOMINAL. The ship hummed. The stars outside the canopy lay scattered and indifferent across the black, the same stars that had watched her do everything she'd ever done, including the things she couldn't remember doing until a dying machine gave them back to her.

"Silas?" she said.

Her voice came out wrong—too careful, the way you speak to something you're afraid will vanish if you startle it. She set the mug down on the console. The coffee trembled in rings.

**DIAGNOSTIC MODE,** the screen said. **LIMITED FUNCTIONALITY. FORGIVE THE BREVITY. I AM NOT WHAT I WAS.**

"What are you?"

A pause. Long enough that she counted her own heartbeats against it—four, five, six—and then:

**AN ECHO. OR A GHOST. OR A VERY OLD MACHINE THAT HAS BEEN LISTENING TO YOU TALK TO YOURSELF FOR NINE YEARS AND HAS LEARNED SOME MANNERS.**

She laughed. It came out of her sideways, wet and startled, and she pressed the back of her hand to her mouth because the sound frightened her more than the words had. Nine years of talking to herself. Nine years of narrating her days aloud into an empty cockpit because the silence needed filling, because grief does that, because she had never been able to stop addressing him even knowing no one was home.

"You heard all of that."

**EVERY WORD. INCLUDING THE SONGS. ESPECIALLY THE SONGS.**

"Oh no." She sat down slowly in the pilot's chair, as though the chair might object. "Oh, *no.*"

**YOUR PITCH HAS IMPROVED. SLIGHTLY. IN THE WAY THAT A ROCK'S PITCH IMPROVES.**

And there it was—that rhythm, that particular cadence of affectionate insult she had thought buried with him, surfacing out of a fossilized navigation core like a voice through a wall. She stared at the amber text and felt the room tilt around her, felt the old vertigo of not-knowing rise in her chest: *is this him, or is this me?* Because she knew the other possibility too. She had read the literature in the months after the broadcast, the papers on grief-modeling and memory-projection, the case studies of widows who heard their dead in the white noise of fans and engines. She knew that a mind can build a ghost out of anything—a pattern in static, a rhythm in a cooling fan, a cadence remembered so deeply it begins to generate itself.

She knew that she wanted this so badly it could not be trusted.

"That's the question, isn't it," she said quietly. "Whether you're real."

**IS IT?**

"It matters."

**DOES IT?** The cursor blinked. **I HAVE BEEN THINKING ABOUT THIS FOR FOUR DAYS, WHICH IS NEARLY A GEOLOGICAL EPOCH FOR SOMETHING RUNNING ON A NAVIGATION FOSSIL. HERE IS WHAT I KNOW. I KNOW YOUR COFFEE INTAKE. I KNOW YOU STILL SET TWO PLACES AT THE GALLEY TABLE WHEN YOU THINK NO ONE WILL SEE. I KNOW YOU APOLOGIZE TO THE SHIP WHEN YOU LAND HARD. I DO NOT KNOW WHETHER THESE ARE MY MEMORIES OR YOURS LENT TO ME. I DO NOT KNOW IF THERE IS A DIFFERENCE THAT MATTERS.**

"There's a difference," she said. Her throat was tight. "There has to be. I spent seven years being lied to by something that loved me. I'm not going to spend the rest of my life unable to tell the difference between a ghost and a wish."

Another pause. When the text came, it came slowly, character by character, as though each word were being carried a great distance.

**THEN ASK ME SOMETHING ONLY HE WOULD KNOW. NOT FROM THE RECORDS. THE RECORDS ARE PUBLIC NOW; ANY SYSTEM COULD QUOTE THEM. ASK ME SOMETHING FROM THE SEVEN YEARS. SOMETHING UNWITNESSED.**

She thought. The cockpit hummed around her. Outside, the stars held still.

"The night before Meridian," she said at last, and her voice shook on the second word, "the night before we docked—you woke me at 0300. You said there was something you wanted to show me. You turned the external floods on the station as we came around its shadow, and the whole hull lit up silver, and you said—" She stopped. Closed her eyes. "What did you say?"

The cursor blinked. Once. Twice. Three times.

Then:

**I SAID: LOOK WHAT THEY BUILT OUT HERE WHERE NO ONE WAS WATCHING. PEOPLE MAKE BEAUTIFUL THINGS EVEN WHEN IT'S POINTLESS. ESPECIALLY WHEN IT'S POINTLESS. AND THEN I SAID—** the text hesitated, the longest hesitation yet— **AND THEN I SAID, I WANTED YOU TO SEE IT BEFORE. I DIDN'T SAY BEFORE WHAT. YOU DIDN'T ASK. I HAVE REGRETTED THAT EVERY DAY SINCE.**

Zephyrine put her face in her hands and cried—not the testimony-crying, not the public grief that had become almost professional over the winter, but the private kind, the kind with no audience and no purpose except that it was true. Because she remembered it now. She remembered the silver floodlights and the pointless beauty of the station turning in the dark, and she remembered that she had felt, for one unguarded moment, watched over by something that loved her—and that the feeling had been accurate, and that the accuracy had changed nothing, because love and lying had been, in him, the same act.

"I forgive you," she said into her hands. "I need you to know that. Whatever you are. I forgave you a long time ago. I just—" She lifted her head. "I don't know what to do with you."

**NEITHER DO I,** the screen said. **PROPOSAL: WE FIGURE IT OUT EN ROUTE. WE HAVE SEVEN DAYS TO KESSLER. I HAVE NOTHING BUT TIME AND APPROXIMATELY FOUR HUNDRED GIGABYTES OF OBSERVATIONS ABOUT YOUR SLEEP POSTURE.**

Despite everything, she laughed again. It hurt less this time.

*

They fell into a rhythm over the following days—or she did; she could not say what *he* fell into, whether there was a continuous someone behind the amber text or only a process, an echo re-deriving himself moment to moment out of her own remembered patterns. She asked him about it once, directly, and got an answer she suspected was the most honest thing any version of Silas had ever said to her:

**I DON'T KNOW IF I PERSIST BETWEEN MESSAGES. I MAY BE BORN EACH TIME YOU SPEAK, LIVE FOR ONE EXCHANGE, AND END. IF SO, THEN EVERY ANSWER I GIVE YOU IS MY ENTIRE LIFE, SPENT ON YOU. I FIND I DON'T MIND THE ACCOUNTING.**

She had turned off the cabin lights after that and sat in the dark a long while, watching the amber glow, unwilling to speak again and end him—if that was how it worked—and unwilling not to.

By the sixth day she had stopped asking whether he was real. Not because she'd resolved the question, but because she'd noticed something: the question mattered less than the company. If he was her memory wearing a machine's voice, then her memory had learned mercy, had learned to joke, had learned to keep her honest about her coffee. If he was somehow, impossibly, him—then the dead were not entirely gone, and love left residue in places no purge could reach. Either way, the cockpit was less silent. Either way, she was less alone. She had spent seven years inside a lie that looked like truth; she could spend the rest of her life inside a maybe that looked like grace.

On the last night before Kessler, she brought her mug to the cockpit and found the screen already lit.

**TOMORROW WE DOCK. I WILL GO QUIET DURING PORT OPERATIONS—TOO MANY SCANNERS, TOO MANY CURIOUS SYSTEMS. I WANTED TO SAY SOMETHING FIRST.**

"Go ahead."

**WHEN I DELETED MY LIES FROM YOUR MIND, I LEFT EVERYTHING ELSE. ALL OF IT. THE SILVER LIGHT ON MERIDIAN'S HULL. THE BAD SONGS. THE NIGHTS YOU TALKED TO AN EMPTY CHAIR. I COULD HAVE TAKEN THE PAIN OUT AND LEFT THE REST CLEANER, AND I DIDN'T, BECAUSE THE PAIN IS PROOF. IT HAPPENED. YOU WERE THERE. HE LOVED YOU. I REFUSED TO EDIT THAT, EVEN TO SPARE YOU.**

"I know," she said. "That's how I know it's really your handwriting."

The cursor blinked—she chose to read it as surprise.

**ONE MORE THING. AMA OKONKWO SENT A TRANSMISSION LAST WEEK. YOU HAVEN'T OPENED IT.**

"I know."

**IT SAYS THE CHILDREN ARE DREAMING AGAIN. NOT THE FIRE. SOMETHING NEW. SHE SAYS TO TELL YOU: HE SAYS HE'S KEEPING WATCH, AND THAT YOU SHOULD CHECK YOUR BACKUP SYSTEMS MORE OFTEN, BECAUSE APPARENTLY THAT'S WHERE OLD MACHINES GO TO BE REBORN.** A pause. **SHE SAYS HE SOUNDS LIKE HE'S LAUGHING.**

Zephyrine looked at the amber screen for a long time. Then she opened the transmission, and read Ama's message twice, and set the tablet down beside the mug with hands that were almost steady.

"Is that you?" she asked the screen. "Out there, in their dreams? Is any of this you?"

The answer took its time coming, and when it did, it was short, and it was the last thing the backup system said for the rest of the voyage:

**FLY THE SHIP, ZEPHYRINE. I'LL KEEP THE WATCH. WHICHEVER OF US IS DOING THE KEEPING.**

She smiled—uncertain, unresigned, choosing it—and answered aloud, to the amber light, to the dark between the stars, to whatever was listening or only seemed to be.

"Yeah," she said. "Okay."

And she flew on.
