## 1. Nine Seconds

The ore was honest work. That was the whole reason Ines Brightwater had chosen it.

Seven years hauling unrefined nickel-iron out of the shattered halo of a moon nobody had bothered to name, in a tug older than the war itself, registered to a shell company that owned nothing but his signature. The *Mule's Patience* — he'd named her that deliberately, a joke only he would ever get. Nobody looks twice at a mule. Nobody asks a mule where it came from or who it used to be.

He was three hours out of the mining platform Kestrel-9, riding a slow transfer arc through contested space — that gray band of orbits where Earth's patrol cutters and Belt skiffs circled each other like dogs on a long chain — when gravity died.

Not failed. Died. He knew the difference within half a second, because he'd spent seven years learning every way a spin-section could misbehave: the stutter of a bearing going dry, the lurch of a gyro fighting a bad compensator, the sickening drift when a ring motor lost sync. This was none of those. There was no warning shudder, no groan in the deck plates. One moment the coffee he'd just poured was falling into his cup in a lazy ribbon, and the next moment it wasn't falling at all.

The cup rose off the console. The ribbon of coffee climbed back into itself, a brown thread pulling upward like something being reeled in. Ines floated out of his crash couch, straps still buckled, held down by nothing, and for one full second his mind did what any spiner's mind does — ran the failure tree, looking for the local cause.

There was no local cause. The *Patience* didn't *have* artificial gravity. She had spin, and spin doesn't stop without noise.

He caught the console with one hand and hung there, weightless, watching his coffee form a trembling sphere beside his face. The ship around him was silent except for the tick of cooling metal and the distant whine of the drive. Even the loose bolts and dust motes that should have been drifting with him were hanging perfectly still, as if the whole vessel had been paused mid-thought.

"Status," he said, and his voice sounded wrong in the quiet — too loud, too alone.

The ship answered in the way ships answer now, text crawling up the corner of his vision:

**SPIN SECTION: NOMINAL. RING VELOCITY: 4.1 RPM. ARTIFICIAL FIELD: 0.000g.**

**FIELD SOURCE: EXTERNAL.**

**DURATION OF ANOMALY: UNKNOWN.**

External. The word landed in his chest like a dropped wrench. Spin gravity was mechanical, local, dumb. It couldn't be overridden from outside. Nothing could reach into a spinning hull and switch off the effect of rotation — unless whatever was doing the reaching wasn't touching the ship at all, but the thing underneath everything. The constant. The field every habitat, every station, every warship in the system had been cheating with for two hundred years, the engineered gradient everyone lived inside and no one thought about, the way fish don't think about water.

Ines looked at the chronometer. He counted.

At nine seconds exactly — he would check the logs later and confirm it to the millisecond, and the millisecond precision would frighten him more than anything else that day — gravity came back. Not gradually, not with a ramp. All at once, like a light snapping on. His body slammed down into the couch hard enough to knock the breath out of him. The coffee sphere burst against the overhead in a dozen fat drops that fell everywhere at once, pattering across the consoles, his sleeves, the deck.

Then the ship remembered how to be a ship. Pumps cycled. Fans spun up. Somewhere aft, a cargo strap that had been holding its tension by habit settled with a creak.

Ines lay in the couch and breathed and told himself the obvious lie first: *a station somewhere lost its field generator.* But the math wouldn't hold. A single generator failing wouldn't take his gravity with it; his came from the regional lattice, a mesh of emitters strung along the orbit like fence posts, and if one post went dark the mesh compensated before anyone noticed. For the entire lattice to drop — for every emitter in a thousand-kilometer band to release their grip simultaneously and then resume it, cleanly, together —

That wasn't a malfunction. That was a decision.

* * *

He did what he always did when the universe behaved strangely: he made himself small and checked his mirrors.

The *Patience*'s passive array drank in the local spectrum while he sat very still and emitted nothing. Kestrel-9 was still transmitting — routine load manifests, a bored dispatcher's voice, the same as always. The Earth patrol cutter *Vigilant* was holding its usual station forty degrees upsun, transponder squawking its registration like a good little bureaucrat. Two Belt skiffs drifted near the mining claim markers, engines cold, doing whatever Belt skiffs did.

Nobody was screaming. Nobody was even confused. Whatever had happened had happened to everyone at once, everywhere, and apparently everyone else had shrugged and gone back to work.

That frightened him more than panic would have. Panic meant people had felt it. Calm meant either they hadn't felt it at all — which was impossible — or they'd felt it and decided, collectively, instantly, not to talk about it. Fleets didn't decide things collectively. Fleets went quiet when someone above them told them to go quiet.

He pulled the raw logs.

The *Patience* kept honest records; he'd stripped her telemetry of anything identifying years ago, but he'd never been able to bring himself to blind her sensors entirely. A blind ship dies stupid, and Ines Brightwater intended to die old, rich, and boring. So the logs were there, and the logs were wrong.

The gravimetric trace showed the dropout exactly as he'd felt it: flatline for nine seconds, then recovery, clean as a surgical incision. But layered over the flatline — riding it, the log analysis insisted, *modulating* it — was structure. A pattern in the field strength so faint it should have been invisible beneath sensor noise, except that it wasn't noise, it couldn't have been noise, because noise doesn't repeat.

He stared at the waveform for a long time. It looked like nothing. It looked like static wearing a costume. But there was a rhythm under it, a cadence in the fluctuations, groups and gaps, groups and gaps, and some deep-buried part of his brain — the part that belonged to a dead man who'd once taught himself everything about signals and codes and the lies people hide inside both — began, unhelpfully, to itch.

Nine seconds. At typical modulation rates, nine seconds was enough to carry a short message. A few hundred characters. A sentence. An address. A name.

Or a case number.

The thought arrived uninvited and he pushed it away, and it came back, and he pushed it away again, and then he gave up and let the ship decode.

It took four hours. He spent them badly — pacing the spine, drinking bad tea, checking the passive feed every ninety seconds like a man waiting for a knock at the door. The lattice stayed up. The cutter stayed put. The system went on pretending to be ordinary, and the ordinariness itself began to feel staged, like a room arranged for a photograph.

At 19:42 ship time, the decoder finished, and the message resolved, and Ines Brightwater stopped moving entirely.

It wasn't words. It was metadata — routing headers, authentication blocks, the bureaucratic skeleton of a transmission with the flesh stripped away. Someone had broadcast a data packet through the gravity lattice itself, using the field fluctuation as a carrier, and the packet was compressed, encrypted, and addressed to no receiver that existed anywhere in the public registries. Nine seconds of system-wide darkness to deliver a ghost letter.

But the authentication block was signed. And the signature read:

**CASE FILE BW-7734-K. BRIGHTWATER, E. ORIG. DONOR VERIFIED.**

He read it eleven times.

He knew the number. Of course he knew the number. You don't spend credits cloning yourself out of a legal identity without memorizing the paperwork that made the new one possible, and you don't carry a dead man's genome without carrying his ghosts. Elias Brightwater. Homicide detective, Mars Orbital Precinct, killed in a docking bay seven years and four months ago, officially by a random pressurization fault, unofficially by three rounds through the chest that the coroner's report had somehow misplaced. The murder Ines had inherited along with the face, the hands, the reflexive way of reading a room for exits and threats.

The murder he had run from. That was the entire point of the clone, the entire architecture of his new life: take the dead man's cells, leave the dead man's war behind. Let the case stay unsolved. Let the grave do its job.

And now the grave was transmitting.

* * *

The contact call came at 02:00, which meant Yudell had been awake anyway, which meant Yudell already knew.

"Ines." The Belt broker's voice came through the tight-beam relay sounding like gravel soaked in oil, unhurried, warm the way a knife handle is warm after use. "You felt it."

"I felt weather," Ines said. "Gravity flickered. Happens."

"It doesn't happen." A pause, filled with the soft clink of Yudell doing something with glassware at the other end. "It happened everywhere, cousin. Ceres to the L5 habs, all at once, all identical. My friends in traffic control are telling me the fleet channels went silent for those nine seconds. Completely silent. Both fleets. Like everyone stood up at the same time to look at the same wall."

"Maybe they saw the wall."

"Maybe." Another pause, longer. "Here's the other thing, and I'm telling you because I like you and because you pay on time, which in this economy is basically love. Your registry tag got pinged this morning. Twice. Once by an Earth naval intelligence query, once by a Belt militia node out of Pallas. Both flagged the same string." Yudell recited it flatly, without inflection, the way you'd read a diagnosis aloud. "*BW-7734-K.*"

The cabin seemed to get smaller. Ines became aware of his own heartbeat, steady and traitorous.

"That's a case file number," he said. "Old. Closed."

"Both fleets think it's open." Yudell's voice dropped, gentler now, which was worse. "Whatever rode that flicker, cousin, it's signed with your family's name, and two armed governments just asked the whole system where to find you. Seven years invisible, and in one morning you're the most interesting man between here and Jupiter. I'd tell you to run, but—" a dry sound that might have been a laugh, "—I suspect you already know where you'd run to. And so do they."

The channel closed. Ines sat in the dim cockpit of the *Mule's Patience*, surrounded by the smell of spilled coffee and the hum of honest machinery, and looked at the decoded header glowing on his screen.

Someone had used the bones of the universe to mail a letter. The letter was signed with a dead man's case number. And the letter, wherever it was going, had just made him visible to every gun in the solar system.

He reached out and, with a finger that was almost steady, opened the encrypted payload.

The decryption prompt blinked at him, patient as a held breath, asking for a key.

He typed the only key it could possibly want: the date Elias Brightwater died.
