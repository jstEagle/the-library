## 5. The Long Way to Ceres

The order came through at 0347 ship-time, wrapped in the kind of encryption that meant someone above her pay grade was frightened.

*PRIORITY FLASH — Voss, M., Lt. Cmdr. — Subject: Brightwater asset. Locate, secure, or terminate. No negotiation. Acknowledge by return.*

Mara read it three times in the dark of her cabin aboard the *Corvus*, letting the words settle like sediment in water. *Terminate.* They hadn't even dressed it up this time. No "detain for questioning," no "render to fleet custody." Just a flat verb with a body attached to it, and her name beside it.

She thought about Ines Brightwater's hands on his own console — steady, unhurried, the hands of a man who had spent seven years learning not to flinch. She thought about the decoded fragment glowing on his screen before she'd made herself look away and pretend she'd seen nothing: a case number, a dead detective's signature, coordinates that pointed at Earth's own admiralty like an accusation written in bone.

She acknowledged the order. What else was there to do? Refusal was a career-ending event; worse, it was a flag that would follow her into every system she ever docked in. So she typed the acknowledgment, watched it crawl away toward Earth at lightspeed, and then sat alone in the dark for a long time, listening to the *Corvus* breathe.

*You serve Earth,* she told herself. *Not them. Not necessarily them.*

The distinction felt thinner every day.

* * *

By 0600 she was in the intelligence bay, running the search pattern she'd been assigned, and lying with every line of it.

The tasking gave her a search cone based on Brightwater's last known vector out of Tycho Drift — which was wrong, and she knew it was wrong, because she had been the one standing in his cargo hold when he'd told her, in the fluent language of freight manifests and customs codes, exactly where he wasn't going. He'd lied to her beautifully. She'd let him. And now she was going to build a monument to that lie and hand it to the people who wanted him dead.

She pulled the drift traffic data from the relay buoys — legitimate work, the kind any competent officer would do — and began constructing a plausible trail. A hauler like his burned fuel on a schedule; she could reverse-engineer his consumption profile, project a course, salt it with just enough genuine uncertainty to make it credible. The trick wasn't making the false trail perfect. It was making it imperfect in believable ways.

"Voss." Ensign Okonkwo's voice from the doorway. "Admiralty liaison wants an update by end of shift."

"They'll have one." She didn't turn around. "Tell them we're narrowing the cone."

Okonkwo lingered. She could feel him weighing something. "Ma'am — is it true the asset's a clone? Of the detective?"

"The file says so."

"My uncle served under Brightwater. The original. Back on the Ceres vice detail." A pause. "Said he was the only honest cop in the Belt."

"Then somebody should tell the admiralty," Mara said, "because they're about to spend a lot of money proving otherwise."

He left. She stared at the plot display, at the elegant fan of projected vectors spreading out from Tycho Drift, and understood that she had just crossed a line she couldn't see behind her anymore. Every hour she delayed, every coordinate she bent, was a decision. There was no neutral ground left on the map. There was only the direction you leaned.

She leaned, and fed the admiralty a course that would take their strike wing chasing ghosts through four days of empty rock toward a debris field where no one had ever been.

* * *

The lie required maintenance. That was the thing nobody told you about treason — it wasn't a single act, it was a garden, and it needed watering twice a day.

She falsified a sensor log to corroborate the ghost trail. She "lost" forty minutes of buoy telemetry to a fabricated solar interference window. She drafted an interim report so dense with qualified language — *probable, consistent with, pending confirmation* — that it would take a board of inquiry a week to extract anything actionable from it. By the third day, the strike wing was committed to the debris field, and the *Corvus*'s official position was that the Brightwater asset had most likely gone dark somewhere in the Mars-Jupiter corridor.

In reality, if her reconstruction of his habits held, he was doing the one thing both fleets feared: taking the long way to Ceres. Slow burns, cold coasting, hugging the rock shadows where no warship dared follow without a chart. It was what she would do, in his place. It was what he had already done once, seven years ago, when he'd first become nobody.

Which raised the question she kept circling like a vulture over a wreck: why did she care what he did?

She ran the standard self-assessment protocols during her off-shift, because that was procedure, because procedure was the last clean thing she owned. *Subject exhibits no coercion indicators. No financial irregularities. No ideological affiliation on record.* All true. Which left only the uncomfortable category the forms didn't have a box for: she had looked at the evidence fragment for eleven seconds, and in those eleven seconds she had seen the shape of the machine she worked for, and she had never been able to unsee it.

Earth had started the war. Not the Belt. Not the miners, not the militias, not the outer colonies with their grievances and their bombs. Her own admiralty had manufactured the first attack, murdered the one man who knew, and then spent seven years and forty thousand lives laundering the lie into history.

And now they wanted her to kill the messenger.

"You serve Earth," she said aloud to her cabin, testing the old liturgy against the new knowledge. It came back sounding different. Smaller. Like a uniform that no longer fit but that she didn't know how to take off, because underneath it there was just Mara, and Mara had been a naval officer since she was seventeen, and without the uniform she wasn't sure anything remained.

* * *

On the fifth day, the garden needed more than water. It needed blood.

Commander Reyes — admiralty liaison, political animal, a man whose smile arrived half a second before the rest of his face — requested a private channel. His image resolved on her cabin display wearing the expression of a colleague sharing a confidence.

"Lieutenant Commander. Your search's going slow."

"We're being thorough."

"Thorough." He let the word sit there, decaying. "We've had a second source on the asset's heading. Independent of your cone. You understand what that means."

She understood exactly what it meant: they had another hunter on Brightwater, and her false trail was about to be rendered irrelevant, and everything she'd risked would be for nothing. Her pulse didn't change. Seven years of reading liars had taught her that the face was just weather; the truth lived in timing.

"It means your second source is better than my sensor suite," she said evenly. "I'd want to know who, before I burn fuel chasing their conclusions."

"I'm sure you would." The smile widened by a degree. "Here's my problem, Commander Voss. Your reports are very good. Almost too good. Lots of hedging. Lots of lost telemetry. And your inspection of the Brightwater vessel — the one where you found nothing?" He tapped something off-screen. "The hold's manifest showed a two-hour gap in environmental cycling. Someone vented and re-pressurized a compartment while you were aboard. Care to guess which one?"

Her stomach dropped through the deck and kept falling. He had her. He might not be able to prove what she'd done, but he could make her life a tribunal, and tribunals didn't need proof, they needed momentum.

"Venting cycles are routine," she said. "Dust management in the cargo bays."

"Mm." He studied her the way she'd studied Brightwater — looking for the seam. Then, unexpectedly, he leaned closer to his pickup, and his voice dropped out of official register entirely. "Between us, Commander. The order stands. Capture or kill. But there's a third option nobody writes down. Some assets stop mattering if they simply... aren't found in time. If the trail goes cold long enough, the political calculus changes, and everyone quietly hopes the problem solves itself out past the Belt." A pause. "Of course, an officer who engineered such an outcome would still be guilty of everything. So I'd advise against it. Strongly."

The channel closed.

Mara sat motionless for a full minute, replaying it. Reyes knew. He had to know — the venting gap, the hedged reports, all of it assembled into a picture he found either damning or useful. And instead of arresting her, he'd handed her a knife and told her, in the same breath, not to use it.

Which meant Reyes wasn't loyal to the admiralty either. He was loyal to whoever inside the admiralty had ordered the hit — and if that faction wanted Brightwater gone quietly, then someone else inside the fleet wanted him found loudly, and Mara had just become terrain in a war she hadn't known she was fighting.

Or — colder possibility — Reyes was testing her, and the moment she acted on his hint, she'd hang herself with her own rope.

Either way, the clock had started. Her false trail was dying. Somewhere out in the dark, other hunters were closing on a real heading, and the only person between Ines Brightwater and a kill order was a woman who couldn't explain, even to herself, why she kept choosing him.

She pulled up the navigation plot and looked at the long, empty arc toward Ceres. Neutral ground. The archive. The one place in the system where evidence, once delivered, became public forever — unownable, unkillable, immune to tribunals and admirals and men like Reyes.

If he reached it, everything ended: the war, the lie, her career, her country as she'd understood it.

If he reached it.

Mara opened a channel she shouldn't have had the codes for — an old intelligence backdoor, one she'd sworn never to touch — and addressed it to a hauler transponder that officially didn't exist. Her fingers hovered over the input for ten seconds. Twenty.

Then she typed six words:

*They have your heading. Run.*

She sent it, wiped the buffer, and sat in the hum of the *Corvus*, waiting to find out whether she'd just saved a man or signed her own court-martial — and whether the answer would arrive as a thank-you, or as the first warning shot of the ships now burning toward Tycho Drift, where the Belt militia had just taken a lone ore hauler into custody.
