## 7. The Price of the Message

The message from Earth arrived the way all dangerous things did: politely, and on schedule.

Ines was three days out of Tycho Drift, running dark on a long parabolic coast toward Ceres, when his comm board lit up with a handshake protocol he hadn't seen in seven years. Earth Fleet standard. Priority flag. A channel that shouldn't have known his ship's name, let alone his heading.

He let it ring for an hour. Then, because ignoring it wouldn't make it stop existing, he opened a relay through a dead mining buoy and answered.

The face that resolved out of the static was older than Ines expected. Silver at the temples, a jaw like a bulkhead, the kind of calm that came from never having been afraid of anything in a very long time. The uniform had no name tape.

"Mr. Brightwater." The man's voice was warm the way engine exhaust was warm. "Or should I say Detective Brightwater? I understand the resemblance is remarkable."

"You have me at a disadvantage."

"Admiral Corren Hale. Judge Advocate General's office — though today I'm speaking as a friend of the court." He folded his hands on a desk that looked real enough to be aboard a ship in high orbit over Earth itself, which was either vanity or theater. "You're carrying something that doesn't belong to you. We'd like to discuss its return."

"The last people who discussed my original's work with him didn't use words."

Hale's smile didn't move a millimeter. "Detective Brightwater was a tragedy. I won't insult you by pretending otherwise. But you're not him, are you? You're a hauler. You haul ore. You've spent seven years being no one, and you're very good at it." He leaned forward slightly, and the camera caught the grain of the desk, the honest wear on its surface. "We're offering to keep you exactly what you are."

Ines said nothing. On the wall behind him, the decoded fragment sat in cold storage, encrypted six ways, whispering nothing.

"Full immunity," Hale continued. "Retroactive. Whatever the fleets think they saw at Tycho Drift, whatever flags exist on your registry — gone. Your genetic donor's case number gets sealed as a closed investigation, natural causes, tragic accident. And a new identity. Not a clone-registry scrub — a *real* one. New name, new biometrics, new history. A man who has never been anyone's copy. You could dock at Luna Station and walk down the promenade and not one sensor in the system would know your face."

"And the evidence?"

"Comes home. To us. Where it will be handled by the proper institutions, with the oversight and discretion such material requires." Hale spread his hands, a gesture of magnificent reasonableness. "Ines. May I call you Ines? Think about what you actually want. You don't want a war crimes tribunal. You don't want your name in every feed from here to Jupiter. You want your ship, your silence, and your nine hours of sleep between burns. We're offering you all three, forever, and all it costs is handing a dead man's grief back to the living."

"That's quite a pitch."

"I've had practice." The warmth drained out of Hale's voice by a single degree, just enough to show the machinery underneath. "I'll be candid, because you strike me as a man who respects candor. If you refuse, this becomes complicated. Complicated things happen to people in contested orbits. Accidents. Depressurizations. The Belt will tell you we killed them; we'll tell you they killed you. Nobody will ever check, because nobody checks anything out here anymore." He paused. "But I'd rather not. I'd rather write you a clean bill and forget you existed. So: seventy-two hours. The channel stays open."

The screen went dark.

Ines sat in the pilot's chair for a long time, listening to the ship breathe. The offer was good. That was the obscene part of it — it was *good*. Immunity, anonymity, a life where no one ever again looked at him and saw a dead detective's face looking back. Seven years of invisibility, gift-wrapped, in exchange for one encrypted package.

And somewhere under the ice of Earth's admiralty, the men who had manufactured the first Belt attack would go on reviewing fleet budgets, promoting each other, shaking hands at ceremonies while the war they'd built burned through the Belt like a slow fire.

*Handled by the proper institutions.* His original had probably heard those exact words, once, shortly before dying.

Ines pulled up the fragment, watched the checksums hold steady, and did not answer the channel. Seventy-two hours was a long time. He set the timer anyway, because a countdown made the choice feel less like surrender and more like arithmetic.

Then the Belt called.

* * *

They didn't use a handshake protocol. They used a hijacked weather satellite and a voice that sounded like gravel soaked in kerosene, and they didn't bother pretending to be anyone official.

"Brightwater." The voice filled his cabin without asking permission. "This is Oyelaran. Tycho Militia Command. You'll remember my people were rude to you."

"They put a gun against my spine. It was memorable."

"Good. Then you'll believe me when I say I can do worse." A pause, and when the voice resumed it had softened into something almost paternal, which was worse than the gun. "But I'm not calling to threaten you. I'm calling to make you a hero."

Ines laughed before he could stop himself. It came out ugly.

"You find that funny?"

"I find it familiar. Earth offered me the same thing this morning. Different packaging."

"Earth offers you a cage and calls it a sanctuary. We're offering you something else." Oyelaran let the silence stretch. "Broadcast the fragment. All of it — the admiralty's fingerprints, the manufactured attack, the false-flag signatures. Every byte. Put it on every open channel in the Belt, unencrypted, so no one can ever claim it was forged. Do that, and you'll never run another cargo in your life unless you want to. The Belt will carry you. Stations will open their docks to you that have been closed to Earth ships for a decade. Your original died trying to give us this truth. You can finish what he started, and be remembered for it."

"And if I broadcast everything?" Ines kept his voice flat. "Not just the half that damns Earth. All of it. Including the parts about your side."

The silence this time had teeth.

"What parts?"

"The parts where Belt intelligence saw the attack coming and let it happen. Where militia commanders traded warning data for weapons contracts. Where the martyrs of Ceres Junction were left exposed because moving them would have compromised an operation." He'd spent two nights pulling those threads out of the fragment, and every one of them had felt like touching a live wire. "Your heroes knew. Some of them profited. That's in there too, Oyelaran. It's all in there."

Another silence. Longer. When the voice came back, the paternal warmth was gone entirely, and Ines understood he was now talking to the thing underneath it.

"You'd shame the Belt. During a war. While our stations burn."

"I'd tell the truth during a war. Apparently that's a novel concept on both sides."

"Careful, Brightwater." Gravel and kerosene, and something colder beneath. "Half the truth ends the war and makes you a father of the peace. All of it ends the war and makes you an enemy of everyone who fought it. Choose carefully which martyrdom appeals to you." A breath. "Seventy-two hours. Same clock Earth gave you — yes, we know about their channel. Everyone knows about everyone's channels now. That's what your dead detective did with his little broadcast."

The connection died.

Ines floated in the quiet afterward, the timer counting down in the corner of his vision: sixty-nine hours, forty-one minutes. Two offers, mirror images. Earth wanted him to bury the truth and live free. The Belt wanted him to bury half of it and live famous. Both of them, in their different dialects, were saying the same sentence: *let the corruption stand, and we will let you go.*

It was tempting. That was the thing nobody warned you about temptation — how reasonable it sounded in your own voice. He could take the new identity. He could take the hero's welcome. Either way, he woke up tomorrow as someone other than the man holding a dead detective's unfinished case, and wasn't that what he'd wanted since the vat?

His original had faced a version of this too, Ines realized. Not offers — threats. But the same fork in the road: carry the whole truth and die for it, or carve off a survivable piece and live smaller. The detective had chosen whole. Had died whole. And then, with the last of his ingenuity, had rigged a nine-second flicker across the entire gravity grid of the solar system just to make sure the whole of it survived him.

Ines looked at his own hands. Someone else's hands, technically. Someone else's stubbornness wired into them.

"No," he said aloud, to the empty cabin, to both the silent channels, to the admirals and the militia commanders and the dead man whose face he wore. "I'm not selling it. Any of it."

He began plotting the burn to Ceres — the long way, through the debris shoals where no fleet liked to follow — and was halfway through the course calculation when his proximity alarm sang one soft, rising note.

A ship had just come out of stealth four hundred kilometers off his bow. Small, fast, Earth hull geometry.

And riding its transponder, coded in a fleet cipher that should have been retired years ago, was a callsign he recognized from the inspection that had nearly ended him weeks ago.

*Voss.*

The channel light blinked green. Waiting.
