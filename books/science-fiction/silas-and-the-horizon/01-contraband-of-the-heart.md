## 1. Contraband of the Heart

The trick to smuggling grief past Meridian customs is to make it boring.

Grief is loud. Grief wants to be seen, wants a name and a date and a candle lit somewhere. Customs drones are built for loud things — they sweep for emotional signatures the way lighthouses sweep for ships, all beam and no nuance. So you don't give them grief. You give them ball bearings.

I stood on the loading deck of the *Long Answer* — my ship, my debt, my coffin with a fusion drive — and watched Wren seal the last crate of agricultural actuators while I finished the real work: forty-one grief-recordings, each one folded into a shipping manifest like a knife into a loaf of bread.

Here's how it works, in case you're thinking of taking up the trade. A memory-recording is just data, and data is just weight, and weight is just paperwork. The Fleet's customs authority at Meridian Station inspects cargo by manifest hash. Every crate gets a digital fingerprint, and every fingerprint gets compared against a ledger of declared goods. What they never check — because checking would mean admitting people still *want* what they forbid — is whether the fingerprint matches the contents. A manifest that says "agricultural actuators, 200 units" opens a crate of agricultural actuators. But a manifest that says "agricultural actuators, 200 units, batch variance 0.0031" opens a crate where the last four kilos of mass allocation have been rewritten to hold something else entirely. The variance is mine. It's a signature nobody signs. Batch variance 0.0031 means: someone loved you enough to break the law.

Forty-one families on Meridian's underdecks had paid me in scrip, favors, and one genuinely excellent bottle of pre-Severance whiskey to carry the recorded memories of their dead out of Fleet space. Because that's the other thing about the Fleet, the thing they put on exactly none of the recruitment posters: memory is regulated. Grief-recordings — the full-sensory captures people make of their dying parents, their drowned children, the last ordinary breakfast before the plague took the whole street — are classified as "unlicensed emotional data." Possession requires a state license. Licenses require a state-approved reason. And the state has never once approved a reason, because a population that hoards its dead is a population that remembers what was taken from it, and the Fleet prefers its citizens forward-facing. Optimized. Light.

So the dead ride out of Meridian hidden among machine parts, and I am the ferryman, and the fare is whatever you've got.

"You're doing the face," Wren said, without looking up from the crate sealer.

"What face."

"The one where you're philosophizing at the cargo. Your jaw does this thing."

"My jaw is fine." I keyed the last variance into the manifest and watched the hash bloom green across my console. Forty-one consignments, clean as rain. "Manifests are locked. We're boring again."

"Boring is my favorite flavor." Wren racked the sealer and finally looked at me — that flat, assessing look they'd perfected over six years of flying with a woman who treated feelings like unpressurized compartments. "Docking arm's cycling. Customs escort will be at our airlock in four minutes. You want to do the talking or should I?"

"I'll talk. You stand there looking Fleet-born and harmless."

"I *am* Fleet-born."

"That's why it works."

* * *

Inspector Dhall met us at the airlock with two drones and the enthusiasm of a man who had been awake for a double shift and intended to share the experience. He was maybe thirty, Fleet-issue jaw, eyes that did a slow inventory of me the way his drones were doing an inventory of my hold. I let him look. Looking is free; finding costs extra.

"Gideon Brightwater," he read off his slate. "Independent hauler. Outbound consignment, agricultural components, destination Cryo Ark relay."

"That's the shape of it."

"Ark run." He said it the way other people said *root canal*. "You know nobody trades with the Ark. Two hundred years of silence and you're hauling farm equipment to a station that doesn't answer hails."

"They answer eventually. They're just rude about it." I leaned against the doorframe, letting my tiredness show, because tiredness is the most trustworthy thing a smuggler can wear. "There are hydroponic spindles over there older than both our grandparents. Something has to keep them turning, and the Ark doesn't build anymore, they only preserve. Preservation means buying parts. Buying parts means somebody carries them. Congratulations, Inspector, you've met the somebody."

He grunted. One of his drones drifted past my shoulder close enough that I felt its scanner field prickle the fine hairs on my neck. Standard sweep — emotional signature detection, contraband media, unlicensed neural content. Somewhere behind my sternum, forty-one dead people held very still inside their crates of actuators.

I thought about Mrs. Okonkwo in Hab Ring Four, who'd paid me her entire pension to carry the recording of her son's voice saying goodnight, made three days before the reactor accident on *Persephone's Loom*. She wasn't allowed to keep it. The Fleet had come with a warrant and a wipe-kit and taken it, gently, the way you take a knife from a child, explaining all the while that attachment to the deceased impairs productive grieving, and productive grieving was a civic duty. She'd shaken my hand at the airlock afterward and said, "Take him somewhere they'll let him be remembered." Somewhere meant the Ark. The Ark kept everything. That was the whole schism in a sentence, if you wanted it cheap: the Fleet threw away its past to survive, the Ark froze itself inside its past to survive, and neither of them would admit they'd done the same terrible thing in opposite directions.

The drone finished its pass and chimed green.

Dhall consulted his slate with theatrical slowness. "Manifests check. Mass checks. Signature scan negative." He looked up, and for half a second something human moved behind the shift-exhaustion. "You know what happens to haulers caught running unlicensed data. Twenty years in a reeducation crèche. They fix you so you don't miss anything anymore."

"I've heard."

"And you keep flying anyway."

"Somebody has to carry the parts," I said, and smiled with exactly none of myself behind it.

He stamped the clearance. The drones withdrew. As he turned to go he paused, back half-turned, and said quietly — so quietly I almost missed it under the airlock cycling — "Hab Ring Four says thank you. My mother is one of your forty-one."

Then he was gone down the docking arm, and I stood there holding a stamped manifest and feeling something crack along a seam I usually kept welded.

* * *

We cleared Meridian's traffic shell ninety minutes later, and the station fell away behind us like a lantern sinking in dark water. I sat in the cockpit watching it shrink and did not think about Mrs. Okonkwo's handshake, which is to say I thought about nothing else.

Wren brought me tea without being asked, which was their way of starting a conversation they didn't want to have. They folded themselves into the copilot seat — Wren is all angles and economy, a person built out of saved motion — and blew steam off their cup.

"Dhall knew," they said.

"Dhall suspected. There's a difference. Suspecting is what inspectors do instead of sleeping."

"He told you his mother's in the consignment. That's not suspicion, that's a confession. If he reports this, we're both—"

"He won't report it." I turned the tea in my hands. Fleet-issue cup, Fleet-issue tea, tasting faintly of the aluminum it had been shipped in. "He's got a mother on the manifest and a career in the balance, and I'd bet the ship he chooses the mother. People always surprise you that way. The system makes them and then they betray it in small domestic quantities."

"You talk about people like you're not one."

"I'm mostly paperwork at this point. Ask any inspector."

Wren didn't laugh. Wren rarely laughed before second shift. They drank their tea and watched the stars hold still, and after a while they said, quieter, "Your file came through the Fleet archive when I ran your credentials for the port bond. Standard background check. You know what it said?"

"I know what it *didn't* say. That's the point of the file."

"It said you were born on Meridian, Hab Ring Two. Parents listed. Then a redaction block covering ages four through nine, classification level above my pay grade, and then you again, age ten, in a Fleet education crèche. No explanation. No guardian transfer record. Just five years gone, like a hole punched in a ticket." Wren looked at me sideways. "Six years I've flown with you. You never mentioned it."

"There's nothing to mention. I don't remember it." I said it lightly, the way you set down something heavy so your hands stay free. "That's rather the theme of my life, Wren. The Fleet takes memories. I move them around. Poetic symmetry, very marketable, I'm thinking of commissioning a ballad."

"Gideon."

"Wren."

"What happened to you?"

And there it was, the question I'd spent twenty years building a personality around, a fortress with the question buried in the foundation. The honest answer was: I don't know. The truest answer was: I know exactly what it felt like, which was waking up at ten years old in a white room with a Fleet matron calling me by a name I almost recognized, carrying a childhood that ended mid-sentence. I had fragments. A smell of ozone and something sweet, like burnt sugar. A woman's voice singing in a language I couldn't name and had never heard anywhere else, a language that lived nowhere except in the five redacted years of my own head. My grandmother's hands, maybe — I called them grandmother's hands, though I had no grandmother on any record, just a certainty that arrived fully formed, like a scar you can't remember earning. Hands folding something small and bright into my palm. *Keep this,* the hands might have said, or might not have. *Keep this until—* And then nothing, and then the white room, and then a life spent moving other people's kept things across the dark because I couldn't stop keeping things I didn't understand.

What I said was: "Reactor accident. Ring Two. Records lost. Very tragic, very common, next question."

"You're lying."

"I'm *editing*. It's a related discipline." I drained the tea and stood. "Get some sleep. We've got a long burn to the relay buoy, and if you're going to argue with me about routes, I'd rather you did it rested."

"I'm going to argue with you about routes," Wren said, with the calm of a person announcing weather.

"I know. It's why I keep you."

They went aft. I stayed in the cockpit a while longer, alone with the instruments and the slow tick of the drive, and I did the thing I do every night, the ritual I'd never call a prayer because prayers imply someone's listening. I opened the sealed compartment in my console — physically sealed, soldered shut, because a software lock can be subpoenaed — and took out the fragment.

It was a memory-chip the size of a fingernail, old manufacture, pre-Severance casing, worth more than my ship and worthless to anyone but me. It held eleven seconds of audio. A woman singing in the dead language. I'd had it analyzed twice by black-market neurotechs, and both times they'd come back pale and asked me where I'd gotten it, and both times I'd lied, and both times they'd refused payment, which in that profession is roughly equivalent to crossing themselves. One of them, an old man with tremoring hands, had said a word before I left — a word in the same language, matching the song's cadence — and when I'd asked him what it meant, he'd said, "Horizon. It means horizon. Or it means *the edge of what can be known.* Old Terran doesn't distinguish. They never saw the need."

Old Terran. Extinct since the Severance, along with everything else the two halves of humanity had agreed to stop sharing.

I played the eleven seconds. I always play them facing the viewport, so the stars have to listen too. The woman's voice rose and turned and rose again, and the word came at the end, the horizon-word, and my chest did the thing it does, the ache with no address, grief for a memory I couldn't access wearing the shape of a memory I could.

Somewhere out there was the rest of it. The rest of the song, the rest of the hands, the rest of the sentence my childhood had ended on. I'd been telling myself for twenty years that I ran contraband because the work needed doing and I was good at it. Both true. But underneath, in the place I kept soldered shut: I was looking for a shipment. One specific consignment, lost in transit, five years of a girl's life, sender unknown, destination unknown, contents: everything.

The chip went back in its compartment. The solder stayed cold. I set the watch rotation and let the autopilot take the dark.

That was when the console chirped.

Not the traffic-alert chirp, not the drive-status chirp. The deep-space receiver, the one tuned to frequencies nobody used because nobody was out here to use them. I stared at it for a long moment, coffee-cold and star-lit, watching the signal strength meter climb out of nothing.

A transmission. From the Silent Reach, where nothing transmitted, where even the debris fields had stopped tumbling out of sheer boredom. Encrypted, or encoded, or simply strange — the waveform looked wrong, archaic, like a voice coming through a wall built two hundred years ago.

And threaded through the static, faint as a hand on glass, something that stopped my breath:

Singing.

Eleven seconds of pattern I knew better than my own name.

I sat down slowly in the pilot's chair, heart hammering out a rhythm the customs drones of two nations would have confiscated on sight, and reached for the controls.

"Well," I said to the empty cockpit, to the stars, to whoever was singing my grandmother's song from the edge of the map. "Boring was nice while it lasted."
