## 9. Truce Expiry

The ceasefire clock lived in the corner of my HUD like a debt collector who'd learned patience. Four hours, eleven minutes. It ticked down in fleet-standard time, indifferent to the fact that both armadas were already inbound, their drive plumes blooming across the long-range display like a pair of bruises going black.

"They're not even pretending to wait for the deadline," Voss said from the copilot's station. She'd stopped pretending a lot of things in the last six hours. Her uniform jacket was unbuttoned. Her voice had the flat calm of a woman who had already done her grieving and was now doing her math.

"Neither am I," I said, and fed power to the array.

The Meridian relay's broadcast array was a cathedral of scaffolding and dead ambition, and I was trying to raise it from the dead with a hand terminal and spite. The salvagers had stripped the primary uplink before we'd run them off, but they'd left the backbone intact—Lattice hardware, my mother's hardware, built to last past the heat death of anyone's patience. It wanted a handshake protocol I hadn't seen since I was eleven years old, sitting cross-legged on a lab floor while Ione Nystrom hummed off-key and traced signal paths in the air.

*Left hand, then right,* she'd said. *The lattice listens in the order you'd wave goodbye.*

I waved goodbye to my mother's machine. Somewhere in the dark, nine hundred kilometers of relay woke up one vertebra at a time.

"Signal's climbing," Voss said. "Twelve percent. Fleet sensors will see it inside the hour."

"Then we'd better have something worth seeing by then."

The archive sat in my terminal's buffer, terabytes of Ione—her voice, her reasoning, her horror, recorded in the last months of her life when she'd understood what Lattice had become. The weapon data I'd already burned. Nine years of gravity-manipulation schematics, gone in a white-hot purge that had made the relay's cooling system scream. What remained was the confession. The proof that the truce—the sacred, exhausted, load-bearing truce that kept two fleets from turning this system into a graveyard—had been engineered not as peace but as a lid. Both sides had the weapon. Both sides knew. And the ceasefire existed so that neither would ever have to admit it out loud.

Peace as mutually assured embarrassment. It would have been funny if it weren't the only thing standing between forty thousand people and vacuum.

* * *

They came early. Of course they came early. Deadlines are for people who believe in rules.

The Concordat vanguard dropped out of translation at 0340, six hours before expiry, and the Assembly answer came forty minutes later, and suddenly the display looked like two fists closing around the relay. Voss's channel lit up with priority traffic—her name, her fleet code, orders wrapped in courtesy.

"Commander Okonkwo wants me to break off and return to the *Verity*," she said. "He also wants to know why a Meridian array is warming up under an unauthorized handshake."

"What did you tell him?"

"That I was investigating." She almost smiled. "It's technically true. It's the only thing I've said that's been technically true since I boarded your ship."

The array hit thirty percent. I could feel the relay's bones humming through the deck plates, a frequency I'd known in utero, probably. My mother's machine, remembering its purpose. Except its purpose had never been weapons. Its purpose had been conversation—a network that could whisper across dead space, that could let isolated stations talk when everything else failed. They'd taken a telephone and turned it into a fist. The oldest story in the fleet.

"Ansel." Voss turned her chair. "When this goes out, there's no version where you walk away clean. The Concordat will want you for destabilizing the truce. The Assembly will want you for the same reason with different adjectives. I can't protect you from that. I can barely protect me."

"I know."

"I need you to say you understand the cost. Not for the record. For me. I'm about to spend my career, and I'd like to know the person I'm spending it on isn't planning to flinch."

I looked at her—this woman who'd boarded my ship with a warrant and a smile, who'd lied to her commander, who'd docked with me in the long quiet and told me about her brother lost in an "impossible accident" that I now knew had Lattice's fingerprints all over it. We'd been chasing the same ghost from opposite ends of the same corridor.

"I understand the cost," I said. "I've been paying it since I was eleven. I'm just finally getting the receipt."

* * *

The salvagers came back at 0450. Of course they did. Nothing in this system dies except the truth.

Their cutter slid into relay shadow with its transponder dark, and I watched four contacts separate from it on EVA thrusters, heading for the maintenance lock we'd already welded once. Voss saw it the same moment I did.

"They're after the archive."

"They're after *anything*," I said. "Their waiver's forged, their employer is one of the fleet intelligence shops, and if they grab a copy of the buffer, the evidence becomes leverage instead of light."

"How long to broadcast?"

"Array's at forty-one percent. Forty minutes if nothing else breaks."

"Something's going to break," Voss said, and started pulling on a vac suit.

I should have told her no. I should have said *stay on the ship, this is my ghost, my mother, my mess.* But she was already checking her seals, and I was already checking mine, and somewhere between us there had stopped being a line where her war ended and mine began.

We met them at the junction hub, in a tangle of conduit and handrails that my mother had probably walked, and it went wrong immediately, the way things do. They weren't salvagers, not really—their movements had that drilled quality, ex-military contractors playing at scrapping. The lead one had a coil gun and no patience.

"Hand over the buffer," he said. "Walk away. Nobody's contract covers killing a fleet auditor."

"Nobody's contract covers this whole operation," Voss said, "and yet."

He shot her.

Not to kill—to drop her, a coil round through the meat of her shoulder that spun her into the handrail, and the sound she made went through me like a hull breach. And I did the thing I'd spent twelve years not doing. I stopped being invisible.

I've hauled cargo through contested orbits for a decade, and the one universal law is that mass wins. I put two hundred kilos of emergency coolant canister into the junction at a dead run, and physics did the rest. The lead contractor went down under it. The others hesitated—hesitation being the tax you pay when your violence meets someone else's desperation—and that bought me the thirty seconds to seal the hub bulkhead with an emergency weld I will be apologizing to the relay's next maintenance crew for.

Voss was sitting against the conduit, hand pressed to her shoulder, blood between her fingers, laughing in a thin, shocked way.

"Technically true," she said. "I told him nobody's contract covered it. I never said I wouldn't get shot."

"Shut up and let me look at it."

"Array status."

"Forty-eight percent. Voss—"

"Array status," she repeated, "and then you can look at it. Priorities, Nystrom. My mother was an adjuster too."

She wasn't, as it turned out. But I understood the shape of the lie, and I let her keep it, and I clamped the wound and we moved.

* * *

Sixty-one percent when the first long-range shots started falling.

Not at the relay—at each other. The Concordat and the Assembly had arranged themselves into the classic pre-battle geometry, two crescents facing off across the dark, and someone's trigger discipline had cracked, or been cracked deliberately, and now the void between the fleets was filling with the light of things burning. The ceasefire clock read two hours and change, but clocks are for people who believe in rules.

"They're going to fight," Voss said quietly. "Even if we broadcast. They've spent too long building toward it. The truce was the excuse; the war was always the plan."

"Then we make the plan expensive." I watched the array climb. "Nobody votes for a war over a weapon that's been publicly exposed and publicly destroyed. The weapon was the secret. Take away the secret, and it's just two broke fleets with a fuel bill."

"And if they decide the exposure itself is the casus belli?"

"Then we're already dead and it doesn't matter." I said it lightly. It didn't come out lightly. Nothing had come out lightly since nine seconds of falling had ended my life as an invisible woman.

The array hit eighty percent. My mother's voice sat in the buffer, waiting. I'd listened to the opening of her testimony four times now, and every time it started the same way—*If you're hearing this, you found the lattice, which means you're either family or a thief, and honestly the distinction has gotten blurry*—and every time it made my chest hurt in a way I didn't have a claims category for.

"Ansel." Voss's voice had changed. I turned.

On the display, a new contact. Small, fast, running dark until it wasn't—the *Verity*'s cutter, Okonkwo's own, burning straight for the relay. And behind it, on the fleet channel, a priority order with Voss's name on it, unencrypted, which meant it was meant to be heard.

*Auditor Voss. You are ordered to withdraw and surrender the relay to Concordat custody. The Meridian archive is classified under Emergency Statute Nine. Failure to comply will be treated as defection.*

"Statute Nine," Voss said. "That's the weapons-secrecy statute. He's not even pretending it's about salvage anymore." She looked at her own hands, then at me. "He knew. Okonkwo knew what Lattice was. He's been sitting on it for nine years, signing the truce documents with one hand and holding the other one over the button."

"Voss—Imara. You don't have to be here for this part."

"Yes, I do." She stood, pale, one-armed, furious. "I spent my whole career believing the ledger balanced. That the fleet was the thing that kept the accidents from happening. And it turns out the fleet *was* the accident." She keyed her own channel, open, fleet-wide, and her voice came out steady as a bearing. "All ships, all commands. This is Fleet Auditor Imara Voss, serial 4471-Kestrel. I am invoking my statutory authority as independent investigator to unseal evidence regarding Project Lattice. The evidence will be broadcast from the Meridian relay in"—she checked the array readout—"eleven minutes. If you fire on this relay, you fire on the record. Choose accordingly."

The channel went silent in the way that only forty thousand people can be silent.

Ninety-one percent. The *Verity*'s cutter closed. The fleets' weapons lit their targeting lances across the dark like a city deciding whether to burn.

"Ninety-six," I said. "Handshake's holding. Mom, if you're listening—" I stopped. Laughed once, wet and graceless. "You're always listening. That was the whole point of you."

The array reached one hundred percent, and Ione Nystrom's voice filled every speaker in the system, and the first thing the fleets heard was my mother saying, clear as a bell across dead space:

*If you're hearing this, you found the lattice, which means you're either family or a thief—*

—and then the *Verity*'s cutter fired a boarding grapple through the relay's outer hull, and the deck lurched, and the broadcast stuttered.

* * *

The array held. Barely. The transmission ran, my mother's testimony pouring into every open channel while the cutter's boarding party chewed through the lock like something with an appetite. Voss stood at the junction bulkhead with a coil pistol in her off hand and her blood on my suit, and I stood at the broadcast console with my hand on the purge control—the one that would wipe the archive rather than let it be taken, my mother's last nine years of voice, the only recording of her that existed anywhere.

The boarding torch cut through the inner lock.

The ceasefire clock, absurdly, faithfully, read zero.

And every fleet channel in the system opened at once—hundreds of voices, both crescents, all of them talking over each other, because forty thousand people had just heard a dead scientist describe, by name, the weapon their admirals had sworn didn't exist.

The lock gave way behind us. Voss raised the pistol. I kept my hand on the purge.

And through the smoke came a voice I knew—not from the archive, not from the fleets, but from a salvage dock and a debt and a warning about rival crews—and it said my name.
