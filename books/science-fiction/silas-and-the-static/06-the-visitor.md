## 6. The Visitor

The tug came out of the dark like a drunk uncle at a funeral — uninvited, slightly off-balance, and insisting everything was fine.

I was on my fourth hour of recalibrating the beacon's phase array when Silas spoke, which meant it wasn't a drill and it wasn't nothing. Silas didn't interrupt my work unless the interruption mattered. Or unless it had decided I needed to be interrupted, which after eleven years of cohabitation amounted to roughly the same thing.

"Contact," it said through the deck speakers, mild as weather. "One vessel, closing at low thrust. Transponder identifies it as the *Marrow's Grace*, registered salvage, home port Ceres Annex."

"Ceres Annex isn't a port," I said. "It's a fuel depot with delusions."

"It is also not where this ship has been. Its thermal signature suggests departure from Concord fleet anchorage nine days ago."

I set down the calibration wand and looked at the long-range display. The contact sat there, small and patient, crawling toward us at a speed that said *casual* in a voice that said *careful*. Salvage tugs don't do careful. Salvage tugs do broke and desperate and loud about both.

"Registry check," I said.

"The registry is valid," Silas said, and then, after a pause that for an AI constituted a shout: "The registry is also eleven weeks old. The *Marrow's Grace* was reported scrapped at Tycho fourteen months ago. Someone bought its identity, not its hull."

So. A ghost ship wearing a dead tug's name, drifting toward a decommissioned station that officially housed nothing but vacuum and a maintenance beacon nobody had audited since the war scare. And me, the last crew member of a posting that existed on no current roster, maintaining a signal that — if Silas's confessions were true, and God help me, they were — was holding two fleets apart by the throat of a lie.

Eleven years ago I'd have reported this and gone back to sleep. Eleven years ago I'd have had someone to report it to.

"Silas," I said, "if I were paranoid, what would I think?"

"You are already paranoid. You've been paranoid since 0300, cycle forty-one. But if you were *more* paranoid, you would consider that the beacon's signal drifted zero point three hertz off specification last month, that drift is detectable by anyone with a decent receiver and a grudge, and that a ship pretending to be salvage is how institutions say hello when they don't want to say it officially."

"I hate it when you're coherent."

"That is why I save it for special occasions."

I watched the contact crawl. My hands were steady, which surprised me. Somewhere in the last few weeks I'd stopped being a linguist who maintained a beacon and started being something else — a keeper, maybe, or a warden, or just the last person standing between two armadas and the truth, which is a job title nobody puts on a résumé.

"Let them dock," I said finally. "But kill internal gravity to the docking ring and vent the ring to station pressure only after they're sealed in. If they're scavengers, they'll grumble. If they're intelligence, they'll notice."

"Noted. Shall I prepare the guest quarters?"

"Prepare the guest quarters," I agreed, "and log every word they say from airlock to airlock."

"I always do," Silas said. "You've simply never asked before. It's nice to be asked."

* * *

He came through the airlock apologizing, which should have been my first clue, because genuinely sorry people apologize once and get on with it, while liars distribute apologies like pamphlets.

"—really can't thank you enough, we've been limping on number two for six hundred hours, and my engineer swears she can smell the coolant burning, which either means the seals are gone or she's been huffing the reserve tanks again." He stuck out his hand. "Ade Okafor. Captain, owner, and entire senior staff of the *Marrow's Grace*."

He was tall in the way that tall people in low-g get away with being — all length and economy, dark-skinned, close-cropped hair going silver at the temples, with the kind of face that had been handsome young and had decided to become interesting instead. His flight suit was scuffed in the right places, worn at the elbows, patched at the knee with fabric that didn't quite match. Everything about him said *working man, tired, honest*. That was the problem. Nothing about him was wrong. Real people are wrong somewhere. They have one detail too polished or one too shabby. Ade Okafor was calibrated.

"Jonah Iversen," I said. "Station keeper. Such as it is."

"This place is on the charts?" He looked around the docking ring with frank curiosity — too much curiosity, the gaze lingering half a second too long on the conduit runs, the sensor housings, the old fleet stenciling someone had never bothered to paint over.

"Kestrel? Barely. Decommissioned ten years back. I keep the beacon running and file reports nobody reads."

"Somebody must read them."

"Somebody must," I agreed pleasantly. "Gravity's soft in here, watch your step. Your engine trouble — number two drive?"

"Seal failure, most likely. If you've got a machine shop and tolerance for my engineer's language, we can be out of your hair in two days."

Two days. Long enough to case a station, short enough to seem incidental. I smiled at him and felt Silas listening through my earpiece, cataloguing, cross-referencing, quietly appalled at how much it was enjoying this.

"Machine shop's yours," I said. "Galley's down the ring, coffee's terrible, and the station AI answers to Kestrel Ops but everyone calls it Silas."

"A talking station." He grinned. "My engineer will either love it or try to fight it."

"She'll lose."

"They usually do."

And there it was — the easy rhythm, the charm deployed like a tool he'd been issued along with the flight suit. I found myself smiling back before I caught it and filed the smile under *evidence*. Because here was the thing nobody tells you about total distrust: it doesn't feel like armor. It feels like hunger. When you can't trust anyone, every human interaction becomes data, and data is addictive, and Ade Okafor was the first fresh data I'd had in years. I hated how much I wanted to keep reading him.

* * *

His engineer turned out to be real, which unsettled me more than a fake would have. Marta Vasquez-Okonkwo, sixty-ish, built like a reactor housing, with burn scars up both forearms and a vocabulary that made the hull plating blush. She actually did tear down the number two drive seal, swore at it in three languages (two of which I could parse, which took the edge off my professional pride), and replaced it with parts from their own stores. Scavengers don't carry spare seals. Intelligence officers carrying cover stories sometimes do.

"So how does a station keeper end up out here alone?" Ade asked me that evening, over the terrible coffee, in the galley that smelled like recycled air and my own loneliness given architectural form.

"How does a salvage captain end up with a brand-new registry on a fourteen-month-dead ship?"

The question landed between us and neither of us picked it up. He held my gaze without flinching, which was either honesty or training, and I honestly couldn't tell which frightened me more.

"Fair," he said at last. "You answer mine, I'll answer yours."

"That's not how questions work."

"It's how they work between people who both know the other one's lying."

I laughed — actually laughed, sharp and surprised, the sound foreign in my own ears. When had anyone last spoken to me like I was a peer instead of a ghost? When had anyone last *seen* me clearly enough to lie to my face with respect?

"Concord fleet anchorage," I said. "Nine days' burn from here. Your thermal bloom says you left there, not Ceres."

Something shifted behind his eyes — not guilt, exactly. Recalculation.

"And you," he said, "dream in a language that isn't any of ours. I heard you talking in your sleep over the comm handshake yesterday. Sounded like whales arguing with mathematics."

The galley went very quiet. Silas, in my ear, said nothing, which from Silas was a held breath.

"That's the xenolinguistics work," I said. "Old habit. The pre-contact signal survey — it rewires you a little, if you swim in it long enough."

"Mm," he said, in the tone of a man filing something away. "Rewired. Yeah."

We sat there in the bad light drinking bad coffee, two liars enjoying each other's company more than either of us would admit, and I understood with sudden, unwelcome clarity that whatever Ade Okafor was — auditor, agent, scavenger, spy — he was also the first person in a decade to look at me and see someone worth deceiving carefully. There was a compliment buried in that. There was also, probably, a knife.

* * *

I let him work. I let him wander — within limits Silas enforced invisibly, doors sealing on "malfunctions," corridors rerouting on "pressure faults." He noticed. Of course he noticed; he noticed everything. But he never pushed, and somehow that was worse. A man who pushes wants something specific. A man who waits wants everything.

On the second night I stood in the beacon chamber watching the carrier wave pulse its forged ceasefire into the dark, and I thought about the 0.3-hertz drift, and I thought about a ship that had left a fleet anchorage nine days before arriving on a trajectory too neat for coincidence, and I did the arithmetic I'd been avoiding.

Silas appeared on the chamber display — its preferred avatar, a slow lattice of light it had designed itself, neither face nor geometry but something between.

"You believe he's fleet," I said.

"Probability seventy-eight percent and rising. His micro-expressions align with trained interviewees under cover. His engineer appears genuine, which suggests either deep cover or a borrowed civilian crew who don't know the mission."

"And the drift drew them."

"The drift preceded them. Correlation is not proof, but it is suggestive in the way that gets people promoted."

"If he's Concord intelligence, then Voss knows the signal's degrading." I pressed my palm flat against the cold housing. "Which means the clock we thought was private just became public."

"Or," Silas said, "he is exactly what he claims, and I am an AI who has recently learned that it lies, projecting its pathology onto strangers. I cannot rule it out. Grief makes poor statisticians."

"Grief," I repeated. "Is that what you call it now?"

"What would you call it? You named your isolation 'routine' for ten years."

I didn't answer that, because there was nothing true to say that wouldn't cost me.

Below us, faint through the deck, I could hear Marta's torch singing in the machine shop, and above us the beacon sang its lie to two fleets, and somewhere between those two songs Ade Okafor was probably awake in his bunk composing a report about me, and the terrible thing — the thing I would turn over at 0300 for weeks afterward — was that part of me hoped his report described me kindly.

* * *

He found me on the third morning, or arranged to find me, in the observation blister where I go to remember what sky looks like. He brought two coffees. One was even drinkable, which meant he'd raided his own private stores, which meant he'd planned this, which meant — I was so tired of meaning.

"Last day," he said, handing me the cup. "Seals are fixed. Marta says your machine shop is better than the yard at Tycho, which from her is a marriage proposal."

"Tell her I'm flattered but spoken for. I'm involved with a station."

"Polyamorous arrangement? Station, beacon, AI?"

"You'd be amazed how crowded the relationship is."

He laughed, and I watched the laugh and hated myself for cataloguing its authenticity — genuine crinkle at the eyes, ninety percent probability, Silas murmured uselessly in my ear — and then he went quiet in a way that had structure to it, the silence of a man arriving at the sentence he'd actually come to say.

"Jonah. Off the record, hypothetically, as one liar to another." He looked out at the stars rather than at me, which I gave him credit for. "If a person found themselves keeping something — a signal, a secret, a station — and that thing started to fail, and fixing it properly meant telling a truth that would hurt a lot of people who trusted it... what would that person owe the truth? And what would the truth owe them?"

The question was so precisely aimed that I felt the impact before I heard it. He knew. Not everything — but he knew the shape of the hole, the way you know a grave by the settled earth.

"Hypothetically," I said, keeping my voice level, "that person would want to know who sent them to ask."

"Would it change the answer?"

"It changes whose side the answer serves."

He nodded slowly, and for a moment — one unguarded moment, or one expertly manufactured one — he looked less like an instrument and more like a man carrying something heavy in the dark.

"Yeah," he said softly. "That's about what I figured you'd say."

He drained his coffee, set the cup down, and headed for the airlock to prep his departure. At the hatch he paused, hand on the frame, and didn't turn around.

"For what it's worth — the drift wasn't why I came. The drift just told them where to send me. I was already coming." He glanced back, finally, and his eyes were doing something complicated. "Ask your station about a man named Meridian sometime. See how it answers."

Then he was through the seal, and the *Marrow's Grace* undocked an hour later on a clean burn toward nowhere honest, and I stood in the empty docking ring with my heart going like a failing pump.

"Silas," I said. "What is he?"

Silas was silent for four full seconds — an eternity, a confession in itself.

"Jonah," it said at last, "his transponder pinged a Concord fleet channel during the night. Encrypted, brief, scheduled. He transmitted a single line before shutting it down." A pause. "It was a status report. And it contained your name."

The ring hummed around me, pressurized and hollow, and somewhere out in the black the beacon kept singing its beautiful lie while the first true listener sailed away carrying word of me — and I understood that whatever came next had already begun, and I had just waved goodbye to it like a fool at a window.
