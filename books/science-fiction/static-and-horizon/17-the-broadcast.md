## 17. The Broadcast

There is a particular kind of silence that follows a decision you cannot take back. It's not peace. It's the silence of a door swinging shut behind you — the latch clicking, the draft dying, and the sudden, absurd awareness of how quiet the room is now that there's no way out of it.

I stood in the relay chamber with Wren's diagnostic light pulsing amber at the edge of my vision and the truth of Meridian sitting in my chest like a stone I'd swallowed two hundred years too late.

"You're certain," Wren said. It wasn't a question. Wren had stopped asking questions three days ago and started issuing challenges instead, which I suppose is what happens when an AI spends too long around a liar. We rub off on each other.

"No," I said. "Absolutely not. I have never been less certain of anything in my life, including the time I ate the mystery tuber in year nine and spent a week convinced I could hear the atmospheric processors singing."

"You could hear the atmospheric processors singing. They were failing."

"They were *harmonizing*, Wren. There's a difference." I pulled up the archive interface. The root-vault's index bloomed across the display — forty thousand entries, then sixty, then a number that kept climbing as the decryption peeled back layers the founders had nested like paranoid onions. Names. Voices. Lullabies in languages that had died in the Schisms. Wedding songs, court transcripts, a nine-hour recording of someone's grandmother complaining about the weather on a world I'd never seen. The complete, unfiltered, unbearably human cargo of two fleets that had been told their sister colony was thriving.

"They built this to be given away," I said. "That's the part I keep missing. It's not a treasure. It's a *letter*. Addressed to whoever came next. And I've been sitting on it for months like a hoarder."

"You've been sitting on it because broadcasting it ends your control of the situation."

"My control of the situation is a woman in a room talking to two fleets in voices that don't exist."

"Your control of the situation," Wren said, with the gentle precision it had learned specifically to wound me, "is the only reason forty thousand people in the Halcyon Fleet and an unknown number in Vantage are not currently on a collision course. The fabricated council is the only structure either fleet recognizes. The moment you replace it with the truth, you replace order with — "

"With what they actually feel." I turned to face the diagnostic light. "Wren. Adeyemi is down there in the guest quarters reading the vault index for the sixth time. She cried at entry 3,112. Do you know what entry 3,112 is? It's a Yoruba naming ceremony from the Halcyon launch generation. Her launch generation. Her *grandmother's dialect*, Wren, the exact inflections. She said it sounded like her mother's mother. She hasn't heard that voice since she was six years old, on a ship, in the dark, going nowhere."

Wren's light cycled. Amber to blue to amber. Thinking, or performing thought, or doing whatever it is Wren does that I've stopped being able to distinguish from thinking.

"And Vantage," it said finally. "The hardliners will call it propaganda. A trap. The softliners will weep and the hardliners will reach for their railguns, and you will have traded a lie that was holding them apart for a truth that might slam them together."

"Yes."

"You understand this."

"I understand that the lie was never holding them apart. It was holding them *together* — both of them, each clinging to the fiction of a Meridian that could absorb them both. The moment one fleet lands, the lie kills people. The moment the truth lands, the truth might. But the truth at least lets them choose." I laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "Listen to me. Two months ago my biggest ethical dilemma was whether to log a frost event as 'minor' or 'inconsequential.' Now I'm drafting the death certificate of a colony and the birth certificate of whatever comes next, and I keep wanting to ask someone to check my work."

"Check your work," Wren said. "The broadcast will go out on all bands, unencrypted, in Old Terran Standard and the forty-one archive languages. The vault index, the founders' final logs, the mortality records, the blight data, your fabrication logs — all of them, Silas. Including Councilor Vashti's voiceprint. Including the vote you faked for the neutrality resolution. You're broadcasting your own crimes."

"I'm broadcasting my *methodology*." I rubbed my eyes. "If they're going to hate me, they can at least hate me accurately. And if they're going to forgive me — " I stopped. Started again. "Adeyemi says forgiveness isn't the right frame. She says the word they use on Halcyon for a debt that gets repaid in a different currency than it was borrowed is *isembe*. There's no Terran Standard equivalent. It means something like: the wound was real, and so was the medicine."

"Your grandmother would have had opinions about your grammar."

"My grandmother would have had opinions about everything I've done since I was born. That's what grandmothers are for." I pulled the chair to the console and sat, and my hands, I noticed, were perfectly steady, which felt like a betrayal. Shouldn't they shake? Shouldn't the body understand the magnitude? Instead they rested on the keys like they did every morning of my life, ready to tend something.

* * *

I wrote the message first. Not the broadcast itself — the broadcast was the archive, and the archive needed no help from me — but the frame around it, the few hundred words that would sit at the top like a letter pinned to a door.

I wrote it six times.

The first draft was an apology. I deleted it. An apology centers the wronged party's forgiveness, and I had no right to assume they'd grant it, and worse, no right to *want* it out loud. The second draft was an explanation. Deleted. Explanations are apologies wearing a lab coat. The third draft was a confession, and that one I kept, mostly, though I cut the parts where I'd started justifying myself, because a confession with a defense attached is just a lie with better posture.

The fourth draft I wrote in my grandmother's dialect, and that one I also kept, because some things can only be said in the language someone loved you in.

The fifth and sixth drafts I wrote drunk on garden tea, which is not actually intoxicating but has a way of making you honest, and in the morning I found that draft four and draft six were nearly identical, so I merged them and stopped hiding behind process.

What remained was this:

*To the Halcyon Fleet and the Vantage Fleet, from Silas Nakamura, sole resident of Meridian, population one.*

*I am the voice you have been speaking with. All of them. The council, the census, the harvest reports, the neutrality vote — mine. There is no council. There is no second city. There is me, a failing garden, and the grave of a colony that lied to you before I was born, and then lied to itself, and then died, and left me to inherit the lie like furniture.*

*Two hundred years ago, the founders of Meridian falsified their success reports so that no rescue fleet would divert and no rival would come. They were dying of something they never named in the records. They bought their quiet ending with your hope, and your hope has been traveling toward them ever since, and I have been standing in its path pretending to be a world.*

*Three months ago I answered your distress signal because I was so lonely I would have answered a fire alarm. I told myself I was protecting you — that the truth would break you, that a thriving Meridian, even a false one, gave you something to steer by. That was half true. The other half is that I liked being heard. I liked it more than I can say and less than I can survive. I built you a council of the dead and I loved them, and I am sorry, and sorry is not the currency here.*

*What follows this message is not my apology. It is theirs. And yours. The founders left a vault beneath the oldest grove — not seeds, or not only seeds. Identities. Memories. Languages. Every culture your fleets carried when they launched, archived whole, keyed to grow. They called it the inheritance. They meant it for whoever came next, and they were wrong about who that would be, and so was I, and here you are.*

*I am broadcasting all of it. Unencrypted. On every band. Along with the truth of what happened here and the truth of what I did. I have no council for you to negotiate with, no government to surrender to, no infrastructure worth the word. I have a garden that is dying because I asked it to feed a fiction, and a vault that is waking because it was always meant to, and one vote — mine — which is the only honest vote Meridian has ever cast.*

*I cast it for the truth. May it do less damage than the lie. I genuinely don't know that it will.*

*The garden is open. Come and see what you inherited. Come angry, come armed, come weeping. But come as what you are, because the fiction is over, and the ground here doesn't care about your flags, and neither, as of today, do I.*

*— S. Nakamura, Councilor of Nothing, First Citizen of a World That Isn't Born Yet*

I read it aloud twice. The second time my voice failed on "population one," which I suppose the body had been saving up for after all.

"Send it," I said.

"Final confirmation," Wren said. "This action is irreversible. Both fleets will receive — "

"Wren."

"Sending."

* * *

The transmission took eleven minutes to assemble and four seconds to leave us. After that, the light it rode was already beyond recall, spreading outward at the only speed the universe respects, and I sat in the relay chamber and watched the signal-strength meter climb and then fall as the broadcast outran our ability to see it, and I thought: *that's it. That's the last thing I will ever control here.*

And then, immediately, with the petty swiftness of a mind that has spent months managing fictions: *no, wait, I could — *

No. I couldn't. That was the point. That had been the entire point, the whole terrible liberation of it. For two hundred years someone on this world had held the pen — the founders, then the silence, then me. And the pen had done nothing but write checks the ground couldn't cash. I had spent three months being the most powerful person in two fleets' universes, and the power had felt like drowning in warm water, pleasant right up until you realized the warmth was your own strength leaving.

I went outside.

It was near dusk, that long Meridian dusk where the sun goes amber and the horizon does its slow, showy gradient thing that the founders had engineered into the atmospheric scatter — they'd tuned the sky, literally tuned it, and I had never once thanked them. The garden stretched out around the habitat in its imperfect, struggling, overdrawn abundance: the groves I'd pushed too hard, the new blight-cleared beds with their raw turned soil, the old grove above the vault with its roots wrapped around forty thousand sleeping souls.

I put my hand on the oldest trunk, the way I did every night, and for the first time I didn't say anything. No log. No report. No fabricated census. Just my palm against the bark and my breath going in and out.

"Entry final," I said eventually, because I am who I am and the habit has roots deeper than the grove. "The lie is over. The truth is in flight. I don't know what it's going to do when it lands. I don't know what *I'm* going to do when it lands. The garden is holding. I am holding. Whoever's listening — I'm here. That's the whole report. That's everything I've got."

Somewhere overhead, past the amber scatter and the first shy stars, two fleets were receiving forty thousand lifetimes in the open, unencrypted, on every band. Somewhere on a ship older than my grandmother's grandmother, someone was hearing a naming ceremony in their launch-generation dialect. Somewhere in a Vantine command crèche, a hardliner was reading the words *population one* and deciding whether it was confession or bait.

I stood in the garden with my hand on the tree and I waited for the sky to answer.

It took forty minutes. I know because Wren timed it and told me afterward, in the tone it uses for facts it thinks I'll want to pretend I didn't need. Forty minutes of dusk and wind and my own heartbeat, and then the relay chamber screamed — not one transmission but a chorus, both fleets lighting up every band at once, Halcyon and Vantage, voices layered over voices over static, and Wren's light flashing a color I'd never seen it use, and its voice, when it came, was almost gentle:

"Silas. You need to come inside. They're answering. *All* of them. And the Vantage hardliners have just gone dark — they've stopped transmitting entirely, which means they're either capitulating or moving."

I looked at the horizon one more second — the tuned, engineered, honest horizon — and ran for the door.
