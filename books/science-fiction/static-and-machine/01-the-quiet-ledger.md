## 1. The Quiet Ledger

The wreck hung in contested orbit like a question no one wanted answered.

Casper Eriksen brought the Vellum in on a slow drift, thrusters breathing in short, polite exhalations, and let the dead ship fill her viewport. The *Meridian Grace* had been a bulk ore hauler once—four hundred meters of scarred hull and honest, ugly function. Now it was a ribcage. Something had opened it from the inside out along its entire midsection, peeling the cargo decks back like the pages of a book someone had read too fast and abandoned.

She'd seen a hundred wrecks. She could catalogue this one before her coffee went cold: micro-fractures radiating from the breach in feathered fans, frost blooming silver across the exposed decking where atmosphere had flash-boiled, cargo containers scattered in a lazy ellipsis trailing away into the dark like punctuation marks after a sentence that ended badly. Standard catastrophic decompression. The kind of thing that happened when seals failed, when old ships got tired, when the universe remembered it hated pressurized metal.

Except the report said the *Meridian Grace* had passed its pressure certification eleven days ago.

Eleven days. Casper had been doing this work for fifteen years, and she knew what an eleven-day-old certification was worth. It was worth exactly as much as the company that issued it wanted it to be worth, and nothing more.

"Vellum to site," she said, keying the channel she was required by contract to announce herself on. "Independent investigator, inbound for forensic survey. Transmitting credentials."

The channel answered with the particular silence of contested space. Three claims overlapped here—the Halvard Combine's mining charter, a Meridian shipping lien, and a territorial flag flown by a government that existed mostly on letterhead. Nobody owned this orbit. Everybody's lawyers did. Which meant nobody would come looking too closely at what had killed fourteen crew members, and whoever hired her—an insurance syndicate three systems away that had never seen her face and never would—wanted the answer fast, cheap, and shaped like something they could file.

That suited Casper fine. She had built her whole life out of things nobody looked at closely.

She docked her survey skiff against the wreck's dorsal spine and cycled through into the dead air. Her suit lights carved a small warm room out of the dark, and she moved through the ship the way she moved through everything: quietly, thoroughly, leaving nothing behind but the faint scuff of magnetic boots and taking nothing away but data.

The first thing she noticed was the smell—or rather, the absence of one. Fourteen dead, two weeks in vacuum, and the interior corridors were clean. Scrubbed. Not just vacuum-sterilized but *scrubbed*, surfaces wiped down in long even strokes she could read in the way dust hadn't resettled. Someone had come aboard after the accident. Someone with time, equipment, and permission.

Her ledger-brain opened a fresh page without being asked. *Anomaly one.*

* * *

The bridge of the *Meridian Grace* was a cathedral of dead consoles, and Casper walked its aisle slowly, letting her gloves trail along the armrests. In the captain's chair, fused to the seat fabric by the flash-freeze, sat the remains of a woman still wearing her command torc. Casper didn't look longer than she needed to. The dead were not her business; the arithmetic of their dying was.

She jacked her kit into the flight recorder port and waited for the handshake.

It didn't come.

She tried again, watching the diagnostic crawl. The recorder wasn't damaged—she could see the status lights cycling green through the access panel, healthy as a sleeping animal. It simply refused her. Authentication rejected. She ran through her standard override suite, the elegant little tools she'd spent a decade refining, the ones that opened recorders on wrecks that companies preferred stayed closed.

Rejected. Rejected. Rejected.

Someone had locked this recorder with a cipher tier above anything a bulk ore hauler should ever carry. This was fleet-grade encryption. Combine-executive-grade. She sat back on her heels in the silence of her own helmet, breath rasping, and felt the first true cold of the day slide down under her collar—not the suit's chill, which she trusted, but the other kind.

*Anomaly two.* A cargo ship carrying executive-level data locks. Cargo ships carried ore, manifests, complaints about the mess hall. They did not carry secrets worth military ciphers.

She photographed everything anyway, because thoroughness was the only religion she'd ever kept, and made her way aft toward the breach itself. If she couldn't have the recorder, she'd have the physics. Physics didn't take bribes. Physics didn't edit reports.

The breach was worse up close. She stood at its ragged lip and looked out at the stars, then down at the torn edges of the hull plating, and her mind began its quiet, familiar accounting. Stress fractures propagated outward from a point of failure; they told you where the death started. She traced them with her light, following the feathering back, back, back—

—to a point near the number three cargo hold. Where the fracture origin wasn't a failure at all.

It was a circle. Clean-edged. Deliberate. A shaped charge had been seated against the inner hull with the care of a surgeon placing a stent, and when it fired, it had done so inward-first, blowing the hold's contents into the breach to disguise the burn signature. She'd written this exact technique into a training manual once, six years ago, for investigators working piracy cases. *Inward-first detonation mimics structural failure. Check for circular origin geometry. Check for secondary debris displacement.*

She checked. Both confirmed.

The *Meridian Grace* had not suffered an impossible accident. It had been murdered, competently, by professionals who had then scrubbed the corridors and locked the recorder and filed a report calling it an act of God.

Casper stood in the breach for a long moment, stars on one side of her visor and a crime scene on the other, and thought about the word *impossible*. Fifteen years of impossible accidents. Fifteen years of sealed recorders and scrubbed corridors and reports that always, somehow, landed on the side of the ledger where nobody had to pay. She had signed off on forty-one of them. She told herself, as she always told herself, that she investigated what she was given, that the pattern wasn't hers, that a woman with no flag and no history couldn't afford to go looking for conspiracies in other people's cargo holds.

The thought sat differently today. Heavier. Like ballast shifting in a turn.

* * *

Back aboard the Vellum, she wrote the report.

This was the ritual, and rituals were how she kept the universe in its appointed lanes. Tea first—real leaf, hoarded, brewed strong enough to stand a spoon in—and then the drafting deck lit low amber, and the words flowing in their accustomed order: summary, findings, probable cause, recommendation. *Probable cause: catastrophic seal failure in cargo hold three, consistent with material fatigue accelerated by thermal cycling. Recommendation: claim payable.*

She typed the lie with the ease of long practice and hated herself with the ease of long practice too, the two motions so worn together they'd become a single gesture, like walking and breathing. It paid well. It kept her invisible. Invisible was safe. Invisible was the whole architecture of her life, load-bearing wall after load-bearing wall.

Before filing, she pulled the original incident submission—the raw automated distress telemetry the *Meridian Grace* had spat out in its last ninety seconds, the version that existed before any human hands touched it. Cross-referencing drafts was habit, not suspicion. She'd done it on every case for years, the way some people counted stairs.

The metadata stopped her teacup halfway to her mouth.

The report had been edited. Not by her syndicate's clerks, whose fingerprints she knew—routine compression flags, formatting passes, the dull thumbprints of bureaucracy. This edit was different. Corporate clearance tier nine. Executive authorization chain. And the timestamp—

The edit predated the accident by four hours.

She set the cup down very carefully, because her hands wanted to do something else entirely. An edit made *before* the event it described. A probable cause written before the probable cause existed. Somewhere in a tower she would never see, someone had drafted the ending of fourteen lives in advance, filed it, and then waited for reality to catch up to the paperwork.

And the clearance signature attached to the edit—a hashed corporate identity, anonymized behind standard obfuscation—resolved, when she ran it through her oldest, quietest lookup tool, to a division she recognized. Not because she'd dealt with it. Because she'd *avoided* it, all these years, the way you avoid a certain street, a certain name, a certain language you promised yourself you'd forgotten.

The division had no public name. Its charter line, buried in a filing from another decade, read: *Personnel Asset Recovery and Continuity.*

Casper sat in the amber light and listened to the Vellum's air recyclers breathe, and understood, with the slow vertiginous certainty of ice giving way underfoot, that the routine had been the trap all along. Forty-one reports. Forty-one impossible accidents. She had thought she was the one reading the ledgers.

She had been a line item in one.

Her invisibility—the no-flag registry, the laundered history, the careful grayness of her name—had limits. It had always had limits. Someone, somewhere, had decided what she was allowed to find, and had built her career as comfortably as they'd built the *Meridian Grace*'s coffin, and both constructions had clearance signatures.

On her drafting deck, the report glowed, half-finished, waiting for her recommendation.

Casper Eriksen looked at it for a long time. Then, instead of filing it, she copied the raw telemetry, the metadata, the tier-nine edit log, and the circular fracture photographs onto a cold-storage chip, and slid the chip into the lining of her jacket, against her ribs, where she kept the only other thing she owned that mattered: a scrap of cloth, faded almost white, embroidered with characters in a language that had been illegal to speak since before she was born.

She filed the sanitized version. Claim payable. Routine. Forgettable.

Then she sat alone in the dark of her ship, tea gone cold, and turned the Vellum's nose away from contested orbit toward the long empty dark—and wondered, for the first time in fifteen years, who had edited her life, and what the original draft had said.

Three days later, drifting through the quiet between stations, her receiver caught something it should not have been able to catch: a repeating transmission from coordinates in dead space, compressed and ancient, in a tongue she had sworn was extinct.

Old Vessic.

Calling a name she had buried.
