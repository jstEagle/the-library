## 8. Burn Toward Dead Space

The debris field had a name once. On the old charts it was called the Halvard Shoals, after a mining concern that had gone bankrupt before Casper was born, leaving behind the stripped carcasses of three orbital refineries and eleven years of discarded hull-plate, slag, spent coolant tanks, and the frozen excrement of a thousand work crews. Nobody renamed it when the maps were redrawn. Nobody named anything out here. Dead space began at the edge of the Shoals, and dead space did not keep names.

Casper flew into it with her running lights off, her transponder cold, and her heart going like a trapped bird against her ribs.

Behind them, at the edge of sensor range, the Kestrel Combine's interceptors were burning hard on a converging vector. Three contacts. Maybe four—the fourth signature kept flickering in and out behind the third, as if it were deliberately shadowing its sibling's exhaust to confuse exactly this kind of count. Juno had spotted the trick forty minutes ago from the signal bench, her voice cracking halfway through the report: *There's another one, there's definitely another one, they're hiding it.*

Four interceptors against one aging cargo hauler with a patched hull and a manifest of ghosts.

"Talk to me about the field," Casper said, because talking was better than listening to the sound of her own pulse.

Sefa sat strapped into the second seat, the old woman's hands folded in her lap like a woman waiting for a bus. She had not spoken since the burn began. But she had insisted on coming forward, away from the relative safety of the aft cabin, and Casper had not argued. There was something steadying about her silence—a quality of it that was not absence but attention.

"Juno has the chart," Sefa said quietly. "She is drawing it for you."

"I'm drawing it," Juno confirmed over the comm from the signal bay, and Casper could hear the tremor under the teenager's forced brightness. "Mostly. The refinery cores are the big masses—I've got those plotted off the passive returns. Everything else is... texture."

"Texture kills ships," Casper said.

"So does a Combine interceptor," Juno said. "Pick your poison."

Casper almost smiled. Almost. "Feed me the cores first. We'll thread the texture."

The Vellum slid into the Shoals at a quarter gravity, running dark in every sense Casper could manage: no active sensors, no comms, no drive flare larger than a whisper. Navigation by passive return alone was like walking a familiar hallway with your eyes closed—you remembered where the walls were, and you prayed nobody had moved the furniture in the last decade. The first refinery carcass rose out of the black on the forward array: a shattered ring of trusses and tank farms, tumbling with the slow, majestic indifference of something that had been dying for sixty years and intended to go on dying long after everyone watching was gone.

Casper took them under it. Close enough that the hull plating ticked with thermal exchange as the Vellum passed through the shadow of a coolant tank the size of a chapel.

In the cockpit's dim red light, Sefa turned her head and watched the wreckage crawl past the viewport. Her lips moved—not in prayer, Casper thought, though she couldn't be sure. In Old Vessic, maybe. Counting something. Naming things. A language needed speakers the way a fire needed air, and maybe the old woman was keeping hers alive the only way left to her, whispering it to dead metal in the dark.

*Vessic*, Casper thought, and the word hurt in a place she didn't have a name for. *We are all cargo now.*

* * *

The reactor alarm went up ninety minutes into the field, and it went up quietly, which was somehow worse than a klaxon. A soft triple chime from engineering, amber instead of red, the ship's way of clearing its throat before saying something terrible.

Idris answered it before Casper even keyed the comm. She heard his boots in the corridor, heard the engineering hatch cycle, and then his voice, flat and professional in a way she'd learned to distrust: "Cas. You're going to want to bring us out of the burn for a minute."

"We can't stop in the field. Stopping means drifting, and drifting means we're a rock among rocks until someone with a telescope finds us."

"I know." A pause. She could hear him breathing, hear the clank of a panel being dropped. "The number-two injector assembly took damage back at the depot. When that investigator's team swept us, they pulled a coolant line for inspection and their reattachment was garbage. It's been degrading since. We're at eighty-six percent on the secondary loop and falling. If it hits seventy while we're under thrust, the injector floods, and then we have a very exciting fire in a very small room."

"How long to failure at current load?"

"At current load? Six hours. At evasion thrust?" He laughed, short and without any humor in it. "Two. Maybe less if the field shakes us around."

Casper closed her eyes for half a second. Half a second was all she allowed herself. Behind them, four interceptors. Ahead of them, six hours of debris. Between those facts, a failing heart in her ship's chest.

"Can you fix it?"

A longer pause this time. Through the comm she heard Juno's voice in the background of engineering, small: *What can I do, what can I do* —and Idris saying, gently, *hold this, just hold this right here.* Then: "I can fix it. Not from in here. The injector manifold's external access is slagged—it's been slagged since before you bought her, don't look at me like that, I've read the maintenance history. To get at it I have to go out through the number-two service lock and do it by hand, in the field, while we're moving."

The words landed in Casper's stomach like ballast.

An EVA inside an active debris field, on a ship running dark, under thrust, with Combine interceptors sweeping the Shoals' edges for exhaust blooms. If Idris drifted ten meters off-hull, she would never find him again. If he slipped, if a fragment of sixty-year-old slag found him at closing velocity—

"No," Casper said, and heard how much her voice shook, and hated it. "Absolutely not. We slow to station-keeping, you do it tethered, we lose time—"

"We don't have time. That's the whole point. And slowing down makes us louder, relatively speaking—we'd be the only stationary object in a moving field. Every passive array on those interceptors is looking for exactly that." A breath. "Cas. I was fleet medical for nine years. Do you know what my job actually was, most days? Standing next to people while things happened to them that I couldn't stop. Holding a hand. Writing letters home. I am done holding hands. Give me the wrench."

She looked at Sefa. The old woman was watching her with an expression Casper couldn't read—or could read too well. It was the look of someone who had watched other people make this exact kind of decision before, about other people's lives, from the wrong side of the glass.

"He goes," Sefa said softly. "Or we all stay here and the Combine decides what happens. I have been the one who waits, girl. It is worse than the one who acts. Believe me."

Casper keyed the comm. "Idris. Tether double-line. Juno suits up with you as far as the lock—she runs your tether winch and your comms relay, nothing else. You do not let go of the hull. You do not freelance. You come back."

"Yes, Captain," Idris said, and she could hear the grin in it, the idiotic, infuriating, brave grin, and something in her chest cracked along a seam she hadn't known was there.

* * *

They suited up in the aft airlock bay while the Vellum threaded the gap between two refinery cores, the debris drifting past the tiny porthole like snow seen from a train. Casper helped Idris check his seals herself, her gloved hands clumsy with adrenaline, checking each latch twice, three times, running her palms over the suit's seams as if touch alone could make them true.

"You'll feel me on the line," Juno was saying, her own suit too big in the shoulders, helmet tucked under her arm, her braid escaping its tie already. "Winch response under two seconds. I've been practicing the tension curves on the sim all week, I—" She stopped. Looked up at Idris. All the bravado drained out of her face at once, leaving something younger and more frightened underneath. "Come back. Okay? That's—that's the whole procedure. Come back."

"Best procedure I ever heard," Idris said, and ruffled her hair with a gauntleted hand, ruining the braid entirely. Juno didn't complain.

Casper stood at the inner lock hatch as it cycled. Through the small armored window she watched the outer door open onto nothing—the Shoals, the black, the slow wheeling ruin of Halvard—and watched Idris step out onto the hull like a man stepping off a pier into deep water, tether paying out behind him, Juno's voice in all their ears counting meters.

"Fifty meters to the manifold access. Forty-five. Casper, his line's clean, drift's clean, he looks—" a breath, "—he looks good. He looks good out there."

He did not look good. He looked like a flea on the flank of a leviathan, a single suited figure crawling along a hull plate the size of a plaza, with the whole grinding weight of the debris field turning above him. Casper watched until her eyes burned and made herself stop watching and start flying, because the ship still needed a pilot and terror was not a flight plan.

* * *

For the next hour, Casper flew the most careful, most terrified course of her life.

Every input was a negotiation. Thrust in feathered taps, never a sustained burn, keeping the Vellum's acceleration profile down in the noise floor where the Combine's passive arrays would read it as just another tumbling fragment. She rolled the ship twice to put mass between them and the presumed sensor lines. She coasted through a canyon between two slag heaps so close that the proximity alarm gave up and displayed a solid amber bar, and Sefa, strapped in beside her, reached over without a word and laid one hand flat on the console, as if to hold the ship steady by will alone.

And through all of it, Juno's voice, and Idris's voice, in her ears.

"Panel's off. Oh, that's ugly. Cas, their reattachment job wasn't garbage, it was sabotage-grade lazy, they cross-threaded the collar—"

"Less commentary, more wrench."

"Wrenching. Wrenching now." A grunt of effort. "Juno, give me slack on the line, I need to get my shoulder into this housing."

"Slack in two seconds. Don't you dare float off."

"I would never."

"You absolutely would, you have zero respect for momentum, Idris, I've seen you walk."

Casper found, to her surprise, that she was smiling. Small and involuntary, there in the red-lit dark, with death drifting past the viewport in billion-tonne quantities. It was Juno's voice doing it—the kid had stopped shaking somewhere in the last twenty minutes. Somewhere between *what can I do* and *slack in two seconds*, fear had burned off and left competence behind, the way ore leaves metal. And Idris trusted her. She could hear it, the easy rhythm of a man working with someone watching his line, someone who would pull him home. Nine years of holding hands in the fleet, and the first time anyone held his, he was hanging off a hauler's belly in a graveyard.

That was the thing about terror, Casper thought, feeding the thrusters a whisper of correction as a spent fuel rod tumbled past close enough to read the manufacturer's stencil. Terror was a solvent. It dissolved everything that wasn't essential. Pride, privacy, the careful walls people built around themselves—all of it went into solution, and what precipitated out afterward was just the truth of who was aboard. Who held the line. Who came back.

The Vellum shuddered.

Not debris. Debris impact had a specific sound, a flat clang she knew intimately. This was deeper—a harmonic groan through the whole frame, and the reactor alarm climbed from amber to red, and Idris swore with real feeling in her ear.

"What was that?" Casper demanded.

"The injector. The collar let go entirely—the vibration from your last roll finished it. I've got raw coolant venting past the manifold, Cas, and it's freezing on contact with the hull, and I am standing in it." His voice stayed level, but she could hear the effort of level now, the cost of it. "If I don't seal this in the next few minutes, the secondary loop starves and we're done. Full stop. Drifting rock."

"Options?"

"One. I clamp the bypass valve shut by hand and reroute through the emergency junction. It's a thirty-minute job compressed into however many minutes we have. And I need power to the junction relays while I do it, which means somebody downstairs throwing breakers in sequence, blind, on my count."

"I'll do it," Juno said immediately.

"No," Casper and Idris said together.

"It has to be someone who can hear him and reach the breaker room fast," Sefa said from the second seat. Her voice was calm as deep water. "You are flying. I am old. The child is quick. Send the child."

"She's fourteen," Casper said.

"I was twelve during the second evacuation," Sefa said. "Send the child."

* * *

Juno ran.

Casper felt it more than saw it—the faint rhythmic thunder of boots through the deck plates, forward to aft, the kid's breathing loud on the open channel as she covered the length of the ship in under a minute. Then the sound of the breaker room hatch, and Idris's voice outside Casper's ear, distant and tinny through Juno's suit relay, calling numbers.

"Relay one. Relay three. Hold. Hold—relay two, now, now, now—"

The lights dipped. The deck hum changed pitch. Somewhere below Casper's feet, a fourteen-year-old girl threw switches in the dark on the count of a man hanging in a coolant blizzard, and the Vellum's failing heart stuttered, caught, and beat.

"Good," Idris breathed. "Good, that's good, I've got flow—Juno, relay five, gentle, gentle—there. There. Oh, that's beautiful. Cas, I'm sealing the collar. Thirty seconds. Talk to me about the sky."

Casper checked the passive returns. Her mouth went dry.

"Contact. One of the interceptors. It's cut the corner of the field—it's sweeping the outer Shoals along our original entry vector. Range forty thousand klicks and closing on a search pattern. It hasn't seen us. Yet. But if you take much longer, it won't matter how quiet we are, because we'll be a dead ship in its path."

"Twenty seconds."

"Idris—"

"Ten seconds. Juno, kill relays five and two, restore one and three, on my mark—mark!"

Lights flickered. The hum climbed. And then Idris's voice again, ragged, laughing, alive: "Sealed. Sealed! Secondary loop climbing—eighty-one percent, eighty-four—Cas, get us out of this thing's path, I'm coming inside."

"Not yet," Casper said, and swung the Vellum into a long, silent coast behind the largest of the refinery cores, letting the wreck's bulk swallow them. "Everyone hold position and hold breath. It passes in ninety seconds if we're lucky."

Ninety seconds. Casper had lived whole lifetimes in less. She watched the contact bloom on the passive display—a hard-edged wedge of military efficiency sliding through the Shoals' outskirts, its own running lights blazing, arrogant with certainty, hunting exhaust blooms and transponder whispers and finding nothing but the dead. It passed within eleven thousand kilometers. Close enough that if its crew had looked out a viewport with decent optics, they might have caught the Vellum's silhouette against the refinery core, a shadow on a shadow.

It passed. Its signature receded. The Shoals closed around the Vellum like water around a stone.

* * *

Idris came back through the inner lock gray-faced and frost-burned, ice crusted in the seams of his suit, and collapsed onto the deck plating with the boneless grace of a man whose body had finally been given permission to shake. Juno hit her knees beside him, started unlatching his helmet with hands that wouldn't quite work, and ended up just gripping his forearm, hard, saying nothing at all.

"Hey," Idris croaked. "Told you. Best procedure. Came back."

"You absolute idiot," Juno whispered, and put her forehead against his shoulder pauldron, and stayed there.

Casper stood in the lock bay doorway and let herself lean against the frame. Her legs felt like borrowed equipment. Through the corridor behind her, she could see Sefa in the cockpit, still strapped into the second seat, still keeping watch over the passive displays with the patient vigilance of someone who had survived worse than this and expected to survive this too.

"Reactor?" Casper asked.

"Stable. Ugly, but stable. Bypass will hold till we can do a proper refit—if there's a proper refit anywhere on the far side of this." Idris tipped his head back against the hull and looked up at her, and his eyes were clearer than she'd ever seen them, the fleet-drift gone out of them entirely. "You flew us through that. I heard the whole approach. That was the finest dead-stick ghost-run I've ever been attached to, and I've been attached to some legendary ones."

"We're not through yet."

"No." He smiled, tired and unhidden. "But we're through *that*. And Cas—when we get wherever we're going. Whatever's at the end of this signal. I want you to know I'm not here because I owe you for the passage anymore. Just so we're clear. That ledger closed about forty minutes ago, somewhere around relay five."

Casper looked at him—at the frost melting in his hair, at Juno still anchored to his arm, at the whole improbable, impossible architecture of people her ship had collected in a handful of weeks—and found she had no dry answer ready. Fifteen years of practice at being forgettable, and she couldn't remember a single rehearsed line.

"Rest," she said finally. "Both of you. We've got six hours of field left, and then dead space."

"And then dead space," Idris echoed softly, and closed his eyes.

Casper returned to the cockpit. Sefa surrendered the second seat without a word, but as Casper settled in and brought the passive displays up, the old woman paused at the hatchway, one hand on the frame.

"You flew well," Sefa said. "All of you. The ship, the boy, the girl. Even you, girl, especially you." Her eyes were dark and old and suddenly very direct. "This is what a crew is. I had forgotten. Perhaps I wanted to forget. But watch the long-range returns when we clear the Shoals."

"Why?"

"Because," Sefa said, "for the last hour, something at the edge of dead space has been transmitting. Very faintly. On a frequency only these old ears would think to listen for." She tapped her temple. "It is repeating the same phrase. In Vessic. And the phrase, Casper Eriksen, is *come home*."

She left Casper alone with the displays, the dark, and the words, ringing in the static like a bell struck once, still deciding whether to fade.
