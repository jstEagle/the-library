## 16. Sabotage in the Dark

Juno had learned the relay's crawlspaces the way she'd learned radio: by touch, by failure, by the slow accumulation of a private map no one else could read.

The maintenance tunnels behind the archive stacks were older than Oyelaran's forty-year tenancy, older than the Combine's charts, maybe older than the Purging itself. They were built for machines the size of children, and Juno was nearly that size, folded into a junction where three conduits met, her cheek against cold alloy, her breath fogging in air that tasted of ozone and old dust and something faintly sweet—the coolant Idris had warned them not to breathe too much of. Her headlamp was off. She didn't need it. She knew this stretch by heart now: four meters forward, a junction box humming at 60 hertz like a nervous animal; past that, the vertical shaft down to the tender bay, its ladder rungs worn smooth by hands that had died or left before she was born.

Above her, muffled through two decks of plating, the Kestrel Combine's boarding tender sat clamped to the relay's dorsal airlock like a tick.

She counted their footsteps when they passed overhead. She'd gotten good at it—good enough to tell Idris that there were nine, not eight, good enough to know which one limped, which one chewed something crunchy on night watch, which one walked with the flat even rhythm of someone who'd been fleet-trained and hated every second of it. She'd named them all after frequencies. The limper was 88.1. The cruncher was shortwave. The fleet-walker was carrier wave, because he never stopped, never wavered, just went on and on through the dark whether anyone was listening or not.

Casper had told her once, back before Meridian Dock, before any of this: *You listen better than anyone I've ever shipped with. Don't ever let anyone make you feel small for being quiet.* Juno held onto that now, in the cold, in the sweet-tasting dark, because what they were about to do was so far past anything Casper had ever sanctioned that it felt less like a plan than a fall.

*We don't kill them,* Casper had said, the last time they'd gathered in the archive's reading room, Sefa's blanket around her shoulders, Idris still gray-faced from his escape from the tender. *We don't take a single life we don't have to. We make their boat unable to fly. That's it. That's the whole mission.*

*And if they come after us anyway?* Idris had asked.

*Then we run faster,* Casper said, and nobody laughed, because everyone knew the Vellum couldn't run anywhere anymore.

So: the tender. Nine soldiers, a fusion-adjacent drive system, a docking clamp assembly that weighed as much as a cargo hauler's spine—and, somewhere in its guts, a power distribution trunk that Juno had mapped from stolen schematics projected onto the inside of her eyelids during sleepless hours. Cut the trunk cleanly, with the right tool, at the right moment, and the tender would be a very expensive paperweight clamped to a dying station. Do it wrong, and you'd vent the bay, fry yourself, or hand the Combine an excuse to stop being careful.

Juno lay in the junction and listened to carrier wave walk his rounds above her, and waited for the station's rotation cycle to bring the bay's ambient power down to its lowest ebb.

Sefa was supposed to be watching the ladder.

That had been the compromise, the one Casper had fought for and lost: Sefa would not go into the tunnels. She was seventy-something years old, half-blind in one eye, recovering from weeks of hard travel, and she was the living archive—they needed her voice intact more than they needed her hands. But Sefa had looked at Casper with an expression Juno was only beginning to learn how to read, the old woman's face a landscape of patient, immovable weather, and said something in Vessic that made Casper go still.

*What did she say?* Juno had asked.

Casper's jaw had worked. *She said she carried me out of a burning settlement when I was four years old, and she has not yet finished carrying me.*

So Sefa came. Sefa sat at the base of the vertical shaft with a signal mirror and a hand lamp shuttered to a sliver, and her job was to watch the ladder and flash the warning pattern if anyone came up or down, and she had accepted this role with the grave dignity of a woman accepting a medal.

Juno checked the time against the rotation cycle. Three minutes to minimum power.

In the dark, in the hum, she thought about the handshake protocol. It seemed like a lifetime ago that she'd cracked it—a different girl, a smaller crime, shame still fresh enough to taste. *You pinged us to the Combine,* Casper had said, and Juno had stood in the Vellum's cramped galley waiting for the rage, and Casper had instead pulled out a schematic and said, *Show me how you did it. If you can find us, you can find them.* That was the moment, Juno understood now. Not the vote, not the broadcast plans, not even the fire over dead space. That moment, when Casper chose mentorship instead of fury, had been the moment Juno stopped being a stowaway and started being crew.

She owed the ship a sabotage. She intended to pay in full.

The hum dropped. Power minimum. Juno slid forward on her elbows, four meters of tight crawl, and reached the junction box. Its access panel came away with a click that sounded, in the silence, like a gunshot. She froze. Counted ten breaths. Nothing moved but the dust.

Inside the box, the trunk cable ran thick as her wrist, wrapped in insulation the color of dried blood. The cutting tool Idris had modified sat in her chest pocket, warm from her body heat: a ceramic blade in a ceramic housing, no spark, no signature, silent. He'd spent two days on it, working in the Vellum's ruined engineering section by lamplight, cursing softly in three languages, and when he'd handed it over he'd said, *This cuts anything that isn't alive. Try to keep it that way.*

One cut. Clean, perpendicular, through the trunk at the point where the tender's systems drew their feed. The tender would switch to internal batteries automatically—that was fine, that was planned—but the batteries were old, misrated, and Idris had calculated they'd hold maybe six hours before the tender's captain faced a choice: unclamp and limp home, or stay clamped and go dark. Either way, the boarding operation starved.

Juno set the blade against the cable. Breathed out. Pressed.

The insulation parted like skin. Underneath, copper gleamed dull and honest. She sawed, slow and steady, feeling the resistance change, feeling the strands give one by one—

Below her, faint as a heartbeat, the signal mirror flashed.

Three pulses. Warning.

Juno's whole body went cold. She kept cutting—stopping now meant a half-severed cable, a fault, a spark, worse—and strained her ears down the shaft. Boots. On the ladder. More than one set. Coming up fast, toward Sefa, toward the junction where Juno lay exposed with a dead man's tool in her hand and blood-colored insulation curling off the blade.

They weren't supposed to know. Idris had been sure they weren't supposed to know. Which meant either Idris was wrong, or someone aboard the tender had gotten curious about the quiet old woman who'd been seen near the shaft access, or—

It didn't matter. None of it mattered. What mattered was that in ninety seconds, boots would reach the top of that ladder, and Sefa would be sitting there in plain sight with a signal mirror in her lap, and Juno would be trapped in a crawl space with no exit except down.

She finished the cut. The cable parted. Somewhere below, the tender's lights must have flickered; somewhere below, alarms she couldn't hear began to scream. She shoved the blade into her pocket, kicked the panel closed, and threw herself backward down the crawl toward the shaft, moving on elbows and knees, faster than she'd ever moved in her life, the walls scraping her shoulders, the sweet coolant taste thick in her throat.

She reached the shaft mouth in time to see everything and do nothing.

Two of them had come up the ladder—carrier wave and another one, big, unhurried, sidearms drawn but lowered, the posture of men doing a job they'd done a hundred times. Sefa stood at the base of the shaft, between them and the ladder's foot, small and straight-backed, her shawl dark against the gray deck. She wasn't running. Juno realized, with a lurch, that Sefa had put herself deliberately in the narrowest part of the passage, where two people could not pass abreast, where an old woman's body was worth exactly as much as a barricade.

The tall one—carrier wave—said something Juno couldn't hear. His weapon came up a few degrees.

*Sefa!* Juno's voice cracked out of her, too loud, echoing down the shaft, giving herself away completely, and she didn't care, she was already swinging onto the ladder, dropping, rung to rung, the world reduced to metal and gravity and the terrible arithmetic of distance.

What happened next happened in the time it took her to fall three rungs.

The tall soldier raised his stunner—not lethal, she'd understand later, they wanted prisoners, the Combine always wanted prisoners—and fired, and Sefa moved. Not away. Into it. She stepped sideways, into the beam's path, arms spreading wide the way Juno had seen her spread her arms over the translation table, over the old texts, over everything fragile and precious that needed covering, and the charge took her full in the chest.

An old woman's heart did not agree with a stunner tuned for adults in armor.

Sefa went down without a sound. That was the worst part, the part that would live in Juno's dreams for years afterward: no cry, no cry at all, just the soft collapse of a body folding the way cloth folds, and then the soldiers turning, startled, toward the noise Juno was making on the ladder, and then—

Light. Noise. Casper.

Juno never fully reconstructed those seconds. Later, piecing it together from Idris's account and her own broken memory, she understood: Casper had been coming down the secondary access with a pry bar and fifteen years of an investigator's instinct for exactly where violence would happen next, and she had arrived the way weather arrives, total and undeniable. The pry bar caught carrier wave across the wrist; his stunner skittered away into the dark. The other soldier got a hand on Casper's collar and Casper did something short and brutal that Juno had never seen her gentle captain do, and then Idris was there too, filling the passage, and the two Combine men made the calculation that soldiers make when the math turns bad, and retreated down the ladder, dragging their dignity behind them.

And then it was quiet, except for Juno, kneeling beside Sefa on the cold deck, saying her name over and over in a voice she didn't recognize.

Sefa's eyes were open. That was the mercy and the horror of it—she was conscious, she was present, she watched Juno's face with an expression of such gentleness that Juno felt her own face crack apart.

*I'm sorry,* Juno said. *I'm sorry, I'm sorry, I cut the cable, it worked, it worked, you have to—*

Sefa's hand found hers. The grip was weak but it was deliberate, and it squeezed twice, and Juno knew the pattern—it was the acknowledgment pulse, the handshake, the first thing Sefa had ever taught her in Vessic sign. *Received. Understood.*

Idris was already working, hands moving with the terrible calm competence of a man who had triaged a hundred casualties and hated every skill he possessed. His face told Juno everything before his mouth could. Stunner charge directly through the cardiac region, geriatric patient, delayed evacuation, no medbay, no autodoc, the Vellum's medical kit consisting of tape and hope.

*Get her to the reading room,* Idris said quietly. *Warm. Lights low. Go.*

They carried her. Casper took the shoulders, Juno the feet, and Sefa weighed almost nothing, which was its own kind of unbearable—to hold the whole weight of a language, a history, a people, in your hands and find it lighter than a crate of salvage.

The reading room smelled of old paper and lamp oil, anachronisms Oyelaran refused to surrender, and it was there, under the low amber light, that Sefa asked for the recorder.

Oyelaran produced one from some hoarded drawer—an ancient handheld unit, chunky, with a physical button, the kind of machine that would still work in fifty years when everything else had turned to static. Idris had done what he could, which was not enough: warmed her, monitored her, slowed whatever was failing in her chest without being able to name it or stop it. Sefa had listened to his report with her eyes closed, and then opened them and looked at Casper, and spoke in Vessic, long and low, and Casper's face did something Juno had never seen it do—came apart and reassembled, came apart and reassembled, like a signal fighting interference.

*She wants the recorder,* Casper translated, her voice scraped raw. *She says the broadcast is missing something. It has documents, data, testimony from the dead. It doesn't have a living voice. She says a language only survives if someone speaks it while they're still breathing.*

*No,* Juno said. *No, she's fine, she just needs rest, Idris said—*

Idris said nothing. He looked at the floor. That look was the truest sentence in the room.

Sefa beckoned Juno close. Her hand was cool now, cooler than the deck plates had been, and she pressed the recorder into Juno's palm and closed Juno's fingers around it, both of her old hands wrapped around one young one, and she spoke slowly, in Vessic first and then in trade-common, each word placed like a stone in a wall:

*You will hold it steady. You will press the button when I nod. And whatever I say, you will not stop the recording, and you will not cry loudly enough to be heard on it. Can you do this, little frequency?*

Juno hadn't known Sefa knew her private name for the soldiers. She hadn't known Sefa noticed anything she did. A laugh came out of her that was mostly grief, and she nodded, and thumbed the record button, and the little machine's indicator glowed red like a single coal.

Sefa straightened against the cushions as much as she could. She looked at Casper. She looked at Idris, at Oyelaran hovering in the doorway, at last at Juno. And then she began to speak.

It was Vessic, all of it, flowing and formal and old, a register none of them had heard from her before—not the patient teaching voice, not the wry kitchen-table voice, but something liturgical, something that sounded the way the signal had sounded the first night Juno heard it crackling out of dead space. She spoke of the colony, and the burning, and the ships that came to sell the survivors. She spoke names—dozens of names, a ledger of the vanished, recited from a memory that had carried them alone for sixty years. She spoke of a child she had carried out of a fire, and Juno saw Casper turn her face to the wall and shake silently. And at the end, her voice thinning but never breaking, she spoke a blessing over the recorder itself, over the machine and the message and whoever would hear it, in words Juno didn't understand and would spend the rest of her life learning to.

When Sefa finally fell silent, the recording light still burning, her eyes stayed open a moment longer, fixed on Juno with that same gentle attention.

Her lips moved. No sound came. But Juno read it, the way she read everything, the way she'd been taught by a woman who believed nothing transmitted was ever truly lost:

*Received. Understood.*

The red light glowed on. Outside, in the dark of the tender bay, the sabotaged ship sat dying on its batteries—and somewhere beyond the hull, Juno knew, the Combine was already counting the hours until they came to finish what the stunner had started.
