## 17. Sefa's Last Transmission

Pain, Sefa had learned in her ninety-one years, was a language too. It had its grammar, its tenses, its vocabulary of fire and pressure. The wound in her side spoke to her now in long compound sentences, each one longer than the last, and she listened the way she had once listened to her grandmother recite the genealogies of Vess—patiently, attentively, knowing that what was being said would not be said twice.

The med bay of the relay station smelled of ozone and antiseptic and, beneath it, the cold mineral breath of a place that had been sealed too long against vacuum. Idris had done what he could with the equipment Oyelaran hoarded like a dragon's treasure: a coagulation field that flickered when the station's power cycled, a drip of synthesized plasma, a blanket woven from some synthetic fleece that could not have been less like the wool of Vessic highland sheep and yet reminded her of it anyway, because warmth is warmth, and the body does not check its provenance.

She was dying. She knew this the way she knew weather coming, the way she had known, at nine years old, that the ships on the horizon were not traders. There was no drama in the knowledge. It simply arrived, settled into her bones, and made itself at home.

What surprised her was how much there was still to do.

* * *

They had moved her, over Idris's objections, into the relay's archive chamber—not because she had asked aloud, but because Juno had seen her looking at it. The girl missed nothing. She had the eyes of someone who had grown up listening for footsteps she wasn't supposed to hear, and so she caught the small hunger in Sefa's gaze when the old woman's head turned toward the room where forty years of Oyelaran's custody sat humming in the dark.

Now they had propped her cushions against the archive's main console bank, and the machines breathed around her—a sound like slow tidal respiration, like the sea caves of Vess's southern coast where her mother had taken her once, only once, before the Purging took everything else.

Casper came in quietly. She always did. Even now, even with the Combine fleet burning hard toward them and eleven days gone from the countdown and the whole architecture of their survival balanced on a knife's edge, Casper Eriksen entered rooms as if she were trying not to disturb the air. Fifteen years of practice at being no one. Sefa had watched her for weeks now, the way one watches a bird that has forgotten it has wings.

"You should be resting," Casper said.

"I am resting." Sefa patted the console beside her. "This is what rest looks like, at my age. Sitting still while important things happen around you."

A ghost of a smile crossed Casper's face and vanished, the way all her expressions did—brief weather over deep water.

"I need to record something," Sefa said. "In Vessic. For the broadcast."

Casper went very still. Sefa had learned to read that stillness too. It was not reluctance. It was the stillness of a woman being handed something she did not believe she was allowed to hold.

"The archive has testimony," Casper said carefully. "Documents. Fleet records. Program manifests. Names."

"Names." Sefa nodded slowly. "Yes. And names are bones, child. Bones tell you a person existed. They do not tell you a person *spoke*." She laid her hand flat against her own sternum, against the voice that was failing there. "When your broadcast goes out—and it will go out—the whole settled dark will learn what was done to us. They will learn about the Harvest Program, the indentures, the wrecks dressed up as accidents. They will learn we were victims." Her eyes, clouded with age and pain, fixed on Casper with an old sharpness. "But they will not learn we were *people*. Not unless they hear us. A language dies twice, Casper Eriksen. Once when the last speaker stops breathing, and once when no one remembers what it sounded like. The first death I can't prevent anymore. The second—I can."

* * *

Juno brought the recorder. Of course she did; the girl had become the ship's signal-keeper without anyone appointing her, the way water becomes a river without anyone deciding. She set the small device on the console with the exaggerated care of a teenager handling something sacred, then hovered, uncertain whether she was wanted.

Sefa beckoned her close.

"You," she said, "will sit where I can see you. Not because I need an audience. Because you are the reason."

Juno blinked. "I don't— I'm not Vessic. I don't even know where I'm from, really. Nobody ever told me, or nobody knew."

"And yet you cracked a handshake protocol written by a dead civilization in six days, using grammar you reverse-engineered from static." Sefa smiled, and the smile cost her something; she paid it anyway. "Languages do not care about blood, child. They care about attention. You gave this language more attention than anyone has given it in two hundred years. That makes you kin. Kinship is a verb, not a noun. Remember that."

Juno's chin trembled, just slightly, and she sat down cross-legged on the deck plating where Sefa could see her, hugging her knees, making herself small and attentive in the way of students who understand they are in the presence of the last of something.

Idris stood in the doorway. He did not come in. He had the look of a man who felt his uniform still clinging to him even after he'd burned it—guilt worn like a second skin—and Sefa had decided weeks ago that she would not absolve him, because absolution was cheap, but she would include him, because inclusion was not. He would stand in doorways for the rest of his life, she suspected, half in and half out, and perhaps that was its own kind of penance, and perhaps it was its own kind of belonging. She let him be.

Oyelaran did not come at all. But through the open channel Juno had patched between the archive chamber and the hermit's nest of consoles deeper in the station, Sefa could hear the faint rhythmic clicking of ancient keys, faster than usual. Listening. Always listening. Forty years of guardianship did not end just because the guardian distrusted company.

Sefa closed her eyes. Breathed. Let the pain recede to the edge of her awareness, where she could keep it like a dog kept outside a fence.

Then she opened her mouth, and spoke in the tongue of the dead.

* * *

*"Vessic. Sel-anneth koru vash."*

*I speak in the name of the living.*

The words came slowly at first, rusted at the hinges. Two centuries of suppression lived in her throat; she had been beaten for speaking it as a girl in the indenture camps, had hidden it inside lullabies disguised as nonsense syllables, had taught it to her children in whispers under blankets, had buried it so deep in herself that sometimes, in middle years, she had feared she had lost it entirely. But language goes down to the marrow. Language is what the body says when the mind forgets. And now it rose in her like groundwater finding a crack, and she let it.

She began, as all Vessic speech began, with the land that no longer existed.

*"Sel-vess anna tor. Sel-vess anna kiri-halenn."*

*We came from the high valleys. We came from the sheep-country and the salt wind.* She described the terraced fields cut into mountainsides that had been old when Earth's nations were young; the way the morning fog came up the valleys like a living thing, patient and gray; the bells the shepherds hung on their doors, tuned to five notes, so that a stranger approaching in the night would always be announced by music. She described the winter festivals, the bread baked with ash and honey, the songs sung in rounds so that no single voice ever had to carry the whole melody alone—the song was the *between*, the overlap, the place where voices met.

Her voice steadied as she went. The pain retreated further. This was the strange mercy of it: the language was analgesic. Speaking Vessic was like returning to a house she had been locked out of for eighty years and finding the stove still warm, the kettle still on, as if she had only stepped out for a moment and the world had waited.

She told them about the Purging—not the sanitized version, not the version in the histories where it was called a "corporate restructuring of assets"—but the true one, in the words her grandmother had used, words that had been banned from every official record: the day the sky filled with ships that did not answer hails; the week the colony's fusion plants were switched off remotely, district by district, like lights in a house going dark room by room; the offers that were not offers—sign the indenture contract or freeze; the children separated from parents at the processing stations because the Program's actuaries had determined that unattached minors assimilated more efficiently into labor pools. She said the word for it that her people had coined in those first terrible years, a word that had never been permitted translation into any trade tongue:

*"Vess-korath."*

*The Unmaking.*

And then, because grief alone was not the inheritance she wished to leave, she turned to other things. She recited the genealogies—not names, which would mean nothing to strangers, but the pattern of them, the way Vessic families traced themselves back through mothers, through grandmothers, a litany of women reaching back four hundred years to the first landing barges. She sang three bars of a shepherd's song, the one about the fog and the lost lamb, her voice thin and wavering and absolutely clear. She taught, in the space of sixty seconds, the five-note door-bell melody, note by note, so that anyone listening might hang it in their own doorway someday, on some other world, and be announced by music.

She spoke to the descendants—the scattered ones, the ones who would hear this broadcast and learn for the first time what they were. Some of them, she knew, had grown up as she had, with half-remembered syllables and no explanation, grandparents' secrets, forbidden lullabies, a name for the moon that matched no official chart. To them she said:

*"Sel-anneth koru vash. Sel-anneth koru sel."*

*You are not orphans. You are exiles, and exile ends.*

She told them the language was theirs to take. That it did not require permission. That a tongue survived not in archives but in mouths—in kitchens, in doorways, in the private names lovers give each other, in the words mothers murmur over cradles. That the Program had stolen their world and their history and their families, but had failed, had *failed*, to steal this, because you cannot confiscate what lives in the space between two people who refuse to stop saying it.

When she reached the end, she paused. The archive chamber hummed. Juno was crying silently, tears running freely down her face, making no sound at all, hugging her knees tighter. In the doorway, Idris had finally stepped inside. His hand rested on the doorframe the way a man rests his hand on a coffin rail.

Sefa looked at Casper.

"There is one more part," she said, in Standard now, her voice frayed to thread. "But it is not for the broadcast. It is for you. Turn the recorder off for this."

Casper hesitated—then reached out and stopped the device.

"I carried a message for forty years," Sefa said. "Given to me by the last archivist of the Vessic diaspora council, before the council dissolved and its members scattered into labor contracts across three systems. She told me: *One day someone will come asking the right questions. When they do, give them the words.*" She drew a breath that hitched in the middle. "I thought for decades that no one would ever come. I grew old waiting for a question that was never asked. And then a cargo hauler with no flag decoded a distress signal in a dead language, and came asking exactly the right questions, and I understood—" her voice broke, and she let it break, because Vessic mourning did not hide its breaking, "—I understood that the message was never meant to be delivered by me. It was meant to be *become*. I was not the messenger, child. I was the seed. And seeds exist to be buried."

"What were the words?" Casper asked. Her voice was rough. Sefa had never heard it rough before.

Sefa spoke them in Vessic first, slowly, so Casper could hear their shape:

*"Korath sel-anna. Vess iru halen."*

Then, in Standard, translating for the first time in her life a sentence she had sworn never to translate:

*"You were never erased. You were only waiting to be remembered."*

Silence held the chamber. The machines breathed. Somewhere far off in the station's guts, the countdown ticked on beneath the threshold of hearing, patient as gravity.

"Now," Sefa said, and pressed the record key herself with a hand that shook but did not miss, "we finish. The last section. Listen well, both of you, because after this, I will say these words only once more, and then never again."

She straightened against her cushions as much as the wound allowed, lifted her chin, and addressed the recorder—addressed the future—with the full remaining authority of her ninety-one years and the last living breath of a murdered world:

*"Sel-vess anna tor. Sel-vess anna kiri-halenn. Korath sel-anna. Vess iru halen."*

*We came from the high valleys. We came from the sheep-country and the salt wind. You were never erased. You were only waiting to be remembered.*

*Hear us. Say it after us. Say it in your kitchens and your doorways. Teach it to your children as a secret, and then teach it to them as a gift. We are the last voice of Vess, and after us, you are the first.*

*Sel-anneth koru vash. Sel-anneth koru sel.*

*Exile ends.*

She let the final syllable ring and fade into the hum of the machines, and then she closed her eyes, exhausted in a way that had nothing to do with the wound, and felt—for the first time since the ships came over the horizon when she was nine years old—entirely finished. Not defeated. Finished. A book closed properly, spine uncracked, set on a shelf where hands could find it.

Juno scrambled forward and stopped the recording, checking the file with trembling fingers, and then looked up at Sefa with wet eyes and asked, barely audible, "Can I— would you teach me? All of it? While you—"

"Yes," Sefa said. "That is exactly what you will do. Starting tomorrow. Every day I have left. Bring your impossible memory and your stubborn heart, and we will fill them both."

It was Casper who noticed it first—or rather, it was Casper whose face changed first, that careful blankness cracking into alarm. She was looking past Sefa, at the console behind her, at the countdown display Juno had mirrored there for the crew's reference.

"Sefa," Casper said quietly. "The timer. It changed."

Sefa turned her head. The numbers that had read eleven days for weeks now read ten—and below them, in characters that had never appeared before, a new line of text was assembling itself letter by letter, in Old Vessic, in the archive's own dead hand:

*A voice has spoken. Custody may pass. Choose within one cycle.*

Behind them, from deep in the station, Oyelaran's typing had stopped.
