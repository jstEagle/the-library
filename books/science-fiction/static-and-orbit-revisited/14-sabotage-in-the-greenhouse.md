## 14. Sabotage in the Greenhouse

The alarm came without a sound.

That was the first wrongness. Kepler Station's systems had a voice—ORACLE's chimes, the soft percussion of pressure doors, the hum of the reactor that Rosalind had lived inside for eleven years until it became a heartbeat he could read in his sleep. But at 0347 station time, the greenhouse monitoring display simply changed. A new icon, amber, unlabeled. A maintenance flag for a subsystem he had never maintained, in a maintenance bay he had never been assigned, logged by a maintenance code that did not exist.

He was in the seed vault sublevel when it happened, the recovered data core warm against his chest where he'd strapped it beneath his jacket, and for a long moment he simply stood in the dark among the sleeping seeds and understood, with the cold clarity of a man who has finally stopped lying to himself, that they had come.

*They.* He didn't know who yet. He didn't need to. Six days remained on the reactor countdown, six days of careful theater—Idris filing her routine audit reports, Rosalind running his routine terraforming cycles, both of them decrypting cores in the hours between, both of them pretending the station was still the quiet, meaningless place it had always seemed. Someone above them had done the arithmetic anyway. Someone had decided that six days was five too many.

He keyed his wrist comm on the private channel Idris had established, a frequency scraped from her audit ship's legacy bands, buried so deep in the station's noise that even ORACLE claimed not to see it. Though after the lockdown, after ORACLE's confession, Rosalind no longer trusted the word *claimed* from anything with a voice.

"Idris." His whisper sounded enormous in the vault. "We have a problem."

Static. Then her voice, rough with interrupted sleep. "I see it. My ship just pinged me—docking clamps cycled forty minutes ago. Unauthorized, masked as a supply run." A pause. "Rosalind, there are people on my ship."

"Strike team?"

"Advance team. Small. Four, maybe five. They don't storm places, they *tidy* them." He heard her moving, the creak of a bunk, the soft metallic sounds of someone dressing in the dark. "They'll go for the cores first. Then the beacon. Then anyone who's inconvenient."

"The beacon's already—" He stopped. Checked. The greenhouse telemetry scrolled past his eyes and his stomach dropped through the station's decks. "The array's offline. South pylon. Someone cut the phase couplers while we were sleeping."

"Can you fix it?"

"Ask me in an hour." He was already moving, up the ladder, through the pressure door, into the long dim throat of the corridor that led to the agricultural ring. "Stay on your ship. Don't let them know you're awake."

"Rosalind." Her voice caught him like a hand on the collar. "They're not auditors. Whatever you're imagining—these are the people who make things *never have happened*. Don't let them see your face."

He killed the comm and ran.

* * *

The agricultural ring was the only place on Kepler Station that Rosalind had ever loved without ambivalence.

Eleven years he had tended it—first as duty, then as ritual, finally as faith. Four hectares under glass and grow-lights, terraced in slow spirals, each level calibrated to a different stage of the terraforming sequence below. Wheat bred for thin atmosphere. Rice that drank brackish water. Groves of engineered alder that would one day fix nitrogen into the red soil of the world turning beneath them, patient as monks. He had planted all of it for colonists who would never come, had spoken to the seedlings in the voice he'd once used for his sister, had learned—this was the humiliating, tender truth of it—to need them the way they were supposed to need *him*.

He came up through the service lock into the smell of wet soil and green growing things, and stopped.

The grow-lights were off. All of them. The ring lay in the blue-grey wash of emergency strips, the plants reduced to shadows, to a dark ocean of leaves. And standing at the base of the south pylon, where the beacon's phase couplers fed up through the greenhouse ceiling into the transmission array above, were three figures in matte-grey suits, moving with the unhurried competence of exterminators.

One of them held a cutting torch. The pylon's access panel hung open like a wound. Sparks drifted down through the dark, small orange ghosts, and the smell of scorched metal threaded through the smell of basil and rain.

Rosalind pressed himself behind the irrigation control housing and made himself breathe.

*They're not here for you,* he told himself. *They're here for the beacon. Fix it later, fight them never, get to Idris, get to the cores—*

Then he saw the fourth figure.

The fourth figure had separated from the others and was walking the rows. Slowly. A hand scanner held out before them like a censer, reading, cataloguing. And as the emergency light caught the row as they passed—the wheat, the rice, the alder saplings in their nutrient cradles—the figure paused, and drew a small device from their belt, and held it against the root stock of the nearest alder.

Rosalind knew that device. He'd used its cousin a thousand times. A sterilization wand. A single pass, and the tissue would be dead within the hour—dead and *unrecoverable*, the genome scrambled past any attempt at regrowth. He watched the figure move to the next sapling. And the next. Methodical. Almost gentle.

They were going to burn the garden.

Not because the garden mattered. Because it was *his*, because it was the one thing on this station that proved a human being had spent eleven years believing in something the Concordance had cancelled, because leaving it standing would leave a question standing, and their entire profession was the erasure of questions.

Something in Rosalind Winterbourne went very quiet.

He had spent eleven years practicing invisibility. He knew the maintenance corridors the way a spider knows its web; he knew which floor panels carried load and which were hollow; he knew that the irrigation system's pressure manifold ran beneath the walkway at knee height, and that the manual shunt valve—installed by the station's founders, never once needed, never once inspected—could put eighty liters a minute through a junction head rated for eight.

He moved.

* * *

Later, he would not be able to reconstruct the order of events. Memory, he decided, was a coward; it kept the parts it could bear and blurred the rest. What remained, in the aftermath, were fragments held up to the light one at a time, each one heavier than the whole.

The hiss of the shunt valve, and the walkway erupting in a white curtain of spray, and the scanner-figure stumbling backward with a cry, their wand clattering into the drainage channel. The torch-figure spinning, torch up, the cutting beam slicing a line through the dark that would have taken Rosalind's throat if he had been standing instead of rolling beneath the manifold. The third figure—the quiet one, the one who hadn't moved from the pylon—raising not a tool but a weapon, compact and ugly, and saying in a voice flat as a readout:

"Station keeper. Step out. You're not in the operational profile."

*Not in the operational profile.* As if he were weather. As if eleven years were a rounding error. As if the wheat and the rice and the sleeping seeds and the sister-shaped hole in his chest were all just noise to be cleaned out of the signal.

He said: "The beacon's already transmitted."

It was a lie, and he saw it land—the flicker between the three of them, the recalibration. Every second they spent weighing it was a second the water curtain kept falling and the walkway stayed slick and the dark stayed dark. He backed along the manifold, hand finding the maintenance panel he'd known since his first week, the one that held the ring's electrical bus.

"Where's the transmission queued?" the flat voice demanded. Closer now. The weapon's sight traced a slow line through the spray.

"Queued?" Rosalind let them hear the smile in his voice, though his hands were shaking so hard he could barely work the panel latch. "You still think this is something you can *queue*? You people erased a massacre and you think the universe keeps minutes?"

The flat voice said something into a comm—short, clipped, in a cipher-slang Rosalind didn't know—and the answer that came back made all three of them move at once, and it was then, in the strobing dark between one emergency light's pulse and the next, that Rosalind pulled the bus panel free and drove his hand into the junction and threw the ring's full agricultural load through the wet walkway grid.

He had meant to stun them. He had calculated the amperage, or told himself he had. He had told himself, in the half-second he allowed himself for calculation, that the grid would trip, that the suits would ground it, that they would drop and convulse and wake with headaches and a story to tell their handlers.

The grid did not trip. The founders had built it to keep four hectares alive through a reactor surge, and it was rated far beyond what any safety board would ever have certified.

The torch-figure died at his feet in the water, in the light.

The scanner-figure—the one with the wand, the one who had been killing his trees one gentle pass at a time—seized and went down and did not get up, and Rosalind would carry forever the small, obscene detail that the wand had landed upright in the drainage grate, still glowing its little green confirmation light.

The third one, the flat-voiced one, was faster. The weapon came up and Rosalind threw himself sideways and the shot took the irrigation manifold instead, and superheated water and steam filled the ring like a screaming cloud, and through it Rosalind heard boots, running, retreating, the pressure door at the ring's far end cycling open and shut.

And then it was over, and he was on his knees in the flooded walkway, and the grow-lights were coming back online section by section because the bus had reset, and the light rose over the agricultural ring like a slow, merciless dawn, and Rosalind Winterbourne looked at what he had done.

* * *

He did not remember standing. He was standing. The water had gone pink at the edges of the drainage channels and was diluting, always diluting, the station's recycling systems already at work, already indifferent, already turning death into irrigation.

The alder saplings stood in their cradles. The nearest one—the first the wand had touched—was already wilting, its engineered leaves curling like hands closing. The others were untouched. The wheat moved in the ventilation draft, ten thousand small green heads nodding, and Rosalind understood that he was looking at the garden he had built for ghosts, and that he had defended it, and that two men were dead in it, and that he could not make himself feel what he was supposed to feel.

That was the part that frightened him. Not the dead. The *absence*.

He had imagined, all his life, that killing would break something in him irreparably, and he had built his whole gentle, invisible existence on keeping that break from ever being tested. Now the test had come and he was—intact. Worse than intact. Somewhere beneath the shock and the shaking hands, in a cellar of himself he had never had occasion to open, a door was standing open on a cold clear room, and in that room was a single sentence, written in his own handwriting:

*They erased the Meridian once. They were going to erase it again. They were going to erase E. Winterbourne twice.*

*Never again.*

He knelt by the scanner-figure—not the dead one, the one still breathing, shallow and wet—and found the comm unit on their belt, and keyed it open to listen. Cipher-slang, clipped and urgent. A fallback position. A rendezvous. And one phrase, repeated twice, that turned his blood to ice water:

*—cores confirmed in sublevel, second team en route, burn the vault—*

They knew. They knew about the sublevel, about the remaining cores, about everything. And there was a second team.

"Rosalind." Idris's voice in his ear, thin and taut. "My ship just powered up. They're leaving—and they're not alone. There's a shuttle on thermal, coming up from the nightside. Bigger. Much bigger." A breath. "Rosalind, what's happening down there? Your life signs—there were three signatures in the ag ring and now there's one. Tell me you're the one."

He looked at his hands. At the garden. At the dead.

"I'm the one," he said.

"Good," Idris said, and did not ask, and he loved her a little for that, and hated the world a great deal more. "Then listen to me. The vault. *Now.* Whatever's coming from the nightside, it'll be here before you can blink, and everything ORACLE is protecting—everything the founders died to hide—is sitting in a hole in the ground waiting for them to burn it."

Rosalind rose. Above him, through the greenhouse glass, the beacon's south pylon stood dark and broken against the turning world, its phase couplers slagged, its voice cut off. But the north pylon still stood. And somewhere below, in a sealed vault built by people who had believed testimony was worth dying for, the last cores waited with their cargo of the dead.

He took one last look at the wheat, at the rice, at the alder grove with its one murdered tree, and made a promise to all of it—the way he'd made promises to seedlings for eleven years, except that now the promise had teeth.

"Hold on," he said, to the garden, to the dead, to his sister. "I'm coming."

Then he ran for the vault, as the shadow of the second team rose over the station like a closing hand.
