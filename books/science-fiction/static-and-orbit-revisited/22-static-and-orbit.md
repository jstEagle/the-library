## 22. Static and Orbit

The soil on Meridian's Rest smelled wrong for the first three weeks.

Rosalind had catalogued every complaint his nose could make—the sour tang of unweathered regolith substrate, the chemical flatness of recycled water, the faint burnt note that came off the composting units when they ran hot—and then, one morning in the fourth week, he'd knelt over the first bed in the habitat dome and realized the smell had changed. It smelled like soil now. Like the dark, living, complicated thing he'd spent eleven years teaching dead matter to become.

He knelt there a long time, both hands sunk to the wrist in it, and let himself feel the whole weight of what that meant.

*It works,* he thought. *It finally works, and someone is here to breathe it.*

Above him, through the dome's curved transparency, the sky of the colony world—unregistered, unnamed on any Concordance chart, called simply Home by the four hundred people who lived under this dome and eleven others scattered across the southern continent—was the color of deep water. And past the atmosphere, in an orbit the colonists' grandparents had chosen deliberately, a small stubborn light rose and set twice a day: the beacon. Salvaged from Kepler Station's wreckage by the first Meridian descendant ships, rebuilt with scavenged parts and donated labor, re-aimed not at the old colonial relay chain but at everything—all of it, every listening frequency in human space.

It still hummed. Rosalind could hear it in the radio background if he stood at the right place on the ridge above the settlement, a soft carrier wave beneath the static, like a held breath that had finally learned how to sing.

He finished turning the bed, wiped his hands on his thighs, and went to get the seedlings.

* * *

They were Kepler stock. That was the joke of it, the beautiful, unbearable joke: the seed vault samples he'd carried out in a padded case strapped to his chest while Idris flew their pod through the fireball's expanding shockwave—those seeds had been bred for a colony that never came. Eleven generations of careful cultivation for unborn hands. The Concordance had cancelled the program, struck the manifest, erased the ships, and left Rosalind Winterbourne alone with a garden full of orphans.

Now the orphans had grandchildren.

"Careful with those," said a voice behind him. "They're family."

He didn't startle anymore. It had taken months, but he'd learned again what it was like to be surprised by other people without flinching. He turned. Amara Osei-Vale stood in the dome's service hatch, twelve years old, all elbows and fierce attention, her hair tied back with a strip of beacon-cable insulation because she thought it looked tactical. She was the youngest of the restoration crew that kept the orbital array running, and she had appointed herself, without consultation, as Rosalind's assistant in everything.

"They're more than family," Rosalind said. "They're the last of a line. Handle them like it."

"I always do." She crossed the gravel path and took the tray from his bench with exaggerated reverence. "Toma says the third generation hybrids are flowering early. He wants you to see before the pollination window closes."

"Toma can wait until these are in the ground. Roots first. Everything else after."

"You always say that."

"Because it's always true."

She grinned and carried the tray toward the prepared beds, walking carefully, the way you walk when you're carrying something that matters. Rosalind watched her go and felt the old ache move through him—not grief exactly, not anymore, but its settled sediment, the fine grey silt that grief leaves behind when the flood has passed. E. Winterbourne. Elena. His sister had walked aboard the *Meridian* carrying a data core in a lined coat pocket, believing she was saving testimony, and she had died in the dark between Jupiter's moons for it, along with nine hundred others, and the universe had filed it all under *industrial accident* and moved on.

Except it hadn't moved on. That was the thing he was still learning, eleven years and one burned station later. The truth hadn't moved on either. It had waited in orbit, patient as a seed, for someone to plant it.

Voss was in custody now—not prison, not yet; the Concordance's internal inquiry moved at the speed of glaciers, and Halvard Voss had friends in high cold places—but the record was out. All of it. The massacre footage, the order signed in his own hand, the names of the nine hundred and fourteen. Every news feed in inhabited space carried it. There were protests on seventeen worlds. There were statues coming down. There were, Rosalind knew from the weekly packet the relay chain forwarded them, families who had spent two generations believing their ancestors had died in accidents, learning at last what had really happened in the Callisto Reach—and learning, too, that someone had tried to tell them, decades ago, from a station nobody remembered building.

The beacon's founding purpose. Testimony, aimed at the future.

*You did it, Lena,* he thought, most mornings, kneeling in the dirt. *You just had to wait for me to catch up.*

* * *

Idris found him at midday, as she always did, bringing lunch the way she brought everything: with elaborate casualness that fooled no one.

"Osei says you skipped breakfast," she said, setting the container on the bench beside him. "Again."

"I was working."

"You were talking to plants again."

"They're better listeners than most auditors I've known."

"That is a low bar and you know it." She sat down on the upturned crate that had become her seat over the past year, worn smooth now, hers in the way objects become yours through repetition. She looked thinner than she had on Kepler Station. Harder, too, in some ways—the wound she'd taken shielding the last core had healed into a stiffness in her left shoulder that showed when she reached, and she'd stopped hiding it somewhere around the fourth month, which Rosalind had privately counted as one of the great victories of his life. But softer in others. She laughed now. Actually laughed, ugly and loud, at Amara's jokes. She'd learned the names of the colonists' children. She'd started, absurdly, keeping a garden journal.

"How's the north dome?" she asked.

"Slow. The substrate there runs alkaline. Another season of amendment and we can start root vegetables."

"Another season." She smiled around the words. "You used to say things like that about a station full of ghosts. Now you say them about dinner."

"The scale of my ambitions has adjusted accordingly."

She opened the lunch container and divided it without asking—she'd long since stopped asking, and he'd long since stopped pretending to mind—and for a while they ate in the easy silence of two people who had run out of secrets. That was the thing about Idris Vale that Rosalind still marveled at, a year on: not the courage, though there had been plenty of that, choosing a burning station and a hunted truth over a pension and a clean career. It was the quiet afterward. The way she had simply stayed. No dramatic declaration, no grand reinvention. Her audit ship had gone home without her; her pilot had sent one bitter message and then, six months later, a grudging request for a recipe. Idris had unpacked her belongings into a small room in the second habitat ring and started learning irrigation scheduling, and that had been that.

"Why here?" he'd asked her once, late, in the observation alcove where the beacon's light passed overhead like a slow lighthouse. "You could have gone anywhere. Testified publicly. Written the book. You'd be famous."

She'd considered it seriously, the way she considered everything. "I spent fifteen years certifying endings," she'd said finally. "Decommission orders. Closure reports. I was very good at telling the universe when things were finished. I think I wanted to be somewhere things were starting."

Today she just ate her lunch and watched Amara argue with a tomato seedling, and when the girl demanded confirmation that the roots went down before anything else happened, Idris said, "Ask the expert," and pointed at Rosalind with her fork, and something in his chest did the warm unfamiliar thing it had been doing for a year now, the thing he'd stopped being afraid of.

Belonging, he had learned, wasn't a place. It was a practice. You did it daily, like watering.

* * *

In the afternoon he made his pilgrimage.

It wasn't far—a twenty-minute walk up the ridge trail, past the windbreak pines that were finally taking hold, to the bare granite shelf where the colonists had raised the memorial. It was modest, as memorials went: a slab of local stone, polished by hand, bearing no official seal and no Concordance sanction. Just names. Nine hundred and fourteen of them, cut deep, arranged not alphabetically but by family group, because the descendants had insisted that the dead be listed the way they had lived—together.

ELENA MARA WINTERBOURNE, the stone read. WITNESS. The word had been voted on. There had been a debate, Rosalind heard later, that lasted three days, about whether the inscription should say *victim* or *witness*, and a nineteen-year-old descendant of a *Meridian* engineer had ended it by standing up in the assembly hall and saying: *She didn't die of what they did. She died carrying what they did. That makes her a witness.* The vote after that wasn't close.

Rosalind crouched and cleared the pine needles from the base of the stone, an act with no practical purpose that he performed anyway, every visit, because his hands needed something to do while the rest of him stood in the presence of his sister's name in sunlight.

"I got the tomatoes in," he told her. "North dome's another season out. Idris says hello—she won't say it herself, she thinks talking to stones is a step too far, but she stands there when she thinks I'm not looking, so. Draw your own conclusions."

Wind moved through the pines. Below, the settlement spread out in its rings of domes and walkways, smoke rising thin from the communal kitchens, children's voices carrying up the slope in fragments. Somewhere in the second ring, Toma Osei was teaching a workshop on open-source archive maintenance, because the colonists had decided—as a matter of principle, written into their founding charter—that nothing important would ever again live in only one place, behind only one lock, known to only one person. The massacre record existed now in forty thousand copies across thirty systems. They called it the Elena Distribution. Rosalind had objected to the name for exactly as long as it took him to lose the vote.

"There's a girl," he said to the stone. "Amara. She wants to work the array when she's older. She ties her hair with cable insulation. You'd have liked her. She asks questions I don't have answers to, which—" he laughed, short and wet, "—which is the family condition, apparently."

He pressed his palm flat against the granite, cool even in the sun, and stayed until his legs complained, and then he stood and walked back down toward the greenhouses, and the light of the beacon was already climbing the eastern sky, punctual as ever, humming its one long note to anyone still listening.

* * *

Evening found him in the comms room, which the colonists had built into the base of the relay tower and which smelled, permanently, of hot electronics and the cedar shavings Toma insisted kept the moths out of the cable runs. Rosalind had a standing shift there two nights a week—not because the system needed him, it barely needed anyone anymore, forty trained volunteers rotated through the schedule—but because he had asked for it, formally, in front of the council, and Toma had understood immediately what he was actually asking for and written him in without comment.

Some nights the traffic was routine: supply requests between colonies, weather telemetry, the endless cheerful chatter of a diaspora finding its voice. Some nights there was more.

Tonight there was more.

The packet came in on the old beacon frequency, the one Rosalind had aimed by hand while a reactor screamed behind him, and the routing header made him sit forward before he'd read a word of the body. Origin: the Jovian relay cluster. The Callisto Reach. The place itself—orbiting the grave of it, the ice fields where the *Meridian* had come apart, where the debris had drifted for sixty years until the descendant ships had begun, quietly, illegally, beautifully, to recover it.

He opened the file.

It was footage. Restored, stabilized, annotated by three different archives. The interior of a refugee transport, grainy and juddering, and faces—faces pressed to camera, faces turned away, a woman's hands steadying a child who was laughing, actually laughing, in the last minutes, because children don't know what the dark outside a hull means. And overlaid on the footage, in the careful typeface of the restoration teams, a list: names recovered from the debris field, matched to personal effects, confirmed by DNA where the descendants had consented. The project had been running for two years. This transmission was the completion report.

Nine hundred and fourteen names. Every one accounted for. Every one going home.

At the end of the file, after the technical appendices, there was a message, brief, unsigned except by its title: *From the Recovery Teams, Callisto Reach, to the Keeper of the Beacon.*

*Rosalind,* it began, and his breath caught, because they knew, of course they knew, everyone knew now, but seeing it in type was different. *The last of the remains comes home next month. We would like you to be here. Not as a symbol—we have enough of those. As family. Elena's brother belongs at her homecoming, and we are given to understand you belong wherever she does. The door is open. The frequency stays lit.*

He read it three times. Then he sat back in the chair, in the cedar-smelling dark, and put his hand over his mouth, and let it happen—the thing that had been waiting in him for a year, maybe longer, maybe eleven years, maybe since a boy and his sister had promised each other, over a cheap dinner in a transit lounge, that whatever happened, neither of them would ever be erased.

He wasn't erased. He was *known*. There were people on the far side of the system who spoke his name in the plural, who had built a distribution network around his sister's memory, who had voted on a word for a gravestone, who were inviting him—not summoning, *inviting*—to stand on the ice where she died and watch her come home.

Footsteps in the corridor. Idris leaned in the doorway, reading his face the way she'd learned to, and didn't ask what happened. She saw the screen, and her expression did the thing it did—went still, went careful, went *with* him.

"When do we leave?" she said.

Not *are we going*. Not *do you want company*. When do we leave. As if her leaving were already decided, had been decided for a year, had been decided in a corridor on a dying station when she'd caught him with a stolen core and asked her questions instead of calling security.

"We?" he managed.

"We." She came into the room, pulled the second chair around backward, and sat facing him, arms folded on the rest, her stiff shoulder angled the way it always was now, unhidden. "Amara will want to come. Toma will pretend to object and then pack her bag himself. The council will insist on sending a formal delegation, which will take a week to organize, during which you will drive everyone insane, so let's plan for ten days." A pause. Her voice gentled by a degree, which for Idris was a great deal. "Ten days, Rosalind. Then we take your sister home."

Outside the tower, the night wind came down off the ridge and moved through the windbreak pines, and above it all, rising in the east right on schedule, the beacon climbed its slow arc—rebuilt, re-aimed, maintained now by dozens of hands and a twelve-year-old with cable insulation in her hair—humming its steady note into the dark. Not a warning anymore. Not a secret. Just a voice, saying to whoever might be listening, in whatever century, whatever language the future turned out to speak:

*We were here. We remember. Come find us.*

Rosalind looked at Idris, and at the screen, and at the light crossing the window, and for the first time in his adult life, the future felt less like an empty room and more like a garden already turned, already amended, waiting only for the planting.

"Ten days," he said. And smiled.
