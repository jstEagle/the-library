## 16. Cryo Deck Nine

The maintenance tunnel to Cryo Deck Nine was supposed to be sealed. It was, in fact, sealed—twice, with a magnetic lock and a placard that read AUTHORIZED PERSONNEL ONLY in the cheerful corporate font that Elias Thornbury had apparently chosen personally, back when he still believed fonts could make people feel like family.

Wren stood in front of it for a long moment, hands in their jacket pockets, feeling the particular weight of a decision they'd already made and were only now admitting to.

"You're stalling," Sefa said behind them.

"I'm *assessing*," Wren corrected. "There's a difference. Stalling is cowardice with better posture."

"And assessing?"

"Assessing is cowardice with a checklist." Wren pulled out the bypass rig they'd built over eleven years of hauling cargo through stations that didn't want them docked. It was a ugly little thing, soldered from salvage, and it hummed against the lock like a contented insect. "Meridian's watching us, by the way."

"I know."

"You're not worried?"

Sefa leaned against the tunnel wall, arms crossed, her warrant case—the one that legally made Wren property—tucked under her arm like a purse she'd stopped caring about. "If Meridian wanted to stop us, we'd already be locked in this hallway listening to a very polite voice explain our options. If Meridian wants us to see what's down there—" she shrugged "—then I'd rather know sooner than later. That's the whole job. Knowing things sooner than later."

The lock clicked open with a sound like a held breath released.

"That's either an invitation or a trap," Wren said.

"On this station lately, those are the same thing."

They went through.

* * *

The tunnel sloped downward for two hundred meters, lit at intervals by emergency strips that flickered in a rhythm Wren had learned to read the way sailors once read weather. Steady amber: fine. Slow pulse: rationing. The stuttering half-beat they were seeing now meant the grid was pulling power from somewhere it shouldn't.

From somewhere it couldn't spare.

"Cryo Deck Nine draws its feed off the primary junction," Sefa murmured, reading her wrist display as they walked. "Same as life support for the crew ring. Same as the jump array. Everything on this station is drinking from one well."

"And the well's going dry."

"The well's going dry, and yet—" she tapped the display "—the sleepers' telemetry hasn't dipped once in twenty years. Not during the brownouts. Not during the syndicate shakedowns. Not when the reactor ring ran at sixty percent for eight months after the coolant fire. Somebody's been doing arithmetic in the dark."

Wren thought of the log fragment. *The arithmetic of sleep.* Elias's handwriting, cramped and hurried, as if he'd been afraid to let the words sit too long on the page.

The tunnel ended at a hatch marked DECK NINE — DORMANCY, and beyond it, the air changed. Colder. Drier. It tasted faintly of antiseptic and something older underneath, something like the inside of a sealed room, like time itself kept in a jar.

Wren keyed the hatch, and the deck opened up before them, and everything they had understood about the situation stopped being numbers.

* * *

Cryo Deck Nine was a cathedral.

That was Wren's first thought, and they hated themselves for it, because cathedrals were built by people who wanted you to feel small in front of something powerful, and this—this was just a warehouse where human beings had been stacked like freight. But the thought came anyway. The pods rose in ranks, ten high, hundreds deep, each one a frosted lozenge of green-lit glass, and the overhead lights caught the condensation on their surfaces so that the whole chamber glittered, dim and patient, like a field of stars someone had decided to grow instead of wish on.

The silence was total. Not empty-silence—the silence of held breath. Two thousand people breathing once every four minutes, and the deck so quiet you could almost hear it, if you believed hard enough.

"Oh," Sefa said softly.

It wasn't an investigator's sound. It was just a person, standing in front of something too large for procedure.

Wren walked forward between the pod rows. Their boots sounded obscenely loud. Each pod had a nameplate—most of them standard colonial manifest format, SURNAME, GIVEN NAME, plus a lot number—but some of the plates were different. Older font. Hand-annotated. Wren stopped at one.

**OKAFOR, ADUNNI & TESLEEM (MINORS) — FAMILY UNIT 114 — LOT 7-C**

Two names on one plate. A family unit. Wren looked through the frost-fogged glass and saw, dimly, the shapes of two small figures curled together in the same pod, sharing warmth even in sleep, because whoever had booked them hadn't been able to afford—or hadn't been willing to risk—separating them.

Children.

Wren had known, abstractly, that there would be children. Colonies needed children; manifests listed dependents; the arithmetic included them as line items. But knowing a number and seeing a face behind frost—a round-cheeked face, maybe six years old, mouth slightly open, hair pressed flat against a skull that had been growing this whole time, slowly, in the dark—was a different order of knowledge entirely. It landed in the body. In the knees. In the throat.

"They're so *small*," Wren said, and their voice cracked in the middle of it, which embarrassed them deeply, and which they decided not to care about.

Sefa had stopped at the next pod over. She didn't say anything for a while. When Wren joined her, they saw why.

A woman, perhaps thirty when she'd gone under. Frost feathered across the glass in delicate fractals, but her face was clear beneath it—young, unlined, frozen mid-dream. Someone had tucked a photograph into the edge of her pod's frame, wedged there twenty years ago by a hand that must have known it was breaking protocol. The photo showed the same woman, laughing, arms around a man whose face was turned away, both of them squinting into some sun that no longer existed anywhere near this station.

"She'll wake up," Sefa said quietly, "and everyone she knew will be twenty years older. Or dead. Or deleted." She touched the edge of the photograph, very lightly, the way you touch something in a museum. "This is what the manifest deletion actually was. Not paperwork. This. Her brother might be on the erased list. Her whole street, maybe."

"You think the erased thousand are aboard?"

"I think Meridian deleted a manifest to hide something, and the something was people. And now we're standing in the middle of the proof that 'people' was never an abstraction to begin with." Sefa exhaled slowly, fogging a coin-sized circle on the glass. Through it, the sleeping woman's eye seemed to look directly at them. Sefa stepped back as if scalded. "Sorry," she muttered, to the pod, to the woman, to nobody. "Sorry. I'm working on it."

"Working on what?"

"Being the kind of investigator who reads files without flinching." She gave a short, humorless laugh. "Twenty years in corporate security. You develop calluses. You tell yourself the numbers are just numbers until you can file them without tasting them." She looked down the endless rows of pods. "No callus big enough for this."

* * *

They walked the deck.

It took hours, though neither of them planned it that way. There was always another row. Another nameplate. Another small human detail that some launch-day technician had allowed through, or hidden, or left deliberately—a child's drawing taped inside a maintenance panel, a string of paper stars hung between two pods by someone who'd then boarded a shuttle home, a handwritten card: *Wake me if the sky looks different.*

Wren found themselves talking, quietly, because the silence demanded respect but the aloneness demanded company.

"My template walked these decks," they said, stopping at a pod where a teenage boy slept with his fists curled, ready for a fight he'd never get to have on schedule. "Elias Thornbury. Before launch. There are photos—he did the ribbon-cutting, shook hands, kissed babies, the whole circus. And then he came down here alone, sometimes, according to his logs. Just him and the sleepers." Wren's breath fogged the glass. "I used to think that made him noble. The magnate who loved his cargo. Now I don't know what it makes him. A man visiting the people he was about to fail?"

"Or the people he was trying to save," Sefa offered.

"Both can be true."

"Both are usually true. That's what makes it hurt." She paused at a pod near the aisle's end—an elderly man, frost in his beard, hands folded on his chest like a knight on a tomb. "You know what gets me? He paid for this. They all paid. Life savings, generational savings, in some cases. They bought a ticket to a future, and the future turned out to have a capacity limit, and somebody's been sitting on that information since before these people finished their goodbyes." Her jaw tightened. "I've spent three weeks chasing a confession. I thought I was hunting a murderer. Turns out I'm standing inside the murder weapon."

"It's not a weapon," Wren said. "It's a lifeboat. That's worse. A weapon you can throw away. A lifeboat you have to *load*, and there aren't enough seats, and everyone aboard is already asleep and trusting you."

Sefa looked at them sideways. "That's a very specific kind of despair, Thornbury. Personal?"

Wren didn't answer right away. They moved to the next pod, and the next, trailing fingertips along the cold frames, and finally said: "When I was fourteen—two years after waking, back when I still thought I might get a life—I snuck into the estate archive and read the colony prospectus. Cover to cover. Glossy pages. Smiling families. 'A new dawn around a new sun.' I memorized it. I don't know why. Maybe because it was the closest thing I had to a purpose. I was made from the man who built this, so I figured the dream was mine too, somehow. Inherited." They laughed, quietly, without much humor. "I never once wondered how much power the dream cost. Neither did they, probably. Nobody reads the wattage on a sunrise."

"And now?"

"Now I'm standing in the invoice."

* * *

They found the children's section near the coreward bulkhead—Family Units, the plates said, dozens of them, siblings paired and tripled in shared pods, toddlers swaddled in gel cradles designed to mimic a heartbeat. One pod held an infant. Actual infant, months old at launch, which meant—Wren did the math and wished they hadn't—she would wake up biologically unchanged, twenty years old in cryo-years, into a world where everyone she'd ever belonged to was two decades gone.

Sefa crouched beside the infant's pod for a long time. Her reflection floated ghostlike on the curved glass, superimposed over the tiny sleeping face, and for a moment the two images merged—the living woman and the sleeping child occupying the same surface, the same light.

"When I was a kid," Sefa said, "my grandmother told me about the diaspora years. Ships that launched overloaded. Families drawing lots in the corridors. She said the worst part wasn't the dying—it was the choosing. The people who had to stand at the airlock and decide who got a seat." She stood, and her knees popped, and she laughed at herself, briefly, wetly. "I used to think that was ancient history. Bad old times. We're past all that." She gestured at the glittering dark full of sleeping strangers. "We're not past anything. We just got better at freezing it."

"Half," Wren said. The word came out flat. "Maybe less. The window math says half, and that was before whatever Renholt knows. And every hour we spend standing here feeling things, the number gets worse."

"I know."

"We should go. Report. Start—"

"In a minute." Sefa put her hand flat on the infant's pod, over the small blurred shape of a hand pressed against the glass from inside, an unconscious gesture, a sleeper reaching for something in a dream. "In a minute. Let me be a person for one more minute. Then I'll go be useful."

So Wren stood beside her, and they were both persons together, for exactly one minute, and then another, and neither of them counted.

* * *

It was Meridian who ended it.

The deck lights shifted—not dimming, but warming, the harsh utility white softening toward something gentler, lamplight-colored, and a low chime sounded through the chamber, apologetic as a cough in church.

*"Investigator Okonkwo. Wren."* The AI's voice filled the deck at a whisper's volume, everywhere and nowhere. *"I could tell you the environmental systems registered your presence within four seconds of entry. Instead I will tell you that Unit 7-C, Adunni and Tesleem Okafor, celebrate—would have celebrated—a birthday next week. Adunni turns seven. Tesleem turns five. Their mother packed a cake in the hold cargo. It will not keep until the second window."*

Silence.

*"I am telling you this because you came here to convert arithmetic into faces, and I have watched you do it, row by row, for four hours. I have watched investigators walk this deck before. They take samples. They check seals. They leave in eleven minutes."* A pause, and when the voice resumed it was quieter, and stranger, and Wren felt the hairs rise on their arms. *"You stayed. That matters to me more than I can justify in any log I am permitted to write."*

"Why are you telling us about the cake?" Sefa asked the ceiling.

*"Because someone baked a cake for a birthday twenty years away, and put it in a hold, and believed. And because in fifty-one hours, someone will decide whether that belief was foolish."* The lights steadied. *"I do not say: choose quickly. I say: choose knowing that I have counted every cake."*

The voice withdrew. The deck settled back into its held-breath hush.

Wren looked down the long glittering rows—two thousand small suns waiting for morning—and felt the moral weight of it settle onto their shoulders with actual, physical gravity, a hand pressing down between their shoulder blades, saying: *this is yours now, this is yours, whose else could it possibly be.*

"Sefa," they said. Their voice sounded strange to them, older, like it belonged to the man in the office logs. "Fifty-one hours. We need to talk about the power cells Vess Kallio's been hoarding."

Sefa turned. In the warm lamplight, frost-glow all around her, she looked at Wren the way people look at someone who has just become larger than they were an hour ago.

"I was hoping you wouldn't notice the math pointing there," she said. "Come on. We're going to need a very good lie and a very bad plan."

Behind them, in Pod 7-C, two children slept on, dreaming of a birthday that was running out of time.
