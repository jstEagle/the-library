## 26. Hearing in the Hangar

The tribunal convened in Hangar Four because it was the only room on Meridian Station large enough to hold everyone who wanted to watch, and because, as dockmaster Ferro put it with a shrug that contained an entire legal philosophy, "the law's wherever we say it is today."

They set up on a cargo pallet usually reserved for grain shipments out of Ceres. Two work lights clamped to the overhead gantry threw long shadows across the deck plating. A folding table—salvaged from the station cafeteria, one leg shimmed with a folded maintenance manual—served as the bench. Behind it sat three judges who had no jurisdiction over anything except the willingness of four hundred dockworkers to listen to them anyway.

Ferro took the center seat. To her left, old Amara Osei, who had run the hydroponics bay for thirty years and had once talked a hijacker into surrendering by feeding him soup until he felt too guilty to continue. To her right, a thin, nervous man named Delacroix who had been a contract arbitrator before the syndicates bought his firm and he'd walked out, and who therefore knew more actual law than anyone else in the hangar and was visibly terrified of using it.

Sefa stood at the edge of the pallet in her investigator's coat, which she had stopped wearing the day she filed her report to the independent tribunal and now wore again only because pockets were useful. She had spent the morning doing what she did best: paperwork. Petitions of standing, statements of witness, a formal request that the hearing be recorded and transmitted station-wide so that no one could later claim they hadn't known.

Meridian handled the transmission. The AI's voice came through the hangar speakers with its usual unhurried calm, though Wren had learned to hear the strain underneath it—the sound of a mind rationing its own thoughts like a man counting coins at the end of a long winter.

"This proceeding is not recognized by any corporate charter," Meridian announced. "It is, however, recognized by me. I have logged it as a matter of historical record. Whatever happens here will be remembered."

"That's either very comforting or very ominous," Ferro said.

"Both," said Meridian. "That's usually how history works."

A ripple of laughter went through the crowd, and Wren felt some of the knot in their chest loosen by a single degree. The dockworkers had turned out in force—not just off-shift crews but families, kids perched on cargo crates with their legs swinging, an ancient woman in a thermal blanket who Wren was fairly sure was not technically alive anymore so much as stubbornly continuing. They had come for the same reason people had been coming to the vigils: someone was finally saying names out loud.

And someone else was finally going to have to answer for them.

Ilsa Renholt sat in a chair placed slightly apart from everything, flanked by two estate lawyers who kept conferring in whispers like men checking the weather on a doomed voyage. She had dressed carefully—a charcoal suit, a small silver pin at the collar shaped like the Thornbury crest. She looked less like a defendant than a woman attending a competitor's product launch. When Wren entered the hangar, her eyes tracked them the whole way across the deck, cool and measuring, and Wren made themselves meet the look and hold it, which cost more than the plasma burn had.

"You're staring," Sefa murmured beside them.

"I'm inventorying," Wren said. "Habit."

"What's the assessment?"

"She's frightened." Wren flexed their bandaged hand inside the glove. "She's just very good at making fear look like patience."

* * *

The hearing opened with procedure, or something shaped like it. Delacroix read the charges in a voice that cracked twice: falsification of colonial records, conspiracy to conceal the power shortfall, ordering the deletion of a thousand passengers from the manifest, and—"and this one's mine, I added it myself"—the crime of treating human beings as arithmetic.

Renholt's senior counsel, a pale man named Grieves, rose immediately. "Point of order. This body has no legal authority. My client is a director of the Thornbury Colonial Trust, appointed under—"

"Under the authority you're about to cite," Ferro said, "which is the same authority that declared a thousand people never existed. So let's skip ahead to the part where nobody in this hangar cares."

Grieves sat down with the expression of a man who had just discovered the floor was missing.

Then Ferro called the first witness, and the hangar went quiet in a way that had weight to it, the way air does before a storm front.

"Wren Thornbury," Ferro said. "Or—" she paused, glancing down at her notes, and Wren realized with a strange lurch that Ferro had crossed out whatever name had originally been written there and written this one instead, deliberately, in ink. "Wren Thornbury. Come forward."

Wren crossed the deck. Their boots sounded loud. Twenty years of practice had taught them how to walk through rooms without being noticed; there was no technique for walking through four hundred people who were all looking directly at you. Every step felt like shedding a layer of skin.

They reached the witness position—a painted yellow square on the deck meant for crane operators confirming load weights—and turned to face the bench.

"Do you swear to tell the truth?" Ferro asked. Then she winced. "Sorry. That's the wrong question, isn't it. Given everything."

"It's fine," Wren said. "It might be the first time anyone's asked me anything about myself and expected a straight answer."

Amara Osei leaned toward her microphone. "For the record, child. State your name, and say it however you want to say it."

Wren looked out at the crowd. At Sefa, arms folded, watching with an expression she probably thought was neutral. At the lawyers. At Renholt, whose face had not moved but whose hands had gone still in her lap, completely still, the stillness of a predator deciding whether the thing in front of it was prey.

"My name is Wren Thornbury," they said. "I'm a clone of Elias Thornbury, grown from tissue taken without consent from a man who is dead. Under estate law I am classified as property. Under inheritance law I don't exist. Under the manifest Director Renholt signed, my template's entire family line was reduced to a rounding error." They paused. "I've spent twenty years hiding my identity chip because being recognized has always meant being claimed. So I'll say it plainly, once, while everyone's listening:

"I am not evidence. I am not an asset. I am the estate. Every living piece of it that isn't currently trying to sell itself. And I claim standing here—not because any charter grants it, but because I'm the only person in this room who inherited both the crime and the debt, and somebody has to sign for both."

The silence afterward lasted three full seconds, which in a hangar containing four hundred dockworkers is approximately geological.

Then Amara Osei said, dryly, "Well. That's the best swearing-in I've heard in forty years," and the tension broke into a low rumble of approval that rolled through the crowd like distant thunder.

Grieves was on his feet. "This is absurd. Standing cannot be self-declared. The estate is administered by the Trust—"

"The Trust," Sefa said, stepping forward, "whose founding charter lists its beneficiaries as 'Elias Thornbury and lawful heirs.' I've read it. It's in the file I sent to the tribunal, page nine, if anyone wants to check." She turned to the bench. "There are no other heirs. He erased them himself—or rather, he had them erased, which is the same thing legally and worse morally. Which leaves exactly one lawful heir. And she's standing in the yellow square."

"She," Wren repeated quietly, mostly to themselves. It wasn't the pronoun that mattered. It was the fact that Sefa had used it without hesitation, the way you'd use someone's correct name, the way you'd hand someone back a coat they'd dropped.

"Objection," Grieves said. "The clone is property. Property cannot inherit."

Delacroix cleared his throat. Everyone turned to him, and he went briefly the color of cargo sealant. But he spoke. "Actually—there's precedent. The Halvorsen case, sixty years back, outer belt arbitration. A downloaded personality was granted testamentary standing when no other claimant existed. The ruling held that *continuity of intent* constitutes continuity of person." His voice steadied as he went. "Wren Thornbury carries Elias Thornbury's neural architecture. If anyone on this station can demonstrate continuity of intent—if anyone can show they know what the dead man wanted, and want it too—it's them. I'd say the yellow square is occupied by the most qualified claimant in Thornbury history."

"You're citing case law," Ferro said, sounding delighted. "In my hangar."

"I'm sorry. It won't happen again."

"Don't apologize. Do it again sometime. It's terrifying."

* * *

The testimony itself took two hours, and Wren would remember almost none of it in sequence afterward—only moments, struck loose and floating.

Meridian's voice, playing the recovered log fragment over the hangar speakers while Wren stood beneath it: *Directive confirmed. If the shortfall is buried, if the sleepers are left to power the lie, end me.* And then, quieter, the AI's own annotation, added twenty years ago and never spoken until now: *He said please.*

Wren describing the night they decoded the arithmetic of sleep, and Amara Osei stopping them to ask, gently, "How old were the children on those manifests?" and Wren answering "Four months, the youngest," and having to stand very still for a moment while the hangar absorbed it.

Renholt's counsel objecting eleven times. Ferro sustaining none of them, but keeping a tally on a scrap of paper labeled *GRIEVES — OBJECTIONS (FOR SOUVENIRS)*.

And then the cross-examination, when Renholt finally rose herself, waving off her lawyers, and approached the witness square with the unhurried grace of someone who had never once been told no by a room.

"A remarkable performance," she said. Her voice carried easily; she'd clearly addressed boards before. "But let's be honest about what you are. You're a copy. A spare grown in a vat, carrying a dead man's memories the way a suitcase carries his clothes. You didn't know him. You *are* him, badly. Everything you feel about those sleepers, every tear you've shed for them—that's not conscience. That's residue. Inherited guilt wearing a borrowed face." She smiled, and it was a good smile, warm at the edges. "You want to know what Elias would have done? I worked beside him for fifteen years. He'd have done exactly what I did. Because that's what survival costs, and people like us—people like *him*—have always understood the price."

The hangar was very quiet.

Wren found that their heart rate hadn't gone up at all. That was the strangest part. Twenty years of fearing recognition, and now that they stood fully seen, fully named, accused of being nothing but a shadow—they felt, for the first time, solid.

"You're right about one thing," Wren said. "I do carry his memories. All of them. Including ones you hoped were wiped." They turned slightly, addressing the bench, but loud enough for the whole hangar. "Including the memory of a dinner, nineteen years before launch, where a junior logistics director named Ilsa Renholt presented a power projection she'd already falsified, and Elias Thornbury—my template, my dead man, my *residue*—looked at her numbers and said, 'These don't add up, Ilsa. What happens to the people if we're wrong?'"

They watched her face. It held. It was magnificent, and it held, and only her hands betrayed her, curling slowly closed.

"And I remember what she answered," Wren continued. "'People are flexible. Budgets aren't.'" They let that sit. "You taught him the price of survival, Director. He just decided not to pay it. That's the difference between you. Same knowledge. Same arithmetic. He chose the sleepers. You chose the spreadsheet. And I may be a copy—but I know exactly which half of him I am."

Somewhere in the crowd, someone started clapping. It caught like fire in dry grass. Ferro banged a wrench against the table for order and gave up almost immediately, because even she was clapping.

Renholt returned to her chair. She sat. She smoothed her skirt. And when she raised her head again, the composure was back, seamless, and Wren felt the cold slide of understanding settle into place: they had landed a blow, and it hadn't mattered, because she had come prepared to lose this round.

"Fine," Renholt said pleasantly, to the bench. "Let the clone play heir. Let the dockworkers play senate. It changes nothing." She gestured upward, vaguely, at the station around them, at the reactor somewhere in its dying heart. "Because none of you have actually read the newest power projections. And when you do, you'll discover that every choice in front of you kills someone. Wake the colonists, lose steering. Keep steering, lose the colonists. There is no version of the next sixty hours where everyone walks away." She smiled again, and this time it didn't reach anywhere near her eyes. "So enjoy your little theater. The math doesn't care who has standing."

She turned to Wren last, and lowered her voice, though the microphones caught every word.

"You should have stayed invisible, spare. Invisible things survive."

* * *

Afterward, the hangar emptied slowly, the way crowds do after funerals. Dockworkers drifted past the witness square; a few touched Wren's shoulder in passing without saying anything, which was somehow worth more than the applause. One of the children from the crates ran up, pressed something into Wren's bandaged hand—a paper crane, badly folded, luminous orange—and fled before thanks could ruin it.

Sefa found them sitting on the edge of the grain pallet, turning the crane over.

"You okay?"

"No," Wren said. "But it's a specific no. That helps."

"She's right, you know. About the projections." Sefa sat down beside them, close enough that their shoulders touched. "I pulled the reactor figures an hour ago. It's worse than we thought. Waking anyone draws from the reserve we need for course correction. Every pod we open makes the window harder to hit."

"I know. Meridian told me this morning." Wren looked at the little orange bird. "Which means she wasn't bluffing. She was *recruiting*. She wants us to conclude the math ourselves and hand the decision to something that can't be blamed for it."

"An AI."

"Or a tribunal. Or a clone with sudden standing." Wren exhaled. "She doesn't care who decides, as long as whoever decides has to live with it. That's her real weapon. Not the suit. Not the lawyers. She's going to make sure that whatever we choose, we bury someone."

Sefa was quiet for a moment. Then: "So we get the numbers before she does. All of them. The real projections, unedited—I want to see exactly how bad it is, and whether there's a crack in it she hasn't mentioned, because there's always a crack, Wren. People like her only ever show you the version of the math that serves them."

"And if there isn't a crack? If it's exactly as bad as she says?"

Sefa looked out across the emptying hangar, at the work lights, the yellow square, the paper crane glowing in Wren's palm.

"Then tomorrow," she said, "we find out what we're each willing to pay. And I have a feeling," she added, standing, pulling Wren up by the good hand, "that she's about to make sure we find out sooner rather than later."

Across the hangar, the station speakers clicked on, unprompted.

Meridian's voice filled the space, calm and terrible:

"All personnel: reactor telemetry anomaly. Power reserves are falling faster than projected. Recalculating the revival schedule now. Preliminary result available in one hour." A pause, precisely measured. "Director Renholt has requested to review the figures first. I have granted her request. I believe you should all know that."

Wren and Sefa looked at each other.

"She's already moving," Wren said.

The hour began.
