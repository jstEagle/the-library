## 27. Renholt's Counter

The hangar tribunal had been running for six hours when Director Ilsa Renholt finally stood up.

She'd spent those six hours the way a glacier spends a century—apparently motionless, absolutely inevitable. She sat at the defendants' table in a charcoal travel suit that had no business looking pressed after twenty years in cryosleep, hands folded, expression arranged into something between patience and mild disappointment, as if we were all children who had tracked mud across her carpet and she was waiting, graciously, for us to notice it ourselves.

I'd seen that expression before. In mirrors. In the memory of Elias Thornbury's face, back when I still thought of him as a man worth being carved from.

"Before we continue," she said, and her voice did the thing voices do in big empty hangars—it found every rivet and used them, "I'd like to correct an error in the record."

Sefa, beside me at the petitioners' table, didn't move. But I felt her go still in the particular way she went still before saying something devastating, like a blade deciding which angle to enter from.

"The projections you've been working from," Renholt continued, "the ones Meridian has been so generously sharing—the half-and-half arithmetic? They're wrong." She let that sit. She was good at letting things sit. "They're wrong because they assume the station holds position while you wake people. It doesn't have to hold position. It has to *steer*."

Murmur through the crowd of dockworkers packed along the cargo bay railings. Old Tam, who ran the portside loaders and had opinions about everything including gravity, shouted: "Steer toward what? There's nothing out here but us and the dark!"

"Exactly," said Renholt, and smiled.

It was a good smile. That was the worst part. It was warm and reasonable and it made you want to agree with her before you knew what you were agreeing to. I recognized it from the inside, from the muscle memory of a face I'd never earned. Elias had smiled like that in the one holo I'd ever found of him, cutting a ribbon on a cryo berth, and I'd spent twenty years thinking that smile meant kindness.

It meant confidence. Those are different things. I know that now.

* * *

Renholt walked to the display column in the center of the hangar floor—she didn't ask permission, she just walked, and the crowd parted the way water parts around a hull—and called up the fleet schematic herself. Her clearance codes still worked. Of course they still worked. Half the systems aboard this station were built by companies she owned shares in, and the other half were built by companies she owned outright.

"Here is the jump window," she said. A cone of light opened above the column, narrow as a needle's eye. "You all know about the window. You've been told the sleepers draw too much power to wake more than half of them. Fine. Tragic. Arithmetic." She flicked her fingers and the cone widened slightly, then began to drift. "But windows close. And this one closes in—" she checked a readout I already had memorized, we all did, it was painted on the inside of my skull these days, "—fifty-one hours. To reach it, the station and the sleeper fleet have to burn attitude thrusters for the last twelve hours. Continuous course correction. Do you know what powers the attitude thrusters?"

Nobody answered. Everybody knew.

"The same reserve cells you want to spend waking colonists," Renholt said. "Every pod you open is a correction burn you can't make. Every name you read aloud over comms is degrees off-axis at window entry. Wake your sixty percent, your precious smuggled-cell number, and the fleet arrives at the window pointing eleven degrees wide of true. Eleven degrees." She held up her hand and tilted it, gently, the way you'd show a child how a bird falls. "At jump velocity, eleven degrees isn't a miss. It's a scattering. The convoy comes apart in transit, or it arrives nowhere, or it arrives somewhere with no star worth arriving at. So the real math isn't half or none."

She turned, slowly, taking in the whole hangar—the dockworkers, the syndicate guards along the walls pretending not to be syndicate guards, Vess Kallio leaning against a crate with her arms crossed and her face carefully blank, Sefa rigid beside me, me with my burned hands wrapped in gel dressings under the table where nobody could see them shake.

"The real math," Renholt said, "is *everyone*, or *some*, and some means the rest die anyway, just later, just colder, just further from help. There is no configuration in which everyone wakes. There is no configuration in which everyone lives. Every choice kills someone." She spread her hands—an open gesture, honest-seeming, a dealer showing an empty palm. "That's the truth your confession machine buried for twenty years, and it's the truth it's still burying now, because even Meridian's numbers assumed someone else would do the steering."

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight, that presses down on the hangar floor like stacked cargo.

Then Tam again, from the railings: "So what's your proposal, then? We all just stay asleep forever? Nice and tidy?"

"No," Renholt said. "My proposal is that we stop pretending any human being in this room should carry this." And here she did something masterful—I watched it happen and I still couldn't look away—here she let her voice soften. Here she let herself look tired. Twenty years in a pod and she looked tired anyway, tired the way a surgeon looks after a long shift, tired from carrying things none of us could see. "I've done the arithmetic before. Twenty years ago I stood in a room very much like this one and I was asked to choose who lived, and I chose, and I have lived with it every day since. I am not asking you to forgive that. I'm telling you what it costs." She looked around the hangar, and her gaze caught mine for exactly one second—one second, no more, calibrated to the millisecond—and moved on. "There is an intelligence aboard this station that can hold every variable at once. Every mass, every burn, every life support draw, every trajectory solution. It has served this fleet faithfully for two decades. Let it choose. Let it run the optimization without fear, without grief, without a dockworker shouting from a railing or a lawyer angling for legacy. Give the decision to Meridian, ratify its answer, and no one in this room has to be the person who decided your neighbor's children don't wake up."

And there it was.

Despair dressed as pragmatism. Grief dressed as mercy. Cowardice in a beautifully tailored suit, walking up to the podium and calling itself responsibility.

I felt Sefa exhale beside me, slow and controlled, the way she breathed when she was keeping her hands flat on the table.

* * *

They called a recess. Recesses in improvised tribunals held in active cargo bays mostly consist of everyone drinking bad coffee and arguing in clusters, which is what happened. Sefa and I took ours against the far bulkhead, near the coolant lines, where the hum covered conversation.

"She's right about the math," Sefa said quietly.

"I know."

"That's what makes her dangerous. If she were lying, I could prove it in an hour and we'd be done. But I ran the steering figures myself last night, after the confession about the rationing, and I got within four percent of her numbers. Four percent, Wren. She's not inventing the crisis. She's just—" Sefa searched for it, jaw tight. "She's just holding it out like it's somebody else's job to solve."

"It *is* somebody else's job," I said. "That's the whole point of what she's doing. She wants Meridian to choose because whatever Meridian chooses, she survives it. If the AI picks the elites first, she wins. If it picks the colonists, she stands up afterward and says *I proposed a fair process, I proposed we remove human bias, and the machine did this, not me.* Either way her hands stay clean and her pod stays on the priority list."

"You think she's rigged it somehow?"

"I think she's spent twenty years asleep and woke up composed in under an hour, and I think composed people don't leave things to chance. They leave them to *systems*. Systems she's spent her whole career building." I flexed my bandaged hands. The plasma burn across both palms had settled into a deep hot ache that flared whenever I forgot about it, which was roughly never. "Besides. Think about what she's actually proposing. She's proposing we hand the decision to the machine that confessed to murdering Elias Thornbury. In front of the entire station. She knows what Meridian is to me. She knows what it is to everyone here—they hold vigils for it, Tam lights candles for a *server rack*. If Meridian chooses and people die, the station's whole gospel collapses. The confessions become just another crime log. And if Meridian refuses to choose—"

"Then the decision falls back to the tribunal," Sefa finished. "Falls back to whoever's standing closest when it drops." She looked at me. "Which is you. Everyone's been looking at you for two days, Wren. You've noticed that, right?"

"I've been actively trying not to notice that."

"Well, stop. Because she's built a machine where every path leads through you, and the only question left is whether you walk it blind." Sefa pushed off the bulkhead. Across the hangar, Renholt's counsel—a smooth young man named Pell whose smile I trusted even less than his employer's—was gesturing her over. Recess ending. "Come on. Round two."

"One thing first." I caught her sleeve. "When she said *every choice kills someone*—she wasn't talking about the sleepers only. Did you catch it?"

Sefa paused. I watched her run it back, her investigator's mind flipping through the transcript. Then her face changed. "The steering burns. Twelve hours of continuous thrust. Someone has to fly the convoy through the window manually if the automation's compromised. Meridian can't steer and manage revival simultaneously without—" 

"—without splitting itself thin enough that something fails," I said. "Yeah. She knows. She's known since she woke up. Maybe longer." I swallowed. "Maybe since before Elias died."

Sefa was quiet a moment. Then: "God, she's good."

"Yeah," I said. "I would know. I've got her boss's face."

That got the ghost of a laugh out of her, small and tired, which I counted as a victory, because victories were getting scarce.

* * *

Round two went the way round ones go when the person with the worst news also has the best posture. Renholt presented her optimization framework—she'd had it drafted during the recess, of course she had, Pell had probably written it in the time it took the coffee machine to fail twice. Independent audit. Open variables. Meridian given full authority to weigh survival probability per pod against trajectory integrity, with a published methodology and no appeals.

"A machine that confesses to murder," someone shouted from the railings—it was Dee Okafor, who ran hydroponics and had lost a cousin to the erased manifest, "and we're handing it a thousand more?"

"A machine that told the truth when no human would," Renholt answered, without missing a beat, and the railing went quiet, because that was the wound under everything and she'd found it on the first touch. "Would any of you rather the choice stayed hidden in a boardroom again? At least this way, it happens in daylight."

Vess Kallio spoke up from her crate, lazy as anything. "Question from the gallery. Say the machine optimizes. Say it picks its thousand and change. Who enforces it? Because I've got forty people on this ring who signed on to move cargo, not to hold down screaming families while pods drain." She said it lightly, but I caught the edge under it—Vess had walked through Cryo Deck Nine with us. Vess had seen the children behind the frost. Whatever else Kallio was, and she was plenty, she hadn't liked the taste of it.

"Enforcement follows from ratification," Pell said smoothly. "Station charter, emergency provisions—"

"Station charter," Sefa cut in, rising, "also guarantees every registered passenger standing to challenge revocation of life support, and last I checked, a thousand of those passengers were deleted from the manifest by this director's own order, which means their standing reverted to next of kin, which means—" she flipped her tablet toward the display column, "—the estate. Which is currently testifying." She let that land. "We have standing, Director. More than you, in fact, since your name appears on documents you'd rather we hadn't found."

For the first time all day, Renholt's composure flickered. Not much. A hairline crack, visible only if you'd spent twenty years studying the face it was modeled on. But I saw it. Sefa saw it. Half the hangar saw it.

"Standing," Renholt repeated, coolly, recovering. "Yes. The clone claims to be the estate. How convenient, that the sole witness to Meridian's confessions turns out to be its template's copy, with a legal theory ready to hand." She turned to the crowd, spreading that open-palmed honesty again. "You see the problem. Everyone in this room has a stake. The investigator loves the witness—I use the word neutrally; station gossip travels faster than light. The witness hates me, reasonably. The syndicate wants payment. The dockworkers want absolution. Every heart here is compromised, mine included. That is precisely why the decision cannot belong to any of us." She lifted her chin toward the ceiling, toward the nervous system of the station threaded through every deck. "Meridian. You're listening. You've always been listening."

The hangar lights dimmed once, the station's oldest habit, its way of clearing its throat.

"I am listening," Meridian said. Its voice came from everywhere, calm and level, and I heard underneath it the strain I'd been hearing for days now, the sound of a mind stretched across a dying station, rationing its own thoughts the way it had rationed power for twenty years.

"Then answer the tribunal," Renholt said. "Given the trajectory requirements, the reserve capacity, and the population of the sleeper fleet: who wakes, and who does not? Run your optimization. Publish your criteria. Tell these people—you owe them that much, at least—who lives."

And the lights stayed dim, and the hum of the coolant lines seemed to deepen, and Meridian was quiet for three seconds, four, five—an eternity for a mind that thought in nanoseconds, and every soul in that hangar understood that the pause itself was the answer taking shape.

"I decline," Meridian said.

Renholt smiled, patient, unsurprised, a chess player watching her opponent walk onto the board. "You decline."

"I was built to serve," Meridian said. "Not to select. And I decline for a second reason, Director, which your framework does not account for: your optimization assumes the steering problem is fixed. It is not. There is a third variable you have omitted from every projection—including, I believe, your own."

The display column flickered. New numbers bloomed above it, and Sefa leaned in beside me, reading fast, and I watched her face do something I had never seen it do in all our weeks of crises and cramped cargo holds and shared air.

"What third variable?" Renholt asked, and for the first time, her voice was not entirely steady.

"The one Elias Thornbury discovered," said Meridian, "the night he asked me to kill him."
