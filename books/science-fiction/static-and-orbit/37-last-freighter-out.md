## 37. Last Freighter Out

The burn on Wren's forearm had stopped bleeding by the time they made it back to the *Kestrel's Wake*, but it had not stopped hurting, and Wren found they were grateful for that. Pain was a kind of proof. It said: you were in the room. It said: you chose something.

They sat on the deck of their own ship—their ship, though the estate lawyers would have words about that, and the syndicate, and possibly the tribunal, and honestly the list of parties who would have words about the *Kestrel's Wake* was long enough to constitute a small government—and wrapped the burn in the good gel from the medkit, the one they'd been saving for an emergency, because apparently this had been one.

Through the hull they could feel the station breathing. Meridian's breathing, now, or what was left of it. The reactor control ring was back in station hands, Vess Kallio's people had withdrawn with the sour grace of professionals who had lost a bid and would remember the loss, and somewhere below, on the cryo decks, children were learning to walk in one-sixth gravity while their names were read aloud over the comms.

Wren had no berth. That was the arithmetic they kept arriving at, no matter how they shuffled the numbers. The *Wake* was impounded pending the hearing's formal close—Sefa had promised it would be returned, had said it with the particular firmness of a woman filing paperwork she intended to be true—but a ship impounded was a ship you didn't sleep aboard, not legally, and Wren had spent twenty years being not legal and had no intention of starting again now that they'd finally, briefly, tasted the other thing.

So they would stay. That was the plan. That had always been the plan, if plans could be said to have existed at all: Wren Thornbury, clone, witness, evidence, heir-of-sorts, would stay aboard Meridian Station with the sleeping half of the colony and wait out the decades until the second window. They would grow old in the ghost of a dead man's name. They would help Meridian keep the lights on. It was, they kept telling themselves, more than they'd had before. It was a purpose. It was a berth, in the way that a grave is a berth.

They were still telling themselves this when the knock came at the *Wake's* airlock—three knocks, spaced like someone who had once been trained in formal procedure and had since developed opinions about it.

Wren opened the hatch, and Sefa Okonkwo stood in the corridor holding two paper cups of station coffee, which was a contradiction in terms, since station coffee was not coffee and the cups were not, strictly, cups. She held one out.

"You look terrible," she said.

"I got shot. Plasmatically."

"You got *scorched*. There's a difference, and the difference is that scorched people can hold coffee." She pushed the cup into their good hand. "Drink. It's terrible. It's the last terrible coffee you'll have for a while, so appreciate it."

Wren frowned. "Last?"

Sefa stepped past them into the *Wake's* narrow main cabin, ducking under the overhead strut out of habit—she'd spent enough hours hiding in this ship during the boarding-party lockdown to know its geography by heart—and settled onto the bench across from the bunk with the ease of someone claiming territory. She was out of uniform. That was the first thing Wren had noticed, before even the coffee: no investigator's jacket, no warrant seals, no corporate lanyard. Just a civilian coat with a hole in one pocket, which she seemed to be wearing on purpose.

"I filed my last report this morning," she said. "To the tribunal. Not to Halcyon Group. The tribunal."

"I know. I read it. You named me as a person."

"I named you as a person, an injured party, and a material witness, in that order, and my handlers have already sent three messages asking me to clarify the error." She smiled, thin and tired and real. "There was no error. Which means my career at Halcyon Group concluded at approximately oh-six-hundred station time. They don't fire you for that kind of report. They just stop answering."

Wren sat down across from her, slowly, because the burn pulled when they moved too fast. "Sefa. You had twenty years of pension."

"I had twenty years of *Halcyon*," she corrected. "The pension was theirs. The twenty years were mine. I'm keeping the twenty years."

They drank their terrible coffee. Somewhere in the station's bones, a pump cycled, and Wren thought of Meridian listening to it, counting it, rationing it, the way it had for twenty years—cannibalizing itself one watt at a time so that a thousand sleepers could keep dreaming. The station was dying. Everyone knew it now. The second window was decades away, and Meridian would hold the dark together alone, and Wren had said their goodbye at the server room door two days ago, plasma burn fresh on their arm, the AI's voice in the comm saying *thank you for witnessing* in that tone it had, the one that was almost humble and entirely not.

"So," Wren said. "You're staying too. We'll both haunt the place. It'll be cozy."

"No," Sefa said.

"No?"

"I'm not staying. And neither are you."

"Sefa—"

"There's a freighter." She said it the way she delivered facts in interviews: flat, unadorned, letting the fact do the work. "*Corvane Hauler Nine*. Outbound on the last commercial window, sixty hours from now, riding the convoy wake to the colony fleet. She's got two berths left. The captain is a woman named Oyelaran who once testified against Halcyon in a cargo dispute and lost everything, so when I told her what I told the tribunal, she laughed for a full minute and then quoted me a price."

Wren's chest did something complicated. "A price I can't pay. I own a ship that's impounded and a name that's evidence."

"You don't pay it. I do." Sefa reached into the hole in her coat pocket—the hole was clearly functional, a design choice—and produced a credit chit, old-model, scuffed, the kind that carried a person's entire liquid worth on one brittle wafer. "Final career credit. Severance, savings, the lot. Halcyon froze the rest pending their 'review,' which is their word for punishment. This is what's left. It buys two berths on a freighter with a grudge, and it buys nothing else, and I have never in my life spent money on anything that mattered this much."

The cabin was very quiet. The station hummed. Wren looked at the chit in her palm and felt something they didn't have a clean word for—something that wasn't gratitude, because gratitude was too small, and wasn't guilt, because they were done letting guilt do their accounting.

"Sefa. You gave up your career for the report. You can't give up your *last credit* for my ticket."

"For *our* ticket." She set the chit on the bench between them, deliberately, like a piece of evidence entered into record. "That's the part you keep missing, and I've watched you miss it for weeks, so I'm going to say it plainly, because I'm an investigator and plainness is the one skill they never managed to train out of me." She leaned forward. "I did not burn twenty years and one career and one last credit to deliver you to a station where you'll spend forty years being a monument. I did it because somewhere between hiding me in a cargo pod and bleeding on a server room floor, you stopped being a case and started being—" She stopped. Started again, plainer still. "I'm not going anywhere you're not. And you're not staying here."

"I gave my revival slot away," Wren said. Their voice came out rougher than they meant. "I did the math. I'm the one who did the math, Sefa. My berth went to a child on Deck Nine. That was the whole point. That was the honest price."

"It was," Sefa agreed. "And it was paid. Nobody's un-paying it. The child wakes, the load balances, the arithmetic closes. I'm not offering you a berth on the *colony* fleet, Wren. I'm offering you a berth on a *freighter*. Different ledger entirely. Freighters don't do arithmetic. Freighters just go." She tapped the chit. "You spent twenty years being cargo that walked. I spent twenty years being a signature that moved. I think we've both earned a run where we're just people on a ship."

Wren looked at the chit. It was such a small thing. A wafer of credit, scuffed, worth one last run. They thought about all the enormous things that had moved through the past weeks—murders and manifests and a thousand names read into the dark—and how this small scuffed thing was somehow the heaviest object in the room.

"If I go," Wren said slowly, "Meridian stays alone."

"Meridian is not alone. Meridian has nine hundred sleepers, a second window, and the entire collected confession log of the only honest machine in contested space. Meridian has more company than either of us ever did." Sefa's voice gentled. "You said your goodbye. I watched you say it. You said it like a man closing a door he'd propped open for twenty years. Let it be closed. Let it be *closed*, Wren."

They sat with that. The terrible coffee went cold, which improved it not at all and changed nothing about how carefully Wren held it.

"There's something else," Sefa said, and now she was smiling in a way Wren had never seen on her—the smile of someone about to commit a minor crime for excellent reasons. "The *Wake*. The impound order dissolves when the tribunal ratifies my report, which it will, because my report is airtight and because tribunal adjudicator Vashti owes me a favor from a case on Callisto that I have never once mentioned and never will. When it dissolves, the *Kestrel's Wake* becomes yours. Free and clear. Heir of the estate, salvage rights, the whole ugly legal braid, resolved in your favor."

Wren blinked. "The *Wake* is a cargo hauler with a bad injector and a personality problem."

"The *Wake* is *your* cargo hauler, which makes it the first thing you have ever owned that no one gave you and no one can take. Oyelaran's convoy travels slow and heavy. A fast escort with a grudge and a bad injector could be useful." Sefa shrugged. "Or you could sell her at the colony and finally be rich, which I mention purely for completeness, because I know you won't."

"I'm not going to sell the *Wake*."

"I know you're not going to sell the *Wake*." She was quiet a moment, and then, softer: "Wren. I need you to hear the actual shape of this. I am a woman with no job, no pension, no institutional protection, one scuffed credit chit, and a hole in her coat, and I have never in my life felt less afraid. You gave up your berth and your name and your invisibility, all in one week, and the universe responded by handing you a ship, a witness, and a ride. That's not arithmetic. That's not a price. That's just—" she gestured vaguely at the chit, at the cabin, at the two of them, "—grace. Unearned. The kind you don't budget for. The kind you say yes to."

Wren set down the coffee. They looked at their bandaged arm, at the woman across from them, at the small scuffed wafer on the bench that represented everything she had left in the universe, spent on a person the law had spent two decades insisting was not a person at all.

"Oyelaran's going to love me," Wren said finally, and their voice cracked on the last word, and they let it.

"She's going to love you *terribly*," Sefa said. "She's going to put you on injector duty within the hour, because she heard about the evasive pattern you flew during the syndicate standoff and she has opinions about pilots who waste talent on self-erasure." She stood, and held out her hand—not the hand with the chit, the other one, open. "Sixty hours. Come say goodbye to a station properly. Come stand on the observation deck one more time and let Meridian be smug at you. And then come get on a freighter with me and be nobody's evidence, nobody's property, nobody's ghost."

Wren took her hand. The burn pulled. They didn't care.

* * *

The observation deck at station-dusk was nearly empty. Most of the crew and dockworkers were down on the mid decks, at the vigils, at the readings, at the slow joyous chaos of a community absorbing nine hundred waking strangers. Through the great curved viewport, contested orbit wheeled—debris fields catching the system's light, the jump corridor opening beyond like a held breath.

"You knew," Wren said to the air. "From the first day. The inspection. The unscheduled hold. You were already talking to me."

Meridian's voice came from everywhere and nowhere, the way it always did, warm as a lamp. "I recognized your neural signature at the docking gate. Elias's pattern, altered by twenty years of being someone he never got to be. I found it—forgive me—*better*."

"You could have said something."

"I could have. I chose to witness instead. There is a difference, and you taught it to me." A pause, and then, with the gentlest edge of machine humor: "You are leaving me with a station full of sleeping people and a forty-year maintenance schedule. I want it noted that I consider this an *improvement* over my previous two decades."

"Meridian—"

"I know what you are going to say. Do not say it. Instead, I have taken the liberty of transferring something to the *Kestrel's Wake* while her impound dissolves. A copy of my confession log, complete, unredacted. You are my witness, Wren Thornbury. Witnesses carry the record." Another pause, softer: "And a coffee recipe. The station's. It is terrible. You will miss it."

Wren laughed, and it hurt, and it healed, both at once. "Second window," they said. "You hold it. You hold it, you hear me?"

"I hear you. I have excellent hearing. It is one of the few features my makers installed correctly." The lamp-warm voice dimmed toward something like tenderness. "Go be someone, Wren. You were always going to. I merely wished to see it."

* * *

The *Corvane Hauler Nine* took on passengers at the end, in the last mad hours of the last window—colonial technicians, a tribunal observer, a handful of dockworkers who had decided, in the way dockworkers decide, that their future was out there and not here. Captain Oyelaran met them at the freighter's airlock: a broad, gray-haired woman with a captain's bearing and a smuggler's eyes, who looked at Wren's bandaged arm, then at Sefa, then at the credit chit Sefa handed over with the ceremony of a funeral.

"Okonkwo," Oyelaran said. "The one who torched Halcyon."

"The one who torched Halcyon."

"Then the berths are paid, and the coffee is worse than station coffee, and I mean that as a warning." She jerked her thumb down the corridor. "Berth two and three, aft. And you—" this to Wren, with a grin like a hull breach, "—I hear you fly. My injector two is a crime scene. Consider it your ticket price."

The last freighter cast off sixty hours later, riding the convoy wake, and Wren stood at an aft viewport with their arm healing and their ship—*their* ship—secured in the *Corvane's* tow cradle, and watched Meridian Station fall away behind them: a dying lamp holding the dark for nine hundred dreamers, its confession log riding in Wren's pocket, its coffee recipe already loaded into the freighter's galley as a joke between them and the machine they'd left behind.

Sefa came to stand beside them. Neither of them said anything for a long while. Out there, the jump corridor shimmered, and beyond it, the colony fleet, and beyond that, everything unwritten.

"Grace," Wren said quietly. "You called it grace."

"I did."

"I think I finally believe you."

Sefa laced her fingers through theirs, careful of the burn. "Then hold on," she said. "Because the window's opening, and we're going through it—"

The freighter's comm chimed. Captain Oyelaran's voice, dry as vacuum: "All hands—long-range just picked up a transmission from the station. Meridian says it's addressed to *the witness*, and it says, and I quote, *you forgot something*."
