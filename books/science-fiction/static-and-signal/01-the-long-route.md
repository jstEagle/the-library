## 1. The Long Route

The tomatoes were the first to forgive her.

Casper Pemberton stood in the greenhouse at dawn with the watering wand in her hand and her boots leaving crescent prints in the wet gravel, and she said, out loud, to nobody: "I know. I know. Two days late. You're allowed to be dramatic about it."

The tomatoes, being tomatoes, did not accept the apology. Their leaves had gone slightly furred at the edges, the way they did when they'd been thirsty too long and had decided, collectively, to make it her problem. She gave them a long, slow drink and felt the particular satisfaction of a debt being paid—small, domestic, the kind of satisfaction the universe had apparently decided was the only kind she'd be getting.

Outside, Verdance turned beneath a sky the color of a bruise healing. The terraformers had done good work here, whoever they'd been, all those years ago. Rolling hills stitched with engineered grass that never grew taller than a hand's width. A river with the precise, apologetic meander of something designed by committee. And the settlements—twelve of them, laid out in a ring like seats around a table—each one built to hold ten thousand people and each one holding, instead, the wind.

She had stopped counting the years somewhere around year six. It was easier that way. The colony ships had turned back after the Silence Accords, the whole sector had gone quiet as a held breath, and Verdance had been left the way you leave a cake in the oven when the guests cancel: finished, warm, and for no one.

Casper had stayed anyway. Someone had to.

* * *

Her mornings had a shape, and she trusted the shape more than she trusted most people.

First the greenhouse—tomatoes, beans, the stubborn dwarf wheat, and the citrus trees she'd grafted herself from cuttings her mother had smuggled aboard the supply freighter in a hollowed-out ration tin. Her mother had labeled the tin CITRUS – DO NOT DECLARE and Casper had laughed for a full minute the first time she'd found it, alone in a cargo bay, laughing at a joke from a woman three years dead.

Then the water reclamation shed, where she checked filters that never clogged and flushed lines that never fouled, because the engineers who built Verdance had built it to outlast its people, and had succeeded beyond anyone's hopes.

Then breakfast, eaten standing at the counter of the settlement house, listening to the radio she kept tuned to the relay band out of what she told herself was professionalism and knew, privately, was hope. The band was static. It had been static for a decade. She'd named the static, at one point, during a bad winter—she'd called it Gerald—and had long conversations with it. She didn't tell anyone this, mostly because there wasn't anyone.

Today, though, the static had a seam in it. A thin, rhythmic tick underneath the white noise, like a clock heard through a wall.

Casper set down her coffee and leaned toward the radio.

"Hello, Gerald," she said. "You've got something to say?"

The tick continued, patient, indifferent. She listened for a while, then made a note in her maintenance log—*anomalous low-band artifact, relay 7, monitor*—and told herself it was probably nothing. Harmonics. Atmospheric shear. The relay's ancient hardware dreaming.

She was very good at telling herself things were probably nothing. It was, she'd found, a core competency of long-term solitude.

* * *

The run to Settlement Four took most of the day, out along the gravel track in the old utility hauler her mother had nicknamed the Mule. The Mule was older than Casper's entire career and had opinions about hills, but it had never once left her walking, and she honored it the way you honor a colleague who has seen things.

Settlement Four was a relay maintenance stop: a squat dish array on a hill, a bunker of servers, and a perimeter of windbreak trees that had grown into something almost beautiful. Casper climbed the tower with her tool bag over her shoulder and spent the afternoon doing what she did every third day—replacing corroded couplings, re-seating boards, running diagnostics on hardware designed by people whose names she only knew from schematics.

And, in the tower's shadow, she did the other thing. The thing that had started as a joke and become a ritual.

She recorded a message.

The relay's maintenance console had an audio buffer meant for engineer-to-engineer notes: *coupling four replaced, torque specs per manual, recommend full inspection in six months.* Instead, Casper leaned toward the microphone and said:

"Good morning, future people. This is Casper Pemberton, your caretaker, your landlord, and—depending on how you feel about the tomato situation—possibly your adversary. Update from Verdance: the beans have come in. All of them. I don't know what I did right and I'm worried I won't be able to do it again. The river is behaving. The weather is behaving. I have fixed the thing that was broken at Relay Four, which was, in fact, the thing I said it was last time, so please note in your records that I was right and someone owes me a drink."

She paused. The wind moved through the windbreak trees with a sound like slow applause.

"You're probably wondering why I keep doing this. Honestly? I wonder too. But my mother used to say that a signal isn't a message until someone's listening, and a message isn't finished until someone answers. So I figure these recordings are—half-finished messages. I'm doing my part. Whenever you get here, you do yours. Deal?"

She killed the buffer, archived the file under the date, and stood for a moment in the tower's shadow, looking out at the twelve empty settlements, each one a promise that had never been collected.

"Deal," she said to nobody, and went back down to the Mule.

* * *

Her mother's tools lived in a roll of oilcloth that Casper carried in the hauler's cab the way other people carried photographs. Inside were instruments with decades of wear ground into their grips: a signal probe with a cracked housing, a tuning fork that hummed at a frequency no longer in any manual, a soldering iron with a wooden handle worn to the shape of a hand that wasn't Casper's.

Mara Pemberton had been a signal engineer, and then, after the inquiry, she had been a disgraced signal engineer, and then she had been a woman who took a caretaker's job on the far side of nowhere and raised her daughter among relays. She'd died eight years ago, quietly, in the settlement house, with the radio on. Casper had inherited the tools, the job, the loneliness, and—though she didn't know it yet, though she wouldn't know it for some time—a message.

What she knew today was only this: the tick under the static had followed her home.

She noticed it that evening in the settlement house, when she tuned the radio to the relay band and there it was again, the same thin rhythm, except now it wasn't on Relay Seven's band. It was on all of them. Every channel, underneath the white noise, patient as a heartbeat.

Casper sat down slowly on the stool by the window.

"Okay," she said. "That's new."

She pulled her mother's roll of tools across the table, not because she needed a tool—there was nothing to fix, nothing visibly wrong—but because the weight of them was steadying, the way a hand on your shoulder is steadying. She tuned through the bands one more time, listening. The tick kept its rhythm. It didn't waver. It didn't drift. It was too regular to be natural and too quiet to be intentional, which was, she thought, either a very careful signal or a very good accident.

She wrote in the maintenance log: *Artifact now present on all bands. Rhythmic. Source unknown. Monitoring.*

Then, because the ritual was the ritual, and rituals were how she kept the loneliness from curdling into something worse, she keyed the relay's outbound buffer and recorded her evening message:

"Good evening, future people. Casper again. Small update: something new on the radio tonight. Probably nothing. Probably harmonics. But if you arrive someday and find a decade of my logs full of me saying *probably nothing*, I want it on record that I noticed things. I was attentive. Give me a raise."

She paused, thumb over the key, and then, quieter:

"My mother used to say the sky is never empty, it's just shy. Goodnight, whoever you are. I'll be here."

She archived the file. She washed her cup. She went to bed with the window open to the sound of the engineered grass moving, and she slept the sleep of a woman who had made peace with being forgotten, and who had, mostly, made it honestly.

The tick under the static ticked on, all night, in a rhythm that—had anyone been counting, had anyone thought to count—was not random at all.

It was counting down.
