## 5. His Handwriting, Her Hand

The case files arrived at her door in a courier's satchel that smelled of cold metal and old adhesive, and Ivo set them on the desk in her quarters without opening them for the better part of an hour.

She told herself it was method. A good investigator let documents rest before disturbing them, the way a good surgeon washed her hands. But the truth was simpler and less flattering: she was afraid of the paper. Somewhere in those folders was a man's handwriting, and his handwriting would be *her* handwriting, and she had not yet decided how she would survive seeing it.

Terminal Station's night cycle hummed through the walls—a low, tidal sound, the station breathing its recycled air. Outside the narrow viewport, the ice plain of Vessna-4 stretched to the horizon under two moons, white on white on blue-white, so bright with reflected starlight that it seemed lit from beneath. The colony's habitat ring curved away from her window like a rib of some enormous patient animal. Forty-one years of human life, packed into a steel arc on a frozen world, waiting for her signature to end it.

She made tea from the ration tin—bitter, over-steeped, perfect—and sat down at the desk, and opened the first folder.

* * *

The files were organized with a precision that unsettled her more than disorder would have. Tabbed by incident. Cross-referenced by witness statement number. The murder of Elias Ravensworth, founder of Terminal Station, forty-one years ago, reduced to a spine of gray folders and a single evidence photograph: a stretch of corridor outside the comms array access, marked with chalk lines that had long since faded from the actual floor but lived on here in amber-toned print, crisp and permanent.

No weapon. That was the first thing. She'd known it from the summary, but seeing the gap in the physical file—the space where a weapon inventory should have been, occupied instead by a slip noting only *"no instrument recovered"*—made it real. No weapon, no struggle marks beyond what a fall could explain, no motive anyone had ever articulated above a murmur. The station's medical examiner had recorded cardiac arrest secondary to blunt trauma, and then, in a note appended years later in different ink: *cause of impact undetermined.*

A man had died alone in a corridor at 0312 station time, and the file had closed around the fact like water closing over a stone.

Ivo read witness statements until the tea went cold. There were eleven of them. Eleven people who had been awake or nearby on the night shift, eleven accounts of the hours surrounding the founder's death, and they fit together the way fitted stones fit—not seamlessly, but deliberately, each one shaped to bear weight against its neighbors.

The array technician, a young Oduya—she recognized the name from her arrival manifest, the same man who had shown her the comms deck yesterday, older now, quieter—reported hearing "a door cycle" at approximately 0300. The duty medic reported being summoned at 0320. The night steward reported seeing the founder alive at 0245, "in good spirits, headed toward his quarters." The security log showed no entries between 0100 and 0330, which the log's custodian explained as "a scheduled maintenance window."

Every contradiction had an explanation attached, pre-loaded, ready. Nobody said *I don't know.* Nobody said *I've thought about it differently since.* They said what they said the way a choir says its part—individually rehearsed, collectively harmonized.

She caught herself marking the margins in red pencil, a habit from her training, and stopped when she realized the annotations were accumulating in a hand she didn't want to look at yet. Small, slanted, economical. The letters leaning forward like runners at a starting block.

*Later,* she told herself. *First the facts.*

But the facts were a corridor with no weapon in it, and the corridor led nowhere except deeper into the files, and the deepest folder—the one tabbed in the founder's own coding system, *PERSONAL / CASE / FINAL*—sat at the bottom of the satchel like a stone at the bottom of a well.

She reached for it at 0200, station time. The same hour he had died, though she didn't plan it that way, and perhaps that was the point of everything on this station: nothing was planned that way, and everything happened that way anyway.

* * *

The final folder contained notebooks. Seven of them, cloth-bound, their covers softened by handling, smelling faintly of graphite and cold. Six were full. The seventh was not.

Ivo opened the sixth first, because its last entry was dated three days before the death, and because she was still, at that hour, pretending to be an investigator rather than whatever she actually was.

His notes were beautiful. That was the word that came and would not leave. Beautiful the way functional things are beautiful when made by someone who loved making them—dense, clear, unhurried. He wrote in a hybrid shorthand, standard colonial notation threaded with private abbreviations, and his thoughts moved across the page in paragraphs that read like someone thinking at full speed and writing just fast enough to keep up. Fleet logistics. Water reclamation yields. A complaint about the coffee. A tender, frustrated analysis of a council dispute over school funding, resolved in his own hand with a compromise so elegant she found herself nodding at the page.

And everywhere, threaded through the mundane machinery of running a colony, the signal.

*Carrier wave persists. Source still unresolved. O. suggests antenna self-resonance; doesn't hold. Ran the numbers again at 0400. Something is transmitting. Or something is listening and we are the noise it hears.*

She read that sentence four times. *We are the noise it hears.* It was poetry, or it was paranoia, or it was both, and it was written in the same hand that had just solved the council's school dispute with three lines of arithmetic.

Her own hand.

She had avoided it as long as she could. Now she turned back a page, then another, letting the handwriting accumulate until avoidance became impossible, and there it was: her own script, rendered by a stranger. The same forward lean. The same way the descenders of the g's hooked left instead of right. The same cramped ampersand he used—she used—in place of the word *and*, a habit she'd picked up somewhere in the vats' education stream and never questioned, because where would she have picked it up? From him. From the pattern of him pressed into her developing mind like a fossil into limestone.

She wrote her name in the margin of a blank page, just to see them side by side. *Ivo Hartwell.* Then she copied out one of his sentences beneath it, verbatim, and set the notebook at arm's length and looked.

Two hands. One writer. The clone-certification board had called it "acceptable divergence within tolerance." Sitting alone at 0300 with a dead man's diary, tolerance felt like a euphemism for horror. She was not looking at forgery. Forgery would have been easier. This was inheritance—the way you inherit your mother's laugh without deciding to, except your mother was a man who had died before you were decanted, and the laugh was his entire nervous system.

*I came back wrong,* the girl Sef had said, three days ago, in a classroom full of children who hadn't blinked at her face.

She put down the pencil.

* * *

The seventh notebook was the problem.

It was barely a third full, and the entries thinned as the pages turned, the intervals between them stretching from days to weeks, the handwriting itself degrading—not into illegibility, but into something worse: deliberation. As if near the end he had begun composing every sentence twice before committing it once.

The last dated entry was four days before his death. After that, six pages of nothing. Then, on a leaf near the end of the book, something strange.

A page that had been erased.

Not cleanly. Whoever had done it had used a hard eraser and a heavy hand, and the graphite did not surrender completely—it never does—and so the ghost of the text remained, pressed into the paper's tooth, readable if she tilted the page against the light and let the shadows do the work. She fetched the desk lamp, angled it low, raking light across the surface the way archaeologists rake sunlight across buried walls.

Words rose out of the paper like drowned things surfacing.

The top third of the page was unrecoverable—scrubbed to gray velvet, the erasure total. But the middle held. Line by line, tilting the lamp, breathing carefully so as not to smudge what four decades of pressure had preserved against its own destroyer's will, she reconstructed fragments:

*...the Dream is not localized. Twelve subjects, identical architecture of imagery. Lattice structure beneath the ice, geometry consistent across all reports. Counting. All twelve hear counting...*

Her skin tightened. She thought of the children in the classroom, watching her arrive as if she were a scheduled lesson. *You came back wrong.* She thought of the carrier wave Oduya had shown her, the one nobody could source or shut off, the static that sounded—she had thought it, and dismissed the thought, and now it returned with interest—almost like breathing.

*...not certain whether transmission or reception. The distinction may not apply. If the children are antennas, then we built a colony on top of a mouth...*

Below that, the erasure resumed, harder, angrier—she could see the grooves the eraser had cut, the paper nearly worn through in places, as if he had come back to this page more than once, trying again to kill the words. And then, near the bottom, in a passage that had apparently been written *after* the erasure—fresher graphite, laid over the wounded page like a bandage over a wound—

Two lines. Clear. Unhurried. Deliberate.

*If they ask what the children see, say nothing.*

And beneath it, smaller, almost hidden in the margin, angled as if written quickly and meant to be missed:

*Not even you. Especially not you.*

Ivo sat very still.

The station hummed. The ice glowed. Somewhere below her feet, beneath two hundred meters of glacier, something either transmitted or listened, and the distinction might not apply, and twelve children heard counting in their sleep, and a man had spent his last days writing warnings to a person who did not yet exist, erasing them, rewriting them, erasing them again—as if he couldn't decide whether to protect the secret or arm her against it.

*Especially not you.*

He had known. Not suspected—known. Known that a copy would come walking through the airlock wearing his face, carrying his case, asking his questions. He had known it four days before he died, and he had written to her across the gap of his own death, and the message he had chosen, out of everything he might have said, was *say nothing.*

To whom? The fleets were decades away. The council? Mara? Himself?

Or—to her. To the investigator she would become. Because an investigator asks what the children see. An investigator interviews witnesses. An investigator pulls threads, and somewhere at the center of this station was a thread that, pulled, would unravel something worth more than the truth.

She looked at her name in the margin, in her hand, in his hand, indistinguishable.

"You knew I'd read this," she said aloud, to the empty room, to the dead man, to herself. Her voice sounded strange in the recycled air—young, female, wrong. "You knew exactly what I'd do."

Because she was already doing it. Even now, part of her mind was cataloguing: *twelve subjects, identical imagery, lattice geometry, counting.* Part of her was drafting tomorrow's schedule—return to the school, request the dream reports, cross-reference against the carrier wave's pulse intervals. Part of her was reaching for the thread with both hands, because reaching for threads was the only thing she had ever been made for.

And part of her—the part that had flinched at Sef's words, the part that had stood too long in front of the mirror this morning, learning a face she'd never chosen—understood, suddenly and completely, why he had erased the page.

Some knowledge is a load-bearing wall. Pull it out and the whole structure comes down on everyone inside.

*Say nothing. Especially not you.*

She closed the notebook gently, the way you close a door on a sleeping child.

* * *

She did not sleep. At 0430 she dressed, pinned the station seal to her collar, and walked the quiet ring toward the residential arc, telling herself it was insomnia, telling herself it was procedure. The corridors were dimmed to night levels, amber strips along the floor, and her footsteps made the only sound in the world.

Mara Voss's door was the fourth in the partners' row. Behind it, the founder's widow—or the woman who had been the founder's partner for thirty-one years, the manifest's careful phrasing—was either asleep or awake, and Ivo found she could not walk past.

She raised her hand to knock and stopped, knuckles a centimeter from the panel.

*If they ask what the children see, say nothing.* Fine. But the file had gaps, and gaps were her native language, and there was one witness whose statement she had read five times without finding a single seam. Eleven statements, politely contradictory. One statement, seamless. In forty-one years of investigation she had learned that the seamless ones are never innocent—they are simply the best kept.

*Mara Voss. Statement consistent under four re-interviews. No discrepancies. No affect anomalies. Grief presentation: appropriate.*

Appropriate. As if grief had a regulation uniform, and Mara had put hers on correctly every time, for four decades, without a wrinkle.

Ivo lowered her hand. Not tonight. Tonight she had one thing she needed, and it wasn't testimony—it was the look on a woman's face when she opened the door and saw the dead man standing in the corridor at dawn, asking questions.

She knocked.

For a long moment there was nothing, and Ivo had time to hope the woman slept deeply, time to turn and go, time to be a coward in peace. Then, behind the door, soft footfalls—the unhurried steps of someone who had been awake all along—and the panel slid open, and warm lamplight spilled into the corridor, and Mara Voss looked up at her with eyes gone bright and wet and terribly, instantly awake.

"Oh," Mara said, and her voice broke gently in the middle, like bread. "There you are."

She stepped aside to let Ivo in, and Ivo crossed the threshold into the dead man's home, carrying his warning in her pocket like a stone, knowing already that she was going to break her first promise to him before breakfast.

"I'll make tea," Mara said, to no one, to everyone, to a ghost. "You always get cold."
