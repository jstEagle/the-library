## 14. Mara Breaks

Mara Voss had learned to measure grief in objects.

Not in years—years were too blunt an instrument, and she had forty of them now, forty years of waking in a bed built for two and reaching for warmth that had never once been there. No. She measured it in smaller things: the second cup she still caught herself filling some mornings before her hands remembered; the coat on its hook by the airlock that no one wore but that no one dared move; the way the station's corridors bent around the founder's old quarters like water around a stone, everyone flowing past, no one entering.

She measured it tonight in the teapot.

Two cups. She'd set out two cups again, out of habit so deep it had become architecture, and when Ivo Hartwell arrived at her door—the knock soft, apologetic, a sound Mara knew better than her own heartbeat because Elias had knocked exactly that way for thirty years—Mara looked down at the table and understood that the universe was not subtle. It never had been. It simply waited for you to catch up.

"Come in," she said, before the knock finished. "You were going to anyway. He always did."

Ivo stepped inside, and Mara made herself look at the face properly this time. It was easier in her own quarters, under her own lights, with tea between them as a kind of treaty. The face was Elias's face—the long jaw, the gray coming in early at the temples just as it had, the small scar over the left eyebrow from a ladder accident in the station's first winter that Mara had kissed a thousand times until it became a landmark rather than a wound. But the eyes behind it moved differently. Elias's eyes had held everything at once: the signal, the lie, the love, the fear, all of it layered like ice cores. This woman's eyes held one thing at a time, completely, and moved between things like someone reading.

"You set out two cups," Ivo said.

"I'm an old woman with old habits." Mara poured. "Sit down, copy of my love. The chair by the window is his."

"I can stand."

"He stood for forty years of meetings. Sit in the chair." She heard the edge come into her own voice and did not soften it. Some part of her had been waiting forty years to give orders to that face again, and she hated herself for how good it felt, and she let herself feel it anyway. Grief, unexamined, curdles into cruelty. She'd had four decades to learn that lesson and only recently begun to pass the examinations.

Ivo sat. The window behind her showed the ice field under the long dusk, blue-black and patient, and somewhere beneath it, though neither of them said so, the lattice. The dream. The thing they had both spent their lives orbiting without naming.

"You've been to see Oduya," Mara said. It wasn't a question. News traveled through Terminal Station the way heat traveled through its pipes—invisibly, thoroughly, warming every room whether you wanted it warmed or not.

"This morning."

"And he told you about the array. The carrier wave. How he keeps it humming."

"He told me what he was willing to tell me." Ivo turned the cup in her hands without drinking. "Which was a great deal, and also not enough. He kept saying there was a piece missing. That the story didn't close. He said—" and here the clone hesitated, and Mara watched her hesitate, watched the borrowed face perform uncertainty that Elias would have disguised, "—he said if anyone could close it, it was you."

Mara laughed. It came out wrong, dry and cracked, a laugh that had been sitting in storage too long. "Oduya. Forty years an engineer and still delegating the hard work." She set down her own cup. Her hands were steady. She had rehearsed steadiness the way other people rehearsed speeches. "He's right, though. I am the missing piece. I've been the missing piece since the night your original died, and I have carried it so long I'd almost convinced myself it wasn't there. That's the trick of a secret, you know. You don't hide it. You build a life around it until the life is the wall and the secret is just... the room you don't enter."

"The room you don't enter," Ivo repeated softly. "That's what the case file is, isn't it. A room with the door painted shut."

"Elias painted it himself." Mara rose and went to the cabinet where she kept the things that mattered—not many, after forty years of curation; grief distills—and took out a flat tin, dented at one corner, that Ivo's eyes tracked the way a compass tracks north. "He told me once: *If anyone ever comes asking with my face, Mara, open the door.* I thought he was being poetic. He was being literal. He was always both at once; that's what made him impossible and what made him Elias." She set the tin on the table between them. "Open it."

* * *

Inside the tin lay a medical slate, its casing yellowed, its seal broken long ago and carefully re-fused—a forgery of a different kind than the one Oduya had confessed to, Mara thought, a forgery of silence. Beneath it, folded into eighths, a page covered in Elias's handwriting, dense and slanting, the script deteriorating toward the bottom as if the hand itself were failing.

Ivo read the slate first. Mara watched her read it. Watched the professional detachment—the detachment she'd seen in the girl's file photos, in the way she walked the corridors taking notes—crack and fall away like frost off a window when the heat comes up.

"Dosimetry report," Ivo said. Her voice had gone very quiet. "Cumulative exposure. This is—these numbers are—" She looked up. "This is fatal. This is years-fatal. Decades ago."

"Reactor shielding fault, the first winter. Before the array was even finished. Eleven people got dosed; seven died within the decade. Elias hid his numbers because if the Concordat auditors had seen them, they'd have shuttered the colony in year three, and everything he was building—the station, the survey program, the children born under this sky—would have ended before it began." Mara sat back down across from the clone and found, to her surprise, that the telling was easier than she had feared. Not easy. Easier. Like setting down a load you've carried so long your spine has grown around it. "So he falsified his own medical records. First lie. Everything else grew out of that one, the way a tree grows out of a seed. You know about the seed already, I think. Oduya's seed. The forged signal."

"I know the fleets believe this system holds proof of contact. I know the evidence was manufactured to keep them from each other's throats. What I don't know—" Ivo stopped. Started again. "The murder file has no weapon. No motive. Witnesses who contradict themselves politely. I assumed cover-up. I assumed someone killed him and everyone agreed to forget."

"No one killed him." Mara said it plainly, and heard, in the plainness, forty years of nights saying it to the ceiling. "No one killed him because no one could have. He was already dying, Ivo. Slowly, invisibly, in a body that was eating itself alive with radiation damage while he smiled at council meetings and calibrated instruments and tucked our neighbors' children into their bunks during evacuation drills. The sickness was going to take him sometime in those years regardless—painfully, privately, uselessly. And Elias Voss did not do useless."

Ivo's hand had gone to the folded page but hadn't opened it yet. "The array visits. The logs show him going alone, twice, the week he died."

"At night. In pain he never once mentioned to me directly—I found out from the dosimetry, which he'd hidden in a supply crate labeled *gasket spares*, because he knew I'd never inventory gasket spares and he knew I'd eventually inventory everything else." Mara's mouth did something that was almost a smile. "He went to the array to die, the first time. I'm nearly certain of it. He sat out there in the dark with the static coming down like snow and he tried to decide whether to simply stop. And then he came back inside, and he woke me, and he said, *Mara, I've had a terrible idea, and it's a beautiful idea, and I need you to hate me for the rest of your life.*"

The window light had gone from dusk to true dark. Neither of them moved to brighten the room. Some truths prefer low illumination.

"He staged it," Ivo said. Not a question anymore. The detective in her had clearly arrived ahead of the rest of her, assembling the shape of it from the pieces Mara was handing over, and Mara could see the assembly happening behind Elias's eyes—the same intelligence, moving faster, less burdened. "The death. The missing weapon, the contradictory witnesses—they weren't covering a crime. They were performing one."

"A murder with no murderer. An unsolvable case, delivered to whoever came looking." Mara nodded slowly. "Think about what he understood that no one else did. The forged signal was holding—but a forgery is a wall with a date on it. Sooner or later a fleet examiner runs the numbers, finds the seam, and then the whole structure comes down and takes the peace with it. Unless. Unless the evidence of the signal was wrapped inside something *unignorable*. Something that would make any investigator dig deeper, longer, more reluctantly—not to expose the lie but to solve the death. He made himself the lock on the door of the lie. He made his own corpse the seal."

"He killed himself," Ivo said, and her voice finally broke its composure, a hairline fracture running through it, "to become a mystery."

"He arranged his death so that it would demand investigation forever." Mara corrected gently, because the distinction mattered—it had taken her twenty years to learn why it mattered. "There's a difference, and the difference is love. He didn't want to die. God, Ivo, he wanted to live more than anyone I've ever known—he used to stand at the viewport and count the stars out loud like he was afraid they'd be rationed. But he was dying either way, and he asked himself the question I think every dying person asks—*what is my death for?*—and he answered it the only way he could. His death would be for the colony. For the peace. For the thing under the ice, whatever it was, that he had come to believe deserved protecting from two armadas full of men with doctrines." She reached across the table and, before she could stop herself, touched the clone's hand. The skin was warm. Of course it was warm. "He spent his last week writing instructions. To Oduya. To the council. To me. And one letter he sealed and addressed to *the investigator who comes with my face*, because he knew—you have to understand, he *knew*—that the corporation would send a clone. It was cheaper than sending a stranger. He counted on the cheapness. He counted on you."

Ivo pulled her hand back. Not unkindly. Carefully, the way you withdraw from a hot surface you don't blame. "The witnesses. They contradicted themselves politely. They were following his script."

"They were following his script badly, because none of them are liars by nature. That's why the contradictions are polite instead of clean. Oduya wept through his deposition and then apologized for weeping. Councilwoman Reyes gave three different accounts of the evening and each one was true from a different angle, because Elias had told each of them a different true thing and asked them to hold it. He distributed the truth the way you distribute load-bearing weight, so no single person carried enough of it to collapse." Mara laughed again, and this time it hurt going out. "We were all masons in his alibi. Every one of us. And the terrible thing—the thing I need you to hear, because you're the only one left who can carry it—is that it worked. Forty years. Two fleets circled this system for forty years and never went to war, because a dead man's unsolvable murder sat between them like a chessboard neither could finish. Children were born. Children dreamed. The lattice sang its counting song under the ice. And the whole time, the keel of the peace was a dying man's last act of stagecraft."

* * *

Ivo unfolded the page at last.

Mara watched her read Elias's handwriting—the real handwriting, not the half-erased fragment the clone had described finding in the official files, but the private draft, the one written in the voice he used only with Mara, late at night, when the station was asleep and the static was loud. The hand shook toward the end. The dosimetry was winning even as he wrote.

"What does it say?" Mara asked, though she knew. She had read it so many times the words lived in her mouth like her own teeth.

Ivo read aloud, quietly, as if the room might object:

*"Mara—if you're reading this to her, then it worked, or it failed, and either way you kept your promise, which means you loved me the exact right amount: too much to obey me and too little to disobey me. To the one wearing my face: I'm sorry about the face. I'm sorry about the case. I'm sorry that the first gift your life receives is a dead man's burden wrapped in his handwriting. But listen. Listen the way you listen to the static. The signal may be forged. The forge is real. Something under that ice has been patient for longer than our species has had fire, and patience like that deserves a witness, not a war. If they ask what the children see, say nothing—until the day saying nothing becomes the lie. Then say everything. You'll know the day. I promise you'll know the day. —E."*

Silence filled the room the way water fills a hull—completely, from below.

"He wrote that to me," Ivo said. "Before I existed."

"He wrote it to whoever you turned out to be. That's not the same thing, but it isn't nothing, either." Mara gathered the tin back toward herself, then stopped, and pushed it across the table instead. "Keep it. It was always going to be yours. That's the ambush of inheritance—you don't choose it, it chooses you, and then one day you find you can't put it down without putting yourself down with it."

Ivo took the tin. Held it against her chest the way you hold a thing that might detonate or might bloom; from this distance, Mara thought, the two were indistinguishable.

"Why tell me now?" the clone asked. "Why break forty years of silence tonight?"

Mara looked out the window at the ice, at the dark, at the faint auroral shimmer that the engineers insisted was charged particles and that Mara had privately, stubbornly believed for forty years was attention—the universe leaning closer to look.

"Because the dreams changed," she said. "Because Sef drew a figure in the lattice with your face, and my neighbor's daughter drew the same figure, and twelve children drew the same figure, and I am an old woman who has spent her whole life keeping a dead man's secret, and I looked at those drawings and I heard him say *you'll know the day*." She turned back. Her eyes were wet and she let them be; she had earned her tears, all forty years of them. "And because yesterday a courier pigeon landed on the comm tower with fleet markings I haven't seen since before you were decanted. Two of them. Two fleets. Both asking to visit."

Ivo went very still. "When?"

"Soon," Mara said. "Days. Maybe weeks. And I thought: he bought us forty years with his death, and the bill has finally come due, and I will not let his last act be spent buying nothing." She reached out and closed the clone's fingers tighter around the tin. "So now you know everything, Ivo Hartwell. The sickness, the staging, the script, the seal. Now you know what love looks like when it decides to become an alibi. Go and decide what yours looks like—because the examiners are coming, and this time there's no dead man left to stand between them and the truth."

* * *

Mara walked her to the door. In the corridor's cold light, the resemblance was almost unbearable again—the set of the shoulders, the way Ivo paused at the threshold and looked back, exactly the way Elias had looked back on mornings he left for the array, as if the station were a living thing that might notice absence.

"One more thing," Mara said. "The night he died—or the night he *left*, I've never decided which word is kinder—he said something I didn't understand until tonight. He said, *A lie is just the truth wearing armor.*" She studied the clone's face, searching it for the man, finding instead the woman, and deciding, finally, after forty years, that this was not a betrayal. "I used to think that was a comfort. Tonight I think it's an instruction. Whatever you do with what's in that tin—check the armor. See if anything underneath is still breathing."

Ivo nodded once, and the corridor swallowed her, footsteps fading toward the comms deck, toward Oduya, toward the array that hummed its patient note into the dark.

Mara stood a long time in her doorway. Then she went back inside, took down the coat from its hook by the airlock—the coat no one had worn in forty years—and laid it across the empty chair, and sat down across from it, and began, at last, to mourn.

Outside the window, far out over the ice, the aurora brightened. Once. Twice.

Like a countdown.
