## 28. The Children Testify

Sef had never seen so many uniforms in one room.

They filled the assembly hall like weather — Concordat grey on one side of the aisle, Meridian black on the other, their insignia catching the overhead lights in small cold glints. Between them sat the station council in their patched work clothes, looking like a row of gulls stranded between two warships. The air smelled of recycled atmosphere and boot polish and something sharper underneath: fear, maybe, or the ozone tang of too many active recorders.

Sef counted them without meaning to. It was a habit from the dream. In the lattice you counted the lights, because the lights changed when you weren't paying attention.

Forty-one adults in uniform. Twelve children in a line at the front of the hall, standing on the worn deck plating where the founder used to give his harvest addresses. And one woman with her face.

Ivo Ravensworth stood to the left of the children's line, hands folded, wearing her long coat like armor. She hadn't looked at Sef all morning. Sef understood why. Looking would be a signal, and signals could be read by everyone in this room, and some of the people reading them wanted very much to know what passed between the station's children and the woman who had come back wrong.

*You came back wrong,* Sef had told her once, in the corridor outside the school, and meant it as a warning. Now she understood it had also been a greeting. The dream had been rehearsing them for years. It just hadn't told them for whom.

The Meridian examiner, Vane, sat nearest the front, thin and patient as a heron. Halvorsen of the Concordat was broader, warmer-looking, his smile the kind that arrived fully assembled. Both of them had spent three days requesting custody of the children. *Protective educational transfer,* the Concordat papers said. *Scientific guardianship,* said the Meridian ones. Nobody used the word that lived underneath both documents, the word Sef had learned from listening at vents her whole life:

*Specimens.*

Mara Voss stood behind the children instead of beside Ivo, which Sef thought was deliberate. Mara's hand rested on the shoulder of the smallest dreamer, a boy named Tull who still sucked his thumb when he thought no one saw. Today he wasn't sucking his thumb. None of them were afraid, exactly. Fear was for things you couldn't see coming.

Everything about today had been visible for weeks.

* * *

It had started at breakfast, the way the important things always did — quietly, in the space between one ordinary moment and the next.

Sef had been sitting with the others in the school galley, pushing reconstituted oats around her bowl, when Tull put down his spoon and said, "There's a new part."

Every head at the table came up. Not fast. That was the strange thing, Sef thought later — how slowly they all turned, like they'd been waiting for someone to say it first and were only confirming what they already carried.

"The counting stopped," Tull said. He was six. He pronounced each word with the care of someone handling glass. "Now there's words."

"Words in the old language," said Wren, who was nine and kept the best maps of the lattice in a notebook she hid inside her arithmetic primer. "Not the counting-down kind. The other kind."

"And," Sef said, because someone had to, and because her own chest had been tight since she woke, "there's numbers now. Real ones. Not dream-numbers." Dream-numbers drifted; they multiplied when you looked away. These were hard-edged, fixed, the way the number of days until a ship arrives is fixed. "Coordinates."

Ivo had appeared in the doorway before Sef finished saying it. She did that lately — arrived at the exact moment the dream moved, as if she could feel its pulse through the station's bones. Maybe she could. Oduya said the array's carrier wave had shifted pattern two nights ago, and Oduya did not say things like that lightly anymore; he said them the way a man reports a change in a heartbeat.

Ivo had looked at the twelve of them around the table, and Sef had watched her do the arithmetic every adult in the room was doing: twelve children, one new verse, identical details. Then she had closed her eyes for exactly one breath, and when she opened them she said, "I need you to tell me everything. And then I need to ask you something very difficult."

What she asked was this: would they speak? Not to her. To the fleets. Both of them, in the same room, on the record, refusing custody out loud and then telling the dream anyway — the newest verse, the coordinates, the date.

"They'll try to take you," Ivo said. "That's what today is. They'll offer you protection, education, opportunity. They'll make it sound like a gift. And if you speak, if you give them the verse, you're giving them the one thing they can't get any other way, and some of them will hate you for the generosity of it." Her voice didn't shake. But her hands, folded on the table, pressed against each other like two people holding on. "So I'm not ordering you. Understand that. There is no order I can give that makes this right. I'm asking."

Sef had looked down the table at the others. Tull, six years old, who dreamed deepest and woke crying the least. Wren with her hidden maps. Old Kesh, eleven, who hadn't spoken above a whisper since his father took the long walk out the airlock two winters ago. Eleven faces, and every one of them already knew.

"We've been practicing our whole lives," Sef said. "We just didn't know what for."

* * *

Now the hall waited, and Station Master Okonkwo read the terms aloud in her dry, careful voice: the children were wards of Terminal Station, the custody petitions were refused by unanimous vote of the council, and the children themselves had requested to address the assembly.

Halvorsen rose first. His voice was warm gravel, made for reassurance. "No one here wishes these children any harm. The Concordat's interest is their safety. A phenomenon of this nature — shared cognition of unknown origin — warrants professional observation in a secure facility. Their parents would want—"

"Their parents are in this room," said Mara Voss, not loudly, and the temperature of the hall dropped several degrees.

Vane rose next, and where Halvorsen was warmth, Vane was precision. "Meridian makes no claim on the children. We ask only for structured interviews, supervised, with parental consent, conducted—"

"With respect," Sef said.

She hadn't planned to speak first. But the words were in her mouth the way the verse was in her head, arriving whole, and she found that standing in front of two fleets was not so different from standing in the lattice: you spoke because the light was waiting for you to speak.

Both examiners turned. The recorders turned — she could feel them, dozens of lenses, drinking her smallness.

"You keep talking about us like we're a door you can walk through," Sef said. "A phenomenon. A warrant. My grandmother taught me that when someone talks about you instead of to you, it's because they don't want to hear your answer." She lifted her chin. "So we're going to give you our answer. All of us. Together. Because that's how it comes to us, and splitting us up would be like — like reading one page of a letter and pretending you got the whole thing."

Silence. Somewhere in the back, a recorder hummed through a frequency shift.

Ivo stepped forward then, and her voice filled the hall the way the static filled the array — low, steady, everywhere. "For the record: the children speak voluntarily. No adult has scripted them. Every word will match what they have independently reported to me over the past weeks, and I invite both delegations to compare transcripts afterward. You will find twelve separate accounts converging on identical text." She paused. "That convergence is the finding. Everything else is commentary."

Then she stepped back, and it was theirs.

* * *

The twelve of them joined hands.

It wasn't choreographed. It was simply what the dream did — at the edge of sleep, in the lattice, you reached for the nearest light and held on. Here, in the waking world, in front of forty-one uniforms and the whole terrified weight of Terminal Station, Sef felt Wren's fingers on her left and Tull's small damp grip on her right, and beyond them the chain ran down the line until Kesh at the end stood holding the hand of the last child, completing the circuit.

And the hall went strange.

Sef couldn't have explained it to an adult. Later, she heard crew members describe it as a draft, a pressure change, a trick of the lights. But the children knew what it was. It was the feeling of the lattice noticing. Of being counted.

Wren began. Her voice was thin but true, reciting the oldest verse, the one they'd all known for years — the lattice under the ice, the corridors of light, the patience. Then Tull, describing the counting that had become words. Then each child in turn, adding their piece, and the miracle — the terrible, undeniable miracle — was that none of them stumbled. Twelve children, ages six to eleven, weaving a single continuous account as though it had been written for many voices, which, Sef supposed, it had.

Then it was her turn, and the new verse came up through her like water through ice.

She didn't decide to say it. She opened her mouth and the dream spoke, using her the way the tide uses a channel.

*"Beneath the white, beneath the deep,*
*the listeners keep what the keepers keep.*
*Nine-and-nine-and-ninety turns,*
*and now the turn comes home.*
*Where the third river meets the stone,*
*where the light lies down in rows of seven:*
*nine days, nine days, nine days,*
*and the door remembers who knocked first."*

She said the numbers plainly after, because Wren had insisted the numbers must be plain — poetry was for the shape of it, but coordinates were for the finding of it. Latitude and longitude rendered in the survey system every fleet shared, depth in meters, bearing in degrees. Numbers that pointed down through the glacier, through the rock, to a place no drill had ever reached.

And then the date. She said it twice, so no recorder could claim distortion. Nine days from today.

When she finished, her knees were shaking, and Tull's hand squeezed hers hard enough to hurt, and she realized all twelve of them were breathing in the same rhythm — in for four, hold for seven, out for eight — the exact cadence of the carrier wave, the one Oduya had spent forty years failing to shut off.

* * *

The silence afterward had texture. Sef had never known silence could have texture, but this one felt like frost forming — crystalline, spreading, delicate enough to shatter.

Halvorsen broke it first, because silences made him itch; Sef could see it in his jaw. "Children experience confabulation," he said to the hall, gently, reasonably, as though explaining weather. "Shared environments produce shared narratives. This is well documented. What the Concordat requires is controlled study to determine the source of—"

"Source," repeated Kesh.

Everyone looked at him. Kesh, who whispered. Kesh, whose father was a smear of regret in the station's memorial log. He stepped forward out of the line, and his voice came out at full volume for the first time in two years, and it cracked in the middle like ice underfoot.

"You keep asking where the signal comes from. You've been asking for forty years. That's the wrong question." He pointed one finger down, through the deck, through the ice, through everything. "It doesn't come from anywhere. It's been *here*. Waiting. And it's done waiting. Nine days. You can study us until then if it makes you feel useful. But you can't stop it, and you can't take it, and you can't take *us*, because—" his voice wavered, steadied— "because it doesn't belong to any of you. It belongs to whoever answers."

He looked at Sef. She nodded. The line tightened its grip.

Tull, six years old, finished it. He let go of Sef's hand, walked two steps forward until he stood alone in the open space between the children and the fleets, small enough that the podium would have swallowed him, and looked up at Halvorsen and Vane with the frank, unarmored gaze of a child who has never yet needed to lie.

"You're scared," Tull said. "That's okay. We were scared too, for a long time. But it's not a scary thing. It's a *lonely* thing. It's been alone longer than anybody." He wiped his nose with his sleeve, ruining the gravity completely, and half the hall made a sound that was almost a laugh and almost a sob. "Nine days. Somebody should knock. It's rude to make it wait again."

* * *

Afterward, in the corridor behind the hall, the adrenaline fell out of Sef all at once, the way it does, leaving her sitting on the floor with her back against cold plating while the other children slumped around her like puppets with cut strings.

Ivo crouched in front of her. Up close, Sef could see what the recorders couldn't: the woman was trembling too.

"You did it," Ivo said. "All of it. Exactly as—"

"As we dreamed it," Sef said. "Yeah." She hugged her knees. "Did you feel it? When we held hands?"

Ivo was quiet for a long moment. "I felt something," she admitted. "Like the station inhaling."

"It notices you now. In the dream. You're in the newest-old part, the part before my verse. There's a figure with your face standing where the light bends." Sef studied her. "It's not a bad part. It's a *waiting* part. Like the lattice saved you a place."

"I know," Ivo said softly. "I've been dreaming it too. For weeks." She said it like a confession, and maybe it was one — the founder's copy, dreaming the colony's dream, the boundary between investigator and investigated dissolving the way ice dissolves in springwater.

Mara appeared at the end of the corridor, moving fast, and for a moment Sef thought she was going to scold them all — the whole station had been holding its breath for days, waiting for the adults to either break or band together, and nobody could predict which. Instead Mara knelt down among the children and pulled them into her arms, all of them at once, a tangle of limbs and hair, and Sef felt her shoulders shaking and understood that Mara was crying, and that they were the good kind, the kind that come after decades of holding.

"You were magnificent," Mara said into the pile of them. "All of you. Terrible, impossible, magnificent children."

From the mouth of the corridor, a shadow detached itself from the wall. Vane. The Meridian examiner had a way of appearing without arriving, and he stood now with his hands clasped behind him, watching the huddle of children with an expression Sef couldn't name — not hunger, exactly. Something older and wearier.

"Forgive the intrusion," Vane said. "I wanted to say something without the recorders running." He glanced down the corridor, confirmed its emptiness. "In thirty years of inspections, I have watched governments lie to each other with charts and witnesses and perfectly calibrated sincerity. What I watched in that hall was the opposite. Twelve children who could not lie if their lives depended on it." His gaze settled on Sef. "The date concerns me. Nine days is not enough time for either fleet to reach consensus on anything. Consensus takes months. Panic takes minutes. Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

Sef did. She wished she didn't.

"When the countdown ends," Vane continued, "both fleets will be at high alert, each convinced the other intends to seize whatever opens. Your verse may be the most dangerous document in the system — or the only thing that saves it. It depends entirely on what happens in the next nine days." He paused, and something flickered behind his eyes, some card not yet shown. "Miss Ravensworth. A word. Privately. There is a matter I have been withholding that you will need before day's end — something my government has known for rather longer than yours has."

Ivo rose slowly. "Known about what?"

Vane's heron-stillness didn't waver. "About the signal," he said. "The real one. The one your founder's forgery was built on top of." He turned toward the corridor's dark end. "Walk with me. We have eight days and twenty-two hours, and I suspect you'll want to sit down for the rest."

Sef watched them go — the woman with her face and the man like a folded knife — and felt the dream stir at the edges of her mind, patient as ever, counting down.

Nine days. Somebody should knock.

She just hoped, when they did, that what answered remembered how to be gentle.
