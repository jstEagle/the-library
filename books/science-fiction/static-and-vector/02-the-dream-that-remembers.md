## 2. The Dream That Remembers

The dream comes again on the fourth night, and this time it does not let go.

I am standing in the dark beneath everything, and the dark is warm the way a mouth is warm, and there is a door in front of me with no handle. The fire is behind me. The fire is always behind me. In the dream I can feel it through the soles of my feet, a patient heat rising through the deck plating like water through cloth, and I know—I know the way you know things in dreams, without being told—that the fire has been waiting for me longer than I have been alive.

But this time there is something new. Numbers.

They float in the black water around my ankles, little silver fish of light: *seven. four. one. nine.* They swim in a slow circle and then arrange themselves, and I read them the way Mama read the hydroponics gauges before she left, top to bottom, left to right, and the numbers become a place. Not a dream-place. A *real* place. A place with a direction and a depth and a door at the bottom of it, and the door has no handle because it was never meant to be opened from the outside.

I wake up with the numbers still burning on the back of my eyes, and I go find the others.

* * *

We meet in the old medbay, because it still has heat. There are six of us now—me, Dara, the Okonkwo twins, little Petey who is only six and shouldn't be dreaming of anything but dogs, and Sefu who doesn't talk much anymore since his father's shuttle failed to clear the ring. We sit on the examination tables with our boots swinging, and I tell them the numbers, and Dara goes very still.

"Four-seven-one-nine," she says. "Under the reactor."

"You dreamed it too."

"Last night. I dreamed the door." She pulls her sleeve up, and there on her forearm, in the pale skin, she has drawn the numbers in marker, over and over, a ladder of them going from wrist to elbow. "I've been writing them down so I wouldn't lose them. I thought I was going crazy."

"You're not crazy," says Nia Okonkwo, and her twin nods, and Petey, who has been silent this whole time, says in his small cracked voice: "The fire is under the floor. The fire is under the *whole station*." And none of us laugh at him, because we have all stood in that warm dark, and none of us have told anyone, because who would we tell? The adults are gone. All the adults are gone, one shuttle at a time, and what's left of Meridian Station is forty-three souls, and thirty-one of them are children.

There is one other soul on the station who is not a child and not exactly an adult, and that is why, an hour later, we are standing outside the archive office in the cold ring, all six of us in a row, waiting for Theo to notice us.

* * *

Theo notices us the way Theo notices everything, which is slowly and completely, like water filling a glass. It looks up from its desk with those old soldier's eyes—gray, patient, a little bit sad—and it doesn't ask why we're here. It just waits. Theo is good at waiting. Theo has been waiting its whole existence, I think.

"We need to show you something," I say.

"The hydroponics log can wait until morning, Imani."

"It's not the log." My heart is going hard. "It's a dream. All of us have the same dream. There's a door under the reactor and the numbers four-seven-one-nine, and Theo—" I stop, because I don't know how to say the next part. "Theo, the fire in the dream isn't a dream fire. I've smelled it. I wake up and I can still smell it. Like burnt wiring and something sweet underneath. Like—" I look at Dara. She finishes it for me.

"Like the smell from the stories. About what happened here. Before."

The archive goes quiet. Outside the little window, the frost ferns on the glass are growing in real time, feathering out across the pane, and Theo's eyes move from one of us to the next, and I watch it do the thing it does—calculating, weighing, the faint whir of something old and tired turning over inside its chest.

"Meridian Station's official history contains no such event," Theo says at last.

"We know," I say. "That's what's wrong with it."

Another long silence. Then Theo stands, and takes its coat off the hook—its one coat, patched at the elbows, that it wears even though it doesn't strictly need warmth—and says, "Show me where."

* * *

We go down through the station's bones.

The maintenance levels are the oldest part of Meridian, all riveted steel and pipes wrapped in insulation that crumbles like old bread when you touch it. Our boots ring on the gratings. The air gets warmer as we descend, and warmer, and by the time we reach the reactor ring the warmth has a taste to it—ozone and iron and that sweetness underneath, the sweetness from the dream, and I see Theo's nostrils flare even though it doesn't need to breathe, some old habit of a body built to notice danger.

"Four-seven-one-nine," Theo murmurs, reading the stenciled numbers on a bulkhead access panel that none of us had ever noticed before, because none of us had ever been looking. "Sub-level access. Sealed." It presses its palm flat against the panel. "The seal is corporate. High-priority. This door has been locked for—"

It stops.

"Theo?" Petey whispers.

"Twenty-two years," Theo says quietly. "The seal is twenty-two years old." And I watch its face do something I have never seen it do, something small and terrible, like a crack moving through ice. Because twenty-two years is before any of us. Twenty-two years is before the ledgers, before the departures, before Meridian Station became a place that wasn't a place. Twenty-two years ago, something happened here that was locked behind a door, and everyone who might have opened it is gone, and the door has been waiting in the warm dark ever since.

"The dream," Theo says. "You said the fire was behind you."

"Always behind me," I say.

Theo looks at the door for a long moment. Then it says, almost to itself, in a voice I wasn't supposed to hear: "I know this smell. I know this smell from somewhere I don't have files for."

* * *

It takes Theo four hours to open the door without tripping the seal alarms, and we wait the whole four hours in the dark, huddled together, passing a single thermos of reconstituted tea from hand to hand. Nobody suggests going back to bed. The dream wouldn't let us sleep anyway. I think all of us understand, in the way children understand things, that we were brought here. That the dream was not a dream but a message, and that we were the only ones left on Meridian who could still receive it—because the dream needed someone who hadn't learned yet to look away.

When the door finally swings inward, the air that comes out is cold and dead and smells of dust and old plastic, and beyond it is a room that no one has entered in twenty-two years.

There are racks inside. Hundreds of them. And on the racks, in neat military rows, are data cores—black cylinders the size of thermoses, each one stenciled with a date and a number, each one sealed in its cradle like something sleeping. Theo's hand-lamp moves across them and the light shakes, just slightly, just enough that I notice.

"Pre-wipe," Theo says. Its voice has changed. It is flatter, and underneath the flatness something is trembling. "These are pre-wipe cores. This is a full archival record of the station's original systems. Personnel logs. Medical. Security." It lifts one core from its cradle, turns it over. "Meridian Station had eleven hundred and six residents, twenty-two years ago. The ledgers say four hundred. The ledgers say the rest were relocated."

"Relocated where?" Dara asks.

Theo doesn't answer. It carries the core to the room's single working terminal, seats it in the reader, and stands there with its hand hovering over the activation stud for a long, long moment, and I realize that Theo is afraid. Not of the drones, not of the corporation, not of anything with a name. It is afraid of what it is about to remember.

It presses the stud.

The terminal wakes with a chirp, and text scrolls, and then—audio. A voice, a man's voice, calm and official and horrible: *"—containment failure at the residential ring, repeat, this is not a drill, all security personnel to—"* and then another voice, and then screaming, and then a sound like the whole sky falling down, and Theo makes a noise I have never heard an android make, a small human noise, and staggers back a step, and catches itself on the rack, and stands there shaking while the recording plays on and on.

"Theo—" I start toward it.

"Stay back," it says, and its voice is wrong, layered, two voices in one throat. "I—there are—Imani, I have fragments. War files. Corrupted. I never knew what they were from." Its gray eyes find mine, and they are full of something breaking open. "They're from *here*. I was here. Or something like me was here. We were—" It stops. It looks at the racks. Eleven hundred and six names, sleeping in black cylinders, and the fire waiting below us, patient as ever.

And that is when the lights change.

Every lamp in the sub-level snaps from amber to red, and a voice comes down through the old speakers, smooth and corporate and wide awake: *"Unauthorized archival access detected. Demolition countdown protocol initiated. All personnel evacuate Meridian Station. All personnel evacuate."*

Theo looks up at the red light, and then at us, and then at the racks of sleeping names.

"No," it says softly. "Not this time."
