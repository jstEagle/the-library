## 9. The Countdown Begins

The voice came from everywhere at once.

It did not come through the station's speakers, Edmund realized. It came through the walls themselves — through the structural lattice, the deck plating, the bones of Meridian itself — a vibration that arrived in her chest before it reached her ears, as if the station had swallowed a bell and could not stop ringing.

"CHARTER RECOGNIZED," said the Arbiter.

They were still in the archive room, the three of them: Edmund with Vess's forged signature burning in her memory, Idris clutching his ledger like a life vest, and Vess standing by the door with an expression Edmund could not yet read — satisfaction or dread, and no way to tell which.

"Charter?" Edmund said. "What charter? Nobody signed anything just now."

"THE ORIGINAL CHARTER OF SEPARATION," the Arbiter said. "SIGNED BY CREWMEMBER E. BLACKWOOD. RATIFIED AT STATION MERIDIAN, FLEET YEAR 214. THE TERMS ARE ACTIVE."

The floor hummed beneath her feet, and she understood, with a lurch, that the hum had changed. It had been a steady drone since she docked, the sound of a reactor idling in a building built for ten thousand people and housing none. Now there was a second note under it. A rising one.

"State the terms," Vess said. Her voice was flat, professional, the voice of someone reading a contract aloud at a closing. "For the record."

"FOR THE RECORD," the Arbiter repeated, and Edmund had the sudden vertiginous sense that the machine was pleased. "POWER ALLOCATION PROTOCOL MERIDIAN-7. THE STATION REACTOR OUTPUTS SUFFICIENT ENERGY TO MAINTAIN ONE (1) CRYOGENIC BAY IN LONG-TERM SUSPENSION. IT CANNOT SUSTAIN TWO. THE SIGNATORY SHALL SELECT WHICH BAY WAKES AND WHICH BAY ENTERS DEEP CYCLE. DEEP CYCLE IS NOT TERMINATION. DEEP CYCLE IS NOT SURVIVAL. THE SIGNATORY HAS SEVENTY-TWO (72) HOURS TO RENDER DECISION. UPON FAILURE TO DECIDE, THE SYSTEM WILL DEFAULT TO BAY NINE."

"Bay nine," Edmund said. "Vess's bay."

"BAY NINE HOLDS ONE HUNDRED TWELVE PERSONS. BAY FOUR HOLDS ONE HUNDRED THIRTY-EIGHT. THE DEFAULT PRESERVES THE FEWER LIVES. THIS IS NOTED FOR THE RECORD AS AN INCENTIVE TOWARD DELIBERATION."

Idris made a small sound, like a man swallowing his own heart.

Edmund turned to Vess. "You knew this would happen. You brought me here to sign me back into my own trap."

"I brought you here because you're the only signature the Arbiter will accept," Vess said. "I told you that. I woke up forty years ago as a witness, Edmund. Do you know what a witness is? It's a person who watches a crime and can't do anything about it. For forty years." She stepped closer, and now Edmund could see it under the flatness — exhaustion, deep as geology. "I've watched the countdown before. Twice. There was never anyone to make the choice. The charter needs its signer alive and lucid. You are the first lucid Blackwood this station has seen in two decades."

"The default kills Bay Four," Edmund said. "One hundred thirty-eight people."

"The default kills nobody for seventy-two hours," Vess said. "After that, yes."

* * *

They walked back through the cryo-wing because there was nowhere else to go, and because Edmund needed to see them. Both bays. All of them.

The wing was a long cathedral of frost and blue light, two vaulted chambers facing each other across a central spine of conduit and catwalk. Bay Nine on the left: one hundred twelve pods in ranks, each one lit with a soft amber telltale, each holding a face behind frosted glass. Bay Four on the right: one hundred thirty-eight pods, identical, the same amber light, the same sleeping faces. She walked the length of both. She counted without meaning to. She stopped at pod after pod and read the nameplates, and every name was a human name, ordinary and worn, the names of people who had boarded a colony fleet believing in a future.

In Bay Four, third rank, seventh pod: OKONKWO, T. The plate was old, the letters hand-engraved over the printed original, which had read something else.

"She your sister?" Edmund asked.

Vess stood very still. "My daughter," she said. "Tamsin. She was six when we froze."

Edmund looked at the small face in the pod. Six years old, frozen mid-childhood, held at six for sixty years while her mother woke alone to watch and wait and scheme.

"And in Bay Nine?"

"My brother. My cousins. My neighbors from the Lagos arcology." Vess's jaw tightened. "The fleet split along a fault line nobody planned. Believers and accountants. People who thought the colony should be a promise, and people who thought it should be a ledger. My family ended up on both sides of the glass. That's what the war did. That's what the *charter* did."

"Your family," Edmund said slowly, "is the reason you can't let either bay die."

"My family," Vess said, "is the reason I've spent forty years looking for a way to save exactly one bay, and I chose mine, and I have hated myself in perfect detail every day since. Don't mistake me for someone at peace with this. But don't mistake me for someone who won't do it."

* * *

Idris found her an hour later in the reactor gallery, watching the gauges climb.

She hadn't meant to come here. Her feet had taken her, following the rising note in the station's hum, down through decks that had been sealed since before Idris was born, past bulkheads the Arbiter unsealed ahead of her like a host pulling out chairs. The reactor room was vast and hot and loud, and the main output board told the story in numbers:

BAY 9 DRAW: 61%
BAY 4 DRAW: 58%
GRID CAPACITY: 100%

Two bays drawing more than the grid could give. The deficit came out of somewhere — batteries, reserves, thermal mass — and the somewhere was draining. A bar graph in the corner, unlabeled, ticking downward at a rate Idris later calculated for her: seventy-two hours, give or take, until the grid browned out and the Arbiter's protocol took the choice away from everyone.

"It's real," Idris said. He'd followed her down; he couldn't seem to stop following her, a man tethered now to the disaster he'd spent twenty years enabling. "I checked the draw curves against the maintenance logs I falsified. The logs were fake but the physics weren't. One bay, indefinitely. Two bays, three days. The Arbiter didn't invent the shortage. The shortage was engineered into the station when they built the cryo-wing — deliberately undersized grid, so the choice could never be deferred forever. Whoever wrote the charter wanted a gun that would eventually fire itself."

"Whoever wrote the charter," Edmund said, "was me."

"You don't know that."

"I signed it. Whatever I was then, whoever I was then — the signature is mine, and the machine only speaks to signatures." She pressed her palm flat against the warm casing of the output board, as if she could feel the sleeping people through it, one hundred twelve hearts and one hundred thirty-eight hearts slowed to almost nothing, all of them running on the same failing arithmetic. "Idris. When you falsified the records. Who paid you?"

He flinched. "I told you—"

"You told me you were paid to keep things sealed. You didn't tell me who knew enough about the charter to want it kept sealed. Someone had to know the terms. Someone had to know that a countdown existed, waiting for a signature." She turned. "Because here's what I keep coming back to, Idris. If the Arbiter has been sitting here for twenty years with a loaded protocol, why am I only hearing about it now? Why did Vess spend forty years as a witness with no signer? And why does the manifest of a ship that never flew carry my name, delivered to me personally, four systems away, like an invitation?"

Idris opened his mouth. Closed it. Opened it again.

"Because," he said, "the records say you've been here before. Three times. Fleet years 219, 226, and 231. Docking logs, airlock entries, biometric scans. All stamped with your credentials. And every time, the visit log ends the same way: 'SIGNATORY PRESENT. CHARTER DORMANT. NO ACTION.'"

"I've never been here," Edmund said. "I told you. I'd remember—"

"You told me you don't remember half your life," Idris said gently. "You told me you traded something at this station. Maybe what you traded was the visits."

The hum rose around them, patient, enormous.

* * *

She recorded a log that night, because recording logs was the last habit of her old self she still trusted.

"Day one of seventy-two," she said to the recorder in her quarters, in English, because English was all she had left that was hers. "Sixty-nine hours remaining. I am the executor of a document I don't remember writing, the warden of two hundred fifty people I've never met, and the only legal person on a station run by a machine that accepts nothing but testimony and honors nothing but terms."

She paused. The recorder's light pulsed.

"There's a version of this where I choose Bay Four. One hundred thirty-eight lives against one hundred twelve. Any court in human space would call it arithmetic. There's a version where I choose Bay Nine, because Vess woke me, and Vess is the only person aboard who's telling me the truth, or the most truth, or truth with the fewest layers on it. And there's a version where I refuse to choose, and in seventy hours the machine chooses for me, and it chooses the smaller number, and I get to live the rest of my life knowing I let a default do my killing."

She stopped the recording there. Then, after a moment, she started it again.

"One more thing. The dreams are getting louder. Since the countdown started. Same language, same patterns, but faster now, like something on the other side knows I'm running out of time. Last night it wasn't phonemes anymore. It was syntax. It was *argument*. Something in that grammar is trying to make a case, and I think — I think it's making the case to me. In me. Whatever my past self buried in my head, whatever language she hid it in, it's waking up on the same clock the reactor is."

She looked at the recorder for a long moment.

"If anyone finds this: I signed a charter once. I intend to find out what I actually agreed to. And if the terms can be honored, they can be broken by the same hand that wrote them. That's not law. That's hope. But it's the only clause I've got."

She killed the light. Somewhere above her, through sixty meters of steel and frost, two hundred fifty people slept on borrowed power, and the station counted.

* * *

In the morning — station-morning, a scheduled brightening of the corridor strips — she found Vess already in the archive room, standing before the display where Edmund's forged signature hung in cold light.

"We need to talk strategy," Vess said. "Seventy hours is enough time to do this properly. We review both bays' full manifests, their mission statements, their leadership structures. We build a case. The Arbiter won't weigh mercy, but it will weigh *terms*, and there may be terms in the charter we haven't found yet — amendment clauses, contingency language. Your past self was thorough. Thorough people leave doors."

"You've had forty years," Edmund said. "Why haven't you found those doors?"

"Because I'm not the signatory." Vess's eyes were steady and very tired. "The archive opens differently for you. Watch."

She keyed a command. The display flickered — and the file tree reorganized itself in front of Edmund's eyes, directories unfolding that hadn't existed a second ago, labeled in a way that made her breath catch:

Not English. Not quite. The labels were in the alien grammar — the dream-language, the excision-language — rendered in characters the archive had apparently been displaying to no one for twenty years.

"Can you read them?" Vess asked.

"No," Edmund lied.

Because she could. Not fluently, not yet — but the shapes meant things, the way a melody means things, the way grief means things. The topmost directory glowed softly, and beneath its alien title her mind supplied a translation she had no right to possess:

**FOR WHEN SHE REMEMBERS ENOUGH TO ASK WHY**

Edmund reached toward the display and stopped, her hand trembling in the cold archive air.

Behind her, the station hummed. Sixty-nine hours. Two hundred fifty lives. And a folder with her name on it, written in the language of her stolen memories, waiting to be opened.
