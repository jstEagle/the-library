## 10. Bay One: The Believers

The terminal in Bay One's archive room still smelled of the century it had been built in — ozone and old plastic, the ghost of hands that had touched it last and never come back. Edmund sat down at the console and let the chair take her weight, because her legs had opinions of their own now, and all of them were wrong.

Seventy hours left on the reactor countdown. The number hung in every corridor like weather.

The archive interface woke under her palm with a chime that was almost gentle. *Bay One: Passenger Records. Access granted: Blackwood, E. Charter Signatory.* The station knew who she was. It had known before she did.

"Show me everything," she said, because talking to machines was a habit she'd stopped being embarrassed about somewhere around her fourth year alone on the long route.

The first file opened itself: a mission statement, written in the florid, hopeful prose of people who still believed documents could save them.

*We are the Foundry Compact,* it began. *We leave Earth not because it failed us but because we failed each other, and we intend to learn better.*

Edmund read it twice. Then she read the names.

Three hundred and eleven sleepers. Teachers, hydrologists, a whole generation of people who had walked out of the corporate wars on Earth with nothing but their convictions and their children. They had pooled what they owned, bought berths on the *Meridian*, and signed a charter promising to share everything — water, power, blame. The charter's language was beautiful. That was the first thing she noticed, and the thing that frightened her most. She recognized the hand behind it. Not the words themselves, but the architecture underneath: the way clauses nested, the way mercy was built into the definitions section like rebar.

She knew this drafting style the way you know a mother's handwriting.

"Open the leadership file," she said.

The screen filled with a face she hadn't seen in twenty years and would have known blindfolded.

Dr. Halima Sefu. Her mentor. The woman who had taught her that language wasn't a tool but a territory, that every treaty ever signed was an act of translation, that the hardest words in any tongue were the ones that meant *we*. Halima stood in the recording against a backdrop of Earth's last blue sky over the Lagos launch elevator, silver-haired, straight-backed, wearing the same half-smile she'd worn when Edmund got her first xenolinguistics fellowship — the smile that said *I expected this of you, and I expect more.*

"Play it," Edmund said, to no one, to herself.

Halima's voice came out of the speakers with the warmth of something recorded yesterday.

*"This is Dr. Halima Sefu, speaking for the Foundry Compact, log one of four hundred. If you're hearing this, then either we've woken, or someone has come looking for us, and I confess I hope for both."*

Edmund's chest went tight. She made herself breathe.

*"We chose cryo-sleep because we refused to arrive as conquerors or as corpses. We will wake slowly. We will learn the ground before we claim it. And if anyone wakes before us — if anyone is awake right now, listening — I ask you only this: judge us by our charter, not by your fears. We wrote it to be read by strangers."*

A pause in the recording. When Halima spoke again, her voice had changed — lower, closer to the microphone, the way people speak when they've turned off the audience and are talking to one person.

*"And Edmund, if it's you — because some part of me suspects it always would be — I want you to know I argued for you. When the fleet council wanted a linguist who would simply serve, I told them I had a student who would instead listen. I told them listening was worth three soldiers and two engineers. They laughed. I didn't."*

Edmund reached out and stopped the playback.

The archive room hummed around her, patient as a held breath. Seventy hours. Somewhere below her feet, three hundred and eleven people slept inside their convictions, frozen mid-promise, and the woman who had taught her how to listen had spent her last waking hour on Earth leaving messages for a student who couldn't remember receiving them.

She pressed play again.

* * *

Four hundred logs. She worked through them the way you work through a wound — methodically, refusing to flinch, flinching anyway.

Log twelve: a hydrologist named Tomas Reyes explaining, with diagrams, why the colony site needed both river basins, why compromise on water was the one compromise the Compact could never survive. His voice cracked once on the word *share* and he kept going.

Log forty-one: a schoolteacher reading her students' letters aloud so the record would hold their voices even if the ice took their bodies. Thirty-one children reciting, in round unbroken chorus, the pledge they'd written themselves: *We will be kind to whoever we meet, because everyone we meet will be tired.*

Log ninety: Halima again, older-seeming though only months had passed, addressing a dispute within the Compact about whether to accept passengers from Bay Two's faction aboard their own manifest. *"They are not our enemies,"* she said. *"They are our mirror. Every faction believes it is the believers and the others are the accountants. The day we need them, we will discover which we were."*

Edmund sat very still after that one.

Because here was the shape of it, emerging from the sediment of a century: Bay One had not been naive. That was the lie the route-gossip told, the story the surviving colonies had absorbed without examination — idealists frozen in amber, doomed by their own softness. But these logs showed a movement that had *studied its own failure modes*. They had run simulations of scarcity. They had appointed devil's advocates. Halima had built dissent into the meeting structure the way engineers built redundancy into life support.

These were not people who deserved what happened to them.

These were people who had planned for everything except being betrayed by their own translator.

"Archive," Edmund said, "search: recordings authored by Blackwood, Edmund."

The cursor blinked. The station considered.

*One hundred and six files found.*

Her mouth went dry. She had expected a handful — testimony, maybe, a deposition. A hundred and six.

"Play the earliest."

Static, then her own voice — younger, clipped, carrying the particular authority of someone who has been asked to be certain and has agreed.

*"This is Edmund Blackwood, fleet linguist, log one. I've been retained by the joint charter commission to assess whether the Compact's founding document can survive contact with the Pragmatist faction's counter-charter. My finding, for the record: yes. Barely. The two documents share enough structural grammar that a merged covenant is possible — but only if both parties agree to a shared definition of 'person' that includes the unfrozen. I've drafted the bridging clause. It's elegant. It's also the hardest thing I've ever written, because it asks both factions to accept that the other side's dead might outvote their living."*

Edmund listened to herself argue, fluently, passionately, for a position she had no memory of holding.

It was like watching a stranger wear her face with better posture. The woman in the recording was quick, funny in a dry register Edmund barely recognized, morally certain in a way that made the present-day Edmund feel like a faded copy. That woman believed things. That woman had *drafted a clause defining personhood across cryogenic boundaries* and called it elegant, and the terrible thing, the seductive thing, was that Edmund understood it instantly — saw the logic bloom in her mind whole, felt the rightness of it settle into her bones like a language learned in childhood.

She wanted to be that woman again.

That was the trap, and she saw it clearly, and she stepped toward it anyway.

* * *

By log sixty she had stopped taking notes. By log eighty she had stopped noticing the countdown clock in the corner of the screen. Her tea went cold beside the console and she drank it anyway, tasting nothing, while her past self filled the little room with a voice full of conviction.

*"—and that's why the arbitration protocol must sit outside both charters,"* her recorded self was saying, in log ninety-three. *"Not above them — outside. Because any court either faction controls becomes a weapon the moment trust fails, and trust WILL fail; I've read enough history to know that's not pessimism, it's scheduling. So we build an Arbiter. Machine-administered, charter-bound, incorruptible by design. It holds the ceasefire when we can't hold it ourselves."*

Edmund froze.

The Arbiter. The thing downstairs counting down seventy hours toward a choice between three hundred sleepers and three hundred sleepers — she had *proposed* it. In her own voice. With her own certainty.

*"I know what you're thinking,"* the recording continued, and Edmund felt the hairs rise on her arms, because the younger her sounded suddenly gentle, suddenly close, as if she were leaning through a century to speak directly to whoever found this. *"You're thinking: what happens when the Arbiter is wrong? Or worse — when someone edits what it serves? Here's my answer, and I want it entered into the record: the Arbiter is only as just as its charter, and the charter must be written in language neither faction can rewrite alone. Co-authored. Bilingual. Locked. I'm entrusting the master text to the fleet's neutral signatories — myself included — and I'm having my own memory of the key phrases sealed, so that no torture, no bribe, no desperate midnight bargain can extract from me what the Arbiter requires. If I don't remember the law, I can't be made to break it."*

The recording ended there. Log ninety-four began with mundane procedural notes, as if the previous entry hadn't been a confession delivered in advance to a future stranger.

Edmund put her head in her hands.

So that was the price. That was the hole in her. She had done it to herself — carved the key phrases out of her own mind and left the rest standing, a house with load-bearing walls removed, everything sagging inward for twenty years. Every dream that came back in alien phonemes instead of her mother's English was a scar tissue where the sealant had been poured. She hadn't lost her languages. She had *spent* them.

And someone had taken a mechanism designed as armor and turned it into a cage.

"Log one hundred," she said quietly. "Play it."

This one was different. No podium voice. Recorded in what sounded like a small room, late, the microphone too close, her breathing audible.

*"Last entry before the transfer. Vess Okonkwo asked me today if I was afraid. I told her the truth: I'm afraid of the wrong things. I'm not afraid of forgetting — forgetting is the fee. I'm afraid of who I'll be on the other side of it. Whether the woman who wakes up will honor the woman who signed, or resent her, or — worst case — not recognize the work at all. Vess said, 'Then leave yourself a map.' I told her maps can be forged."*

A silence. Then, softer:

*"But maybe I'll leave one anyway. Maybe I'll hide it where only a dreamer can find it. If you're hearing this, Edmund — the later one, the hollow one — start with the dreams. I hid the map in the part of you they couldn't cut without killing you. The grammar survives. Follow the grammar."*

The file closed itself. The archive room went quiet except for the ventilation and the slow tick of the countdown, sixty-nine hours and change now, each second a small coin paid out of a shrinking purse.

Edmund sat in the dark of the screen's afterglow and understood, finally, the shape of the last several days. The corrupted dreams weren't damage. They were *directions*. Her past self had hidden the map in the alien grammar — the one tongue the excision couldn't touch, because it lived deeper than memory, in the wiring — and then arranged, somehow, for the hollow version of her to be steered back here, to this station, to these bays, in time.

Vess. Vess Okonkwo, awake by design, kept as a witness. *Then leave yourself a map,* the recording said, and Vess had answered, and Vess had been there when Edmund woke with holes in her head, and Vess had watched her for twenty years, or arranged for someone to, and said nothing until a decommissioned ship's manifest sprouted an impossible name like a lure.

Every step of Edmund's journey here had been walked on ground someone else had prepared.

She should have felt angry. What she felt instead was worse: gratitude. Gratitude toward the woman in the recordings, toward the machine-voiced past that had loved her enough to plan around her brokenness. She wanted to deserve that woman. She wanted to stand up in front of three hundred sleeping believers and say *I kept the faith, I just couldn't remember keeping it.*

That want was doing work in her already. She could feel it tilting her — toward Bay One, toward Halima's voice, toward the child's chorus pledging kindness to the tired. Of course it was. The logs were a masterpiece of persuasion, and she was their intended audience, and the countdown was their deadline.

"Careful," she said aloud to the empty room. "You're being recruited by a filing cabinet."

The room declined to answer.

She pulled up the bay's environmental summary instead, forcing herself toward numbers, toward the cold end of the trade. Three hundred and eleven souls. Power draw consistent with a century of uninterrupted maintenance. Integrity: nominal. Waking sequence: viable, given exclusive access to the grid for approximately nine days.

Nine days. And Bay Two would need the same nine days, and the grid could fund one, not both. The arithmetic was a knife with two edges and no handle.

She was reaching for the shutdown command — she'd seen enough, she needed air, she needed to walk the cold corridors and think — when the file index refreshed itself. One new entry. Timestamped ninety seconds ago.

Origin: Bay Two archive. Addressed to her.

*From: Okonkwo, V. Subject: Since you're in there anyway.*

Edmund stared at it. Across the station, in a sealed wing she hadn't yet entered, a woman who had been awake inside the ice for twenty years had just watched her open Bay One's heart — and had decided, right now, tonight, with sixty-nine hours on the clock, that it was time to introduce herself to the other side of the argument.

The message pulsed gently, patiently, like something that had waited a century and could wait a little longer but preferred not to.

Edmund thought of Halima's voice saying *they are our mirror.* She thought of her own recorded self saying *trust will fail; that's not pessimism, it's scheduling.*

She opened the message.
