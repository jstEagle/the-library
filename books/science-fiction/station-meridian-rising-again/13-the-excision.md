## 13. The Excision

The key was not a key.

That was the first thing Edmund understood as she sat in the dark of the archive alcove, the alien grammar still humming behind her teeth like a struck tuning fork. Keys open things that belong to someone else. What she had carried in her sleep for eleven years — what the excision had left buried under the rubble of her stolen languages — was not permission. It was authorship.

The dream had given her a sequence: nine phonemes, arranged in an order no human throat would choose, each one a compression of meaning so dense it made her chest ache to hold it. She had woken at 0400 station time with her mouth already moving, whispering syllables into the cold air of her bunk like a woman praying in a church she'd forgotten she built.

Now she spoke them into the archive terminal's input field, and Station Meridian listened.

The screen did not flash or chime. It simply *changed* — the way a room changes when a light comes on behind a wall you'd assumed was solid. A directory bloomed beneath the standard registry interface, one she had never seen in three days of digging through this station's guts. It was labeled only with a string of characters in the same grammar she'd just spoken, and beneath it, in plain English, in a font choice that felt almost intimate:

**EDMUND BLACKWOOD — PERSONAL ARCHIVE — ACCESS GRANTED BY PRIOR AUTHORIZATION OF E. BLACKWOOD**

Prior authorization. Her own name, granting her access to something she did not remember creating.

Her hands were steady on the console. She noticed that, and noted the noticing — the old xenolinguist's habit, observing herself as data. Steady hands. Dry eyes. A pulse of ninety-two that should have been a hundred and forty. Some part of her had been waiting for this door for over a decade, and that part had apparently done its grieving in advance.

She opened the first file.

* * *

It was audio. A timestamp: fourteen years before the Meridian's supposed decommission. Before the launch that never happened. Before everything.

Her own voice filled the alcove, younger by some measure she couldn't quantify — not younger exactly, but *unbroken*, a voice that had never yet been asked to carry what hers carried now.

"Log entry, day two hundred six of mediation. This is Edmund Blackwood, chief negotiator, Meridian colonial charter process. Recording for the record and for whoever ends up holding this."

A pause on the recording. She could hear it — the breath before a hard sentence, a habit she still had.

"The ceasefire is holding. Barely. Bay One wants a charter written in the language of principle — rights, dignity, the whole vocabulary of the people who left Earth because they believed in something. Bay Two wants a charter written in the language of arithmetic — rations, power draw, generational solvency. They are not wrong about each other. That's what makes it dangerous. If either side were simply evil, we could have a war and be done with it. Instead they're both *right*, and the charter has to hold both truths without letting either one crush the other."

Edmund sat very still, listening to herself describe a negotiation she had no memory of conducting. It was like reading letters from a twin. Like hearing a stranger who knew her tells.

"I've drafted a structure I'm calling the alternating covenant. Both bays wake in shifts. Shared council. Shared power grid, shared water reclamation, shared tribunal for grievances. Neither faction governs alone; neither faction sleeps through the founding. It's fragile. It depends entirely on both sides trusting the mechanism more than they distrust each other." Another pause. "Trust is a language nobody speaks natively. You can only learn it from exposure. So we build the exposure into the law itself, and let the law do the teaching."

The recording ended. There were four hundred and eleven more files.

She opened them in sequence, and the shape of a life assembled itself around her like bones finding their sockets.

Day 214: her voice, exhausted, mediating a dispute over cryo-bay maintenance rotations. Day 231: her voice, sharp with anger, confronting the fleet quartermaster — a man named Aldous Renk — about discrepancies in the asset manifests. *"You've moved three cargo modules' worth of equipment off the books, Mr. Renk. Where is it going?"* And his answer, smooth as oiled machinery: *"Wherever it's needed, Ms. Blackwood. The colony won't judge us. The colony will be asleep."*

Day 268. The voice changed. Tighter. Afraid.

"They know I know. Renk's people accessed my quarters while I was in the cryo-wing — Idris saw the logs, he'll confirm it if anyone ever asks him, which no one will, because asking questions is how you disappear out here. So I'm doing the thing I said I'd never do. I'm building the excision protocol."

Edmund's breath caught. On the recording, her past self kept talking, brisk now, clinical, the way she sounded when she was most frightened — she recognized that too, the way fear made her precise.

"Not for them. For me. Here's the logic, for the record: the ceasefire holds only as long as both factions believe the covenant is legitimate — signed freely, sealed properly, no coercion. But Renk is going to try to forge a charter amendment. He's going to try to make the Arbiter program enforce *his* terms instead of mine. And when he does, he'll need me out of the way — not dead, dead creates martyrs and investigations, but *erased*. Unable to testify. Unable even to remember that there was anything to testify about."

"So I'm designing the protocol myself. Memory-excision via linguistic pathway disruption. It works because memory and language share architecture — you can't recall what you can't parse. I designed it to be surgical. Targeted. Reversible, if you keep the key. The key is the sequence itself, encoded in a language pattern deep enough that surface excision can't touch it. I'm hiding my own testimony inside my own wound."

A long silence on the recording. When the voice came back, it was quieter.

"If you're hearing this, Edmund — future Edmund, post-excision Edmund — then it worked, and then it went wrong. Because the plan was: I testify, the forgery fails, the covenant stands, and then I *undo* the excision and go back to being one whole person. That was always the plan. The protocol was supposed to be a tourniquet, not an amputation."

"But someone got to it first. Someone took my scalpel and turned it into a knife. So listen carefully: the protocol has a signature. Every excision carries the grammatical fingerprint of its designer. Mine has mine. If the excision you're living with doesn't match my signature — if it's crude, if it took whole languages instead of single memories, if it left you dreaming in xenoglossy — then it wasn't me who cut you. It was someone using my tool without my hands."

"And that means they didn't just steal your memories. They stole your *work*. Your design. The thing you built to protect eight thousand sleeping people. Find the signature. Find the deviation. That's where they'll have left their fingerprints, because no one uses a language they don't understand without leaving traces in the grammar."

The recording ended.

Edmund sat in the alcove's dim light for a long time, listening to the ventilation systems breathe, feeling the station's hum come up through the floor and into her spine.

She had not betrayed anyone.

The thought arrived without drama, without the catharsis she might have expected, and that absence was somehow worse — grief with nowhere to land. For eleven years she had carried a suspicion she'd never voiced, not even to herself, not even in the privacy of her own skull: that somewhere in the holes in her memory was something she had *done*. That the missing years weren't taken from her; that she had traded them away, paid them out like currency for something she couldn't face. Why else would a person stop asking? Why else would she build a life on the long routes between silent colonies, ferrying other people's cargo past other people's graves, never once looking back?

She hadn't stopped asking because she didn't want to know.

She'd stopped asking because the excision had been designed — *her* design, refined by someone else's cruder hands — to make the question itself unparsable. Not to hide the answer. To dissolve the question. She had built a protocol that could make a person unable to *ask*, and someone had aimed it at her like a weapon, and she had spent eleven years mistaking the muzzle for her own face.

* * *

She found the signature analysis tools in the archive's deeper directories — her own tools, annotated in her own handwriting converted to text, tutorials she had apparently recorded for exactly this contingency. Future-proofing. The past self leaving instructions for the present self like a mother leaving notes in a lunchbox.

The excision that had been performed on her was dated thirteen years ago. Eleven years before she'd noticed the dreams going wrong — no. She corrected herself. The dreams hadn't gone wrong eleven years ago. They'd gone wrong thirteen years ago, and it had taken her two years to notice, because noticing was precisely what the protocol prevented. The realization landed in her stomach like cold water.

She ran the comparison.

Her design, as documented in the archive: surgical. Forty-one targeted memory clusters, all related to a single event — the forged charter amendment, Renk's meeting, the evidence chain. Designed to heal clean. Designed to leave the surrounding self intact, the way good surgery leaves the healthy tissue healthy.

The excision actually performed: a massacre.

Whoever had used her protocol had not understood it — or had understood it perfectly and chosen cruelty anyway. Instead of forty-one clusters, they had cut entire linguistic regions. Her childhood language — her mother's kitchen, the rain on the roof, every word of it — gone, because those memories were stored in the same neural architecture as her fluency, and the butcher had cut along the language line instead of the memory line. Her facility with seven human tongues, reduced to fragments. Her ability to dream in any of them, severed at the root.

And in place of precision: scar tissue. The xenoglossy. The alien phonemes that had haunted her sleep for a decade. She had thought they were contamination — foreign material growing into the wound.

They were not contamination.

She ran the pattern match, and her hands finally shook.

The phonemes in her dreams matched the archive's stored copy of her own memory-key at ninety-four percent fidelity. The remaining six percent was deviation — drift introduced by whoever had modified the protocol after stealing it. Her past self had hidden the key inside the deepest layer of the excision, expecting the surgeon to be Edmund Blackwood, careful and reverent. Instead the key had been roughly handled, partially damaged, and left to circulate in her damaged language centers like a splinter working toward the surface.

For ten years, her nightmares had been trying to hand her the evidence.

She laughed. It came out wrong — a single dry note with nothing behind it, the laugh of a person whose sense of humor had been excised along with everything else. She laughed again, harder, and this time it cracked into something else, and she put her head down on the console among the archive terminals and cried for the first time since docking at Meridian. Not elegantly. Not briefly. She cried for the languages, which she could name now: Yoruba first, her mother's voice saying her name in it, *Adéwálé*, little crown has come home. Then Portuguese, her father's. Then the others, the ones she'd collected like shells on a beach, each one a friendship, a posting, a love affair with a way of thinking. Thirteen years of amputation, and she had called it burnout. She had called it aging. She had called it the price of the trade route, and kept flying.

When she finished, she wiped her face on her sleeve, and the grief closed over like water over a dropped stone, and underneath it, rising slow and vast, was something with far better posture.

Rage.

Not the hot kind. The structural kind. The rage of an engineer reviewing sabotage — calm, itemized, forward-looking. She opened a fresh file in the archive and began, in English, in plain declarative sentences, to write down everything she now knew, because her past self had taught her the first rule of testimony: *write it down while the knowing is fresh.*

Item: Edmund Blackwood served as chief negotiator of the Meridian colonial charter. Item: she brokered the alternating covenant between Bay One and Bay Two. Item: fleet quartermaster Aldous Renk moved colony assets off-book and attempted to forge a charter amendment granting the Arbiter program enforcement authority over terms the factions never agreed to. Item: to prevent her testimony, Renk or his agents stole her memory-excision protocol and deployed it against her, crudely, destroying languages and histories far beyond the protocol's intended scope. Item: the memory-key survived, embedded in her dreams, and she recovered it. Item: the original recordings exist. Four hundred and eleven files. Her voice. Her seal. Her signature on a covenant that said *both bays wake.*

Item: the countdown currently running in the reactor core is enforcing a forged document.

She sat back. The words glowed on the screen, ordinary and enormous.

Then she thought of Vess Okonkwo, awake in Bay Nine for thirteen years, keeping witness, steering Edmund toward Bay Two's records with the gentle pressure of a handler, and the rage acquired its second item. Vess had known. Maybe not everything — but enough. Enough to let Edmund walk into this station blind, to let her piece it together file by file, because a witness who arrives at the truth on her own is worth more than one who's told. Or because Vess wanted something specific from the truth, something that required Edmund to reach certain conclusions and not others.

And she thought of Idris, the clerk who falsified the flight record, who watched her dock and said nothing, whose terror had a specific flavor she'd seen before in deposed officials and defected pilots: the flavor of a man sitting on something heavy, waiting for someone strong enough to help him lift it.

Two threads. One leading up through the cryo-wing to a woman who had waited thirteen years in the cold. One leading down through the port registry to a man who had kept twenty years of secrets in a drawer.

The countdown read fifty-eight hours. Fifty-eight hours to decide which half of humanity's last colonial generation would live, per the terms of a charter signed by a woman who had never existed — the erased Edmund, the fiction Renk's surgeons had built out of her body.

But the real Edmund had signed something else.

She pulled up the original covenant — the alternating charter, sealed with her cryptographic signature, timestamped, witnessed, filed in the archive her past self had hidden inside the station's bones. It was beautiful. Even now, even gutted and grieving, she could see the craft in it: the balancing clauses, the shift-rotation mathematics, the tribunal structure that gave neither faction a permanent majority. Eight thousand people, asleep in two bays, held together by a document written in the language of trust by a woman who understood that trust was learned, not given.

The Arbiter would not accept testimony. Idris had told her that, and the machine's behavior confirmed it. The Arbiter parsed documents, not people. It enforced charters, not justice. It was, in the end, a legal instrument — and legal instruments had a weakness that no amount of processing power could patch.

They could be amended.

By the original signatory.

Edmund looked at her own name on the covenant — *E. Blackwood, chief negotiator, for the peoples of Bays One and Two* — and felt the last piece of herself click home. Not the xenolinguist. Not the freighter pilot. Not the victim. The author. The one who wrote the law, and therefore the one who could rewrite it.

Someone had used her language against her. Fine. Language was a tool, and tools belonged to whoever picked them up.

She had seventy-eight hours of work ahead of her, a new charter to compose in a grammar the Arbiter couldn't refuse, and two people aboard this station who each held a piece of what she needed — one of whom might be an ally, and one of whom was definitely running a longer game than she'd admitted.

She saved the file, sealed it with her signature, and stood up in the dark of the archive alcove, taller than she'd been when she sat down.

First, she decided, she would go find Idris. Because a man who keeps a falsified ledger for twenty years is a man who knows where all the bodies are filed — and Edmund Blackwood was done being the only witness to her own life.

She was halfway to the door when the archive terminal chimed behind her — a sound it had not made once in her three days aboard.

Someone else had just accessed the hidden directory.
