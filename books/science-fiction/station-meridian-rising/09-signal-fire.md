## 9. Signal Fire

**Silas**

The relay rose out of the dark like a cathedral someone had abandoned mid-prayer.

It was taller than the Meridian's schematics suggested — kilometers of lattice and dead solar sails, wrapped in the faint green breath of the aurora the children had dreamed. Up close, it didn't look like a machine. It looked like a wound that had learned to keep itself.

"Docking collar's on the third spoke," Wren said from the copilot's seat, her voice too steady, the way it got when she was scared and refusing to spend the fear. "Caretaker says the transmitter core is at the crown. We go up the spine."

Silas cut the thrusters and let the ship drift into the collar's embrace. Behind them, in the hold, forty-one memory cores had been singing for six hours — a chord below hearing, felt in the teeth, in the fillings, in the wet architecture of the inner ear. He'd stopped trying to sleep. Sleep was where the coordinates lived.

The caretaker's voice came through the ship's comm, thin and doubled, as if two men were speaking in unison and only half of each survived the trip. *"Jamming will begin when the signal does. You will have minutes. Perhaps fewer."*

"Then we don't waste any," Silas said, and popped the airlock.

* * *

**Wren**

The spine of the relay was a ladder bolted to the inside of a lightning strike. Wren climbed with her gloves burning, the structure groaning around them in frequencies she felt in her sternum. Above, through gaps in the lattice, the stars wheeled slow and indifferent. Below, the Meridian hung at the collar like a moth pinned to a door.

Silas climbed ahead of her, older than his eleven years on this route had made him, and she thought: *he's been carrying forty-one people up a mountain for a decade and called it cargo.*

The transmitter core was a sphere of frosted glass at the crown of the tower, big as a chapel, veined with light that pulsed in time with the singing from the hold. The caretaker was already there — or the shape of him, a projection stuttering at the edge of coherence, a man made of apologies.

*"The routing is not the problem,"* he said. *"The problem is that the signal is true. Truth at this bandwidth will burn out every relay between here and Verity. I could never bring myself to do it. I told myself I was protecting the dead. I was protecting myself."*

"Then stand back," Silas said, "and let us do what you couldn't."

Wren was opening the routing panel when her wrist display screamed. A drive signature, hard and hot, decelerating out of the dark. She knew the profile the way you know a voice in another room.

"Vantablack," she said. "Nineteen minutes out."

* * *

**Silas**

Ilsa Reyen didn't knock. She broadcast.

Her voice filled the relay's comm on every channel at once, warm and terrible, a woman who had learned that volume could substitute for argument. *"Silas Nakamura. You're standing on the thing that killed your sister. Step away from it and I'll let you keep the cores. That's more mercy than the war gave either of us."*

Silas keyed the transmitter. "You knew. You knew the relay was shut down on purpose, and you've been salvaging its bones for years. Why stop us now?"

A pause. When she answered, the warmth had drained out, leaving something older and more tired. *"Because the signal doesn't just name the Halcyon officers who ordered the relay cut. It names the Verity convoys that kept flying through the silence afterward. It names my colony's charter ships. It names my father. You burn that truth out there, Silas, and it doesn't land on the guilty. It lands on everyone. On the children who are dreaming this. On Ama."*

"On the lie," Silas said. "It lands on the lie. The children survive it. They've been surviving it their whole lives — that's what the dream *is.*"

*"I'm coming up the spine in twelve minutes,"* Ilsa said. *"Don't make me climb past you."*

The channel died. In the frosted glass of the core, Silas saw his own reflection and, for half a heartbeat, something else — a woman's silhouette standing beside his, hand raised, as if pressing her palm to the inside of a window.

The singing in the ship's hold changed key.

*Noor,* he thought, and his hands went still on the routing panel. *Noor, is that you?*

* * *

**Wren**

The jamming started as a headache.

Wren was elbow-deep in the routing array when the first interference wave rolled over the relay — not sound, not light, a pressure that made her vision stutter and her teeth ache. The caretaker's projection tore into ribbons and reassembled itself, screaming in two voices. Somewhere below, the Vantablack had begun firing on the tower's anchor points, patient, methodical, cutting the tree down rather than climbing it.

"The array's fighting the signal," she shouted over the groan of the lattice. "It's been fighting it for eleven years — the whole relay is a jammer wearing a transmitter's skin!"

*"Then you must let the signal go around it,"* the caretaker said, his doubled voice fraying. *"There is a path. Through the memory cores. They were built to carry human minds — they can carry truth the same way. But the cores must be awake to do it, and waking them fully, at this distance, at this bandwidth—"*

He didn't finish. He didn't have to. Wren did the math and hated every number in it. Full bandwidth through the cores meant the cores burned out. Whatever the sleepers were, however much of them was still in there, this would be the end of the road.

"Silas," she said quietly, over the private channel. "It's their choice. You said it would be their choice."

Silence on the line. Then, rough as gravel: "I know what I said."

* * *

**Silas**

He opened the channel to the hold and spoke to forty-one people who had been waiting eleven years for someone to tell them the truth.

"You died when the relay fell," he said. "It didn't fall. It was cut — deliberately — to bury what happened in the war. The signal that's been reaching into your sleep is the proof, trying to get out. I can route it through you. Through all of you. It will be the last thing your cores do, and I won't pretend to know what that costs, because I've never been the one in the box." His voice cracked and he let it. "Noor. I'm sorry it took me this long. Wake if you want to. Rest if you want to. But if you're awake — help me finish it."

The singing stopped.

The silence was worse — forty-one heartbeats' worth of held breath, the whole hold listening. Then, one by one, the cores began to answer. Not with the melody. With their names, spoken in their own voices, a roll call of the dead confirming attendance: *Okafor. Lindqvist. Amari. Vasquez. Nakamura, Noor.*

Her voice came last and closest, as if she were standing at his shoulder instead of four decks down.

*"Hey, little brother. You got old."*

Silas put his hand flat against the comm panel, the closest thing to holding her hand the universe would allow. "I got old. Are you — can you do this?"

*"Forty-one of us, and one signal, and eleven years of patience."* A laugh in her voice, bright and sharp as the day she'd died. *"We've been rehearsing in our sleep, Silas. Route us."*

* * *

**Wren**

She threw the switch, and the tower became a throat.

The signal came up through the spine like fire through a fuse — not the buried transmission alone, but the transmission braided with forty-one voices, the dead testifying over the top of the evidence, naming what had been done and who had done it and what it had cost. Wren watched the readouts climb past every safety threshold and keep climbing, and she didn't look away, because someone had to witness this with their eyes open.

The jamming fought. The relay screamed. Below them, the Vantablack's cutting charges blew the third anchor and the whole structure lurched, a slow-motion fall that the lattice caught at the last possible angle.

Then boots on the spine. Ilsa Reyen came over the crown of the tower with two of her crew, plasma torch lit, face set in the expression of a woman doing the thing she'd promised herself she'd never do.

"Silas," she said. "Last chance."

"It's already gone," Silas said, and pointed up.

Wren followed his finger. Above the crown, above the aurora, the signal had found the old deep-space array — the one dish everyone had forgotten, aimed at nothing, aimed at everything — and it was leaving. A thread of light unwinding into the dark, toward Verity, toward Halcyon, toward every listening child.

Ilsa looked at it for a long moment. Then she killed her torch.

"My father," she said, "kept a recording of the convoy manifests. I always thought he kept it out of guilt." She sat down heavily on the lattice, an old woman's motion in a young body. "He kept it out of pride. God help us all."

* * *

**Silas**

The transmission took eleven minutes. It felt like eleven years.

When it finished, the cores in the hold went dark one by one — not all at once, but in sequence, like windows in a building where people were finally, deliberately, turning off their lights. Each one flickered once before it died. A goodbye. He counted them. He counted all forty-one, because someone had to, because he had spent eleven years not counting and he would never not count again.

The last core held its light a moment longer than the rest.

*"Silas,"* Noor said, faint now, a signal losing its carrier. *"The caretaker's systems — there's a fragment of me in the relay buffer. Enough. Enough to wake into, if someone gives me a body of light to live in. Enough to stay."* A pause. *"I didn't choose to burn out. I chose to send them home first. You'd have done the same. You always were the slow one."*

The core went dark, and the relay buffer's indicator light came on: amber, patient, waiting.

Wren's hand found his shoulder. Below them, the Vantablack's crew were standing down, and Ilsa Reyen was looking at the sky like a woman watching a door she'd nailed shut swing open on its own.

"Both colonies received it," Wren said softly. "Full bandwidth. There's no taking it back."

On the horizon of the dark, two points of light were already changing — the beacons of Verity and Halcyon, flickering, recalculating, beginning the long clumsy work of turning toward each other after eleven years of silence.

And on the relay's internal network, in a buffer no one had built for a person, a fragment of his sister waited to find out what she would be when she woke.

"Take me home," Silas said, "and bring her with us."
