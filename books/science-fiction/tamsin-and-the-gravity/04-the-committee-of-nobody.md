## 4. The Committee of Nobody

The trouble with keeping a secret for forty years, Dov Okonkwo had learned, was that you forgot how to say it out loud.

He rehearsed the whole way down. Spine 4 was a thirty-minute walk from the insurance office to the old hydroponics level, and he spent every minute of it composing and discarding opening lines. *Freya, there's something you should see.* Too vague. She'd want vagueness explained. *Freya, I'm about to commit about nine kinds of treason.* Too honest, too early. He was an engineer; he believed in load-bearing honesty, but even load-bearing honesty needed to be introduced to a wall gradually.

She walked beside him with her hands in her coat pockets and her eyes doing what they always did — reading the station like a document. He'd noticed it in their first meeting, three days ago, when she'd interviewed him about the lift failure. She didn't look at things; she *filed* them. Every cracked bulkhead, every jury-rigged air scrubber, every patch of rust shaped like a country that no longer existed on any map — it all went somewhere behind her eyes, cross-referenced, timestamped. He had spent his whole life around machines that recorded everything and understood nothing, and here was a woman who did the opposite.

"You're taking me somewhere off the official map," she said. Not a question.

"Is that a problem?"

"It's a pattern." She said it lightly, but he heard the weight under it. "Every time I've asked a question on this station that has an answer someone doesn't like, I get invited somewhere unofficial. The canteen manager wanted to talk about the lift in the back room. The dockmaster preferred his boat. Now you." She glanced at him. "Either everyone here is hiding the same thing, or everyone here is hiding different things and doesn't want them colliding."

Dov laughed before he could stop himself. It came out rough — he didn't laugh much anymore. "And which do you think it is?"

"I think," she said, "that on a station everyone forgot existed, the only thing left worth hiding is the fact that you're still here."

He stopped walking.

She stopped a step later and turned, and he saw her register his face, and he watched her do the thing she did — take the expression, weigh it, file it. He thought: *this is either the biggest mistake of my life or the only right one.*

"Keepers," he said. "That's what we call ourselves. It's not a good name. It's the name of a maintenance roster from before the war, and we kept it because nobody ever looks twice at a maintenance roster." He wiped his palms on his thighs. "There are eleven of us. Twelve, counting you, if you want it. And before you say anything — before you decide anything — I need you to understand that we didn't send whatever went out during the flicker. We maintain the array. We don't use it. There's a difference, and the difference is the whole reason we're alive."

"Show me," Freya said.

* * *

The way in was through a coolant exchange on Sublevel 6 that hadn't functioned since Dov's father was young. It still looked like it functioned — that was the trick. Pipes ran through it, gauges hung on it, a maintenance placard in faded colonial standard promised inspections every ninety days. The placard was older than Freya, probably. Dov put his shoulder to a panel that weighed nothing because the hinges were maintained with the devotion other men gave to their children, and it swung open onto a stairwell that smelled of machine oil and old coffee.

"You've been maintaining the door to a secret," Freya said, "with a maintenance schedule."

"Secrets rot faster than machines if you don't tend them." He started down. "My grandmother taught me that. She was on the original roster."

The stairs went down further than Sublevel 6 should have allowed. Meridian Station had been built in layers across a century, and the deepest layers were the oldest, and the oldest layers had been built by people who assumed the war would never reach them. The walls changed character as they descended — prefab composite giving way to riveted steel, then to something older still, hand-welded, the seams uneven, the work of colonists who had built for permanence because permanence was all they had to believe in.

Lights came on ahead of them. Not automatically — he heard the switch thrown, heard the low voices cut off mid-sentence, heard the particular silence of eleven people deciding, all at once, whether to be afraid.

The chamber at the bottom had been a fusion control room once. Now it was something between a workshop and a chapel. Cable runs draped the walls like ivy. Desks made from salvaged shuttle seating faced a wall of screens, most of them dark, a few of them showing the slow green heartbeat of systems that officially did not exist. In the corner, someone had set up a kitchenette — a kettle, a shelf of mismatched mugs, a tin of tea leaves so old the brand on it was from a company that had been dissolved in the first year of the war. People had spent their lives in this room. It showed in the way the chairs had molded to particular bodies, in the reading glasses on every surface, in the child's drawing taped to one console — a stick figure standing under a yellow sun, labeled *PAPA* in letters that climbed uphill.

Dov had drawn it for his daughter, Ilse, when she was four. She was nineteen now. She was also, he realized, standing by the kettle with her arms crossed, wearing the expression she'd inherited directly from him — the one that meant *you brought a stranger into the house.*

"Everyone," he said, and his voice cracked on the second syllable, and he had to start over. "Everyone. This is Freya Almeida. She investigates accidents. She's the one who's been asking about the lift, and about the flicker."

"An investigator," said a woman from the far desk. Mirela Senn — archivist, oldest of them, sharp as a broken cable. Her eyes were the pale gray of a screen at minimum brightness, and they went through Freya the way Freya went through rooms. "An *insurance* investigator. Sent here by the same syndicate that signed off on Meridian being written off the ledgers. And you brought her *here*."

"I brought her here because she found the power draw," Dov said. "On her own. In three days. With equipment she bought at the chandlery." He looked around the room, at the faces he'd known his whole life — Tomas with his burn-scarred hands, the Adeyemi twins who'd never agreed on anything except the array, old Petros who'd stopped speaking in full sentences after his brother died in the lift, three weeks ago, three weeks ago, the lift, the lift that had killed three people and that Freya Almeida had come to call an accident that defied every model. "We can't outlast her, Mirela. We can only outlast her *usefully*."

"Or," said one of the twins — Ravi, Dov thought, it was always hard to tell when they were scared, "she reports us, and in a month there's a 'gas leak' on Sublevel 6, and in two months there's no Sublevel 6, and in three months there's no us."

The room did the thing rooms full of old secrets do: it held its breath.

Freya hadn't moved. She stood just inside the doorway with her hands still in her pockets, and Dov watched her take in the chamber the way she took in everything — but slower this time. He watched her eyes catch on the child's drawing. On the tea tin. On the eleven chairs, and the way they'd been worn into the shapes of eleven particular lives. He watched her arrive, visibly, at the understanding that this was not an operation. It was a *household*. It had been a household for longer than she had been alive.

"I'm not going to report you," she said.

"You'll forgive us if that doesn't settle it," Mirela said.

"It shouldn't." Freya pulled a hand from her pocket and held it up, palm out — a gesture that meant nothing in particular and therefore meant *I have no weapon, no leverage, nothing but this.* "You're right not to trust me. I've spent six days on this station and I've already found one impossible power draw, one impossible lift failure, and a decommissioned array that drew enough current during the flicker to run a foundry. I have questions I can't answer, and I've learned that when I can't answer a question, it's usually because someone is paying good money for me not to." Her voice was level, but Dov heard the thing underneath it, the same thing he'd heard in the corridor — not fear exactly. Appetite. The hunger of a mind that had been fed lies for too long. "So here's what I can offer. Not trust. Verification. I'll show you everything I've found, and you tell me if I've got it right, and if I've got it wrong, you tell me that too, and I'll go away and stop being your problem. But if I've got it right—" she looked at Dov, and something passed between them that he would spend the rest of the night trying to name, "—then you need someone on the *outside* of this room who knows what you're protecting. Because whatever went out during those nine seconds, it didn't go out by accident. And the people who sent it are going to want their equipment back. Or their witnesses."

Silence. The green screens pulsed.

It was Petros who broke it — Petros, who hadn't spoken a full sentence in three weeks, who sat in the corner chair with his brother's jacket over his knees like a cat that wouldn't accept the cat was gone. He looked at Freya for a long moment with his wet red eyes, and then he said, in a voice like a door opening on a room no one had entered in years:

"She has the face of someone who's lost somebody."

Dov's chest tightened. Freya's expression didn't change — didn't change *visibly* — but he saw the small stillness come into her, the way a machine's readouts flatten just before a fault. *Filed,* he thought. *Timestamped. Cross-referenced. Refused.*

"Most of us have," he said quietly.

"Then she speaks the language," Petros said, and turned back to his brother's jacket, and that was that. Petros had been the roster's vote-counter for thirty years. Nobody overruled him on matters of grief.

* * *

They showed her everything.

Dov had imagined this moment a hundred times — the reveal, the unveiling, the dramatic sweep of the hand toward the truth — and in every version he was the one who knew things and she was the one who learned them. The reality was worse and better. The reality was that within twenty minutes she was correcting them.

"The array's power draw during the flicker," she said, standing at the main console with her finger on a readout Dov had stared at for nine days without seeing what she saw. "You logged it as a passive draw. Capacitor trickle. But look at the waveform — it's not a trickle, it's a *ramp*. Someone spun the core up to transmission load and back down inside nine seconds. That's not a malfunction. That's a controlled burn by someone who knew exactly what they were doing and wanted to leave no thermal signature." She looked up at Dov. "Your logs say the core was cold. Your logs are wrong, or your logs were edited."

The room went very quiet.

"Nobody edits the logs," Tomas said. His burn-scarred hands were flat on the desk, pressing down, as if the desk might lift off otherwise. "The logs are the whole point of us. Forty years. Every amp, every volt, every hour of my father's life and my life. Nobody *edits*—"

"Then someone accessed the array without going through your logs." Freya said it gently, and Dov understood suddenly that gentleness was not her native language — that she was translating into it, deliberately, the way he translated into patience. "Which means there's a way in that you don't know about. Or a way in that one of you does know about."

"Careful," Ravi said.

"I'm always careful," Freya said. "It's the job. But careful isn't the same as blind, and I didn't come down here to flatter you." She turned back to the readouts. "There's something else. The transmission itself — what I could reconstruct of it from the scatter on my instruments — wasn't pointed at anything in-system. The bearing is out. *Way* out. Deep space. Which means whoever sent it wasn't talking to either fleet. They were talking to something beyond the war."

"Beyond the war," Mirela repeated. She had come to stand beside Freya at the console, and Dov watched the two women — the archivist and the investigator, the keeper of the past and the auditor of the present — regard the same numbers from opposite ends of forty years. "There's nothing beyond the war. There's been nothing beyond the war for thirty years. That's the whole shape of our lives."

"Then someone believes otherwise," Freya said. "Or someone's betting everything that there is."

Dov stood back and let them work, and something happened in him that he hadn't felt in years and didn't have a name for. It took him a while to recognize it, because it was so old, because it belonged to the young man he'd been before the purges, before the roster went underground, before *Keeper* stopped being a job title and became a whole identity worn under a false one. It was the feeling of a system coming back online after a long brownout. The feeling of a circuit remembering it was built to carry current.

*Being needed.* That was the name of it. Not the abstract being-needed of the colony, which needed him the way a dying man needs a nurse — gratefully, hopelessly, without expectation of recovery. This was specific. This was a woman standing at his console, in his grandmother's room, asking questions that only the Keepers alive on Meridian could answer, and needing the answer to be true. He watched her work and he thought: *she could burn us. She could walk out of here and file a report and we'd be a rounding error in someone's quarterly cleanup.* And underneath that thought, terrifyingly, warm as the kettle: *but she won't. She's like us. She keeps things.*

He caught Ilse watching him from across the room. His daughter had said nothing all evening — she'd poured tea, she'd refilled Petros's cup, she'd done the quiet hostessing that was her way of being present without being exposed. But now she was looking at her father with an expression he'd never quite seen on her before, and when he raised an eyebrow she just shook her head, almost smiling, and mouthed something he couldn't read.

Later, walking her home through the old corridors, he'd ask. She'd say: *"You looked like Grandmother's pictures. From before. That's all."* And he'd have to stop walking for a second and pretend to check a pressure gauge that didn't need checking.

* * *

It was past second-shift's end when the meeting finally broke apart into the quiet negotiations of old colleagues — who would walk whom home, who had first watch on the core, whether there was enough tea for tomorrow. Freya stood by the door with her coat on, and Dov walked her to the stairwell, and there they paused in the amber light of the emergency strips.

"You understand," she said, "that I have to come back."

"I'd be disappointed if you didn't."

"And you understand that I'm going to keep investigating. The lift. The flicker. All of it. Being inside the room doesn't mean I stop being what I am."

"It better not." He held the panel open for her. "We've had forty years of people who stopped being what they were. It's how the war started — everybody stopped being what they were and started being what their governments told them." He hesitated. "There's one more thing. About the lift."

She turned. The light caught the tiredness under her eyes and turned it into something almost like silver.

"The three who died," Dov said. "Oren Vask, Lida Chen, and Petros's brother, Anselm." He made himself say the name. It was the first time he'd said it aloud since the funeral-that-wasn't, because the Keepers didn't hold funerals, because funerals were records, and records were dangerous. "They weren't just maintenance crew. Oren and Lida were Keepers. And Anselm—" his voice caught, and he pushed through it, because load-bearing honesty, because she deserved it, because Petros upstairs had already paid more than anyone should for the truth, "—Anselm was the one who found the way in that isn't in our logs. Six weeks ago. He told us he'd found a secondary access to the array, a route that bypassed the roster's systems entirely. He wouldn't say how he found it. He said he needed to check something first."

Freya had gone very still. "And then the lift fell."

"And then the lift fell," Dov said. "Three days later. With him in it." He looked at her, and let her see everything he was carrying, because he was tired of carrying it alone and because he had decided — sometime during the last four hours, sometime between the tea and the waveform — that she was the person he was going to carry it *with*. "You said the lift failure defied every actuarial model. I believe you. But I want you to understand what you're actually investigating now. Not an accident. Not even a cover-up." He swallowed. "Somebody killed our people to keep a door shut, and we don't know who, and the door is still open somewhere, and whoever used it nine days ago is still on this station."

Freya stood in the amber light for a long moment. When she spoke, her voice was the flattest he'd ever heard it, and he understood that the flatness was the sound of a woman holding something enormous very carefully, the way you hold a core at transmission load.

"Then we'd better find them," she said, "before whoever killed Anselm does."
