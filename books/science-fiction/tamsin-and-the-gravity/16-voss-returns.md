## 16. Voss Returns

The knock came at 0400 station time, which told me everything before I opened the door.

Nobody knocked at 0400 on Meridian unless they wanted you to know they didn't care what hour it was. Nobody knocked twice, softly, the way a civilized person would — this was three measured raps, spaced like a metronome, the knock of someone who had never once in his life waited for permission.

I'd slept four hours in six days. I was sitting on my bunk with Mirela's recovered transmission fragment open on my slate, reading it again for the fortieth time, as if repetition might wear its edges smooth and make it something I could hold without bleeding. It didn't work. It never worked.

Three knocks. I knew who it was before I touched the panel.

Casimir Voss stood in the corridor wearing the same immaculate civilian coat he'd worn to his first "audit," but something had changed underneath it. The urbane mask was still there — the easy smile, the patient eyes — but it sat differently now, the way a uniform sits differently once you've seen the man ordered to put it on. Behind him, where last time there had been two polite assistants with clipboards, there were four people who moved like soldiers pretending not to, and one of them carried a case that hummed faintly at a frequency I'd learned, over the past weeks, to associate with things that could end arguments permanently.

"Ms. Almeida." He inclined his head. "May I come in? Or would you prefer we continue our acquaintance in public, where you can perform reluctance?"

"You've gotten worse at pretending," I said.

"I've stopped trying." He stepped past me into the room without waiting, which was answer enough, and looked around my quarters with the mild curiosity of a man appraising a hotel he intended to have demolished. The narrow bunk. The actuarial texts stacked on the shelf. The photograph of Tomas I kept face-down when strangers visited and hadn't bothered to turn down now, because I was tired of performing for men like him.

He noticed it anyway. Of course he did.

"Your brother," he said. Not a question.

"Get out of my room or say why you're here."

He turned, and the smile finally came off, and what was underneath was worse than the smile: a tired, competent man doing arithmetic with human lives and finding the sums acceptable. "Eleven days ago your sensors showed three fleets converging. You'll be pleased to know the estimate has improved. Nine days now. Perhaps eight, if the Concordat's third squadron pushes their burn." He said *Concordat* the way other people said *weather* — a fact of the environment, neither good nor evil, simply present. "My faction's battle group will arrive first. And Ms. Almeida, I am authorized to tell you something I was not authorized to tell you last time."

"Which is?"

"That Meridian is going to die."

* * *

He said it the way I write casualty projections. No drama. No relish. Just the number, placed on the table between us like a ledger entry.

I made myself sit down. Made myself pick up the kettle and pour two cups of the terrible reconstituted tea the station grew in vats, because my hands needed a task that wasn't shaking. Voss accepted his cup and didn't drink it, which told me he'd been briefed on our water recycling failures and considered them part of the problem rather than part of the hospitality.

"Define 'die,'" I said.

"Glassing," he said. "Kinetic saturation followed by thermal strike. The colony's population density makes conventional assault inefficient, and the message — whatever portion of it exists here — cannot be permitted to reach either fleet's adversaries intact. My command believes the risk calculus favors total destruction of the array and everything adjacent to it." A pause, precise as a scalpel. "Everything adjacent includes approximately eleven thousand people."

"Eleven thousand two hundred and forty," I said. "As of Tuesday's census. We lost two in the core fire."

Something flickered across his face — not guilt; I don't think Casimir Voss had access to guilt, only to its administrative equivalent — and was gone. "You've been counting."

"It's my job. Counting is my job. Somebody taught me that every number is a person who trusted the world enough to stand in a particular place at a particular time." I set my cup down. "You're telling me you're going to erase eleven thousand two hundred and forty acts of trust because a dead governor wrote a letter."

"The letter proves both governments committed the atrocity that started this war jointly. Do you understand what that means, Ms. Almeida? Not one side's crime. Both sides'. If it lands, every fleet in this system learns that its own high command manufactured the enemy. Discipline doesn't survive that. Wars don't survive that — but neither does anything else, for a while. There are people above my pay grade who believe a decade of chaos is an acceptable price for truth, and people below it who believe no price is acceptable, and I am the instrument of the second group." He drank, finally, grimacing slightly at the tea. "But you already know all this. You reconstructed the original crime yourself. Paper trails are your religion. So let us skip to the part where I make you an offer, and you pretend to consider it, and then we discover whether you're the person your file says you are."

"My file," I repeated. "You've read my file."

"I've read everything about you, Freya. That's rather the point of what comes next."

* * *

He laid it out with the terrible elegance of a well-structured contract, and I realized, listening, that this was the truest thing he'd ever said to me: every clause was real. That was the horror of it. Not a trap, not a bluff. An offer he could keep.

Evacuation capacity: two heavy shuttles, arriving under his battle group's escort, disguised as salvage tugs. Capacity per shuttle: four hundred souls in crush-load conditions. Eight hundred colonists, selected from a list he would permit the Keepers to draft themselves — a concession he presented as generosity and I recognized as strategy, because nothing destroys a secret society faster than asking it to rank its own children.

In exchange: the complete transmission package. Coordinates, encryption keys, the full text of Governor Tamsin's proposal, the authentication signatures Mirela had spent months reconstructing from forty years of degraded archives. Everything. Delivered before the fleets arrived, verified by his technicians, after which Meridian would be evacuated of those eight hundred and then struck — but struck *after*, so that the deaths would be quick, and the survivors would be rich, resettled, anonymous under new identities in Concordat territory, and alive.

"And the other ten thousand four hundred and forty?" I asked.

His silence lasted exactly long enough to be honest.

"They stay on the list," he said finally, "or they go on the census of the dead. Those are the columns available. I didn't build the spreadsheet, Ms. Almeida. I'm just the auditor."

"I'm the auditor," I said. "You're the erasure."

He inclined his head, conceding the point the way a chess player concedes a pawn — aware of it, unbothered, already three moves deeper into a board I couldn't see. "There's a personal clause," he said. "I'm authorized to add it, given your file."

"Don't."

"Almeida, Tomas. Second Lift Infantry, Concordat First Expeditionary. Killed in the Meridian Corridor action, war-year one, age twenty-two. Official record: artillery misfire during a defensive engagement." He watched me the way I watch instruments — for the tremor, the anomaly, the reading that doesn't match the model. "Unofficial record, which my government has possessed for nineteen years and your syndicate helped bury: his unit was positioned inside the staging zone for the false-flag operation. The atrocity your message exposes. He wasn't killed by an accident, Freya. He was killed by the opening move of the war itself, and then written into the cover story as one of its martyrs. They used your brother's death to sell the lie."

The room went very quiet. Somewhere in the station's bones, a coolant pump cycled, indifferent as gravity.

I had spent nineteen years not thinking about how Tomas died. I want to be precise about that, because precision is the only honesty I've ever been good at: not *failed* to think about it. *Not* thought about it, actively, daily, the way you don't touch a wound — a discipline, a practice, a small private actuarial fraud in which I priced his death at zero and refused to audit the books. And this man had walked into my room at 0400 and torn the whole ledger open in a single sentence, because he understood that grief is leverage, and leverage is what he'd come to collect.

"You think that makes me want to give you the message," I said. My voice came out level. I was almost proud of it. "That learning the truth about Tomas makes me want to help you bury it deeper."

"I think," Voss said gently, "that it makes you want the war to have meant something. And I am offering you the only version of meaning that survives: eight hundred lives, bought and paid for, out of the fire. Grief wants a transaction, Ms. Almeida. I've seen it a hundred times. People don't bargain with the truth. They bargain with the pain."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then in nine days, everyone on this station dies, including Dov Okonkwo's daughter, including Mirela Senn and her archives, including every name on your list of erased accidents — and the message dies with them, and the war continues for another generation, and somewhere in year thirty of it some other investigator finds another impossible accident and starts counting again." He set down the untouched half of his tea. "I am not the villain of this story, Freya. I'm the deadline. Villains want something from you. Deadlines just arrive."

* * *

After he left — he left the way he'd arrived, without waiting for permission — I sat on my bunk for a long time with the photograph in my hands, face-up for the first time in weeks.

Tomas at seventeen, on the observation deck of the transfer station at Ceres, squinting against the sun-lamps, holding up a model glider he'd built from salvaged parts. He'd wanted to fly atmosphere, real weather, rain on his wings. He'd joined the infantry because the flight academy fees were impossible and the enlistment bonus wasn't, and I had been three years into my actuarial certification by then, already earning, already able — I told myself later, in the years when the not-thinking failed at 3 a.m. — to have covered the difference. I hadn't offered. I'd calculated that he should learn to fund his own ambitions, and the calculation was sound, and my brother flew no wings except the ones they put on his memorial plaque.

Everyone I love, I thought, dies of my good math.

And Voss had known. Had reached into nineteen years of scar tissue and found the exact seam. That was the unbearable part — not the offer, which was monstrous in the ordinary way offers are monstrous, but the accuracy of it. Because he was right about the temptation. Some traitorous chamber of me, the chamber that keeps the books I don't audit, had already run the numbers: eight hundred souls against the probability that broadcasting would save anyone at all, weighted by certainty. His offer was certain. The message was a wager. Every instinct I had trained for twenty years said take the certain eight hundred.

Every instinct I had trained for twenty years had also told me the cargo lift failure was an accident, and the flicker was a malfunction, and the Keepers were a cult, and my career was a neutral instrument for pricing disasters rather than a machine for burying them.

I turned the photograph back down. Not hiding him. Just — setting him aside until I could look at him properly, which I couldn't do yet, because looking at him properly meant deciding what he would have done, and I wasn't ready to know the answer, because I suspected I'd known it since I was nine years old watching him glue balsa wood together in the kitchen.

The door chimed. Dov's voice through the panel, rough with lack of sleep: "Freya. Council's convened. Mirela says the vote can't wait — and there's something else. Long-range picked up a fourth contact. Small, fast, running dark, inbound on our position." A pause. "It's not one of the three fleets."

I stood up. My hands had stopped shaking somewhere during the conversation with Voss, and I understood, finally, that they hadn't stopped because I'd calmed down.

They'd stopped because the decision had already been made, somewhere beneath the arithmetic, in the place where my brother was still seventeen and still building things that were meant to fly.

"I'm coming," I said.
