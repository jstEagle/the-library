## 20. Aftermath, Actuarial

The report took eleven days to write.

Not because it was long — though it was, eventually, four hundred and six pages with appendices — but because I kept stopping. Every time I reached a name, I had to stop. Not for the actuarial reasons I'd once have given: liability exposure, actuarial precedent, the tidy taxonomy of loss that had structured my entire career. I stopped because a name is not data. A name is a door, and behind every one of them was a room I had to walk into before I could write the next line.

I wrote it in the Keepers' archive, at Mirela Senn's old console, because my own quarters still smelled of smoke and because Dov had quietly moved my things there without asking, the way he did everything — as if care were just another maintenance task on a long list nobody else would ever see. The console hummed beneath my hands with the residual warmth of forty years of secret keeping. Somewhere below us, the array stood silent and exposed, its shielding stripped away by order of the arbitration fleet, broadcasting nothing now except telemetry to anyone who cared to look. Everyone was looking.

The document began, as all my reports began, with a classification header. Then I deleted the header. Then I sat there for a long time looking at the blank space where the syndicate's seal used to go, and finally typed:

*This is not an insurance report.*

*This is testimony.*

*My name is Freya Almeida, adjuster third class, Meridian Station field office, and everything I am about to state I either witnessed directly or verified against primary records I will enumerate in full. I am filing this outside every channel my employer maintains, because my employer is named in what follows.*

It felt like cutting a mooring line. Forty years of training, of methodical detachment worn like armor plate, and the whole structure came down to a single sentence of insubordination typed in a basement.

* * *

Here is what the record showed, when I finally assembled it whole.

Seven accidents. Seven, across thirty-one years, each one flagged in the syndicate's own files as statistically anomalous, each one closed with a notation I now understood had been written by someone whose job was not to explain the anomaly but to bury it. The cargo lift failure that brought me to Meridian — three dead — was only the most recent. Before it: a coolant breach in Sublevel Four that killed two men and flooded the chamber where the array's original power coupling had been housed. A decompression event in a maintenance shaft no ship's manifest could account for. A fire in a records annex that burned eleven thousand documents and one archivist whose death certificate listed her cause of death as "cardiac event, pre-existing." She had been twenty-six years old.

Each accident destroyed evidence. Each accident occurred within ninety days of documented array activity. Each accident was investigated — by me, or by predecessors whose names I found in the margins of margin notes — and each investigation concluded with language so carefully bland it read like a confession written backward: *no further action warranted; cause consistent with age-related systems degradation; recommend closure.*

Age-related systems degradation. The station was old, yes. But old things fail randomly. These failures were surgical. And someone, somewhere up the chain, had known it — had known it for three decades, had been reading my reports and my predecessors' reports and feeding back the same anesthetic conclusion, because the alternative was to notice the pattern, and noticing the pattern meant asking who benefited from seven perfectly placed catastrophes.

The answer was in the payroll ledgers. It was always in the ledgers; I should have known that about myself sooner. A disbursement code I'd seen ten thousand times without ever querying it — MERIDIAN-CONTINGENCY-7 — traced back through three shell subsidiaries to a discretionary fund administered jointly by the syndicate's risk division and, through a routing sequence Mirela helped me decrypt from the governor's papers, liaison officers attached to both war ministries. Both sides. Funding the erasure of the peace proposal's custodians, together, in secret, for thirty-one years, while their governments spent eleven billion lives prosecuting the war that proposal would have ended before it properly began.

I sat with that number for a long time. Eleven billion. I had spent my career pricing human life in currency units — depreciation schedules, dependency multipliers, the cold arithmetic of who a dead person was owed to. I had told myself it was honest work, that someone had to do the counting, that numbers were a kind of respect because they refused to lie. And all along the largest number of all had been sitting underneath my ledgers like a body under floorboards, and the counting itself had been the burial.

* * *

I named them all.

That was the part that mattered, I think — more than the financial forensics, more than the decrypted routing codes, more than the chain of command that ran from the contingency fund up into offices on three capital worlds. The arbitration tribunal could have gotten the money trail from any competent auditor. What they could not get anywhere else was the names.

Accident One: coolant breach, Sublevel Four. Dead: Tomas Reyes, 54, hydroponics technician. Ilse Marchetti, 61, retired teacher who volunteered in the Keepers' rotation because, per Dov, she liked the quiet of the deep levels and the company of machines that asked nothing of you. I found her name in a duty roster Mirela had kept in a waterproof case for thirty years, along with a note in her handwriting: *Ilse always stayed late. Said the dark down there felt earned.* I wrote that sentence into the testimony verbatim. The tribunal lawyers flagged it as non-standard. I overruled them. Non-standard was the entire point.

Accident Two: decompression, Maintenance Shaft C. Dead: Yusuf Adeyemi, 38, structural engineer, father of two, one of whom — I confirmed this against station census records with a feeling I can only describe as pressure behind the eyes — is Dov Okonkwo's oldest friend, and was nine years old when his father went into that shaft to check a seal he had been ordered to check by a work order generated four hours after the array's last pre-flicker diagnostic.

On and on. Seven accidents, fourteen dead, plus the archivist in the annex fire, plus — this was the entry that cost me the most — the ones who hadn't died but had been erased anyway. The Keepers who had been reassigned off-station over the years, transferred at the syndicate's quiet request to postings so remote they might as well have been decommissioned. I found eleven of them alive, scattered across the rim, and I put their names in too, marked *surviving witness,* because being erased while still breathing is its own category of casualty and the ledger had never had a column for it. I added a column.

Mirela read the draft twice. The second time she cried, briefly and without noise, the way people cry who learned to do it in rooms where sound carried. Then she said, "You got Ilse's note right. She did say that. About the dark feeling earned." And that was the whole eulogy, and it was enough, and I understood then that this was what testimony actually was — not justice, which is a machine, but witness, which is a choice made one name at a time.

* * *

The syndicate collapsed nineteen days after I filed.

It didn't collapse dramatically. That surprised me. I had imagined something cinematic — headquarters sealed, executives marched out in restraints, the logo coming down off the tower in flames. Instead there was a Tuesday when the claims processing system went offline for scheduled maintenance and never came back, and the regional offices started answering their comm lines with a recorded message referring claimants to provisional receivership authorities, and the tower logo didn't come down at all; it just stopped being lit at night, so that within a month most people had forgotten exactly where it had hung.

The investigations, though — those were loud. The tribunal subpoenaed everything, and because I had enumerated my sources with the obsessive completeness of a woman who had spent twenty years learning that sloppy documentation is how the guilty survive, they got everything. Three ministers resigned in the same week. Two did not resign and were arrested instead, one of them mid-speech, which the feeds replayed until even I was tired of seeing it. The joint funding mechanism became the centerpiece of what the commentators were already calling the Reckoning Trials, and my testimony was entered into the record as Exhibit A, which meant that strangers on six worlds now knew my voice, my face, and the exact cadence of my grief.

I had expected to feel exposed. What I felt instead was lighter, in a way I'm still learning to describe. For twenty years my detachment had been load-bearing; take it out and I kept waiting for the structure to come down on me. It didn't. It turned out I had other beams.

Dov found me on the observation deck the evening the trials formally opened, standing where I seem to stand whenever the station gives me a choice about where to be. Below us Meridian turned, scarred and patched and lit unevenly, its secrets gone, its name in every feed on the dial.

"You know what I keep thinking," he said. He leaned on the rail beside me, in no hurry, the way he never was. "All those years we kept the array secret, I told myself it was survival. Necessity. But some nights — and I never said this to anyone, not even Anouk — some nights it felt like complicity. Like we were keeping the message safe *for* the war. Holding it in trust for the thing that killed everyone it was written to save."

"And now?"

"Now I think silence is a tool, same as anything. Depends what you build with it." He looked at me. "You built something."

"I built a document."

"You built fourteen funerals that were never held. You built a column that didn't exist before." He tapped the rail. "Ilse Marchetti's sister sent a message to the station office yesterday. First contact in thirty years. She thought Ilse died of a bad heart alone in a records annex. Now she knows Ilse died holding a door shut so the rest of us could keep a promise." He shrugged, embarrassed by his own sincerity, and I let him be embarrassed rather than tell him I'd read that message myself, three times, and that the last line of it — *thank you for making her visible* — was folded up in my pocket like a talisman I had no right to carry and carried anyway.

* * *

There was one more entry I almost didn't make.

It wasn't required. The tribunal wanted institutions, mechanisms, culpable structures; individual grief was outside their jurisdiction. But on the last page of the testimony, after the appendices, after the certification of sources, I added a personal addendum and filed it unredacted, knowing exactly what it would cost me in privacy and not caring.

*Addendum: the undersigned's brother, Elian Almeida, served aboard the CSV Halcyon Reach under Commander Dessaline Vraic, killed in action in the first year of the war. Commander Vraic has been implicated in the staging operation described herein. The undersigned states for the record that her brother's death — like every death of the war's first year — occurred inside a fiction manufactured jointly by both belligerents, and that no actuarial model, however complete, ever accounted for the possibility that the risk being priced was not real. This addendum is offered not as legal argument but as fact. He believed in the war. The war did not exist.*

I read it aloud once, alone, to hear whether it was true. It was true. That was the test I applied to every line of those four hundred pages, and it was the only one.

Grief, I discovered, does not end. It doesn't resolve like a claim. But it can be given somewhere to go, and it turns out the somewhere is other people — the living, the named, the sisters and sons and old friends who had spent decades carrying losses shaped like accidents and were finally handed the truth of their actual shape. Every message that reached the station office in those weeks was another weight set down. I answered all of them. It took hours a day. I have never minded less.

* * *

On the last night of drafting, Mirela brought me tea and stayed, sitting across the archive table among the governor's papers, watching me close the final file.

"Done?" she asked.

"Done." I looked at the word count and laughed — a short, involuntary sound. "Four hundred and six pages. My longest report before this was sixty."

"What happens to it now?"

"It's public record. Anyone can read it. Anyone will." I paused. "That's the point."

She nodded slowly, and then said the thing I've turned over every day since. "Forty years we hid the truth to keep it safe. Took an insurance adjuster to figure out the safe place was out loud."

After she left I sat in the humming dark of the archive and let myself feel it — not triumph, which is a young man's emotion, but something quieter and more durable. Reckoning. The ledger balanced at last, not in currency but in visibility, every erased person restored to the record, every buried mechanism dragged into the light where it could finally stop working.

Then my console chimed.

A priority transmission, routed through the arbitration fleet, addressed to me by name and title — my new title, the one I hadn't chosen and couldn't refuse: *Witness Almeida.* The tribunal was convening a closed session. They had recovered additional material from the array's deep buffers during the dismantling survey — transmissions predating the flicker, predating the governor, possibly predating the colony itself. And there was a signature on the earliest fragment that matched no personnel record in any database on any world.

They wanted me in the hearing chamber at 0800.

They wanted to ask me about the name *Tamsin.*
