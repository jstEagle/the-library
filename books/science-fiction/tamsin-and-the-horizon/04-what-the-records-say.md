## 4. What the Records Say

The archive hall of Horizon's administrative tower smelled of cold plastic and older cold beneath it, the way a room smells when it has been climate-controlled for longer than anyone alive can remember. Mara stood at a public terminal with his courier credentials glowing amber in the reader slot — third-tier access, enough to read, never enough to ask why.

He had come down from the *Kestrel* that morning with a courier's ordinary errands stacked in his satchel: manifests to countersign, a customs waiver for the seed-stock replacement drums, a letter from an Earthside widow to a grandson who had, according to every registry on this world, emigrated to Horizon forty-one years ago and then dissolved into the population like sugar into water. No record of the grandson. No record of the arrival. Just the name, and the widow's handwriting going soft at the edges where her pen had hesitated.

Mara had carried unanswered letters for nineteen years. He knew their weight the way sailors know weather. But this one itched differently, because the grandson's name was one he almost remembered saying aloud, once, in a voice younger than his own.

*Begin search,* the terminal prompted.

He typed: **Founding generation. Founding incident. Reactor.**

The screen filled with the official history, and it was beautiful, the way official histories are beautiful — clean, symmetrical, complete. *On 14 Voris, Year One of Settlement, a coolant failure in the Meridian reactor complex resulted in the loss of 212 members of the founding generation. The settlement mourns them. Their sacrifice made Horizon possible.*

There was even a memorial page. Two hundred and twelve names in a tasteful serif font, arranged in columns of eight. Mara scrolled them slowly, the way you'd walk a wall of the dead, and something at the base of his skull began to hum, low and wrong, like a wire carrying current it wasn't rated for.

He didn't know any of these names.

That should have been nothing. He was Earth-born; these were Horizon's founders; there was no reason on either world for him to know them. And yet the hum said otherwise. It said: *you have read this list before.* Not the names — the list. The shape of it. The specific grief of its typography.

He pulled the incident report.

It was thorough. Coolant loop failure, cascade timing, evacuation routes, casualty figures cross-referenced against habitat assignments. Footnoted. Certified. Signed by three review boards. Mara read it twice, and on the second reading he stopped noticing what it said and started noticing how it said it — because the prose had that particular texture he'd learned to distrust across two decades of corporate paperwork: no adjectives that weren't load-bearing, no sentence that admitted uncertainty, no human being anywhere in it. Reports written by people who were frightened read differently. This one read like it had been laundered.

He opened the appendices.

Appendix C listed the affected hab rings. Ring Four. Ring Five. Ring Six. Lower rings, all of them — the same lower rings Tamsin had named, sitting cross-legged on the landing pad's edge, swinging her feet over the drop and telling him about fire climbing the stairwells while adults screamed names that weren't in any registry.

His mouth went dry. He typed the girl's dream details into the query field one by one, testing each against the record like a man tapping ice to hear how thin it had gone.

Fire in the lower hab rings: consistent with the reactor report.

Adults screaming names not in any registry: the report accounted for all 212 dead by name, rank, and habitat assignment. Every name registered. Every death tidy.

Except — he leaned closer — except that the casualty appendix ended at 208. Four names short of the headline figure. A reconciliation note explained the discrepancy as a "counting correction applied post-publication." Applied post-publication. As if the dead could be audited downward after the fact, four souls adjusted away like a rounding error in a grain shipment.

Tamsin had said the adults screamed names that weren't in any registry.

Four names that had been in a registry, and then weren't.

The hum at the base of his skull became a ringing. He pressed his palms flat against the terminal's cool housing and breathed, and the breath came out shaking.

* * *

> **ARCHIVAL INTERLUDE — RECOVERED FRAGMENT, PROVENANCE UNKNOWN**
>
> *[audio, degraded]*
>
> "...telling you the count is wrong. I counted them myself off the ring manifest. Two hundred and twelve went down those stairs and two hundred and eight came out of the report. Where are the other four? Who signs off on losing four people between a staircase and a—"
>
> *[recording ends]*

* * *

He spent the next six hours doing what couriers do best: checking things against other things.

He requested the incident report from three separate archives — the administrative tower's public record, the medical authority's historical database, and the education ministry's civics curriculum repository, where the founding tragedy was taught to children in a unit called *How Horizon Endured*. Three archives. Three servers on three different subnets, maintained, presumably, by three different departments with three different budgets and three different degrees of carelessness.

All three reports were identical.

Not similar. Identical. Down to the paragraph breaks. Down to the footnote ordering. Down to — and here Mara felt the floor tilt gently beneath his chair — down to a typo.

Page eleven of the incident report, section 4.2, contained the phrase *"the coolant manifold experienced catatrophic failure."* Catatrophic. Missing an *s*, the error of a tired typist or a hasty hand. And all three copies, on three separate servers, administered by three separate authorities, contained the same misspelling in the same character position, rendered in the same font, on the same line.

Documents drift. That was the first law of archives, older than any archive: every copy is a mutation, every transmission a small erosion. Two independent copies of a living document will diverge within a generation the way two rivers diverge from a single source. For three copies to agree this perfectly, they had to be carved from the same master — stamped out, distributed, frozen. Not preserved. *Fixed.*

And fixed documents are not history. Fixed documents are decisions.

Mara sat back in the chair and looked up at the archive hall's high ceiling, where light panels glowed the color of old milk, and understood — really understood, in the body, in the teeth — that he was not looking at a cover-up. Cover-ups were personal things, the sins of frightened men with something to hide. This was larger and colder. This was an institution remembering on behalf of everyone, the way a prosthetic limb moves on behalf of a missing one. Somewhere upstream of all three servers sat a single source, and whoever tended that source did not make mistakes, did not revise, did not grieve. They simply issued.

The vertigo came then, rising through him like water filling a hold. Because if the record was issued rather than kept, then everything he had ever delivered — every manifest, every certificate, every sealed packet of "historical materials" he'd ferried between the worlds in good faith — might be issued too. His whole career, his whole honest, lonely, careful life, had been the circulatory system of a lie. He had been the blood. He had never once been allowed to see the heart.

* * *

> **ARCHIVAL INTERLUDE — EDUCATION MINISTRY, UNIT 7: "HOW HORIZON ENDURED"**
>
> *Lesson text, standard edition:*
>
> "The founding generation gave everything so that you might have anything. On the night of the Meridian accident, they acted with courage and order. There was no panic. There was no confusion. Remember them calmly, as they would have wished."
>
> *Marginal annotation, handwritten, unsigned, in a child's careful print:*
>
> "But my grandmother says there was screaming."

* * *

He found the redactions at the end of the day, when the archive hall had emptied and the light panels dimmed themselves to a dusk that never quite arrived.

Third-tier access showed redactions as black bars with reference numbers — the bureaucratic equivalent of a locked door with the lock's price engraved beside it. Most of the barred sections in the incident report were predictable: corporate liability assessments, insurance schedules, the names of reviewing officers. He skimmed them with a courier's practiced indifference.

Then he reached Appendix F. *Personal effects recovered from Rings Four through Six.*

The entire appendix was redacted. Not summarized — redacted, every line, forty-one entries' worth of black. And beneath the black, visible only because the censor had been careless with the header metadata, the item categories still showed: *clothing, personal. correspondence, private. photographs, family. toys, juvenile.*

Toys. Juvenile.

Children had died in the lower rings. Children whose existence the tidy casualty list of 208 registered nowhere, because the counting correction had folded them away along with the four unregistered names. Children whose toys had been catalogued, itemized, and then erased from the account of their own deaths.

Mara thought of Tamsin on the landing pad, her feet swinging over the void, describing the dream with the terrible precision only children and witnesses possess. She had said more than fire. She had said — and at the time he'd filed it under nightmare-logic, under the random furniture of sleeping minds — she had said she saw a *red coat* hanging in a stairwell, and a woman running back up the stairs toward it, and hands pulling the woman away, and the woman screaming a word over and over that Tamsin couldn't pronounce but had drawn for him once in the dust of the pad: a looping, doubled letter, like a name beginning and beginning again.

He queried the toy inventory category. Third-tier access returned nothing. But the metadata index — the dumb, honest skeleton beneath the flesh of the record — still held the entry counts. Forty-one entries. And one of them, entry F-19, carried a fragmentary description field the redaction had missed:

*Item: coat, child's, red wool. Recovered Ring Five, stairwell B.*

Stairwell B. The same stairwell Tamsin had drawn, unprompted, in the dust — fire at its foot, the red coat at its turn, the woman's doubled name screaming upward through the smoke.

A detail redacted even from him. A detail that existed nowhere in any document he could legally open. A detail that lived now in exactly two places in the universe: a sealed corporate archive, and the shared sleep of forty children.

Mara closed the terminal. Sat very still in the milk-colored dark. The ringing in his skull had resolved into something almost like words, though whose words he couldn't say — words arriving from a room in himself he had never been given the key to.

*You've walked stairwell B,* the words seemed to say. *You've heard that name.*

He told himself it was fatigue. He told himself a great many things, the way men do when the alternative is to listen.

Outside the archive hall, Horizon's evening shift moved through the corridors, orderly and calm, a civilization performing its own continuity. And somewhere above, in the residential spires, forty children were going to bed — going down into the same burning dream, keeping faith with the dead the way no record on this world would.

Mara picked up his satchel and started walking, faster than courtesy required, toward the one person who could tell him whether the voice in his skull was memory or madness.

He found Tamsin waiting for him at the vault terminus gate, past curfew, past permission, her breath fogging in the cold air, her eyes certain and afraid.

"You remembered something," she said. It wasn't a question.

Behind her, far down the corridor, two figures in administrator gray turned the corner — and stopped.
