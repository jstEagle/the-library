## 5. The Night Mara Forgets

The dream did not come to him the way dreams come to children.

It came like water finding a crack in a wall — patiently, without violence, following the path of least resistance until the whole structure was quietly saturated. Mara fell asleep in the guest quarters Voss had assigned him, in a bed made up with linens that smelled of industrial starch, and he woke inside someone else's night.

Or his own. That was the horror of it. He could not tell, at first, whose memory it was, because it fit him like an old glove: worn soft in all the places he had worn it himself.

* * *

He is twenty-three years old and the corridor lights are the wrong color.

He knows this immediately, the way you know a room has been rearranged — not by cataloguing what's changed but by the animal certainty that something is off. The lights should be white. They are amber, then red, then amber again, cycling through some emergency protocol no one has explained, and the cycling makes shadows move on the walls of Ring Three like slow animals pacing behind bars.

There is smoke. Not much of it. Enough.

He is carrying a toolkit he doesn't need. That detail arrives with terrible clarity: the weight of the case in his left hand, the way the handle bites the crease of his fingers, the way he keeps gripping it though there is nothing left to fix. A junior technician does not fix what is happening tonight. A junior technician walks where he is told to walk, and tonight he has been told to walk toward the shuttle bays, and instead he is walking toward the hab rings because he heard screaming through a bulkhead and his feet made the decision before his fear could veto it.

A woman is on her knees in the junction. She has grey in her hair and a name tag he cannot read — the letters swim when he looks at them directly, the way fish scatter under a boat — and she is holding the hand of a man who is not moving. She looks up at Mara and her face does something he will spend thirty years failing to remember and failing to forget.

She says a name. It isn't hers. It isn't his. It belongs to someone who is already gone, or who is about to be, and she says it the way you say a word in a language you're losing — carefully, completely, as if pronunciation were the last place the person could still exist.

Then hands take Mara's shoulders from behind. Gentle hands. Official hands. *This section is closed, technician.* And because he is twenty-three, and because the toolkit is heavy, and because obedience had been drilled into him so deep it had become indistinguishable from personality, he lets himself be turned around.

He walks toward the shuttle bays. He does not look back. He tells himself — even now, even inside the memory, he can feel himself telling it — that there was nothing he could have done. That testimony would have changed nothing. That survival is not a crime.

The lie fits him like the glove. Worn soft in all the places he wore it himself.

* * *

Mara woke with his heart slamming and the taste of ozone in his mouth, which was absurd, because there was no fire here, no ring three, nothing but the hum of Horizon's ventilation and the pale rectangle of the door. He sat up. His hands were shaking, and they smelled — this was the part that undid him — they smelled faintly of machine grease, though he hadn't touched an engine in days.

*Tamsin,* he thought. Her name arrived unbidden, and with it the understanding of what was happening. The link. Whatever channel the children shared, whatever conduit ran between their sleeping minds, it had found him too. Or he had found it. Or — worse, more precise — something in him had been waiting thirty years for a door to open, and had walked through it the moment one appeared.

He got up, drank water that tasted of recycled minerals, and told himself he would not sleep again.

He slept again.

* * *

The second sequence is shorter and crueler.

He is in a hearing room. No — not a hearing room; those came later, with tables and microphones and the careful theater of due process. This is before all that. This is a small office with a window onto the dark side of the colony, stars nailed motionless to the black, and a man across the desk whose face is a smooth oval of shadow because Mara's mind, even now, refuses to render it.

*You saw people in Ring Three,* the shadow says. Not a question.

*I saw a malfunction,* young Mara says, and hears his own voice do something he has never forgiven: it steadies itself. Deliberately. The way you steady a tool before you use it. *A coolant event. I filed my report.*

*You filed your report.* The shadow almost smiles. *Good. Accidents are simpler than intentions. Simpler for everyone. Especially for the families.*

And here is the sentence that has been buried under thirty years of routine and salt air and the long quiet route between worlds, surfacing now whole and glistening like something drowned:

*Especially for you,* the shadow says. *You're young. You have a career. Memory is a professional liability, technician. We have a procedure. It's painless. It's voluntary.*

*Voluntary.*

That word. He turns it over in the memory like a stone in a riverbed, feeling its weight, its smoothness, the way it has been polished by every pass of his own self-exculpation. Voluntary. He signed. Of course he signed. There was a stylus, there was a slate, there was the particular relief of a man being offered a door out of a burning building and being allowed to call it his own idea.

He remembers the last thing he chose to keep: the woman's face in the junction, held for one final second like a breath held underwater. Then he let it go. Then the water closed over everything, and when he surfaced he was a courier with a clean record and a vague, sourceless shame he had spent decades attributing to loneliness.

It wasn't loneliness. It never was. It was the ghost-weight of a signature.

* * *

He stopped fighting it after that. There was no point; the link had him, and some traitorous part of him — the part that had carried unanswered letters for thirty years, that had kept flying a route no one thanked him for — wanted to know. Wanted it the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth.

So on the third night he lay down deliberately, in the dark, and said her name into the empty room like a password. *Tamsin.*

And the dream took him by the hand.

* * *

Ring Two. The residential rings, before the fire, before whatever the fire actually was — because the fire, he understood now, was the smallest and most honest part of it. Fires happen. What happened in Ring Two was not weather. It was policy.

He is walking through a market. Sensory detail floods him with the indiscriminate generosity of real memory: the smell of yeast from the communal ovens, wet paint on a stall frame, a child's shoe squeaking against deck plating. People speak four languages around him and laugh across all of them. There is a mural on the far wall — founders' faces, painted by someone who loved them, each one mid-gesture, mid-laugh, caught becoming rather than being.

He knows these faces.

Not from history. From break rooms. From maintenance rotations. The tall woman with the burn scar who ran hydroponics and called every junior tech *sprout*. The thin man who fixed the air scrubbers and sang off-key and gave Mara half his lunch ration once, unprompted, saying *you look like a boy who forgets to eat.* These are not names in a registry. These are people who knew how he took his coffee.

They are going to die tomorrow. He knows this the way you know things in dreams, totally and uselessly, and he wants to grab them — the scarred woman, the singing man, all of them — and shake them and shout *leave, get on a shuttle, go anywhere,* and his mouth won't open, because this is memory and memory does not permit intervention. Memory only permits witness. That is its cruelty and its function.

The last thing he sees in Ring Two is the mural. Later — he knows this too, from the other sequences, the ones arriving now in fragments like wreckage washing ashore — later the mural will be painted over in institutional grey, and the grey will be described in official documents as *routine surface maintenance*, and everyone who could contradict that description will be dead or signed.

He wakes weeping, quietly, into the starch-smelling pillow, and does not make a sound, because the walls of Administrator Voss's guest quarters are very likely listening, and he has learned — has always known, beneath the forgetting — what this colony does with witnesses.

* * *

In the morning he went to find her.

Tamsin was in the education block's courtyard, sitting alone on a bench with her back straight and her chin up in the way of a child who has decided that dignity is armor. When she saw him she didn't smile. She studied his face with the terrible patience of the young, looking for something.

"You dreamed it," she said. "All three nights."

"Yes."

"What color were the lights?"

"Amber. Then red."

Her shoulders dropped a fraction, tension leaving her body like air from a valve. "Nobody believes me about the lights." She patted the bench beside her, an oddly formal gesture, a hostess's gesture, eleven years old and ancient. "The woman in the junction," she said, carefully, watching him. "When I dream her, I always wake up with a word in my mouth. A name that isn't mine. Do you know it?"

And Mara opened his mouth to say *no* — the reflex of thirty years, the reflex that had been installed along with the forgetting, smooth and immediate and free of charge —

— and then he stopped, because Tamsin was watching him the way the shadow in the office had watched him, waiting to see which version of him would answer, and he understood that this was the moment. Not the broadcast, not the archive, not whatever confrontation waited with Voss. This one. This bench. Whether he lied to a child to stay safe, one more time, in one more small and reasonable voice.

"I don't know the name," he said slowly. "But I think I know why none of us can read it. I think they took it out of us. All of us. And I think I helped them take it out of me."

Tamsin nodded, unsurprised, the way children are unsurprised by the confessions of adults — as if she had known all along that the grown-ups' silence was not ignorance but wound.

"The treatment starts Monday," she said. "Voss announced it this morning. 'Restorative therapy.' Forty kids. They go in smiling." She looked down at her hands. "Milo went in yesterday. Pilot program. He came back and asked me why I kept talking about a fire. He laughed at me, Mara. Milo laughed at me."

Something cold slid down Mara's spine and settled, familiar as an old coat. He knew that laugh. He had heard it in his own throat, once, in a hearing room with a window full of stars, when a man with a shadowed face told him forgetting was voluntary and he had smiled with relief.

"They'll come for me too," Tamsin said. It wasn't a question and it wasn't fear, quite. It was logistics. "I can hold the link open longer than they think. But I need to know what's worth holding it open *for*. You remembered the rings. You remembered the faces." She leaned closer, fierce, urgent, her eyes bright with a purpose no child should have to carry. "Is there anywhere the real records could still exist? Paper, film, anything they couldn't edit? Because everything in the colony archive agrees with itself too perfectly, and I've read enough of your memories now to know — perfect agreement is what a lie looks like when it's been maintained."

Mara stared at her, and the cold thing in his spine turned over.

Because he knew of exactly one place like that. One place where paper survived, where the corporation's tidy digital consensus had never reached. A vault beneath a seed repository on a dead world, at the end of a silent route flown by one old man whom nobody bothered to watch anymore.

Earth. The Earth terminus. He had been landing supplies there for thirty years, walking over it, eating lunch above it, never once asking what the lower levels held — because asking questions, some smoothed-over instinct whispered, was not in his job description.

"Tamsin," he said, and his voice came out rough. "How soon can you be ready to travel?"
