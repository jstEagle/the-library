## 10. Tamsin and the Horizon

The first ship in a year came down through Horizon's grey morning like a slow silver leaf, and Tamsin stood on the landing apron with forty other children and watched it fall out of the sky.

It was not Mara's ship. It was bigger, uglier, hung with corporate registry tags that had been freshly painted over — someone had tried to sand them off and hadn't quite managed, so that under certain light you could still read the ghost of a name: *Meridian Consolidated Growth & Settlement*. The same name that had been carved into the archive door beneath the seed vault's Earth terminus. The same name Voss used to say like a prayer before he said it like a threat.

But the hatch opened, and people came out — actual people, reporters and archivists and one weeping old woman who turned out to be the daughter of a founder, of a woman whose name existed nowhere on Horizon until three weeks ago, when the broadcast went through both worlds like a bell being struck. The woman knelt down on the apron and pressed her palm flat against the ground, as if she could feel her mother through the metal, through the soil, through the years.

Tamsin watched her for a long time. Then she looked up at the sky, where the courier route now showed as a thin bright stitch being sewn between two stars, and she thought: *that's his work too, even if he doesn't know it.*

* * *

A year is long when you're eleven going on twelve. A year is long enough for the lower hab rings to be rebuilt with windows this time, real windows, because the inquiry found that the founders had died partly because the fire suppression failed and partly because no one outside could see in. Now you could see in. Now anyone walking the ring road could look down into the gardens the children planted over the mass grave, because it was a mass grave, everyone knew that now, and the colony had voted — actually voted, with their hands raised in the assembly hall — to leave the dead where they were and grow things above them.

Tamsin's plot was beans and something called moonflower, which the seed vault had sent up in the first resupply. She liked the moonflowers best. They only opened at dusk, all at once, as if they'd agreed on it beforehand.

The dreams were gone. That was the strange part, the part she still couldn't sit comfortably with. Forty-one children had carried a massacre in their sleep for years, and then the truth had been spoken aloud, written down, mourned properly — and the dreams simply stopped, like water finding its level. Dr. Iles, who had replaced Voss (Voss was awaiting trial on Earth; Tamsin hoped it was cold there), said trauma leaves when it's witnessed. Tamsin thought it was more like the smoke finally having somewhere to go.

She didn't miss the dream. But some nights she woke anyway, out of habit, and lay listening to the hab ring breathe, and checked — the way you check for a lost tooth with your tongue — that the memories were still there. The fire. The names. The junior technician crouched behind a coolant manifold with his hands over his ears.

Him. Always him, at the center of it, like a stone at the bottom of a well.

* * *

Mara lived in the small house by the assembly hall, the one with the crooked porch rail he kept meaning to fix. He grew tomatoes badly and read everything, voraciously, like a man eating a meal after a long fast — because that was what it was, she supposed. Every book was new to him. Every face. Every morning he woke into a life with no floor under it except other people's words.

He remembered nothing. Not the route, not the vault, not the night of the fire, not the choice he'd made in the archive beneath the Earth terminus when Voss offered him his own past back like a wrapped gift and he'd said *no, give it to them instead* — and paid with everything he was. The upload link had taken it all. Twenty years of courier runs, a childhood home on Earth with a blue door, the smell of his mother's bread, his own name in his own voice. All of it poured out of him to hold the connection open while the massacre recordings burned their way through the corporate firewalls and into every screen on two worlds.

What was left was a quiet man of about fifty who smiled easily and flinched at nothing and trusted one girl completely, for reasons he could not explain and did not try to.

"Tell me again," he'd say, almost every visit, settling onto his bad porch chair while Tamsin sat on the steps. "Tell me about the blue door."

"It was blue," Tamsin would say. "Not sky-blue. Deeper. Like the sea looks from above when it's about to storm. Your mother painted it herself and your father teased her about it for eleven years and she never once repainted it."

"And I ran away from home at nineteen."

"You took the last shuttle off-world with a duffel bag and forty credits and you never called, and you regretted it every single day, and you still never called, because you were stubborn in exactly the way you are now, except now you don't remember being stubborn so you can't do anything about it."

Mara would laugh at that — he laughed easily too — and then he'd go quiet, looking out at the tomato plants that refused to thrive, and he'd say the thing he always said:

"I believe you. You know that? I believe every word."

And Tamsin would nod and look at her hands, because there was a version of this where it was sweet, and a version where it was the loneliest thing she'd ever heard, and most days it was both at once.

* * *

In the spring — Horizon's spring, which smelled of thawing regolith and the mineral green of the first hydroponic cuttings moved outdoors — the school got a new assignment: write the history of the founding, in your own words, using whatever sources you trust.

Most kids wrote three paragraphs. Reactor accident, revised: corporate purge, forty-seven dead, records falsified, truth restored by broadcast. Done.

Tamsin sat in front of her slate for four evenings and couldn't start.

Because here was the problem nobody talked about: the official record was complete now. The inquiry report was public. The names were carved on the memorial wall in the assembly hall, all forty-seven, in letters deep enough to catch the light. And yet the record was also *empty*, somehow, in the way a photograph of a person is empty. It said what happened. It did not say what it was to be there. It did not contain the sound the fire made, or the way Administrator Voss's voice had gone gentle when he offered the bargain, or the exact weight of a man choosing to forget himself so that children wouldn't have to.

That lived in her. Only in her. In her and in the thirty-nine other dream-children, and in Mara's body somewhere, in the shape of habits he couldn't account for — the way he always slept facing the door, the way he counted exits in any room without seeming to, the way he hummed a tune no one on Horizon or Earth had ever heard and could not name.

Memory survives by being handed to someone new. She didn't know where she'd learned that — maybe from Dr. Iles, maybe from the dream itself, maybe from the ghost of a guilty technician whispering up through the well-stone of her. But she believed it the way she believed in gravity.

So on the fifth evening she opened a fresh file, and instead of the assignment she typed:

*Tamsin and the Horizon. Being a true account of the founding massacre of the colony Horizon, the forty-one children who dreamed it, and the courier who gave everything he was so that it could be told. Written by Tamsin Okonkwo-Reyes, aged twelve, who was there in her sleep.*

She stopped. Read it. Deleted *Okonkwo-Reyes* — no, kept it, it was her name, she should sign her own name — and then deleted the whole title and retyped it slower, the way you plant a seed.

Then she began at the beginning, which was not the fire. The beginning was a smell: woodsmoke in a bedroom at 0400, on a morning in the lower hab rings, when an eleven-year-old girl woke up knowing something had burned somewhere, sometime, to someone — and went to find out what.

She wrote until the ring lights dimmed for night cycle. She wrote about the dream, and the administrators' smooth dismissals, and the day a courier named Mara Kavanagh landed under a cold official welcome and looked at her across the arrivals hall like a man seeing his own childhood walk toward him wearing someone else's face.

* * *

She read it to him in pieces, over the summer. She hadn't planned to. It just happened — he'd ask what she was working on, and she'd say *you*, and he'd laugh, and then one evening she just started reading aloud on the crooked porch while the moonflowers opened along the fence in their silent unanimous way.

He listened with his hands folded, very still. When she reached the part about the coolant manifold — about the young technician who heard the screaming and covered his ears and ran — Mara's breath changed. Not a flinch. Something older than flinching. Like a body remembering a wound the mind had been spared.

"He ran," Mara said quietly, when she finished that section.

"Yes."

"And then he spent twenty years carrying supplies to the children of the people he abandoned."

"Yes," Tamsin said. "Letters too. Nobody answered them. He brought them anyway."

Mara looked out at the darkening garden for a long time. The first star of the courier route pricked through above the western ridge, right on schedule, stitching itself toward Earth.

"Do you hate him?" he asked. "The boy who ran. Do you hate him?"

Tamsin considered it honestly, the way she considered everything now, because honesty was the whole point of her — she was the record now, she understood that, she had accepted it the way you accept a heartbeat.

"No," she said. "I think he was scared and alone and everyone around him was lying, and he was nineteen. I think the running was the worst night of his life and he spent the rest of it trying to walk back toward the fire without ever letting himself remember there'd been one." She paused. "I think he came back. That's the ending. He came back, and when they offered him himself, he chose us instead."

"That's a good ending," Mara said.

"It's not the ending," Tamsin said. "It's the middle. I'm writing the middle."

He laughed, and the laugh cracked a little in the center, and she pretended not to notice, because some mercies are just good manners.

* * *

In autumn, the second courier ship of the reopened route brought mail — real mail, sacks of it, the first answers to twenty years of unanswered letters. Most were addressed to the dead, and the archivists read them aloud at the memorial wall on Founders' Day, and the whole colony stood in the cold and listened to the voices of people forgiving people who could no longer hear it.

One letter was addressed to *M. Kavanagh, courier, retired or otherwise.* Tamsin carried it to the little house herself.

Mara held it unopened for a moment. "Who's it from?"

"Your sister," Tamsin said. "You have a sister. She lives near the old blue door. She writes with a left slant and she apologizes in advance for her handwriting, which is fine, actually."

Mara looked at her — that look, the one that was equal parts wonder and surrender, the look of a man being told who he was by the only book that contained him. Then he opened the letter with careful fingers, and read, and his eyes filled, and he didn't know why, and he laughed at himself for crying over a stranger's handwriting, and Tamsin sat on the step beside him and let him cry, and wrote none of it down, because some pages belong to no one but the people living them.

Later, walking home under the stitched light of the route, she opened her slate and added a line to the end of the manuscript, though she knew it wasn't the end:

*He remembers nothing. I remember everything. Between us, it adds up to one whole man, and one true story, and a road between two worlds that stays open as long as someone keeps walking it.*

Below that, she typed a single question mark, and left it there — because the route was open now, and ships were coming, and somewhere out in the dark between the stars there were other silent colonies, other erased generations, other children who might be dreaming somebody's smoke.

Somebody would have to go and ask them what they dreamed.

Tamsin was already asking her mother if she could take the trip.
