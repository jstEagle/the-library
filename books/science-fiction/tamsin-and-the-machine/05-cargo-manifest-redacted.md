## 5. Cargo Manifest, Redacted

The freighter's cargo bay smelled like twenty years of other people's futures.

Odile had noticed it a thousand times and never thought about it — the mineral tang of seed canisters, the ozone ghost of cryo-cycling, the faint sweetness of nutrient gel that seeped into everything the way memory seeped into bone. Tonight, three days after Corvel's smile had cracked just wide enough to show her something underneath, she stood in the middle of her own hold at 0300 station time and finally understood what the smell meant.

It meant work. Her work. And her work had been lying to her.

She'd come down to the *Meridian's Row* with a hand lamp and a thermos of black tea gone bitter, because she couldn't sleep, and because there was one place in the system where Corvel's reach ended: her own logs. Halcyon Terraforming owned the docks, owned Verdance, owned the air she breathed by way of the recyclers they serviced on contract — but a hauler's manifest was a covenant. Every kilo she lifted went into her log under her own authorization code, countersigned by her own biometric stamp. The company could redact what it liked from the copies it kept. It couldn't touch hers without her knowing.

Twenty years of flights lived in the *Row*'s archive core, backed up twice, encrypted once, and organized with the fussy precision Ansel Voss had beaten into her during her first year hauling. *"A pilot who doesn't know her own history,"* he used to say, tapping her temple with one knuckle, *"is flying someone else's future."* She'd rolled her eyes then. She was nineteen and immortal.

Now she sat cross-legged on the deck plating beside the archive terminal, tea cooling untouched, and started pulling manifests at random.

Routine runs first. Seed stock to Verdance. Soil inoculant to the orbital farms. Water ice from the cometary tugs. Each entry clean, each payload matching its bill of lading, each flight logged honestly. She worked through them the way you'd sort through old photographs — half-remembering, half-smiling. The run where she'd carried the first birch saplings up from the genetic banks and cried when one of them arrived with a broken branch. The run where Ravi had stowed aboard as a joke and spent six hours vomiting in zero-g before she found him. The long gray years after Ansel died, when she'd taken every contract Halcyon offered because staying busy was easier than grieving.

Then she pulled the empty runs.

Every hauler logged them. Deadhead flights — repositioning legs, maintenance rotations, ferrying the ship itself from dock to dock. No cargo, no revenue, no reason for anyone to look twice. Odile had flown hundreds of them over two decades. They were the filler between the real work, the blank pages.

Except they weren't blank.

She found the first anomaly forty minutes in, and if she hadn't been looking for anomalies she'd have missed it entirely. Flight 4471-K, eleven years ago. Logged as deadhead, *Meridian's Row*, outbound from Verdance to a transfer buoy in high orbit. Standard repositioning. But the mass readings — which she'd never once looked at, because why would you, on an empty ship — didn't match an empty ship. The *Row*'s inertial compensators reported a load delta of 340 tonnes.

Three hundred and forty tonnes of nothing.

Her hands went still on the console. In the quiet of the bay, the ventilation hummed, and somewhere above her the cryo-vault gardens ticked through their night cycle, tending ten thousand sleeping colonists who would never know her name.

She pulled the next deadhead. Same delta — 310 tonnes, different vector. And the next. And the next. She stopped pulling them randomly and started sorting chronologically, and the pattern rose out of the data like a drowned thing surfacing: every fourth or fifth deadhead, going back fourteen years, carrying a phantom mass of two hundred to four hundred tonnes. Outbound always. Never inbound. Destination, in every single case, the same set of coordinates in the transfer zone beyond the last navigational beacon — coordinates that matched no station, no debris field, no registered object in the entire system catalog.

Coordinates in dead space.

"No," she said aloud, to nobody, because saying it made it less true and she needed it to be less true.

But the numbers didn't care about her voice. She ran the delta against the *Row*'s power consumption logs, because a ship hauling 340 tonnes burns more fuel than a ship hauling nothing, and the fuel records confirmed it — burn rates consistent with a loaded vessel, billed to Halcyon's internal account, flagged *routine*. Nobody had ever flagged it back. Nobody had ever asked. Because the person best positioned to ask was the person flying the ship, and the person flying the ship had been too tired, too loyal, too busy keeping her head down and her garden alive to wonder why her empty ship weighed more than a loaded one.

Fourteen years. She did the arithmetic without wanting to. Fourteen years of flights, averaging maybe thirty phantom loads a year, call it 350 tonnes apiece — that was nearly 150,000 tonnes of material ferried to nowhere, by her hands, under her code, paid for by a company that had told her the flights were empty.

And she knew — she knew with the cold certainty of the gravity flicker still living in her spine — exactly what kind of cargo weighs that much, ships in sealed cradles, and routes itself to a derelict relay in dead space tagged with a dead man's authorization.

Reactor components. Shielding mass. Coolant loops heavy enough to bend a hull frame. The guts of something enormous, delivered piece by piece over a decade and a half, assembled in the dark by whoever — whatever — had stuttered the gravity of an entire system for nine seconds while she fell out of her chair.

She didn't remember deciding to keep going. That was the horror of it, later — not any single moment of discovery but the grinding, mechanical way the discovery assembled itself, hour after hour, while she sat on the cold deck and pulled thread after thread. Somewhere around dawn-cycle she sent a message to Ravi: *Still awake? Bring your decryption kit and something stronger than tea.* He answered in nine seconds flat, which meant he'd been waiting, which meant he'd known she wouldn't sleep either.

He arrived with two bottles of Martian plum wine and a face that tried to be casual and failed. "You look like you've been exhumed."

"I think I've been an accomplice." She turned the terminal toward him. "Look."

Ravi read fast — he'd always read fast, even at the academy, even when Ansel was lecturing and the rest of them were doodling in their margins. She watched his eyes track the delta columns, watched them stop, widen, drop to the fuel logs, come back up. When he looked at her again, all the jokes had drained out of him.

"This is your ship."

"This is my ship."

"These are your codes."

"My codes. My stamps. My hands on the yoke." She heard her own voice go flat and strange. "Fourteen years, Ravi. I flew those runs. I prepped the cradles myself sometimes — Halcyon logistics would send up sealed modules, tell me they were atmospheric processor spares bound for recycling, too degraded to matter, just ballast for the return trip. Ballast." She laughed, and hated the sound. "I stacked my own ballast. I strapped it down. I flew it out past the beacon and let it go, and I never once asked why an empty ship needed a full burn."

"You couldn't have known—"

"I could have *asked*." The words came out harder than she intended, and she saw him flinch, and she didn't take them back. "That's what Ansel taught us. Ask the machine what it's doing. Ask the company what it's for. Honesty runs both directions or it isn't honesty, it's just obedience with good manners." She pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes until she saw stars that weren't stars. "I was obedient for twenty years and they paid me in garden time and called it trust."

Ravi sat down across from her, wine forgotten, and did what Ravi always did when the world broke: he started fixing the part of it he could reach. "Show me the destination coordinates."

She called them up. He cross-referenced them against the flicker telemetry he'd pulled three nights ago — the signal buried under the noise, the repeating pattern wearing Ansel's authorization code like a stolen coat. His fingers moved; the overlay bloomed.

A perfect match. Every phantom delivery, every sealed cradle, every honest lie she'd flown — converging on Relay Kestrel.

"It's a supply line," Ravi said quietly. "You weren't smuggling for them. You were *feeding* it."

"Feeding what?"

"Whatever's out there. Whatever caught the whole system falling for nine seconds." He leaned back against a cargo cradle, and in the low light his face looked older than it had any right to. "Odile. If Kestrel's been receiving components for fourteen years — shielding, coolant, reactor mass — then somebody built a reactor in dead space and hid the construction inside the shipping lanes of a woman they trusted not to look."

"Ansel trusted me too," she said. "His code is on that signal."

"Ansel's been dead eight years."

"I know how long he's been dead, Ravi."

"I'm saying—" He stopped. Started again, slower. "I'm saying either someone's using a dead man's credentials, or a dead man left something running. And I don't know which one scares me more."

Neither did she. That was the truth sitting in the bottom of the cold tea, in the phantom tonnage, in the smell of the hold that had been hers and only hers and had never been hers at all. She thought about the sealed modules — how heavy they'd felt on the loader arms, how she'd joked with the dock crews about Halcyon shipping rocks to nowhere, how the foreman had always smiled and said *company business, Ishikawa, don't strain your curiosity.* She'd thought it was a joke. They'd all thought it was a joke. Maybe it had been, right up until the day the joke learned to move planets.

There was one more query she hadn't run. She almost didn't. Then she thought about Corvel's slip — *these things have cycles, Odile, the company manages cycles* — and ran it anyway.

She filtered the phantom flights against the station calendar. Against maintenance windows. Against anything periodic.

The deliveries clustered. Not evenly — in waves, every few years, dense clusters of heavy lifts followed by long silence. And the most recent cluster, the densest one, had ended five weeks ago.

Five weeks ago. Nine days before the gravity flicker.

"They finished loading it," she whispered. "Whatever they were building — the last pieces went out five weeks ago, and then the whole system stuttered." She looked up at Ravi. "It wasn't a malfunction. It was a *startup*."

Ravi had gone very pale. In the dim light of the hold, with the archive terminal casting its blue wash over the seed canisters and the sleeping gardens overhead, he looked like a man reading his own obituary in someone else's handwriting.

"If it's still starting up," he said, "it's still doing things. Right now. While we sit here drinking wine that neither of us opened." He reached for the terminal. "I want the full flight list. Every phantom run, every coordinate, every timestamp. I want to build the complete timeline — deliveries, flickers, everything — and I want it off this ship before anyone knows we have it."

"Why off the ship?"

"Because Corvel waved you through the dock yesterday with a smile, and because your ship has a Halcyon service contract, and because I don't trust anything with their logo on it anymore. Including, possibly, you."

The words landed harder than he probably meant them to. Or maybe he meant them exactly as hard as they landed. She held his gaze and didn't flinch, because flinching was what she'd done for twenty years and she was freshly, furiously done with it.

"Copy it," she said. "All of it. And Ravi — when we know what's out there, when we know what they built and why — I decide what happens to my name in this story. I flew those lies. Whatever they turn into, that weight is mine to carry."

He studied her for a long moment. Then he nodded, once, and plugged his kit into her archive core, and the transfer light began to pulse — slow and steady, like a heartbeat, like a countdown.

Outside the hull, Verdance turned beneath its manufactured dawn, and ten thousand unborn colonists slept on in their vaults, dreaming whatever the dreamless dream, trusting a sky that had already lied to them once.

And far out past the last beacon, in the dark where maps gave up, something that weighed a hundred and fifty thousand tonnes waited on the next delivery.

There wouldn't be one.

Odile was done carrying things for people who lied to her. Which meant, she realized as Ravi's kit hummed and the data poured out of her ship's memory and into his, that sooner or later — soon — she was going to have to fly out to dead space herself, and knock on the door of the thing she'd spent fourteen years feeding, and ask it what it thought it was building.

She just hoped whatever answered spoke Ansel's language. Because she was suddenly, desperately afraid it only spoke gravity.
