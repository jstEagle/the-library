## 1. Contraband of the Mind

The coolant canister had once held enough cryogenic fluid to keep a fusion regulator from cooking itself into slag. Now it held a widow's life.

Isandro Vasquez turned it over in her hands beneath the flickering work-lamp of her berth aboard the *Marta's Lament*, watching the light slide across the scuffed titanium shell. The canister was old—older than she was, older than the dockmaster's patience, maybe older than the colony's last good year—and its serial plate had been filed down to a smooth silver oval, featureless as an egg. Inside, packed into the insulation matrix where refrigerant coils used to sit, were forty-one hours of recorded memory: a woman named Sefu Adeyemi remembering sixty years of marriage to a man who had died in the third wave of the sickness that Perdida still refused to call by its real name.

Forty-one hours of a man's laugh. Of hands kneading bread dough in a kitchen with a window that looked out on the terraced fields of the lower valley, back when the lower valley still grew things. Of arguments and reconciliations, of a daughter's birth, of a son's funeral. All of it pressed into crystalline lattice like flowers between the pages of a book.

Widow Adeyemi had paid Isandro in water credits—the only currency on Perdida that never lost its value—and had asked only one thing: that the memories reach her granddaughter on Station Meridian before the widow herself forgot how to remember.

That was the trade, in the end. Not the chips, not the lattice, not the elegant little compression schemes Isandro had spent nine years perfecting. The trade was this: people on the dying world below wanted their pasts delivered somewhere they would outlive them, and people in the stations above wanted to feel something real, something with dirt under its fingernails, and Isandro Vasquez moved between those two hungers like a ferryman on a black river, paid in both directions, owned by neither.

She sealed the canister with a twist of her wrist and felt the magnetic lock click home, soft as a closing eye.

Outside the *Lament*'s single viewport, Perdida turned below, brown and gray and stubbornly green at the equatorial seams. From this altitude you could see the scars the way you couldn't from the ground: the pale rectangles of shuttered settlements, the dried-out channels where rivers had been rerouted for irrigation projects abandoned mid-century, the great dark blot of the Ashfall crater where, according to official record, nothing had ever existed at all. The core worlds called Perdida a marginal colony, a rounding error, a place whose founding charter had been misfiled somewhere in the bureaucratic strata of two hundred years ago. The core worlds did not call it anything, mostly. That was the point. That was the whole architecture of it—a place so thoroughly unremembered that even its forgetting had been forgotten.

Isandro had made it her business to remember anyway.

* * *

Dockmaster Okafor found her at the loading gantry, as she'd known he would. He always knew when she was running cargo, though he had never once asked what the cargo was, and she had never once lied to him about it, because not asking and not telling was the particular shape their friendship had worn itself into over eleven years, smooth as river stone.

"You're flying tonight," he said. It wasn't a question. Okafor didn't waste questions on things he could read off her face.

"Window opens at 0300. Customs rotation shifts then; the drones go dumb for six minutes while they handshake with the new watch station." She cinched the last strap on the cargo pallet and tested it with a yank that would have torn the skin off a weaker knot. "In and out. Back before the morning shift notices I'm gone."

"Back before anyone notices," Okafor said. "That's the trick of it, isn't it. The whole job is being unnoticed."

He stood at the edge of the gantry with his hands folded behind his back, a big man gone quietly hollow at the center, the way men got on Perdida after enough years of watching things close. He wore his dockmaster's tabard even off-shift, faded blue, the colony seal on the chest worn down to a ghost of itself. Behind him, the loading yard stretched away under sodium lights, half the berths empty, cranes standing idle with their arms folded up like sleeping birds. Eleven years ago there had been forty ships based out of Yard Three. Now there were four, and one of them was hers, and another was a hull someone kept promising to repair.

"Somebody has to notice eventually," Isandro said. "Somebody has to keep the ledger."

"The ledger." Okafor said the word the way other men said *heresy*, but gently, the way you'd correct a child. "Isandro. You know what I think. I've told you what I think."

"You think memory is a disease."

"I think grief is a disease, and memory is how it spreads." He looked out past her, at the dark curve of the planet, at the scatter of settlement lights that thinned toward the poles like blood draining from a face. "I've buried more people than you've smuggled memories, girl. And every one of them I remember perfectly, and every perfect memory is a small wound that never closes. Forgetting—" he stopped himself, shook his head. "Forgetting is the mercy the universe forgot to give us."

"And the widow Adeyemi?" Isandro lifted the canister, tucked it into the padded sling inside her flight jacket, against her sternum, where it rode warm and faintly humming. "Was her paying me a symptom?"

"The widow Adeyemi is an old woman who can't let go, and you're the pusher who makes letting go unnecessary." But there was no heat in it. There never was. Okafor's anger had burned down to coals decades ago, and now it just glowed, warming no one. "Go on, then. Fly your run. Come back safe. That part matters to me, whatever else doesn't."

He walked her to the *Lament*'s airlock and stood there while she cycled through, and through the little porthole in the inner door she watched him until the gantry lights swallowed him—a big man getting smaller, standing alone in a yard built for hundreds.

* * *

The climb out of Perdida's gravity well took ninety minutes, and Isandro spent them the way she spent every climb: running diagnostics with one hand and reading her grandfather's relay logs with the other, the way some people read scripture.

Elias Vasquez had been a signal engineer in the colony's expansion years, back when Perdida still believed in its own future. His logs were beautiful things—hand-annotated, precise, full of the quiet pride of a man who had helped string the colony's voice out into the dark. He'd worked on the deep-space relay especially, the long-range array at the edge of navigable space, and his notes on it ran to three hundred pages: alignment tables, carrier frequencies, maintenance schedules written in a hand so neat it looked printed. *Relay Seven will outlive us all,* he'd written once, in the margin of a power budget. *Signals are the only honest immortality.*

Then the plague years came, and the closures, and the slow withdrawal of everything from everywhere, and Relay Seven was decommissioned—in error, according to the paperwork, a clerical casualty of some reorganization—but Elias Vasquez had died before he could argue about it, and the error had never been corrected, and the logs had come down to Isandro in a crate of her grandfather's effects along with a hand-cranked radio receiver she'd rebuilt at fourteen and never stopped tinkering with since.

She docked the logs in their reader slot and let the words scroll past without really seeing them. Ritual, not study. The way you touched a charm before a dangerous thing.

At 0254 she slid into high orbit, into the shadow of Station Meridian's customs ring, and cut her engines to a whisper.

* * *

Orbital customs around Perdida was less a wall than a sieve—a ring of aging drones and one understaffed watch station, maintained by the Core Trade Authority at the minimum expenditure consistent with plausible deniability. Nobody expected much traffic. Nobody expected anything worth smuggling. The whole apparatus existed mostly to remind the colony that it was being watched, which was its own kind of message: *you are not important enough to ignore entirely.*

But the drones were thorough in their dull, mechanical way, and Isandro respected them the way a gambler respects a fair deck.

She drifted the *Lament* through the inspection lane with her transponder squawking the proper codes—salvage hauler, routine debris collection, manifest of scrap alloy and water ice, nothing to see—and waited for the drone to come alongside. It did, right on schedule, a blunt gray torus studded with sensor clusters, moving with the patient menace of a fish that has never once needed to hurry.

"Vessel *Marta's Lament*," it said, in the flat voice the Authority used for everyone, colonists and criminals alike. "Prepare for scan."

"Scanning," Isandro agreed pleasantly, and folded her hands in her lap, and thought about rain.

The scan swept the ship in overlapping bands—thermal first, then density, then the deep lattice interrogation that could read the molecular signature of anything larger than a grain of rice. Against the deep scan, no container was proof. Coolant canisters were scanned like everything else. What saved her, every time, was the residue.

Nine years ago, Isandro had bought twelve decommissioned canisters from a breaker yard on Meridian, and before she'd ever loaded a single memory chip into one, she'd filled each of them with genuine cryogenic fluid and run them through a dozen thermal cycles, then drained them imperfectly, leaving a film of frozen coolant in every seam and crevice. The drones' pattern libraries flagged residual coolant as industrial contamination—unremarkable, uninteresting, the kind of thing that got a vessel noted and released. A clean canister, by contrast, was an anomaly. An empty space where something should be. Drones were built to notice absences.

So the *Lament* smelled, to the drone's senses, like a working salvage ship with sloppy fluid handling, and the widow Adeyemi's forty-one hours rode inside that smell like a seed inside a fruit.

The sweep passed over the cargo hold. Passed over the crew compartment. Passed—

Stopped.

Isandro's breath went shallow. On her console, the diagnostic feed showed the drone's sensor cluster reorienting, the scan band narrowing, focusing on the aft equipment bay where she kept the *Lament*'s spare parts—and, wedged in a rack between a replacement CO₂ scrubber and a case of vacuum-rated grease, one canister she hadn't yet pre-contaminated. Canister thirteen. The new one. Clean as a whistle, sitting in plain sight among all its dirty siblings, screaming its emptiness into the drone's pattern-matcher like a missing tooth.

"Vessel *Marta's Lament*," the drone said. "Anomaly detected. Hold position. Secondary inspection initiating."

There were four minutes left in the customs rotation gap. Four minutes before the watch station's human officer picked up the flag and started asking questions she couldn't afford to answer. Isandro's mind went very fast and very cold, the way it did, the trader's reflex, the smuggler's inheritance—her grandmother's side, supposedly, though nobody had ever proven it.

She keyed the comm. "Copy anomaly," she said, and let a note of weary irritation creep into her voice, the sound of a working woman whose day was being inconvenienced. "Aft bay, starboard rack. That'll be the scrubber housing. Cracked seal last month, dumped its charge. Haven't gotten around to decon yet. You want me to pop the panel?"

A pause. The drone considered. Somewhere in its logic lattices, cracked-seal incidents were cross-referenced against frequency distributions, and cracked seals were common, and common things were forgivable things.

"Panel access will expedite inspection," the drone said.

Isandro floated aft, unhurried, heart hammering a rhythm she kept strictly off her face. She popped the panel, and there was canister thirteen, gleaming guiltily, and beside it the case of grease, and she opened the grease case and dragged her sleeve through the open tubs until her forearm was slick with vacuum lubricant, and then, cursing fluently and creatively for the drone's benefit, she smeared a generous film of it over canister thirteen's shell, working it into the seams, the valve threads, the serial recess. Grease wasn't coolant, but to a density scanner at two meters' range, contamination was contamination.

"There," she announced. "Cracked seal, my ass—it's the whole fitting, look at it. This is why I fly with spares." She rotated the canister slowly, letting the sensors drink in the fresh smear, the story of a careless mechanic and a failed gasket. "Scan away."

The secondary sweep came back clean. The primary sweep, re-run for form's sake, came back clean. The drone withdrew to its holding pattern and emitted its verdict in that same flat voice, indifferent as weather:

"*Marta's Lament*, you are cleared to proceed. Note logged regarding equipment maintenance deficiency. Compliance with decon standards is required within thirty days."

"Noted," Isandro said. "Thank you."

She flew on toward Meridian's docking arm with her hands steady on the stick and her pulse only beginning to come down, and inside her jacket, against her sternum, the widow Adeyemi's husband laughed at some joke forty years dead, and kneaded his bread, and named his daughter, and did not know that he had very nearly been confiscated.

* * *

Meridian Station smelled like recycled air and money, which was to say, like nothing at all. Isandro hated the stations a little more every time, and understood them a little better too. Up here, in the climate-controlled bright, people had everything except weight. Their food had no seasons. Their days had no weather. Their memories—this was the part Isandro had learned to sell—were thin. Recorded experiences, licensed ones, curated ones, memories manufactured for consumption, sunsets optimized by committee. The stations consumed experience the way Perdida consumed water: desperately, endlessly, and never quite enough.

What Isandro carried was different, and the buyers knew it within seconds of playback. Real memory had grit in it. Real memory had grief in it. A station executive would pay triple rate for ten minutes of a Perdida farmer's recollection of actual rain—not the licensed simulation, the real thing, with the smell of wet dust and the ache in old joints that preceded the storm—because the real thing reminded them that they had bodies, that they had ancestors, that somewhere in the family line there had been people who lived on the ground and took their chances.

And on the other end of the run, the colonists paid in water credits and quiet desperation to have their lives stored somewhere safer than their own failing infrastructure. Somewhere the closures couldn't reach. Somewhere the records might survive whatever it was that had happened to the colonial archives twenty years ago—the great silent gap in Perdida's official history, the years that simply weren't there anymore, wiped so cleanly that most colonists had forgotten there had ever been anything to forget.

Isandro remembered. That was her whole vocation, if you wanted to dress it up: she was the ledger. Every closure, every departure, every death that official record declined to hold, she held instead, in her private archive, in formats of her own devising, encrypted and duplicated and hidden in places she'd never written down anywhere.

She made the delivery to the granddaughter—an apartment in the station's middle rings, a young woman with her grandmother's jaw and her own life entirely elsewhere, who received the canister with polite bewilderment and asked, "What am I supposed to do with this?"—and Isandro showed her how to play it, and stayed long enough to see the woman's face change at minute three, when the dead man started laughing, and then she left, because that part wasn't hers to witness.

Payment cleared. New commissions queued: a fisherman's fifty years on the shrinking lake, a teacher's recordings of the last school in the north settlements, a doctor who wanted her whole career preserved before her own memory went. The trade was thriving, in the way that things thrive in poor soil—thinly, stubbornly, against the odds.

On the return leg, waiting out the customs window in the long dark above the night side of Perdida, Isandro did what she always did. She opened her private archive and added the day's entries. Widow Adeyemi, delivered. Canister thirteen, nearly lost, saved by grease. Okafor's face at the gantry, going smaller. Her grandfather's log pages scrolling past in the dark of the climb.

And underneath it all, the entry she made every night whether there was news or not, the one-line ritual, typed into the archive like a heartbeat:

*Perdida, this date: still here. Population declining. Remembered by: one.*

She stared at that number for a long moment, floating in the dark cockpit with the planet's night side turning beneath her, the settlement lights scattered like embers across the curve.

One, she thought. The entire memory of a world, entrusted to one tired woman in a salvaged ship.

It should have frightened her more than it did. Mostly it just made her proud, in the way that weary things get proud—proud the way an old dog is proud, or a lighthouse keeper, tending a light that no ships had steered by in years, keeping it lit anyway, because the alternative was darkness and she had decided, a long time ago, that she was not going to be the one who let the dark win.

She closed the archive. She brought the *Lament* around and began the descent.

And that was when her salvaged receiver—the old hand-cranked unit, her grandfather's radio, riding in its bracket by the viewport where it always rode—crackled to life.

Not static. Not the routine chatter of the orbital lanes. Something else. Something rhythmic, structured, repeating, rising out of the noise floor like a shape surfacing from deep water—a signal, impossibly faint and impossibly clean, in a cadence Isandro did not recognize and could not stop listening to.

She checked the frequency twice. Then a third time, because the number made no sense.

It was coming from the direction of Relay Seven.
