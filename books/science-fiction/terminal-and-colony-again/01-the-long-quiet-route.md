## 1. The Long Quiet Route

The Vesperine hung in the customs queue above Meridian Station like a patient animal waiting to be let through a gate, and Juniper Marchetti was already bored of the wait before it began. That was the trick, she'd learned years ago: be bored first. Boredom was contagious. A bored smuggler read as a bored freighter pilot, and a bored freighter pilot was the most invisible thing in the corridor.

She sat in the cockpit with her boots up on the console's dead panel, watching the queue lights crawl. Ahead of her, a grain hauler out of the Calloway side was being scanned with the enthusiasm of a man being asked to work on his birthday. Behind her, nothing but vacuum and the long dark between Meridian and Halloway Ring. The war had been going for six years now, long enough that everyone on both sides had stopped calling it anything at all. It was just *the situation*, the way weather was weather.

"Vesperine, this is Meridian Orbital Customs," said a voice in her ear, flat as old paint. "You are cleared for approach lane four. Have your manifest ready."

"Copy, Customs," she said, and yawned into the pickup so he'd hear it. "Manifest's been ready since I left port. Unlike some people, I don't enjoy paperwork."

A pause. Then, almost human: "Nobody enjoys paperwork, Vesperine. Lane four. Don't make me come out there."

"Wouldn't dream of it."

She brought the freighter around in a lazy arc that used exactly as much fuel as necessary and not a liter more. The Vesperine was an old Meridian-class short-hauler, forty years of honest service baked into her hull, the kind of ship customs officers saw three hundred times a shift and never remembered once. Her transponder sang a clean song: *Vesperine, registry M-7741-Kilo, owner-operator J. Marchetti, licensed memory courier, bonded and boring.* Every word of it true except the name, and even the name was only half a lie — she'd been Juniper Marchetti so long now that she sometimes answered to it in her sleep.

That was the art of it. Not the forged chips or the spoofed seals, though those mattered. The art was becoming someone so unremarkable that reality itself stopped checking your references.

* * *

Lane four ended at a docking cradle on the station's shadow side, where a customs officer named — according to the little brass plate she'd memorized on her last four visits — Officer Duvall waited inside an airlock booth with the posture of a man who had died at his post some years ago and hadn't yet informed payroll.

"Courier Marchetti." He didn't look up from his terminal. "Cargo declaration."

"One sealed memory canister, class-two commercial, client-encrypted." She slid the manifest across the counter along with her courier's bond chit. "Origin Halloway Ring, destination Meridian surface relay. Contents: estate records, probate division. Somebody's grandmother's will, probably. They always make you carry those by hand, like the mail would steal them."

Duvall took the canister when she passed it through the transfer slot. It was the size of a thermos, matte grey, unremarkable, worth more than his annual salary if she sold it to the wrong person and worth exactly its shipping fee if she did her job right. He held it against the scanner bed and watched the readout paint itself across his screen.

Juniper watched him watch it. This was the part that never got easier, no matter how many times she ran it — the eleven seconds where her whole life sat on someone else's desk and all she could do was stand there looking mildly impatient.

The scanner chimed. Duvall glanced at the result, then at her, then back at the result, with the particular weariness of a man hoping the anomaly would resolve itself so he wouldn't have to fill out a form about it.

"Seal integrity reads ninety-one percent," he said.

"Ninety-one is within tolerance."

"Tolerance is ninety-five."

"It's *ninety-five* for new seals." She tapped the manifest. "Class-two commercial, Duvall. Half these canisters have been recirculated since before the blockade went up. You want me to flag every one under ninety-five, you'll be filling out forms until the war ends, and we both know how that's going."

He looked at her. She looked back with the expression of a woman who had made this exact argument four times this month and won every time, because it was true, and truth delivered with sufficient boredom was indistinguishable from authority.

Duvall stamped the manifest. "Ninety-one percent. Logged and waived." He passed the canister back through the slot. "You're cleared for surface relay, Marchetti."

"Pleasure, as always." She pocketed the canister, warm from the scanner bed, and was two steps gone before he'd finished stamping the second copy.

In the corridor outside, she allowed herself one breath — slow, controlled, the kind her brother Tomas used to call her *reset breath*, back when there was a Tomas to call things anything. Eleven seconds. She'd done it in nine today. Getting sloppy.

No. Getting good. There was a difference, and the difference was that sloppy people hurried.

* * *

The rules came to her the way liturgy comes to the devout: without effort, in order, complete.

**Never open the cargo.** A memory canister was a locked room with someone else's life inside it, and the moment you looked, you became part of what happened next. She carried griefs, confessions, last wills, corporate secrets, love letters recorded in the trembling voice of dying women. None of it was hers. Curiosity wasn't a sin in her trade; it was a liability with a body count.

**Never take sides.** Fleet, Colony, syndicate, church, whatever passed for government out here — they were all just customers with different letterheads. The day she picked a team was the day she became a target instead of a fixture. Fixtures survived wars. Targets got names on manifests.

**Never return to Calloway Station.**

That third rule she kept differently than the others. The first two were professional discipline. The third was a scar she didn't touch, and she knew the difference, and she didn't care who knew it, because nobody knew it, which was rather the point.

She stowed the canister in the Vesperine's forward locker — the one bolted directly behind the cockpit bulkhead, the one she could reach from the pilot's seat in under two seconds — and began pre-flight while the station's dockhands, all three of whom existed mostly as rumors, pretended to inspect her coupling clamps.

Her ledger lived in her head and nowhere else, which was either paranoia or professionalism depending on who you asked, and she'd stopped asking. Forty-one contracts this year. All clean. All quiet. Memory courier work was the best job in a war nobody wanted to end: both sides needed their pasts moved around, and neither side wanted to know what was in the boxes.

"Marchetti." One of the dockhands, a woman with a welder's burn down one cheek, leaned into the cockpit doorway. "You hear about Aldrin-9?"

Juniper's hands kept moving through the checklist without her. "Scrapped years ago. Before my time on this route."

"Manifest surfaced last week. Some archive purge over on the Ring." The dockhand shrugged. "Whole crew list dumped into the public net. Old ships, old ghosts. Thought maybe you'd want a look — collectors are paying stupid money for pre-war fleet rosters."

"Why would I want that?"

"No reason." The woman grinned, showing a gold tooth. "Just figured a courier who deals in memories might like a piece of history."

"I deal in other people's memories. History's not paying my fuel bill." Juniper closed the locker. "Clear to detach?"

"Clear whenever. Quiet route tonight. No patrols till the 0400 rotation."

Of course there weren't. The corridor between Meridian and Halloway was the one stretch of space both sides had quietly agreed not to look at too hard, because both sides' officers had mistresses and debts and mothers on the other colony, and a silent corridor served everyone. Six years of war had produced exactly one lasting institution, and it was the traffic pattern Juniper flew twice a month.

She detached cleanly, fell away from the station without a ripple, and pointed the Vesperine's nose at the dark.

* * *

Four hours out, with the autopilot holding a course so straight it could have been drawn with a ruler, Juniper finally let herself think about the dockhand's words, the way you let yourself touch a bruise to check whether it still hurts.

*Aldrin-9.*

She knew the name the way you know a word you've heard in another room — present, meaningless, filed. Pre-war fleet auxiliary, decommissioned, scrapped. Everyone knew it. It was in the histories, the ones schoolchildren on both colonies memorized: *the auxiliary transport Aldrin-9, retired honorably two years before the war.*

And somewhere in a purged archive on Halloway Ring, a crew manifest had surfaced. Public net. Collectors bidding.

Tomas had loved ships like that. Had known their registries the way other boys knew ballplayers. If he were alive, he'd have—

She stopped the thought at the edge, the way she always did, and set it down gently on the far side of the line she'd drawn six years ago, and did not step over it.

Instead she pulled up her ledger — the real one, the mental one, the one that never touched a chip — and audited the year the way she did every run, contract by contract, origin by destination. It was less accounting than prayer. Everything in order. Everything accounted for. Forty-one clean runs, forty-one sealed rooms she'd never opened, forty-one clients who trusted her precisely because she was nobody, from nowhere, going nowhere that mattered.

Except.

There was one entry that lived at the bottom of the ledger, older than the rest, carried forward year after year like a balance she couldn't close. A canister she'd taken on commission eight years ago — before the war, before the blockade, before everything — from a client whose face she'd never seen and whose payment had cleared through three shells so deep the money had come to her laundered into respectability. Class-two commercial, the manifest had said. Estate records.

She'd carried it ever since. Through the war's first year, when the corridors burned. Through the year the syndicates carved up the routes. Through every customs scan, every inspection, every bored officer's scanner bed. Ninety-one percent seal integrity, logged and waived, again and again and again.

It rode in the forward locker right now, behind the cockpit bulkhead, two seconds from her hand.

Eight years was a long time to carry something for a client who never asked after it. Clients checked in. Clients sent routing updates, dead drops, the occasional nervous message. This one had simply paid the storage retainer, every quarter, like clockwork, like rent on a grave.

Juniper sat in the dark cockpit with the stars crawling past at a pace too slow to see and told herself the story she always told: *it's just business. Some probate lawyer's nest egg. Some family that forgot it exists.*

The story fit. It had always fit.

But tonight, for the first time in eight years, she noticed that the story had a seam in it — a place where the edges didn't quite meet — and once you'd seen a seam, you couldn't unsee it, and once you couldn't unsee it, you started wondering what was inside.

She caught herself reaching for the locker release and put her hand back on the armrest, deliberately, like setting down a glass she shouldn't finish.

Rule one. Never open the cargo.

"Tomorrow," she said aloud to the empty cockpit, and hated how much the word sounded like a promise.

Halloway Ring was fourteen hours out. She'd dock there, deliver the estate canister to its surface relay, pick up her return contract, and be gone before anyone remembered she'd come. In and out. Invisible. That was the plan, and her plans held; they always held; holding plans was the one thing in the universe she was unquestionably good at.

She slept badly in the pilot's chair, which she never did, and woke with the Ring's docking lights strobing against the canopy, and told herself the unease was just the bad sleep, just the coffee, just the long quiet route doing what it always did to a woman alone with her thoughts.

At 0600 station time, the Vesperine slid into her berth on Halloway Ring, and Juniper Marchetti — flawless identity, clean ledger, nine-second reset breath — stepped out onto the dock to audit her accounts.

The first thing she pulled was the public archive dump. Not because of the dockhand. Because of the seam. Because eight years of retainer payments deserved a routine explanation, and routine explanations were her religion, and she intended to say her prayers and move on.

She found the Aldrin-9 manifest in under a minute. Decommissioned auxiliary, crew of twelve, final listed voyage a ferry run that the fleet's own records showed was never flown. Scrapped before the war. Ghost paper. Collectors' trivia.

She scrolled the crew list the way you scroll anything — fast, indifferent, already halfway to closing the file.

And then her own name stopped scrolling for her, because there, fourth from the top, in a typeface that had been public property for a week, in a voyage that never happened, on a ship that was scrap before the fighting started:

*MARCHETTI, T. — flight systems.*

Tomas. Her brother. Listed as crew on a ship that never flew, dead six years, carried on a manifest that had surfaced the same week her anonymous client's retainer hit her account for the sixty-fourth consecutive time.

Juniper stood very still in the archive alcove while the station noise went on around her — announcements, footsteps, a child crying two decks up — and felt the cold arrive, not all at once but in layers, the way cold arrives in deep water.

Her sealed past had a crack in it.

Something was leaking through.
