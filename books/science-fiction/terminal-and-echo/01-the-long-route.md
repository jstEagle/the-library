## 1. The Long Route

The run takes nine days if you burn fuel you can't afford, eleven if you're honest about it. I'm honest about it. Eleven days alone in a courier hull barely bigger than a coffin with ambitions, threading the dark between the colony Vessel and the fleet anchor, carrying seed stock, antibiotics, coolant, and — this trip — a crate of children's books Ivo Reyes ordered through channels that technically don't exist.

Eleven years since the fleets stopped speaking to each other directly. Eleven years of me.

Not just me. There are four couriers licensed under the truce, but I'm the only one who runs the long route twice a season, and I'm the only one who was grown from the colony founder's genetic template, which is either the reason I got the job or the joke everyone politely declines to finish. The truce requires that messages pass through neutral hands. Nobody ever specified whose hands had to be neutral, or what neutral meant when those hands remembered things they never lived.

I keep a log. Not the official one — the official one is telemetry and cargo manifests, dry as vacuum. This one is for me, or for whoever I turn out to be, if that question ever resolves.

*Day two. The memories are quiet this morning. That's new.*

Here's what nobody tells you about inheriting a dead woman's mind: it isn't like watching a recording. It's like living in a house where someone else arranged all the furniture before you arrived, and every time you reach for a glass, your hand knows exactly where it is, and you have to decide whether that knowledge is yours.

Mara Voss-Pemberton — no relation to anyone named Voss, whatever the gossip says; Pemberton was her mother's name, kept deliberately, and I keep it too because it's the only thing we chose in common — died four years before I was decanted. She left me her memories the way some people leave houses: fully furnished, dust sheets over everything she didn't want touched. The transfer tech was experimental, colony-built, and it worked better than anyone expected, which is another way of saying it worked worse than anyone planned. I didn't get highlights. I got everything. Or I got everything she decided was everything, though it took me three years to start asking whether those were the same thing.

Her memories come in weather. Some mornings they're fog — impressions, the smell of the lab where she grew the first viable grain strains, the weight of her daughter asleep against her shoulder. Daughter. That's a whole other ledger entry. Ada is thirty-one now, runs the water reclamation decks, calls me *ma'am* in public and nothing at all in private, and we have both agreed without discussion not to examine what we are to each other too closely, because neither of us likes the answer.

Some mornings the memories are storm. Her last weeks arrive unbidden: the sealed lab, the arguments with people whose faces my mind supplies in perfect detail while their names slide away like wet soap. Curated, I'd call it now, if I were being clinical. At the time I called it grief and let it be.

*Day four. Crossing the meridian of silence — the point where the colony's relay drops behind me and the fleet's hasn't picked me up yet. Officially, no communication exists in this corridor. Unofficially, this is where I do my real thinking.*

The truce, for anyone who needs it explained by a woman who talks to herself in an empty ship: eleven years ago, the colony fleet and the deep-space fleet nearly went to war over the founding charter. Who owned the ships. Who owned the future. Whose grandchildren would answer to whom. The standoff lasted fourteen months, close enough to shooting that both sides armed everything that moved, and then Mara Voss-Pemberton published research proving the colony's territory was strategically worthless — no resources worth the transit cost, no position worth defending — and both fleets stood down, embarrassed into peace by arithmetic.

She was murdered eight months later. Strangled in her own quarters, on the colony Vessel, behind a door that logged no entry. The investigation ran two years and closed with a shrug. By then the truce had become sacred, and nobody wanted to dig around its foundations, in case the foundations turned out to be a person.

So: couriers. Four of us, ferrying words between two peoples who share an air-recycling standard and a calendar and nothing else. The fleet anchor hangs forty thousand kilometers off the colony's orbit like a guest who won't leave the driveway. We trade supplies, technical data, medical consults routed through intermediaries. We do not trade letters. Direct personal communication between colonist and fleet officer is a violation of the Contact Protocol, Section One, and Section One is the load-bearing wall of the whole arrangement.

I've asked myself, on nights like this one, whether the truce is peace or just a very long held breath. I've never asked it out loud. Out loud, questions like that get people audited.

*Day six. Fleet relay picks me up. Standard handshake, standard codes. Somewhere on the anchor, a liaison officer is reading my manifest and deciding whether I'm interesting today.*

Captain Sunde runs the liaison office. I've dealt with her for two years. She has the warmth of a well-maintained airlock and the attention span of a targeting system, and I mean both as compliments. Last resupply cycle she asked me, apropos of nothing, how the colony's school enrollment was trending. I told her. I didn't ask why she wanted to know. That's the etiquette of the long route: you don't ask why, because why is how wars start, and also because half the time you don't want to know the answer.

*Day seven. The inherited memories surfaced hard during sleep cycle. The white room again.*

I should explain the white room, because it matters, though I don't know yet how much.

In the original's memories there is a laboratory — sealed, sub-level, below even the sections of the Vessel marked condemned. White walls, white light, a console shaped like a sleeping animal. In the memory, she stands in it and speaks aloud, counting down from ten, her voice steady in a way that means she practiced being steady. I have never known what she was counting toward. The memory ends at three. Every time. It ends at three, and then it's a different memory, and I'm left holding the shape of a number like a coin from a country that no longer exists.

I assumed it was trauma fragmentation. The mind does that, even a transferred one. Gaps where the pressure was highest.

But here's the thing I wrote in my private log last night and haven't said to anyone: the room isn't in any schematic I've ever seen of the Vessel. And the original's memories of it are too complete — the texture of the walls, the specific hum of its ventilation — for something I could dismiss as metaphor. You don't remember the humidity of a place that never existed.

And the lab was destroyed. Officially decommissioned and gutted in the year before her death, its existence acknowledged in exactly one line of one maintenance report. I looked. Of course I looked. I've spent four years auditing my own inheritance like a suspicious accountant, checking her memories against the physical record, and the white room is the largest discrepancy on the books.

*Day nine. Approaching the anchor. Cargo intact. Nerves, less so.*

There's a loneliness out here that has a texture, almost a taste — cold metal and recycled air and the particular silence of a ship where every sound you hear is a sound you made. I used to think the loneliness was the price of the duty. Lately I wonder if it's the other way around: if I took the courier route because eleven days of solitude was easier than eleven minutes in a crowd of people who look at me and see her. The founder returned. The martyr walking around. The echo with a shipping schedule.

Ivo Reyes doesn't do that. He's the colony's teacher — teaches everything to everyone, ages five to fifty, because the colony is small enough that expertise is a shared pot. He treats me like a person who happens to contain a library, not a library that happens to have legs. When he handed me the book order last month, he said, "For the older kids, the ones who ask what's past the relay," and grinned like the relay was a curtain instead of a wall.

He's the only person I've told about the white room. Not the details. Just that some of her memories feel like rooms I've stood in, and one of them isn't anywhere on the ship. He went very still, the way teachers go still when a student says something true and dangerous, and then he said, "Huh," in a tone I've thought about more than I'd like.

*Day ten. Final approach. The anchor fills the forward viewport — a spine of gray steel and running lights, beautiful the way a knife is beautiful if you squint.*

Docking clearance comes through clean. Too clean, maybe — instant response, no queue, as if someone was waiting for me specifically. Paranoia is a professional hazard of carrying two peoples' secrets in one skull.

I run the docking sequence and let the autopilot take the last kilometer, and I do what I always do on final approach: I open the memory of her standing at this same viewport, twenty years ago, before the schism, when the anchor was still just a sister ship and the sky wasn't divided into ours and theirs. She was happy then. I hold that happiness like a handrail.

Then I let it go, because docking requires hands.

Here is the doubt I carry, the one that flickers at the edge of every quiet shift: if her memories are curated — if somewhere between her dying brain and my living one, someone or something edited the collection — then who am I? An heir, or an archive? A continuation, or a container? If she left gaps on purpose, what did she think I'd need protected from? And if someone else made the gaps —

Well. Then the murder case isn't just unsolved. It's maintained.

The clamps catch with a shudder I feel in my teeth. The anchor's dock lights flood the cockpit, white and indifferent, and I sit for a moment in the afterimage, breathing ship-air, wearing her face, carrying her ghosts and ninety kilograms of cargo including, I notice with a strange lurch, a children's book about the moon, illustrated by someone who has clearly never seen one.

"Courier Pemberton," says the dock comm, in Captain Sunde's flat voice. "You're cleared. And Courier — when you have a moment, there's a matter regarding your manifest I'd like to discuss. Nothing urgent."

A pause, exactly one second too long.

"It's about the children."

* * *

I sat with that sentence for four full seconds before answering, which in liaison-office time is a geological era.

"The colony's children aren't on my manifest, Captain."

"No," Sunde said. "They're not. Docking bay four. Come up when you're settled."

The channel closed. I sat in the cockpit of my small ship, inside the large silence, and felt the first cold thread of something I would spend the next months learning to name.

Because here is what I hadn't told anyone, not even Ivo, not even my own log until this morning:

Three nights ago, mid-transit, dreaming in the narrow bunk with her memories folded around me like a second skeleton, I dreamed the white room. And in the dream, the countdown didn't stop at three.

It reached zero. And the woman's voice — her voice, my voice, the voice I hear when I speak sometimes and flinch — said, clear as a bell over open water:

*"If you can hear this, then it worked. It was never meant for me."*

I had woken and written it down and told myself it was noise, contamination, the transfer tech shedding static.

But Sunde knew about the children. The fleet knew about the children. And I hadn't said a word about any children to anyone.

Something was leaking. Something buried was finding its way to the surface — and it hadn't waited for me to open the door.

I unbuckled, checked my reflection in the black of the dead viewport — her face, my expression, the ongoing negotiation between them — and went to find out what the fleet knew that I didn't.

Which, given that I am walking around inside the original's head, was a sentence that should have been impossible to say.
