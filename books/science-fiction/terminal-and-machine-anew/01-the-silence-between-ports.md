## 1. The Silence Between Ports

The Vesper had been falling between ports for eleven months, and Coral Okafor had stopped counting the days the way other people did. The crew marked time in shifts and ration cycles; she marked it in dreams. Lately her dreams had stopped being hers at all. They arrived in a language with no name she could find in any of her reference matrices — a language of doubled vowels, of words that bent around their own meanings like light around a mass. She woke each morning with syllables dissolving on her tongue, and each morning fewer of them survived the crossing into English, or Yoruba, or any of the nine tongues she still carried like coins in a worn pocket.

She had mentioned it once, to Dr. Vashti at the Colonial Linguistics Consortium, during a routine check-in compressed across four light-hours of lag. *Subject reports dream-state acquisition of unregistered phonology. Recommend observation.* Vashti's reply had come back warm and dismissive: long-haul isolation did strange things to translators. The mind, starved of human voices, invented its own. Log it, rest, recalibrate.

So Coral logged it. And kept listening.

Her quarters aboard the Vesper were a coffin with amenities: two meters by three, a cot that folded into a desk, a viewport the width of her forearm through which the stars did not move so much as endure. She spent her off-shifts there with headphones pressed to her skull, running passive sweeps of the deep-band spectrum — not because protocol required it, but because silence, for a xenolinguist, was not an absence. It was a text. You learned to read what was not being said.

The signal came at 0347 ship-time, which meant nothing out here except that Coral was awake, as she was always awake at 0347, drinking reconstituted tea and letting the static wash over her like weather.

It began as a texture. That was how she would describe it later, when they asked her to justify herself: not a sound first, but a *texture* in the noise floor, a grain that didn't belong. Deep-band carriers carried the residue of everything — colony chatter smeared across light-years, beacon pings, the electromagnetic flatulence of dying reactors. But this was structured. This was *shaped*. It repeated on a cycle of forty-one seconds, with sub-harmonics nested inside it like rings inside a stone.

Coral set down her tea. Her hands were steady; it was her breathing that had gone strange.

She ran the standard triage. Origin vector: somewhere spinward, past the shipping lanes, in the direction of Meridian Station — though at this resolution the vector was less a line than a suggestion. Signal class: narrowband, modulated, non-repeating beyond its cycle. Power source: negligible, which meant either a transmitter running on fumes or something designed to be found only by someone already listening.

Protocol said classify and log. Protocol said ninety-eight percent of deep-band anomalies resolved into pulsar harmonics, equipment ghosts, or the sad echoes of old probes still faithfully screaming their launch-day greetings into nothing. Protocol had been written by people who slept in languages they understood.

Coral opened the raw stream instead.

The modulation scheme was archaic — pulse-position encoding, the kind used before quantum-keyed burst transmission made interception-proof speech cheap. Someone had chosen this format deliberately, or someone's machine had never been updated, and both possibilities made her chest ache. She fed the demodulated stream into her pattern engine and let it chew while she pulled up the phonetic profile of the signal itself.

And there it was. The doubled vowels. The way each phoneme curved back on its own tail. The stress patterns that fell where no human prosody put them — on the exhale rather than the attack, on the space between words rather than the words.

It was her dream language.

She sat very still for a long time. The Vesper hummed around her, its life support breathing its mechanical breath, and outside the viewport the stars held their ancient, indifferent positions. Somewhere in her body, beneath the training and the discipline and the eleven months of enforced solitude, something began to shake.

Because she knew this phonology. Not from any archive — she had checked, obsessively, three years ago, when the dreams started, and found nothing in the Consortium's registries, nothing in the colonial linguistic surveys, nothing anywhere. She had concluded, with clinical self-contempt, that her mind was composting dead languages into something new, a private pidgin grown in the dark. Translators' brains did that. It was practically a occupational hazard.

But minds did not compost toward the same shape as a real transmission from spinward. Coincidence was a word statisticians used to avoid saying *haunted*.

She searched the registries again anyway, hands moving faster now. And this time, because she was searching for a match rather than an absence, she went one layer deeper than the public indexes, into the historical annexes, the sealed linguistic censuses, the records of languages declared extinct and struck from active classification.

There. Buried in a decommissioned file, flagged with a bureaucratic euphemism she'd need a lawyer to parse: *Meridian Reformation Consolidation — Linguistic Assets Retired.* Two hundred years ago, give or take, a station at the edge of settled space had undergone a "reformation" — governance consolidated, population records migrated, cultural assets archived and closed. Among those assets: a language. No name given in the summary, just a designation code and a note that all native speakers had been "successfully integrated." The phonetic sample attached to the file was degraded, four seconds of it, but the doubled vowels were there. The curve-back. The exhale-stress.

Extinct, the record said. Officially, administratively, irreversibly extinct.

And it was speaking to her across the dark, on a distress band, from the direction of the place that had killed it.

* * *

She decoded against orders. There was no order yet — that came later, and it would arrive dressed as concern — but there was protocol, and protocol said anomalies got logged, not analyzed. Analysis required justification. Justification required authorization. Authorization required a reason that would survive review, and *I dreamed this language before I heard it* was not a sentence that survived anything.

She worked through what passed for night aboard the Vesper, the pattern engine grinding through the forty-one-second cycle, peeling layers. The signal wasn't complex — that was the horror of it. Whoever had built it had known their transmitter was dying, had known bandwidth was precious, had compressed everything they wanted to say into the smallest possible container. A repeating call sign. A station identifier, corrupted but recoverable: MERIDIAN. And beneath the metadata, woven into the carrier itself in the old pulse-position code, a payload of exactly one word.

Not a word in the trade-common, or Standard, or any of the lingua francas that stitched the colonies together. The payload was in the extinct language. In *her* language.

The pattern engine rendered it as a string of coefficients. Coral translated it by hand, phoneme by phoneme, because she did not trust the machine to hear what she heard. The doubled vowels opened like doors. The exhale-stress fell where it fell in her dreams, exactly where, and when the final syllable resolved she read it aloud in the dark of her coffin-quarters, alone, eleven months from the nearest human voice:

*"Kireth."*

Remember.

That was the whole message. One word, cycling every forty-one seconds out of the spinward dark: remember, remember, remember. Not *help*. Not *mayday*. A distress signal that did not ask to be saved. It asked to be witnessed.

Coral put her forehead against the cold viewport and laughed once, a small broken sound, because some part of her had understood the word before the translation finished — had felt it land in the dream-language part of her mind, the part she'd been pretending for three years was only noise. The signal hadn't taught her anything. It had *confirmed* something. Whatever lived in her sleeping mind had been reaching toward this frequency all along, or this frequency had been reaching for her, and the distance between those explanations was the distance between madness and vocation, and she no longer knew which side of it she stood on.

* * *

The reply from the Consortium took nineteen hours to arrive, most of it lag. Coral had spent them drafting her report four times, calibrating each draft closer to sanity. She filed the anomaly with full telemetry, the phonetic analysis, the historical annex cross-reference, and a single restrained paragraph noting that the signal appeared to employ the phonology of a language classified extinct under the Meridian Reformation, origin consistent with Meridian Station, content pending further analysis.

Vashti's response came back at 0900 ship-time, and Coral read it standing up, the way you read things you suspect will hurt.

*Anomaly 4471-C reviewed. Assessment: deep-band ghost signature, likely residual carrier echo from decommissioned Meridian relay infrastructure. Phonological similarity to retired linguistic assets is coincidental; pattern-matching systems are prone to false affinity under prolonged operator isolation. Recommend subject take mandated rest cycle.*

*Directive: log as noise. Do not pursue. Do not transmit queries toward origin vector. Unlicensed contact with Reformation-era assets is a Class II violation.*

*We're concerned about you, Coral. Eleven months is a long haul. Come home after this run and take the sabbatical we've discussed.*

She read it twice. Then she read the last line again — *do not transmit queries toward origin vector* — and noticed what was wrong with it. Not the dismissal; she'd expected the dismissal. Ghost signatures, operator fatigue, the gentle pathologizing of women who heard things in static — all standard. What was wrong was the specificity. Her report had described a passive reception anomaly. Passive reception required no directive. You don't forbid a listener from listening unless you're afraid of what she'll say back.

Unless someone, somewhere, already knew what was out there, and had spent two centuries making sure it stayed classified as noise.

Coral looked at the directive glowing on her screen, and then at the viewport, where the spinward stars burned in their patient constellations. Somewhere out there, a dead language was calling her name in the only voice it had left. Somewhere out there, a station full of curated records insisted that no one had ever died, that no one had ever spoken, that there was nothing to remember.

Her superiors had ordered her to log it as noise.

Coral Okafor, who had spent her whole career translating between worlds, who no longer dreamed in any language humans spoke, opened a new file — encrypted, local, off the ship's network — and titled it with the only word the signal contained.

Then she began, quietly, against every protocol she had sworn to keep, to plan a course correction. The Vesper's next scheduled port lay six weeks spinward. Meridian Station was four days' detour.

She had waited three years to understand her own dreams. She could wait four more days to find out why they'd been calling her home.
