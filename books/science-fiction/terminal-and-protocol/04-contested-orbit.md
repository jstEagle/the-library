## 4. Contested Orbit

The patrol ship came out of the shadow of Vell's second moon like a knife drawn slowly from a sheath—no alarm, no challenge, just the sudden geometry of it filling Beatrix's forward display, hull grey as old bone, running lights strobing the boarding pattern she'd seen a hundred times from a hundred safe distances.

*Freighter Kestrel's Mercy, registry Calloway-9, this is Patrol Cutter Anvil. Prepare for inspection. Kill your drive and stand by.*

Beatrix's hands were already moving before her mind caught up. That was the trick of seven years of careful living: the body learned obedience faster than the mind learned fear. She killed the drive. She dimmed the cabin lights to inspection standard. She wiped the tea cup Jun had left on the console and set it in the cleaner, because inspectors noticed clutter, and clutter invited questions, and questions were how people like her died—not with a bang but with a manifest discrepancy.

The decode was still open on her secondary screen.

She had fallen asleep over it three hours ago, cheek against the cold frame, the Vessic glyphs scrolling in amber behind her eyelids when she closed them. *Little wren. Little wren, if you are hearing this.* The header repeated it like a bell struck over and over, and every repetition landed somewhere beneath her ribs where she kept the things she did not think about.

Now an armed cutter was sliding alongside her ship, and the decode was sitting on her screen like a confession.

She had perhaps four minutes before their boarding party crossed the gap on tethers or a shuttle. Four minutes to make forty terabytes of forbidden linguistics look like ballast telemetry.

Her fingers moved. Not fast—fast was suspicious; fast was a woman with something to hide. Her fingers moved at the speed of boredom. She opened the ballast mass ledger, the one that tracked water and slag trim across her tanks, and began folding the decode into it, glyph by glyph, rekeying each Vessic character as a fractional density reading. A language became arithmetic. The nickname that no living person should know became a decimal drift in tank seven, the kind of rounding error every freighter carried and no inspector ever bothered to chase. She buried the corpus under eleven layers of mundane redundancy, salted it with genuine sensor noise she pulled from the last six months of logs, and let the whole thing settle into the ship's data like sediment into riverbed.

When she finished, her heart was beating exactly the way it beat during cargo transfers. That frightened her more than the patrol ship did.

* * *

They boarded through the dorsal lock, four of them in matte-grey armor with the insignia of Meridian Consolidated—the corporate colors, not the fleet's, which told her this wasn't routine law enforcement. This was a company sniffing for something specific. The officer who removed his helmet had a face like a folded map, all creases and no expression, and eyes that went where he wanted them to go rather than where courtesy suggested.

"Captain Thackery." He pronounced it carefully wrong, testing her. "Voss. Meridian Compliance."

"Captain Dunmore," she said, before she could stop herself, and then made the correction sound like his error, not hers. "Registry says Thackery. I answer to both. Paperwork's a mess since the last transfer of ownership." She smiled the small, tired smile of a woman who hated paperwork, and watched him decide whether to believe her.

He believed her. Or he filed her under *boring*, which amounted to the same thing.

His team fanned out through the freighter with the practiced indifference of men who had done this a thousand times. One took the engine room. One took the crew quarters—Jun's bunk, Jun's half-finished model glider hanging from the ceiling by fishing line, Jun's collection of cheap romance novels in three scripts. Beatrix felt a spike of protectiveness so sharp it surprised her. Jun had signed onto a ghost ship with a ghost captain, and he didn't even know what he was protecting.

"You've been in contested space nine days," Voss said, walking her aft at a pace designed to feel casual. "Long route for a short haul."

"Fuel prices." She shrugged. "You wait for the cheap windows or you don't eat. You know how it is."

"I know how it is," he agreed pleasantly. "We're tracking an unregistered transmission. Origin somewhere in this corridor. Narrowband, deep-codec, old encryption architecture. Ring any bells?"

"No bells." She said it lightly, and then, because liars who say too little are more suspicious than liars who say too much, she added: "Half the wrecks out here broadcast garbage on dead frequencies. Decay noise, reactor ghosts. If you're chasing signals, you'll be chasing echoes till you retire."

Something flickered behind his creased face. She had said *reactor ghosts* without thinking, and only after the words left her mouth did she understand what she'd done—she'd named the thing she was hiding, casually, accurately, wrapped in the tone of a tired hauler who'd heard it all. It was the oldest trick in interrogation: volunteer the truth in disguise so the lie has somewhere solid to stand.

And it had come to her effortlessly. That was the part that unsettled her, walking beside him through her own corridor, past her own patched bulkheads. Lying used to be agony. At the academy, at the tribunal, standing in front of twelve scholars who called her delusional, she had been incapable of saying anything but what she believed, and it had ruined her. Seven years later, the words came smooth as water finding level.

Except—

Except that wasn't quite true either. Because somewhere between *registry says Thackery* and *reactor ghosts*, her mind had reached for Vessic. Not the words—the shape of it. The grammar of concealment lived in that language like a spine inside a body. Vessic had no word for *lie*. It had *vesh-kari*, the telling of a second truth, and its entire structure was built for it: verbs that meant different things depending on which side of the sentence you stood on, negations that affirmed, questions that answered themselves. A language built by a people who had survived centuries of occupation by saying one thing to their rulers and another to each other, until the double meaning wore grooves deep enough to hold a whole civilization.

She hadn't spoken it aloud in seven years. But it turned out she'd been thinking in its architecture the whole time, and now, walking this grey officer through her ship, she could feel it rise in her like water into a dry channel—as if the language itself had decided to conspire, had been waiting all these years for a use worthy of it.

*Vesh-kari,* she thought. *The second truth.* I am a boring hauler. I am also a daughter receiving mail from the dead. Both statements are true. Only one of them keeps me alive.

"Your co-pilot," Voss said. "Jun Okafor. He's been with you two years?"

"Three." Another effortless correction, delivered with the confidence of ownership. "He came aboard at Tarnholt station. Why?"

"Routine." He stopped at the cargo hold and ran his scanner along the first container. Water reclamation parts, declared and dull. "Your long-range array. Modified?"

"Standard refit. The array's older than I am. Half the time I get static and prayers."

"Prayers?"

"Decay harmonics." She waved a hand. "Old relay bands. Sounds almost like voices if you listen long enough. Ask any hauler—we all hear ghosts out here." She laughed, short and dry, and the laugh was the best lie of all, because it cost her something real to make it.

* * *

They spent ninety minutes aboard. Ninety minutes in which Beatrix performed the role of Beatrix Dunmore, hauler, so completely that she began to lose track of which woman was the costume. She showed Voss her manifests with the weary patience of the perpetually audited. She let them scan her ballast tanks, and the decode sat inside the telemetry like a stone at the bottom of a well, invisible because it had become the well itself. She offered them water, and they declined, and she noted with distant amusement that refusing hospitality was itself a kind of tell, and that she had catalogued it automatically, the way she once catalogued verb classes.

At the airlock, Voss paused.

"You were academy-trained once," he said. "Linguistics. There's a flag on your biometric file. Expelled."

The corridor went very quiet. Somewhere aft, Jun's footsteps stopped.

"Seven years ago," Beatrix said. "You've got a good memory, Officer Voss."

"We track people who claim they heard transmissions in their sleep." His voice stayed pleasant, but the pleasantness had edges now. "Delusional, the tribunal said. But here you are, hauling through contested space, and here we are, hunting a signal nobody should be able to send." He let the silence stretch. "Funny coincidence."

"Funny coincidence," she agreed. "Though if my dreams were transmissions, Officer, don't you think someone would have sent me something worth hearing?" And she smiled, and the smile was vesh-kari, the second truth wearing the first one's clothes, and she watched him accept it because the alternative was admitting the company had murdered a linguistics department's credibility seven years ago and he preferred not to know.

"Safe routes, Captain Thackery-Dunmore." He dropped his helmet back on. "We'll be in the corridor a while."

"I know," she said. "I'll be elsewhere."

* * *

The cutter detached and fell away toward the moon's shadow, and Beatrix stood at the viewport watching it shrink until it was a scar of light, then nothing.

Her hands started shaking only afterward, which was correct protocol for adrenaline, and she let them, alone in the corridor, because there was no one to perform steadiness for anymore. Except there was. Jun came around the corner holding two cups of tea, and he looked at her with those patient, watchful eyes that missed less than she pretended.

"They gone?"

"Gone."

"You lied to them." Not an accusation. An observation, almost wondering. "Fluently. I've never heard you lie before. I didn't know you could."

Beatrix took the tea. It was too hot and too sweet, the way he always made it, and she held it against her sternum like a compress.

"I couldn't," she said. "Once."

"And now?"

She looked at him—at the man who had covered for her for three years without asking why, who had logged her strange hours and stranger silences and said nothing—and felt the full weight of what her double life had become. It fit her. That was the shame of it, the thrill of it, the thing she would not say aloud: the lying fit her better than the truth ever had. Seven years of playing a dull woman in a dull ship, and it had taken a patrol cutter and a dead language to show her how naturally the mask sat on her face. Vessic had risen in her like a mother tongue. Like inheritance.

Like a voice waiting to be spent.

"Now," she said, "it comes easy. That's the problem, Jun. Things that come easy—" She stopped. Drank the terrible tea. "Get the array warmed up. Full power. We're going to need it soon."

"Warm it up for what?"

She looked at the viewport, at the dark where the cutter had vanished, where somewhere a signal was counting down toward silence, calling her *little wren* in a language only the dead remembered.

"For answering," she said.
