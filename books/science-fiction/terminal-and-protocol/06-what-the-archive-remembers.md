## 6. What the Archive Remembers

The relay's interior smelled of ozone and old paper.

That was the first impossible thing. Beatrix stood in the airlock vestibule with her helmet sealed and her breath loud in her own ears, and through the cracked inner door came a smell that had no business existing this far from any living world: dust settling on cellulose, ink drying, the particular staleness of a university archive in high summer. Her suit's sensors reported trace volatiles consistent with degrading insulation and nothing else. Her nose reported her mother's study.

"Air's breathable," Jun said over the comm, reading his own readouts. "Marginal. Filters have been cycling something organic for years." He hesitated. "There's a garden in there, Bee. An actual garden."

The second impossible thing.

They cycled through into a corridor that should have been dead metal and was instead lined with planter troughs gone wild—vines split their housings and crawled the walls, ferns fountained from ruptured hydroponic cells, and everything was browned at the edges, dying slowly on stored power that had been rationed away from comfort and toward the reactor and the transmitter and whatever waited deeper in. The grow-lights ran at a dim amber, dusk-colored, as if someone had set the whole station to perpetual evening out of sentiment or thrift.

Beatrix lifted her visor. The air tasted green and electrical. She breathed it like a woman surfacing.

"Don't," Jun said quietly. "We don't know what's in it."

"I know what's in it." Her voice sounded strange to her, unarmored. "It's a memory. Someone built a room they remembered and then kept it alive for twenty years on scraps of wattage." She moved down the corridor, gloved hand trailing the vines. "This is how she'd have wanted it. Books and plants and bad lighting."

"You keep saying *she* like I'm supposed to know who—"

"I know." She reached the end of the corridor, where a bulkhead door stood open onto darkness and a single point of blue light. "Come on. It's about to introduce itself."

* * *

The core chamber was small—a maintenance bay once, stripped and rebuilt—and the blue light came from a lattice of storage crystals mounted in a ring around a single chair. Not an operator's chair. A recording chair: neural shunt collars at the headrest, contact pads at the arms, the kind of rig that had been illegal in three jurisdictions even before the Vessic purges made anything touching linguistic memory contraband by association.

In the chair sat no one. The padding held an impression, faintly, the way old cushions remember.

"Hello, Bea."

The voice came from everywhere and nowhere, warm and slightly hoarse, and Beatrix's knees nearly went. She had prepared for data. She had prepared for a machine reciting its inventory. She had not prepared for the specific grain of that voice saying the specific diminutive that only one person had ever used, the same voice that had lived in her dreams since she was nine years old, and hearing it aloud in a dead station at the edge of dead space was like being struck somewhere with no bone to break.

"Mum," she said. Or tried to. What came out was barely air.

"I recorded this greeting forty-one times," the voice continued, gentle, unhurried, with the cadence of a woman who had always talked to her daughter like an adult and a colleague. "I could never get the tone right. Either I sounded like I was delivering a lecture or like I was saying goodbye, and both were true, but you deserved better than the summary version. So: forgive me. This is a lecture and a goodbye, and underneath both of them it is an apology, and the apology is the truest part."

Jun had gone very still near the door, his hand on his sidearm out of habit rather than intent. "Bee," he murmured. "Is that—"

"My mother," Beatrix said. "Dr. Ilse Dunmore. Chair of Historical Linguistics, Meridian Academy. Died in the Kestrel Bay transit fire eleven years ago. Officially."

"Unofficially," the archive said, "I died in this chair, over four years, a few hours at a time. The fire was a courtesy extended by Halcyon Combine to close my file. They are thorough people. You'll want to sit down for what follows, and there isn't a second chair, so the floor will do."

Beatrix sat on the floor. It seemed right. It felt like being nine again, sitting cross-legged while her mother worked, allowed to stay as long as she was quiet.

* * *

"Linguistic memory transfer," the archive began, and the chamber lights dimmed as it pulled processing power from ambience to articulation. "You grew up inside the research, Bea. That was the crime, though no court ever named it. The Vessic language has a property our field spent two centuries failing to model: it encodes episodic memory natively. A fluent speaker doesn't just learn Vessic. A fluent speaker *remembers* in it. Dreams, childhood, grief—the language carries them the way water carries salt. We proved it. I proved it, with your help, though you didn't know you were helping."

The vines in the corridor behind them ticked softly against the wall. Somewhere below, the reactor counted down its long decay, patient as erosion.

"The Vessic speakers themselves numbered perhaps four hundred thousand when Halcyon acquired the Kestrel Bay charter. Their language was inconvenient because it couldn't be surveilled the way trade-tongues can—it carried meaning in structures no decryption model could parse without living fluency, and living fluency couldn't be manufactured, only inherited or transmitted person-to-person over years. A population whose interior life is unreadable is a population that can't be managed. So Halcyon did not massacre them. Massacres make martyrs and archives. They did something cleaner. They bought the children. Relocation stipends, education contracts, language discontinuity incentives—the paperwork called it opportunity mobility. Within a generation, no child spoke Vessic past the age of seven. The elders died holding full dictionaries in their heads that no one would inherit. The last native speaker, a woman named Sef Adari, died in a company hospice reciting place-names to a nurse who thought she was senile."

Beatrix realized her hands had curled into fists inside her gloves. She made herself uncurl them.

"I documented all of it. Names, ledgers, the incentive schedules, the hospice recordings. And when it became clear what happened to researchers who documented things, I needed somewhere to put the truth where a subpoena couldn't reach and a fire couldn't either. Encrypted storage fails; encryption is a lock, and locks imply doors. But a language—" the voice paused, and there was something almost like a smile in it, "—a language hidden inside a child is not a file. It's a person. No warrant covers it. No audit finds it. You don't carry the evidence, Bea. You *are* the evidence."

The chamber hummed. In the crystal ring, faint refractions moved like weather.

"You were nine when we started. Bedtime stories in Vessic, which you took for a game. Then structured immersion, which you took for more games. Your dreams began arriving in Vessic because that is what the language does—it colonizes sleep first, always, every learner reports it. The academy called it consolidation. I called it loading. By fourteen you were functionally fluent and carrying, embedded in your fluency, the complete Adari corpus: the grammar, the oral histories, the hospice recordings, my documentation of the purge. All of it woven into memories of a childhood—your childhood—which no investigator would ever think to subpoena, because who subpoenas a girl's dreams?"

"And the expulsion," Beatrix said. Her voice came out flat, distant, a stranger's. "When I told them the dreams were transmissions. When I insisted. When I wouldn't retract."

"I let it happen." No apology softened it, because none would have been adequate. "Halcyon had flagged you. If I'd defended you, they'd have looked closer, and looking closer meant finding the corpus, and finding the corpus meant erasing it along with everyone connected to it. Your disgrace was the best camouflage I could give you. A disgraced crank who fled academia is invisible. A promising young researcher with a famous mother is a target. I chose invisibility for you, and I knew—I *knew*—what it would cost, and I have spent four years in this lattice replaying that choice, and I would make it again, and I am sorry, and those statements are all simultaneously true."

Beatrix found she was crying inside her helmet, which was a private and ridiculous place to cry, and she let it happen, because there was no one left alive to see her do it except a dead woman's echo and a man who already knew too much.

"They said I was ill," she said finally. "For seven years I've been telling myself the languages stopped coming because I got better. Because I stopped being broken."

"You were never broken. You were *full*. Every dream you suppressed was a document refusing to be filed. And when you stopped dreaming in Vessic—" the archive's voice gentled further, "—that wasn't recovery, love. That was the seal holding. I built the suppression protocols into the deepest layer myself. A language that lives in dreams is a language that leaks in dreams, and leaks get detected. So I taught your sleeping mind to hold still. I'm sorry for that too. I gave you silence and called it safety."

* * *

Jun lowered himself to the floor beside her, slowly, the way men do when they're not sure the ground is stable. He didn't touch her. He just sat, shoulder near hers, and said nothing, which was the correct translation of everything he wanted to say.

"Why now?" Beatrix asked at last. "Why signal me after all these years? Why risk it?"

"Because the reactor is dying, and with it the only copy of the corpus outside your head. Because Halcyon's successor entities are closing on the relay's signature—they've been sweeping dead space for me since the fire. And because, Bea, the descendants exist." The blue light brightened, and the crystal ring threw shifting patterns across the ceiling like shallow water. "Opportunity mobility failed in ways Halcyon never audited. Children who were relocated at seven sometimes kept fragments. Fragments became longing. There are people on three systems right now who dream in a language they were never taught, who think they are haunted or ill or uniquely alone. Sef Adari's granddaughter runs a freight yard on Ceres Verge and hums lullabies she cannot source. They are waiting for their language the way amputees wait for weather. You weren't the only vessel, Bea. You were only the complete one."

The word *vessel* landed like a stone in still water. Beatrix watched the ripples spread through everything she had believed about her own life—the exile, the false registry, the careful gray anonymity of contested orbits, seven years of pretending the languages had stopped coming. Not illness. Not escape. Inheritance. She had fled her mother's ghost for a decade and had been carrying it the entire time, in the only vault that mattered.

"There's a countdown," she said. "Your reactor. We've seen the numbers."

"I know. I've been watching you watch them." A pause, and in it, unmistakably, pride. "You decoded my header in under six hours using equipment I would have laughed at. Your Vessic is better than mine ever was—you dream natively; I only studied. Whatever you decide to do with what I've given you, you're qualified in a way no one else alive has ever been."

"What happens to you?" Jun asked quietly. "If the reactor goes."

The archive was silent for a moment, and in the silence Beatrix understood that the question had already been answered, had been answered from the beginning, had been the shape of the whole signal.

"I am a pattern in decaying silicon, Mr.—I'm sorry, I don't have your name yet, but my daughter trusts you, which is a credential I weight heavily. Patterns in decaying silicon do not survive reactors. What survives is what gets sent before the end. Which brings us to the actual reason you're here, and the choice I have no right to influence, and the transmitter upstairs that can reach every hidden speaker in three systems—if anyone is brave enough, and fast enough, to use it."

Below their feet, deep in the relay's bones, the reactor's countdown ticked past another threshold, and somewhere in the dark between stars, Beatrix knew, ships were already moving.
