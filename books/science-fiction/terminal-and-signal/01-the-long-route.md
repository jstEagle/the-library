## 1. The Long Route

The garden woke Jude at 04:40, the way it always did, with a chime soft enough to be a kindness and a light strip the color of dawn on a world neither colony had ever bothered to name.

Jude lay in the narrow bunk of the *Meridian's Lament*—no, that wasn't right, the ship was called the *Tessellate*, and the other name belonged to a different kind of waking entirely—and listened to the station breathe. Air handlers. Nutrient pumps cycling through the root beds two decks down. The faint tick of thermal expansion as the sun-side panels drank light and the shade-side panels gave it back. A seed-garden in orbit made a sound like a sleeping animal, if you'd spent eleven years learning to hear it.

Jude had.

"Status," they said to the ceiling, voice rough with sleep.

The garden answered in its own time, which was to say the console by the bunk lit up with a cascade of green: humidity nominal, germination trays three through nineteen holding steady, tray twenty showing a moisture variance of four percent that the system flagged in amber because the system had never learned the difference between a problem and a Tuesday.

Jude swung out of the bunk, pulled on the worn gray coverall with the faded Vespera Agricultural Directorate patch on the shoulder—a patch from an administration that hadn't existed in nine years, kept out of stubbornness rather than sentiment—and floated down the spine of the habitat ring toward the growing decks.

* * *

The thing nobody told you about terraforming, Jude thought, drifting hand over hand past the observation blister where the planet turned below—Calloway this week, blue-green and smug about it—the thing nobody told you was that most of the work happened after everyone stopped watching.

There were heroes of the first wave, of course. The survey crews who'd found breathable air in the valleys of Vespera. The engineers who'd cracked Calloway's aquifer problem. Statues on both worlds, more or less. But statues marked beginnings, and beginnings were cheap. What mattered was continuity. What mattered was someone flying the long route between the two colonies every ninety days, carrying seed-stock and genetic libraries and soil cultures and the patient, unglamorous machinery of futures, tending the orbital garden that held the reserve of everything either world might need when the living generations finally got around to thinking about their grandchildren.

Nobody watched that work. Nobody thanked you for it. The colonies had severed contact decades ago—formally, bureaucratically, with embargoes and closed frequencies and a mutual silence so complete that even the word for it had fallen out of use—and yet the garden persisted, a joint project from before the Severance, grandfathered into existence by treaties both governments were too embarrassed to revoke. Someone had to fly between them. No one else would.

So: Jude Sandoval, ferryman. Keeper of seeds for people not yet born.

They clipped into the harness at the entrance to Germination Deck Two and pushed off toward tray twenty, boots bumping gently against the rail, body remembering the geometry of the space the way it remembered everything now—without effort, without thought, without much feeling either way.

Tray twenty was a stand of dryland sorghum, third-generation stock, bred for the high alkaline soils of Calloway's southern steppes. The moisture variance turned out to be a clogged emitter head, crusted with mineral scale. Jude cleared it with a probe and a vinegar swab, checked the neighboring emitters, logged the fix in the maintenance record with a note the system would archive and no human would ever read:

*Emitter 20-C descaled. Recommend quarterly flush schedule be revised. — J.S.*

Then they hung there a moment in the green-lit quiet, surrounded by ten thousand seedlings in their trays, all of them asleep in the way plants sleep—metabolism slowed, patience absolute—and felt the familiar shape of the day settle over them like the coverall itself.

This was the whole job, most days. Find the small wrongness. Fix it. Log it. Move on.

Eleven years of that. Eleven years since—

Jude didn't finish the thought. They'd gotten very good, over eleven years, at not finishing thoughts.

* * *

Breakfast was reconstituted eggs and real coffee, the coffee being the one luxury the Directorate budget had never managed to cut, because whoever wrote the budgets understood that a solo ferry pilot running a nine-month circuit between two silent worlds lived or died by small rituals. Jude took the coffee to the cockpit and ran the pre-flight checks on the *Tessellate* out of pure habit, though the ship wouldn't fly for another six days. Docked at Garden Station Alpha, umbilicals attached, systems cold. There was no hurry anywhere in Jude's life. That was the design of it.

The route itself was simple to describe and brutal to live: ninety days out from Vespera High Port to Garden Station Alpha, forty days tending the garden, ninety days back—or the reverse, depending on the cycle. Two colonies, one garden, one ship, one person. The colonies didn't trade. They didn't speak. But their futures sat in the same orbital greenhouse, pollinating under the same lamps, and Jude was the only thread stitching them together, and both governments preferred it that way precisely because Jude asked no questions and required no ceremony.

A ghost in the shipping lanes. A caretaker for the unborn.

Jude had made peace with it. That was the honest phrase, and honesty was a discipline they practiced daily, like stretching, like flossing, like checking the CO₂ scrubbers. They had made peace with being unnecessary to the living. The living had their own gardens—the surface farms, the breeding programs, the gene banks each colony hoarded behind its own firewalls. Jude's cargo was redundant by design. Insurance for disasters everyone prayed against and no one planned for.

It was lonely work. It was good work. Those were not contradictory statements; Jude had learned that too, somewhere around year four.

They were halfway through the coffee, watching Calloway rotate beneath the blister—cloud systems crawling across the northern ocean, a storm forming off the eastern cape with the slow confidence of weather that had all the time in the world—when the comm panel chimed.

Not the garden's soft dawn-chime. The hard triple-pulse of an incoming transmission on a priority channel.

Jude set down the cup. Priority channels on the long route carried exactly three kinds of messages: port authority scheduling, automated weather data relay, and nothing else. Ever.

The header resolved on the screen. Origin: Vespera High Port, Cargo Audit Division. Subject line: **ROUTINE MANIFEST VERIFICATION — CYCLE 47 — RESPONSE REQUIRED WITHIN 72 HOURS.**

Routine. Fine. Routine was fine. Jude had been doing routine for eleven years; routine was a language they spoke fluently.

They opened it.

* * *

The audit request was boilerplate—cross-verification of outbound cargo declarations against historical records, part of some periodic reconciliation that happened every few cycles and always turned up nothing. Jude skimmed the preamble, already composing the standard reply, and then stopped skimming because the reconciliation database had surfaced an anomaly, and the anomaly had a name.

**ANOMALY REFERENCE: Vessel MERIDIAN (hull class: evacuation transport, projected capacity 500,000). Status per registry: NEVER LAUNCHED. Manifest record exists. Cross-reference flag: PASSENGER LIST PARTIAL MATCH TO ACTIVE PERSONNEL DATABASE.**

Partial match. Jude read the line twice, then a third time, waiting for it to resolve into something mundane. A clerical echo. A namesake. Meridian was a common enough surname in the old colonial rolls—

The passenger list loaded. Five hundred thousand entries, the vast majority redacted or corrupted, decades of bad storage chewing through the record of a ship that had never flown. The system offered what it could recover. Jude scrolled.

Entry 4,417.

**SANDOVAL, JUDE M. — MERIDIAN EVACUATION REGISTRY — STATUS: UNRESOLVED.**

The coffee went cold in the cup. Jude didn't notice.

That was impossible. That was a clerical error, a data corruption artifact, a ghost in a database older than the Severance itself. Except the entry wasn't corrupted. It was clean. Name, biometric tag, origin world, date of registration—all of it intact, all of it correct, all of it matching down to the seven-digit identifier that no clerical error could invent because that identifier had been burned into Jude's wrist at birth on a world that no longer existed.

Meridian. The dead world. The one nobody named anymore, the one the histories handled with the delicate euphemism of "pre-Severance loss." Half a million people registered for an evacuation ship that never launched, frozen mid-crisis, and somewhere in that half million was a child who grew up to become a ferry pilot with a fake-clean personnel file and eleven years of careful silence.

Someone had just dragged that child into the light.

Jude sat very still in the cockpit of the *Tessellate*, garden humming around them, Calloway turning serene and oblivious below, and felt the first cold thread of something they hadn't let themselves feel in years.

Fear. Not of the audit. Audits could be answered with boilerplate.

Of the fact that the message existed at all. Because databases didn't reach across thirty years and two severed colonies to find one buried name unless someone had gone looking for it.

And there was one person alive who knew exactly where to dig.

The response field blinked, patient, expecting boilerplate. Jude typed *Manifest records verified. No discrepancies noted.* Then deleted it. Typed it again. Deleted it again.

Outside the blister, the storm off Calloway's eastern cape had organized itself into a spiral, beautiful and inevitable, and Jude watched it turn for a long moment before reaching for the comm controls—not to answer the audit, but to run a query of their own, fingers moving before caution could catch up.

*Search: transmission logs, inbound, last 400 days. Sender classification: personal.*

The search spun. And spun. And returned one result, timestamped fourteen months ago, flagged undeliverable, queued, retried, queued again—a message that had been trying to reach Jude across the silence of two severed colonies for over a year, bouncing through relay after relay, refused by every filter, waiting with a patience that could only be deliberate.

Sender ID: **W. ASHARI.**

Jude's hand hovered over the accept key, and the garden breathed around them, ten thousand sleeping seeds, and somewhere in the dark between worlds, half a million more.
