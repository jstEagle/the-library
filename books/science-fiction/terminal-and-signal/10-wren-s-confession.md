## 10. Wren's Confession

*To Jude Sandoval, passenger 4,417, Meridian, still listed as embarked.*

*You found the manifest. Of course you did. You were always the one who read the fine print on seed packets while the rest of us argued about politics. I could have buried that record deeper, but I wanted you to find it — eventually, when it was safe, or never, if it wasn't. Instead you found it in nine days. You always were faster than my caution.*

*So now I owe you the truth, and the truth is long. Pour something. Sit in the garden if you can — I know how you think better among growing things. This letter is going to take the length of a burn cycle.*

*Let me start where you'll want to start, which is not where I want to start. Let me start with the schedule.*

---

The vaults under Vespera's southern ice are quiet in a way Wren has spent eleven years learning to hear through. Half a million hearts slowed to four beats an hour, each one a small sound like snow settling. She walks the rows at night because the day staff ask questions, and because at night she can pretend the aisles are orchards and the pods are fruit she has not yet let ripen.

She has written this letter forty times. In her head. On paper she then burned in the recycler, watching her own handwriting curl and go gray. The version she is transmitting now — burst-packeted, encrypted to a cipher only Jude would bother to break, keyed to the ghost manifest so that opening it confirms what Jude already fears — this version she writes in one sitting, fast, before the courage curdles into strategy again.

*Here is what you will have guessed, because you are not stupid, only kind, and kindness makes people slow to suspect the worst of the people they've loved.*

*The wake schedule is rigged.*

*There. It's said. I can't take it back and I won't.*

---

*You remember the Severance. Of course you do; you ran from it two years before it finished killing Meridian, and I have spent eleven years deciding whether I blame you for that. I've decided no, mostly, on good days. On bad days I decide you're the reason I stayed, because if you'd asked me to come I would have, and we both know it, and you didn't ask.*

*Meridian died slowly and then all at once. The atmosphere went first — you know the chemistry better than I do, you used to explain it to me over dinner like weather reports. Then the water table. Then the governments, which had spent thirty years blaming each other for the sky, discovered they could not survive the accusation and stopped talking entirely. The evacuation was supposed to be orderly. Half a million people made it to the cryo-ships. The ships never launched. Calloway closed its corridor. Vespera closed its port. And the fleet sat on the pad while the last of Meridian's air turned to rust, and the sleepers inside those hulls became the only Meridianians left alive, and both colonies decided that was someone else's problem.*

*I was twenty-six. I was a cargo clerk with a gift for manifests. I got myself frozen onto the Meridian's lead ship by falsifying a priority code, which is the first dishonest thing I ever did and felt exactly like the honest thing, because everyone else was doing it with money and influence and I did it with competence. That's the whole history of injustice in one sentence, isn't it. Someone competent with a grudge.*

*The ships drifted for six years. Then Vespera towed them in — not out of mercy. Out of salvage logic. Half a million trained colonists in cold storage is an asset, if you can stomach the accounting. Calloway protested. Vespera pointed out that Calloway had refused them docking when they were alive. Both statements were true. That's the embargo for you: two true statements, aimed like guns.*

*I woke up eight years ago. Steward track. I was good at it. I was very good at it. And somewhere in my fourth year, when I had access to the wake sequencing and the genetic registries and the demographic projections, I sat down at my desk one night and did the arithmetic that broke me open.*

---

Wren stops walking. She is at pod row 4400, near the section where the Meridian's passenger list runs into names she recognizes — not Jude's; Jude is filed under 4,417, a clerical ghost, a seat assigned and never filled, and she chose that number deliberately, chose it as a message and a wound, because 4,417 was the berth number of the apartment they shared on Meridian for fourteen months, back when the air still tasted like rain instead of iron.

She looks at the sleeping faces. She knows some of them. Her old instructor. A woman who sold her oranges outside the transit hall. A boy who would be forty now if he'd been allowed to keep waking up.

*Here is the arithmetic. Vespera has a viable gene pool for maybe ninety more years before the recessive load starts expressing — you know this, it's in every public health bulletin, dressed up in careful language. Calloway has perhaps sixty. Two small populations, inbred by necessity, each pretending the other doesn't exist, each quietly dying of its own closed circle. The half million in my vaults carry the widest genetic spread humanity has produced since Earth. They are, quite literally, the cure for what's killing us. All of us. Both worlds.*

*A full random wake schedule — fair, blind, honest — would bring up roughly equal numbers of Meridianian, Vesperan-adjacent, and unaffiliated genomes per shift. I modeled it. I modeled everything, Jude, you know how I model things, I once built a spreadsheet for our grocery budget that had error bars. A fair schedule stabilizes both colonies' genetics within four generations. It also requires cooperation between two governments that have not exchanged a diplomatic cable in nineteen years, joint administration of a resource each considers strategic, and trust.*

*We don't have trust. We have me.*

*So I wrote a different schedule. One I could execute alone, with the authority I actually have. It prioritizes Vesperan-compatible genomes in the early shifts — the ones whose tissue profiles match our medical infrastructure, whose skills match our shortages, whose children would be born into a colony that could absorb them. I classified it as genetic triage. Triage is a beautiful word. It means: I am choosing who lives first, and I have a good reason, and if you saw my reason you would agree, and anyway the alternative is everyone dies equally, which sounds noble until it's your child at the back of the line.*

*I told myself it was temporary. I told myself I would build the case for a joint schedule, gather the evidence, force the governments to the table. Eleven years, Jude. I have been building that case for eleven years and the case is airtight and the table is still empty, because Calloway believes — correctly — that whoever administers the vaults owns the future, and Vespera believes the same, and neither will put their hand on the same knife handle.*

*And so the schedule stands. Rigged. Mine. Every year I sign off on the next shift's manifest and every year the first hundred names are Vesperan bloodlines and every year I tell myself the same sentence: better a rigged rescue than an honest catastrophe.*

*That's the sentence, isn't it. That's the whole confession compressed to one line. You can hate it now. Take your time. I'll wait. I'm good at waiting; it's most of my job.*

---

She transmits the first third and keeps writing, because the packet protocol demands sequential segments and because stopping would give her time to delete.

Around her the vault hums. She has given this place eleven years of nights. She knows which coolant pumps sing flat. She knows the exact pod — row 3,112, a man named Osei Ferran — whose vitals flickered for nine hours three years ago while she sat beside him holding his hand through the access port, talking to him about nothing, about the market on Meridian, about whether oranges still existed anywhere. He stabilized. She never told anyone. Some part of her has been married to these sleepers longer than she was ever married to anything else, including Jude, including the idea of Jude, which is a different and more durable thing.

*Now the manifest. Your name on it. You deserve to know why, and the honest answer is ugly, so here it is: I needed a courier I trusted absolutely, and there is exactly one person in two systems I trust absolutely, and I destroyed that trust myself, so I had to reach for the ghost of it.*

*In six months, Calloway will have the vault frequencies mapped. Their signals section is good — better than ours, honestly; they've had less to do. When they map them, they will find the wake keys, and they will demand them, and Vespera will refuse, and we will be at the edge of a war fought over sleeping bodies, which is the stupidest war imaginable and also the most likely. The keys are quantum-sealed. They can't be copied by either government's machinery. They can be carried, by hand, by one person, in a sealed case, along a shipping lane nobody watches because nobody flies it.*

*Nobody flies it except you.*

*One last route, Jude. Vespera to the vault relay, keys in hand, before Calloway's window opens. I'm asking you to help me finish the rigged rescue. I know what that sounds like. I know you're doing the math right now — you, who did the grocery spreadsheet — calculating how many Calloway grandchildren pay for my triage. I've done that math every night for eleven years and I will do it tonight and I still sign the schedule, because the alternative isn't fairness, the alternative is a locked room where everyone suffocates politely.*

*But here is the part I need you to hear without strategy attached, the part underneath the request:*

*I miss you. Not the way people say it at the end of letters. I mean I have organized my entire moral life around the shape of the person you were when you left, arguing with you in my head every single night, and you have never once changed your position, because you aren't here, and I have become very tired of winning arguments against a memory. Come. Argue with me in person. Bring the garden's ledger if you want ammunition — I know you think in botany, you always did, everything is soil and pruning and what deserves light. Fine. Come tell me what deserves to grow. Tell me to my face that half a million people should stay frozen while two governments negotiate for another decade. Tell me, and if you're right, I'll burn the schedule myself and hand you the matches.*

*That's not manipulation. Or it is, but it's also true, and I've stopped being able to tell the difference, which is probably the most honest thing in this letter.*

*The keys leave the vault in forty days. The window closes in fifty. You fly the long route in nineteen days when you push it, and you always push it, because you've made peace with being unnecessary to the living and necessary only to the future, and I have never once believed that peace was real.*

*Come prove me wrong. Come prove me right. Either way, come.*

*Passenger 4,417 never boarded. There's still a seat.*

*— W.A., Chief Steward, Keeper of the Sleepers, liar, triage officer, yours in whatever tense survives between us*

---

Wren sends the final segment and stands in the cold blue light of row 4,417 — the empty berth, the placeholder pod, kept pressurized and powered for eleven years at nontrivial expense, flagged in the system as *reserved*, though reserved for whom she has never entered into any log.

She presses her palm flat against the pod's lid. It is warm, faintly, the way stone is warm after sun.

"Your move," she says to no one, to everyone, to the whole sleeping dark.

Then she turns off the lights and walks out, and behind her half a million hearts beat four times an hour, patient as seeds, and somewhere between the stars a ship with a garden in its hold has just gone very slightly off course.
