## 19. Standoff at the Vaults

Callum Reyes had spent eleven years as a dock clerk learning one lesson above all others: nothing in space happens fast. Ships take days to brake. Cargo takes weeks to clear. Bureaucracy takes years. So when the Calloway fleet dropped out of transit over the Meridian vaults and found the Vesperan fleet already there, holding position in a perfect defensive crescent, his first thought was not *war*. His first thought was: *of course. Even this will be slow.*

He stood on the bridge of the *Calloway's Mercy*—a name that had always struck him as bureaucratic optimism—and watched two fleets stare at each other across forty thousand kilometers of black. Between them, below them, hung the vaults themselves: a cluster of ancient habitat modules strung along a dead orbital ring, running lights flickering amber, every external lock standing open.

Open. That was the word that kept snagging in his mind. Every hatch on the vault structure was open, and every open hatch was broadcasting its status to both fleets in plain, unencrypted text. No codes. No claims of ownership. Just a list, scrolling on every tactical display, of half a million sleepers and thirty years of wake-shifts, mixed colony by mixed colony, signed by no government at all.

"They're not moving," said Lieutenant Okafor, at tactics. "Vesperans are holding formation. Weapons cold."

"So are we," Callum said. "Because?"

"Because nobody wants to fire first." Okafor's voice was flat with professional contempt. "Sir, we have them outgunned three to one."

"We do." Callum didn't look away from the vaults. "And if we fire, what exactly do we hit? The vaults are between us. Every shot that misses them hits the sleepers. Every shot that doesn't miss—" He let it hang.

Okafor didn't finish the geometry for him. She'd already run it. Everyone on the bridge had run it. That was why the standoff existed: not because either fleet was afraid of the other, but because both fleets were afraid of the same thing, and the thing was sitting right there in the middle, unlocked, unclaimed, and full of people who had been dead-adjacent for sixty years.

"Status on the broadcast," Callum said.

"Still repeating. It's coming from inside the vaults—someone burned the Steward's authority to keep our jammers off it. We can't shut it down without cutting vault power, and cutting vault power kills the cryo systems." Okafor paused. "Whoever did it knew exactly what they were doing."

Callum knew who. He'd known for weeks, since the interrogation room, since Jude Sandoval sat across from him with garden dirt still under their fingernails and told him, calmly, that he was going to spend the rest of his career being sorry about this. He hadn't believed them then. He believed them now.

The broadcast was playing again on the open channel—the true manifest, the rigged schedule, Wren Ashari's own voice reading her justification into the record like a woman dictating her epitaph. *Genetic triage. I chose continuity over fairness. I would choose it again.* And beneath her voice, the wake keys, free to anyone, the unlock sequence for half a million lives, transmitted to both worlds simultaneously so that neither could pretend the other held them.

It was, Callum thought, the most elegant act of sabotage he had ever witnessed. Sandoval hadn't stolen anything. They'd made theft impossible.

"Commodore Vasquez is requesting your assessment," Okafor said. "Again."

"Tell her the same thing I told her an hour ago. We cannot seize those vaults. Not because we lack the firepower—because seizing them requires admitting they exist, and admitting they exist requires explaining why we've embargoed them for sixty years, and explaining that requires telling four million people on Calloway that their government let half a million human beings stay frozen rather than share them." He rubbed his eyes. "Same goes for Vespera. That's why everyone's just floating here. We're not in a standoff over territory. We're in a standoff over embarrassment."

Okafor almost smiled. Almost. "That's not going to hold forever, sir. Fleets drift. Nerves fray. Somebody's ensign is going to sneeze near a firing solution eventually."

"I know."

Below them, the vaults turned slowly in the light of Meridian's dim sun, amber lights blinking their patient, idiot rhythm. Half a million people in there, dreaming through all of it. Callum found himself envying them.

* * *

The message came in on a channel that shouldn't have existed—a dock clerk's frequency, low-band, the kind of thing nobody monitored because nobody important used it anymore. Callum was in his cabin when it arrived, and he almost dismissed it as noise. Then he saw the authentication header, old-style, the kind used before the Severance, and his skin went cold.

It wasn't from Vespera. It wasn't from Calloway Command. It was from the vaults themselves—or from someone speaking with the vaults' voice.

*To the officer who filed the report on the Sandoval queries,* it began, and Callum sat down slowly on his bunk. *You noticed what no one else noticed. You followed procedure until procedure became a weapon, and now you're watching it point at sleeping people. I know how that feels. I'm asking you to do the hardest thing a proceduralist can do: say something true, out loud, where both fleets can hear it.*

There was no signature. There didn't need to be. Only one person aboard these vaults knew about his report, and she was currently under arrest in her own antechamber, having surrendered herself the moment the fleets arrived—as if she'd been waiting for an audience.

Callum sat with the message for a long time. Outside his porthole, the two fleets drifted in their slow orbit, close enough now that he could see running lights moving along the Vesperan hulls, tiny and patient, like insects crossing a leaf.

He thought about his career. Eleven years of clean filings, correct forms, quiet promotions. He thought about the moment in the interrogation room when Sandoval had looked at him—not angry, not pleading, just *looking*, the way you look at weather—and said: *You think you're enforcing the law. The law ended sixty years ago. Everything since has been two governments pretending the other one died.*

He thought about what happened next if nobody spoke. Okafor was right; standoffs decay. In twelve hours, maybe twenty-four, some captain would maneuver too aggressively, some weapons officer would misread a burn, and the vaults would become collateral damage in a war over who got to inherit them. Both worlds would mourn the sleepers publicly and privately breathe relief: the problem solved itself, no decisions required, no lies exposed.

Exhaustion, Callum decided, was its own kind of clarity. He had been awake for thirty hours. He was tired of being the machinery. He was tired of filing things away.

He stood up and went to find the commodore.

* * *

Vasquez listened to him in her ready room with the particular patience of a commander deciding whether to have an officer relieved of duty.

"You want me to propose joint stewardship," she said. "To the Vesperan fleet. On an open channel."

"I want you to let *me* propose it. On an open channel, in my own name, so that when Command disavows it—and they will—you have deniability and I have a court-martial to look forward to." Callum kept his hands behind his back, parade rest, because if he didn't give them something to do they'd shake. "Ma'am, neither fleet can claim those vaults. Claiming them means confessing the embargo. But both fleets can *guard* them. Guarding requires no confession. It only requires admitting the other side exists—which, technically, we've never done."

"That's insane."

"Yes, ma'am."

"It's also—" She stopped. He watched her jaw work through several sentences she declined to say aloud. Finally: "It's also the only move that doesn't end with half a million corpses or a war nobody can afford. Which means someone will have to say it first, and whoever says it first hands the other side the moral high ground for a generation."

"Not if both sides say it at once," Callum said. "Not if it's said on an open channel, addressed to no fleet—to the vaults themselves. To the sleepers. Let it be a proposal to *them*. Then it isn't surrender. It's stewardship. Nobody yields to anybody. Everybody answers to the dead."

Vasquez stared at him for a long moment. "Reyes, you've been spending too much time around terraformers."

"Yes, ma'am. Probably."

She turned back to the viewport, toward the amber-lit vaults. When she spoke again, her voice had changed—flatter, more tired, more honest. "My orders are to secure the vaults for Calloway. If I transmit your proposal, I'm disobeying."

"If you don't, ma'am, in eighteen hours you're going to be securing wreckage."

Another silence. Then, quietly, in the voice of a woman setting down something heavy: "Get me a channel. Open band. Unencrypted. If we're doing this, we're doing it where everyone can hear, including my own government, including theirs, including whatever's left of the crew in those vaults."

* * *

They broadcast from the *Calloway's Mercy* at 0400 ship-time, and the Vesperans answered within the hour—which meant, Callum realized later, that someone over there had been waiting for exactly this, holding their own version of the proposal in a ready room, equally unable to say it first.

His words were simple. He'd drafted them seven times and thrown all seven drafts away and spoken from memory instead, tired enough to be truthful:

*"This is Dock Clerk First Class Callum Reyes, Calloway Registry, speaking on behalf of no government, because no government can speak honestly about what hangs below us. For sixty years our worlds have maintained an embargo built on mutual blame for the death of Meridian. Neither of us caused it alone. Both of us let the survivors stay frozen rather than face each other. Those sleepers are not cargo. They are not leverage. They are the last citizens of a world we both abandoned, and they are currently the only thing keeping our fleets from destroying each other.*

*"I propose, openly, before both fleets and both worlds: joint stewardship of the Meridian vaults. Shared administration. Mixed-colony wake shifts, thirty years, overseen by a council drawn from both worlds and from the vault crews themselves. Neither side surrenders. Both sides answer. To the sleepers, who deserve better than to be inherited like disputed property.*

*"I am a dock clerk. I file things. This is the only honest filing of my career. I invite Vespera to co-sign."*

The reply came from the Vesperan flagship, and it was read aloud by a woman whose voice cracked on the third sentence—an admiral, or someone pretending to be one, admitting on an open channel that her government had also been waiting for permission to stop lying.

*"Vespera co-signs."*

On the bridge of the *Mercy*, nobody cheered. That wasn't the kind of moment it was. It was quieter than cheering—the sound of two machines, running sixty years past their design life, finally allowed to stop.

Okafor stood beside Callum at the viewport, watching the fleets begin, slowly, clumsily, to intermingle their picket lines—two formations that had trained against each other for decades trying to figure out how to fly together.

"Somebody's going to write history about tonight," she said.

"No," Callum said. "Somebody's going to *file* it. History gets written by people who weren't tired enough to tell the truth."

Below them, the vaults turned in the dark, hatches open, amber lights blinking—and deep inside, on a restored uplink that no one had ordered repaired, a garden signal was beginning, faintly, to sing.
