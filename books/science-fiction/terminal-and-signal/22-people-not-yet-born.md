## 22. People Not Yet Born

The route hadn't gotten shorter. That was the first thing Jude noticed, coming back to it after four years planetside. The long route between Vespera and Calloway was still eleven days of nothing but the hum of the drive and the slow green patience of the garden riding in its cradle behind them, and it still asked the same question every single day: *who is this for?*

For years Jude had answered that question with silence. Now there were voices on the comm.

"—and then Okafor says the trellis code is wrong, but it's not wrong, it's just old, it's from before the Severance—"

"Tell Okafor the Severance ended," Jude said into the open channel, not unkindly. "That's kind of the whole point."

Laughter, two voices' worth. Amara and Devi, the first wakers, shift one of shift one, numbers 12 and 13 on a manifest that had once been a weapon. They'd been awake for three years now, old enough to walk the rows without supervision, young enough to treat every seedling like a miracle, which, Jude supposed, it was.

They were walking the rows right now, their suit lights bobbing between the beds like fireflies. Jude watched them on the feed while the ferry's autopilot held station at the garden's docking collar, and let the loneliness of the long route be a different thing than it used to be. Not absence. Just space. Room for other people to arrive in.

* * *

The garden itself was new. That had taken longer than everything else—the accord, the fleets standing down, the thirty-year schedule signed by councils who hated each other slightly less than they feared each other. Replanting had been Jude's sentence, though nobody called it that. When Wren surrendered, when the joint council gave her the vaults to tend under guard, someone had to give the seed-garden back its future. Callum Reyes had looked across the table at Jude with his clerk's flat patience and said, "You broke the uplink running dark. You fix it." And that had been that.

Four years of splicing, grafting, coaxing Meridian stock back from the edge of viability. Half the original genome library had degraded in the vaults during the embargo decades; Jude had rebuilt it from the garden's own survivors, generation after generation, until the beds held something older and stranger than either colony remembered planting. Vesperan wheat that grew in Calloway soil. Calloway lichen that ate Vesperan regolith. Things with no home world at all anymore, which made the garden, Jude thought, the only honest institution left.

Amara crouched at bed nine, where the first true hybrid had flowered last month—a pale, stubborn bloom with roots in both worlds' genetics and no allegiance to either. She'd named it before anyone could stop her. She called it *Wren*, because, she said, "it survived being told what it was supposed to be."

Jude had laughed until it hurt, and then gone back to the ferry and sat in the dark for an hour. Some doors stay ajar. You don't have to walk through them. But you hear them sometimes, in the names children give things.

* * *

Devi found Jude in the ferry's galley at end-shift, drinking reconstituted tea and reading the day's schedule off the council feed. Thirty years of wake-shifts, mixed colonies, administered by a body that included Callum Reyes and, in a supervised capacity that everyone pretended wasn't extraordinary, Wren Ashari. Shift six was coming up next year. Two hundred sleepers. The manifest, the true one, hung in public archive now on both worlds, passenger 4,417 crossed out in red by some archivist with a sense of humor: *declined passage.*

"You're going back out," Devi said. It wasn't a question. He'd learned to read Jude the way you learn weather.

"Route needs flying. Seed-stock for the Calloway expansion beds. Nobody else will do the long run."

"Nobody else *gets* to," Amara said from the hatchway, grinning. "It's your garden. We just work here."

Jude looked at them—these two people who had been cargo once, entries on a rigged list, bodies scheduled by someone else's arithmetic—and felt the old fear surface one more time, the fear that had lived under the routine since the day the Meridian's ghost manifest scrolled past: *someone decides who wakes.* It would never fully leave. Jude suspected it wasn't supposed to. It was the load-bearing wall of the whole fragile thing—that both worlds had agreed, exhausted, to stop deciding alone.

"Shift six has a Calloway majority," Devi said quietly. "First time. People noticed."

"People should notice," Jude said. "That's how you know it's real."

* * *

Pre-launch took two days. Jude walked the rows one last time with Amara, checking seals, checking the uplink that now reached both worlds openly—no more dark runs, no more cold transponders, the garden broadcasting its telemetry to anyone who wanted to watch, which turned out to be thousands of people on both colonies who had decided that watching a garden grow was a form of politics.

At the airlock, Amara hesitated. "Do you ever go see her?"

"The vaults are on the route," Jude said. "I fly past them every run."

"That's not what I asked."

Jude sealed their helmet and didn't answer, and Amara, who was twenty-six years old and had already survived being frozen and unfrozen and re-rigged into history, accepted the silence with a grace that made Jude's chest ache. The young were like that now. Both worlds' young. They'd inherited a catastrophe honestly labeled, and they carried it without flinching, and they asked direct questions, and they waited out the silences.

* * *

Eleven days out, Jude flew past the vaults.

They hung in Vespera's high orbit, the great dark cylinders of the Meridian's survivors, running lights steady, guarded now by ships from both fleets parked in uneasy courtesy a thousand kilometers apart. On the vaults' exterior, visible only from this approach angle, someone had painted—no, grown, Jude realized, it was moss, engineered moss in slow photosynthetic letters—a single line of text across the third cylinder:

*FOR PEOPLE NOT YET BORN.*

Wren's handwriting. Jude knew it instantly, the way you know a voice through a wall. The council had approved the message as a public monument. Only Jude knew the handwriting was hers, because only Jude had spent nine years reading her notes over her shoulder in another life, on another world, before everything.

Jude held station for a full orbit. Didn't hail. Didn't dock. The vaults' external comms were open—all comms were open now, that was the whole point—and so the silence was a choice, and the choice was a message too, and maybe she read it and maybe she didn't.

Then the vaults' beacon flickered. Once. Twice. A pattern any gardener would recognize: the old pre-Severance signal for *greenhouse conditions nominal, growth proceeding on schedule.*

Jude laughed, alone in the cockpit, wet-eyed, and keyed the reply with the same old code: *acknowledged. harvest pending.*

* * *

Calloway received the seed-stock without incident. The expansion beds went in under joint crews—Vesperan and Calloway hands in the same dirt, which would have been unthinkable five years ago and was now merely difficult, which is what progress looks like when it's honest. Callum met the ferry personally, older, grayer, still carrying his clerk's ledger everywhere in physical form like a talisman against forgetting.

"Passenger manifests for the return leg," he said, deadpan, handing over a folder of actual paper. "All accounted for. No anomalies."

"No ghosts?"

"One," Callum said, and his mouth twitched. "Page 4,417. Filed as *arrived eventually, by other means.*"

Jude took the folder. "He did, didn't he."

"We all did," Callum said. "Some of us just took the scenic route." He paused at the airlock, and for a moment the methodical dock clerk showed through the councilor: "The sixth shift. I'll be there when they open the lids. Both of us should be. Somebody has to write down what hope looks like when it's documented properly."

"I'll bring the flowers," Jude said.

* * *

The run back was eleven days again, and this time the garden module rode empty, clean and waiting, and Jude filled the silence with something new: recordings. Amara and Devi's voices describing the beds. The hybrid's bloom cycle. The sound, captured on a stray mic, of two hundred colonists on Calloway singing badly at the expansion site while the first Vesperan wheat came up in their soil.

By the time the garden's cradle docked home, the recording was already in the archive, tagged and public, filed under a designation the archivists had argued about for a week before settling on the obvious: *for arrival.*

Jude walked the rows at end-shift, alone, the way it had always been, and it wasn't lonely anymore. It was custodial. The difference between those two things was four years and half a million waking people and one door left ajar.

At bed nine, the Wren hybrid had set seed. Jude collected it carefully, labeled it, and added it to the distribution list—not for the councils, not for the vaults, but for the open archive, free to anyone, anywhere, forever.

*Anyone who wants to grow something,* the tag read. *No manifest required.*

And somewhere behind Jude, in the deep quiet of the vaults, six hundred sleepers waited for morning—shift six, mixed colonies, undocumented by anyone's agenda except time itself—while above them the moss letters drank the light of two suns and slowly, patiently, spelled out the only promise that had ever mattered:

*You are expected. Come as you are. The garden is yours.*

The route was long. It would always be long.

But for the first time since the Severance, everyone on it was going to arrive.
