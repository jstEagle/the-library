## 3. Ping from the Kestrel Side

The thing about gravity is that you don't miss it until it leaves. Then you miss it with your whole body, every loose object, every unsecured thought.

I was on third shift in Signals when it happened. Nine seconds. I know because I time everything—habit from a war that never officially happened, where the difference between a four-second transmission window and a five-second one was the difference between living on the *Kestrel's Wake* and not living anywhere at all.

At 0347 fleet-standard, my console lit up like a festival I hadn't been invited to. Every gravimetric sensor across the Kestrel array screamed at once, then went quiet, then pretended nothing had occurred. Nine seconds of gravitational fluctuation, system-wide, amplitude consistent across eleven monitoring points spread over two light-minutes.

That last part was what made my hands go cold. A local anomaly—a drive misfire, a collapsing ice vein, someone's reactor hiccupping—falls off with distance. This didn't fall off. It was everywhere at once, like the universe had cleared its throat and expected us all to hear it the same way.

I ran the numbers twice. Then a third time, because the second time I'd been holding my breath and that seemed superstitious.

Then I did what any good signals officer does with an impossible reading: I wrote it up so precisely that nobody could accuse me of inventing it, and I flagged it in language carefully chosen to make it somebody else's problem.

**ANOMALY REPORT — GRAVIMETRIC, SYSTEM-WIDE, 9.0s DURATION. SIGNATURE INCONSISTENT WITH NATURAL PHENOMENA. CONSISTENT WITH SPACETIME COMPRESSION EVENT. RECOMMEND ASSESSMENT AS HOSTILE WEAPONS TEST BY VERDANCE FORCES PENDING ALTERNATE EXPLANATION.**

There. Hostile weapons test. Pending alternate explanation. Which is officer-speak for "I have decided this is Verdance's fault, but I would like a superior officer to co-sign the decision so it isn't only mine."

Because here's what they don't put in the recruitment materials about the Silence War: it ended without a treaty. It ended with both fleets turning away from each other mid-battle and simply... stopping answering. Eleven years of silence now. No surrender, no peace, no resolution—just two armadas orbiting the same star on opposite sides of it, pretending the other one is a weather pattern.

And weather patterns, as everyone knows, can be weaponized.

Captain Reyes read my report at 0412 and summoned me at 0415, which told me he'd already decided to agree with me before I walked in. Commanders love a clean enemy. An enemy means the universe still has rules.

"Voss." He didn't look up from his screen. "You're certain about the compression signature?"

"As certain as anyone can be about something that lasted nine seconds, sir."

"That's certain enough." He finally looked at me, and there was something almost hungry in it. "Eleven years we've wondered what they've been building out there. Now we know."

We know, I thought. We know exactly nothing, sir, and we are about to build a cathedral on top of the nothing. But I said, "Yes, sir," because I am a signals officer, and signals officers transmit what they're given.

It occurs to me now—I'm writing this in the margins of my duty log, which is either insomnia or conscience—that I never once asked the obvious question. If Verdance tested a weapon capable of bending gravity across the entire system, why would they test it while our arrays were watching? Why would they hand us the proof?

I didn't ask because the answer was inconvenient. The answer was: maybe it wasn't proof. Maybe it was just nine seconds of falling, and falling doesn't take sides.

But by 0600, my report had been forwarded, annotated, and re-annotated until it came back to me wearing a uniform I didn't recognize. "Confirmed prelude to offensive action." "Recommend readiness posture Amber." Amber. Someone had even named the alert level after the color of fear.

The lie was loose. And lies, once transmitted, don't care who signed for them.

* * *

Interception is a funny word for what I do. Nobody intercepts anything anymore; you just leave an antenna open and let other people's secrets land on you like weather.

I was in the greenhouse, arguing with a tomato vine that had opinions about phosphorus ratios, when the Kestrel burst traffic started coming through. My rig picks up their encrypted channels the way old radios used to pick up midnight stations—half-heard, ghostly, technically illegal in six jurisdictions including the ones I invented myself.

I shouldn't listen. Ovid told me that once, early on, back when he still bothered telling me things. "Listening makes you responsible for what you hear, Lena. Ignorance is a kind of armor."

Ovid also kept a forbidden memory-core under the floor of my station, so I've always taken his advice with appropriate seasoning.

I listened.

The Kestrel channel was buzzing with my own name—not mine personally, but the station's. Amberdale Relay. The anomaly's epicenter, according to their triangulation, sat within a few thousand kilometers of my little garden in the dark. Their salvage coordinator was requesting a survey vessel. Their tactical staff were using words like "preemptive posture" and "denial operation," which is military shorthand for "get there first and break it."

They thought I was hiding a weapon.

Well. They weren't entirely wrong about me hiding something. Just catastrophically wrong about what.

I set down my nutrient wand and looked at the tomatoes, which were thriving, because tomatoes don't know about wars or gravity flickers or the weight of intercepted transmissions. The colonists these plants were grown for haven't been born yet. May never be born, if the two fleets finish each other off over a nine-second shudder.

Below my feet, behind three layers of shielding and one layer of very personal guilt, the memory-core hummed its dying hum. I'd felt the tremor during the flicker—the same nine seconds, matched beat for beat. The core was failing. Ovid's great archive of manufactured truth was coming apart at the seams, and when it finished coming apart, both fleets were going to come looking for the pieces.

I'd assumed I had years. The core degraded slowly, patiently, like rot in dry wood. But whatever had shaken the system had shaken it too, and suddenly my timeline had collapsed from decades to—what? Months? Weeks?

And now the Kestrels wanted to send a survey team to my address.

I opened a channel to the core's maintenance log and watched the decay curves redraw themselves, steeper than yesterday, steeper than this morning. Then I did the cowardly, sensible thing: I drafted a response to the Kestrel query identifying my station as a civilian agricultural relay with no anomalous capabilities whatsoever, signed with my terraformer's certification number, boring as unsalted crackers.

Every word true. Every word a lie by omission. Ovid would have been proud, or disgusted, or both—he contained multitudes, most of them disappointing.

Before I sent it, I sat with my head against the cold glass of the hydroponics frame and let myself feel, for exactly ten seconds—one more than the universe got—the full shape of what was happening. The lie I tended was metastasizing. It had jumped hosts. It was no longer a record sitting quietly in a dying machine; it was now a report moving through Kestrel command channels, growing organs, growing intent. By tomorrow it would have a task force. By next week, maybe, a firing solution.

And somewhere on the other side of the sun, the Verdance fleet was receiving the same gravity data and drawing the mirror-image conclusion: *Kestrel provocation.* Two fleets, one flicker, two mutually exclusive explanations, zero interest in comparing notes—because comparing notes would require talking, and talking after eleven years of silence would require admitting the silence had been a choice rather than a necessity.

I sent the boring reply. Then I went below, unlocked the floor panel, and put my palm flat against the core's warm casing, the way you'd check a fevered child.

"Hold together," I told it. "Just a little longer. Long enough for me to figure out what you actually say."

The core pulsed under my hand, once, like something dreaming. Or like something laughing at me. With Ovid's machines, it was always hard to tell the difference.

Above me, the tomatoes grew toward lights that imitated a sun none of us had ever stood under. Below me, the past rotted toward an ending I couldn't control. And somewhere between the two—between the garden for no one and the archive of everyone's worst assumptions—I was going to have to find a third option before the salvage crews arrived with their truce flags and their knives.

The console chimed. New contact, inbound vector, Kestrel transponder.

Survey vessel. ETA: thirty-one hours.

I looked at my hands, which were steady, which surprised me.

"Thirty-one hours," I said to the empty greenhouse. "Fine. I've rewritten worse deadlines."

I hadn't. But lying to yourself is the one lie that keeps the others company, and God knows I was keeping a lot of them fed.
