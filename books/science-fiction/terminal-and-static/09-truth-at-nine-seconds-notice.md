## 9. Truth at Nine Seconds' Notice

**Dara Voss — Kestrel signals, *Cormorant's Wake***

I have spent eleven years listening to the dark between ships, and in that time I have learned exactly one universal truth: nothing good has ever arrived as a full-spectrum broadcast marked PRIORITY OVERRIDE.

So when Lena Amberdale's station lit up every channel at once — raw archive packets, unencrypted, unedited, spilling across the system like someone had kicked over a filing cabinet in a hurricane — my first instinct was to reach for the jamming protocols.

My second instinct was to keep my hands very still and let it play.

The first packet was a weapons log. Kestrel frigate *Meridian*, the opening hour of the Silence War, firing solutions locked and confirmed. I knew that ship. Everyone in Kestrel knew that ship. We'd been told the Verdance struck her down without provocation, that her crew died screaming into a comm channel nobody answered.

The log showed something else. It showed *Meridian* firing first — and showed, three seconds earlier, a Verdance ship receiving a spoofed contact report that made *Meridian* look like an incoming raider. Both fleets pulling triggers on ghosts. Both ghosts manufactured by the same hand, from the same forged signature, in the same nine-minute window.

I sat in my chair in the signals bay and did not move for a long time.

Eleven years of listening, and the whole time the war's first shot had been a lie with excellent production values.

Behind me, Ensign Okonkwo said, "Ma'am? Command wants to know if the broadcast is authentic."

And I heard myself say, in a voice that didn't sound like mine: "It's authentic. That's the problem."

* * *

**Ilya Renko — Verdance engineering, cutter *Sable Hook***

Here is what nobody tells you about the truth arriving: it doesn't come gently. It comes all at once, in the middle of a docking bay, while you're holding a cutting torch and pretending you don't have orders to seize a memory-core by force.

I was standing three meters from Lena Amberdale when her broadcast went out. I watched her do it. She walked past both boarding parties — Kestrel on the port side, us on starboard, everyone's hands drifting toward holsters in that polite way people do when the truce is wearing thin — and she put her palm on a console and said, quietly, "I'm sorry it's like this," and sent eleven years of lies to every ship in the system.

Then she turned around and looked at us, both crews, both flags, and said, "You should probably sit down."

Terraformers. I swear they're all the same. Give them a garden and they'll tell the truth to an armed mob.

I didn't sit down. I read the packets instead, standing, my torch still in my hand like an idiot, scrolling through pre-war fleet communications I had no clearance to see and every right to. And there it was — the third-party traffic. The dead broker. Someone — some faction, some machine, some ghost with a grudge older than either fleet — had fed both sides doctored sensor data until we shot each other out of the sky over nothing. Over *manufactured* nothing.

The Verdance dead. The Kestrel dead. My sister was on the *Aurelian Wake* when it went down in the second year. I had spent six years hating a faceless enemy for that.

The faceless enemy turned out to be a forgery. The enemy with a face turned out to be us, both of us, aiming at shadows.

A Kestrel marine near me — young, maybe twenty-two, the kind of young where the war is the only country they've ever lived in — read the same packet I was reading and made a sound I will hear until I die. Not a shout. A small sound. The sound of a person discovering that the thing they built their grief on was scaffolding over an empty room.

He looked at me. I looked at him. We were, by every regulation either fleet had ever printed, enemies standing in the same docking bay.

Neither of us reached for a weapon. It felt like the most dangerous thing either of us had ever done.

* * *

**Admiral Sefa Kestrel-Halloway — flagship *Long Answer***

They named me for the war. Sefa Kestrel — the first Kestrel admiral's daughter, born in the year of the first engagement, raised on the story of Verdance treachery the way other children are raised on lullabies. I have signed the orders. I have written the letters to the families. I have stood at forty-one funerals and told forty-one crowds that our dead were murdered by the enemy, because that is what the record said, and the record was our only scripture.

The record was a forgery. The scripture was written by a dead man with a grudge or a contract, we may never know which, and maintained — this is the part that keeps me awake tonight — maintained for years by a terraformer with a garden and a conscience.

My weapons officer stood at my shoulder and said, "Admiral, Verdance has gone to combat stations. All of them. Fleet-wide."

Of course they had. So had we. That's the choreography; you arm because they arm because you arm. Eleven years of war and the opening move never changes.

"Time to weapons range on their forward screen?" I asked.

"Fourteen minutes. Sixteen if they hesitate."

I looked at the broadcast still scrolling on the main display. The *Meridian* log. My father had served on the *Meridian*. He died believing. We all died believing; some of us just did it slowly.

"Admiral," the weapons officer said again, gentler. "Orders?"

And here is the thing I will never be able to explain properly to the tribunal, or to my daughter, or to myself at three in the morning. I looked at the raw record of our dead — our actual dead, the real ones, the ones who died to a lie — and I understood that if I gave the order, they would have died twice. Once to a forger's fiction, and once to my refusal to grieve honestly.

"Match their readiness," I said. "Do not fire. And get me Verdance Command on an open channel. Unencrypted."

The bridge went very quiet. Unencrypted. In Kestrel, saying that out loud is close to heresy.

"They'll see everything we say," my comms officer said.

"Yes," I said. "That's rather the point. Eleven years of encrypted lies got us here. Let's try the truth at full volume and see if it kills us any faster."

* * *

**Commander Anselm Vey — Verdance fleet command, *Patient Stone***

I want it on the record — someone's record, any record — that I ordered my fleet to combat stations because that is what the data demanded. Kestrel arming, an anomaly at a neutral station, a broadcast of unknown provenance. Any commander in history would have done the same. I will not apologize for the choreography.

I will, however, tell you about minute nine.

Minute nine is when my own gunnery crews started reading the broadcast instead of watching their boards. I know because the firing solutions came back late. Twice. And when I went down to the battery decks to remind my people what readiness meant, I found Chief Okafor sitting with a Kestrel casualty list pulled from the raw archive — names, dates, hull numbers — cross-referencing it against our own engagement records, and she looked up at me with red eyes and said, "Sir, the *Cormorant's Wake* lost people at Halcyon Drift. We thought we hit them. We thought — sir, we were told we were avenging *Aurelian Wake*."

"And?"

"And it was the same day, sir. The same *hour*. We were both avenging the same forged attack. We were avenging each other."

I stood on my own battery deck, surrounded by my own armed crew, and I could not find a single sentence that made arming those guns make sense. Not one. Eleven years of doctrine, and the raw record had dissolved it in nine minutes flat.

"Commander," my XO said at my elbow, low. "Kestrel's flagship is hailing. Open channel. Plaintext. The admiral herself."

"Put her through," I said. "Audio only. And Anselm —" my XO started, and I cut him off. "I know what I'm doing."

I didn't. Nobody did. That was the whole shape of the hour: two fleets standing at the edge of the worst decision of their lives, holding the first honest document either of them had ever seen, and no doctrine anywhere for what to do when the war turns out to be a eulogy for a crime that never happened.

I answered the hail. Admiral Kestrel-Halloway's voice came through raw, unfiltered, tired as bedrock.

"Commander Vey," she said. "I've read your dead. You should read mine."

"I have," I said.

A pause. On my battery decks, on her bridge, in the vacuum between us, eleven years of silence sat there like a third fleet.

"Then I propose," she said, "that we hold fire for one hour. Not a truce. Not a treaty. Just — one hour where nobody adds to the list. We've both got enough names."

I looked out the viewport at her forward screen, guns hot, pointed at my people. And mine at hers.

"One hour," I said. "And Commander to Admiral — someone has to decide what we do with the terraformer."

* * *

**Dara Voss — *Cormorant's Wake*, one hour later**

The hour held. Barely. There were incidents — a Verdance picket that jumped early, a Kestrel gun crew that needed to be talked down by their own chaplain — but the hour held, the way a bridge holds: not because it's strong, but because everyone on it has decided, for now, to stand very still.

I sat in my signals bay with the raw archive open and did the only thing I'm actually good at. I listened. To the fleets. To the open channels nobody had bothered to encrypt anymore, because what was the point now? I listened to Kestrel soldiers reading Verdance casualty lists out loud, names in their mouths like communion. I listened to Verdance gunners asking their officers, quietly, furiously, *who do we even shoot?*

And beneath all of it, faint, almost lost in the noise floor, I caught one more signal. Small. Personal. Point-to-point, from the station in the unfinished garden.

Lena Amberdale. Broadcasting on a channel only a signals officer would bother to monitor.

Not a manifesto. Not an apology. Four words, sent to both fleets and to no one, the way you'd set a lantern in a window:

*The garden is ready. Come.*

I looked at the packet for a long time. Then I did something that will absolutely end up in my file, and forwarded it to the Verdance engineer I'd argued sensor logs with in a docking bay while the world ended — the one with the sister on the *Aurelian Wake*, the one who'd stood beside me and not reached for a weapon.

Her reply came back in under a minute.

*I'll bring seeds. You bring the truth about who's flying the shuttle, because Command will never authorize this.*

"No," I said aloud, to the empty bay, to the whole broken honest system. "They won't."

I started plotting a course anyway.
