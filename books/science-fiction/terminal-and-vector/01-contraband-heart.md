## 1. Contraband Heart

The coolant lines of the *Magpie's Debt* ran cold and clean through her belly like a second circulatory system, and Paloma Blackwood knew every meter of them the way a man knows the veins in his own wrist. He lay on his back in the maintenance crawlspace beneath the cargo deck, cheek against the insulated pipe, and fed the last of the memory-shards into the access port at junction nine. Each shard was no larger than a thumbnail, matte black, cool as a river stone. Each one held a human life compressed to crystal—birthdays, betrayals, the smell of someone's mother's kitchen, the exact weight of a child asleep against a shoulder. Whole existences, sealed in silicon, worth more per gram than gold and easier to hide than a lie.

He sealed the port, wiped his prints from the housing with a rag that smelled of solvent and old coffee, and listened to the ship breathe around him. The *Debt* was twenty years old and honest about it: she groaned when she turned, ticked as her hull cooled, complained in frequencies only a pilot who loved her would bother to hear. Seven years he'd flown her out of Halcyon Gate, and in seven years she had never once betrayed him.

People were another matter.

* * *

Customs at Halcyon Gate was a cathedral built by accountants. The inspection bays rose in pale tiers, floodlit white, every surface scrubbed to a shine that said *nothing here has ever been touched by human hands*. Authority inspectors moved between ships in their slate-grey coats with their slate-grey faces, running wands over seams, scanning manifests with the flat attention of men reading obituaries. Everything about the place was designed to make you feel seen. That was the trick of it. Halcyon Gate didn't actually see anyone. It saw paperwork, and paperwork could be loved into compliance for the right price.

Paloma's price had been stable for three years now: Inspector Dae-Ho Yun, bay fourteen, four hundred credits a run plus a standing tab at the Lantern Room that Paloma paid without ever being asked. Yun was not corrupt so much as tired, which Paloma understood better than most men alive. Tiredness was just corruption that hadn't found its price yet. He'd learned to recognize it the way sailors recognized weather.

"Blackwood." Yun stood at the *Debt*'s boarding ramp with his wand holstered and his tablet already open, and there it was—the particular slackness around his eyes that meant the shift had been long and the bribe had been remembered. "Coolant recirculation flagged for secondary sweep last month. You're lucky the order got lost."

"Lucky," Paloma agreed gravely. "I light candles."

Yun almost smiled. Almost. He walked the cargo deck at the pace of a man touring a museum he'd seen a hundred times, pausing at the pallets of agricultural desiccant, the crates of medical polymer stamped with serial numbers that would resolve, if anyone ever bothered to check—and no one ever bothered—to legitimate suppliers on two different stations. His wand never left its holster. At the coolant access panel he lingered exactly four seconds, which was three seconds longer than necessary and one second shorter than suspicious, and then he signed the manifest with a thumbprint that glowed green as a bottle fly.

"You're clean," Yun said, meaning *you're paid*.

"I'm always clean," Paloma said, meaning *thank you*.

And that was all it was. That was the whole religion of it: seven years of small green lights, of manifests that resolved, of a face that customs cameras recorded and no one ever looked at twice. Paloma Blackwood, registered hauler, owner-operator, tax-compliant, unremarkable. A man made of paperwork, and the paperwork was boring, and boredom was the best armor ever forged.

He walked back up the ramp carrying his clearance chit like a communion wafer, and if there was a hollowness in his chest where satisfaction should have been, he'd gotten good at not hearing it echo.

* * *

The Lantern Room sat three berths down from the customs tiers, wedged between a water-recycling plant and a chapel that served six religions badly. It was a dockside bar in the oldest sense: low ceiling, amber light, air scrubbers that had given up on ambition and settled for adequacy. The clientele were haulers, riggers, customs clerks off shift, and the kind of quiet professionals whose professions were never discussed. Paloma had a stool at the end of the bar that fit him like a socket fits a bolt, and the bartender, a heavy woman named Oksana with forearms like mooring cable, poured his drink before he'd finished sitting down.

"Rough run?" she asked.

"Smooth run." He turned the glass a quarter-turn on the bar, an old habit, aligning himself with nothing in particular. "That's what makes it rough."

Oksana snorted and moved off down the bar. Around him the room did what the room always did: traded rumors the way other places traded currency. A rigger two stools down swore the Authority was doubling patrols on the Ceres approach. Someone's cousin had seen a jump freighter come back from the outer dark with her crew missing and her logs intact, which was either a ghost story or a salvage opportunity depending on your constitution. The talk washed over Paloma like warm water. He drank and let it wash.

He'd been doing this long enough to know the shape of his own life the way you know the shape of a cell you've lived in. Morning: pre-flight checks, shard placement, the careful arithmetic of weight and heat signature. Afternoon: customs theater, Yun's tired eyes, the green thumbprint. Evening: the Lantern Room, one drink, sometimes two, never three, because three was when men started talking and talking was how smugglers died. Night: alone aboard the *Debt*, running diagnostics he didn't need to run because the alternative was lying in his bunk listening to the silence where a family used to be.

Seven years since Earth. Seven years since the Blackwood district burned, and the news feeds called it an industrial accident, and everyone who might have said otherwise stopped answering messages. Seven years of being no one, which was safer than being someone, and lonelier than he ever admitted aloud, even to himself. Especially to himself.

"—the Vesperine," someone said, and the name went through the warm water of the bar like a knife through bathwater.

Paloma didn't turn. Turning was amateur. But he listened, the way he listened to everything, with the whole surface of his mind.

The speaker was a salvager named Corrigan, a big man with a beard going grey in patches, holding court near the corner table. "Third time I've heard it this month," Corrigan was saying. "Ghost ship. Registered out of Halcyon, decommissioned before it ever flew—here's the part I like—decommissioned *before it ever flew*. Paperwork says she was scrapped in orbit, but scrappers say they never touched her. No yard has her hull. No breaker has her plates. She's on the books and she's nowhere."

"Somebody fat-fingered the registry," Oksana said from down the bar, unconvinced.

"Three separate registries? Fat-fingered three times, same ship?" Corrigan shook his head. "My brother-in-law works records over in the civil annex. Says the file's weird. Says it's *clean* weird. Like somebody built it fresh, top to bottom, and then locked it. And get this—" he leaned in, dropping his voice to the register men use when they want to be overheard—"the ownership chain goes back to a name nobody can find. Some Earth-side name. Pre-Exodus."

The bar murmured. Ghost stories were currency here; a registry anomaly was small change. But something cold had slid down Paloma's spine and pooled at the base of it, and he took a slow pull of his drink and studied his own reflection in the back-bar mirror—grey-eyed, weathered, forty-one years old and looking older, a man assembled entirely out of careful habits.

Pre-Exodus. Earth-side. Before the colonies, before the Authority consolidated the corridors, before the Blackwood district and everything that came after.

*Vesperine.* He turned the word over. It sounded like a hymn sung in a language he'd half forgotten. It sounded like evening prayers. It sounded, absurdly, like his mother's name for the hour after sunset, the blue hour, the time she'd called—

He set the thought down carefully, the way you set down something hot, and told himself it was coincidence. The universe was full of coincidences; that was practically its job description. A ghost ship with a strange manifest meant nothing. Names echoed. Words rhymed across decades for no reason at all.

But he finished his drink faster than usual, and he didn't order a second, and when he walked back down the dock to the *Magpie's Debt* he took the long way, past the civil annex windows, past the dark glass where the registries slept behind their locks.

The station hummed around him, ten thousand lives stacked in steel, and somewhere in that humming was a file with his century written all over it, and he didn't know that yet. All he knew was that for the first time in seven years, the silence in his chest had changed key. Weary competence had carried him a long way—through checkpoints and bribes and the long gray middle of a life spent being no one. But weariness, he was beginning to suspect, had been a kind of sleep.

Something out there in the paper dark had his name on it.

He climbed the ramp, sealed the hatch, and sat for a long time in the *Debt*'s dim cockpit with the systems idling around him like a sleeping animal's heartbeat, watching the customs tiers burn their white light against the black.

Tomorrow, he told himself, he'd make some calls. There was a records broker down in Spoke Nine who owed him favors—three of them, precise and banked like fuel—and a man who'd spent seven years building invisibility knows exactly how to go looking for a thing without leaving fingerprints.

Outside the viewport, Halcyon Gate turned slowly against the stars, beautiful and blind.

Somewhere in its belly, a ship that never flew was waiting to be remembered.
