## 7. Inspection

The Vesperine's berth smelled of cold iron and old ozone, the way all dead ships did, but this one had a smell underneath it that Sefa Idris couldn't name until she was three steps up the boarding ramp: dust that had been disturbed. Not freshly—within days, maybe hours—but disturbed, and then left to settle again, like a room where someone had once stood very still for a long time.

She stopped at the top of the ramp and let her hand rest on the hull plating. Cold. Not station-cold, not the ambient chill of an unpowered bay, but deep cold, the cold of something that had been drained and sealed and forgotten on purpose. Her breath fogged faintly in the light of her shoulder lamp.

Behind her, at the bottom of the ramp, Inspector-General Oduya's team was still arranging itself with the ceremonial slowness of men who liked to be seen arriving. She could hear them: the clatter of hard cases, the murmur of protocol, Oduya's voice pitched exactly loud enough to carry—"Log everything, Lieutenant, everything, the Authority wants a full material record before we release findings." Findings. As if the findings weren't already written in some office two decks above the harbor master's floor, waiting only for the paperwork of discovery to catch up.

Sefa had been given the assignment eleven days ago, and in eleven days she had learned one thing with certainty: nobody above her wanted the Vesperine examined. They wanted it *processed*. There was a difference, and the difference was the width of her career, which had already been cut down once for noticing such things.

"Idris." Oduya's voice, closer now. "You're on point. Sweep for hazards, then hold at the airlock until we're suited."

"Yes, sir."

"And Idris." He paused beside her, a broad silhouette against the bay's work lights, his face unreadable in the way that powerful faces learn to be. "This is a records anomaly. A ghost in the manifest system. Nothing more. Don't make it into anything."

"No, sir," she said, because she had learned that too—the small obedient sentence, the one that cost nothing now and everything later.

He moved off. She stood alone at the top of the ramp, hand still on the cold hull, and thought about a name.

*Paloma Blackwood.*

She'd pulled the file the second night, after her shift, in a records terminal nobody monitored because it sat in a corner of the customs annex where the heating vent rattled and everyone assumed the terminal was broken. It wasn't broken. The Blackwood file was right where it should have been, thin as a pamphlet, closed fourteen years ago with the tidy finality of a case nobody had ever intended to reopen.

*District 9 fire. Structural collapse during containment operations. Eleven fatalities, including Marisol Blackwood, resident. Surviving minor child, Paloma Blackwood, unaccounted for; presumed relocated to kinship care off-station.* Presumed. The word sat there like a loose rivet. No follow-up. No verification. A child erased from a burning district with the same administrative stroke that erased the district itself, and no one had ever asked what happened to him, because asking would have meant admitting there was something to find.

Sefa had sat in the rattling corner for a long time that night, reading the incident report line by line, feeling the old familiar pressure behind her sternum—the one that had gotten her demoted, the one her mentor Callas had once called "your disease, Sefa, you keep *caring* past the point of usefulness."

The fire report said accelerant residue consistent with industrial solvent. The structural analysis said load-bearing members showed thermal failure patterns inconsistent with the official burn timeline. Two technicians had signed off on both documents. One of them, she'd checked, had been promoted twice since.

She'd closed the file. She hadn't been able to close it in her head.

Now she stepped through the Vesperine's airlock, and the ship swallowed her lamp light whole.

* * *

Inside, the dark had texture. That was the first thing she understood about the ship—that its darkness wasn't empty but layered, strata of silence laid down over years, and her boots broke through them one layer at a time. The corridor beyond the airlock ran straight toward the bow, ceiling panels hanging by their clips, conduit bundles draped like severed tendons. Emergency strips along the floor edges glowed faint amber when her weight passed over them, waking just enough to show her the next few meters and dying again behind her.

Her suit's environmental readout ticked quietly in her ear: interior temperature four degrees Celsius and falling toward equilibrium, atmosphere trace-oxygen, non-breathable without filtration. Fine. Her mask handled that. What it didn't handle was the sense—not fear, nothing so honest as fear—that the ship was listening.

She swept forward methodically, the way she'd been trained, hazard markers logged aloud into her recorder because protocol demanded voice confirmation even when no one would ever listen to it. Coolant lines intact. Cargo restraints engaged. Deck plating sound. At the junction to the central hold, her lamp caught something that stopped her mid-sentence.

A scuff mark in the dust. Boot-shaped. Recent enough that its edges were still crisp.

And beside it, on the bulkhead, the faintest shine where a hand had gripped a stanchion, wearing the oxide film smooth.

Someone had walked this corridor within the last day. Someone breathing filtered air, moving carefully, alone.

Sefa stood very still. Protocol said withdraw and report. Protocol said unknown intruder aboard a vessel under Authority investigation constitutes a security event requiring immediate escalation. Protocol had a whole beautiful choreography for this moment, and she knew every step of it, and she also knew—with the certainty of a woman who had watched protocol get bent around a falsified inspection stamp while she stood in the room holding the real numbers—exactly how this would play out upstairs. Oduya would seal the ship. The intruder would be written up as salvage-scavenger, trespasser, vermin. The investigation would become a security action, and security actions didn't produce public findings. Security actions produced sealed files.

Whatever was on this ship that someone had walked so carefully to reach would vanish behind a classification code, and Sefa Idris would spend the rest of her career knowing she had turned around at the junction.

Her hand hovered over her comm. Behind her, far back down the corridor, she could hear the distant percussion of Oduya's team beginning their approach, suits clanking, voices bouncing off metal.

She keyed her comm to the private channel and said, clearly: "Boarding party is forty meters out and closing. You have maybe four minutes."

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight.

"I know you're here," she said. "The dust told me. And whoever you are, you came looking for something the Authority is about to bury. So either run—and they'll catch you in a dead ship with one exit—or show me why I should be slow reporting what I've found."

Nothing. Ten seconds. Fifteen. Somewhere deeper in the ship, a coolant line ticked as it contracted, and the sound was enormous in the dark.

Then, from the black mouth of the central hold, a voice—low, roughened, controlled the way a man controls a voice when he's decided panic is a luxury: "You're the one they sent ahead."

Not a question. Which meant he'd been watching them board. Which meant he'd had time to leave and hadn't taken it.

"Yes," Sefa said.

"They gave the sweep to a demoted inspector." A pause. "That's not trust. That's deniability. If anything goes wrong down here, it was your error, not theirs."

The words landed harder than they should have, because they were true, and because she had assembled the same arithmetic herself in the annex office eleven days ago and filed it under *things I am not paid to think*. "Four minutes," she said instead. "Convince me."

A soft sound in the hold—a foot shifting on deck plate. Then: "Come see. Or don't. But if you call them in first, you'll never hear it. They'll strip the hold and classify the contents before the hour's out, and whatever this ship has been carrying will die in a vault with the people who burned District 9 still standing at their windows watching it burn."

*District 9.* He said the number like a wound he'd stopped dressing.

Sefa's heart was beating somewhere up near her throat. Every instinct trained into her across nine years of service said: *unknown hostile, unsecured environment, withdraw.* Every instinct that had survived those nine years unsuppressed said: *he knows things the file doesn't say, and he chose to stay and say them to you.*

She stepped into the hold.

* * *

The central cargo bay of the Vesperine was a cathedral of stillness. Her lamp swept across rack after rack of shipping cradles, hundreds of them, bolted in ranks that receded into darkness beyond her beam's reach—and in each cradle, nested in foam and restraint webbing, a memory-shard canister. Industrial grade. Archive housings, the kind used for judicial record storage, each one stamped with a retention seal that had been broken and resealed by hand.

Thousands of shards. Maybe tens of thousands. Enough recorded testimony to fill a tribunal hall for a decade.

"Oh," she said, and the syllable came out of her small and involuntary, the sound of a woman seeing the size of something she'd only imagined.

"You see it," the voice said, closer now, off to her left. "Good. Because in about three minutes a man who answers to Oduya is going to walk into this room, look at all of this, and tell the harbor authority it's decommissioned data stock awaiting certified destruction. I've read his predecessor's reports. They always say that. *Awaiting destruction.* It's the most efficient lie the Authority ever built—everything true gets scheduled for erasure, and the schedule makes it legal."

A shape resolved at the edge of her light. A man, tall, lean in a worn enviro-rig with the filtration unit scavenged-looking, hood back, face half-lit. Younger than the file had made her expect. Eyes that held the particular exhausted steadiness of someone who had been afraid for so long that fear had worn down into something functional.

Paloma Blackwood. Unaccounted for; presumed relocated. Standing in a dead ship full of buried testimony, holding a single shard canister in both hands like a man holding water in a desert.

"Step into the light," Sefa said. Her hand rested on her sidearm, not drawing it, and she noticed that she was keeping it undrawn deliberately, performing restraint for herself as much as for him. "Slowly."

He did. And up close he looked worse than tired—he looked like grief that had learned logistics. His gaze went to her badge, to the rank tab that marked her as what she was, and something in his expression tightened, braced for the arrest that protocol owed him.

"Sefa Idris," he read off her chest. "Customs Enforcement, Grade Four." A humorless breath. "Demoted from Three, if the service gossip I bought last month was accurate. For refusing to sign an inspection you didn't perform."

"That's not public record."

"Neither am I." He lifted the shard slightly. "But here's the thing about us, Officer Idris. We're both entries somebody else wrote. You got a file that says you failed. I got a manifest that says I own this ship—a ship decommissioned when I was nine years old. Somebody built me a paper history, and I've spent seven years living inside it thinking it was mine." His voice stayed level, but she heard the strain under it, wire under cloth. "You want to know what's actually in these canisters? Not contraband. Not entertainment stock. Testimony. Judges. Witnesses. Victims. Everything the Authority collected during the District 9 proceedings and then buried when the verdict came out wrong."

"There were no District 9 proceedings," Sefa said. "The fire was ruled accidental. There was no trial."

"That," Paloma Blackwood said, "is because the trial happened, and they lost, and then they burned the courtroom along with the district. And my mother was standing in it."

The hold was silent except for the tick of cooling metal and, far away, the approaching clangor of the boarding party. Thirty seconds of argument flared and died inside Sefa—*withdraw, report, seal, protocol*—and then died completely, because she had heard that sentence, and there was no protocol on any bookshelf in Halcyon Gate that covered hearing it.

"Show me," she heard herself say.

He hesitated—one heartbeat, weighing her, deciding—and then turned to the playback port set into the base of the nearest archive rack, a standard judicial interface, and pressed the shard home. The port's status ring lit a patient amber. He fed power from a portable cell, and the ring shifted to green, and a playback indicator began to pulse.

"This is Judge Aurelio Vance-Kettering," said a voice from the small speaker, dry, precise, the voice of a man accustomed to rooms falling quiet when he entered. "Session record, supplementary deposition, District 9 adjudication, sealed under Executive Order 44-C. For the record: I am confirming that the containment operation authorized for the ninth district on the night of the fire was not a containment operation. It was a purge. I ordered the acceleration of the burn timeline myself, on instruction from the Colonial Executive, in order to destroy the evidentiary record held in the district courthouse annex. Casualty projections were reviewed and accepted. Marisol Blackwood's testimony was identified as the primary target of the—"

"*Stop,*" Sefa said.

The recording stopped. Amber ring. Silence.

Her hands were shaking. She looked down at them and made them stop, the way she'd been taught, the way she'd always been able to, and found that this time the teaching wasn't reaching the muscle. Marisol Blackwood. The name from the thin file, from the casualty list, from the line she'd read eleven times in a rattling corner of the annex: *including Marisol Blackwood, resident.* Resident. As if a woman whose testimony was worth burning a district for had been nothing but an address.

"She was going to testify," Sefa said. It wasn't a question either. She was starting to understand that this whole night was going to be made of things that weren't questions.

"She was the testimony," Paloma said. "Everything else was decoration."

Down the corridor, the boarding party's lights appeared—faint, bobbing, two minutes out, maybe less. Sefa felt the moment arrive the way she'd felt it once before, in an inspection bay six years ago, with a falsified stamp on a table in front of her and Callas's hand almost gentle on her shoulder: *Sign it, Sefa. The numbers are wrong, yes, but the institution is bigger than the numbers.* She had refused then, and it had cost her a grade, a mentor, and any illusion she'd carried about the service. She recognized this moment as that moment's older, larger sibling.

If she reported now—if she withdrew and called it in—Oduya's team would flood this hold, and by nightfall the canisters would be logged as hazardous data stock, and by week's end they would be ash in a certified incinerator, and Judge Vance-Kettering's dry precise voice would never have existed for anyone but her. She would carry thirty seconds of confession in her head for the rest of her life, and no tribunal anywhere would ever hear it, because she had followed procedure at the exact crossroads where procedure was the weapon.

"Officer," Paloma said quietly. He'd seen it land; she could tell he'd seen it land. "I need about twenty minutes to pull the primary archive cores. After that, you can arrest me, shoot me, or forget you ever saw me—I genuinely don't care anymore which. But if your people walk in here in the next two minutes, none of us gets a choice."

Every alarm she owned was ringing. Duty, training, self-preservation, the whole choir. And beneath the choir, steady as a coolant pump, the thing Callas had called her disease: *caring past the point of usefulness.*

Usefulness. She almost laughed. Six years of being demoted for useless caring, and here, in a dead ship at the bottom of a cover-up, it was suddenly the only useful thing in the room.

"Airlock junction, aft of the hold," she said, already moving, pulling a maintenance override chip from her kit. "There's a secondary access through the coolant gallery—it's flagged for decommission work, no sensor coverage. I can log the hold as structurally compromised, delay their entry by claiming atmospheric instability. It buys you fifteen minutes, maybe eighteen." She turned and looked at him, and let him see her face, and saw him understand what it was costing. "After that, I report what I reported. And Paloma Blackwood—you will give me a copy of everything in this hold. Not for you. For a tribunal. One that actually happens."

Something moved through his expression that she'd never seen on a fugitive's face: relief, and suspicion, and underneath both, cautiously, hope.

"Deal," he said.

She keyed her comm. "Oduya, actual. Idris. Hold section shows atmospheric degradation—coolant breach signature, possible oxidizer contamination. Recommend delay of entry pending atmospheric scrub. Say again, recommend delay."

A pause. Static. Then Oduya's voice, irritated, unhurried: "How long?"

"Fifteen minutes, sir. Maybe twenty."

Another pause, longer. In it, Sefa stood in the amber dark of a murdered ship, listening to a judge's confession still warm in her ears, and understood that she had just crossed a line drawn so finely she'd only feel the cut later.

"Twenty minutes, Idris," Oduya said finally. "Then we come in regardless."

"Acknowledged, sir."

She killed the channel. Behind her, in the dark of the hold, she heard Paloma Blackwood begin to move among the racks, quick and quiet, a man gathering up the dead.

And she thought, with a clarity that felt almost like falling: *There's no coming back up this ramp the person I was going down it.*
