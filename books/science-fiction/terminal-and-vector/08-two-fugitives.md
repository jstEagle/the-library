## 8. Two Fugitives

**Paloma**

She hadn't moved her hand from her sidearm in eleven minutes. He knew because he'd been counting, the way you count anything when the alternative is thinking.

They sat across the Vesperine's mess table—a scarred slab of composite bolted to a deck that still held the cold of wherever the ship had been sleeping for seven years—between them a single lamp Sefa had clipped to the bulkhead, its light pooling yellow and small, like something they were both afraid would be taken away.

The shard was still in the playback port behind him. It had gone quiet thirty seconds ago, and neither of them had reached for it. The judge's voice hung in the recycled air anyway. *Authorize the sweep. Full district. No survivors of record.* Paloma could have recited it now. Could have recited it in his sleep, in his grave, in whatever came after both.

"You're not arresting me," he said. Not a question. He'd watched her decide not to, watched her hand drift off her weapon like a woman setting down something heavy she'd carried too long.

"I should." Her voice was low, roughened by the ship's dry air. "You're in possession of stolen judicial property. Classified evidence. You broke into a sealed bay with a forged credential—" She stopped. Pressed her thumb against the table's edge as if testing whether it was real. "And I'm sitting here telling you my chain of evidence out loud."

"Because you already know what happens if you file this."

"I know what happened the last time I filed something true." She said it flatly, without self-pity, which was somehow worse. The lamp buzzed. Somewhere aft, the hull ticked as it warmed toward ambient—the ship waking up around them like an animal remembering it had a body.

Paloma turned his cup a quarter-rotation on the table. Water. He'd offered her water because it was all he had, and she'd taken it, and that small transaction sat between them like a treaty neither had signed.

"Then we understand each other," he said. "You can't take me in. I can't run—not yet, not through Halcyon Gate with the cordon they'll throw up the moment your superiors find this bay empty. Which means for the next few hours, Officer Idris, you and I are the same problem wearing two uniforms."

Her eyes came up. They were dark, unamused, and sharper than any inspector's scanner he'd ever bribed. "I don't wear your uniform."

"No," he agreed. "Yours still mostly fits. That's what makes this hard."

* * *

**Sefa**

The shard playback was still under her skin. Thirty seconds—that was all he'd given her before his finger covered the port, and thirty seconds had been enough to rearrange the architecture of her career like a quake rearranges a skyline.

Judge Aurelio Vance-Kettering. She knew the name. Every customs officer in contested space knew the name; his seal was on half the interdiction orders Sefa had spent ten years enforcing. And in those thirty seconds he had spoken in the flat, administrative voice of a man reading a grocery list, ordering the deaths of four thousand people in a district whose only crime had been hosting a witness.

Sefa had grown up believing the service was a wall between people like that and everyone else. She'd learned, expensively, that it was more often a curtain. She'd pulled one thread of it herself—refused to falsify one inspection—and the institution had demoted her, reassigned her, and waited with bureaucratic patience for her to either learn or leave.

Now a smuggler with a dead family and a hold full of ghosts was offering her another thread.

"The archive," she said. "All of it. Not copies. Not excerpts you've curated. The full manifest of shards, catalogued, with provenance intact so it holds up in front of a tribunal."

"And there it is." Paloma smiled without warmth. "Your tribunal."

"It's real. It exists. The Oversight Conclave has standing authority to convene—"

"The Oversight Conclave," he said, tasting each word, "whose members were appointed by the same colonial administration that razed my district. Whose last three investigations closed with findings of *tragic accident*. You want me to hand the only copy of the truth to the people who buried it, and trust them to dig it back up."

"I want you to hand it to *me*, and trust *me*."

"That's worse. I've known you for forty minutes."

"Thirty-eight," she said, "and you broke into my crime scene, so let's not pretend you came here to make friends."

The lamp flickered—some fault in the clip, or the ship's dying power cells—and in the stutter of shadow his face changed, just slightly, the competence cracking to show the grief underneath. She'd seen that look before, on witnesses, on survivors, on the rare honest ones. It was the look of someone who had already lost everything negotiable and was now negotiating with the remainder.

"Why do you care?" he asked suddenly. "Not the institutional answer. You've been demoted for caring once already. Why burn twice?"

Sefa looked at her hands. They were steady. They had been steady through the tribunal that ended her mentorship, through the hearing where her own reports were read back to her in a tone usually reserved for confessions. Steady hands were the only inheritance her father had left her, and she guarded them jealously.

"Because I stood in a courtroom once," she said, "and told the truth, and watched the truth get filed. And I told myself the system would catch up eventually. That patience was part of integrity." She met his eyes. "It's been six years, Blackwood. Your mother has been dead six years, and the system hasn't caught up. I'm done being patient on its behalf."

* * *

**Paloma**

His mother's name in her mouth did something to the air.

He kept his face still—he'd had seven years of practice keeping his face still—but inside, where the shards lived, something shifted its weight. This woman had said *your mother* the way people say names at funerals: carefully, knowing the ground is soft.

"All right," he said. "Terms."

Sefa straightened slightly. Negotiation was a language she apparently spoke too; everyone in contested space did, they just used different dialects. Hers was probably clauses and citations. His was coolant lines and bribed inspectors.

"One," he said. "You get me past the cordon. Transponder codes, inspection windows, whatever you can feed me from inside. When they find this bay disturbed, they'll lock down Halcyon Gate within the hour, and I need to be through it before the lock becomes a net."

"Two," she said, picking up the count without hesitation. "Afterward, the full archive comes to me. Unedited. Catalogued. With your testimony attached—you were there, you found it, you can authenticate the recovery chain."

"My testimony attaches my name to it permanently."

"Yes."

"You understand what you're asking. There's no version of this where I stay invisible. Vane's people, the Authority's enforcers, every bounty board from here to the Rim—they'll all have my face by the end of the month."

"I understand exactly what I'm asking." Her voice didn't waver, and he hated her a little for it, and respected her a great deal more. "I'm asking you to trade the thing you value most for the thing you want most. That's not extortion, Blackwood. That's just arithmetic."

He laughed—one short, involuntary sound that surprised them both. "Arithmetic. My mother taught mathematics. She'd have liked you." He hadn't meant to say it aloud. It escaped before the gate came down, and he saw her register it, file it, and—this was the strange part—not use it. Just let it sit there between them, a small piece of cargo neither had declared.

"Three," she said, gentler now, or pretending to be. "Nobody dies who doesn't have to. Not inspectors doing their jobs. Not dockhands. If your escape costs lives, the deal's off and I take my chances with my conscience and a cell."

"And if mine does?"

"Then I grieve efficiently and move on," she said, and this time the dryness was deliberate, a door opened to show him she had one. "But I don't think you're that man. You've had a loaded shard in a playback port for twenty minutes and you haven't once threatened me with it. A smarter fugitive would have."

"A smarter fugitive wouldn't need you."

"True." She almost smiled. It transformed her face and then vanished, rationed. "Four. We write nothing down. No recordings, no messages. From this moment until we're clear of the cordon, this conversation never happened and I was never aboard. If you're caught, I arrest you convincingly. If I'm caught, you never met me."

"And if one of us sells the other out?"

She considered the question honestly, which he found more unsettling than any lie would have been. "Then we were always going to," she said. "Better to find out now than after the jump window."

* * *

**Sefa**

They shook on it, and her hand remembered how to be steady even while the rest of her felt like a hull breach.

His grip was calloused in the specific pattern of someone who worked with his fingers in machinery—coolant lines, she'd guessed earlier, and the residue on his cuffs confirmed it. A careful man. A tired one. Seven years of invisibility had worn grooves in him the way water wears stone, and she recognized the erosion because she'd been eroding too, just slower, in better light.

She pulled out her slate and began, from memory, sketching the cordon protocols that would go live the moment her superiors discovered the Vesperine's bay seal broken. Gate One would close first—it always did, the reflex of administrators protecting the paperwork rather than the perimeter. Gate Three would stay open longest because its scanner array had been down for maintenance for two months and nobody senior wanted to sign the requisition. Corruption's gift to the desperate: the rot always left gaps.

"Gate Three," she said. "Window opens at 0400 station time for scheduled ore haulers. Forty minutes, maybe less if they rotate the watch early."

"Forty minutes through a scanner array that's down?" His eyebrows rose. "That's not a gap, that's an invitation. They'll have eyes on it anyway."

"They'll have *one* set of eyes on it. Mine, if I put in for the shift." She heard herself say it and felt the floor tilt slightly beneath her career. Volunteering for Gate Three watch during the exact window a fugitive slipped through—any competent investigator would read that signature in seconds. "Which means my badge ID gets logged at that gate, during that window, connected to whatever transponder you're flying."

"So you're burned the moment we clear."

"If we clear. Yes." She said it evenly, but her pulse had picked up, traitorous, audible in her own ears. Six years of careful, diminished, honest mediocrity, and she was about to spend it all in a forty-minute window on a gate nobody maintained. "Which is why the archive matters. If I'm going to be burned, I intend to be burned holding something worth the fire."

Paloma studied her across the lamp light for a long moment. Then he reached into his jacket and produced a shard—not the judge's, something older, its casing scuffed, labeled in faded hand-print: *B. BLACKWOOD — TESTIMONY, PARTIAL.*

Sefa went very still. "Is that—"

"Evidence," he said quietly. "From the recovery. I'm showing you it exists so you know what you're trading for. I'm not playing it for you." His jaw tightened. "Some things aren't for strangers. Even allies. Especially allies."

"I understand." And she did, more than he probably wanted her to. Everyone kept one door locked. Hers was a tribunal chamber six years ago; his was a district that no longer existed except in stolen memory.

She looked down at her slate, at the cordon map taking shape in her handwriting, and made herself say the last hard thing.

"When we're through the gate," she said, "you give me the archive. Not after you're safe. Not after some private reckoning you've scheduled with yourself. At the first neutral port, in person, full catalogue. Because the second we're clear, every incentive you have is to disappear with it, and I will have spent my entire life chasing you on faith."

"And what do I have?" he asked. "What stops you from taking the archive and filing it into the same drawer where they filed my mother?"

"Nothing," Sefa said. "Same as nothing stops me from shooting you right now and recovering the evidence solo. Trust isn't a guarantee, Blackwood. It's a bet. I'm telling you my odds. You tell me yours."

The lamp hummed. The hull ticked. Out beyond the dead storage bay's cracked viewport, Halcyon Gate turned its slow wheel of lights, oblivious, beautiful, corrupt—a city of windows with no idea what was about to come through them.

"0400," Paloma said finally. "Gate Three. What transponder do you want me flying?"

* * *

**Paloma**

She gave him a code string written on paper—actual paper, torn from the margin of an inspection form, because paper didn't sync, didn't log, didn't testify. *Ore hauler Meridian Dawn, registry Kestrel-Free Holdings.* A shell company, naturally; half the legitimate traffic in contested space belonged to shells. The Authority didn't mind ghosts in its registries. It only minded ghosts that talked.

He memorized the string and burned the paper in the lamp flame, watching the code curl into ash, feeling the strangeness of the hour settle over him fully for the first time. Seven years of running alone, and now his survival depended on a woman whose job description included catching him, whose career he was about to incinerate, and whose handshake had been steadier than his own heartbeat.

"You could still walk away," he said as she gathered her slate. "Report the bay disturbance anonymously. Let the cordon happen. Keep your badge, your pension, your clean record of clean failures."

"I tried walking away once." She clipped the lamp off, and the dark rushed in around the memory of the judge's voice. "It didn't take."

At the hatch she paused, silhouetted against the corridor's emergency strip-lighting, and looked back at him—or past him, at the playback port where his mother's voice slept in silicon.

"0400," she said. "Don't be late, Blackwood. Windows like this only open once."

Then she was gone, footsteps fading down the Vesperine's cold spine, and Paloma stood alone in the dark with a ship full of the dead and a rendezvous with the living.

He touched the shard in his jacket pocket—his mother's partial testimony, warm from his body heat—and made himself a promise he wasn't sure he could keep: that if this bargain held, if they actually cleared the cordon together, he would find the courage to play her that shard. Not as evidence. As introduction.

Outside the viewport, Halcyon Gate's lights wheeled on, and somewhere in their rotation, unseen, the first watch rotations of the cordon were already beginning to move.
