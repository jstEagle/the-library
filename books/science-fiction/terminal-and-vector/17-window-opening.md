## 17. Window Opening

The relay resolved out of the dark the way a lighthouse resolves out of fog—first as nothing, then as a rumor, then as a fact too large to deny. Sefa saw it before the nav display named it: a spindle of gray metal turning slowly against the starless field of dead space, its running lights dead, its docking collar open like a mouth that had forgotten how to speak. Nine days of coasting had stripped her of the habit of seeing anything at all. Now there was something to see, and her eyes hurt with it.

"Six hours and eleven minutes," Paloma said from the pilot's chair. His voice was rough from disuse; they had spoken less each day, conserving words the way they'd conserved fuel, conserved air, conserved everything except the shards, which they had spent freely, playing them in the long evenings like families play old recordings of the dead. "Window closes at 0400 station-relative."

"I can read a clock," Sefa said, but without edge. Somewhere in the last week the sharpness between them had worn down into something else—not softness exactly, but a shared tolerance, two blades sheathed in the same drawer.

She unbuckled and floated to the viewport. The relay grew as they approached, resolving into detail: solar arrays furred with micrometeorite scarring, a comms spine crusted with antenna housings, the whole structure canted slightly off-axis, as if it had been leaning away from its work for years. No traffic. No transponders. The relay served the jump points at the edge of contested space, and contested space had stopped sending ships through it long ago. It existed now mostly as an argument for budgets no one wanted to make.

"It's still on the registry," she said. "Barely. Class-C relay, automated operations, manual override provision." She recited it the way she'd once recited inspection codes in her head during sleepless nights—a rosary of regulations, worn smooth by repetition. "Which means somewhere inside there's a console that will accept a live credential."

"And yours is still live."

It wasn't quite a question. She answered it anyway. "Suspended credentials don't expire. They just wait." She turned from the viewport. "Like me."

Paloma didn't smile, but something moved at the corner of his mouth, a tectonic shift he chose not to let become an expression. He'd gotten good at that—the almost-smile, the almost-touch, all his feelings held at approach speed and never granted clearance. She understood it now. She'd spent nine days learning the geography of a man who had built himself out of checkpoints.

"Docking in forty minutes," he said. "You should eat."

"I'll eat after."

"You said that yesterday."

"And I was right yesterday."

He let it go. That was another thing she'd learned about him: he knew which arguments were worth the oxygen.

* * *

The Vesperine's docking clamps engaged with a sound Sefa felt in her teeth more than heard—a deep structural groan, metal remembering pressure after years of neglect. The relay's interior was cold in the way abandoned things are cold: not hostile, just indifferent, the temperature of a place where no body had bothered to generate heat. Their breath fogged in the access corridor. Their boots rang on grating that hadn't been walked since before either of them could name.

The relay's spine stretched ahead of them, lit only by their suit lamps and the occasional emergency strip that stuttered awake as their movement triggered ancient motion sensors. Dust—or rather the absence of it; dead space was a vacuum, and vacuum kept things clean—made every surface look new and dead at once. A museum of function. Everything here worked, probably. Nothing here mattered, anymore, to anyone but them.

The broadcast core was three decks up, behind a door that took Paloma four minutes and a stolen maintenance code to open. Inside, the room was small, circular, dominated by a single console beneath a dome of reinforced glass through which the stars turned with the station's slow rotation. The console was dark until Sefa laid her palm against the reader, and then it woke with a chime so cheerful it felt obscene.

**MANUAL OVERRIDE PROVISION — AUTHENTICATE**

She stared at the prompt. Her hand stayed where it was.

Behind her, Paloma was feeding the archive into the relay's buffer—the shards slotted one by one into a reader that drank them greedily, progress bars crawling across a secondary screen. The testimony of judges and witnesses and victims, eleven years of buried voices, uploading into a machine that would hold them until someone told it to let go.

"All it needs," Paloma said quietly, "is you."

* * *

She had rehearsed this moment for nine days, and discovered in the moment itself that rehearsal was useless.

Her credential sat in her implant like a coin in a pocket she'd forgotten she owned: **IDRIS, SEFA M. — CUSTOMS SERVICE OF THE HALCYON AUTHORITY — GRADE 4 INSPECTOR (SUSPENDED)**. Suspended pending tribunal. Suspended because she had refused, eighteen months ago, to sign a falsified inspection on a cargo hauler whose holds had been full of people being moved without consent. Suspended because her mentor, Ondrej Vasik, had looked at her across a desk and told her that the service was a river and rivers carry silt and a clean record was a luxury of officers who never touched real water.

She had spent her whole life believing the credential meant something. Not power—meaning. That when she pressed her thumb to a reader, the thing that answered was not just a database but a promise: *this person stood at the gate, and what passed the gate passed because she allowed it.* Every inspector lived inside that promise or drowned. Vasik had drowned and called it swimming. She had refused to swim and called it integrity, and the service had called it insubordination, and both of them had been right.

Now the console waited under her palm, patient as any bureaucracy, asking the only question that had ever mattered:

*Who authorizes this?*

If she authenticated, her name went out with the archive. Not metaphorically—literally, embedded in the transmission header, stamped into every packet, unforgeable, undeniable. **AUTHORIZED BY: IDRIS, S.M., CSHA-4.** Every receiver in contested space would know that a customs officer of the Halcyon Authority had opened the gate. Not a hacker. Not a ghost. One of theirs, using the very instrument of institutional trust to publish the institution's crimes.

They would call it treason. Some would call it heroism, briefly, before the tribunals and the committees got hold of it and sanded it down into procedure. Either way, the name would be hers forever. There was no anonymity in a credential. That was the entire point of a credential—that it could not be disowned.

"They can't take it back," she said aloud. She hadn't meant to speak. "Once I authenticate. Even if they arrest me tomorrow, even if they scrub my record, the transmissions already sent will still carry the header. You can delete a person. You can't delete a signature."

"No," Paloma agreed, from behind her. "That's rather the point."

She turned. He stood by the shard reader with his arms crossed, watching her with an expression she had learned to read in the dark of the Vesperine's cockpit: not impatience. Something worse. Recognition. He knew exactly what she was weighing, because he had weighed it at the storage bay, holding a shard of his mother's voice, deciding whether to become visible.

"You could walk away," he said. "I mean it. I could spoof a credential—badly, slowly, maybe not at all—but if we miss the window, there'll be others. Eventually. You don't owe this archive your name."

"Yes, I do," she said, and was surprised by how steady it came out. "That's the whole point of a credential, Paloma. It's not a key. It's a promise. I made it when I was twenty-two years old, standing in a hall in Meridian with four hundred other recruits, and the oath wasn't 'obey.' The oath was 'the gate does not open unless it should.' And for eighteen months I've been telling myself that refusing to falsify that inspection was enough—that keeping my own hands clean discharged the promise. But a promise you only keep when it costs nothing isn't a promise. It's a preference." She looked back at the console. "This is the gate. And it should open."

The stars turned above them in their slow wheel. Somewhere below, the Vesperine hung in the clamps, cold and patient, full of ghosts.

"There's something else," she said. "Something I haven't said out loud." She made herself turn and face him fully. "When I pulled your file—Blackwood, Paloma, the district purge—I did it because it was my assignment. But I kept pulling. After. Cases the service had closed and shelved and salted. Eleven years of them. I have a private archive of my own, Paloma. Half of what's on those shards corroborates things I found alone in the dark, with no one to tell. If I authenticate this, I'm not just signing your justice. I'm signing mine. Every night I chose the archive over the tribunal. Every case I carried instead of reported." Her breath fogged and faded. "I'm not sacrificing myself for you. I'm finishing something I started a long time ago and was too afraid to complete."

Paloma crossed the room slowly, the way he did everything, as if approaching any point of contact might trigger an alarm. He stopped close enough that she could see the exhaustion carved under his eyes, nine days of dead space written on him like a ledger.

"When I transmitted my mother's shard to myself, just to hear it," he said, "I thought I was stealing something back. But that's not what theft feels like. Theft feels like winning. This felt like—" He searched. "Like paying a debt. To people who can't collect." He glanced at the console. "Whatever happens next, whatever they do to your name—you'll have paid yours."

"That's not comfort."

"No. Comfort's for people who kept their hands clean."

Despite everything—the cold, the clock, the fear coiled tight in her chest—she laughed. It came out ragged, half-frozen, absurd in the little dome of turning stars, and Paloma's almost-smile finally arrived, whole.

* * *

She sat down at the console at 0312, forty-eight minutes to window-close.

The authentication sequence was simple, insultingly simple, the way the most consequential things always are. Credential query. Biometric confirm. Authorization statement, to be spoken aloud, recorded, embedded in the header of every packet that left this machine forever.

She placed her thumb on the reader. The implant woke against her skin, warm as a living thing.

**AUTHENTICATE: IDRIS, SEFA M., CSHA-4 (STATUS: SUSPENDED). WARNING: USE OF SUSPENDED CREDENTIAL CONSTITUTES ACTIVITY OUTSIDE SANCTIONED DUTY. ALL TRANSMISSIONS WILL BE PERMANENTLY ATTRIBUTED. PROCEED?**

The warning scrolled in calm bureaucratic amber, and Sefa Idris, who had spent her whole life inside institutions, who had believed in the gate and the promise and the oath, who had lost her career and nearly her life to the gap between what the Authority said it was and what it did—Sefa Idris read the warning and felt, for the first time in eighteen months, entirely sane.

Duty, she thought. Everyone thinks duty means obedience. But duty is just love wearing a uniform. I loved the promise. I never loved them.

"Proceed," she said.

The biometric sweep ran. The console chimed. And then the authorization statement prompt appeared, cursor blinking, waiting for her voice.

She straightened in the chair. Above her, through the dome, the stars wheeled toward the jump point like witnesses taking their seats.

"This is Inspector Sefa Idris, Customs Service of the Halcyon Authority, Grade Four, suspended, speaking under manual override provision at Relay Kestrel-Nine." Her voice echoed slightly in the small dome, and she let it. Let it be heard. "I am authorizing broadcast of a sealed judicial archive, eleven years suppressed, concerning the Blackwood district purge and related proceedings. I do this under the authority of the oath I swore, which the Authority has broken, and which I have not. The gate opens because it should. Authenticate."

The console accepted it. The buffer locked. Progress bars bloomed across every screen—upload, packetize, address, transmit—and the relay's comms spine, silent for years, began to wake, antenna housings rotating, power flowing out along structures that had forgotten their purpose and remembered it all at once.

Sefa watched her name assemble itself in the transmission header, letter by letter, permanent as a scar: **AUTHORIZED BY: IDRIS, S.M.**

There. Done. Un-deletable. Hers.

Paloma's hand settled on her shoulder—his first unprompted touch in nine days, light as a man setting something down carefully.

"Six minutes to window," he said.

And that was when the relay's ancient proximity alarm woke for the first time in a decade, shrieking through the deck plates, and the tactical display painted a new contact at the system's edge, running dark no longer, burning hard for the relay with a transponder that resolved, letter by letter, into a name they both knew.

Vane.

Six minutes to window. Six minutes for the first packets of the archive to clear the relay's spine. Six minutes for Vane to arrive and try to stop what could no longer be stopped—or die trying.

Sefa didn't move her hand from the reader.

"Let him come," she said. "The gate's already open."
