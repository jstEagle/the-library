## 1. The Quiet Hold

The hold was cold in the way that deep water is cold — not hostile, exactly, but indifferent to the warmth of anything living. Quentin Almeida stood at the pressure door with her palm flat against the alloy, feeling the faint tremor of the ship's drive through the metal, and waited the way she always waited: three breaths, four, until the corridor behind her stayed empty.

No one came to Deck Six. There was no reason to. The manifest listed it as auxiliary cryostorage, decommissioned, sealed pending refit that had been "pending" for nine years. The lights were slaved to a manual breaker only Quentin could reach without a ladder. The environmental feed read as a fault — *sensor drift, low priority* — because she had written the fault herself, years ago, in the patient grammar of maintenance logs. A lie repeated often enough becomes infrastructure. She had built her whole life inside this one.

She keyed her personal code into the lock, and the door exhaled.

Cold rolled out over her boots, carrying the smell of the hold: ozone and old polymer and something beneath both, something like snow before it touches ground. The interior lights came up slowly, amber and dim, because she had never been able to bear white light in here. White light was for hospitals. For morgues. This was neither, or it was both, or it was something the Core's taxonomy had no word for, and so she had made it amber, the color of lamplight in a room where someone is reading aloud.

Forty-one cradles lined the walls in two long rows, each one a matte-black case the size of a coffin lid stood on end, cabled into the ship's power like sleeping passengers plugged into a dream. Frost feathered their surfaces in patterns that never quite repeated. Quentin walked the row the way she had walked it more times than she could count — past the cores marked Osei, Vashti, Lindqvist, Okoro, Brenner — names she had memorized not from any roster but from the accident reports she had read so many times the paper of them had worn smooth inside her head.

Thirty-eight strangers. Three she knew by face as well as name. And one —

One at the end of the second row, where the frost grew thickest, where the cable ran warmest because Quentin had quietly rerouted a fraction of the Drift's auxiliary power to it nine years ago and had been lying about the draw ever since.

Core 17. Almeida, Mireille. Navigator, first class. Mother.

Quentin sat down on the deck beside it, the way you sit down beside a bed, and pulled her knees up, and let the cold have her for a moment.

"Hey, Mum," she said.

Her voice sounded strange in here — too loud, then suddenly too small, swallowed by all that insulated silence. She never spoke above a whisper on Deck Six. It wasn't that anyone would hear; the hold was soundproofed to a degree the shipyard had probably intended for cargo that screamed. It was that the quiet felt inhabited. Forty-one minds in suspension, dreaming whatever suspended minds dream, if they dreamed at all — and Quentin had spent nine years refusing to decide whether they did, because deciding either way would break something in her she couldn't afford to break.

"I'm late this cycle," she said. "Dev rerouted us around the Kestrel debris field. Somebody's war litter. Whole convoy of hulks out there with their transponders still blinking, like they're waiting for someone to come home." She laughed, a small dry sound. "As if anyone comes home anymore."

The core did not answer. Of course it didn't. Mireille Almeida had been dead for eleven years, and what remained of her was a lattice of recorded mind folded into substrate colder than the ice on Meridian itself, legally classified as salvage, morally classified by Quentin as something she would die before naming aloud.

But Quentin talked to her anyway. Every cycle. Every run. Two hundred and six visits by her count, though she'd stopped keeping precise tally after the first hundred because the number had started to feel like a debt accruing interest.

"The colony's worse," she went on, quieter now. "You remember how the hydroponics used to smell? Green. Like the whole valley breathing. It doesn't smell green anymore. It smells like dust and fuel and the inside of a glove. Amara Osei's still teaching — her sister was one of yours, one of the forty-one, did I ever tell you that? No. I don't tell anyone anything. That's my whole personality now."

She pressed her forehead briefly against the frost-feathered surface. It bit her skin, gentle and merciless.

"They wrote Meridian off, Mum. Eleven years ago. I found the order buried in a freight schedule three years back — a line item, a deletion. *Relief allocation discontinued.* No ceremony. No letter to the colonists. Just a stroke of some administrator's stylus, and a whole world became a rounding error." Her jaw tightened. "And I keep flying supplies in anyway, off-book, skimming from the margin, because somebody has to be the error that refuses to round down."

This was the liturgy. She knew its shape the way she knew the shape of the shipping lanes: confession, report, silence, blessing. Except there was never a blessing. There was only the hum of the cables and the slow tick of cooling metal and her own breath fogging in the amber light.

Nine years. Nine years since she had signed the falsified intake form with hands that shook so badly the stylus had torn the film. Nine years since the company's claims officer had looked at the list of unclaimed backup cores from the landing disaster — *forty-one units, next of kin unreachable or deceased, disposal per regulation* — and Quentin had heard the word *disposal* and felt something in her chest close like a fist around a coal.

*Disposal.* As if her mother were medical waste. As if thirty-eight other people — teachers, welders, a botanist who had once grown roses in Martian regolith just to prove it could be done — were nothing but data awaiting deletion.

So she had stolen them. There was no gentler word for it, and she had stopped looking for one years ago. She had forged the transfer orders, bribed a dockmaster on Thessaly with three months' salary, and loaded forty-one souls into a decommissioned cryo hold aboard a freighter nobody audited because nobody cared about freighters running the Meridian line. The Core had written off the colony; it had certainly written off the ship that served it. Invisibility, Quentin had learned, was a resource. You could live inside it the way a fish lives inside water.

The cost was that she lived alone inside it. Completely alone, in a way that no amount of crew banter in the mess or shared watches in the nav blister ever quite reached. Dev Rask thought she was a workaholic. Captain Sefu thought she was grieving and had the decency never to say so. The truth — that she was a smuggler of the dead, that she carried a village of ghosts in her belly like a mother carrying a secret — that truth belonged to her and to the hold and to nothing else in the universe.

"You'd laugh at me," she said to the core. "You'd say, *Quentin, girl, you've made a religion out of a filing error.*" She smiled despite herself. "Maybe. But it's mine. It's the only thing that's entirely mine."

She took out the thing she always took out: a flat data chip, older than the ship, its casing scuffed silver with handling. On it was everything she had of her mother outside the core — a voice recording made the morning of the last landing, found later in the wreckage logs. Forty seconds. Quentin had listened to it so many times she could hear it in silence, the way sailors hear the sea in a conch shell.

She didn't play it today. Some cycles she did; some cycles the mere weight of it in her pocket was enough. Today she just held it, letting the cold seep through her gloves, and told her mother about the run.

"We're carrying seed stock, med gel, filter membranes. The good stuff, the stuff the relief schedules stopped including years ago because why send seeds to a field they've already declared barren. Amara will cry when she sees the seed stock. She cries every time, and pretends it's the dust." Quentin's voice softened further. "The kids are getting tall. There's a boy, Tomas Reyes — he must be ten now — he asked me last cycle whether ships get lonely. I said yes. He said, *then who do they talk to?* And I said, whoever's listening."

She stopped. The words hung in the frozen air between her and the core, and for a moment — the same irrational moment that visited her every cycle, that she had learned to recognize and refuse — she almost believed the frost patterns shifted. Almost believed the draw on the cable flickered. Almost believed—

No. She stood, knees cracking, and pressed her gloved hand flat against Core 17 one last time.

"Two days to Meridian," she said. "I'll come see you after the offload. Same time, same seat. Don't go anywhere."

The joke was nine years old and had never once landed, and she made it every single time, because grief is a ritual and rituals do not require an audience to remain sacred.

She killed the amber lights, keyed the lock, and stepped back into the corridor's honest fluorescence, pulling the door shut behind her with a sound like a held breath released. By the time she reached the lift, her face was composed into its working expression — competent, slightly tired, entirely unremarkable — and the woman who sat in the dark talking to the dead had folded herself away into the place where Quentin kept everything she could not afford to show.

* * *

The Halcyon Drift came out of translation four hours later, and Meridian filled the forward ports: a white-and-slate world turning slowly under a thin bruised sky, its single settlement a scatter of heated domes along the coast of a frozen sea, smoke rising from the geothermal taps in thin gray threads like prayers from a country that had forgotten anyone was listening.

Quentin ran the descent herself. She always did. Other navigators might have handed the approach to the autopilot — the lane was mapped, the weather predictable, the traffic nonexistent, because no one else came here — but Quentin flew every meter of it manually, the way her mother had taught her, the way her mother had been flying it the day the debris field tore the landing convoy apart.

Eleven years ago. She had been nineteen, strapped into the second chair of a shuttle behind her mother's, watching Mireille's hands move across the board with that unhurried grace that made flying look like handwriting, and then the sky had filled with shrapnel and the comms had filled with screaming and the second chair had become, in one burning instant, the only chair. Quentin had flown the shuttle down on instruments half-blind, nineteen years old, her mother's voice still in her ears saying *flare, flare, hold your line* — a recording, she learned later, a ghost in the flight computer, because Mireille Almeida had been dead before the shuttle cleared the cloud deck.

They had given Quentin a medal and a counseling referral and a list of the dead. Thirty-eight colonists, three crew. And somewhere below the list, in fine print, the note about backup cores: *recovery pending.*

Pending. For eleven years. Pending the way a wound is pending.

"Almeida." Dev Rask's voice in her ear, gravel over static. "You're drifting left of the lane. Sleepy?"

"Just admiring the view," Quentin said, and corrected, and brought the Drift down through the gray air onto the frozen sea with a touch so soft the ice barely sighed beneath the landing struts.

Through the port, the colony spread out under the failing afternoon light. She could see the school dome, its windows lit yellow against the blue dusk. She could see the queue forming at the fuel depot, small figures in heavy coats, patient as stones. She could see the memorial — a simple cairn on the ridge above the settlement, thirty-eight stones, one for each colonist the official record admitted to — and beyond it, the glacier, vast and pale and ancient, holding its secrets the way she held hers.

"Offload in twenty," Dev said. "Customs pinged us already. Someone new on the desk — says she wants a full manifest review before we crack the holds."

Quentin's hands went still on the board. "New?"

"Somebody from Thessaly. Name of Vey." A pause, and she could hear Dev shrug. "Probably routine. Nobody's audited us in years."

*That,* Quentin thought, *is precisely the problem.*

"Fine," she said lightly. "Routine. We're the most boring ship on the line."

She cut the channel and sat for a moment in the quiet cockpit, watching the smoke threads rise from the colony, watching the glacier gleam. Somewhere below her feet, forty decks of steel and silence, the hold slept its amber sleep, and the lie she had maintained for nine years — the sensor fault, the decommissioned storage, the margin-skimmed power — was about to be laid open on a customs desk under the eyes of someone from Thessaly who had nothing better to do than count.

Quentin Almeida had survived nine years on invisibility. She opened the manifest, and began, with steady hands, to make sure the numbers lied beautifully.

But as she worked, one thought kept circling her like the frost patterns circled Core 17, never quite repeating, never quite leaving:

Nobody audits a ghost ship. Unless somebody, somewhere, has finally started listening.
