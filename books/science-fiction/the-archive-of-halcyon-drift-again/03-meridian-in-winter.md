## 3. Meridian in Winter

The cold came in through the seams of things.

That was what Amara Osei had learned in her eleven years on Meridian — that cold was not a wall you could see or brace against, but a patient infiltration. It came in through the hairline fracture above the classroom door, through the gap where the window frame had settled a half-centimeter out of true, through the soles of boots and the cuffs of sleeves and the small unguarded places between a child's scarf and a child's chin. It did not announce itself. It simply arrived, the way bad news arrived on this planet: quietly, officially, and always later than it should have.

She stood at the front of Room 4 of the Meridian Consolidated School with her hands behind her back so her students would not see them shaking, and she watched the thermometer by the door do something thermometers were not supposed to do.

It was going down.

Not fast. The grid had failed three times before, and she knew its grammar now — the way the temperature would hold for an hour after the lights dimmed, as if the system were gathering itself for one last act of dignity, and then begin its slow slide. Fourteen degrees. Thirteen-point-five. The number ticked like a clock counting toward something no one had voted for.

"Teacher," said Priya Nkemi from the second row, "the light's doing the thing again."

"I see it, Priya."

"Is it the thing like last month? Or the other thing?"

"The other thing," Amara said, because there was no honest answer that was also a kind one, and she had made her peace long ago with choosing kindness over honesty in matters of infrastructure. "Coats on, everyone. Under-desks. You know the drill."

They did know the drill. That was perhaps the saddest sentence in the whole lexicon of Meridian — *they know the drill* — and Amara carried it around inside her chest like a stone she had swallowed years ago and never quite digested. Twenty-three children, ages six to twelve, moving with the practiced efficiency of people three times their age. Coats unhooked from pegs. Blankets pulled from the emergency bin that was supposed to be replenished quarterly and had been replenished twice since the war began, both times by the colony's own hands, because the supply manifests from the Core listed Meridian's requisitions as *deferred*, and *deferred*, in the language of the colonial administration, was a word that meant *never* but without the discourtesy of saying so.

The Halcyon Drift had come two weeks ago. It always came — that was the one mercy of the schedule, the one thread of constancy in a tapestry otherwise unraveling. Quentin Almeida, the navigator, had stood in the cargo bay doorway while the loaders worked, and Amara had asked her, the way she asked every cycle, whether there was any word about the heating couplings they'd requested fourteen months ago.

And Quentin had looked at her with those gray eyes that seemed to hold some private weather of their own, and had said, "There's always a word, Ms. Osei. Whether anyone says it out loud is another question."

Amara had thought about that sentence for two weeks. She was thinking about it now, watching thirteen degrees become twelve.

* * *

By midday the classroom had organized itself into the shape that cold dictated: desks pushed together at the center of the room, away from the exterior walls, blankets pooled over knees, bodies packed close enough that the children's collective warmth became a small stubborn economy of its own. Amara had learned this trick from her grandmother, who had grown up in a place called Accra in a century Amara had only ever seen in archives — that heat shared is heat multiplied, that a room full of touching elbows can outlast a room full of machines.

The youngest ones slept in the afternoon, which was fine; sleep burned less than fidgeting. But the older ones stayed awake with the particular wide-eyed vigilance of children who had learned to read the behavior of adults the way sailors read weather. They knew that if Ms. Osei kept talking, everything was probably all right. And so Amara kept talking.

"Where were we?" she asked.

"The ice city," said Tomas Reyes immediately, from the front. Tomas was ten, thin as a radio antenna, with the kind of attention that seemed to cost him something physical — he leaned into stories the way other children leaned into wind. "The one under the glacier. With the doors."

"Right. The ice city." She hadn't planned to return to it today; she had a lesson on fractional arithmetic queued up in her head, fractions being one of the few luxuries of abstraction a failing colony could still afford. But Tomas was looking at her with that lean, and the little ones were stirring, and arithmetic could wait until the grid came back. Stories, she had found, generated their own warmth. Not literally — she'd checked, once, out of desperation, holding the thermometer near a particularly engrossing telling of *The Mouse Bride* and observing no measurable change — but in every way that mattered.

"So," she said, settling onto the edge of her desk, which creaked in the old familiar way, "the survey team has been walking on the glacier for six days, and their instruments have started lying to them."

"Lying how?" Priya demanded. Priya took fiction personally, as a contract whose terms must be honored.

"Lying by omission. Their depth sensors say the ice goes down four hundred meters everywhere except one place, where they say it goes down four hundred meters too, but the numbers come back wrong. Too clean. Like someone filled in the answers ahead of time."

"That's not lying," objected Marcus Wei, who was nine and destined, Amara privately believed, for a career in pedantry or engineering, possibly both. "That's just bad data."

"It's lying if the ice means it," said Tomas, and several children laughed, and Amara felt the laugh move through the bundled bodies like a small current, and thought: *this*. This was the job. Not the curriculum, not the assessments that went up the wire to an administration that no longer read them. This — twenty-three warm bodies in a cold room, arguing about whether ice could lie.

She told the next part slowly, the way she'd learned to, letting the story breathe. The survey team following the anomaly. The storm coming down off the high plateau like a white curtain being drawn across the world. And then — here she lowered her voice, and twenty-three faces leaned in, and even the sleeping ones seemed to sleep differently — the sound beneath their feet. Not cracking. Not shifting. A hum. Organized. Patient. The sound, one of the surveyors would later write in a log nobody ever read, *of something waiting to be asked a question.*

"And then?" Priya whispered.

"Then the storm closed over them, and they had to make a choice. Keep walking for the station, six hours in whiteout conditions — or go down, toward the sound, and shelter in whatever was making it."

"Which did they pick?"

Amara smiled. "That," she said, "is tomorrow's story."

The groan that went up was gratifying, communal, and entirely worth the price of Marcus calling her *deliberately cruel* in a tone of deep betrayal. Outside the frosted windows, the light was doing the thing again — thickening, going amber, the sun dropping behind the ridge early as it always did in deep winter, as if the day itself were trying to get home before dark.

* * *

She walked the older children to the community hall at the day's end, because the school's backup generator was rated for eight hours and the hall's was rated for forty, and because the hall was where the fuel line formed.

The queue stretched from the hall's eastern door down the length of Hab Street and curled out of sight past the water reclamation sheds. Amara counted heads out of habit — a teacher's reflex, counting her charges — and stopped somewhere past two hundred. Two hundred people, standing in weather that would freeze exposed skin in eleven minutes, waiting for rationed fuel pellets that the administration's own schedules said should have been free-flowing since the grid upgrade five years ago. The upgrade that had been announced with great ceremony, funded with great fanfare, and then quietly struck from the relief manifest the same year the war reached the Outer Reach, though Amara had never been able to prove the connection. Documents on Meridian had a way of becoming provisional.

"Ms. Osei." Dev Rask's sister-in-law, Marta, was three places back, wrapped in a coat that had been patched so many times it was less a garment than an archive of its own repairs. "Any word from the ship?"

"They came two weeks ago. Same as always."

"But the couplings—"

"Deferred," Amara said, and heard the word land in Marta's face the way it landed in her own chest every time: that soft administrative thud, the sound of a door closing without slamming.

Marta nodded and looked at her feet. Around them the queue did what the queue always did, which was the most remarkable thing Amara witnessed on any given day, more remarkable than auroras or the ice storms or the twin moons rising over the refinery ruins: it held. People shifted weight from foot to foot. Someone passed a thermos backward down the line without being asked. Old Yusef Okafor, who had not slept a full night since his wife died, told a joke about the governor that was funny precisely because the governor had never once visited, and the laughter moved down the queue like the warmth in her classroom, person to person, a currency that did not appear on any manifest.

Nobody rioted. Nobody cursed the Core, at least not loudly, not today. There was a dignity in the line that Amara both admired and grieved — admired because it was real, chosen, forged daily out of nothing; grieved because dignity was being asked to substitute for justice, and everyone in the queue knew it, and stood anyway.

Her neighbor Ito caught up to her as she peeled off toward her own quarters. He was carrying fuel pellets for his family and, without being asked, pressed a handful into her pocket — six pellets, maybe seven hours of heat.

"You've got kids in your building," he said, when she protested. "Old Mrs. Adeyemi. Take it."

"Ito—"

"Take it, Amara." His breath hung white between them. "We keep each other. That's the whole system now. Everything else is paperwork."

* * *

That night she sat in her apartment under three blankets with a three-pellet heater glowing sullen orange in the corner, grading nothing, because there was nothing to grade against — the Core had stopped returning assessment scores two years ago, and Amara had continued assigning work out of pure professional stubbornness, maintaining standards for an audience of zero. She told herself it was for the children. Some nights she believed it.

The wind came off the glacier in the evenings. Everyone on Meridian knew its voice — the long low exhalation that the colonists' grandparents had named *the sleeper's breath*, back when naming things was still an act of optimism rather than endurance. Tonight it sounded different. She noticed it the way you notice a wrong note in a song you've known all your life: not consciously at first, but as a small prickle along the arms, a sense that the world had cleared its throat.

She got up, crossed to the window, and scraped a porthole clear with her sleeve.

The glacier lay under the moons like a sleeping animal, blue-white, vast, unchanged. Except — she frowned — the wind. The sleeper's breath came *down* off the ice, katabatic, gravity-driven, a fact of physics as reliable as anything on this unreliable world. Tonight it seemed to be doing something else. Tonight it rose, fell, rose again, in a rhythm almost regular. Almost patterned.

Almost, she thought, and then laughed at herself and did not finish the thought, because the thought was *almost like breathing*, and that was the kind of thought you kept to yourself on a colony where the heating grid was failing and the administration had stopped writing back.

She went to bed. She slept badly, and dreamed of nothing she remembered, and woke at 0400 to the sound of the grid restarting — the fans stuttering, the pipes knocking their old familiar knock, heat crawling grudgingly back into the walls.

In the morning, Priya Nkemi was waiting for her outside the school, alone, stamping her feet, her breath streaming.

"Teacher." The girl's eyes were enormous. "I have to tell you about my dream. But you're going to think it's stupid."

"Priya. Have I ever once thought you were stupid?"

"No." A pause. The wind moved through the street, and Amara felt it again, that rhythm, that almost-pattern, rising off the ice beyond the rooftops. "But this one wasn't mine," Priya said. "I don't know whose it was. There was a door under the ice, and something behind it was counting."
