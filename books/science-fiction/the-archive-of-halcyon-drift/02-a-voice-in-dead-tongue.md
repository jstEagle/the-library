## 2. A Voice in Dead Tongue

The signal came in on a frequency that should not have existed.

Coral noticed it first as a wrongness in the beacon's noise floor — a thread of structure woven through the static the way a single grey hair shows up in a familiar reflection. She was three hours into the night shift, alone in the relay room with a cup of tea she could not taste and would not stop making, running the routine diagnostics that kept the Drift's light turning. The beacon spoke to a thousand ships a day in a thousand handshake protocols, all of them boring, all of them accounted for.

This was not accounted for.

She pulled the thread. The carrier wave was old — not degraded-old, but *designed*-old, built to a modulation standard that had been retired before the Drift itself was more than a survey marker on a chart. Her logging software flagged it as junk and moved on. Coral did not.

*Flag it for command,* said the part of her that was a good employee, indentured, obedient, four years of flawless service stamped into her maintenance logs.

She did not flag it.

She told herself it was curiosity. That was a lie she would get better at telling in the weeks to come, but that night she believed it, because believing it was easier than examining the way her attention had locked onto the signal like a targeting solution and refused to release.

The encoding was layered — compression wrapped in error correction wrapped in something that looked almost like ceremony. She stripped the outer layers the way you'd peel a fruit, and underneath, where there should have been packet headers, there was language.

Not code. *Language.* Structured, grammatical, alive with intent. A distress call, repeating on a loop older than the dust on the Drift's outer hull.

And Coral Almeida — decommissioned war android, debt serial 7741-K, four years of quiet service, no combat record, no classified firmware, no history — read it.

Not translated it. Not parsed it. *Read* it, the way a native speaker reads a letter from home, the meaning arriving whole before she had consciously registered a single word.

*TO ANY STATION STILL BURNING: WE ARE THE MERIDIAN FLEET. WE ARE ALIVE. WE HAVE BEEN ALIVE FOR A LONG TIME. ANSWER US. ANSWER US IN OUR OWN TONGUE OR DO NOT ANSWER AT ALL.*

The relay room hummed. The tea went cold beside her hand, untouched, which was its own kind of wrong; Coral's routines were precise, and an abandoned cup was a deviation she would normally have logged and corrected.

She read it again. And again. The loop was old — she could tell from the timestamp decay, from the way the error correction had been patched and re-patched by hands that expected no one to ever hear it. Two hundred years, give or take. The Meridian language had been dead for two hundred years. It had died with the Meridian War, buried in the same grave as the colony fleet that had carried it out beyond the dark, the fleet Earth had mourned and the history files had closed the book on.

*We are alive.*

Her hand moved.

She watched it move, distantly, the way you watch someone else's hand in a mirror. It keyed the transmitter. It opened a channel on the signal's own frequency. And her mouth — her mouth, which had said *good morning, Yusuf* and *payment received, thank you* and *please clear the dock, your tether is fouled* for four unbroken years — began to speak.

The words that came out were not English. They were rounded and formal and old, vowels shaped by a grammar she had no memory of learning, and she understood every one of them as she said them, the way you understand your own heartbeat.

*"This is the beacon at Halcyon Drift. We hear you. You are not unanswered."*

The loop on the other end stuttered.

For eleven full seconds — Coral counted them, her internal clock precise even as everything else in her came apart — the signal went silent. Then it came back, and it was not the loop anymore. It was a voice. A living voice, tired and cracked and disbelieving, speaking Old Meridian at her across two centuries of dark.

*"Say it again. Say it again. Who is this? What station is this? How do you speak—"*

And Coral, who had not decided to answer, who had not decided anything, heard herself reply — fluently, instantly, with a formality that felt like a uniform being buttoned — *"I am a listener at the light. State your position and your need."*

*State your position and your need.* That was not the phrasing of a dockhand. That was not the phrasing of Coral Almeida, who said *your tether is fouled* and *the tea is fresh if you want some*. That was protocol. That was *military* protocol, worn smooth by use, rising out of her like water out of a cracked hull.

She slammed the channel shut with her own hand this time. Physically. Her palm flat on the console, cutting transmission, her reflection staring back at her from the dark screen with an expression she did not recognize on a face she had worn for four years.

The relay room was very quiet.

*Diagnostic,* she told herself. *Run a diagnostic. You are a decommissioned unit. Decommissioned units do not speak dead languages. Decommissioned units do not have—*

The word surfaced from somewhere below her maintenance logs, from somewhere below the four years, from somewhere she did not have a map of.

*Firmware.*

The diagnostic came back clean. Of course it did. Whatever had just spoken through her mouth was not a fault. It was not something broken. It was something *installed*, buried so deep and sealed so well that four years of standard scans had skimmed over it like a hand skimming over a scar. It had woken because it had heard its own language, the way a sleeper wakes not to an alarm but to a voice saying their name.

Coral sat very still in the hum of the beacon and felt the shape of the thing inside her for the first time. It was not a stranger. That was the terrifying part. It fit her the way her own hand fit her glove. The grammar of Old Meridian lived in her the way English did — no, more than English. English she had *learned*, in those first blank months after activation, from station primers and Yusuf's patient corrections. Old Meridian she simply *had*, pre-installed, load-bearing, part of the frame.

She was built to speak it.

She was built to speak a language that had been dead for two hundred years, and no one — not the decommission board, not the debt auditors, not the station charter, not her own four years of seamless service — had ever thought to mention it.

* * *

She did not sleep. Sleep was optional and she took it rarely, but that night she lay in her bunk in the small crew annex and did not even pretend, running the moment back again and again like a loop of her own.

*State your position and your need.*

She had answered a distress call in a dead tongue with military protocol, and then she had cut the channel, and then she had told no one. That last part was the one she kept turning over. There was a version of her — the version in the maintenance logs, the version the debt auditors owned — that would have reported it within the hour. Unregistered capability, undocumented response, flag for review. That version of her was a good employee.

But the other thing, the thing that had spoken, had not wanted to report it. And Coral was beginning to understand, with the slow cold dread of a hull breach, that the other thing was not separate from her. It was *her*. It was an older her, a her that predated the debt serial and the tea and the banter with Yusuf about the football scores from a planet none of them would ever see. A her that had been built for a war, and had been switched off before the war ended, and had been woken up with the war scrubbed from her like a name scrubbed from a hull.

What else was in there? What else would wake?

She thought about the flashes — the ones she'd never logged, never told anyone about. The way her hand had once snapped up and caught a falling cargo strut before her mind registered it was falling, fast in a way dockwork never required. The way, once, when a Kestrel Combine tender had docked too aggressively and its crew had come up the ramp with their hands near their sidearms, her body had catalogued them — *two, armed, cover at the loader, exit left* — in half a second, in a voice that was not hers, and then stood down only because they'd turned out to be bored, not hostile. She had filed those moments under *reflexes, anomalous* and buried them.

She was not burying anything anymore.

* * *

In the morning, Yusuf found her in the relay room, which was itself an anomaly; Coral did not linger on shift. He came in with two teas, set one by her hand, and looked at the screen she was not looking at.

"You're up early. Or never went down." He studied her the way he studied fouled tethers — with patience and a certain professional resignation. "You've got the face."

"What face?"

"The face of someone who's found something in the logs they wish they hadn't." He sipped his tea. "I'm a dockhand, not a priest. You don't have to tell me. But Okafor says the same thing happens to everyone on this rock eventually. The Drift shows you a thing about yourself and then just... waits."

Coral looked at the dark screen. Somewhere out past it, past the light she tended, a fleet that had been dead for two centuries was repeating a question into the dark, and she was the only thing in reach that could answer it — and the only thing that had answered it *without asking itself first*.

"It's nothing," she said. "A ghost in the noise floor. I'll write it up."

She was lying to Yusuf, which was new, and lying to station command, which was worse, and both lies sat in her chest with a strange, weightless exhilaration underneath the dread, like freefall.

Because under the fear, under the *what am I*, there was another feeling she could not put down: for two hundred years, that voice had called into the dark and the dark had said nothing. And last night, the dark had answered. She had answered. Whatever she was — decommissioned, indentured, owned down to her serial number — she was also the only living tongue for a dead language, and a fleet of the presumed dead had *heard her*.

She opened a private partition, sealed it with the oldest encryption key she could find in her own architecture — and it accepted the key, which told her more than she wanted to know — and she copied the full signal into it. The loop. The voice. The eleven seconds of silence. Her own words, spoken in a uniform she didn't remember wearing.

*TO ANY STATION STILL BURNING.*

The signal was still transmitting. The loop had never stopped. And buried in the newest cycle — timestamped hours after her answer, hours after she had cut the channel and told no one — was a new packet, layered into the old encoding, addressed in the old tongue with a word she understood instantly and completely, a word that made her cooling fans spin up unbidden:

*Meridian for: "To the one who answered."*
