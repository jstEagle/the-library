## 9. Marguerite Montrose, Age Zero

The restricted registry lived in a room that smelled of cedar and old paper and, faintly, of someone's lunch — a fact Marguerite found oddly comforting. Even the most forbidden corner of New Lagos was still a place where a human being had once eaten a sandwich.

She had gotten there through a combination of patience, charm, and the colonial archive's one great weakness: an assistant archivist named Bisi who was twenty-three, chronically overworked, and constitutionally incapable of refusing anyone who brought her pastries. Marguerite had been bringing her pastries for six days. On the seventh, she mentioned — casually, while Bisi licked custard off her thumb — that she needed to see the restricted births registry for claim verification purposes.

"Restricted," Bisi said, with the particular emphasis of someone who has been trained to say that word.

"Routine," Marguerite said, with the particular emphasis of someone who has spent eleven years making restricted things routine. "Standard cross-verification. The underwriters insist."

Bisi looked at the pastry box. She looked at Marguerite. Somewhere behind her eyes, a small internal court weighed professional integrity against almond croissants, and Marguerite watched the verdict land.

"Ten minutes," Bisi said. "And if anyone asks, you picked the lock, which I'm fairly sure you could do anyway, because honestly that lock is from before my mother was born."

"I would never," said Marguerite, who absolutely could.

* * *

The registry itself was disappointing at first glance: a single climate-controlled cabinet, four drawers, and a ledger system so primitive it might as well have been carved into bark. But Marguerite had learned something in this colony's archive already, something that had been tickling the back of her mind since the two-hands ledgers in Chapter Two of her own investigation — that New Lagos did not hide its secrets. It filed them.

It organized them. It cross-referenced them. It put them behind locks that any competent investigator could defeat, in rooms that any curious person could reach, and then it trusted to the colony's beautiful, cultivated incuriosity to keep everyone away. The archive didn't want to be found, exactly. It wanted to be *findable*. There was a difference, and the difference felt like an invitation, or possibly a trap, and after eleven years in insurance fraud Marguerite had stopped being able to tell those apart.

She pulled the drawer for the founding decade and began.

Births were recorded on heavy card stock, one per colonist, in a careful clerk's hand — and there it was again, that second hand, appearing every few years like a tide coming in. The first hand was round and confident. The second was smaller, more angular, with a distinctive way of crossing its t's that made each letter look slightly startled. Marguerite had spent enough evenings with the maintenance logs by now that she recognized it on sight.

The startled-t hand belonged to whoever had rewritten history. And the startled-t hand appeared on birth records too — but only, she noticed as she worked through the drawer, on a specific subset of them. Records with a small notation in the upper corner. A notation she'd seen three times before in the last hour and hadn't understood:

**C/L.**

Cryo-listed.

She found the pattern the way you find all patterns in archives: by getting annoyed. Fourteen birth cards carried the C/L stamp. Thirteen of them also carried a second stamp, diagonal across the first, in red ink gone brown with age: **TRANSFERRED — SUBLEVEL**, followed by dates. All within the same eighteen-month window. The last lottery window, if Tomi's arithmetic about reactor capacity was right.

The fourteenth card had the C/L stamp. It had no transfer stamp. Instead, in the startled-t hand, someone had written across the bottom, in ink pressed hard enough to emboss the card:

**EXTRACTED. FORGIVE ME.**

Marguerite sat very still in the cedar-smelling room with her heart doing something unprofessional.

Then she turned the card over.

* * *

The reverse held the full record, and Marguerite read it the way you read your own obituary: with total attention and a strange floating sensation, as though the words were happening to someone standing nearby.

**MONTROSE, MARGUERITE ADEOLA.**
Born: New Lagos Colonial Hospital, 14 March.
Mother: Montrose, Adaeze R., Archive Clerk, Second Class.
Father: [LINE STRUCK THROUGH] — see annex, sealed.
Status at birth: healthy. Weight normal. Cried loudly, per attending nurse ("a good sign; strong lungs").
Disposition: **CRYO-LISTED, LOTTERY COHORT 7.**

Below that, in the same startled-t hand, an addendum dated nine days later:

*Extraction unauthorized. Subject removed from cryo roster prior to intake. Falsified departure manifest filed: subject recorded as deceased, cause listed as neonatal respiratory failure. Body not produced (cremation claimed, no record with mortician services). Subject transported off-world via supply rotation, manifest falsified by undersigned.*

*Undersigned accepts full responsibility. Undersigned requests that this card be preserved in the restricted registry rather than destroyed, against the day the truth can be borne.*

*If you are reading this, I am sorry. You were never supposed to find out this way. You were supposed to find out never.*

Marguerite read it four times. The words did not rearrange themselves into anything kinder.

Her birthday was wrong. Not the day — the day was right, the fourteenth of March, the day she had celebrated for forty-one years with varying degrees of enthusiasm and one memorable incident involving a rented pony — but the *provenance* was wrong. She had a birthday cake story. She had a hospital bracelet her mother kept in a jewelry box, plastic gone brittle, printed with a London hospital's name. She had a whole childhood, fully furnished, Earth-standard issue: damp holidays in Cornwall, a state school with a leaky roof, a mother who worked double shifts at a records office and came home smelling of paper dust and rain.

A records office. Her mother had worked in a *records office* on Earth, and Marguerite had grown up thinking nothing of it, the way you think nothing of everything when you're a child, and now the memory turned over in her chest like a stone showing its underside. Of course. Of course her mother had chosen records. Falsifying a life was just archiving done aggressively.

The hospital bracelet. She thought about the hospital bracelet with a fresh, sick clarity. It had never quite looked right — the printing faded unevenly, the plastic style a decade out of date even when Marguerite was small — and she had once asked her mother about it, age seven or so, and her mother had taken it away and never shown it to her again. She'd assumed embarrassment. Sentiment. Some private grief about the circumstances of her birth.

It was a prop. Her entire childhood contained props, and she had walked through it for forty-one years touching the scenery without ever once noticing it was painted plywood.

"No," she said aloud, to the empty room, in the tone she used on claimants who had just told their third contradictory version of events. It was a good tone. It had cracked better liars than this.

But this wasn't a liar. This was a card. Cards didn't lie; people lied *on* cards, and the person who had written on this one had signed her name in a hand Marguerite knew — knew intimately, knew from birthday cards and grocery lists and the single letter her mother had ever written her, sent the week before she died, containing nothing but a recipe for pepper soup and the sentence *"You are more than where you grew up."*

She had thought it was sentimentality. Mothers said things like that.

It had been a warning label.

* * *

She did what she always did when the ground opened under her: she worked. Method was a floor you could stand on when the floor of yourself gave way. She photographed the card twice, front and back, then sat with her notebook and made herself write down facts in order, because facts in order were the only prayer she'd ever believed in.

Fact one: Marguerite Adeola Montrose was born in New Lagos.

Fact two: she was cryo-listed at infancy. Lottery Cohort 7. Which meant that somewhere downstairs, in the blue-lit hall the children dreamed about, there was a glass coffin with her name on it — or there had been, until her mother scratched her off the roster and wrote a dead baby onto a departure manifest instead.

Fact three: her mother had committed three kinds of fraud to do it — medical, logistical, and archival — and had then raised her daughter on Earth under a forged history so complete it had survived scrutiny by schools, doctors, passport offices, and one extremely thorough insurance company background check that Marguerite herself had passed at age twenty-six.

Fact four, and this was the one that made her put down the pen and press the heels of her hands into her eyes: her employer's file listed her birthplace as Earth. London. St. Mary's, Paddington. She had verified that file herself during her security clearance. She had *signed* it.

She was not investigating a crime in New Lagos. She was evidence of one. She was the loose end the founders' ledger had never tied off, walking around in a good coat with a laminated credential, asking questions whose answers were her.

There was a version of this realization that was funny, and Marguerite groped for it, because humor was how she breathed. *Insurance investigator discovers she is the uninsured loss.* She said it aloud. It didn't help. Some jokes only work later, from a safe distance, with wine.

What helped instead — barely, unexpectedly — was anger. It arrived late, the way her anger always did, sliding in under the shock like a cat under a door. Because look at what her mother had done. Look at the *audacity* of it. One woman, an archive clerk, second class, with no power except total fluency in paperwork, had reached into a lottery designed by powerful men to be unanswerable and plucked out one baby. Hadn't argued. Hadn't petitioned. Had simply edited reality and walked out through the supply rotation carrying her daughter and a falsified manifest, and had then spent thirty years being ordinary on another planet so that the edit would hold.

Marguerite had inherited that. Not the money — there had never been money — the *method*. The patience with documents. The instinct that every locked thing wants opening. She had built a whole career on skills she'd thought she learned from her mother's example, and it turned out she'd learned them from her mother's crimes.

"You could have told me," she whispered to the card, to the startled-t handwriting, to a dead woman nine weeks buried under Cornish soil that probably wasn't even Cornish soil in any meaningful sense. "You could have told me when I was grown. You had decades."

But the addendum answered her, in its way: *against the day the truth can be borne.* Her mother had been waiting for a day that could bear it. And maybe — Marguerite turned this over slowly, hating it — maybe that day had never come because the truth couldn't be borne yet. Maybe it still couldn't. Maybe there were four hundred sleepers downstairs and a countdown and a colony eating its own future, and maybe her mother had known that telling her daughter *you were born here, you were meant to sleep here* would drop her into the middle of it, the way — 

The way she now was.

"Oh," Marguerite said quietly. "Oh, you absolute woman."

Because there was one more possibility, and it fit her mother like a tailored coat. The card had been preserved, not destroyed. Preserved *in the restricted registry*, behind a bad lock, in a room that smelled of cedar and someone's lunch, findable by anyone persistent enough to bring pastries to an exhausted assistant archivist. Her mother had spent her life in archives. She knew exactly how archives behaved. She knew that secrets filed carefully are secrets waiting.

Her mother hadn't hidden the truth. She had *catalogued* it, pending retrieval.

And Marguerite was the retrieval mechanism. Born here, exported there, educated in suspicion, employed in investigation, and returned — by coincidence? By the impossible death claim? By the dream she'd been having since the night she landed, the blue hall, the counting woman, the voice counting *her*?

She looked down at the card one more time, at the struck-through father line, at the sealed annex reference. One thread at a time. She photographed the annex reference too, then closed the drawer with the gentleness you use on sleeping things.

* * *

Bisi was at her desk outside, pretending very hard to be busy with a filing task that involved holding a folder upside down.

"All done?" Bisi asked. "See anything interesting?"

Marguerite considered several answers. The true one — *I found myself* — was too large for a Tuesday morning and too dangerous to say in a corridor with ears. So she smiled the smile that had gotten her through eleven years of claims work, warm and slightly tired, a woman with nothing stranger in her bag than forms.

"Administrative discrepancies," she said. "You know how it is. Paperwork outliving the people who wrote it."

"Tell me about it." Bisi sighed with feeling. "Half this archive is people arguing with dead clerks."

"Yes," Marguerite said. "I'm beginning to get that impression."

She left the pastry box on Bisi's desk — she'd bought it that morning, before she knew, back when she was still a person with an ordinary job and an ordinary childhood — and walked out into the colony's beautiful, terrible daylight.

New Lagos was showing off, the way it always did in the late morning: the boulevard trees in flower, the market loud with somebody arguing cheerfully about fish prices, children running somewhere with the specific urgency of children who have been told not to run. A normal town. A town that had eaten four hundred futures to keep tasting like this, and had slept soundly for thirty years, and had never once asked why its lights never flickered.

One of the running children — a girl of perhaps eight, braids flying — skidded to a stop directly in Marguerite's path, looked up at her with the frank appraisal of the very young, and said:

"You're the lady from the dream. The one the counting woman talks to."

Marguerite's whole body went cold and pleasant, like stepping into shade.

"What does she say to me?" Marguerite asked. Her voice came out steady. Eleven years of practice; her mother's training, though neither of them had called it that.

The girl shrugged, already losing interest, already half-turned toward whatever the other children were doing. "Names," she said. "Lots of names. But yours most." And then she ran off, and Marguerite stood alone on the sunny boulevard holding a leather case full of photographs of her own birth certificate, understanding with perfect clarity that the next document she needed was not in the births registry at all.

It was the lottery papers. Cohort 7. And she knew exactly which clerk's hand had touched them last.
