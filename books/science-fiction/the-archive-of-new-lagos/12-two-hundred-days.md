## 12. Two Hundred Days

The council chamber of New Lagos occupied the top floor of the administration building and had been designed, Marguerite suspected, by someone who had once read a book about democracy. There was a horseshoe of desks, a public gallery with exactly forty seats, a ceremonial gavel that had never once been used for anything but opening the harvest festival, and a window wall that looked out over the terraced gardens so that every deliberation about the colony's future could be conducted in view of how pleasant the present was.

Mayor Adeyemi sat at the center of the horseshoe with his tea things arranged before him like fortifications: pot, strainer, two cups, a small plate of chin chin he would later offer around as though generosity were an argument.

Marguerite had asked to be heard under the agenda item "Miscellaneous Petitions," which she privately considered the most honest filing category in human history.

"Ms. Montrose," the mayor said warmly, "the council welcomes you. I understand you've concluded your insurance investigation?"

"I've concluded the part I was hired for," she said. "The death claim is legitimate. The man who died last month was legally alive, which means the payout is owed. What I found along the way is not on my invoice."

She let that sit for a moment. Then she opened her case — a battered leather satchel that had crossed two star systems and one very suspicious customs officer — and began.

* * *

She had rehearsed it in her hotel room until three in the morning, pacing between the bed and the window, talking to the potted fern she'd named Councilor Fern because it was the only audience that didn't interrupt. But standing before the actual council, she abandoned the rehearsal and simply told them what she'd done, in order, the way you'd tell a friend about finding out their house was built on something.

The ledgers first. She passed out photocopies — real paper, because she'd learned that screens could be dismissed and paper had weight. Forty years of reactor maintenance logs, alternating handwriting styles like a conversation conducted with oneself. Output figures flat to within a tenth of a percent across four decades, which no machine in history had ever managed without help.

"A reactor this steady isn't well-built," she said. "It's well-edited."

Then the power budgets. She walked them through the arithmetic slowly, kindly, the way you'd tell someone their dog had been eating from the neighbor's bowl for thirty years. The cryobay sublevel drew its reserves from the same grid as everything else. Every heated greenhouse, every school's lights, every cup of tea in this very chamber — all of it drawn against a battery meant to keep four hundred people alive underground. The colony hadn't been living within its means. It had been living within *someone else's* means.

"The sleepers were budgeted for fifty years of dormancy," she said. "They have thirty-one years left on paper. They have two hundred and fourteen days on the terminal downstairs."

A murmur went through the gallery. Councilor Okonkwo, who ran the agricultural portfolio and had the permanent squint of a man auditing sunlight, leaned forward.

"That terminal could be malfunctioning."

"It could," Marguerite agreed. "So I brought photographs. Timestamped. And I brought Tomi Balogun's preliminary readings, which I'll get to, and I brought the dreams."

At the word *dreams*, something changed in the room. It was subtle — a stillness, a collective decision not to look at each other — but Marguerite had spent eleven years reading faces across conference tables, and she knew what a room looked like when everyone in it shared a secret they'd agreed not to name.

"Six children at the primary school have described, independently, the same dream. A blue-lit hall. Rows of glass coffins. A woman counting them aloud. Adults have started reporting it too." She paused. "I have had it myself. Every night since I arrived. And the woman doing the counting has my mother's face."

Councilor Eze, oldest member of the horseshoe, said into the silence: "Dreams are not evidence."

"No," Marguerite said. "They're testimony. The evidence is underneath us, in a sealed sublevel, holding four hundred citizens your charter says don't exist. The dreams are just the only correspondence they're able to send."

* * *

The room split the way rooms do when truth arrives without an appointment.

Denial took the north side of the horseshoe. Councilor Eze declared the whole thing "an investigator's theater" and demanded to know who had authorized her access to restricted levels. Rage took the south: Councilor Nwosu, young and loud, stood up and demanded to know why *she* was the one telling them, where had the council been, what else had they not been told — a question aimed less at Marguerite than at the mayor, though it landed on everyone.

And then there was the third response, the one Marguerite hadn't planned for.

"Tomi," said Councilor Nwosu, turning toward the gallery. "You did the numbers. Tell them."

A young woman stood up from the third row of the public seats. Mid-twenties, coveralls with the sleeves rolled, a tablet held against her chest like a shield. Marguerite had met her only twice — once when bribing the caretaker's assistant for sublevel access, once when Tomi had shown up uninvited at her hotel door at midnight, saying, "I checked your figures because I assumed you were wrong, and I want you to know exactly how wrong you weren't."

Tomi walked to the front of the chamber without being introduced, which itself caused a small constitutional crisis among the councilors.

"I'm a grid engineer. Third shift, thermal systems." Her voice was steady in the way of someone who had decided to be steady and was paying for it. "I pulled the raw telemetry myself. Not the logs — the logs are fiction, we can argue about fiction all afternoon. The actual sensor data from the reactor's own monitoring array, which apparently nobody thought to falsify because nobody reads it anymore."

She turned her tablet to face the horseshoe.

"Cryobay reserve drawdown matches Ms. Montrose's projections to within two percent. At current consumption, thermal failure in the sublevel occurs in two hundred and eleven days. Three days sooner than the terminal estimate, because the terminal doesn't account for summer load." She looked up. "I've run it four times. I had my supervisor run it blind, without telling him what it was for. He got two hundred and thirteen days and then asked me why I looked like that."

Someone in the gallery laughed, high and nervous, the laugh of a person who had just realized they might be dreaming about their neighbor's grandmother.

"So it's true," Councilor Nwosu said. "Two hundred days."

"It's true," Tomi said. "I wish it weren't. I liked my life before this week. I had hobbies."

That got another laugh, thinner than the first. Marguerite found herself liking Tomi enormously — the particular courage of a person who copes with catastrophe through precision and jokes, in that order.

Mayor Adeyemi had not spoken. He sat with his hands folded, watching Marguerite with an expression she couldn't fully parse — not anger, not surprise. Something closer to grief, or perhaps to accounting. When he finally spoke, his voice was gentle.

"Ms. Montrose. You understand what you're asking. If these numbers are right — and I'm not conceding they are — then this council must choose between a disaster we can prepare for and a panic we cannot. Four hundred people waking into a colony that cannot feed them. Rationing. Illness. Perhaps deaths above ground to prevent deaths below." He spread his hands. "You've presented a diagnosis. Diagnoses are easy. Prognoses are hard. Treatment is harder."

"I'm not asking you to treat it today," Marguerite said. "I'm asking you to stop pretending the patient is healthy. Two hundred days goes fast. I've watched councils spend longer than that deciding on a water tariff."

"We will convene a special session," the mayor said. "A full technical review. Discreetly."

"Discreetly," Marguerite repeated. "Mayor, four hundred people are broadcasting their location into your children's sleep. Discretion left the building some time ago. It's down in the sublevel, counting."

The session ended without a vote, which Marguerite understood perfectly well was the point. No motion carried, no position recorded, nothing minuted except "Miscellaneous Petitions, item six, received." She packed her papers while the councilors dispersed in clusters, and noticed that Tomi waited for her by the door, arms crossed, radiating the specific impatience of an engineer whose calculations have been referred to committee.

"They're going to sit on it," Tomi said, falling into step beside her on the stairs.

"Of course they are. That's what committees are for. A committee is a device for converting urgency into minutes."

"You're not angry?"

"Oh, I'm furious," Marguerite said. "But fury is a poor filing system. We have two hundred days. I intend to use them."

* * *

That night she dreamed the blue hall again, more vividly than before. The counting woman reached her — reached *her* specifically, past the coffins, past the rows of sleeping faces — and said, in a voice like a ledger being read aloud:

*"Nineteen names remain. Ask the undertaker about the second fire."*

Marguerite woke at 2:14 a.m. with her heart going like a failing pump and wrote the sentence on the hotel stationery before it could dissolve. She was still awake, sitting cross-legged on the bed in the lamplight, when she smelled it.

Smoke. Faint, wrong, chemical — not woodsmoke, not cooking. The kind of smoke that comes from paper burning somewhere it shouldn't.

She was in the corridor in her coat before she'd decided to move, hammering on the night manager's door. By the time they got her room open with the master key, the damage was contained and precise: a small fire in the wastebasket, extinguished by the sprinkler head directly above it, which had soaked the bed, the carpet, and — she saw immediately, with a coldness that had nothing to do with the water — the desk.

Her desk. Where her working papers had been laid out in neat stacks. Photocopies of the ledgers. The power budget analysis. Tomi's telemetry printouts.

All of it pulped into gray sludge, except one item, which lay on the wet desk face-up and untouched: the original claim file she'd been sent from Earth. The one that said *Marguerite Montrose, born Lagos, Earth.*

Whoever had set the fire had wanted the evidence gone and the message delivered, and had accomplished both with a single match. The message being: we know who you are, we know what you found, and we can reach you while you sleep.

The night manager, a soft-spoken man named Emeka who kept a cricket bat under the front desk for reasons never explained, kept saying "this has never happened, madam, never in fourteen years," while Marguerite stood dripping in the doorway of her ruined room and felt something unexpected settle over her instead of fear.

Calm. Not peace — calm the way a blade is calm. Somewhere below her feet, four hundred people had been sending her the same plea for weeks, and now someone upstairs had answered it with arson. The truth had enemies. Good. Things without enemies were usually not worth much.

She fished the sodden stationery off the desk, the note she'd written at 2:14, ink feathered but legible:

*Nineteen names remain. Ask the undertaker about the second fire.*

Emeka offered her the sofa in the lobby and a towel and, after a moment's consideration, tea from the emergency kettle. She accepted all three.

"Madam," he said, pouring, "if you don't mind my asking — and you don't have to answer — is this about downstairs?"

Marguerite looked at him over the rim of the cup.

"Why do you ask?"

"Because the caretaker's assistant bought new boots last week," Emeka said mildly. "Very good ones. And Mr. Okafor the undertaker came in Tuesday asking if anyone had visited you, and I said only the engineer girl, and he nodded like a man confirming a delivery." He refilled her cup. "This colony keeps secrets the way other places keep weather, madam. Everyone knows the forecast. Nobody says it out loud."

In the morning, Marguerite checked out of the hotel with her salvaged notes sealed in plastic bags, moved into the archive building's guest cot with the tacit blessing of nobody and the practical assistance of Tomi, who arrived carrying a spare hard drive and a thermos and the announcement: "I made copies of everything. Off-site. Three sites. One of them is a hollowed-out irrigation pump nobody's opened since the founding."

"You hid backup data in a pump?"

"I hid backup data in *several* pumps. Diversify your infrastructure." Tomi set the thermos down. "Also, I did what you said. I asked the undertaker about the second fire."

Marguerite went very still. "And?"

"And he invited us both to dinner tomorrow night." Tomi's mouth twitched. "He said, and I'm quoting, 'Tell her to come hungry, and tell her some stories take a whole meal.' He also said—" Tomi hesitated, checking the words carefully, the way engineers check units. "He said, 'The nineteen names are the ones that were never supposed to be there. Not even in the lottery.'"

Outside the archive window, New Lagos went about its morning — gardeners in the terraces, children running to school, the reactor's cooling towers breathing their white breath into a sky too blue to trust. Two hundred and nine days, by Tomi's count.

Marguerite poured herself a cup of tea and began, in her own hand, a fresh set of notes.

The truth had enemies now. Fine. She had always worked best with opposition; it gave the paperwork shape. Tomorrow there would be dinner, and an old man's confession, and nineteen names that had never been meant to exist.

Tonight, she suspected, the counting woman would count again — and this time, Marguerite intended to dream carefully enough to write down every number.
