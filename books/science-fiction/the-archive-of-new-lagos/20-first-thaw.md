## 20. First Thaw

Tomi had calculated the thaw sequence forty-one times. She'd run it in simulation, run it backwards, run it at half-speed, run it while eating jollof rice one-handed because she refused to leave the terminal even for lunch. Forty-one times, and every time the numbers came back the same: two hundred sleepers could wake safely if the power draw was staggered across nine days, and the colony's grid could absorb them without browning out the desalination plant or — this was the part she'd triple-checked, because she knew which complaint would arrive first — the ice cream freezer at Mama Bisi's cantina.

"You checked the ice cream," Marguerite said, reading over her shoulder on the morning of the first thaw.

"I checked the ice cream," Tomi confirmed. "Priorities."

"Good priorities." Marguerite was holding two cups of tea and gave her one, which was how Tomi knew she looked as bad as she felt. Nine days of not sleeping properly, of dreaming the blue hall even while awake, of seeing rows of glass lids behind her eyelids whenever she blinked too long. "How are you doing it? The math that says goodbye to people?"

Tomi took a sip of tea. It was too sweet, the way Marguerite made it, the way Earth people apparently made it, and she found she didn't mind. "I'm not saying goodbye," she said. "I'm saying *see you later* with a schedule attached. There's a difference. Engineers live in that difference."

"And if the difference collapses?"

"Then I'll have been wrong publicly, which is the worst thing that can happen to an engineer, so believe me when I tell you I've checked everything twice."

Marguerite laughed — a small, tired sound, but real. Outside the reactor control room window, the colony was already gathering. Tomi could see them from here: hundreds of people walking toward the cryobay entrance in the grey-gold light of early shift, carrying flowers, carrying drums, carrying children on their shoulders who were chanting names they'd learned in dreams before they'd ever learned to read them off a list.

"They're early," Marguerite said.

"Everyone's early. Nobody wanted to be late for this." Tomi set down her cup. "That's new for New Lagos. We're usually late for everything except complaints about the water pressure."

* * *

The cryobay had never held this many living people. That was the first thing Tomi noticed, standing at the thaw console with her hand hovering over the authorization key. For thirty years this room had been a secret, then an accusation, then an argument, and now it was a *venue*, and the room didn't seem to know what to do with itself. The blue emergency lighting had been supplemented — Tomi had insisted — with warm amber work-lamps strung along the aisles, so that instead of looking like a morgue the hall looked like what it actually was: a hospital ward where everyone happened to be sleeping very, very deeply.

Two hundred glass lids. Two hundred faces beneath them, frost-rimed and still. And between the rows, moving slowly, touching nothing, three hundred colonists who had spent the last month learning names.

That had been Marguerite's idea, and like most of her best ideas it had sounded slightly insane when she proposed it: no waking until every sleeper's name was read into the colonial record, spoken aloud, entered by hand into the amended archive. *You don't wake strangers,* she'd said. *You wake people you know.* So for four weeks the colony had studied. Schoolchildren made flashcards. Mama Bisi pinned photographs above her serving counter, one sleeper per day, with a short biography written underneath — *Chidinma Okafor, hydrologist, sang alto, afraid of deep water* — and by the end even the mayor's faction had stopped calling the sleepers "the inventory."

Tomi walked the aisles now, doing her final pre-thaw inspection, and found she couldn't do it clinically. She stopped at pod 114. Adeyemi Folarin — no relation to the mayor, though the shared name had caused one excruciating dinner conversation — fishery engineer, age fifty-one frozen, father of two children who were now in their thirties and standing somewhere in this crowd. His wife had come every day for a week just to sit beside the glass.

"Pod 114, nominal," Tomi said quietly, and moved on, because if she stopped at every pod she would never start, and if she never started, the countdown would make the decision for all of them.

At the console, Marguerite stood where she'd insisted on standing — not center stage, but to the side, near the archive terminal, her mother's ledger open beside her like a witness. Mayor Adeyemi stood further back than any mayor had ever voluntarily stood at a public event. He had conceded the vote, but he hadn't conceded anything else; his faction had been gutted by the sabotage investigation, two of his allies arrested, his own hands conspicuously clean in a way that convinced nobody, and today he wore the expression of a man attending the funeral of his own relevance. Still, when Tomi caught his eye, he nodded once. Small. Grudging. Real enough.

The children went first, because the children had demanded to go first. Six of them — the original six dreamers, plus forty more who'd joined since — filed up to the microphone someone had rigged near the console, and read the first twenty names aloud, slow and careful, the way you read names that matter. When they finished, the whole hall said the names back. It echoed off the glass lids, and Tomi felt it in her sternum, and she pretended her eyes were watering because of the coolant humidity, which was a lie she intended to maintain indefinitely.

Then it was time.

She keyed the sequence. Somewhere below them, valves opened that had been closed for decades, and the sound came up through the floor like a long-held breath finally released. On the main display, the first ten pods shifted status: STABLE → THAW INITIATED. Warming curves began to climb, gentle as sunrise, ninety minutes per degree, no faster — Tomi had fought the council for those ninety minutes and won, because fast thaws killed people and she had buried forty of them already and would not bury more for the sake of ceremony.

The ceremony, therefore, would take all day. Nobody minded. People brought food. Someone produced a guitar. Mama Bisi set up a folding table near the entrance and began handing out meat pies to anyone within arm's reach, which was everyone, because everyone was within arm's reach of Mama Bisi; that was simply physics.

* * *

By midday, the first sleepers were stirring.

It happened without drama, which Tomi found herself absurdly proud of. No alarms, no crises, no heroic last-second interventions — just the slow biology of bodies remembering themselves. Pod 7 opened first: an old man, seventy-three years frozen, who surfaced through the anesthesia haze blinking at the amber lights and the crowd and the guitar player, and said, with the first breath that carried voice:

"Is this the dream?"

And a woman in the front row — his daughter, gray-haired now, sixty-one years old and suddenly eleven again — said, "No, Papa. This is the morning after it."

He cried. Half the hall cried. The other half pretended they weren't crying, which in New Lagos meant crying slightly louder than necessary to cover it. Tomi stood at her console and watched her telemetry — heart rates climbing, temperatures normalizing, oxygen saturation good, *all good*, row after row of green — and let herself feel, for exactly four seconds, the thing she'd been refusing to feel for a month: that it might actually work.

Then pod 19 beeped a soft warning — potassium drift, routine, handled — and she was back in her body and her numbers, because hope was fine but telemetry was better.

They woke them in batches of ten. Between batches, the families did the introductions, and this became the strangest and warmest part of the day, the part Tomi knew would be retold for generations. Grandchildren meeting grandparents. A woman introducing her frozen husband to the son he'd never met, born eight months after the lottery took him. Chidinma Okafor the hydrologist, awake and wrapped in blankets and weeping over meat pies, being told by three separate teenagers that she was famous, that her photograph had hung in Mama Bisi's cantina for a week, that everyone knew she was afraid of deep water, and her laughing through tears and saying, "Well, that's going to be awkward, because I'm told we're short on hydrologists."

"We are extremely short on hydrologists," Tomi confirmed. "Please eat your pie."

The mayor's faction stood down that afternoon, formally, in the least dramatic way possible — which, given the sabotage scandal, was the only way left to them. Councilor Ekwueme, the mayor's chief ally, appeared at the cryobay entrance around four o'clock, stood watching the wakes for a long time, and then approached Tomi at the console with his hat literally in his hands.

"Engineer," he said. "I want you to know my resignation is submitted. Effective tonight." He looked out at the hall, at the families, at the old man from pod 7 now sitting upright and arguing cheerfully with his daughter about whether the guitar player knew any real songs. "I told myself for thirty years that we were keeping them safe. Keeping *us* safe. That the arithmetic forgave us." He shook his head slowly. "But arithmetic doesn't ask permission, does it. That was always the sin. Not the numbers. The not asking."

Tomi didn't know what to say to that, so she said the engineer thing: "We could use people who understand the power budget. The rotation is going to be harder than the thaw."

Ekwueme almost smiled. "I know. I've seen your figures." He put his hat back on. "Send me the maintenance schedules. I'll be useful or I'll be gone, but I won't be in the way."

It wasn't absolution. Tomi didn't think anything in New Lagos was going to be absolution for a long time. But it was a beginning, and beginnings were her favorite kind of problem: messy, underdetermined, full of edge cases.

* * *

The goodbyes started at dusk.

This was the part Tomi had dreaded — the mirror half of the ceremony, the two hundred colonists whose names had come up in the rotation lottery, who would lie down tonight so the newly woken could have their light and heat and water. She'd modeled the power flows a hundred times. She had not modeled the emotional ones, because there was no equation for a mother kissing her children goodnight knowing she wouldn't see their faces for six months.

They did it in the same hall, which had been Marguerite's idea again, and Tomi was starting to develop a professional suspicion that the novelist's ideas were load-bearing. The first-shift sleepers walked the aisles of pods that were now emptying — two hundred lids open, two hundred people sitting up wrapped in blankets, drinking tea, learning the century — and said their farewells not to a machine but to the people they were trading places with.

Adeyemi Folarin from pod 114 stood at the foot of the pod assigned to a schoolteacher named Funmi Alade, and the two men — one fifty-one years frozen, one fifty-five and tired — shook hands like colleagues, because they were, sort of: both fishery engineers, separated by thirty years of stolen time.

"The reefs will have changed," Folarin said. "Tell me what I should expect."

"Colder. Stranger. Some species gone, some new ones nobody's catalogued." Funmi smiled. "You'll hate it for a month and then you'll never want to leave it. Same as always."

"That's the job," Folarin agreed, and lay down.

Tomi watched them go, batch by batch, into the glass. A grandmother tucking in her own daughter, promising to leave notes in the archive the way you'd leave notes in a lunchbox. Two brothers arguing about who got the better pod — pod 88 had a view of the door, apparently, and this mattered enormously. A young man lying down while his fiancée held his hand until the sedation took him, and then standing there a moment too long, and then letting go.

Nobody had to be forced. That was the miracle Tomi kept waiting to disbelieve. The vote had passed by nine votes, and nine votes was a margin thin enough to walk through, and yet here were two hundred people — including some who had voted against the whole plan — lying down anyway, because the alternative was a colony that abandoned its dead, and it turned out that when you showed people the faces and spoke the names, the arithmetic finally asked permission, and permission was given.

Near midnight, the last lid of the first cycle closed. Pod 200: an elderly woman named Iya Ronke, who had insisted on being among the final volunteers because, she announced to the entire hall, "someone responsible should sleep with the first shift, and none of you children qualify." The hall laughed. The lid sealed. Her vitals settled into their long slow rhythm on Tomi's display, green and steady, and the count on the wall board updated:

**AWAKE: 400. SLEEPING: 200. NEXT ROTATION: 183 DAYS.**

For the first time in thirty years, the numbers balanced.

* * *

Tomi stayed late, as usual, running post-thaw diagnostics while the cleaning crews folded chairs and the guitar player finally went home. Marguerite found her there past two in the morning, two cups of tea in hand, too-sweet as ever.

"Status report, Engineer?"

"Grid stable. Sleepers stable. Ice cream freezer stable." Tomi accepted the tea. "Historic day. Nobody complained about the water pressure once."

"A miracle." Marguerite sat on the console desk, which Tomi normally forbade, and allowed tonight. "Your mother would be proud of you."

"My mother would ask why I didn't check the ice cream freezer *first*." But Tomi smiled, and it reached her eyes, and they sat in the quiet hum of the bay together for a while — the hum of two hundred sleeping hearts, of pumps and valves and a reactor finally honest about its own arithmetic.

Then Marguerite said, quietly, "There's something I need to show you. From the ledger."

Tomi set down her cup. "Now?"

"Now. Before tomorrow gets loud." Marguerite opened the master ledger to a page near the end, and turned it toward the light, and Tomi saw that her face had gone carefully blank in the way of someone delivering news they've rehearsed not reacting to. "My mother kept a final entry. Dated the year I was born. It names a designated arbiter for the thaw sequence — someone chosen before any of this, with authority over the rotation order." She tapped the line. "It isn't me. She wrote my name in as a decoy, in case the founders' faction ever came looking. The real arbiter—the person she trusted with the actual sequence, the person who has legal override on every pod in this room—"

Marguerite turned the page, and Tomi read the name, and the tea went cold in her hands.

"—is you, Tomi. It's been you since before either of us was born."
