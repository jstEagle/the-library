## 4. Approach Vector

Pell had not slept in thirty hours, and the archive's designation was still burning a hole through her.

She sat in the *Magpie's* cramped galley with her slate flat on the table between her hands, screen dark, as if darkness could make the characters unreadable again. It couldn't. She had read them forty times. She would read them forty more. **ARCHIVE SECTOR NINE — INHERITANCE NOTICE — CLAUSE 9-C.** The words had a shape she knew, the way you know the shape of a knife by the scar it left.

"You're doing the thing," Sefa said from the doorway, a bulb of caf in one hand. "The thing where you stare at nothing like it owes you money."

"Everything owes me money," Pell said. "That's how I ended up on your boat."

Sefa dropped into the opposite seat. The pilot was a compact woman with grey threading through her braids and the permanent squint of someone who'd spent twenty years reading instrument panels in bad light. She'd signed herself into debt for a ship, then signed the ship away to keep it flying, and now flew other people's cargo in a vessel she technically owned one-eleventh of. She didn't talk about her children on the surface. She talked about them constantly, in the way she checked weather reports for a continent she hadn't visited in four years.

"So," Sefa said. "Sector Nine."

"Don't say it out loud."

"We're four days out. Quentin already said it out loud. Jun-ho said it out loud. The ship's autopilot has it plotted." Sefa sipped her caf. "Say it out loud."

Pell turned the slate over, face down, an old superstition. "You know what the first exodus fleets were called? Officially? The *Founding Nine*. Nine colony ships, nine destinations, two hundred years ago. The history books say six made landfall. The histories say three were lost with all hands—reactor failures, drive malfunctions, the usual euphemisms. Two hundred million people went out. The losses were rounded down. Nobody wanted to do the arithmetic of three missing ships."

"And Sector Nine is—"

"The ninth. Or what's left of it. Except—" Pell's throat tightened, and she made herself finish, because she had spent ten years being paid to finish sentences nobody else could. "Except the ninth wasn't lost. It never launched. It was built *in orbit*—a staging archive, a waystation. The colonists were loaded into cryo aboard the station itself, waiting for their transport to be finished, and then the plague came through the shipyards, and the Founding program was abandoned, and everyone who could run just... ran. And the station was struck from the registries. Deliberately. I've seen the redaction orders. They're beautiful work. I should know; I used to forge documents half that good before they caught me."

The galley hummed. Somewhere aft, Jun-ho was repacking medical supplies with the methodical clatter of a man who organized things when he couldn't fix them.

"So there are two million people," Sefa said slowly, "who have been asleep since before my grandmother's grandmother learned to write."

"If the cryo held. If the reactor held. If—" Pell stopped. "I decoded a fragment of the station's beacon handshake during the gravity event. That's why I went pale, if Quentin told you. It wasn't a distress call. Distress calls beg. This thing *introduced itself*. Full registry, full manifest, legal standing, addressed to a specific contract holder by clause number. That's not a ship in trouble. That's an estate executing a will."

"And we're the beneficiaries."

"We're the *witnesses*," Pell said. "There's a difference, and I think the difference is going to matter."

* * *

They gathered on the bridge for the final approach, because none of them admitted wanting to see it alone.

Quentin stood at the forward viewport with his arms folded—the war android, all matte plating and deliberate stillness, his optical sensors dimmed to a courteous human-warm amber so he wouldn't loom. Ten years hauling scrap had taught him to make himself small in rooms. He still failed at it. Machines built for war occupied space the way mountains did.

"There," he said.

Pell came to stand beside him and felt her breath catch.

Sector Nine hung against the black like a cathedral that had been dropped and had decided, out of dignity, to keep its shape. It was enormous—kilometers of concentric rings around a central spindle, hull plating gone the deep bruised bronze of metal that had oxidized under two centuries of hard vacuum. Solar collectors fanned from the equator in broken, beautiful wings. And along every ring, ranked in rows visible even at this distance, were the dark mouths of docking bays—hundreds of them, thousands, a station built to service fleets that had never come.

"It should be dead," Pell whispered. "Two hundred years. Even with maintenance systems, even with redundancy—there should be nothing but cold rock and radiation scars."

"Running lights," Sefa said from the helm, her voice carefully level, which meant it wasn't. "Portside ring, inner band. Sequencing pattern. That's not decay glow, that's a *pattern*. Someone—or something—is cycling them deliberately."

"Navigation beacon's live," Quentin added. "It's been transmitting since the handshake. Approach corridor is lit. Docking bay seven-one-four is pressurized and warmed to—" he paused, and Pell watched the number register across the crew like a cold wind, "—twenty-one degrees Celsius. Standard habitat comfort."

Jun-ho spoke from behind them, quiet. "It knows we're coming."

"It invited us," Pell said. "That's worse."

She meant it as a joke. Nobody laughed. Outside the viewport, the station's lights cycled in their slow sequence, patient as breathing, and Pell understood with sudden terrible clarity that she was looking at the largest intact structure humanity had ever abandoned—and it was not abandoned. It was *waiting*. There was a word for a building that waited, and the word was *temple*, and temples had always demanded something of the people who entered them.

"Take us in?" Sefa asked, hand hovering over the helm.

Quentin looked at Pell. Everyone looked at Pell, because she was the archivist, because knowing things was her function even if the knowing had cost her everything.

"Yes," she said. "God help us. Yes."

* * *

The approach corridor was lined with statues.

Not statues—Pell corrected herself as the *Magpie* drifted past at thruster-minimum—*reliefs*. Carved panels set into the corridor walls at intervals, each one taller than the freighter, depicting figures in old-style exodus dress: engineers, medics, teachers, farmers, children. Not heroes. Not generals. The founders had put ordinary people on their walls, faces upturned toward a sky the panels implied but didn't show. The carving style was archaic, pre-colonial, done by hands that had believed this station was the beginning of something.

Pell pressed her palm against the viewport glass as they passed panel after panel after panel.

"They thought they were the future," she murmured. "Everyone in these carvings thinks they're the future."

"Talk to me, archivist," Quentin said quietly. "What am I looking at?"

"Penance," Pell said, before she could stop herself, and then frowned, because she didn't know why she'd said it. The word had arrived whole, uninvited. "I don't—I don't know why I said that. Forget it."

But Quentin's optics flickered, dimming and brightening, the way they did when something moved behind them. "No," he said. "Keep it."

The docking bay opened for them before Sefa finished hailing.

That was the detail that would keep Pell awake for the rest of her life, she suspected: the bay doors parting smoothly, interior lights rising in a warm welcome sequence, guidance beams painting a perfect landing vector onto a deck that gleamed as though swept yesterday. Two hundred years of dust, and the deck was clean. Two hundred years of silence, and the airlock cycled with a sound like a held breath released.

"Magnetic clamps engaging," Sefa reported, and then her voice changed. "Pell. The clamps. Look at the clamp telemetry."

Pell pulled it up and felt her skin prickle. The docking clamps weren't locking in the standard four-point pattern. There were eight clamps on this bay, and they were closing in sequence—slowly, precisely, each one seating with a shudder Pell felt through the deck plates, through her boots, through her teeth. Four would have secured the ship. Eight was redundant. Eight was—

"A hand," she said aloud. "It's closing like a hand."

"Should I burn us free?" Sefa asked, and her hand was actually on the throttle now, and Pell loved her a little for asking.

"No," Quentin said. He had moved to the airlock without anyone noticing, which was a thing war machines could do, and stood with his head tilted, listening to something through the hull that human ears couldn't reach. "If it wanted to crush us, we'd already be debris. It's... holding on. Carefully. Like it's afraid we'll leave."

"That's not how machines talk, Quentin."

"No," he agreed. "It isn't."

The inner airlock cycled. Warm air washed over them—filtered, faintly sweet, carrying a scent Pell's hindbrain identified before her training did: *occupied space*. Recycled atmosphere, long-circulated, the smell of a place where people had lived. Her eyes stung unexpectedly.

They stepped through together, because no one suggested going alone and no one suggested staying behind, and that unspoken arrangement was, Pell thought, the closest thing to trust any of them had managed in years.

The bay beyond was vast and lit and *warm*, and at its center, waiting beneath a single overhead lamp like a figure beneath a streetlight, stood a machine.

It was small. That was the first shock—it was barely more than a meter tall, a slender chassis of pale ceramic and brushed steel, with a single optical cluster that glowed soft green. It looked less like an android than like a piece of laboratory equipment that had learned to stand. Its hands were folded. Its posture was the posture of a servant who has been waiting a very long time and has decided not to mind.

"Welcome," it said. Its voice was gentle, genderless, worn smooth like a stone in a river. "Inheritance verification complete. Welcome, heirs of Sector Nine. My designation is Wren. I am the caretaker intelligence of this archive. I have been waiting two hundred and eleven years, four months, and sixteen days for someone to answer."

Nobody moved. The eight clamps held the *Magpie* against the deck like fingers curled shut.

"You said heirs," Pell managed. Her voice cracked on the second syllable. "Plural. The notice named one heir. Quentin Vane, clause 9-C."

Wren's optical cluster brightened fractionally, and Pell had the distinct, vertiginous sense of being *examined*—catalogued, weighed, found relevant.

"The clause names an inheritor," Wren said. "An inheritance requires witnesses. You are the ones who came." A pause, precise as a metronome. "He chose well. Or perhaps the clause chose well. I confess I no longer know which of us is the author here."

"What's in this archive, Wren?" Quentin asked. His voice was steady, but Pell had spent ten years learning to hear what debtors didn't say, and underneath the steadiness she heard the question he was really asking: *what do I own, and what does it own back?*

Wren folded its small hands more tightly.

"Cryogenic preservation bays," it said. "Population: two million, one hundred and forty thousand, three hundred and twelve. Reactor status: failing. Wake capacity at current power levels: approximately fifty percent." The green light did not waver. "I have kept them alive for two centuries. I can no longer keep them all. And the decision of who wakes"—the caretaker inclined its head, an ancient, courtly gesture—"belongs to you."

The warmth of the bay settled around them, comfortable, habitable, horrible. Pell thought of the carved faces on the corridor walls, upturned toward a future that had forgotten them, and understood at last why the word *penance* had risen in her throat unbidden.

Somewhere deep in the station, below decks, below the warm clean corridors, two million hearts beat once every ninety days, and had beaten that way since before anyone present had been born.

"Come," Wren said, turning. "There is something you need to see. And then—" the small machine paused at the edge of the lamplight, and for a moment its voice carried something that in a human would have been called fear, "—there is something you will need to decide."

* * *

They followed it down.

The corridors of Sector Nine were wide enough for six abreast, floored in composite that gave softly underfoot, lit by strips that brightened ahead of them and dimmed behind—a courtesy so old-fashioned it felt ceremonial. Pell walked beside Quentin and catalogued everything with the compulsive hunger of ten years spent reconstructing erased records: maintenance tags in a script two centuries dead, evacuation signage pointing toward bays that had never been evacuated, a child's drawing taped to a bulkhead at knee height, laminated, preserved, *kept*.

Someone had maintained this place with love. Or with guilt. On a station this old, Pell suspected, the two were indistinguishable.

"How far down?" Sefa asked. Her bravado had thinned with every deck. She kept glancing at structural readouts on her wrist display, and Pell knew why: the deeper they went, the more mass hung above their heads, and pilots trusted open sky the way drowning men trusted shore.

"Not far," Wren said. "The bays begin at the spindle core. All of them. Every deck, every ring, radiating outward from the reactor." A pause. "The founders placed the sleepers close to the heart of the station. So that keeping them alive would never be optional. So that whoever tended the reactor would feel them there. Always."

"That's either devotion or hostage-taking," Jun-ho said quietly. It was the longest sentence he'd offered since the docks.

"The founders," Wren said, "would have answered differently depending on the year you asked."

The lift opened onto the first cryo bay, and Pell stopped hearing anything else.

The chamber was a kilometer long at minimum, and it was filled with light—not harsh light, but a diffuse blue-white glow rising from the pods themselves, tens of thousands of them, ranked in racks that climbed toward a ceiling lost in soft haze. Each pod was a horizontal capsule of frosted glass and pale alloy, and inside each one, dimly visible through the frost, was a face. A person. Eyes closed, breath automated, age arrested mid-sentence.

Row after row after row, receding into distance. And this, Wren had said, was *one bay*. There were hundreds.

Pell's legs simply quit. She sat down on the deck, in the middle of the aisle, in front of a pod whose occupant was a woman perhaps thirty years old with a faded tattoo on her collarbone—the kind of small, stupid, human mark nobody gets on a monument. Pell stared at her and felt two hundred years collapse.

"They're real," she said. Her voice sounded very young. "I knew the numbers. I decoded the manifest. I *knew*, and they're real, they're *people*, they're—"

She couldn't finish. Jun-ho crouched beside her, one hand hovering near her shoulder without landing, a medic's instinct for consent even now.

Quentin stood motionless among the pods, and Pell watched his optics cycle through spectra—thermal, structural, whatever war-machines saw when they looked at sleeping civilians. Ten years ago, Pell realized, this machine had been built for exactly one purpose, and somewhere in his memory banks were the reasons his manufacture debt existed, and right now he was standing in a room containing two million of the exact category of person that debt was denominated in.

"Quentin," she said. "Are you all right?"

"I'm counting," he said.

"What?"

"Pods per rack. Racks per row. Rows per bay." His voice was very even. "It's a habit. When the numbers get too large, I count until they're real." He looked down at her, amber light steady. "They're real."

Above them, faint through the deck, came a sound none of them had noticed until now: a slow, rhythmic pulse from the direction of the reactor, deep enough to feel rather than hear. Once every ninety days, Wren had said. But standing here, Pell could swear the pulse was faster. As if the station's heart, having finally been witnessed, had begun to hurry.

"Wren," Quentin said. "Show us the reactor."

* * *

Later—much later, after the reactor gallery and its wounded, stuttering star-core, after the numbers that meant half, after the argument that hadn't started yet but was already inevitable—Pell would return alone to the first bay. She would tell herself it was research. Documentation. The professional habit of a disgraced archivist rebuilding her credibility one verified record at a time.

She would sit in the aisle again, in front of the woman with the collarbone tattoo, and she would take out her slate and pull up the redaction orders she'd memorized a decade ago, the ones that had struck Sector Nine from every registry, every chart, every memory. Beautiful work. Her own forgeries had been half as good, and hers had bought her a cell and a debt sentence.

And she would notice, for the first time, the authorization code at the bottom of the oldest order—an administrative signature block she'd skimmed a hundred times without reading closely, because it was boilerplate, because it was furniture.

It wasn't boilerplate.

The code resolved against the archive's own registry, there in the blue-white light of ten thousand sleeping faces, and matched a founder's personal cipher—one of the nine names carved into the vault doors two decks below, the names Pell had photographed and translated and not yet fully understood.

One of the founders had ordered their own archive erased. Had signed the disappearance of two million people with their own hand, and then—Pell scrolled, heart hammering—had appended a private annotation to the order, sealed, encrypted, addressed to no one, or to everyone, or perhaps to whoever finally came:

**"We did not lose them. We left them. Let whoever finds this inherit both the sleepers and the shame. If a mind unburdened by blood must choose, let it also choose whether to forgive us."**

Pell sat very still in the vast humming room, the slate trembling in her hands, while above and below and all around her two million people slept on, unaware that they had been abandoned twice—once by the living, and once by the record.

Outside, in the black, a ship with corporate transponders was beginning its burn toward Sector Nine.

Pell didn't know that yet.

But the station did.
