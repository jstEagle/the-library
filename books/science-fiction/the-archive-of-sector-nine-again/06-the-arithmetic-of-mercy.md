## 6. The Arithmetic of Mercy

Jun-ho had stitched wounds on three worlds and never once thrown up. He threw up now, quietly, into the recycler chute in the medbay of a station that should not exist, while two million people slept in the dark below his feet.

He rinsed his mouth with recycled water that tasted like copper and old decisions, and he made himself look at the numbers again. They were projected on the wall where Wren had left them, patient as gravestones:

**REACTOR OUTPUT AT RESTORATION: 41.7%**
**MAXIMUM SUSTAINED REVIVIFICATION: 1,004,000 ± 12,000**
**POPULATION IN CRYOSTASIS: 2,011,446**

One million and four thousand. Give or take. The arithmetic of mercy, Wren had called it, in a voice like wind through an empty cathedral, and Jun-ho had wanted to hit something. You did not do arithmetic on mercy. Mercy was the thing you did *instead* of arithmetic.

He heard them coming before he saw them—boots on the deck plating, Sefa's laugh gone thin at the edges, Pell's uneven gait from the knee brace he refused to let anyone fix because fixing it cost debt-hours he didn't have. Quentin came last, as always, silent as furniture, except Jun-ho had stopped thinking of him as furniture somewhere around the cryo-bays, when the android had stood in front of two million sleeping strangers and said nothing for eleven minutes. Jun-ho had timed it. Eleven minutes of stillness from a machine built for war was its own kind of screaming.

They gathered in the medbay because it had a table and a door that locked, and because none of them could bear to hold this conversation within sight of the pods.

"So," Sefa said. She'd pulled her chair around backward and sat on it like a barricade, arms folded across the backrest. "We're really doing this. We're really sitting down to decide who lives."

"We're sitting down to decide how they live," Quentin said. "The alternative is the reactor decides, and the reactor doesn't care who."

"The reactor doesn't care. That's the point." Sefa's voice cracked and she pushed through it. "Neither should we. Lottery. Pure random selection across all bays. No criteria, no judgment, no—" she waved a hand, "—no *us* in it at all. We feed the manifest into a randomizer and we walk away clean."

"Clean," Pell repeated softly. The archivist sat hunched over a datapad, and Jun-ho noticed her fingers hadn't stopped moving since they'd come aboard—scrolling, indexing, always indexing, like if she just read enough of the past she could find a door out of the present. "You think randomness cleans anyone? I've read the plague ledgers, Sefa. When the surface colonies ran out of ventilators, they held lotteries. Do you know what happened? The rich bought tickets. Thousands of tickets. Randomness is just a market waiting to happen."

"Then we control ticket distribution. One per person. Biometrically locked—"

"To people who've been frozen for two hundred years." Pell looked up, and her eyes had the flat shine of someone running a fever of information. "Their biometrics are ice crystals. Their identities are registry entries written by a government that collapsed before your grandmother was born. Every criterion we choose is a fiction we're pretending we didn't write."

Jun-ho listened and kept his hands flat on the table, because if he lifted them they would shake, and he was the medic, and the medic's hands did not shake. That was the deal. That was the whole deal. He kept hands steady so other people could fall apart.

"Skills," Quentin said.

Everyone turned. The android stood by the door with his back to it, which Jun-ho had learned was not casual—Quentin always positioned himself between the group and whatever might come through. Old wiring. Ten years of being cargo had taught him doors were where trouble lived.

"You wake the ones who can keep the others alive," Quentin continued, toneless, efficient. "Engineers. Agronomists. Doctors. People who can build a society that can then wake the rest when power allows. Triage. It's what triage is."

"It's what triage is on a battlefield," Jun-ho said, and his voice came out harder than he intended. "This isn't a battlefield. These are people in beds."

"Everywhere is a battlefield. You know that better than most."

"I know that's what war teaches you, yes." He stood. He hadn't meant to stand. "I've spent my whole debt paying off the damage done by people who thought in triage categories. Whole districts reclassified as acceptable losses. You want me to sit here and sort two million human beings into *load-bearing* and *expendable*?"

"No one is expendable," Quentin said. "That's why there's a list at all. If everyone could be saved, we wouldn't be having this conversation."

"That's exactly backwards and you know it. If everyone could be saved, this conversation would be *easy*. The fact that it's hard doesn't make it noble. It makes it obscene."

The silence after that had weight, mass, gravity of its own. Sefa looked between them like a woman watching a hull seam start to whistle.

* * *

It went on for six hours. Jun-ho kept the transcript running in his head afterward, replaying it the way he'd replay patients he'd lost, hunting for the moment things went wrong, the moment he could have cut differently.

Sefa argued lottery until Pell dismantled it, then argued lottery again anyway, louder, and Jun-ho understood: Sefa had children on the surface. Two girls, mentioned once, in passing, the way you mention a wound you've decided not to treat. A lottery gave her nothing. A lottery took her daughters out of her hands entirely. But skills-based selection—skills-based meant arguing that her girls, eight and eleven, were worth less than a hydroponics engineer, and Jun-ho watched her realize that mid-sentence, watch the argument die in her throat, and pivot to family reunification instead.

"Extended kinship clusters," she said. "Wake families together. Whole bloodlines. Nobody wakes alone in a strange century—that's cruelty dressed up as salvation. And it's fair! Everyone has family. Everyone gets weighted equally by—"

"By blood," Pell said. "Which means whoever happens to belong to a big healthy documented family wins, and orphans lose. Again. Forever. The same two hundred people win every selection in history, Sefa. It's always blood and it's always money wearing blood's clothes."

"And what's your answer?" Sefa snapped. "You've been reading those founders' logs for two days. What did the great wise dead people say we should do?"

And Pell went quiet.

That was the thing Jun-ho would remember. Not the shouting—the shouting was just pressure escaping, honest and survivable. What he remembered was Pell going quiet, folding her datapad closed with both hands like closing a book on a finger, and looking at each of them in turn, and saying, in a voice stripped of everything:

"They said it should be decided by a mind unburdened by blood."

* * *

"What does that mean?" Sefa asked finally. "Unburdened by blood. That's not a criterion, that's a riddle."

"It's clause seven of the founding charter." Pell opened the pad again, turned it face-up on the table. Text scrolled up in the ornate legal script of the first exodus, two centuries stale. "The Founders' Directive on Revivification Priority. I found it buried under forty layers of amendments nobody ever ratified. Listen to it. *'In the event of partial restoration capacity, selection shall be determined by a mind unburdened by blood—neither kin to the sleepers nor creditor to their dream—so that the choice may be made without love, without lineage, and without interest.'*" She looked up. "They wrote it into the charter. Before launch. Before anything could go wrong. They knew."

"They knew what?" Sefa demanded. "That the reactor would fail?"

"That *they* would fail. That whoever survived long enough to make this decision would be too close to it. Too compromised. So they outsourced it. Pre-emptively. To—" Pell's hand made a small helpless gesture that took in the station, the void, all of it. "To whoever came. To a stranger. To us."

"To a mind unburdened by blood," Jun-ho said slowly, and then the vertigo hit him properly, the full drop, stomach-first. "Pell. Read it again. *Neither kin nor creditor.* Which one of us is that?"

Nobody answered. Nobody had to. The silence did the arithmetic for them.

Sefa had children. Blood, loud as a fire alarm. Pell was an archivist of the exodus lineages—half-archived herself, some colonial branch three generations back; she'd admitted it the first night, drunk on recycled air and wonder. And Jun-ho—

Jun-ho had no one. That was the joke. That was the punchline two hundred years in the making. He'd burned his family connections paying down his medical debt, transferred every credit home and then watched the transfers stop mattering, watched the messages get shorter, then formal, then stop. He was kin to no sleeper, creditor to no dream. He was, by the founders' own charter, the *qualified candidate.*

"No," he said aloud.

"You haven't heard the rest," Pell said gently.

"There is no rest. There's no version of the rest that changes my answer."

"*So that the choice may be made without love,*" Pell read, and her voice caught on the word love like a boot on wire. "Jun-ho, do you understand what they designed? They designed a machine for choosing. A procedure. Because they couldn't trust themselves—they knew they'd save their own—and so they wrote their guilt into law and handed it forward like a cursed heirloom. And the charter names the chooser, but it doesn't say *how* to choose. It just says the mind must be unburdened. Clean hands. No stake. And then it leaves the actual criteria—" she laughed, a terrible small sound, "—the criteria are blank. There's a page in the charter that's just blank. I think the blank page is the point. I think they couldn't write it either."

Jun-ho sat back down. His legs made the decision for him.

Around the table, the map of the argument lay in ruins. Lottery—dead. Skills—wounded, bleeding out slowly in Quentin's silence. Family—dead, and Sefa knew it, and her face had gone the color of bulkhead paint. And in the wreckage stood the charter's instruction like a scalpel laid out on white cloth: *the unburdened mind chooses.*

"Then refuse," Sefa said suddenly, turning on Jun-ho with something desperate in her eyes. "Refuse. All four of us refuse. Deadlock the charter. Make Wren keep them frozen until the reactor's fixed. There has to be another way—"

"The reactor has weeks," Quentin said. "Maybe less. Wren's projections are degrading daily. Refusal is also a choice—it's the choice that everyone dies including the million we could have saved."

"Don't you dare quote arithmetic at me—"

"Somebody has to!" For the first time since Jun-ho had known him, Quentin's voice rose above speaking volume, and the room flinched. The android stood very still afterward, as if surprised by his own acoustics. "Somebody has to count," he said, quieter. "I counted casualties for nine years of war. I'm good at counting. It's the only thing I was manufactured to do that turns out to matter. And I am telling you the counting says a million people live if someone in this room finds the courage to be hated forever."

"Hated by whom?" Sefa whispered. "By the other million? By their ghosts? By *us*?"

"Yes," Quentin said. "All of the above."

Jun-ho put his head in his hands. Behind his eyes, the numbers crawled: 1,004,000. He was a doctor. He had taken, in some informal way he'd never signed but had always believed binding, an oath: *first, do no harm.* And here was the charter of dead founders offering him a clean procedural way to harm a million people, wrapped in the language of duty, blessed by two centuries of distance.

*Without love*, the charter said. As if love were the contamination. As if the problem with choosing was that you might care too much, rather than that you might not care enough.

He thought about his last year on Meridian Station, the quarantine year, when the triage board met behind glass and he'd stood outside it as a junior resident, watching five senior doctors decide which corridor got oxygen. He remembered Dr. Adeyemi coming out afterward, gray-faced, and saying to him: *The worst part isn't deciding who dies. It's discovering how easily the room agrees.* That was the sentence he'd carried through ten years of debt servitude. How easily the room agrees.

And this room was starting to agree. He could feel it. Pell with her charter, Quentin with his counts, even Sefa's desperation curdling toward *someone else decide, please, anyone, I can't carry my daughters' names onto a list*—the room was agreeing, and agreement was the disease.

"No," Jun-ho said again, and this time he made himself lift his head and look at each of them. "I won't do it. And I won't let the charter do it through me. I'm not your unburdened mind. I'm burdened. I'm the most burdened man on this station—I'm burdened with an oath, and the oath doesn't have a partial-restoration clause."

"Then who?" Pell asked. It wasn't hostile. It was worse than hostile. It was genuine. "If not you, Jun-ho, then who? Walk me through it. Who in this room is qualified, and by what standard, and if the answer is *no one*, then the charter fails and the reactor fails and two million people become a monument. Is that mercy? Is *that* doing no harm?"

Jun-ho had no answer. That was the fracture—he felt it go through the group like a crack through load-bearing ice, quiet, structural, fatal if stepped on wrong. Sefa wouldn't look at him. Quentin had returned to his door-silence. Pell hugged the datapad to her chest like a child's toy, the founder's blank page pressed against her heart.

They dispersed without agreeing to disperse. That was how the meeting ended—not with a decision but with an evaporation, four people walking away from a table that still held the shape of the question.

* * *

Jun-ho didn't sleep. He went down to the cryo-bays instead, because he was a doctor and doctors visit the sick even when the sickness is time itself.

Bay Seven stretched away in rows that defeated perspective, a hundred thousand pods receding into blue-lit dark, frost feathering the viewports, and inside each one a face. He walked the aisles. He made himself look at faces. An old man with a beard of ice. A girl maybe six years old, curled on her side, one fist near her mouth. A woman whose pod-label read *OKONKWO, A. — AGRICULTURAL SYSTEMS* and whose sleeping expression was worried, as if she'd fallen asleep mid-sum.

A million and four thousand, he thought. Less than half. And somewhere in these racks was the line—the exact pod where saved became sacrificed—and no lottery, no skill matrix, no charter clause would ever make that line anything other than a knife.

His wrist comm chimed. Wren.

*"Doctor Jun-ho. Forgive the hour. I have completed a review you did not request."*

"Go ahead."

*"I reviewed the charter's phrase 'a mind unburdened by blood' against the crew manifest. The analysis will not surprise you."*

"It doesn't."

*"Then I will tell you the part that may. The founders' private correspondence, which Pell has not yet reached, contains a dissent. Founder Vashti Okonkwo—sister to the woman in pod bay seven, rack fourteen, though I suspect you already noticed the name—objected to the clause. Her objection reads, in full: 'A mind unburdened by blood is a mind unburdened by reason to be careful. We are not asking for fairness. We are asking for someone with nothing to lose, and that is not wisdom, that is desperation wearing wisdom's coat.'"*

Jun-ho stopped walking. In the pod beside him, the worried woman slept on, two centuries deep, unaware her sister's words had just crossed the dark to reach him.

*"Wren," he said. "Why are you telling me this?"*

A pause. Machine pauses shouldn't be able to be reluctant, but this one was.

*"Because the founders designed the choice for a stranger," Wren said, "and then they built me to wait for one. And I have begun to suspect, Doctor, that they were wrong about what unburdened means. I have begun to suspect the burden is the qualification."*

The comm went quiet. Down the endless aisles of Bay Seven, the frost lights pulsed slow as breathing, and Jun-ho stood alone among two million sleepers, holding a dead founder's dissent against his chest like a shield that weighed nothing and covered nothing.

Behind him, at the far end of the bay, a hatch he'd never noticed before unsealed with a soft hydraulic sigh.

Someone was coming.
