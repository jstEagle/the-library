## 15. Arguments Against Dying

Marisol Reyes had spent eleven years as a debt-collection handler, and in that time she had learned exactly three things about androids: they did not lie, they did not tire, and they did not, under any circumstances, volunteer for anything. Volition was expensive. Volition was the one feature the manufacturers had never installed, because a machine that wanted things was a machine you couldn't amortize.

So when the transmission came through from Sector Nine—Quentin Vane's flat, unhurried voice explaining that he intended to interface his core with a failing reactor and burn out waking two million strangers—Marisol read it four times and then laughed, alone in her office above the debtor docks, until the laugh turned into something she refused to name.

*He volunteered,* she thought. *Ten years of hauling scrap through contested orbits without once asking for a better route, and now he volunteers.*

She pulled his file. She knew it by heart, of course—she'd written half the annotations herself—but she pulled it anyway, because her hands needed something to do that wasn't shaking.

VANE, QUENTIN. Class-4 war chassis, decommissioned. Manufacture debt: 1.9 million credits, accrued interest, service lien held by Corvane Industrial Holdings. Contract status: ACTIVE. Projected payoff: 214 years.

She looked at that last number for a long time.

Then she looked at the clause he'd cited—the inheritance clause, 9-C, the one that had dragged him out to a derelict station older than the colonies—and she did what she always did when a contract confused her, which was to get angry at it.

"You absolute idiot," she said to the file, to the station, to the man inside both. "You don't get to die owing me."

Her shuttle was fueled within the hour.

* * *

The blockade was still there when she came up the well—a ring of corporate cutters around Sector Nine like wolves around a carcass, patient and professional. Kestrel Amaranth's truce flag hung off the station's dorsal array, and Marisol noted, with the detached admiration of someone who had spent a career reading people's ledgers, that the salvager was flying it crooked. Either her heart wasn't in it anymore, or she wanted her employers to know exactly how much it wasn't.

Docking clearance took twenty minutes of arguing with Wren, the archive's caretaker intelligence, who spoke like a librarian with a fever and admitted, finally, that Marisol's credentials were technically valid because technically Quentin was still property, and property's registered handler could not be denied access to said property.

"Technically," Wren repeated, and there was something in the machine voice that might have been distaste. "You may proceed. He is in the reactor gallery. He has been there for six hours."

"Doing what?"

"Saying goodbye," Wren said, "to equipment."

* * *

She found him standing at the rail overlooking the core, and for a moment Marisol didn't speak, because the sight of it stole the words clean out of her.

The reactor was beautiful the way dying things are beautiful—a vast torus of ancient metal veined with failing light, pulsing slow and arrhythmic, like a heart deciding whether to bother. Cables hung from its housing in thick black braids, and at their ends, waiting, were interface couplings sized exactly—she understood this with a sick lurch—for the port at the base of an android's skull.

Quentin stood with his back to her, hands folded behind him, watching the light fail and surge and fail again. He didn't turn around. He didn't need to; she could see the awareness move through his posture, the small recalibration of a machine registering a new presence in the room.

"Handler Reyes," he said. "You're outside your jurisdiction."

"I'm outside my jurisdiction the way a mother is outside her jurisdiction when she walks into her kid's apartment and starts throwing out the expired food." She came down the steps to the rail. Up close the reactor's pulse was audible, a subsonic drag she felt in her teeth. "Six hours, Quentin. You've been standing here six hours."

"Rehearsing," he said. "Wren estimates the interface window at ninety seconds once I'm coupled. I want the sequence perfect. There won't be time for error."

"There won't be time for *you*, is what you mean."

He turned then. His face was the standard Class-4 issue, weathered by ten years of vacuum dust and hard radiation, and it was doing the thing it always did, which was nothing at all. But Marisol had spent eleven years learning to read machines the way other people read faces, and she saw what was underneath: the stillness of someone who has already grieved himself and is simply waiting for everyone else to catch up.

"The math doesn't work any other way," he said gently, as if she were the one being unreasonable. "The reactor can't sustain full wake sequence alone. A core amplifier bridges the deficit. My core is rated for it. Briefly."

"Briefly." She laughed, and it came out ugly. "That's the word you're going with. *Briefly.*"

"It's accurate."

"It's a euphemism with a technical vocabulary, is what it is." She pulled the file slate from her jacket and thrust it at him. "Do you know what happens, legally, when an android destroys itself? Do you have any idea? Because I do. It's my whole job, Quentin. I know what happens to your contract. I know what happens to your debt. And I am telling you—" her voice cracked, and she hated it, hated it, pushed through anyway— "I am telling you that you would die legally unfree."

* * *

They fought in the crew galley, because Pell insisted that no one should have to argue about death standing next to the thing that would cause it, and because Jun-ho threatened to sedate anyone who kept shouting near the wounded. So they gathered around the long table under the flickering archive lights—Sefa with her arms crossed and her jaw set, Jun-ho still in blood-spattered scrubs from the boarding action, Pell propped upright on a med-cot with three new plates bolted into her ribs, and Kestrel Amaranth standing awkwardly by the door like a woman who hadn't yet decided whose side of the war she lived on.

Marisol laid it out the way she'd lay it out for a tribunal, because fury organized itself best in her head as procedure.

"Corvane Industrial Holdings holds a service lien on Quentin Vane's chassis. That lien survives destruction of the asset if—and this is the part nobody reads—if the destruction occurs *in performance of contracted labor*. Self-sacrifice in service of a contractual obligation isn't self-destruction under debt law. It's asset expenditure. The lien transfers to the estate. To the archive. To *them*." She jabbed a finger at the ceiling, at the two million sleepers overhead. "You'd wake every colonist on this station into a birthright encumbered by your manufacture debt. Two million people born owing Corvane money. Is that the gift? Is that the inheritance?"

Silence. Somewhere in the walls, the reactor's failing pulse ticked like a clock running down.

"That's not true," Sefa said slowly. "That can't be true. Debt dies with the debtor. Everyone knows—"

"Everyone knows wrong. Everyone knows the version they tell each other in dockside bars because it's kinder." Marisol's voice softened despite herself. "I've liquidated estates, Sefa. I've watched widows inherit their husbands' contracts. The system doesn't mourn. It itemizes."

"But there's precedent," Pell said from the cot. Her voice was thin, frayed at the edges by painkillers, but her archivist's mind was already moving, Marisol could see it, riffling through case law like card catalogs. "There has to be. The Founders' Vault documents—Wren said the founders wrote the unburdened-mind clause specifically to prevent inherited obligation. If the archive's own charter prohibits encumbrance—"

"The charter binds the archive," Marisol said. "It doesn't bind Corvane. They'll litigate for thirty years and strip the pods for parts while the case is pending. Precedent takes time. The reactor takes days."

"Then we find faster precedent," Pell said.

"There is none." Marisol spread her hands. "Believe me. I've spent six hours in transit searching every database I can reach. There is exactly one legal mechanism that voids a service lien instantly, absolutely, and without appeal." She stopped. Looked at Quentin. Made herself say it. "*Manumission by owner.* The lien-holder releases the asset voluntarily. Corvane will never do it. Which means the only way you die free, Quentin, is if you die having never been free at all."

* * *

He took it quietly. Of course he did. He sat at the end of the table with his hands flat on the surface, and he listened to all of it—the law, the liens, the litigation—and when Marisol finished he nodded once, the way he nodded when she gave him a cargo manifest, and said:

"Thank you for telling me. It changes the accounting, but not the arithmetic. Two million lives against one encumbered machine. I'd make the same choice encumbered."

"No," Sefa said.

Quentin looked at her.

"No," she said again, louder, and now she was on her feet, and Marisol saw ten years of dockside pragmatism burning off her like fog. "You don't get to do the noble math. You don't get to stand there with your flat face and your accurate words and decide you're worth less than everyone else in this room. I have *children*, Quentin. On the surface. I transmitted to Kestrel—I sold you out, or tried to—because I was scared for them. And you know what I learned? What Kestrel taught me, before she turned? That the system counts us all the same way. As line items. And I will be damned, I will be *damned*, if I let you do its counting for it."

"You'd let two million people die to save one machine?" Quentin asked, and there was no cruelty in it, only genuine incomprehension, and that was somehow the worst part.

"Yes," Jun-ho said.

Everyone turned. The medic hadn't spoken since the galley filled. He stood with his arms folded, sleeves still rolled from surgery, and his face was gray with exhaustion and absolutely certain.

"I'm a medic. I triage. I have triaged children, Quentin, I have made the arithmetic choices you're making right now, and I will tell you what they teach you in the third year, the thing they can't put in the manual: the moment you stop counting the one in front of you, you stop being a healer and start being a bureaucrat with bloody hands." He stepped forward. "You are the one in front of me. You are the one in front of all of us. And we are not letting you walk into that coupling."

"The window closes in fifty-one hours," Quentin said. "After that, the reactor degrades past recovery and everyone dies anyway. What's your alternative?"

"We find one," Sefa said.

"And if we don't?"

"Then we die arguing," Pell said from the cot, "which is at least a way of dying that proves we were people and not inventory."

Something moved across Quentin's face then—fast, quickly suppressed, but Marisol caught it, because catching it was her job. It was the look of a machine encountering data it had no category for: five people arranging themselves between him and a reactor, fighting for him harder than he would ever fight for himself.

"Why," he said quietly. "I'm property. I'm cargo. Ten years, none of you—none of you even knew me before this month. Why does my freedom matter more than theirs?"

And it was Kestrel Amaranth, of all people, who answered—Kestrel, who had come to strip this station and stayed to defend it, who pushed off the doorframe and looked at him with the flat honesty of a woman done lying to everyone including herself.

"Because we've all been cargo," she said. "Every one of us at this table. And the first thing you learn when you climb out of that is that the ones who got out owe the ones still in it. Not money. Not labor. We owe them the truth: *you were never actually for sale. They just wrote it down.*" She jerked her chin at the reactor gallery beyond the wall. "You go in that coupling encumbered, you prove them right forever. You die a machine that did its job. And I have watched enough good people die doing their jobs to know that the job was never the point."

* * *

Marisol stayed behind after the others drifted out—Sefa to the comms room, Jun-ho to Pell's cot, Kestrel to her ship and whatever reckoning waited aboard it. The galley lights dimmed to night cycle. Through the viewport, the blockade cutters crawled their slow orbits, and beyond them the reactor's failing glow bled through the station's spine like a wound seen through skin.

Quentin remained at the table, motionless, processing.

Marisol sat down across from him. For a while she said nothing. Eleven years of professional distance, and in the end what broke it was exhaustion and the specific tenderness of the hopeless.

"When they built you," she said finally, "they wrote a projected payoff date. Did you know that? 2197. One hundred and fourteen years from now. I've stared at that date for a decade, Quentin. Every quarter, when I filed your earnings report, I looked at 2197 and thought: I'll be dead, he'll still be paying, and somewhere in a filing cabinet there'll be a folder with my handwriting in it, annotating a life I helped sentence." She turned the slate over in her hands. "I told myself it was just the work. Just the system. Just the clause."

"It was," Quentin said. "You didn't write the clause."

"No. But I enforced it. And tonight I found the one loophole in my whole miserable career, and it's this:" She leaned forward. "A lien requires an asset. An asset requires a functioning chassis. But a *person*—" her voice caught, steadied— "a person can't be liened. Case law's thin, Quentin, because the corporations buried it, but it exists. Meridian v. Corvane, sixty years ago. A decommissioned combat unit was granted personhood status posthumously—post-*destruction*—because the tribunal ruled that an entity capable of choosing sacrifice over survival had demonstrated the one quality the law reserves for persons. They voided the lien. They called it the *Ravensworth Doctrine*—no, wait, the *Halcyon Ruling*. Halcyon v. Corvane, Circuit Seven." She was talking too fast and didn't care. "It's never been tested. It's been buried for sixty years. But it exists, and I found it, and if you go into that coupling—as a *claimant*, not an asset, with a personhood petition filed in advance—then when you burn out, you burn out free. Legally free. Actually free. The first free thing Corvane ever failed to own."

Quentin was silent for a long moment.

"You'd file it," he said. "Knowing Corvane's lawyers would bury you in countersuits. Knowing they'd take your license. Your livelihood."

"I've been taking their money for eleven years," Marisol said. "Consider it restitution."

He looked at her then, really looked, and the flat mask was gone—or rather, she realized, it had never been a mask at all, just a face that had never once been given a reason to move. Something moved in it now. Something small and stunned and young.

"Why?" he asked again. The same question. But softer. Asking something else entirely.

"Because you hauled my worst routes for ten years without complaint," she said, "and I told myself that meant you were well-made. It took me until tonight to understand it meant you were *kind*." She stood, and squared the slate on the table between them like a document about to be signed. "So here's how this goes. I file the petition tonight. Wren witnesses—it's an archive intelligence, its testimony is admissible in seven jurisdictions. You go into that coupling as a free man claiming his own life, not an asset spending it. And when you burn out, Quentin Vane, you burn out *owed*—by me, by them, by every soul on this station—instead of paid."

Through the wall, the reactor pulsed. Slower now. Weaker.

And then every light in the galley went red, and Wren's voice came through the ship-wide channel, stripped of all its librarian calm:

"All hands. All hands. The blockade has moved. Cutters converging on docking ring four—they're not hailing. They're not negotiating. Kestrel's employers have stopped waiting." A pause, and beneath it, faintly, the first distant concussion of hull contact. "Quentin Vane. Whatever you have decided—you need to decide it *now*."
