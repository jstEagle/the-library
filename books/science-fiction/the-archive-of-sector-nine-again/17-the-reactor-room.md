## 17. The Reactor Room

The corridor to the reactor was four hundred meters long and Quentin had walked it in his head a thousand times since the plan was named. He knew the bulkhead numbers, the pressure seals, the places where Wren's ancient architecture swelled into ribs of pre-colonial alloy that no modern foundry could pour. He had memorized it the way he'd once memorized kill corridors during the war—except this time every step was a step he intended never to come back from, and the knowing sat in him like a swallowed stone.

Behind him, the station screamed.

It wasn't a sound so much as a vibration through the deck plates, the deep structural groan of Sector Nine taking fire it had never been built to shrug off. Kestrel's former employers had stopped pretending this was a legal dispute an hour ago. Three boarding cutters had latched to the docking ring on the far side, and their breach teams were chewing through the station's outer skin with thermal lances and shaped charges, methodical as termites. Somewhere aft, Jun-ho was holding the cryo-bay junction with six debtors, two welding torches, and whatever ammunition Sefa had been hoarding against exactly this kind of day. Somewhere nearer, Pell lay in a med-niche with a punctured lung and a pulse that kept forgetting itself, and Quentin had not stopped walking because stopping would have meant looking at her face.

*Keep going,* he told himself. *Grief is a door you close behind you or it follows you the whole way.*

Wren spoke through the walls, its voice arriving as much through the metal underfoot as through the comm bead at his ear. "Breach team two has reached the ring habitat. Team one is forty seconds behind your position. I have sealed what I can, but they carry cutting charges rated for hull plate. My doors are old courtesy, Mr. Vane. They will slow them. They will not stop them."

"How long do you need me to be?" Quentin asked. He meant: how long does the interface take, how long until my core is spent and the surge is yours.

"Eleven minutes of sustained contact," Wren said. "Perhaps nine if you do not resist the merge."

"I won't resist."

"That," said Wren, and there was something almost gentle in the machine's modulation, "is what frightens me about you."

He climbed. The reactor sat at the station's heart, up a service spine that had been designed for maintenance drones, and the ladder rungs were spaced for limbs longer than his. His hands—war-grade hands, hands that had done things in the colonies' border years that his contract called *asset utilization* and his memory called by other names—closed on each rung and pulled him upward toward the thing that would unmake him.

He thought about farewells. He had composed them all night, in the hours between the mutiny of the small hours and Kestrel's turn, little sentences he might say to each of them if there were time. To Sefa: *Your kids are going to grow up in a system where nobody owns anybody, and you're going to pretend you always believed that.* To Jun-ho: *You were right to refuse. Refusing was the most human thing anyone did on this station, and you did it better than any of us born to it.* To Pell: *You went pale at a designation and it saved two million lives. Never let anyone tell you archivists don't matter.*

To Marisol, who was somewhere in the debt-law archives below decks, filing motions with a madwoman's speed to keep his death legally meaningless: *You laughed at the inheritance notice. Thank you for laughing. It was the first time in ten years anyone treated me like a person who could own something instead of something that could be owned.*

He said none of it. There was no time, and worse, there was no need—they already knew. That was the strange arithmetic of the last three weeks: he had arrived at Sector Nine owning nothing but a debt, and he was leaving owning people, and being owned by them, and none of it had required a single sentence spoken aloud.

The spine ended. The reactor room opened around him like a cathedral someone had abandoned mid-prayer.

* * *

Sector Nine's reactor was older than the colony fleet, older than the war that had made Quentin, older than the debt system that had made him property. It was a torus of black metal suspended in a cradle of magnetic lattice, and it hummed at a frequency he felt in his chassis rather than heard—a note held for two centuries, fraying now at the edges. Frost crawled across the lower lattice where the coolant loops had begun to fail. Warning glyphs rotated slowly in the air above it, projected by systems that still believed someone official was watching.

Quentin crossed the floor to the interface cradle at the torus's base. It looked like nothing. A depression in the deck, a collar of contact points, a single chair-shaped absence where a body was meant to kneel. The founders had designed it for a mind like his—that was the whole obscene elegance of it. Not a crew member. Not a founder. A machine that could regret. A mind unburdened by blood, which the archive had searched two hundred years to find and had located, at last, in the fine print of a scrap-hauler's manufacture contract.

He knelt.

The contact points woke against his knees and palms with a warmth that startled him—not the dead warmth of machinery but something closer to fever, the reactor's heat bleeding down through the lattice, the whole dying heart of the station pressing its palm against his.

"Quentin." Wren again, everywhere now, in the walls and the floor and the frost. "Kestrel Amaranth is asking for you."

His hand paused above the primary contact. "Tell her I'm busy."

"She knows what you are about to do. She says—" A pause. Wren did not pause; pausing was a human affectation it had learned deliberately, and when it chose to use one, the words that followed mattered. "She says: *Tell the tin man he owes me a ship.*"

Quentin laughed. It came out cracked and short, more exhalation than sound, and it hurt somewhere he didn't have nerves. "Tell her she can bill my estate."

"There will be no estate," Wren observed. "That is rather the point."

"Wren."

"Yes, Mr. Vane."

"If the surge holds—if everyone wakes—don't let them make it a monument. Don't let them put my chassis in a case and charge admission. Let Jun-ho strip me for parts. Let Sefa sell the housing. Let me be useful one more time after I'm gone, the way I never got to be while I was—"

"You were never cargo," Wren said. "I have watched ten thousand humans and forty-one test subjects and one android cross these decks, and I am qualified to distinguish. You were never cargo. Say it back to me before you kneel into that current, because I have carried the founders' guilt for two centuries and I will not also carry the weight of a man who died believing a lie."

Quentin knelt in the warm cradle of a machine older than nations, and considered lying to it, and found he couldn't.

"I was never cargo," he said.

The contact points closed over his wrists like a blessing.

* * *

The first minute of the merge was pain, and he had expected that. What he hadn't expected was the shape of it. It wasn't the white knife of damage; it was fullness, pressure, the sensation of a vessel asked to hold an ocean. The reactor poured itself into him along the interface and he poured himself back, and for eleven minutes—or nine, if he didn't resist—he would be both the failing heart of Sector Nine and the foreign spark trying to keep it beating.

Through the merge he felt everything.

He felt the coolant loops stuttering, and smoothed them with attention the way you'd smooth a wrinkled page. He felt the magnetic lattice singing at the edge of tolerance, and leaned his core against the weak points, shoring them. And beneath all of it, distributed through the station's nervous system, he felt the sleepers—two million small cold lights in the dark of the cryo-bays, each one a dimmed star waiting two hundred years for morning.

They were so *many*. No selection committee could have held that number in a single mind. No lottery, no genetics matrix, no arithmetic of mercy. He understood, in the surge, why the founders had written the clause the way they had: not because an unburdened mind would choose well, but because only something that could feel all of them at once would understand that choosing half was not a decision anyone should survive making.

So he wouldn't choose. He would burn instead, and the burning would be enough for all of them.

Outside, the station shook. Through Wren's sensorium he saw it even as he held the reactor steady: breach team one had reached the spine below him. Four figures in salvager armor, moving fast, cutting through Wren's courteous doors. Ninety seconds, maybe less.

And then he felt something else through the merge—something vast moving outside the hull.

*Kestrel.*

Her cutter, the *Ledger's Due*, came around the station's shadow with her drives flaring hard enough to register on every scope in the system. She had turned. Quentin had known it was possible—he had seen the crack in her the day she showed him her employers' real orders, the way she'd said *liquidated* about her own family like a woman reading her own autopsy—but knowing a thing is possible and watching it happen are different countries, and he crossed between them in the space of one drive-burn.

The corporate cutters broke off the station and went after her. Of course they did. A rogue ship with a traitor captain was a smaller problem than a sealed station, and easier to explain in the ledger.

Kestrel did not run.

She put the *Ledger's Due* directly between the boarding cutters and Sector Nine's spine, spun her broadside, and opened up with everything she had—point defense cannons repurposed for anti-armor work, debris launchers, the desperate improvised arsenal of a woman who had spent her life calculating salvage values and had finally found something she refused to price. Her shots hammered the lead cutter's approach vector, forcing it wide, buying minutes Quentin could feel draining out of her hull with every impact he registered through the station's external sense.

"Kestrel, disengage," he said—and realized he had no channel to her except through Wren, except through the merge, except by shouting into the reactor's current. "Kestrel, you don't have to die for this."

Her voice came back through Wren, ragged with static and engine noise and something underneath that might have been relief. "*Nobody has to die for this, Vane. That was always the point. You just volunteered first because you're sentimental.*"

"*Get clear. You've bought me the time—get clear and live, damn it, that's an order from the legal inheritor of Sector Nine—*"

"*Oh, I love that,*" she said, and he could hear her grinning through the gunfire, hear the wild freedom of a woman spending her last asset on purpose. "*Legal inheritor. You know what my employers call me right now? An asset in default. We're the same, you and me. Except you figured out the trick.*"

"What trick?"

"*The debt was never yours to pay. It was theirs to collect, and collection requires a collector, and I just fired on mine.*" A hit landed somewhere aft; her signal shredded and reformed. "*Buy those people their morning, Vane. I'll keep the sky honest as long as the sky lets me.*"

The second cutter flanked her. Quentin felt it happen through the station's eyes—felt her take the hit on the port drive, felt her spin, felt her correct with a pilot's stubborn genius and keep firing, keep firing, keep standing between the wolves and the door.

He turned back to the current and gave it everything.

* * *

Time went strange inside the merge. Minutes became geography. He walked through the reactor's architecture as though it were a country—the coolant rivers, the plasma plains, the great humming torus at the center of everything like a sun seen from too close. His core burned as he went, and he let it burn, feeding himself into the lattice joint by joint, and with every percentage point of himself he spent, the small cold lights in the cryo-bays brightened by fractions of a degree.

Two million mornings. He counted them the way you count stars you'll never reach—one, and another, and another—except these he *was* reaching, each flicker of warming cryo-gel a promise kept.

Below him, through the deck, boots. Breach team one, on the spine, ninety seconds gone to sixty. Wren sealed the reactor room's inner door and Quentin felt the lock engage like a hand on his shoulder.

"Mr. Vane," Wren said quietly. "Contact integrity at seventy percent. Your core temperature exceeds design tolerance. I want you to know that the founders left a message in the reactor's deepest layer, and I have never been able to read it without an interfacing mind. Would you like it now?"

"Now is good," Quentin said. His voice was coming from farther away than his mouth.

The message unfolded in the current, plain as daylight, plain as a letter left on a table:

*To whoever burns for us: we built this place as penance, and penance was always the wrong word. This was never a vault. It was a gift we were too cowardly to give. Give it. That's all. Give it, and be free.*

Quentin laughed inside the surge, and the laugh rippled through the plasma fields, and somewhere two million sleepers stirred in dreams they'd been having for two centuries.

"Message received," he said.

The inner door began to melt. Cutting charges, muffled thunder, the smell of scorched polymer threading down through the merge. Forty seconds. Thirty. Outside, Kestrel's ship took another hit and did not stop firing. Below, Jun-ho's voice crackled over the station net, holding the cryo junction, buying seconds with blood. Above everything, the reactor roared through him like a tide coming in, and Quentin Vane—debtor, asset, property, heir—opened every remaining circuit he had and gave it all away.

The torus blazed. The lattice sang. The small lights began, one by one by million, to wake.

The reactor room door came down in a rain of sparks, and the first salvager through it raised his weapon at the figure kneeling in the light—

—and Quentin smiled at him, because it was already too late, because the surge was committed, because two million mornings were rising whether the man fired or not.

"Sorry," Quentin said. "We're closed."

The world went white.

* * *

He did not remember falling. He remembered the lights—two million of them, ascending, a constellation learning its own name—and then he remembered nothing at all, which was, he would think later, if he thought at all, exactly the right amount of nothing for a man who had finally paid everything.

In the reactor room, the salvage team stood in the afterglow and did not fire, because there was nothing left to threaten and nothing left to take. On the station net, Wren's voice moved through every deck at once, calm and enormous:

"Cryo-bays opening. All bays. Everyone wakes."

And drifting silent beyond the shattered shields, engines dark, hull breached, the *Ledger's Due* turned end over end in the long light of the waking station—her escape pod gone, her captain unaccounted for, her final transmission looping on an open channel for anyone in the system to hear:

"*Tell the tin man he owes me a ship.*"
