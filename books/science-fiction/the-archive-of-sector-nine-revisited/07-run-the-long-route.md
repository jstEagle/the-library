## 7. Run the Long Route

The shuttle was called the *Mule*, which felt honest of it. It sat in Meridian Downport's long-stay bay like something left behind on purpose — a forty-year-old cargo tug with a scuffed hull, a cargo cradle built for ore pallets, and an engine that predated the Concord's current emissions standards by a decade and possibly two governments. Nobody guarded it because nobody wanted it.

I wanted it the way drowning people want driftwood.

Getting aboard was almost insultingly easy. The port authority had pulled every officer with a functioning conscience to process the boarding actions in the commercial docks — I'd watched three of them march Osei's colleagues past my hiding spot in a maintenance crawlspace, their confiscated data cores carried in sealed cases like evidence at a trial, which is exactly what they were. That left the long-stay bays to a skeleton crew and a door lock that still answered to a maintenance code my grandmother had taught me when I was fourteen, back when she was teaching me everything else about how to move through a world that didn't want to notice you.

"Port code never gets changed," she'd said, tapping the panel. "Because changing it would mean admitting someone might want to break it. Bureaucracies would rather be robbed than embarrassed."

Grandmother Quintana had been dead eleven years, and she was still the most useful person in my life.

I climbed into the pilot's chair, which smelled like cold coffee and someone else's cigarettes, and did the thing you do before you steal a ship: I talked to it.

"All right, Mule," I said. "Nobody's going to miss you. Let's keep it that way."

* * *

Leaving Meridian legally requires a flight plan, a manifest, a customs stamp, and a patience I no longer possessed. Leaving illegally requires one thing: knowing where the sensors aren't.

Every colony world has a sensor shadow. The arrays are built to watch the approach corridors, the shipping lanes, the places where money moves — because that's what security actually guards, whatever the brochures say. The rest of the sky is assumed empty. And on the night side of Meridian, past the old mining approaches that hadn't been used since the second expansion, there was a gap in coverage shaped like a wound: a cone of unwatched dark where the planet's own radiation noise swallowed anything small and quiet.

My grandmother had flown out through that gap for twenty years. She'd called it "the servant's entrance."

I brought the Mule up on thrusters alone, riding the gravity well down instead of fighting it, letting Meridian's bulk hide me from the stations above. My hands were shaking so badly on the yoke that I made myself narrate what I was doing, out loud, in the flat voice I used for seed-vault inventory checks.

"Thruster balance nominal. Descent rate four meters per second. Hull temperature within tolerance." A pause. "Pilot is terrified but functional."

The Mule didn't answer. Ships never do, but I've always found they listen better if you give them something to respond to.

At twelve kilometers altitude I cut thrust entirely and fell. For six full minutes I dropped along the night side in freefall, engines cold, transponder dark, doing my best impression of a piece of slag — and somewhere around minute four, as Meridian's terminator crawled across the viewport and the stars came out hard and white, I realized I was holding my breath and laughing at the same time, quietly, the way you laugh when your body has discovered it's still alive and hasn't consulted you about it.

Then I lit the drive and ran.

* * *

Here's what nobody tells you about fleeing: it's mostly bookkeeping.

By hour two I had stopped shaking and started planning, because fear is a fuel that burns fast and hot and leaves nothing behind, whereas terror converted into checklists will carry you across a system. I inventoried the Mule. She had a fusion torch rated for hauling ore, which meant she could outrun anything her own size and lose gracefully to anything bigger. She had life support for six, water recyclers older than me, and a food locker containing, in its entirety: forty ration bars, three cans of protein paste, and a jar of pickled something whose label had worn off. I decided the jar was a problem for Future Jude and sealed it back into the dark corner it had come from.

She also had charts. Real ones — paper-thin polymer sheets in a case under the nav console, survey plates from the first expansion, the kind of thing museums kept and freighters threw away. Someone aboard this Mule, decades ago, had been the kind of pilot who kept their grandmother's maps.

I know the type. I was raised by her.

And folded into the case, brittle at the creases, was a hand-annotated route plate labeled in faded ink: **LONG ROUTE — QUIET LANES — Q.**

Q. Quintana. My chest did something complicated about that, and I put it away to deal with later, because later was a country I was hoping to reach alive.

The Long Route wasn't on any current chart. Of course it wasn't — that was the point. Before the fleets, before the Concord and the Vantage drew their borders across the Reach like rival children partitioning a bedroom, cargo moved on routes flown by memory and stubbornness: lanes threaded between the debris fields, the failed colonies, the wrecks of the Collapse that everyone else had agreed to stop looking at. The official histories said those regions were empty. The official histories were written by people who needed them empty.

My grandmother had flown the Long Route her whole working life. She'd tried to teach it to me once, when I was nineteen and too busy being angry at the Concord to listen properly. I remembered fragments. Waypoints sung rather than written, because written waypoints get confiscated. Landmarks that weren't landmarks — a pulsar's flicker rhythm, a wreck's silhouette against a nebula, the way a debris field "sounded" on passive radar if you knew its texture. She'd called it reading the road by feel.

"You can't navigate the Long Route," she'd told me. "You can only know it. There's a difference, and the difference is whether you live."

I spread the route plate across the console and weighted its corners with ration bars, and I looked at the line her pen had drawn sixty years ago, curving away from Meridian into the marked-as-void dark, and I understood that I had not chosen this escape route so much as inherited it.

Somewhere ahead of me lay Sector Nine. Somewhere behind me lay a watchlist with my name on it. Between us lay the Long Route, and my grandmother's handwriting, and a jar of unidentified preserves.

I pointed the Mule at the dark and burned.

* * *

For three days, nothing happened, which is the best possible outcome in space travel and the least appreciated.

I slept in shifts too short to be healthy. I ate ration bars and pretended they were food. I ran diagnostics, checked the seed-vault telemetry — the vaults themselves were gone, seized at Meridian with everything else, but the Mule's systems still held the last sync, and I caught myself checking the numbers the way you check a dead relative's voicemail, just to hear the shape of the ordinary — and I decoded more of the flicker residue on the salvaged handheld core I'd smuggled out inside a maintenance toolkit.

The signal was still there, patient as a fossil. Nine seconds of gravitational stutter, and threaded through it, a structure. Coordinates. A region every chart in the Reach marked as void, and a signature underneath that didn't match any fleet protocol I knew — older, denser, layered like sediment. Whatever had blinked the universe, it lived out here, in the dark my grandmother's pen had known about before I did.

On the fourth day, the road tried to kill me.

* * *

It started as a texture change on passive radar. I'd been monitoring it the way she taught me without ever formally teaching me — not reading contacts, just listening to the character of the return, the way you'd hear a change in someone's breathing. For three days the Long Route had sounded like the inside of a quiet room: dust, micrometeorite hash, the soft static of a nebula's edge. Now there was a graininess to it. A grit.

I throttled down and pulled up the old survey plates, cross-referencing by the pulsar rhythm I'd been using as a clock. The plates told me what I already suspected: ahead lay the Kestrel Field — or what the plates called the Kestrel Debris Zone, surveyed once, ninety years ago, and never again, because there was nothing there worth the fuel.

Except debris fields don't stay put. Ninety years of collisions, orbital drift, and slow gravitational sorting had taken whatever the Kestrel used to be and ground it into something new. The plates showed a sparse scatter. Passive radar showed a wall.

I killed the drive and drifted, and did the math I didn't want to do.

The Long Route threaded the Kestrel Field through a corridor maybe four hundred kilometers wide, back when it was four hundred kilometers wide. If the debris had consolidated even modestly — and the radar texture said it had consolidated a lot — the corridor now might be fifty kilometers. Might be ten. Might not exist.

Going around meant days of extra burn, out in the open, visible to any patrol with a decent scope, burning fuel I couldn't spare on a detour that led nowhere except closer to being found. Going through meant threading a needle woven from ninety years of shrapnel, at speed, in a stolen tug, alone.

I sat with that choice for eleven minutes. I know because I counted, the way I count seconds when the universe goes strange.

Then I thought about Osei slipping that warning chit into my toolkit with his face carefully blank, spending his career like a coin he'd never see again. I thought about my name on a watchlist, and officers carrying data cores in evidence cases, and a universe that had blinked for nine seconds while everyone in authority rushed to decide what the blink *meant* before anyone asked what it *was*. Every institution I had ever trusted was currently trying to fit me into a story they'd already written. The only people who moved freely through this Reach were the ones who read the road by feel.

My grandmother flew this field. Not this exact field — the field it used to be. But she'd flown it with worse ships and better reasons, and she'd taught me the only inheritance that mattered: when the chart and the truth disagree, believe the truth, and forgive yourself afterward.

"Okay," I told the Mule. "We're going through. Don't take it personally if we die."

* * *

Threading debris isn't flying. Flying is a conversation between you and physics conducted at mutual convenience. Threading debris is an argument where physics holds all the leverage and you spend the whole time trying to say something interesting before it loses patience.

I went in at a quarter thrust, slow enough to react, fast enough to steer — because a ship moving too slowly through debris isn't safe, it's just a target with a longer exposure time. The trick, the thing my grandmother had tried to pound into a nineteen-year-old who wasn't listening, is that you don't dodge individual rocks. You can't; there are too many, and they're too fast, and by the time you've identified one it's already history. You dodge *patterns*. You read the flow of the field the way water reads a riverbed, and you put yourself in the seams — the places where the collisions have already happened and cleared a lane, however briefly, however narrow.

The seams close. That's the other thing. A seam that's open when you enter it may be shut by the time you arrive, so you're never navigating the field as it is. You're navigating the field as it will be in ninety seconds, based on what it was doing ninety seconds ago, and the only instrument that can make that prediction is the one sitting in the pilot's chair making gut decisions at four hertz.

Passive radar painted the near field in ghost-gray blobs. I turned the resolution down until it stopped looking like data and started looking like weather, because that's how you have to see it — not objects, but currents. Grit everywhere. Then a darker patch, a hole in the hash, drifting left. A seam.

"Mule," I said, "banking left, thirty degrees, on my mark. Trust me."

The Mule banked. Something the size of a filing cabinet tumbled past our starboard side close enough that I could see the weld lines on it — a hull fragment, old, corroded green, some ship that had died here before either fleet existed. I logged it without logging it, the way you note a landmark on a road you hope to never drive twice.

For two hours I flew like that. Hands light. Breath shallow. Narrating in the inventory voice, because the inventory voice doesn't shake: "Seam opening right, closing in approximately forty seconds, committing. Drift correction. Thruster pulse, half-second. Seam narrowing, abandoning, seeking alternate." The words became a rhythm, and the rhythm became a kind of calm, and the calm was a lie I was telling with my mouth so my hands wouldn't hear what my spine knew.

Then the seam I was committed to closed early.

I saw it happen — saw the two big masses flanking my lane shift, a slow collision beginning somewhere ahead, shedding fragments backward down the corridor like a door slamming in slow motion. The math arrived all at once and it was simple: the lane would be choked in roughly thirty seconds, and I was eleven seconds from the choke point, and there was no way through, over, or around that my conscious mind could find.

So I stopped using my conscious mind.

There's a moment in every crisis where thinking becomes the enemy — where the part of you that calculates is slower than the part of you that simply knows, some animal arithmetic running on hardware older than language. I let go of the yoke's logic and took hold of its instinct. I rolled the Mule ninety degrees — presenting her narrow profile to the fragment stream — and dove *into* the debris cloud itself, into the churn, where the pieces were thickest and therefore, paradoxically, the flows were most predictable, because dense fields sort themselves fast. Everything dangerous was big and slow and telegraphed. Everything fast was small enough to shrug off.

For nine seconds — I counted, of course I counted — the Mule fell through a storm of shrapnel with her engines screaming, hull ringing like a bell as gravel-size impacts stitched across her plating, and I flew her the way my grandmother's hands must have flown her ships: not seeing, not calculating, *knowing*, each correction arriving a half-beat before the reason for it did.

Then the churn spat us out the far side into clean dark, and the silence rang louder than the impacts had.

I sat there for a full minute with my heart slamming and my hands welded to the yoke, and then I did the post-flight checks with the inventory voice, which had gone hoarse: "Hull integrity, eighty-seven percent. Port thruster response degraded but present. Life support nominal. Pilot—" I stopped. Started over. "Pilot is fine. Pilot is more than fine."

And the strange thing, the thing I keep turning over since, is that it was true. Somewhere in that cloud, somewhere between the eleventh second and the ninth, the fear had finished converting. What was left in my chest wasn't courage exactly — courage implies a choice made calmly. It was competence. The specific, hard-won knowledge that when the road closes, I am the kind of person who finds the seam, and that this knowledge lives deeper than any register, any archive, any record anyone keeps of me.

They could confiscate my data cores. They could put my name on watchlists and erase it from manifests. They could not take the nine seconds out of my hands.

* * *

I limped the Mule to a coasting orbit behind a dead moonlet at the field's edge and let her cool while I assessed the damage. Eighty-seven percent hull. A port thruster running rough. A long scrape across the dorsal plating where something large had kissed us goodbye. And, when I finally opened the food locker to celebrate survival with a ration bar, the jar of pickled something had shattered, and the smell was indescribable, and I laughed until I cried, alone in a stolen tug in the middle of nowhere, which is probably the most honest I've been in years.

While the thruster cooled, I unfolded my grandmother's route plate again and traced the line forward. Past the Kestrel Field, the Long Route bent toward a set of coordinates annotated in her cramped hand: a waypoint with no name, just a symbol I didn't recognize and a note that read, in ink gone brown with age:

**Quiet harbor. Ask for Calla. Tell her the garden remembers.**

I stared at that for a long time.

I had never heard of a Calla. I had certainly never heard my grandmother use the phrase *the garden remembers* — and yet she'd written it decades before I ever tended a genebank vault, decades before seeds became the center of my life, as if she'd known what I would become. As if the route she'd drawn wasn't just a path through space but a message with a delivery date she couldn't have known, addressed to whoever in her family ended up needing it.

My mother's face surfaced in my memory — estranged, unreachable, another name I couldn't safely transmit to — and I pushed it down. One mystery per day. Today's mystery was a harbor.

I fed the waypoint coordinates into the ancient nav computer, and the Mule considered them with the enthusiasm of a retiree asked to hike, and settled onto a burn vector toward a stretch of void that every modern chart insisted contained nothing at all.

Behind me: two fleets, a watchlist, and a war being assembled out of a lie. Ahead of me: a derelict harbor full of people the records had erased, waiting for a woman who'd been dead eleven years to send them a gardener.

"Long Route," I said to the dark, and to her, wherever she was. "I'm coming. Tell the garden I remember too."

The Mule burned on into the uncharted black, and somewhere far behind us, unseen, something began to track our wake.
