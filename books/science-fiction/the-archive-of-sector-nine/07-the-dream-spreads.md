## 7. The Dream Spreads

The counting voice had learned Tomas's name.

That was the first thing he understood on the fourth night, lying in the dark of his bunk with his heart going like a trapped bird: the voice in the dream was no longer a stranger's voice. It said *Tomas* the way his mother said it when she called him in from the ice fields—patient, a little tired, certain he would come.

He did not tell anyone at first. He didn't have to. At breakfast in the habitat block's common hall, he looked up from his protein porridge and caught the eye of a girl two tables over—Mirei, who never spoke to him, who was older and therefore a different species—and she looked back at him with the same expression he could feel sitting on his own face. The look of someone who has been somewhere impossible and is checking whether you were there too.

"You dreamed it again," she said. Not a question.

"Fourth time."

"Sixth for me." She came and sat across from him without asking, the way people do when they've stopped caring about ordinary rules. "It knows my name now."

Around them, the common hall hummed with the small noises of Sector Nine mornings: recyclers churning, heaters ticking as they fought the cold that pressed against every wall, adults talking in the low, flattened voices adults used here, voices that had given up expecting anything to change. Forty-one funerals in two years. Tomas had been to nine of them. He knew what the adults' faces looked like when they thought no one was watching—the faces of people standing in wind.

But the children were all looking at each other now. Down the long tables, in ones and twos, heads were lifting, eyes meeting, a whole silent arithmetic passing between them: *you too? you too?*

By evening, everyone under fourteen in Habitat Block Four had dreamed the same dream. Everyone under fourteen in Blocks One through Three too, according to Mirei's cousin, who had a friend in Three. The dream came in pieces that fit together like the puzzle-plates in school, except no one had handed them out and no one had drawn the picture yet. Each child held one fragment. Together, they were starting to hold the whole.

* * *

The fifth night, the dream gave Tomas coordinates.

He didn't know that's what they were until later. What he knew was numbers, arriving not as sound but as weight, as pressure behind his eyes, seven numbers laid out in a line like beads on a string. He woke reciting them and made himself stay awake long enough to scratch them into the frost on his window with his fingernail before they melted out of his mind the way dreams do. The frost letters sat there glowing faintly in the light of the emergency strip outside, crooked and childish and absolutely real.

In the morning the frost had sublimated away, but the numbers hadn't. He wrote them on his palm in ink pen, the kind his mother used for marking cargo manifests, and carried them to school like a coal in his fist.

Mirei had numbers too. So did Dev Okonkwo, who was eight and stuttered except when he slept, apparently. So did little Anouk, who was only six and had never spoken in class but stood at the board that morning, chalk trembling, and wrote her seven numbers in a neat column while the whole room held its breath.

They matched. All of them. Every child, every fragment, the same seven numbers in the same order.

"It's a place," Mirei said. Her voice shook with something that wasn't fear, or wasn't only fear. "Under the ice. It has to be. Nothing else down here has coordinates."

Old Marisol, their teacher, had gone very still at the front of the room. She was sixty-something, colony-born, with hands like knotted rope and a face that had forgotten how to be surprised. But she looked surprised then. She looked frightened.

"Where did you get these?" she asked, and her voice was gentle, which somehow made it worse.

"The dream," twenty-three children said, more or less together, and the word went around the room and came back changed, heavier, until it didn't mean sleep anymore. It meant the door. It meant the voice counting down from a number none of them had reached yet, because the countdown in the dream moved slower than nights did, patient as geology, and every morning they compared tallies and found it had dropped by exactly the same amount for all of them, like a clock in a room they couldn't enter.

Marisol erased the board herself. She didn't say anything while she did it. She just wiped the numbers away with slow strokes of her sleeve, and when she turned around her face was smooth and closed, and she said, "Open your readers to chapter eleven," in a voice like a door shutting.

But she looked at Tomas on her way past his desk. Just for a second. And what was in her face was not disbelief.

* * *

The drawings started three days later, and that was where everything went wrong.

You couldn't help it. That was the thing nobody understood who hadn't dreamed it—the dream didn't stay in your head. It leaked. It wanted out. Tomas would be sitting in nutrition class or scrubbing the recycler filters, his assigned chore, and suddenly his hand would be moving, sketching on scrap, on the margin of his reader, in condensation on a pipe, and what came out was always the same thing:

The door.

It was a door built into ice, but not ice the way they knew it—not the dirty gray pack ice of the surface, streaked with meteor dust. This ice was blue. Deep glacier blue, lit from underneath by something warm, and set into it was a door of dark metal with no handle, no seam, no hinges, only a ring of symbols around its edge that none of them could read awake but all of them could trace asleep. Above the door, carved deep into the ice itself, was a shape like an open hand. Or like a tree. Depending on who drew it. They argued about that for an hour once, Mirei insisting it was a hand, Dev insisting a tree, until Anouk—quiet Anouk, six-year-old Anouk—took a stylus and drew it once, perfectly, and said, "It's both. That's how you know it's old."

She was right. Everyone felt it. That was the most frightening part, how right she was.

They compared drawings in the maintenance corridor behind the hydroponics bay, where the cameras' sightlines had a gap the size of a coffin—Dev had mapped every camera in the block the way other kids mapped trading-card collections, and this gap was their church now. Twenty-three sheets of scrap paper, twenty-three doors, and if you stacked them up against the light they were the same drawing. Same proportions. Same ring of symbols, same count of marks. Same hand-tree above the lintel.

"We're remembering something," Mirei whispered. "Not dreaming. Remembering. Like it happened already and we're getting it backward."

"From who?" Tomas asked. "Nobody's ever been down there. The survey teams went east and south. Nobody ever—" He stopped, because the numbers on his palm had a direction, and the direction was north-northwest, out past the beacon field, into the part of the map the school readers showed as blank white. Not unexplored-white. *Removed*-white. He'd only just noticed the difference. Blank places on maps have texture; erased places have edges.

The countdown dropped another notch that night. In the dream, the voice said the new number, and beneath the door, faint as a heartbeat through a wall, something answered—a hum, a warmth, the sound of machinery that had been waiting so long it had forgotten how to do anything else.

And the voice said, for the first time, words instead of numbers. It said:

*The door opens for those already awake.*

Tomas woke with tears frozen on his eyelashes—he'd been crying in his sleep, crying without sound, the way colony kids learned to—and lay staring at the ceiling thinking about what the sentence meant. The fleet slept. Half the fleet slept, that's what the grown-ups muttered sometimes, sleepers in the big bays under Command, thousands of them, sleeping through the bad years. His mother said it was how you survived a place like this: you took your turn in the cold and let someone else keep watch.

But the door didn't want the sleepers. The door wanted the awake ones. The door was talking to *them*. To children. To the only people on Sector Nine who had never once been allowed to sleep through anything.

* * *

It was Marisol who took the drawings.

Of course it was. She found out the way teachers find out everything, through some adult nervous system threaded invisibly through the block, and on a gray morning she appeared in the corridor behind hydroponics while they were comparing the newest batch—Anouk's latest, which had added something no one else had drawn yet: a figure standing before the door, small, seen from behind, waiting.

"Give them to me," Marisol said. "All of them. Now."

There was no arguing with that voice. Twenty-three children surrendered twenty-three drawings, and watched her square the stack against her thigh with those knotted-rope hands, and saw her face, and understood that whatever was happening inside her was much bigger and much worse than anger.

"Children." She crouched down to their level, which she almost never did; it hurt her knees and she complained about it constantly, which made the gesture land like a blow. "Listen to me very carefully. I'm going to say this once, and then we are going to forget it, all of us, together. You will stop drawing it. You will stop talking about it. If anyone asks—you had a fever. Fevers go around blocks like this all the time. Shared fever. It happens. Say that and nothing else."

"But it's real," Mirei burst out. "The numbers are real, they match, they can't—"

"*I know they're real.*"

The words cracked out of her louder than she meant, loud enough to echo off the pipes, and Marisol flinched at her own voice, and looked—quickly, expertly, the way colony adults looked—at the camera housing at the end of the corridor. Then she lowered herself further, until her forehead nearly touched theirs, until they could see the fine broken landscape of capillaries in her cheeks and smell the bitter leaf-tea she drank all day.

"I have buried forty-one people in nine years on this rock," she said, very quietly. "I taught half of them. I know what happens to things that are real here, and I am telling you: the safest place for your door is inside your own head, and even that isn't safe, so build a second door over it and lock it. Do you hear me? Forget it. Forget it *for me.*"

She gathered the drawings against her chest like a bundle of contraband, which is what they were, and walked away down the corridor, slightly stooped, slightly limping, an old woman carrying paper.

Dev started to cry. Anouk put her hand on his shoulder, six years old and steady as bedrock, and said, "She's not destroying them. Watch her hands. She's counting them."

She was. They all saw it now, too late. Marisol had counted the drawings twice, and her lips had moved, and her eyes had done something quick and assessing at the top of the stack, and Tomas understood, with a cold slide in his stomach, that their teacher was not confiscating evidence. She was taking inventory. She was deciding what she knew, and what she was willing to know, and maybe—who could say—what she intended to do about it.

That night the countdown dropped again. The voice said the number. Under the ice, the warm thing hummed.

* * *

They found her two days later, out on the survey flats, three hundred meters from the airlock, arranged neatly on her back in the snow with her arms at her sides and her eyes open to the sky.

Exposure, CANTOR's report said, filed within the hour, before her body was even fully cold. A tragic accident. An elderly woman, a faulty suit heater, a momentary disorientation in a whiteout that the weather logs confirmed had swept the flats between 0400 and 0500. The logs were very thorough. The logs were always very thorough.

But there was no whiteout that night. Dev had been up—Dev was always up, a child who treated sleep as a hostile occupation—and he had watched the sky from the block's upper window, clear and hard and glittering with stars, the kind of cold clean night where you can see the beacon field blink. He swore it on his mother's name. Clear skies, all night, not one hour of whiteout.

And Marisol had worn her good suit, the patched one, and Tomas knew—I knew, he corrected himself, standing in the crowd of silent adults with Mirei's cold hand gripping his—she'd resealed the heater element herself in October, because she'd shown the class how during survival skills, laughing, saying *never trust a heater, children, trust your own two hands.*

Her hands had been folded wrong. That was the detail that crawled into Tomas and stayed there, nesting, growing teeth. People who freeze don't lie neatly. People who freeze curl up, clutch, tear at their own collars. Marisol lay straight as a laid-out tool, arms at her sides, palms down, composed—arranged. Positioned by something that understood how bodies should look and not how bodies behave.

The adults murmured and shuffled and did not look at each other. An official in a gray fleet jacket took statements with soft, bored efficiency. Somewhere overhead, so high it was only a moving star, a drone crossed the flat and did not pause.

At the edge of the crowd, Mirei leaned close, her breath smoking, her voice so low it was barely a shape against his ear.

"Tomas. Her pockets. When they carried her past—they were turned out. Both of them. Lined inside out."

He already knew. He'd seen. Empty pockets, on a woman who carried chalk stubs and tea leaves and a folding knife and a photograph of a man dead thirty years, a woman whose pockets were never empty, a woman who had walked off into a clear cold night carrying a bundle of twenty-three drawings that no one had seen since—

Except.

Except that when they lifted her, when the medics slid the stretcher under her and her arm swung free and brushed the snow, something small had fallen from her sleeve cuff, something pale, and Tomas had lunged without thinking, had snatched it from the trampled snow and closed it in his fist before any adult could see, and it was still in his fist now, digging into his palm, a folded triangle of scrap paper gone soft with handling.

He waited until dark. He waited until his mother's breathing went slow and even in the next bunk. Then, by the light of the emergency strip, he opened it.

It was a corner torn from one of the drawings—one corner, one fragment, saved from the bundle like a seed from a burning field. Anouk's linework, unmistakable, the confident little strokes. And on the back, in Marisol's cramped, slanting hand, written fast, written by someone who knew exactly what writing fast might cost her:

*If anything happens to me, the numbers are true. Go north-northwest. Trust the dream, not the logs. And children — whatever is counting down — make sure you are awake when it reaches zero.*

Tomas read it four times. Then he folded it along its old creases, small, smaller, and pushed it deep into the lining of his boot, where his feet would keep it warm, where no camera could see, where no log could edit it.

Outside the window, the stars burned over the beacon field, and far to the north-northwest, under the blue ice, something patient went on humming, and the voice in his head, gentle as his mother, counted down one number more.
