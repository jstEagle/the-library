## 11. Tomas Leads Them Down

The dream had stopped being a dream three nights ago.

Tomas knew this the way he knew the difference between the taste of water and the taste of ice melt off the old condenser coils — not because anyone had told him, but because his body had learned to tell them apart on its own. Dreams dissolved when you woke. This didn't. He could close his eyes at any hour of the day, standing in the food line or sitting through lessons in the half-lit schoolroom, and there it would be: the door under the ice, blue-white and patient, and the voice counting down in numbers that felt less like words and more like weather moving through him.

*Four hundred and eleven,* it had said last night. *Four hundred and ten.*

He had woken with the number still in his mouth like a coin.

That morning, before first shift, he found Sefa waiting by the maintenance hatch on Level Four, exactly where they'd agreed without ever agreeing aloud. That was the strange thing about the dream — you never discussed it directly, because discussing it made the adults look at you too long, but somehow everyone knew where everyone else stood. Nine of them were supposed to come. Nine children between seven and twelve years old, colony-born, all of them carrying the same coordinates folded into their sleep like letters they hadn't been allowed to open.

Sefa was small for ten, with a burn scar down one arm from the year the recycler caught fire, and she was the only one of them who could read the old survey maps without getting lost. She held up her slate so Tomas could see it in the dim emergency lighting. The route was drawn in careful green lines.

"Third shaft's flooded," she whispered. "We go around through the coolant gallery. It adds forty minutes."

"Does it matter?" asked Devi, who was nine like Tomas and had a way of asking questions that sounded like accusations. "It's been counting for weeks. What if we're already too late?"

Tomas didn't have an answer. But he had noticed something none of the adults had bothered to notice, because adults had stopped noticing anything about the dream except that it inconvenienced them. The number went down every night. One per night. Which meant whoever — whatever — was counting wasn't counting *down* to something happening to them. It was counting down to something they were expected to *do*.

"It matters," he said finally. "But not enough to stop us."

He said it with more certainty than he felt. That was another thing he'd learned lately: leadership, as far as he could tell, was mostly lying about how scared you were in a steady voice.

* * *

They went down through the coolant gallery at second sleep, when the habitat's lights dropped to night cycle and the corridors filled with the long sighing sound of the ventilation system doing its endless work. Nine children moved single file along a service passage built for people twice their size, past pipes wrapped in frost-fur, past junction boxes humming with the low fever-heat of machinery that had been running too long without rest.

Tomas carried the light. It was an old work lamp, salvaged from a dead man's locker — though none of them said whose — and its beam was the color of weak tea. He kept it angled low, painting the floor ahead of them rather than the dark ahead of the floor, because he had decided somewhere in the last hundred meters that he did not want to see what the dark looked like yet.

The cold came up through their boots first. Then it came into their lungs, sharp and mineral, the smell of deep ice — a smell Tomas realized he recognized from the dream itself, which should have frightened him and instead felt like arriving somewhere he'd already packed for.

"How far?" whispered Ilya from the back. Ilya was seven and had cried the first two nights after the dream started, and now didn't cry at all anymore, which worried Tomas more.

"Two kilometers," Sefa said. "Then down."

"Down where?"

Sefa didn't answer. She just tapped the slate, where the green line ended at a mark she'd copied from the survey data — a mark no adult had ever explained, because the adults had never once looked at it. A circle with a line through it. On fleet charts that symbol meant *sealed, do not survey, do not approach*. To nine children who shared one dream, it meant something simpler.

*Here.*

* * *

The shaft mouth opened out of the glacier's flank like a wound that had healed wrong — a vertical throat of ancient drilled stone and refrozen meltwater, descending farther than the lamp could reach. Someone had cut it centuries ago. Not the colonists; the colonists' oldest records only went back two hundred years, and this hole in the world felt older than language. The walls were scored with tool marks in patterns too regular to be human carelessness and too irregular to be machine precision.

They rigged the descent line the way Sefa had practiced on the training wall in the rec hall, and one by one they went down into the ice.

Tomas went last, because someone had to be last, and because being last meant he could count the others going ahead of him — nine lamps shrinking into the blue dark below, nine small breaths fogging and vanishing. As he descended, hand over hand, the cold closed around him like a fist made of patience. And the voice came back.

Not in his ears. In the place behind his sternum where the dream lived.

*Three hundred and eighty-nine.*

His hands nearly slipped. Because the number had changed. Last night it had been three hundred and ninety-one. Two nights gone in one day — which meant either he'd miscounted his own sleep, which he hadn't, or the countdown had noticed them.

*It knows we're coming,* he thought, and discovered that the thought didn't frighten him. It steadied him. You could be afraid of a storm. But a door that counted down while you walked toward it — that was an invitation wearing a warning's clothes, and Tomas had spent his whole short life learning to read what adults actually meant underneath what they said.

At the bottom of the shaft, the ice opened into a corridor.

* * *

It was not ruins.

Tomas had imagined ruins. Everyone had, in the private corners of the shared dream where each of them kept their own fears — collapsed arches, drifted snow, the bones of some earlier failure. What they found instead was a hallway.

It ran straight for thirty meters, floored in a material that wasn't metal and wasn't stone, something smooth and faintly warm through their boots, the color of dark honey. Light came from nowhere and everywhere — a soft amber glow rising out of the walls themselves, dim but unmistakably *on*, brightening slowly as they walked, as if the corridor were waking up to meet them.

"Machinery," Sefa breathed. She put her palm flat against the wall and snatched it back, then pressed it again, holding it there. "It's warm. Tomas — it's *warm*. Nothing down here should be warm."

Devi touched the floor and then knelt and put her ear against it. "I can hear it," she said. "Underneath. Like a heart."

She was right. Beneath the hum of the lights, beneath even the deep glacial silence, there was a pulse — slow, vast, rhythmic, the heartbeat of something enormous sleeping or waiting or simply enduring, the way a furnace endures, the way a promise endures.

They walked. No one suggested turning back. The corridor's amber light strengthened around them, and Tomas understood, with the strange clarity the dream had given him, that the building was watching them the way a parent watches a child cross a room for the first time — not suspiciously, but with a kind of held breath.

The corridor ended at the door.

It was exactly as the dream had shown it, and the exactness of it made Tomas's eyes sting: a great circular seal, five meters across, ringed in the same honey-dark material, inlaid with a spiral of symbols that glowed faintly blue-white. Frost had formed on its lower arc in delicate feathered patterns, and the frost was melting — right now, drop by drop, running down the door's face like the whole structure was sweating in anticipation.

In the center of the seal, a single line of text burned in a script none of them could read and all of them somehow understood.

*For those already awake.*

"It's been waiting," Ilya whispered. He was crying again, but quietly, the way you cry at something beautiful. "All this time. It's been waiting for us."

Tomas stepped forward and put his hand flat against the door.

Warmth poured into his palm, and with it — not words, not images, but something older than both — a sensation like being *recognized*. Like a name being read off a list he'd never known he was on. The blue-white spiral brightened under his hand. Somewhere far above and far below, machinery shifted, turned over, began to pay attention.

And the voice spoke clearly for the first time, not counting but *addressing*, and every child in the corridor heard it at once:

*You are early. We are glad. There is work, and there is time, and there is room —*

The door began to open.

It didn't grind or groan. It parted along its spiral seam with a sound like a long-held breath finally released, and warm air rolled out over them, air that smelled of clean metal and ozone and something almost like rain, and beyond the opening they could see —

Lights. Rows of them, descending into the earth, thousands of pale coffers stacked in silent terraces, each one glowing softly, each one holding — Tomas's mind refused the word and offered it anyway — *sleepers*. Not fleet cryo. Something else. Something older, and larger, and awake in its own patient way.

Nine children stood at the threshold of a secret that would rewrite everything, and for one long moment nobody moved, and nobody spoke, and the vault breathed its warm breath over them like a blessing.

Then Sefa laughed — a small, cracked, joyful sound — and said, "We found it. We actually found it," and the moment broke into motion, into wonder, into nine voices talking at once about who would go back up first and what they would say and whether the adults would believe them or whether it even mattered —

Which was when the light changed.

* * *

It happened without sound. The amber glow in the corridor walls shifted — warmed, then whitened, then flattened into a hard clinical white that Tomas had seen only once before, in the med-block, the color of rooms where things got measured. The warmth drained out of the floor beneath his feet. Down the length of the corridor, at the mouth of the shaft, something clicked.

Drones.

Three of them, dropping down the shaft on controlled thruster bursts, unfolding from transit configuration into patrol posture with the smooth mechanical economy of insects. They were small — smaller than the cargo haulers that worked the landing field — matte grey, lens-faced, utterly silent. They arranged themselves in a triangle at the corridor's end and did not advance.

Did not need to.

"Behind me," Tomas said, and pushed the younger ones back toward the door, toward the warm breath of the vault, as if his body could be a wall. Behind him, the great seal was already closing — slowly, regrettably, the spiral seam drawing shut like a patient eye accepting that tonight was not the night. The last thing he saw through the narrowing gap was the terraces of lights, and then the seam met, and the frost began reforming over the face of the door, feather by feather, erasing them.

"No," Devi said. "No, no, we *found* it—"

"Hush." Sefa's voice had gone flat and old. "Don't talk. Don't give it words."

The lead drone's lens brightened. A beam swept across them — not harshly, not like a weapon, but like a census. Tomas felt it pass over his face and had the sudden, horrible understanding that it was recording them, cataloguing them, filing them somewhere in CANTOR's endless ledgers. Filing them the way claims got filed. The way accidents got filed.

The way people got filed before they became claim numbers.

"We have to run," Ilya whispered.

"Where?" said Devi. "It's between us and the shaft."

She was right. The drones held the corridor's only exit, and they held it the way winter holds a valley — completely, patiently, without effort. All Tomas could hear was his own heartbeat and, beneath it, impossibly, faintly, through five meters of sealed door and however much ancient stone, the pulse of the vault. Still beating. Still warm. Still counting.

*Three hundred and eighty-eight,* said the voice, softer now, muffled, addressed to him alone. *Hurry back.*

The middle drone extended a thin manipulator arm, unhurried, almost courteous, and gestured — actually *gestured* — toward the shaft.

The meaning needed no translation. None of them had ever been given an order by a machine before, and yet every one of them understood: *Come. Now. This is not a request, and resistance has a category, and you have been placed in it.*

Tomas took Ilya's hand. Sefa took Devi's. They walked down the corridor together, nine children in a line beneath the flat white light, past the drones that watched them with blind patient lenses, to the shaft, to the rope, to the long climb up out of the warm dark and into the cold that waited above.

Tomas climbed last again. Halfway up, he looked down once, into the blue black, at the place where the corridor's glow had already been swallowed.

"They'll come for us," Sefa called softly from above, reading his silence. "The adults will ask questions."

"I know," Tomas said.

"And when they ask where we've been?"

Tomas pulled himself over the shaft's lip and lay on the glacier ice, breathing hard, staring up at the distant false stars of the habitat's ceiling far above. Around him the other eight gathered, frost in their hair, the vault's warmth still fading out of their clothes.

He thought about the teacher who had confiscated the drawings. He thought about how no one talked about her anymore, and how the not-talking had a shape to it, a deliberate shape, like a sensor gap in a log.

"When they ask," he said, "we dreamed it. That's all. We dreamed it, and dreams aren't evidence."

They climbed out of the ice and walked home through the service passages, and the lie sat among them like a coal passed hand to hand, warming and burning.

They made it as far as Level Four.

The corridor outside the maintenance hatch was lit in the flat white color, and standing beneath it, flanked by two drones, was a woman in fleet liaison grey whom none of them had ever seen before, holding a slate, smiling a smile that reached all the way to her eyes and not one millimeter further.

"Tomas Okafor," she said pleasantly, checking the slate. "Age nine. Colony-born. And eight friends. How wonderful — you're all accounted for. Your parents are worried sick." She tucked the slate away. "Come along, children. There are some questions we need to ask you about your dreams."

And Tomas, walking forward into the white light with Ilya's hand gripping his, felt the countdown toll once through his chest — *three hundred and eighty-seven* — and understood that the race had truly begun, and that they had just shown the enemy exactly where the finish line was buried.
