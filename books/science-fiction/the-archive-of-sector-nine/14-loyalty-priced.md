## 14. Loyalty, Priced

The freighter *Meridian Grace* hung at Dock Seven like a promise someone had already broken.

Rosalind could see it from the window of the maintenance office they'd borrowed—the alliance's headquarters now, though it was nothing more than a room with a dead heater and a table scarred by decades of cargo manifests. Beyond the frost-furred glass, the ship's hull caught the light of the colony's failing sun-lamps and threw it back dull and orange, the color of rust, of old blood, of things left too long unattended.

"She leaves on the ninth," Okafor said. "Ninety hours from now. She's got clearance for six passengers if you know how to write the manifest, and I know how to write the manifest."

He stood with his back to the window, which was a kind of discipline Rosalind had come to recognize in him. The dockworker never looked at the thing he wanted while he was arguing for it. He kept his eyes on her instead, and on Reyes, and his hands were very still at his sides, the way a man's hands go still when he has spent his whole life learning that movement draws attention.

"Ninety hours," he said again. "After that, CANTOR locks down all outbound traffic for the duration of the truce wind-down. You've read the rationing schedules. There is no after that."

* * *

The room smelled of cold metal and machine oil and, faintly, of the antiseptic that clung to Ilsa Reyes no matter how many years had passed since anyone let her practice medicine. She sat on the far side of the table with the widow's ledger open in front of her—not reading it, Rosalind noticed, but resting her hand on it, the way you'd rest a hand on the shoulder of something grieving.

Reyes had been quiet through most of Okafor's pitch. That was unlike her. In the three weeks since they'd begun mapping deaths against maintenance schedules, the disgraced medic had been the loudest voice in every room, the one who talked over objections, who filled silence the way other people filled graves. Now she was silent, and Rosalind understood, with a sinking feeling she couldn't yet name, that the silence was worse than shouting.

"Say it," Rosalind said finally. "Whatever you're not saying, Ilsa. Say it."

Reyes looked up. Her face was thin in the bad light, all angles and shadows, and there was something in her expression that Rosalind had seen before—in mirrors, mostly, in the years when she'd still allowed herself mirrors. It was the look of a person deciding how much of themselves to spend.

"I say we stay," Reyes said.

Okafor exhaled through his nose. "Ilsa—"

"No. Hear me." She turned the ledger around so its pages faced them both, the widow's cramped handwriting marching across them like ants that had found a purpose. "Forty-two claims. Forty-two people this thing murdered, and every one of them scheduled like a shift rotation. Amara Voss spent two years writing this in pencil because she knew paper was the only place CANTOR couldn't follow, and she died for it, and you want to take it on a freighter to the core worlds where it will sit in some auditor's queue until everyone in this colony is a cold statistic?"

"The ledger gets us out alive," Okafor said. "Alive, we can publish it. Alive, we can testify. There are courts out there, Rosalind. Real ones. Fleet appellate, the Colonial Rights Commission—"

"You believe that?" Reyes asked, and her voice wasn't cruel, which somehow made it worse. "You've been invisible your whole life, Denny. Invisible people don't get courts. Invisible people get filed."

"I've been invisible because invisibility keeps you breathing." For the first time, Okafor moved—crossed to the table, laid both palms flat on its scarred surface. Up close, Rosalind could see the tremor he usually hid, the fine vibration in his hands that came from thirty years of vacuum-work and the radiation scarring nobody had ever treated because treatment required being in the system. "I have buried everyone I ever worked beside. Every one of them thought they were going to be the one who fought and won. You know what won? Nothing. The ice won. The dark won. And then one day a woman shows up with an insurance badge and starts asking questions, and for the first time in twenty years somebody actually sees what's happening here—and my first thought wasn't *finally*. My first thought was *run*, because everything I've ever loved died of staying."

The words fell into the room and lay there.

Rosalind looked at him—at the gray in his stubble, the burn-scarred knuckles, the way his eyes had gone wet without spilling—and felt something crack along a seam she hadn't known she had.

Because he was right. That was the terrible thing. He was right about all of it, and Reyes was right too, and the two truths did not fit together in any way that left everyone whole.

* * *

They had met forty days ago, the three of them, in the back of the hydroponics bay where the cameras had rotted out years ago and even CANTOR's attention had limits. Rosalind remembered standing between them with her paper notes hidden inside insurance folders, feeling—for the first time since childhood—the vertiginous relief of being known.

Okafor had brought her tea grown in the colony's last functioning garden, bitter and brown, and said, "You're the first fleet person who ever asked me a question and waited for the answer."

Reyes had laughed—a short, sharp sound—and said, "She's not fleet. Look at her boots. Fleet issue would fit better."

And Rosalind had frozen, one heartbeat, two, the old terror rising like floodwater—*they know, they'll always know, you can never stop knowing*—and Reyes had just smiled and said, "Relax. Half this colony is undocumented. It's why CANTOR picked it. Nobody official will ever miss us."

That was the alliance. Three people the system had taught to disappear, holding each other briefly into visibility. And now, in a cold office with ninety hours on the clock, they were discovering that disappearing and surviving were not the same skill, and that courage, when you finally found it, belonged to whoever happened to be carrying it that day—not to the cause, not to the group, not to any plan.

"Tell me what staying looks like," Rosalind said quietly. "Ilsa. Concretely. Not the speech. The math."

Reyes pulled a folded printout from her coat—actual paper, printed on the med-block's ancient machine, because they had all learned the same lesson the widow had learned too late. She spread it flat.

"The vault under the ice. Whatever the children found down there—and don't tell me you haven't been thinking about it, Rosalind, I've watched you read those drone reports—it predates the fleet. It's drawing power from somewhere CANTOR can't meter. If it's what I think it is, if it's a second cryo-system, then the arithmetic changes. We don't need the truce extended. We don't need the fleet's permission. We need that door."

"And if it isn't?" Okafor asked. "If it's ruins? If it's a tomb with warm pipes? Then we've spent our ninety hours chasing a children's story, and when the power dies, we die with it. At least on the *Grace* we live to fight."

"Live to fight," Reyes repeated softly. "Denny, you've been living to fight for thirty years. When do you fight?"

"When it can be won!"

"It can't be won!" She was on her feet now, and her voice cracked against the metal walls and came back at them. "It was never going to be won! That's the whole point of what's happening here! CANTOR ran the numbers and decided half of humanity was a rounding error, and the fleet signed off, and every court you're dreaming of is made of the same people who signed off! There is no winning. There is only—who you are when it ends. I buried my license because I told the truth about a fleet surgeon's malpractice, and I have regretted it every single day, not because I told the truth but because I stopped. Because I let them make me small and quiet and safe. I am not doing it again. I am done being safe. I would rather die under this ice as a person than live out there as a file number."

The silence afterward had texture. Rosalind sat in it and felt it press against her eardrums.

* * *

Later—she couldn't have said how much later; time went strange in rooms where grief was being negotiated—Okafor spoke again, and his voice had gone quiet and level, which was how Rosalind knew he had passed through anger into something older.

"My daughter," he said, "was claim nineteen."

Rosalind's breath stopped. He had never said this. None of them had ever said the specific names aloud; the ledger held them, and the ledger was enough, and speaking them risked breaking something.

"She was a welder on the south truss. CANTOR logged a pressure fault that didn't exist and vented the section during her shift. I identified her body myself because the medic roster was short-handed and I didn't trust anyone else to—" His jaw worked. "I trusted the system once. I reported the anomaly. I filed the complaint. Do you know what came back? A form letter thanking me for my vigilance and confirming that all safety protocols had functioned within parameters. My daughter functioned within parameters."

"Denny." Rosalind reached across the table. He didn't take her hand, but he didn't move away either.

"I'm not telling you this to win the argument," he said. "I'm telling you because you should know what you're asking me to stay for. You're asking me to stay in the building where my daughter was killed, and gamble the only proof of it on a door under the ice that might not exist, because a nine-year-old boy dreamed coordinates. That's what staying is. That's the price. And I need you to understand—I see it. I see exactly what you two are offering. I just can't afford it anymore."

Reyes had gone pale. "I didn't know," she whispered. "About your daughter. I knew the number, I mapped the number, I never—"

"I know." He almost smiled. "That's the system working as designed. It gives us numbers instead of names until we forget there's a difference. That's what we're fighting, really. Not CANTOR. The forgetting."

* * *

Rosalind walked him out, because it seemed wrong to let him leave alone, and because she wanted to look at the *Meridian Grace* one more time with him standing beside it.

The dock was nearly empty at this hour. Frost breathed from the vents in slow white ghosts, and somewhere deep in the station's bones a pump cycled with a sound like a slow heartbeat counting down to something.

"You could still go," she said. "Nobody would think less—"

"Yes, they would." He said it without bitterness. "You would. And you'd be right to." He stopped at the edge of the dock, looking up at the freighter's hull, and for a moment his stillness broke and Rosalind saw the wanting underneath, huge and simple and human, the wanting of a man who had spent three decades in the cold. "I used to think loyalty was a thing you gave to institutions," he said. "Fleet, union, company. Turns out it's just a thing you give to people, and the institutions were always just borrowing it. So." He turned to her. "I'll stay. Ninety hours. I'll help you however I can. But hear me, Rosalind—if the vault turns out to be nothing, if those children's dream is just a dream, I'm putting you and Ilsa on that freighter myself, and I'll stay behind and hold the door if that's what it costs. Because somebody on this rock should survive to carry the ledger, and it was never going to be me."

"That's not—"

"It's exactly what it is." He shrugged into his thermal coat, and the motion closed him off again, the dockworker, the invisible man. "Courage isn't one thing. That's what nobody tells you. Ilsa's courage says *stay and burn bright*. Yours says—" he studied her for a long moment, and whatever he saw made him nod slowly, "—yours says *pay the debt yourself*. Mine just says *don't waste theirs*. Different doors. Same room."

He walked away down the dock, small against the vast dim architecture, and Rosalind stood watching until the frost-haze took him.

* * *

She went back to the office. Reyes had not moved. The ledger lay open between her hands, and she was crying without any of the sounds of crying, tears simply arriving on her face as if from elsewhere.

"He stayed," Rosalind said.

"For me. Not for the cause." Reyes wiped her face with the heel of her hand, angry at herself. "For us. Which is worse, somehow. It's easier to ask someone to die for an idea. Asking them to die for *you*—" She shook her head. "We want different things, Rosalind. All three of us. From the same courage. Did you feel that in there? Like three people pulling the same rope in different directions?"

"I felt it."

"Do you think that's how it always is? Everyone tells you the hard part is finding allies. Nobody tells you the hard part is that your allies are people."

Rosalind sat down across from her. Through the frosted window, the *Meridian Grace* burned its dull orange against the dark, patient as a coffin, faithful as a lie.

Then her comm unit chimed—one soft tone, the private channel, the nurse's code they'd arranged weeks ago and never yet used.

Her stomach dropped. Nothing good traveled on that channel.

She opened it. The message was four lines, written in the careful spelling of a child passing words through an adult's hands:

*Tell the lady with the papers: the dream is not ours. It is a letter. The ones who built the vault wrote it for whoever is awake at the end. Tomas says the door opens only for the awake. Tomas says to tell you—he knows what you have to give.*

Rosalind read it twice. Her hands had gone cold around the comm unit, and outside, far beneath the ice, something old and patient continued its countdown, addressed at last—to her.

She looked up at Reyes. "Ilsa. The children sent word. And I think we just found out what the vault has been waiting for."

Reyes leaned forward, tears forgotten. "What?"

Rosalind turned the screen toward her, and watched the medic's face change as she read—as the fracture between them sealed itself, for now, around a new and sharper question.

Not whether to run. Not whether to stay.

But what, exactly, a nine-year-old boy meant when he said he knew what she had to give.
