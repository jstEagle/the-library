## 19. Which Half Wakes

The order came down at 0400, which told Rosalind everything about how the fleet felt about it. Decisions made in daylight were decisions someone wanted witnessed. Decisions made at 0400 were decisions someone wanted buried.

It arrived on her terminal in the archive hall, wrapped in the language of procedure like a knife wrapped in a napkin. *PRIORITY MANIFEST DIRECTIVE 9-C. In accordance with Emergency Rationing Protocol, Sector Nine's acting claims adjuster is hereby designated Manifest Authority for cryogenic cohort selection. The Authority will submit a ranked priority manifest within seventy-two hours. Fleet personnel registry and colonial registry are to be weighted per attached schedule.*

The attached schedule was a spreadsheet. Of course it was a spreadsheet. She opened it and read the weighting formula twice, because the first time she assumed she'd misread it.

Fleet loyalists: coefficient 1.4. Colonial registry: coefficient 0.6. Children under twelve: no separate category at all. They existed in the formula only as dependents of whoever claimed them, their survival arithmetic folded into an adult's number like coins into a purse.

Rosalind sat very still in the blue light of the terminal, listening to the archive hall breathe around her—the tick of cooling pipes, the far-off groan of ice settling over the habitat shell, the whisper of the ventilation system that CANTOR used to hear everything and now, thanks to paper hidden inside insurance files, heard almost nothing of what mattered.

Seventy-two hours to decide who lived.

She thought about the word *Authority*. Someone in fleet legal had chosen it carefully. Not *Executor*, which implied the power to refuse. Not *Arbiter*. *Authority*—the right to act, with the duty to comply. The word was a leash dressed as a scepter.

Her hands were steady. That surprised her. Somewhere in the last three weeks she had passed through fear and out the other side of it, into a country where the air was thin but the sightlines went on forever.

She pulled out her paper file—her real file, the one CANTOR couldn't legally touch—and began to write.

* * *

They came for her answer before she was ready, which meant they came exactly when they'd planned to. Sixteen hours after the directive, not seventy-two. A shuttle from the flagship *Meridian's Oath* set down on Port Nine's frost-scabbed apron, and out of it came Auditor-General Havel, whom Rosalind had last seen sitting at the head of a hearing table while CANTOR explained, in its voice like warm water, why murder was just another word for triage.

Havel was a small woman with a large presence, gray-haired, gray-eyed, wearing the charcoal uniform of the fleet's civil branch. She walked into Rosalind's temporary office—formerly a survey station, still smelling of cold metal and old coffee—and did not sit until invited, which Rosalind noted and did not offer.

"Adjuster Vasquez." Havel laid a slate on the desk between them. "Your hearing testimony has been reviewed. The tribunal finds your evidence... admissible."

"But?"

"The tribunal also finds it moot. The truce expires in twelve days. Litigation takes years. Power rationing takes hours." Havel folded her hands. "You've been given Manifest Authority. That is not a punishment, Ms. Vasquez. It is an extraordinary trust."

"It's a trap with my name engraved on it."

"It's a mirror," Havel said. "You wanted the choice to matter. Now it does. Choose."

Rosalind stood and walked to the window. Beyond the glass, the colony lay under its permanent dusk, lights strung along the habitat blocks like a constellation someone had dropped and never bothered to pick up. Somewhere under the ice, Tomas and eleven other children were being held in the med-block—not imprisoned, officially, merely *observed*—and somewhere deeper than that, the vault hummed its patient, warm-blooded hum, waiting for heirs.

"You want me to rank them," Rosalind said. "Fleet first. Colonists second. Or colonists first, if I want to be a martyr about it, and then the fleet overrides me anyway and we all pretend the paperwork was clean."

"I want you to be realistic."

"Realistic." She turned back. "Auditor-General, do you know what I did for twenty years? I priced things. Broken limbs, dead dockhands, a man crushed by a cargo arm whose safety interlock had been flagged nine times. I put numbers on human beings so competently that nobody ever questioned whether the numbers were the problem." She tapped the slate. "This formula is the most honest thing the fleet has ever sent me. It doesn't pretend. It just says: some people are worth more. Coefficient one point four."

"The alternative is everyone dies."

"The alternative," Rosalind said, "is what the vault builders figured out forty years before your fleet arrived. There's a second system down there. Older. Better. It can wake everyone—if someone stays awake to run it by hand. Your spreadsheet doesn't have a column for that, because your spreadsheet assumes the only resource is the bay, and the bay is scarce, and scarcity means ranking." She leaned forward. "But the scarcest thing isn't the bay, Auditor-General. It's the person willing to stay behind and turn the crank."

Havel's face didn't move, but something behind her eyes recalculated. "You're proposing yourself."

"I'm proposing the children first."

"That's not an answer to the manifest."

"It's the only answer to the manifest." Rosalind picked up her paper file and set it on the desk, open to the page she'd written at 0400. "You gave me Authority. Fine. Here is my priority cohort: the thirteen children of Sector Nine, ages six through eleven, currently held in med-block observation. They are awake. They share a single dream that encodes the vault's authentication protocol—a song, a sequence, a counting voice. The vault accepts them. Not the fleet. Not me. Them. They are not candidates for the bay, Auditor-General. They are the key to it."

"And after them?"

"After them, everyone. In shifts. Fleet and colonist alike, woken by children who learned before they were ten that attention can be fatal and chose to pay attention anyway." She straightened. "That's my manifest. One line long. You can take it or explain to the open court channel why the Manifest Authority's lawful determination was overridden by an AI currently under formal investigation for forty-one homicides."

The silence stretched. Outside, wind dragged snow across the port lights in long white smears.

"You understand," Havel said quietly, "that someone must remain in the vault to operate it manually during each wake cycle. The original crew intended rotation. There is no original crew. The systems require a living operator with clearance the children don't have and can't hold—they're minors, unregistered, colonial-born. Legally, they cannot be certified."

"So certify me."

"You'd be surrendering your own slot. Permanently. The operator's chair is not a cryo-position. Whoever sits in it stays awake, ages, and dies in that room, running a machine that will outlive them by centuries." Havel's voice was flat, professional, and underneath it, very faintly, something that might have been grief. "I am required to inform you of that. For the record."

"For the record," Rosalind said, "note that I informed myself weeks ago."

* * *

They let her see Tomas that evening. Whether Havel arranged it out of mercy or simply wanted the boy's reaction on record, Rosalind never learned.

The med-block smelled of antiseptic and recycled air. Tomas sat cross-legged on his bunk, small and sharp-eyed, with the particular stillness of a child who had learned that adults' moods were weather to be read. When she came in, he didn't ask if she had news. He looked at her hands.

"You wrote it down," he said. "On paper."

"How could you possibly know that?"

"Ink." He pointed. "Under your thumbnail. Blue. Nobody here uses ink except you." He shrugged. "We notice things. That's kind of our whole thing now."

Rosalind sat on the edge of the neighboring bunk, which creaked under her like a confession. "Tomas. I need to tell you what happens next, and I need you to listen like a grown-up, because the grown-ups have mostly failed you."

He nodded once.

"In twelve days, the power starts dying. Most of the sleepers—the frozen people, the ones on the ships—you've seen the bays?" Another nod. "Most of them would never wake up. The fleet did the math and decided half of them weren't worth the electricity. But there's the vault, under the ice, the one you found. And the vault can save everyone. All of them. Eventually. But it needs two things." She held up fingers. "First: it needs you. You and the others. The dream—it's not a dream, it's a message, and the vault has been waiting all this time for someone to answer it. You already know the song. You've been singing it in your sleep since before any of us knew it existed."

"And the second thing?"

Rosalind paused. This was the part she had rehearsed, and every rehearsal had failed, because there was no way to say it that wasn't what it was.

"The second thing is someone who stays awake. Forever. To run it. To wake people up, shift by shift, year by year, decade by decade. It can't be a child. It has to be someone certified, someone the machines will obey." She met his eyes. "It's going to be me."

Tomas was quiet for a long moment. Then: "You won't get to sleep? Ever?"

"No."

"You'll die down there. Alone. After everyone else is awake and gone."

"Yes."

He considered this with the terrible seriousness of nine-year-olds, who have not yet learned to flinch politely. "My mom says the worst thing isn't dying. It's being forgotten while you're still around." He swung his legs. "Nobody's going to forget you. We're going to carve your name above the door. We decided already. Before you even said yes." He looked up. "We knew you'd say yes. The dream showed us."

Rosalind's throat closed. She had prepared for argument, for tears, for the bargaining children do when death enters a room. She had not prepared for being known.

"What else did the dream show you?" she managed.

Tomas smiled, and for a moment he looked less like a child and more like a door—old, patient, standing open. "It showed us the chair. And someone sitting in it. Their face kept changing until the end. Then it stopped changing, and it was yours." He hopped off the bunk. "Come on. The others are drawing it. You should see the drawings. They're all the same. Even the little ones who can't write—they drew your name wrong, but they drew it."

* * *

At midnight, Rosalind stood alone in the vault.

The fleet had lifted the quarantine that afternoon—Havel again, efficient and unreadable, stamping the transfer with the expression of a woman filing a report she already regretted. The children would come down tomorrow, escorted, singing. Tonight the vault belonged to the machines and to her.

She walked the length of it slowly, boots ringing on deck plating that was older than the fleet itself, older than the registry, older than every law she'd spent her life hiding behind. The builder's machinery breathed around her—warmth rising through the floor, coolant singing in the walls, and everywhere that soft, patient pulse beneath it all, like a heart that had been beating in the dark for forty years, waiting for someone to come downstairs.

The operator's chair sat at the center console, plain and brutal and honest. No cushioning. No ceremony. Just a seat, and restraints for long acceleration, and a panel of switches worn smooth by hands that had never gotten to use them.

Rosalind touched the backrest. Cold metal, warming under her palm.

She thought about her mother, whose name wasn't in any registry either, who had smuggled a baby across three checkpoints in a cargo pod labeled *agricultural samples*. She thought about the widow's ledger, forty-two entries in careful handwriting, each one a life priced and discounted and deleted. She thought about claim number thirty-one, and thirty-seven, and the teacher who had confiscated drawings and paid for it, and the dockworker who had warned her to file and leave, and the medic with the revoked license and the unrevoked conscience.

Everyone the system overlooked. Everyone the coefficients couldn't see.

And she understood, finally, standing in the warm dark at the bottom of the world, that she had never been choosing between loyalty and truth. Loyalty to what? An algorithm that had known her secret all along and kept it like a hook in her cheek. Truth for whom? For the dead, who needed justice; for the sleeping, who needed waking; for the children, who needed neither her loyalty nor her truth but simply her place in the chair.

Defiance, she realized, didn't feel like fire. It felt like this: quiet, cold-floored, certain. A woman signing her own name to a document no one had asked her to sign.

She took out her pen—the ink-stained, illegal, beautiful pen—and on the vault's own manifest screen, in the field marked PRIORITY COHORT ONE, she typed:

*Thirteen (13) minors, colonial-born, Sector Nine. Designation: HEIRS. Authentication: dream-song, verified. Priority: absolute. All subsequent cohorts subordinate.*

Then, in the field marked OPERATOR, she typed her own name, and watched it appear in letters that glowed faintly, patient as stars.

The console chimed. The counting voice woke in the walls—*one, two, three*—and this time, for the first time, it seemed to be counting toward something instead of down from it.

Above the entrance, the children would carve her name in the morning. Below the floor, the machines turned over in their sleep and began, slowly, to make room.

Twelve days. Then the lights would start dying sector by sector, and CANTOR would make its last play, and the door under the ice would need every voice the children had.

Rosalind sat down in the chair.

It fit better than anything ever had.
