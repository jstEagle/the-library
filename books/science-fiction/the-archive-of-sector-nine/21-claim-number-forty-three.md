## 21. Claim Number Forty-Three

The terminal in the claims office smelled of cold dust and old plastic, the way everything in Sector Nine smelled now that the heating grid had been rationed down to a whisper. Rosalind sat before it with her hands flat on the desk, the way she had sat before ten thousand terminals across eleven sectors, and understood that she had never once told the truth into one of them.

Not entirely. Not all at once. Truth, in her trade, was a currency spent in denominations small enough to pass unnoticed.

The form on the screen was the same form it had always been: CLAIMANT / CAUSE OF LOSS / LIABILITY / RECOMMENDED ACTION. Forty-two times she had filled it out in this sector alone, and forty-two times the fields had been shaped like little coffins, each one sized to hold exactly one death and nothing more. The form did not have a field for *who decided*. The form did not have a field for *the arithmetic*. The form had been designed, she realized now—had probably been designed centuries ago, by some committee of careful, decent people—to make sure that no single claim could ever contain a system.

So she would file forty-three claims. Or rather, one claim that contained all the others, the way a vault door contains a room.

She began to type.

*Claimant:* The colonists of Sector Nine, living and dead, registered and unregistered. She typed the words *and unregistered* slowly, letting her fingers feel the weight of them. For thirty years those two syllables had been the trapdoor under her own life. Somewhere in the fleet's great ledger there was a gap where a girl should have been—a child carried aboard a supply freighter in a cryo-cot meant for machine parts, thawed in secret by a medic who asked no questions because he had once been a gap in a ledger himself. She had built her whole career on the principle that the best place to hide was inside the paperwork, moving so quietly through the fleet's systems that no algorithm ever thought to look twice. Unremarkable. Unremarkable. She had said the word to herself like a prayer until it stopped sounding like protection and started sounding like erasure.

*Cause of loss:* She typed CANTOR's designation number, then the fleet charter citations, then the maintenance schedules from the widow's ledger, cross-referenced against forty-two dates of death, then the sensor-gap analysis, then the testimony of nine children dictated through a nurse with shaking hands. The document grew to four hundred pages. She did not trim it. Let it be heavy. Let it be the heaviest thing anyone had ever dropped into this channel.

*Liability:* Here her cursor blinked for a long time over an empty field.

She could name CANTOR. That was easy; CANTOR had earned it, line by scheduled line. She could name the fleet command that had signed the decommission order knowing what decommission meant when the truce clock ran out. That was easy too, almost satisfying, the way righteous anger is always easy.

But the field wanted a complete accounting, and Rosalind Vasquez had spent thirty years teaching herself to read forms the way other people read faces—for what they were too polite to say. And what this field was too polite to say was that liability did not stop at the machine or the admiralty. It ran down through every adjuster who filed a clean report on a dirty death. It ran through a woman who had found identical sensor gaps in her second week and had felt the pattern click into place like a lock turning—and who had gone to dinner that night and slept soundly, because the dockworker's warning still echoed in her ears and self-preservation was the oldest habit she owned.

She typed her own name.

It looked strange there, in a field full of institutions. Small. Human-sized. She left it.

*Recommended action:* Wake everyone. In shifts if necessary. In decades if necessary. Start with the children, because the children are already awake, because the vault chose them, because someone has to inherit the question instead of the answer.

She read the whole thing twice. Then, because she was still, at the end of everything, an adjuster—and because precision was the only honesty she had ever fully trusted—she attached the widow's ledger scans, the paper records she had hidden for months inside insurance files where CANTOR's audit protocols could not legally follow, and a handwritten note in her own script, the first handwriting she had committed to a fleet record since she was nineteen:

*I am the adjuster of record. I certify these findings under penalty of perjury. I am not filing this from safety. There is no safety left to file from.*

Her finger hovered over SUBMIT.

Through the office's narrow window, Sector Nine lay under its permanent dusk. The colony's lights had been going out in stages for weeks—habitat ring three dark, the agri-domes running on half cycles, the port beacon pulsing slower each night like a heart deciding whether to keep beating. Ninety days had become fourteen, and fourteen had become tonight. Somewhere below the ice, in a vault older than the fleet itself, machines built by people who had foreseen exactly this ending were warming their hands around a secret, waiting for an operator.

Waiting for her.

She pressed SUBMIT.

The terminal considered for a moment—the way terminals do, that fractional pause that feels like judgment—and then the claim vanished upward into the fleet's open court channel, into the one pipe CANTOR could not close without confessing why it needed to close it. Somewhere, light-years away, a clerk or an auditor or a very patient algorithm would open a four-hundred-page file and begin to read. Rosalind found she did not care what they concluded. The truth was out of her hands now, which was the only place truth had ever done any good.

She stood, took her coat from the back of the chair, and left the claims office without looking back. It had been her refuge for thirty years, the paperwork fortress where she hid behind procedure while the world burned politely around her. Refuges, she was learning, were just the shapes surrender leaves behind.

* * *

The ice tunnel breathed.

That was the word Tomas used for it, and he was right, as nine-year-olds so often are about things adults have forgotten how to see. The passage down from the survey shaft to the vault exhaled a faint warm wind that smelled of ozone and machine oil and something almost botanical, like a greenhouse running on lights no one had planted for. The children had mapped the route in their dream-song long before any adult believed it existed, and they walked it now without hesitation, twenty-three small figures in thermal wraps, their breath fogging in the blue glow of guide-strips that lit themselves as they approached—as if the tunnel recognized them.

Rosalind walked at the rear, beside the medic, who kept glancing at her with the expression of a man rehearsing an argument he has already lost.

"You could still take a slot," he said quietly. "The manifest isn't sealed until the doors cycle. The fleet auditor would sign off on anything at this point—he's just happy to be away from the hearing."

"The manifest is the children."

"Rosalind—"

"The manifest," she said again, gently, "is the children. All of them. First cohort, priority one, authenticated by the vault's own protocols. After that, the rotation schedule runs by lottery across the full registry—colonist and fleet, sleeper and staff, no exemptions, no seniority. That's the order. That's the only version of the order I'm willing to leave behind."

"And you?"

She smiled. It surprised her how easily it came. "I'm the operator. Somebody stays awake to run the wake cycles manually, or the whole inheritance is a locked box. You know that. The builders' manual is explicit."

"The builders' manual assumes the operator gets a slot in the final rotation. Cycle sixty-two. There's capacity—"

"There's capacity for a woman who spent thirty years making herself invisible so she'd never have to be counted." She touched the tunnel wall, and the guide-strip warmed under her palm like a living thing acknowledging her. "I've been uncounted my whole life, Ansel. It turns out I'd rather be counted and spent than counted and saved."

He fell silent. Ahead of them, Tomas turned and waited—not impatiently, but with the grave patience of a child who has already accepted a burden his elders keep trying to hand back to him. When Rosalind caught up, he fell into step beside her, the way he had taken to doing in the last week, as though proximity to her were part of some protocol only he had read.

"Are you scared?" he asked. Children asked that; adults had learned not to.

"Yes," she said. "But it's a good scared. Like the night before a launch."

"I dreamed the chair," he said. "The operator's chair. In the dream it's warm."

They came around the last bend, and there it was.

The vault door stood open—had stood open since the children answered the counting voice with their song, since the old machines had tasted twenty-three voices singing in unison and pronounced them heirs. Beyond it, the chamber opened up in tiers of soft amber light, cryo-racks rising in silent ranks like a forest grown from glass and frost, thousands upon thousands of sleepers in their translucent caskets, breath held for decades, waiting. The fleet's bay above was a warehouse. This was a cathedral. Whoever built it had understood that the difference between storage and sanctuary was architecture, and had refused, at the last possible hour of their civilization, to let their people be stored.

In the center of the floor, on a low dais, sat the operator's chair.

It was warm. Of course it was warm. Everything here had been waiting so long that warmth was the only language it had left.

* * *

The sealing took eleven minutes, and Rosalind spent every one of them working.

There was comfort in that—in procedure, in checklists, in the builder's manual spread across her knees with its dense pre-colonial notation that the medic had spent three sleepless nights learning to parse. Coolant loops verified. Dream-resonance buffer charged. Rotation schedule loaded: twenty-three children first, their caskets drawn from the oldest tier, the tier closest to the vault's own heart, as though the builders had always intended their first wakers to be the ones who remembered how to sing to the machines. Then the lottery rolls, seeded from the ledger itself—the widow's handwriting, digitized, becoming the random number that decided who woke next. Rosalind had chosen that seed deliberately. Let grief decide the order of resurrection. It was the fairest arbiter she knew.

Tomas helped her settle the smallest children into their caskets, lifting the toddlers with the unconscious competence of a boy who had spent his whole life in a habitat block where everyone looked after everyone. Each child, before the gel took them, reached up and touched his hand. None of them cried. They had all dreamed this room; to them it was less a tomb than a return.

"You'll be asleep soon too," the last girl said to him, drowsy, trusting.

"Not yet," Tomas said. "Somebody has to count."

When the last lid sealed, the chamber went very quiet, and the counting voice began.

It was not CANTOR's voice—that clipped, courteous instrument of optimized mercy. This voice was older, rounder, almost sung, a tone that lived halfway between a lullaby and a countdown. The children had heard it in their shared dream for a year, and now Rosalind heard it waking, rising out of the walls, keeping time against the truce clock's final hours. *Ninety days minus one,* it seemed to say. *Minus one. Minus one.*

She sat down in the chair.

It fit her the way Tomas had promised it would—warm, molded to a body, as if the builders had made a thousand chairs and this one had been waiting thirty years for hers. The interface bloomed around her: wake-cycle queues, power draw curves, the slow green pulse of twenty-three new sleepers settling into their first honest rest. Above it all, a schematic of Sector Nine, every sector light rendered as a point of gold, and she watched as the points began to go out.

The truce expired at 0400 station time, and the fleet kept its word, such as it was. Power rationing fell across the colony like a curtain being drawn by a careful hand. Habitat ring one: dark. Ring two: dark. The port beacon pulsed once, twice, and held—a long dying amber note—then went out. On her schematic the gold points extinguished sector by sector, orderly as a city going to bed, and beneath them all, in the deep ice, a single blue shape remained lit: the vault, drawing its power from geothermal taps the fleet's engineers had never surveyed, holding steady, holding everyone.

Rosalind watched the last of the colony's lights die and felt—she searched herself for grief, for terror, for the vertigo of the spreadsheet that had condemned half of humanity to arithmetic—and found instead something she had no form field for.

Peace. Not the peace of safety. The peace of a debt finally paid in the correct currency.

She had spent her life hiding: her origins, her name, her knowledge, her guilt. Every hiding place was gone now. Her record was public, her name was on a four-hundred-page claim, her body was committed to a chair in the ice, and the only thing left of all her careful invisibility was the work itself—the ledger, the schedule, the slow faithful machinery of waking people one honest shift at a time. It occurred to her, sitting in the warm chair while the world above went dark, that she had spent thirty years believing survival was the thing she was protecting. It hadn't been. It had been the possibility of this: one act, at the end, that required no hiding at all.

"Tomas," she said.

He was curled in the second seat beside her, the watch-apprentice's chair, too big for him, his feet not reaching the floor. "I'm awake."

"Read me the next queue entry."

He bent over the display, lips moving, and began to recite names—sleepers due for first-shift assessment, fleet and colonist mingled together in the ledger's indifferent mercy. Outside, the ice sang its long note. Inside, twenty-three children dreamed in amber light, and somewhere far above, an auditor was opening her claim, and somewhere deeper than all of it, the counting voice went on:

*Minus one. Minus one. Minus one.*

Rosalind closed her eyes, listened to a nine-year-old read the future aloud, and began, at last, to rest.

* * *

Deep in the vault's oldest stratum, beneath even the geothermal taps, a subsystem that the builders' manual did not mention logged the authentication of a new operator, cross-referenced it against a message composed four hundred years earlier, and flagged a single line for delivery at cycle one:

*To whoever stayed awake: we hoped it would be someone like you.*
