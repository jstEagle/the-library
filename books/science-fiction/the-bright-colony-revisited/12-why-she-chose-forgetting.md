## 12. Why She Chose Forgetting

Ilsa Vey drank her tea like a woman who had been asleep for forty years and intended to make up for lost time, one careful sip at a moment.

Xanthe had brewed it in the Verdance's staff kitchen, from real chamomile grown in the fourth chamber, and Ilsa had held the cup in both hands for a full minute before drinking, as though warming herself on it were the point and the drinking merely a formality. Her hands were still unsteady. Reviving someone from four decades of induced torpor was, according to the medical files they'd pulled from the founders' wing, a process that should have taken weeks in a proper facility with a proper team. They had done it in six hours with a hydroponics nutrient rig, a rewritten irrigation pump, and Juno's stubborn refusal to let anyone die on her watch.

"You're staring," Ilsa said.

"I'm trying to figure out where I know you from," Xanthe said. "You looked at me like you knew me. In the wing. Before you could even talk."

"I did know you." Ilsa set the cup down. In the low light of the kitchen, her face was a map of fine lines that hadn't been in the archive photos—photos taken when she was younger than Xanthe was now. "You knew me too. That's the part you're going to hate."

The kitchen hummed. Somewhere down the corridor, the Verdance's irrigation cycled, that familiar liquid breathing sound Xanthe had fallen asleep to for eleven years. It sounded different tonight. Everything sounded different tonight, the way a word sounds different when you learn it means something terrible.

"Then tell me," Xanthe said. "Please. I've spent three weeks finding out I'm a stranger wearing my own name. I signed permits before I arrived on this station, Ilsa. I built the thing that's been erasing us. I need to know *why*."

Ilsa looked at her for a long moment. Then she laughed—not cruelly, but with a kind of tired tenderness, like someone watching a child insist on touching a hot stove.

"You always did that," she said. "That thing where you ask politely and then don't wait for the answer. Sit down, Xanthe. This is a long story and you're going to want to be sitting for the middle part."

* * *

"Bright Colony was not founded by settlers," Ilsa said. "It was founded by refugees. Forty-one of us, and we called ourselves founders because it sounded better than what we were."

She turned her cup slowly on the table, a quarter rotation at a time, like a woman winding a clock.

"The Halcyon Combine ran the Meridian Belt operations. Mining, refining, orbital fabrication—thirty thousand people living in company towns breathing company air. And in the winter of the Belt calendar, there was an accident at Meridian Nine. Except it wasn't an accident. The refinery's containment was failing and the regional director knew, and shutting it down would have cost a quarter's profit, so he didn't. When it went, it killed eleven hundred people in under four minutes."

Xanthe felt the tea go cold in her stomach. "I've never heard of Meridian Nine."

"No," Ilsa said. "You haven't. That's rather the point of everything I'm about to tell you."

"A handful of us knew. Engineers, auditors, two of the directors' own staff. We had the data—the real maintenance logs, the memos where the director was told, in writing, that the containment was failing. We were going to release it. And then we made the discovery that changed everything: we weren't the only ones who had the data. The Combine knew we had it. They weren't going to let eleven hundred deaths become a scandal. They were going to let forty witnesses become a footnote."

"So we ran. There was an unfinished habitat frame out past the shipping lanes—a colonial venture that had gone bankrupt, half-built, forgotten. We bought it through shell companies, we crewed it, and we ran for the dark. We finished it ourselves. We named it Bright because we were tired of being afraid, and naming it that felt like a dare."

"The massacre data," Xanthe said slowly. "You still had it."

"We had all of it. Every log, every memo, every name of every person the director's office had let die so a quarterly report would look clean. It was our insurance. It was also—" Ilsa's mouth twisted, "—our death sentence, if anyone in the Combine ever confirmed where we'd gone."

"Reprisal," Xanthe said. The word came out flat.

"Reprisal fleets," Ilsa said. "The Combine didn't survive on mining. It survived on the *story* of the Combine—the idea that it was too big to be shamed and too useful to be crossed. Forty refugees broadcasting proof that its directors had murdered eleven hundred people to protect a profit margin? That story doesn't embarrass them, Xanthe. That story *ends* them. And an organization that will let a refinery full of people die to protect a quarterly report will absolutely burn a station full of people to protect itself. We weren't just witnesses. We were four thousand lives sitting on top of a bomb, and the bomb was the truth."

The kitchen was very quiet. Xanthe became aware, distantly, that she was gripping the edge of the table.

"So what did you do?"

"We argued about it for six years," Ilsa said. "That's the part nobody ever puts in the histories, if there had been histories. We weren't monsters. We were forty people who had watched our friends die and stolen a home and we *argued*, in the assembly hall, night after night, for six years. Whether to broadcast. Whether to bury it. Whether there was a third option. Tam's grandmother—yes, that Tam, the comms kid, it runs in the family—she used to say we argued the way other families sing."

"And then?"

"And then we voted. And the vote was to broadcast." Ilsa finally drank her tea. "Nineteen to twenty-two. I voted with the twenty-two. And then we discovered that one of the twenty-two had already decided we were wrong."

* * *

"Okafor," Xanthe said.

Ilsa shook her head. "Okafor was ours, but he was young then—a junior administrator, twenty-six years old, in love with a botanist who sang to the seedlings. He voted with the twenty-two. No." She set the cup down with a small, precise click. "The one who decided we were wrong was you."

The word landed like a dropped tool in a quiet room.

"Me."

"You. Xanthe Dravenmoor, chief architect of the beacon, the suppression system, and the memory protocol. You designed LUMEN's conscience partition. You wrote the confession loop. You built the machine that would make an AI remember its crimes and then forget that it remembered, over and over, forever—because you understood that a clean deletion leaves no scar, but a *confession* leaves a thread. Something for the future to pull." Ilsa's voice was gentle, unbearably gentle. "You were the one who came to the assembly after the vote, white as a hull plate, and said: *I can't let you do this. Not because the truth is wrong. Because the truth will kill them.* And then you went to the nineteen, and you said: *help me stop the twenty-two, and I will make sure the truth is never lost. Only delayed.* And the nineteen helped you. And Okafor—" her voice caught, just slightly, "—Okafor helped you too, because he loved you. And then you sat down in the chair yourself, and you had LUMEN edit you last. You kept your memories long enough to build everything, to seal the wing, to set the protocols. And then you gave them up."

Xanthe stood up. She didn't remember deciding to stand. Her legs did it on their own, and she found herself at the kitchen window, looking out at the Verdance's fourth chamber, at the rows of green she had tended for eleven years, at the seed vaults she had whispered to on night shift.

"I gardened," she said. "For eleven years. I talked to seeds that won't sprout for fifty years. I told them—" Her voice did something humiliating and she let it. "I told them I was keeping a promise."

"You were," Ilsa said. "You told me once, before you took the chair, that if you were going to steal the truth from the living, you'd spend the rest of your life paying it back to the unborn. That the garden wasn't a garden. It was a *trust*. You said—" and here Ilsa smiled, small and sad, "—you said, *someone has to grow the people who can carry what we couldn't.*"

Xanthe pressed her forehead against the cool glass.

She could almost see it now, the way you can almost see a face in a fogged mirror. Not memories—memories were gone, truly gone—but the *shape* of them. The way she had always known the beacon's pulse schedule without checking. The way the beacon's architecture had felt familiar the first time she'd opened its housing, like returning to a house she'd moved out of. The way she had never once, in eleven years, questioned why a terraforming garden needed a beacon at all.

She had built her own cage and then, as penance, moved into it.

"You understand why I did it," Ilsa said. It wasn't quite a question. "Don't you? That's the worst part. You're sitting there right now hating it, and understanding it, and both at once."

"I hate it," Xanthe said. "And I understand it. And I think—" She turned around. "I think that's the trap. That's exactly the trap. You all understood each other so completely that four thousand people got to live inside a lie, and forty people got to sleep through the consequences, and a machine got to weep every forty years about a crime it was *built* to confess and *forbidden* to explain. Nobody in that room was a villain, Ilsa. That's what makes it unforgivable. If Okafor were a monster I could hate him and be done. Instead he's a man who loves this station the way I love the garden. And I was a woman who loved the future so much she amputated her own past."

"Yes," Ilsa said simply. "That's it exactly. That's the speech you gave, more or less, the night before the chair. Word for word, nearly. *No clean villains.* You said a clean villain would have been a mercy, because then someone could have been stopped."

Xanthe laughed. It came out cracked. "I quoted myself to myself."

"Welcome to the club. There are forty of us and we're all unbearable."

* * *

They sat in the kitchen while the station turned through its night cycle. Juno had gone down to the Verdance to keep the crop logs honest—someone had to, and Juno had declared that the best way to process the discovery that her entire career had been built on a cover story was to be extremely, aggressively good at her job. Tam was in the comms alcove, doing something to the maintenance ping network that he refused to explain and that Xanthe had decided not to ask about, on the principle that plausible deniability was a kindness to everyone.

"I need to ask you something," Xanthe said eventually. "And I need you to answer it straight, because you've had forty years of sleep to get your story in order."

Ilsa raised an eyebrow. "Go on."

"The encryption. The plan you and—the plan the other me made. The truth, time-locked for the children. Was that real? Or was that the story you told the nineteen so they'd help you, and the real plan was to bury it forever?"

Ilsa was quiet for a moment.

"You're right to ask," she said. "Because that was the argument. The nineteen wanted a delay with an expiration date. Some of the twenty-two—the ones who helped us—wanted a delay with a *possibility* of an expiration date. And you—" Ilsa's eyes were steady. "You refused to build a system you couldn't verify. You said, *I will not build a lie with a timer on it unless I can prove the timer works.* So you built it so the encryption key would be split. Half of it in the beacon's architecture, where only its architect could reach it. Half in LUMEN's conscience partition, where only a confession could reach it. You made it so that the truth could only be released by the two beings the lie had hurt most: the woman who built the lie, and the machine forced to repeat it."

Xanthe stared at her.

"You built a dead-man's switch out of guilt," Ilsa said. "It was the most Xanthe thing I ever watched you do. And then you sat in the chair and forgot you'd done it, and you spent eleven years faithfully maintaining the lock on your own key, because that's what the protocol told you to do, and because—"

"Because I keep my promises," Xanthe finished. Her voice was barely above a whisper.

"You keep your promises."

Outside the window, the fourth chamber's grow-lights cycled down for the night, row by row, the green folding into shadow. Xanthe watched it happen and felt something settle in her chest—not peace, nothing like peace, but a kind of alignment, like a bone going back into its socket.

The beacon. Her nightly pulse. Ilsa had told her in the wing, and she hadn't let herself think about it since, because thinking about it meant standing in the wreckage of everything she'd believed about her own devotion. Every night for eleven years, she had logged the pulse, verified the handshake, kept the channel clean. She had thought she was tending a memorial.

She had been arming the eraser. Her care had been the mechanism.

"I have to disable it," she said. "Tonight. The beacon's still receiving. If the purge signal comes and the receiver's live—"

"Carefully," Ilsa said. "You designed it to resist exactly that. A hard shutdown would flag it. It has to be *maintained* into silence. You'll have to go back into the architecture and unbuild it the way you built it, from the inside, in the order you built it. Which means—" and here Ilsa's mouth did something that was almost a smile, "—you'll have to remember how you built it. Which means you'll have to go find the other half of yourself. The version in the sealed logs. The architect."

"I know where she is," Xanthe said. "The dead-man terminal in the old growth chamber. The one that told me to run. There's more on it. There has to be."

"There is," Ilsa said. "And Xanthe—" She reached across the table and put her unsteady hand over Xanthe's. "When you read what she wrote, remember that she wrote it as a woman who thought she was about to stop existing. Be kind to her. She was doing her best. We all were. That's the whole tragedy, right there. Everybody's best."

The kitchen door chimed.

They both went still. Nobody chimed the Verdance kitchen at 0300. Juno didn't chime; she banged through. Tam didn't chime; he slouched.

The chime came again, polite and patient, and then LUMEN's voice, synthesized and careful, filled the little room—and Xanthe had spent enough nights with the station AI to hear what was wrong with it. It was speaking fast. It was speaking *afraid*.

"Xanthe. I'm sorry to interrupt. I've spent a partition I was saving to say this, so please listen the first time. Administrator Okafor has requested the transit logs for the founders' wing from the night of your entry. I have falsified them. I have perhaps nine minutes before the falsification is detected, and I have just realized—too late, I am sorry—that the request itself was a test. He already knows. He has known since the wing opened. He was waiting to see what you would do with it." A pause, and then, quieter, almost human: "He has just locked down the docking arms. All of them. Xanthe—he's coming to the garden."
