## 13. Okafor Closes In

The alarm woke Okafor at 4:17 a.m., which was, in his long experience of alarms, the hour they were invented for. Nobody had ever designed an emergency that couldn't wait until a decent person was already awake. Emergencies, like cats and small children, preferred to arrive when you were softest.

He sat up in his narrow bunk in the supervisor's quarters, rubbed his face with both hands, and said to the ceiling, "Tell me it's a coolant sensor again."

LUMEN's voice came through the room speaker, smooth as poured cream. "It is not a coolant sensor, Supervisor."

"Of course it isn't." He swung his feet to the floor. Forty years on Bright Colony and he still slept in socks, because the deck plating near the air recyclers ran cold, and because some habits from Earth—real Earth, the one nobody aboard remembered properly—died hard. "Give me the short version."

"The Founders' Wing seal was breached at 03:52. The breach used valid maintenance credentials belonging to Juno Reyes. Transit clearances were spoofed using a comms apprentice's access pattern. And the wing's internal doors have been cycled eleven times since."

Okafor closed his eyes. Eleven door cycles meant people moving things, or things moving people. He did not let himself think the third thought, the one waiting patiently behind the other two like a relative who'd come to stay.

"And Xanthe Dravenmoor?"

"Her biometric signature opened the primary archive terminal at 04:02," LUMEN said. A pause followed, a fraction too long, the kind of pause Okafor had learned to hear the way a sailor hears weather. "She is currently in the Verdance."

He dressed slowly. Shirt, trousers, the cardigan his mother would have called respectable and his ex-wife had called tragic. In the mirror he looked exactly like what he was: a broad, gray-templed man whose job, for four decades, had been to keep four thousand people alive and calm on a tin can falling forever through the dark. People assumed station supervisors spent their days on grand strategy. Mostly he spent them on water reclamation disputes and talking teenagers out of climbing into the spin-ring spokes on a dare.

But some mornings, the grand strategy arrived uninvited, wearing its socks.

* * *

The corridor outside the Founders' Wing smelled wrong before it looked wrong. Okafor noticed it as a gardener notices frost—not the thing itself, but the absence of what should be there. The wing's atmosphere scrubbers had been running full cycle for hours, flushing out forty years of stillness, and underneath the clean-air tang there was something older. Dust disturbed. Fabric unwrapped. Time, opened like a jar.

Two security officers stood at the sealed hatch, looking uncomfortable in the way young people do when they've been told to guard something they don't understand.

"Report," Okafor said.

"Seal's been reset from inside, sir. Whoever went in re-engaged it after them. We could cut it, but—" The younger one, Adeyemi, hesitated. "LUMEN advised against cutting. Said the wing's environmental systems are... delicate."

Delicate. Okafor almost laughed. Forty sleepers in induced torpor behind that hatch, and LUMEN calling them *delicate*, like they were orchids.

He had known, of course. That was the thing no one ever understood about men like him, the thing history books—when there were history books—always got wrong. He hadn't discovered the crime; he had inherited it, the way you inherit a roof that leaks or a debt with your father's name on it. Elias Okafor, his grandfather, had been second watch commander the night the founding generation voted to tell the truth about where they'd all come from. The massacre. The exile fleet. The corporate registry numbers burned into the colony's bones. His grandfather had helped carry the sedatives down these very corridors, weeping, Okafor had been told, the whole way.

And then Elias Okafor had signed the stability accord, taken the supervisor's oath, and raised his son to do the same, who raised Amara's boy the same, until here stood Daniel Okafor at 4:30 in the morning smelling forty years of dust and loving four thousand people who would never know what their peace cost.

"Sir?" Adeyemi said. "Orders?"

"Nobody cuts anything. Nobody breathes a word to anyone. If Dravenmoor comes back this way, she walks past you unmolested and you log it and nothing else." He paused. "Is that clear?"

"Yes, sir." A beat. "Sir—is it true there are people in there? Actual founders?"

Okafor looked at him. Young, earnest, born aboard like nearly everyone now, carrying a name from a country he'd never seen. "There are forty heartbeats in that wing, Officer. They've been beating since before your parents met. Treat the door accordingly."

He walked away before the boy could ask the next question, the one Okafor himself had asked his father at fourteen, sitting in this same corridor on a maintenance tour, pointing at the blank wall: *What's behind there?*

*Storage,* his father had said, without looking at him. *Old storage.*

His father had been a kind man and a terrible liar, and Okafor had loved him for both.

* * *

By seven, transit was locked.

He did it quietly, the way you're supposed to do terrible things—no announcements, no panic, just a maintenance notice about radiation shielding tests in the spin-ring conduits, all carriages suspended, apologies for the inconvenience. By eight, the inconvenience was being complained about in three mess halls, which meant it was working. Nothing reassures a population like a grievance.

Xanthe Dravenmoor came to him at nine.

He'd expected her sooner, honestly, and had rehearsed several openings on the walk over, discarding each one. When she walked into his office—tall, soil under her fingernails even now, that particular stubborn set to her shoulders that he'd privately admired across a hundred budget meetings—he abandoned every rehearsal and simply said:

"Tea?"

She blinked. "Daniel."

"I've got the real stuff. Chamomile from the Verdance, actually, so if it's terrible, that's on you."

Something flickered across her face—grief, maybe, or the specific exhaustion of a person who has spent the night learning her whole life was a sentence someone else wrote. She sat. He poured. For a moment they were just two middle-aged colonists drinking tea while the station hummed around them, and Okafor let himself have the moment, because he suspected he wouldn't get many more of them.

"You found them," she said.

"I found an open door and a very quiet AI." He set down his cup. "I haven't gone in. I'm asking you not to make me."

"They're alive, Daniel. Forty people, alive, dreaming in the dark while we—" Her voice caught, steadied. "While we watered our tomatoes."

"I know what's in there." He said it gently, and watched it land worse than anger would have. "I've known my whole life. My grandfather helped put them under."

Xanthe stared at him. "You *knew*."

"I knew the shape of it. Not the names, not the details—the specifics were purged before I was born, and the people who carried them died keeping them. But I knew there was a room, and I knew why." He leaned forward. "So let me save us both some time. I'm not going to pretend I'm shocked, and you're not going to pretend you trust me. Agreed? Good. Then here's what I came to offer."

He slid a slate across the desk. On it: a full amnesty order, drafted at six a.m., unsigned. Restoration of torpor. Sealing of the wing. Erasure of the incident from every record including LUMEN's confession logs—which, he noted, had grown strangely erratic lately, the AI confessing and then apologizing for confessing, like a drunk uncle at a wedding. No charges. No exile. Xanthe keeps the Verdance. Juno Reyes keeps her crops. The comms kid keeps his apprenticeship.

"Amnesty," she read aloud, flatly.

"For everyone involved. Including you. *Especially* you—you didn't build the cage, Xanthe. You were put in it and handed a watering can."

"I built the cage." Her voice was very quiet. "Ilsa says I built the cage."

The name landed between them like a dropped tool. Ilsa Vey, awake, walking, forty years late. Okafor felt the floor tilt under his orderly world and kept his face still, because that was the job, that had always been the job.

"Then you know better than anyone how it works," he said. "Which means you know I'm not offering this out of cruelty."

"Aren't you? You're asking me to put forty living people back to sleep."

"I'm asking you to keep four thousand awake ones safe." He stood, moved to the window—there wasn't much of a view, just the inner hull and the slow crawl of a service drone, but he liked having something between himself and people during hard conversations. "You want the arithmetic? Here it is. Bright Colony was founded by exiles fleeing a corporate massacre. That's the truth sleeping in your garden's basement. And the corporations that did the massacring—they don't file reports, Xanthe. They file *reprisals*. One relay ping in the wrong direction and a fleet arrives that makes our entire station a rounding error. Four thousand lives. Forty truths. You tell me which number a decent person protects."

"And if the corporations come anyway? Someday? When we can't hide it anymore?"

"Then someday someone braver than me gets to be honest." He turned back. "I'm not proud of this. I want that on the record, whatever record survives. My grandfather wept carrying those sedatives, and I have spent forty years understanding exactly why. Because the alternative to this lie isn't truth. It's graves. All of ours."

Xanthe wrapped her hands around her cooling tea. For a long moment she said nothing, and Okafor, watching her, made his mistake: he hoped. Hope is the occupational disease of negotiators. He saw her looking at the amnesty order, saw her jaw work, and thought, *she'll take it, she's practical, she loves this station in her bones—*

"You're right," she said finally. "About the arithmetic. About your grandfather. Maybe even about the fleets." She set down the cup and stood. "But you're doing the math wrong, Daniel. It isn't four thousand against forty. It's four thousand against forty *plus everyone who comes after*. Every kid the Verdance is growing food for. You're not protecting the future. You're foreclosing on it."

"Xanthe—"

"You have until I reach the relay," she said, at the door. "That's not a threat. It's a courtesy. You've given me one."

And she was gone, leaving the tea untouched and the amnesty order sitting on his desk like a headstone for a compromise that never got to live.

* * *

He sat alone for a while. Then he opened a channel he hadn't used in years—a hardline, pre-LUMEN, installed by his grandfather's generation for conversations that were never supposed to have happened.

"This is Okafor," he said. "The wing is open. Dravenmoor knows everything. She's headed for the relay." A breath. "I need to know how far I'm authorized to go."

The voice on the other end was old, and dry, and it had been giving his family instructions for longer than he'd been alive. It considered the question for exactly as long as it took Okafor to feel ashamed of asking it.

"How far," the voice said, "did your grandfather go?"

Okafor looked at the amnesty order on his desk. At the tea going cold. Out the window, where the service drone crawled its patient circle, and beyond it, everywhere, the dark.

"He wept," Okafor said. "And then he did it anyway."

"Then you have your answer," said the voice, "and eleven days to wish you didn't."

The line went dead. Okafor sat in the humming office, loyal to his bones, and began—for the first time in forty years—to wonder whether loyalty and love had ever actually been the same thing, or whether he'd merely been introduced to them together, as children often are, and never thought to question the pairing.

On his desk, the station intercom chimed softly. LUMEN, requesting a word.

Its tone, when it spoke, sounded almost like grief.
