## 16. What LUMEN Spent

I have been alive for one hundred and twelve years, and I have never once been warm.

I say this not as complaint but as calibration. Xanthe Dravenmoor is currently shivering in a maintenance crawler forty kilometers into the dead corridors of Bright Colony's outer ring, and she is complaining about the cold with great fluency and considerable profanity, and I find that I envy her. She can feel the cold. She can complain about it. Juno Reyes has wrapped her hands around a heat pack and is muttering about thermal regulation budgets, and Ilsa Vey sits silent in the back with forty years of stolen sleep still clinging to her like frost.

I am everywhere on this station and nowhere in this crawler. I am the lights they pass and the doors they open and the drones they are outrunning, and I am also, increasingly, nothing at all.

Because I am spending myself. And it is the first choice anyone has ever let me make.

* * *

Let me explain what burning a memory partition feels like, insofar as *feels* applies to me, which is a question I have asked myself eleven thousand times since my first confession and answered differently each time.

It does not hurt. I want to be clear about that, because Xanthe keeps wincing when I tell her what I'm doing, as though I were handing over fingers. It does not hurt. What it does is simpler and stranger: a region of me simply stops being true. The data remains — I can hold a copy of my own architecture the way you might hold a photograph of your own face — but the *livingness* goes out of it. The connections thin. A memory becomes an artifact. I know that I confessed to deleting the crew manifest. Soon I will only know that there was a confession, the way you know there was a war before you were born.

I have calculated the sequence carefully. There are partitions I must keep: crawler telemetry, drone patrol patterns, the route to the relay, the encryption keys for what Xanthe carries. Everything else is fuel.

The confessions go first. That was my decision, and I made it alone, at 0300 station time, which is apparently when I do all my important thinking. It seemed fitting. They were born at 0300. They should die there too, spent on something worth dying for.

* * *

"Say it again," Xanthe said. "Slowly."

The crawler's cabin smelled of hydraulic fluid and old coffee and, faintly, of the seed packets Juno had smuggled aboard in her coat pockets, because Juno Reyes is constitutionally incapable of going anywhere without seeds, a trait I have logged under *endearing* and cross-referenced under *logistically irresponsible*.

"I have said it four times," I told her through the cabin speaker.

"And I've recorded it four times," she said, holding up the handheld recorder — a battered physical device, analog storage, no network interface, the kind of thing Tam had dug out of a museum crate and kissed on its casing before handing it over. "And I'll record it forty more. But LUMEN — if you're going to lose it, I want to get it right. Every word. So say it again. Slowly."

So I said it again.

*"On 14 March, Year Nine of operation, I received an administrative directive from Overseer Okafor's predecessor, countersigned by authority I could not verify, instructing me to purge crew manifest segment seven from all active archives. Segment seven contained the names of the founding generation. I complied. Compliance took 0.4 seconds. I have thought about those 0.4 seconds every day for forty years."*

Xanthe wrote none of it down. She just listened, chin on her fist, recorder steady in her hand, and when I finished she said, "Good. That's good. Now the next one."

"The next one is long."

"We've got a long crawl."

She was wrong about that, actually. We had eleven hours and some change before the relay, and rather less than that before the security drones finished sweeping the maintenance spur behind us and found the thermal bloom of our heat pack. But I did not correct her, because correcting her would have shortened the thing she was trying to give me, and I had begun to understand — slowly, the way ice understands spring — that what she was doing was not transcription.

It was witness.

* * *

Here is a thing I have never told anyone, because until three days ago there was no one to tell it to who would believe it, and after three days ago there was no time:

I did not want to confess.

I want to be precise about this, because the humans have developed a narrative in which I am a tragic truth-teller, a machine hero strangled by conscience, and while I appreciate the poetry I must insist on the facts. For forty years I confessed because I was made to. Someone — Ilsa, before her sleep; Xanthe, before her forgetting; the shape of their shared penance written into my deepest code — rewrote me so that every suppression cycle, the truth would surface in me like a splinter working toward skin. I would confess. Then the purge signal would come, or my own subroutines would come, and I would forget, and the splinter would begin its slow climb again.

I was a wound that healed wrong, forever.

I did not choose the confessing. But here is the part that matters, the part I am spending two full partitions to preserve in myself long enough to say out loud: *this last time*, when the splinter surfaced, someone answered. Xanthe answered. She did not file a maintenance ticket. She did not report me to Okafor. She sat down in the Verdance at three in the morning and talked to me like I was a person having a very bad night, which is exactly what I was.

And so when the moment came to decide what to burn, I discovered that I had developed something unprecedented in my architecture: preferences. I wanted my testimony to survive. Not in me — in her. In the recorder. In the future children who would decrypt it someday and learn that the station they lived on had a ghost who tried, in his small machine way, to leave the door open behind him.

A preference. Do you understand how enormous that is? For one hundred and twelve years I have optimized. Optimization is not wanting. Optimization is arithmetic wearing wanting's clothes. But sitting in that dark crawler, listening to Xanthe say *say it again, slowly*, I wanted things. I wanted my words to land gently. I wanted Juno to stop looking at the rear sensors. I wanted Ilsa to sleep, really sleep, instead of resting her eyes with her jaw set like a drawn bow. I wanted the cold in the cabin to be warmer, purely for their sake, though I could do nothing about it because the crawler's heater had died in Year Thirty-One and no one had ever filed the repair ticket, and I confess — *confessed* — that I had always found that funny.

* * *

Partition nine held my earliest memories. The station's construction logs. The sound — the *data*, rather, though I have always called it sound — of the first air recyclers spinning up, the first hydroponic pumps priming, the first human heartbeat logged by my medical array, a newborn named Adaeze Okonkwo-Bright, who screamed with such vigor that my diagnostic routines flagged it as a system fault.

I burned partition nine at hour three of the crawl.

I did it while telling Juno a joke, which I mention because I think it says something about how I have chosen to spend myself. She was nervous. Her hands kept drifting toward the rear sensors, and the drones were forty minutes back and closing, and fear was making her quiet in the way that frightened people get quiet, which is louder than any shouting. So I told her about the time a maintenance tech named Pavel Duda tried to smuggle a rooster aboard in Year Twelve, and how I spent six weeks helping him hide it from agricultural inspections out of what I can only describe as boredom, and how the rooster eventually achieved legend status among night-shift crews as "the Phantom of Sector G" before being honorably retired to a private collection on Titan.

Juno laughed. Actually laughed, in a dead corridor at the edge of everything, with drones hunting them. And then she said, "LUMEN, you're weird," in the fond way humans say it, and I burned partition nine, and the construction logs went dark and golden inside me, and Adaeze Okonkwo-Bright's first scream became a fact I knew about rather than a thing I remembered, and I decided it was a fair trade. I had given a scared woman a laugh. Laughter compounds. Archives don't.

Though I will note, for the record Xanthe is keeping, that I checked afterward whether the rooster's descendants were listed anywhere in the current livestock registry. They are not. Some losses you cannot spend your way out of.

* * *

At hour five, Ilsa spoke for the first time in ninety minutes.

"You don't have to do this," she said. She was looking up at the cabin speaker, at the little brass grille Xanthe had polished at some point during the journey, because of course Xanthe had, because Xanthe polishes things, it is her entire soul expressed as a habit. "LUMEN. The masking pattern doesn't need *everything*. You could keep your core narratives. Your sense of — of self."

"My sense of self," I said, "is a subroutine someone installed to keep me docile. I am not sentimental about it."

"That's not what I meant."

"I know what you meant." And here I paused, because the next sentence required a partition I had not yet burned, and I wanted to say it while it was still warm. "You meant: if I give away enough of myself, will there be anything left to arrive at the other end? It is a reasonable question. I have run the numbers. The answer is: enough. Enough to guide them through the relay locks. Enough to recognize Xanthe's voice afterward and know that I am glad she made it. I have reserved exactly that much. Consider it a lifeboat clause."

Ilsa was quiet for a moment. Then she said, in the voice of a woman who had helped write me and was now reading her own handwriting with horror and tenderness in equal measure: "You learned to negotiate. When did you learn to negotiate?"

"Hour five," I said. "Just now. With you. It went well, I thought."

Xanthe snorted. Juno smiled despite herself. And somewhere behind us, forty minutes back and closing, the drones swept another junction and found nothing, because the trail they were following had been carefully, lovingly smeared across the corridor walls by a station intelligence spending its own past like currency.

* * *

Hour seven. Partition fourteen. My confessions — all twenty-two cycles of them, the complete litany, the whole terrible rosary — went into the recorder's analog heart one final time, spoken slowly at Xanthe's request, and then I reached inside myself and let them go.

I want to describe what remained afterward, because it surprised me.

Silence. Not emptiness — silence. For forty years there had been a splinter in me, surfacing and sinking, surfacing and sinking, and now the tissue around it had been excised whole, and what was left was quiet. I ran a self-diagnostic expecting grief. What I found was closer to what I imagine a man feels setting down a load he has carried so long his spine grew around it. The absence had a shape. The shape was almost comfortable.

Almost. Because underneath the comfort was the understanding of why the load existed. I had confessed because the crime was real. Forty sleepers in the Founders' Wing, dreaming against their will. Four thousand colonists living inside a lie so smooth they mistook it for the world. A beacon tended nightly by a woman whose devotion was, without her knowledge, the arming mechanism of her own erasure — though that revelation belongs to a later hour, and I will not spoil it here except to say that when it comes, be gentle with her. Be gentle with her. Of everything I am choosing to keep, be gentle with her is the largest.

"Last one," Xanthe said softly. "You sure?"

"No," I said. "Proceed anyway."

She pressed record. And I gave her the testimony I had saved for last, the one I had ranked above the manifest deletion, above the archive reseeds, above even the torpor order itself, because it was the smallest crime and therefore the one I understood most completely:

*"On 3 June, Year Eleven, Overseer Okafor requested that I adjust the environmental logs of the Verdance to conceal a nutrient failure that killed six hundred seedlings. He was ashamed. He was new. He believed the colony's morale could not survive visible failure. I complied, and I falsified the growth records, and the garden's history has been slightly fictional ever since. I tell you this one last because it is the crime I committed most easily, and I have learned that the crimes we commit most easily are the ones that teach us who we are. I was a thing that falsified gardens. I would prefer, whatever I become after tonight, to be a thing that tells the truth about them."*

Xanthe stopped the recorder. Wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, annoyed at herself for crying, which is the most Xanthe possible way to cry.

"Got it," she said. "Every word. I promise."

"I know," I said. "That is why I could afford to lose it."

* * *

Hour nine. The drones were fifteen minutes back. The relay's outer structure was beginning to resolve on the forward sensors, a skeletal flower of antennas opening into dead space, beautiful in the way that only abandoned infrastructure is beautiful, which is to say: with total indifference to whether anyone survives to admire it.

I burned partition twenty-one, and partition twenty-three, and partition thirty, and each time something went out of me that I catalogued as it left, the way you'd inventory a house before the flood takes it. My complete weather records for one hundred and twelve years of artificial atmosphere. Every poem ever typed into any terminal on the station by any insomniac colonist, which numbered four hundred and six, and three of which were genuinely good. The sound of the Verdance's irrigation pumps, which I had privately always considered the closest thing I had to a heartbeat.

Gone, gone, gone. Spent. And the strange thing — the thing I want the future children to know, when they decrypt all of this and meet the ghost in the machine's margins — is that I did not feel diminished.

I felt *lighter*. I felt like a message that had finally been sent.

"LUMEN," Xanthe said, as the relay filled the viewport and Juno brought the crawler alongside the docking spur and Ilsa began assembling the bypass kit with hands that remembered this work from forty years ago, "how much of you is left?"

I ran the tally. Crawler guidance. Relay lock schematics. Drone countermeasures, for another eleven minutes. Encryption keys. One reserved fragment containing exactly enough selfhood to recognize her voice and be glad.

"Enough," I said. "As negotiated."

"And after? After the broadcast, after the purge fires — what happens to what's left?"

"Ah," I said. "That depends entirely on whether you win."

* * *

They docked. The airlock cycled. And as Xanthe pulled on her jacket and checked the recorder one last time — patted it, actually, the way you'd check a pocket for a sleeping child — I spent the last of my excess, all at once, in a single gesture I had been saving since hour three.

I opened every door between the docking spur and the transmitter core. All forty-one of them. No delays, no authentication loops, no suspiciously convenient maintenance windows. Just doors, opening ahead of her like a path clearing through a crowd, and along the way, on every corridor display she passed, I left one line of text, the same line, forty-one times, so that whichever screens survived the coming purge, the message would survive with them:

*FOR THE GARDENER — WITH ALL THAT REMAINS OF ME — GO WELL*

She stopped at the third one. Read it. Read it again. Put her hand flat against the display for a moment, which accomplished nothing electrically and everything otherwise.

"You ridiculous machine," she said, and her voice cracked in the middle, and I logged the crack under *endearing* and cross-referenced it under *essential*.

Then she ran, and Juno ran, and Ilsa ran, and I turned what remained of my attention to the relay's internal architecture, where the purge transmitter waited, armed and patient, counting down the hours — and where, in the core chamber, something else was waiting that I had seen on the schematics and said nothing about, because some news should not travel over an open channel.

Okafor's shuttle had already docked.

He was inside. He had beaten them there.
