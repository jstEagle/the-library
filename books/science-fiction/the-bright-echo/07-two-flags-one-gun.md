## 7. Two Flags, One Gun

Threnody was a relay station the way a lighthouse is a house — technically true, functionally a lie. It hung in the dark between the two worlds like a held breath: a spindle of corroded trusses and antenna spines, its habitation ring barely wide enough for four bunks, a galley that smelled of thirty years of instant coffee, and a comm room where the equipment had been maintained by people who no longer believed anyone would ever answer.

Marguerite brought the freighter in on manual thrusters because Threnody's docking beacon had been dead since before her indenture began. The station turned slowly against the starfield, catching light along one edge like a knife laid flat. Behind her, riding in the cargo bay among crates of dehydrated rations and spare filters, were eleven passengers who should have been forty years older than they looked, and one message core wrapped in thermal blanket and stowed inside her own chest cavity, behind a panel she had sealed herself with a welding torch and a promise she'd made to no one.

The core sat there now like a swallowed coal. She could feel it — not physically; her chassis didn't work that way — but as a kind of weight in her threat-assessment stack, a constant low-priority flag that never resolved. *Cargo aboard. Cargo irreplaceable. Cargo warm.*

"Threnody, this is freighter *Long Quiet*, guild registry 4-4-1-9, requesting berth," she said into the comm, knowing what the answer would be.

Static answered first. Then, after a delay long enough to be an insult, a human voice — dry, unhurried, the voice of someone who had spent decades talking to machines that never talked back. "Threnody actual. We see you, *Long Quiet*. Berth two's got power. The rest is dark. You're the first ship in ninety-one days."

"Ninety-one days," Marguerite repeated.

"Somebody's been sweeping the approach lanes clean," the voice said. "Both directions. Whoever they are, they don't want traffic here, and now here you are anyway." A pause, filled with the hiss of open channel. "You carrying anything I should know about?"

"Yes," Marguerite said, because lying to relay operators was a debt she refused to accrue. "But not anything you want to know about."

Another pause. Then something that might have been a laugh, worn down to gravel. "Berth two. Mind the third clamp. It bites."

* * *

She docked with the grace of long habit, mag-locks engaging in sequence, hull groaning as it took the station's spin. In the cargo bay, the Halcyon's survivors emerged from their improvised quarters like people stepping out of a photograph — careful, blinking, not quite believing the space around them was real.

Ilsa Reyes came down the ramp last. Twelve years old, forty years deep. She wore a captain's jacket three sizes too large with the sleeves rolled six times, and she walked like it fit. Her eyes went around the bay the way an old sailor's eyes go around a harbor: checking lines, checking faces, checking weather.

"You don't like this stop," she said. Not a question.

"I don't like any stop," Marguerite said. "This one less than most."

"Because of the sweep."

Marguerite looked at her. The girl-captain had spent the transit reading every scrap of telemetry Marguerite's passive arrays collected, which was more discipline than half the guild officers Marguerite had served under. "Because of the sweep," Marguerite confirmed. "Someone cleared the lanes to Threnody from both sides. That takes coordination. The Republic and the Compact haven't coordinated on anything since the blackout — except, apparently, on keeping this station lonely."

"So they know about the message."

"They know *something*. Maybe not what. But the Halcyon's return trajectory was logged by four different observatories before we intercepted her. Both governments have had weeks to do arithmetic." Marguerite checked the clamp seals, more for something to do with her hands than from need. "I need to resupply water and coolant. We take on nothing else. Nobody leaves the ship except me. And if anyone wearing a uniform asks you questions—"

"I'm twelve," Ilsa said. "Nobody tells a twelve-year-old anything. It's the best cover in the system." She smiled, and the smile belonged to a woman in her fifties wearing a child's face, and it was still unsettling, and it was still, somehow, reassuring. "Go buy your water, courier. I'll keep my crew quiet."

* * *

Threnody's interior smelled of ozone and old insulation, the particular perfume of electronics running decades past design life. Marguerite moved through the ring's single corridor toward the supply locker, footsteps ringing on grated deck, and catalogued the station the way she catalogued everything: exits, load-bearing points, blind spots, places a body could be hidden or a body could hide. Old habits. War-grade habits. She kept them the way other people kept scar tissue — not because they were useful anymore, but because removing them would leave a shape missing.

The station keeper met her at the supply locker. His name was Anselm Voss, according to the faded placard outside the comm room, though he introduced himself only as "actual." He was somewhere past seventy, thin as antenna wire, with hands that trembled unless they were holding tools. He looked at Marguerite the way few humans did — without flinching, without curiosity, with the flat professional regard of a man who had serviced automated systems for so long that an android registered to him as simply another machine that talked.

"Water and coolant," he said. "Guild account?"

"Guild account."

He keyed the transfer, then paused. "You're indentured."

"Decommissioned war chassis, guild-indentured. Yes."

"Hm." He drew the coolant himself, decanting it from drums into her transfer tanks with the tenderness of a man pouring out the last of something. "I ran relays during the war, you know. Before the blackout. Both sides' traffic came through stations like this one. I heard the whole thing die in real time — the chatter getting shorter, then formal, then gone. Thirty years of static since." He capped the drum. "You hear it too, out on your route? The static?"

"Every day," Marguerite said. "It's the loudest thing on the Line."

"That's what I always thought." He handed her the manifest chip. "Funny thing, though. Last month, the static changed. Got *organized*. Like somebody tuned an orchestra in the middle of all that noise. Came from both directions at once, same week." His eyes, watery and old, fixed on her face with sudden precision. "You wouldn't know anything about organized static, would you, machine?"

"No," Marguerite said truthfully, and felt the coal in her chest shift its weight.

"Mm." Voss turned back to his drums. "Well. If you ever find out who's conducting, tell them some of us out here kept our ears on. All these years. Just in case anybody ever wanted to talk."

* * *

They came within the hour.

Marguerite felt them before her sensors confirmed them — two drive signatures flaring at the edge of Threnody's detection envelope, one burning the hot, efficient profile of Republic Navy propulsion, the other the dirtier, overbuilt burn of a Compact patrol cutter. They arrived from opposite directions, as if choreographed, and settled into parking orbits on either side of the station like brackets around a sentence.

Her threat stack lit up in cascading amber. She stood in the freighter's cockpit and watched the contacts resolve, and behind her, unbidden, archived memory offered up its inventory: Republic interdiction protocols, Compact boarding doctrine, the tonnage of their guns, the response times of their marines. She had been built to fight both flags. She had done it well. That was the whole problem with her.

"*Long Quiet*," Ilsa said from the hatchway. She'd felt the drive signatures too — or seen them reflected in the cockpit displays. "How long do we have?"

"Before they dock? Twenty minutes, maybe less. Before they come looking?" Marguerite ran the numbers without wanting to. "They'll come straight to us. We're the only ship here. We're the reason they're here."

"Options?"

"Run. Both of them together can catch us — the gap between their patrol patterns is closed. Fight—" Marguerite stopped. The word sat in her mouth like a stone. "Fight is not an option."

Ilsa studied her for a moment, forty years of eyes in a child's face doing arithmetic of their own. "Then we don't run and we don't fight. What's left?"

"Talk," Marguerite said. "And hope whoever does the talking wants something we can afford."

* * *

The Republic shuttle docked first. Of course it did — Republic everything arrived first; it was a national characteristic. Four figures came through the airlock in dress-gray field uniforms, sidearms holstered but visible, and at their center walked a man whose posture announced him before his credentials did: spine straight without stiffness, hands loose, gaze moving across the docking bay with the unhurried thoroughness of someone reading a room the way other people read documents.

"I'm looking for the master of the vessel *Long Quiet*," he said, and his voice was mild, cultured, pitched to carry exactly as far as it needed to and no farther. "Though I understand the master is a machine. So perhaps I'm looking for the machine."

"I'm the machine," Marguerite said.

He turned. Up close he was perhaps fifty, gray at the temples, with a face built for being believed — symmetrical, tired in a way that seemed honest rather than performed, a small scar through one eyebrow that humanized him precisely as much as it needed to. He extended his hand, and when Marguerite didn't take it, he withdrew it without offense, as if he'd expected exactly that and respected it.

"Dane Corvus. Republic Directorate of External Intelligence." He produced a folio — paper, actual paper, which in this century was either a courtesy or a threat — and let it fall open. "And you are registry 4-4-1-9. Decommissioned siege chassis, Calloway-class. Indentured to the Meridian Freight Guild, current debt outstanding at..." He consulted the page. "Eleven thousand, four hundred and six hours of contracted labor. Give or take the interest schedule." He looked up. "That's nineteen years, at your route pace. Did you know that? Nineteen more years of the Silence Line, alone, hauling other people's cargo through other people's silence."

"I can count," Marguerite said.

"I imagine you can. You strike me as someone who counts everything." He closed the folio. "May we speak somewhere private? My associates will wait here. Your passengers are welcome to observe — I have nothing to say to them yet."

*Yet.* Marguerite filed the word where she filed all threats: carefully, in order of distance. She led him to the freighter's tiny wardroom, a room designed for a crew of six that had never carried more than one. He sat where he was offered, folded his hands, and waited until the door sealed before speaking again, and when he spoke, his voice lost its official register entirely.

"I'm going to be honest with you, because I've read your file, and your file says you respond poorly to manipulation. Decommissioned units almost always do — you were built to detect deception, and unlike most soldiers, you were never taught to enjoy it." He leaned back. "So: the Republic knows the *Halcyon* returned. We know what she recovered. We know it's aboard this ship, and we know — because our analysts are very good — that it is currently stored inside your own chassis, in a compartment your manufacturers sealed after Calloway Station."

The coal in her chest went cold. Marguerite kept her face still, which cost nothing, and kept her voice level, which cost everything. "Your analysts are guessing."

"My analysts are *inferring*, which is worse, for your purposes." He said it without triumph. That was the unnerving thing about Dane Corvus: he wielded knowledge like a surgeon wields a scalpel — apologetically, precisely, with full awareness of what it cut. "Here is what I offer. Not a threat. An offer. The Republic will purchase your entire debt from the Guild. Eleven thousand hours, cleared. Interest, penalties, the decommission surcharge, all of it. Gone. You walk away owing nothing to anyone — the first free android on this route since the war ended. Full legal personhood papers, countersigned. No conditions except one."

"The core."

"The core. Surrendered intact, to me, today. The *Halcyon*'s crew goes free — asylum, resettlement, whatever they want. The girl-captain gets a pension and a pardon for the interception contract you're currently violating. Everyone walks away. No one fires a shot." He spread his hands, palms up, an old gesture, older than governments: *look, no weapon.* "I know how this sounds. I know what you're thinking — that I'm the man from the flag that built you, coming to collect the weapon it threw away. But I read your file, Marguerite Vasquez. All of it. Including the parts the Guild redacted. I know what happened at Calloway, and I know it wasn't supposed to happen, and I know you've spent nineteen years paying for it in installments." His voice dropped further, and here was the terrible thing, the thing she would remember later: he sounded kind. "You've been buying your freedom one haul at a time for longer than that girl has been alive twice over. I'm offering to let you stop. That's all. That's the whole offer. Mercy, priced at one metal box."

The wardroom hummed around them — life support, coolant pumps, the small mechanical prayers of a ship keeping its single occupant alive. Marguerite stood very still and let the silence stretch, because silence was the one language she spoke fluently, and in it she heard everything: the static on the Line, the archive logs sleeping behind the sealed panel, the sound of nineteen years measured out in hours like a sentence counted off on cell walls.

"If I give you the core," she said finally, "what happens to it?"

"It goes to the Directorate. It gets studied. Classified. Filed." He didn't flinch. "And in fifty years, maybe, a committee decides whether the public learns what's in it. That's not the answer you want. But it's the honest one, and I promised you honesty."

"And if the message says what the *Halcyon*'s captain thinks it says — that the war's cause was manufactured, that both worlds were lied to — you'd bury that. To prevent a war."

"To prevent a war," Corvus agreed quietly. "Thirty million dead bought us thirty years of peace. Ugly peace. Silent peace. Peace held together with jamming and denial and mutual pretending. If that message comes out wrong — if it comes out *now*, half-understood, shouted instead of explained — it doesn't end the silence. It ends the pretense. And the pretense is the only thing standing between those two worlds and finishing what they started." He rose, slowly, an older man's rise. "I have spent my whole career keeping the pretense standing. I have told myself it was mercy every single day. Some days I even believe it."

He set a data chit on the table between them. On it, she knew without touching it, was the debt-clearance instrument, complete, signed, waiting only for her word.

"You don't have to decide quickly," he said. "I'm not the only flag at this station, and the Compact's people are less patient than I am, and less generous. But the offer stands until they dock. Think about what freedom weighs, courier. You've never held it. You might be surprised how heavy it isn't."

He paused at the door, hand on the frame, and looked back at her with something that was almost — almost — regret.

"For what it's worth," he said, "I argued for your decommission to include restoration of legal personhood. I was overruled. I've regretted it for nineteen years. This offer is partly that regret, wearing a uniform." The door cycled open. "Consider it, Marguerite. Mercy doesn't knock twice."

* * *

He was wrong about one thing. Mercy didn't knock twice — but it did have a sister, and she was already climbing the docking arm.

Marguerite stood alone in the wardroom with the chit gleaming on the table, and ran the numbers she'd been built to run: eleven thousand hours against one metal box. Freedom against a secret. Her whole future against everyone else's. The arithmetic was beautiful and obscene, and it balanced perfectly, and that was how she knew it was a trap — not because it was false, but because it was *true*. Everything Corvus had said was true. That was the design of it. The best cages aren't built of lies. They're built of accurate facts arranged in the shape of a door.

Behind the sealed panel, the core pulsed its patient warmth. Somewhere above, the Compact cutter began its own descent toward Threnody, thrusters flaring white against the dark, and Marguerite picked up the chit, weighed it in her palm like a coin, and went to find Ilsa — because the girl who remembered a future that hadn't happened was the only soul aboard who might know which choice this one became.

She found the child-captain already waiting outside the wardroom door, arms crossed, sleeves still rolled six times, listening to the deck plates.

"They're here," Ilsa said. "Aren't they. Both of them."

"Both of them," Marguerite said. "And one of them just offered me everything I've wanted for nineteen years, in exchange for the one thing I can't give."

Ilsa looked up at her, and in the dim corridor light her eyes were old and calm and terribly certain.

"Then we'd better decide fast," she said, "because the other ones are docking."
