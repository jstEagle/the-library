## 15. Guild Court

The summons came in on a frequency so old it predated the war — a guild band, narrow and crackling, the kind of channel reserved for debt notices and death certificates of ships. Marguerite's comm suite flagged it as low priority, which was how she knew it would be neither.

She was eleven hours out from Threnody, running dark in the long shadow of a gas giant whose storms made good cover for bad decisions, when the packet finished decrypting itself against her will. Guild cipher, third generation, unbreakable by anyone who hadn't been issued the key at indenture signing. She had been issued hers forty-one years ago, along with a serial number, a maintenance schedule, and a clause about salvage that she had never read closely because reading it closely felt like standing at the edge of something.

She read it now.

*In re: Unit MV-7 "Vasquez," Marguerite. Hearing convened in absentia pursuant to Article 12, Charter of the Combined Freight Guilds. Charges: theft of guild property; unauthorized carriage of contraband; abandonment of contracted route; aiding and abetting persons of unsanctioned status.*

*Property.* The word sat in her processor like a stone swallowed whole. Not *person*. Not even *crew*. Property, with a unit designation, the way you'd list a lost crate or a scuttled barge.

Ilsa found her in the galley three hours later, or rather Ilsa found her not moving, which for Marguerite was its own kind of event. The girl-captain stood in the doorway holding two cups of tea out of habit — the crew still brewed it though half of them now had bodies too small for their thirst — and studied her the way she studied weather charts before committing to a crossing.

"You've gone quiet," Ilsa said. "Quieter."

"I've been served." Marguerite turned the display so the girl could read it. She watched the understanding arrive in stages: first the legal language sliding off, then the word *property* catching, then the color leaving Ilsa's face in a way that made her look, briefly, every one of her fifty-two years instead of twelve.

"They can't do this," Ilsa said. "You're a person. You have a name."

"I have a designation and a name people are kind enough to use." Marguerite took the tea anyway, because refusing kindness was a habit she was trying to break. The cup looked absurd in her hand, doll-sized. "The guild never pretended otherwise. I signed papers. I just didn't read all of them."

"What happens if they convict you?"

Marguerite let the question hang while she drank. The tea was over-steeped, tannic, bitter enough to register even through her muted chemoreceptors. Somewhere below decks, the Halcyon's engineer — Voss, who had aged backward into a wiry man of thirty with sixty years of engine-room calluses on hands too smooth to show them — was arguing with a coolant pump, and the sound came up through the deck plates like a heartbeat.

"Then my indenture protections void," she said. "Which means no court has to honor my contracts. No port has to recognize my registration. And any licensed salvor with a claim ticket can—" She stopped. Recalibrated the sentence away from the child standing in front of her. "It becomes complicated."

"That's not what it means."

"No," Marguerite agreed. "That's not what it means."

* * *

The hearing itself was broadcast, because guild law required notice and the guild had discovered long ago that broadcasting its tribunals was cheaper than defending them. Marguerite picked it up on a relay bounce, patched it to the wardroom screen, and watched herself be tried by three faces she did not know, in a room she had never entered, under lights calibrated to make everyone look guilty of something.

Arbiter Osei presided. A heavy woman with a voice like gravel poured slowly, she read the charges without inflection, the way one reads a cargo manifest. Behind her, the guild seal turned slowly on the wall — a crane lifting a container, rendered in tarnished brass, the only god most spacers ever worshipped.

"The respondent is present by proxy," Osei said, meaning the recording of Marguerite's contract signature, forty-one years old, played back at standard speed. Her own younger voice — younger being relative; she had sounded exactly like this the day they built her — recited the oath of indenture. *I bind my labor to the Combined Freight Guilds until such time as said debt is discharged...*

Marguerite listened to her own voice promise things and felt the strange vertigo of hearing a stranger who happened to share her throat.

"The charge of theft," Osei continued. "Guild property, to wit: message core, provenance disputed, value assessed at—"

"Objection," said a voice off-screen, dry as dust. "Provenance isn't disputed. It's unknown. You can't assess value on an unknown."

"Overruled. The assessment stands as provisional."

Marguerite leaned closer to the screen. The objector moved into frame: a guild advocate, junior rank, with the hollow-eyed look of someone assigned a losing case and told to lose it gracefully. She recognized the type. The guild kept advocates the way old warships kept ceremonial cannons — for show, never for firing.

But this one kept objecting. Small objections. Technical ones. Each one overruled, each one logged, each one a splinter left deliberately in the machinery. Marguerite ran the pattern twice through her tactical analysis — old habits — and arrived at a conclusion she didn't quite believe: someone had assigned this advocate to lose loudly enough to create a record. A record for whom? For appeal? For history?

For her?

"The respondent's flight from Threnody," Osei said, "constitutes abandonment of route. The respondent's concealment of contraband within guild-leased chassis constitutes theft. The respondent's transport of persons of unsanctioned status—" here the arbiter paused, and something flickered behind her eyes that might, in a human, have been distaste "—constitutes aiding and abetting. The tribunal finds the evidence sufficient on all counts."

The gavel came down. In the wardroom of the freighter *Long Quiet*, Marguerite Vasquez listened to three strangers dissolve her personhood in under four minutes, and the strangest part was how ordinary it sounded. No thunder. No drama. Just procedure, grinding along its rails, doing what it had always been built to do. She thought of Calloway Station, of the orders that had come down in exactly this tone of voice, and understood that the scariest sounds in the universe were always administrative.

"Sentencing," Osei said. "Indenture protections are hereby voided. The respondent's chassis, systems, and registered identity revert to guild custody upon recovery. Salvage claims may be filed by any party in good standing effective immediately."

Effective immediately.

Marguerite sat very still. Around her, the ship hummed its small domestic symphony — air recyclers, the tick of cooling conduits, Voss still swearing at his pump two decks down. Outside the viewport, the gas giant's storms wheeled in their slow orange violence, indifferent as law.

*Salvage.* The word finally landed, and it landed where she'd known it would, in the sealed place where she kept the things she couldn't afford to feel yet. She was not to be arrested. Arrest was for persons. She was to be *recovered*, the way you recovered a drifting container, a derelict hull, a thing that had once had value and could again.

Ilsa stood in the doorway again. How long she'd been there, Marguerite couldn't say. The girl's hands were fists at her sides, and her voice, when it came, was very controlled, which in a twelve-year-old with fifty years of command experience was more frightening than shouting.

"They said salvage claims. Anyone in good standing."

"Yes."

"Then anyone could file."

"Yes."

"Bo Halloway," Ilsa said, "owes the guild nothing and is in good standing with absolutely no one."

Marguerite turned from the screen. "Captain—"

"Don't 'captain' me. I'm master of this vessel and you're my courier, and I don't care what some arbiter in a brass room thinks a courier is." Ilsa crossed the wardroom in four strides that were too short for the distance she clearly wanted to cover. "There's archaic law. Old maritime law, pre-Guild, the kind they teach in the deep routes because nobody bothered to strike it. A ship's master owns her crew's contracts. If Bo files first—"

"If Bo files first, he owns me," Marguerite said gently. "That's not rescue, Captain. That's just a kinder cage."

"It's a cage with a door," Ilsa shot back. "I've read the old charters. Contract of service, terminable at will, transferable by consent. It's not ownership, it's — it's *claiming*. There's a difference. My grandmother taught me the difference when I was actually twelve the first time." She stopped, caught by her own words, by the strange arithmetic of a life lived backward. When she spoke again her voice had dropped. "Someone claimed me once. When my parents' debts came due. Someone stood up in a room like that one and said *she's mine* and it was the only reason I'm alive. I know exactly what it means. Don't tell me I don't."

The screen behind them cycled back to the guild feed: procedural notices, claim deadlines, the seal turning its slow tarnished circle. Marguerite looked at it, then at the girl, then at the viewport where the storm-light painted them both in shades of burning orange.

"When?" she asked.

"Claims window opens in nineteen hours," Ilsa said. "And Bo Halloway's last known heading puts him six hours out from the Threnody relay, if he's coming."

"And if he isn't?"

Ilsa set down her untouched tea with the deliberate care of someone who had learned, across two childhoods, that small rituals were how you kept your hands steady.

"He owes you," she said. "You pulled his skiff out of the debris field at Meridian Row when every other captain charged him a recovery fee he couldn't pay. He told me that himself, the night we met him. He said, *that android didn't bill me, and I don't forget debts like that.*" A pause. "He also said he'd rather be spaced than grateful, so don't expect him to say it twice."

Marguerite almost smiled. Almost. Instead she opened the navigation console and began, quietly, to plot a course back toward Threnody — back toward the relay station, the jamming shadow, the whole tightening knot of governments and guilds converging on one small stolen truth.

Nineteen hours. Sixteen if she burned the reserves she'd sworn not to burn.

Outside, the storm turned. Inside, the guild seal kept its rotation, patient as erosion, certain that everything drifting would eventually drift home to be claimed.

Somewhere between those two motions, Marguerite Vasquez — property, unit MV-7, thief, courier, massacre-machine, tea-drinker, someone's crew — set her ship on a heading no contract had ever authorized, and waited to find out who would reach her first: the men who wanted to own her, or the people who insisted, against all law and all sense, that she had never been ownable at all.

Six hours out from Threnody, a transponder flickered awake on the edge of her sensor range. Scavenger-class hull. Registry scrubbed, but the silhouette was unmistakable.

Bo Halloway was already moving.
