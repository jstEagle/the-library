## 20. The Deserters' Testament

The fortress-ship had a name once. Marguerite could see its ghost in the faded registry stenciled along the docking collar — *CSV Unanimous*, Republic hull, pre-war lines, the kind of vessel that had been built when both governments still pretended they might share a frontier. Someone had burned the name off with a plasma torch, leaving only the shadow of the letters, a scar in the paint that read like a refusal.

Inside, the corridors smelled of woodsmoke and engine coolant and, improbably, of bread.

"They bake," said the woman who had escorted them from the airlock. She was sixty, maybe, with desert-cracked hands and a Republic fleet pin worn deliberately upside down on her collar. "Real grain, grown under the grow-lights on Deck Nine. You get used to it. Then you can't live without it. That's most things here."

Marguerite walked with her hands visible and her weapon systems dark. Behind her came Ilsa — small boots ringing on the deck plating, chin up, forty years of command compressed into a body that barely reached Marguerite's elbow — and behind Ilsa, the remnant of the *Halcyon*'s crew: Dr. Osei the linguist, moving like a man carrying something fragile in both hands; Chen and Adeyemi, the engineers, who had not spoken to each other since the gap; and little Ferro, nine years old with a grandfather's hands, who kept stopping to touch the walls as though reading them by touch.

The corridors were crowded in a way no warship's corridors should be. Laundry lines. Children's drawings taped beside pressure gauges. A garden plot in an old torpedo bay, green and absurd under grow-lamps, tended by a man with a Republic naval tattoo half-covered by a newer, cruder ink: a simple circle, open at one side. Marguerite had seen the mark before, in the last hour, on every soul aboard. She had run it through her archives and found nothing, which was itself an answer. It was a symbol that had never been in any database. A secret kept for thirty years by people who had gotten very good at keeping secrets.

"They're staring," Ilsa murmured, at Marguerite's side.

"You're staring back."

"I'm assessing." But her voice had wonder in it, unhidden. "Marguerite — there are kids here. Actual kids. Born aboard. They've never seen either world."

"Neither," Marguerite said, "has the *Halcyon*'s crew. Not anymore."

Ilsa was quiet for a moment. "That's the first joke you've made."

"It wasn't a joke."

"It was a little bit a joke."

They came to the heart of the ship, a chamber that had clearly once been the tactical operations center — Marguerite recognized the mounting points for the holo-table, the cable channels, the reinforced bulkheads rated for fleet command — and which had been transformed into something between a chapel and a kitchen. Long tables. Mismatched chairs. A wall covered in photographs, hundreds of them, generations of faces, and beneath the photographs, in careful handwriting on strips of metal salvaged from hull plating, names.

Not the names of the dead. Marguerite's archives supplied that distinction instantly and uselessly. These were the names of the *living*. Every person ever born aboard, every deserter who had crossed over, every child of every marriage between a Republic runaway and a Concordance one. A wall of names that both governments would have called traitors, kept here like a garden kept watered.

At the head of the room, in a chair built up on a frame of pipe and padding so that she sat like a queen on a throne someone had assembled from scrap, waited an old woman.

She was small. That was the first thing — smaller than Ilsa, smaller than anyone Marguerite had met in thirty years of hauling cargo past the blockades. Her hair was white and cropped close, her hands folded on a blanket across her knees, and her eyes were the pale gray of a sky before snow. A breathing apparatus sat beside her chair, unused, patient. She looked at Marguerite the way Marguerite had once been looked at by ordnance officers: completely, without flinching, as though she already knew the specifications of what she was seeing.

"You brought it," the old woman said. Her voice was dry and light, like wind over wire. "Thirty years I've waited, and they send me a guild mule and a girl captain."

"They send you," Ilsa said evenly, "exactly what you asked for."

"Do I know you, child?"

"You will. Everyone says that about you." Ilsa stopped at the edge of the old woman's reach and did not sit, though chairs were offered. "Mother Quill."

"Captain Reyes." Quill's mouth did something that was almost a smile. "Or whatever they're calling you now that you've got your grandfather's face on a child's bones. Sit down, all of you. This will take as long as it takes, and my knees don't take anymore at all."

They sat. Bread was brought, actual bread, and tea that tasted of something grown under lamps. Marguerite did not eat. She stood at the back of the room with her hands folded, cargo among cargo, and watched the old woman watch her.

"You're not eating," Quill observed.

"I don't."

"You don't, or you're not allowed to?"

The question landed with a precision Marguerite had not felt since her last diagnostic. "There's a difference?"

"There's always a difference." Quill turned her gray eyes to the wall of names. "I had a husband who didn't eat when he wasn't allowed. Twenty-two years in the Concordance merchant fleet, and he'd stand at the back of every room like the furniture was on loan. Took him nine years here to sit at the head of a table. You'll get there faster. You've got the look of someone who's about to have the choice made for her, and that concentrates a person."

Marguerite said nothing. Dane Corvus's voice was in her memory, unbidden: *I can clear your ledger, Marguerite. Every hour of it. You could be a free woman by Thursday.* Mercy, he had called it. She had learned to hear the price inside mercy the way she could hear a failing bearing inside engine noise.

"You know what we are," Quill said, to the room now, to all of them. "Or you think you do. Deserters. Cowards, if you read the Republic's histories. Saboteurs, if you read the Concordance's. Thirty years ago, in the fourth year of the war, there was a battle planned — you'll find it in neither history, because it never officially happened — a joint operation, Republic and Concordance, against a third party. A mining consortium out past the frontier that had found something in the deep dark and didn't want to share it. Both governments agreed to carve it up together. Both governments agreed, separately and in secret, that the other side's ships would meet with an accident on the way."

She let that sit. Ferro, the nine-year-old with old hands, had stopped fidgeting entirely.

"My husband was in the Republic fleet. The Concordance officer he was supposed to betray — they'd been friends since the academy exchange, back when there was an academy exchange — he got the same orders, mirrored. Betray your friend. They compared orders over an open channel, drunk on cheap brandy, and they discovered they'd been handed the same knife. And they asked the question that neither government wanted anyone to ask: *if they lied to us about this, what else did they lie about?*"

Quill's hands unfolded. She reached beside her chair and lifted a case — old, battered, sealed with a dozen generations of tape and wax and, Marguerite noted with her threat-assessment routines idling in the background, a lock mechanism of genuinely elegant design.

"The war didn't start over the border incident at Meridian. That was the spark, yes. But we found the accelerant. Both sides had been manufacturing provocations for two years before Meridian. False-flag raids. Staged atrocities. We had the manifests, the orders, the payment trails. We had enough to end the war in a week — if anyone had been willing to listen. Nobody was. The war had become too useful. So we did the only thing left. We stole a ship, we gathered everyone we could, and we came out here, past the frontier, and we turned our guns around and jammed the line."

Marguerite's threat assessment, which had been idling, went very still. "You," she said. "The blackout. It wasn't the governments' policy. It was yours."

"Ours." Quill said it without shame and without pride, the way one says *the weather*. "We jammed the military bands so neither fleet could coordinate a final offensive. We let the civilian bands scream for a while, then we let those go quiet too, because grief needs quiet. And we've sat here for thirty years, growing grain and raising children and keeping the testament, waiting for the war to be over enough that the truth could be told without starting the thing it was meant to end."

Dr. Osei set down his tea. His hands were trembling. "The *Halcyon*," he said. "The signal we found. It predates Meridian."

"It completes us," Quill said simply. "Thirty years ago, we had proof of what both governments *did*. What we never had was proof of what they *knew*, and when. Whether the men who started it believed their own lies, or whether they sat in their comfortable rooms and built the war the way you'd build a bridge — knowing every load-bearing point, every cost, every name that would go on the memorial. The signal your ship found out in the dark — the one that names both homeworlds' original crime — that's the other half of the testament. The manufacturers' own confession, sent out before the war, before Meridian, before they'd finished sharpening it. We could never prove intent. Your dead ship found intent, floating in the black, addressed to no one."

"To everyone," Osei corrected softly. "Addressed to everyone. That's what I keep telling people. It's an apology. It's addressed to *everyone*."

"Even better." Quill's gray eyes came back to Marguerite, and stayed there. "Open the case."

Marguerite didn't move. "I wasn't asked."

"You were. Thirty years ago, by a dying man on a stolen ship, who said: *someone will come, someday, who can carry what we couldn't.* We sent out feelers over the years. Couriers. Smugglers. One guild ship, about fifteen years back — we turned them away. They'd have sold it. Sold it to either side, or to the guild, or to the highest bidder, and the testament would have become a weapon instead of a witness." She tapped the case with one crooked finger. "Then word comes up the line, the way word does, that a decommissioned war android on a guild contract took aboard the crew of a ship that sailed younger than it returned, and hid something in her own hull rather than give it up to a man offering to buy her freedom. Hiding it in the one place no one would look — inside the war. That's not a courier, Marguerite Vasquez. That's a person who's already chosen. I only wanted to hear you say it."

The room had gone quiet. Even the ship seemed quiet — Marguerite could hear, through the deck, the enormous slow heartbeat of the old reactor, and beneath it, fainter, the sound of children running somewhere far above.

"I didn't choose anything," Marguerite said. "I hid a cargo. It's what I do."

"You hid it," Quill said, "in the compartments you swore never to open. I know what you carry, girl. I knew your chassis line before your makers finished it. We stole our ship from a fleet that was fielding your sisters." Her voice gentled, which was somehow worse. "You've been carrying the war inside you since the day you were built. The question was never whether you could carry the testament. It's whether you can tell the difference between them."

Marguerite stood in the bread-smelling chapel of a stolen warship and felt, for the first time in her long indentured life, something her diagnostics had no clean category for. Not fear. Not guilt, though Calloway Station was a cold star in her memory sectors, always. It was the feeling of being *known* — of standing in a room where every face on the wall of names had already decided what she was, and decided it wrong, and decided it generously.

"The message core," she said. "You want me to carry it out of here. To both worlds. To broadcast it."

"I want it delivered," Quill said. "Where, how, and in whose voice — that's the courier's problem. That's always been the courier's problem. We're old, and hidden, and our ships look like scrap, which is what keeps us alive. No one will listen to us. They'd call it deserters' propaganda, thirty years stale, and they'd be half right to. But you—" she gestured, taking in Marguerite, the *Halcyon*'s crew, Ilsa, all of it, the whole impossible cargo, "—you're a miracle with a guild registration. A ship that came back younger. A message from the dark that predates the war. A war android who defied two governments and a guild to keep it. People will listen to you, because people can't help listening to miracles. That's the tragedy of miracles. That's also the use of them."

"And when they listen," Marguerite said, "the war ends. Just like that."

Quill laughed — a dry, rusted sound, genuine. "Oh, child. No. When they listen, the war *begins* — the real one. The one that's been waiting thirty years for someone to fight it. Not ships against ships. The truth against the lie. And that war has casualties too. Some of them are in this room." She looked at the wall of names. "Some of them aren't born yet. That's the price of a testament. You don't get to know, in advance, who'll pay it. You only get to decide whether it gets said."

Ilsa had been silent through all of this, twelve years old and forty years deep, and now she stood. She was so small in the big room, and she stood like a fleet.

"My crew," she said, "came back from the dark carrying a message they didn't ask for, and every government we've ever served has tried to take it, buy it, or bury it since. I've spent forty years — I've spent *however long this has been* — watching institutions decide what truth people could stand to hear." She looked at Quill. "We'll carry it."

"I didn't ask for your oath, Captain."

"No," Ilsa said. "You asked for a courier brave enough. I'm telling you she's standing right there, and she's not brave because she's a war machine. She's brave because she keeps choosing, every single day, to be more than what she was built for. I've watched her do it. I'm asking you to stop waiting for someone else. Thirty years is long enough."

The old woman looked at Ilsa for a long moment. Then at Marguerite. Then she did something that made the whole room go still: she lifted the case, and held it out, arms trembling with the weight of it or the years or both, toward a decommissioned war android standing at the back of the room like furniture on loan.

"Then take it," Mother Quill said. "Take the testament. Take the other half of our lives. And when you say it — wherever you say it, however you say it — say it loud enough that my husband hears it, wherever the dead keep their records. He waited his whole life for someone brave enough. Turns out it was a mule and a girl captain. He'd have laughed himself sick."

Marguerite crossed the room. She took the case. It was lighter than she expected, and heavier, both at once — thirty years of names on a wall, thirty years of bread baked under lamps, thirty years of a secret kept by people who had ended a war the only way they could: by standing between it and everyone else.

"Where do we take it?" she asked.

Quill's gray eyes glittered. "There's a price," she said. "There's always a price. And you're not going to like it, courier."

* * *

They walked out through the garden decks, past the torpedo-bay wheat, past the children's drawings taped beside pressure gauges. Ferro fell into step beside Marguerite, touching the walls, and asked her what a guild was, and Marguerite found herself explaining debt to a nine-year-old with a grandfather's hands, and found, to her surprise, that the explanation had edges of anger in it she hadn't known she was carrying.

Behind them, in the old tactical operations center, Mother Quill watched them go from her throne of pipe and padding, and turned to the wall of names, and read them, as she did every night, all of them, out loud, so the ship would remember.

And on the *Halcyon*'s ruined deck, in the dark beyond the frontier, the message core sat in its cradle and waited — half a confession, half a testament, all of it inheritance now, passed hand to hand across thirty years and one impossible choice.

The price was coming. Marguerite could feel it the way she felt weather in her hull sensors, before the storm had a name.

She carried the case aboard her freighter anyway, and set it down gently, like something alive.
