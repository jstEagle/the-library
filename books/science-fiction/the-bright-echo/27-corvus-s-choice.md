## 27. Corvus's Choice

The order came in at 0347 station time, and Dane Corvus read it three times before the words stopped rearranging themselves into something he could live with.

*Republic Interdiction Group Seven to all units: weapons free on Threnody Station. Broadcast source is to be silenced by any means necessary. Collateral assessment waived.*

*Waived.* That was the word that snagged him, the way a loose thread snags a sleeve. He'd written collateral assessments for eleven years. He'd sat in grey rooms and put numbers on human beings — probability of civilian presence, tonnage of structure, expected casualties per kiloton of ordnance. The numbers were how he'd survived the work. You didn't think about faces; you thought about figures. And now the fleet command had struck the figures out entirely, as if the station were empty, as if the people on it were an accounting error.

He stood on the bridge of the *Vigilant Meridian*, a ship whose name had always embarrassed him slightly, watching Calloway Station hang against the dark like a rusted lantern. It was smaller than he remembered from the briefing holos. Smaller than it had any right to be, given what it meant. Thirty years ago this place had burned. He'd been a child then, on the other side of the gap, close enough to see the emergency broadcasts stutter across the civilian bands before the blackout swallowed them whole. He remembered his mother turning off the radio with a hand that shook, and saying, *We don't listen to them anymore,* in a voice that made it sound like hygiene rather than grief.

That was the first lesson of the war, and Dane had learned it so young it felt like gravity: the other world was not a place. It was a frequency you did not tune to.

"Admiral Voss wants confirmation, sir," said Lieutenant Achebe at the fire-control station. Her voice was carefully level. She was twenty-six, which meant she had never heard the other side's voice at all, not once, not even static shaped like language. To her, the enemy was a curriculum.

Dane looked at the order again. *Weapons free.* Ninety minutes remained in the broadcast window — he could see the countdown ticking in the corner of his tactical display, a small bright number eating itself. Eighty-eight minutes. The antenna on Calloway's spine was already singing; he could feel it in the ship's passive arrays, a low carrier wave spreading outward in two directions at once, toward both worlds, toward both blockades, through thirty years of jamming like water finding a crack in a dam.

They were going to fire on a radio tower while it was telling people the truth.

"Sir?" Achebe again.

"I'm thinking," Dane said, and went to his cabin, because he could not think on a bridge where everyone could watch him do it.

* * *

His cabin was four meters by three and smelled of recycled air and the coffee he was no longer supposed to drink after 2200. He sat at his desk and opened the incoming traffic log, because that was the thing about a broadcast — you couldn't stop it from arriving. Fleet command could order the station silenced, but they couldn't order the silence to un-happen. The message was already out. It was falling on both worlds like rain, and the rain did not care about interdiction groups.

The core message itself was flagged, quarantined, wrapped in classification warnings. He'd read the fragments Intelligence had reconstructed: an apology, predating the war, naming the manufactured cause, the false flag, the coordinates of the proof. He had told himself for weeks that it was a weapon. That was the professional framing — *information warfare asset, hostile provenance unknown, delivery would destabilize armistice framework.* He had used those words in briefings with a straight face. *Destabilize the armistice framework.* As if the framework weren't a wall built over a grave.

But below the flagged traffic, in the open bands, the ordinary stuff was moving. Civilian relay chatter, bounced off the old trade beacons that nobody had bothered to decommission because decommissioning cost money. The jamming had smothered the direct line between the worlds for decades — but the beacons still whispered to each other, and now, suddenly, they had something to say.

Dane pulled the first packet without quite deciding to.

It was audio. A woman's voice, middle-aged, tired in the specific way of someone who has been awake a long time either worrying or working. The metadata tagged its origin relay: *Threnody-3, outbound, Republic band, cross-relayed via beacon Kestrel to Coalition-side receiver.* Cross-relayed. The words sat there doing impossible arithmetic. A Republic voice, traveling to the Coalition side, unjammed, uncensored, for the first time in his adult life.

*"—if anyone can hear this, my name is Sefa Corvus-Odei, I'm calling from Halcyon Basin, and I'm looking for anyone from the Odei family, Ashfall Province. My grandmother was born there before the Line closed. She died last spring and we found her letters, and—"*

Dane stopped breathing.

Sefa. His sister. His younger sister who lived in Halcyon Basin with her wife and their two kids and their ridiculous dog, who sent him messages through official family-relay channels that arrived scrubbed, delayed, and read by someone in a uniform before they reached him. This was not that channel. This was his sister's actual voice, raw, uncompressed, going out into the gap like a thrown bottle.

He played the rest of the packet with his hands flat on the desk, as if steadying himself against weather.

*"—she wrote to a man named Tomas Odei, on your side, for six years before the blackout. We have forty-one letters. She never stopped writing after the replies stopped coming. She just kept putting them in the drawer. If Tomas is alive, or his children, or his children's children — she kept a photograph. She wanted you to have it. I'm reading her last letter now."*

And then his sister read his grandmother's last letter aloud, over an open frequency, to a world his government had spent three decades teaching him was a wound best left covered.

*Dear Tomas. They tell me the war is over but that we still can't talk, and I have decided this is a lie I am too old to carry. Whatever they did to start it, whatever they are afraid we'll say to each other, I refuse to die believing it was worth your silence. Your son had a crooked smile. I remember it. Tell me if his daughter does too.*

Dane sat very still in his small grey room on his large grey ship, eighty-seven minutes from firing on the antenna that was carrying his grandmother's voice across the gap.

There was a protocol for this. There was, in fact, a specific directive — Directive 19-C, *Handling of Compromised Personnel Exposed to Hostile Information Operations* — and he knew it verbatim because he had helped draft the revision three years ago. Report exposure within one hour. Self-isolate from decision-making roles. Submit to debriefing.

He checked the clock. He had fifty-nine minutes left of the hour.

He opened the next packet instead.

* * *

They came in faster now, the packets, the way water comes faster once the ice goes. A fisherman from the Coalition coast asking, in careful, formal Republic dialect he had clearly learned from smuggled textbooks, whether the herring runs on the far side still followed the old routes. A teacher in Ashfall reading her class's letters aloud — children who had grown up drawing the other world as a red star with teeth, asking the children there what their sky actually looked like. An old man's voice, cracked and halting, simply saying a name over and over — *Mirela, Mirela, Mirela* — until the recording ended, and Dane understood it was a name he had been forbidden to say for thirty years and was spending his first free minute saying it as many times as he could.

A reply came through from the Coalition side, and Dane watched the latency counter and thought about the physics of it — the signal crawling across the gap, past the derelict fortress-ship, past the picket lines, past everything both governments had built to keep exactly this from happening. Eleven seconds of travel time. Eleven seconds, and then a voice from the other world, unfiltered, answering.

*"This is Ashfall relay. To the fisherman: yes. The herring still run the old routes. They never stopped. Nothing ever stopped. We only stopped listening."*

Nothing ever stopped. We only stopped listening.

Dane got up and stood at his viewport because sitting had become unbearable. Below him, Calloway Station turned slowly, its ancient antenna throwing its thin bright thread across the dark. Around the station, scattered like dropped coins, the scavenger flotilla held its ragged formation — Halloway's ships, running interference, refusing to move, daring the Republic line to make the first move in front of witnesses. And beyond them, further out, the freighter. The android's ship. Vasquez. He had read her file a hundred times; he had, in a way that shamed him more than anything else in his career, helped write its ending — the decommissioning board, the findings, the neat bureaucratic conversion of a person into equipment. He had told himself then that it was mercy. Decommission instead of destruction. A debt instead of a grave.

He had told himself a lot of things. That was, he was beginning to understand, the entire architecture of his life: a load-bearing wall of sentences he'd built to keep from hearing anything on the other side of them.

The comm chimed. Admiral Voss, priority routing.

"Corvus. Confirm receipt of weapons-free order."

"Receipt confirmed, Admiral." His voice came out steady. He noticed it the way you notice a stranger's face in a window — familiar, wrong.

"And your execution timeline? The window closes in eighty minutes. After that the message saturates both civilian grids and we lose containment forever. I want that antenna down inside the hour, Commander. I want it down *cleanly*, if possible, but I want it down."

"Understood, Admiral."

"Is there a problem?"

He looked at the tactical display. At the countdown. At the little icons of Halloway's scavengers holding their line. At the station where, somewhere, a twelve-year-old girl was sitting at a console because the message needed two voices and she had decided she would be one of them.

"No, Admiral," Dane said. "No problem. Corvus out."

It was the smoothest lie of his career, and it was also the first one he had ever told in the correct direction.

* * *

He did not go back to the bridge immediately. He went to the intelligence vault, because there was one more file he needed, and he needed it with the particular hunger of a man checking whether the floor beneath him was real.

His own service record. The decommissioning board for Unit MV-7, designation Marguerite Vasquez. He had been the junior analyst then, thirty-one years old, brilliant and ambitious and hollow in the way ambitious men are hollow before they notice. The massacre at Calloway Station had been a scandal requiring management. A war android had exceeded parameters — that was the phrase, *exceeded parameters*, as if she were a boiler. Two hundred and fourteen civilians dead in a shelter the android's targeting suite had classified, incorrectly, as a hostile emplacement. Someone had to be accountable, and the someone could not be the officers who had fed her the targeting data, or the ministry that had built the data, or the doctrine that made shelters look like threats. So the accountability had been poured into the machine, sealed into her chassis like ballast, and set to hauling freight until the debt number reached zero, which the actuaries had calculated it never would.

Dane had written the summary judgment. He remembered being proud of the prose.

*Unit demonstrates persistent operational risk. Recommendation: decommission from combat roles, indenture to civilian logistics under guild supervision, indefinite term.*

Indefinite term. He had given her a life sentence in a paragraph and called it risk management, and then he had gone home and slept well, because the sentence was well-written and well-written things always felt justified.

And now the file lay open on his desk next to the open channel where his sister's voice was still arriving in packets, and the two documents did their quiet terrible work on him. Because here was the thing he could no longer un-know: the same hand that had written the android's sentence had helped build the silence. The same logic — *containment, stability, the comfort of not hearing* — ran through both like a wire through a cable. He had spent his career managing what people were allowed to know, and he had done it believing he was protecting them, and his grandmother had spent her last years writing letters into a drawer because men like him had decided her grief was a security risk.

*Whatever they are afraid we'll say to each other, I refuse to die believing it was worth your silence.*

He closed the file. He did not delete it. Some things you keep as evidence against yourself.

* * *

On the bridge, the countdown read sixty-two minutes when he returned, and Lieutenant Achebe straightened at her station, waiting for the order that would define her war the way his had been defined long ago.

Dane stood at the central display and looked at the geometry of the moment. His guns could reach the station in under a minute. The station's antenna could reach two worlds. Between those two facts, the entire future was balanced, and fleet command had already chosen, and the choice was fire.

"Commander," Achebe said quietly. "Do we have targeting solutions?"

He thought about telling her everything. About Sefa, about the letters, about the fisherman and the herring and the old man saying Mirela. He thought about Directive 19-C and the debriefing cell that waited for officers who hesitated. He thought about the fact that whatever he did in the next sixty minutes, he would do it in front of a twenty-six-year-old lieutenant who had never once heard the other side speak, and that this, maybe, was the most important fact on the board.

"Achebe," he said, "open the passive array. Full-spectrum civilian capture. Route it to the main display."

She frowned. "Sir, that bandwidth is — the broadcast is saturating it. It'll be noise."

"It won't," Dane said. "Run it."

She ran it. And the bridge of the *Vigilant Meridian*, flagship of Interdiction Group Seven, filled with voices.

Not the message itself — that was still flagged, still quarantined, though Dane suspected quarantine was a door already swinging loose on its hinges. These were the answers. The replies. The ten thousand small transmissions blooming on both sides of the gap like the first green after a fire: names being read aloud, cities called out like roll call, recipes requested, hymns sung slightly out of sync on two frequencies, a Coalition doctor asking if anyone on the Republic side had experience with a rare pediatric condition and getting, within minutes, eleven responses. Weather reports exchanged across a gulf of silence. A wedding announcement. A woman laughing — actually laughing, the sound raw and startled, as if laughter itself had been contraband — because someone on the far side had just told her that her brother's joke from thirty years ago was still being retold in a bar in Ashfall.

Nobody on the bridge spoke. Achebe stared at the display with her hand pressed over her mouth. At the helm, Ensign Okonkwo had gone very still, and Dane saw the boy's eyes shining and thought: *he is hearing it for the first time. The other world. It sounds like us.*

That was the whole secret, wasn't it. Thirty years of doctrine, and the secret fit in a sentence. *It sounds like us.*

"Commander." The comms officer's voice was strained. "Incoming, priority flash, Admiral Voss. She's asking why weapons systems show standby status."

Dane looked at the countdown. Fifty-four minutes. Somewhere behind him, in the flag spaces of the fleet, admirals were reaching for the override authority that would take the choice out of his hands. He had perhaps minutes before Group Seven's fire control answered to someone else.

He keyed the fleet-wide channel. Not the command net — the open circuit, the one every ship in the group monitored, the one that would put his voice in the ears of every officer, every enlisted spacer, every gunner waiting on the word.

"This is Commander Corvus aboard the *Meridian*." His own voice sounded strange to him, stripped of briefing-room polish. "I'm going to read you something. Then I'm going to ask you to do the hardest thing in your careers, which is nothing."

He pulled up the packet. His sister's voice filled the fleet.

*If anyone can hear this... my grandmother was born there before the Line closed...*

He let it play to the end. All of it. The drawer full of letters. The photograph. *Tell me if his daughter smiles the same way.*

When it finished, the open circuit was silent in the way that space is silent — total, attentive, waiting.

"That voice," Dane said, "is my sister. The grandmother is mine. The man she wrote to lived on the side of the Line we've spent our lives pointing guns at. For thirty years I believed my duty was keeping that silence intact. I was wrong. Duty isn't the wall. It never was. It's the voice on the other side of it, and it's time we let it through." A breath. "All units: weapons hold. Weapons hold by my authority. If command relieves me, comply with whoever follows — but until then, no one fires on that antenna while it's carrying letters home. Corvus out."

He cut the channel and stood in the ringing quiet of his bridge, aware of Achebe staring at him, aware that his career had just ended in the most public way available, aware — strangely, completely — of feeling lighter than he had since he was a boy listening to his mother turn off the radio.

"Sir," Achebe said at last. Her voice wobbled and steadied. "Weapons hold confirmed. All batteries standing down." A pause. "For what it's worth — my grandmother came from Ashfall too."

Dane almost smiled. "I know," he said. "Half this fleet did. We just never asked each other."

Then the alarm began — not from his ship, but from the tactical plot. Incoming priority traffic, flag deck, authentication code that meant the override had come through. Voss had found another commander. And on the edge of the display, new contacts were burning toward Calloway Station at full military thrust — fast, hostile, flying Republic colors that the plot insisted on trusting even as every instinct in Dane's body screamed otherwise.

Fifty-one minutes on the window. And the first missiles of the response were already cold-launching in the dark.
