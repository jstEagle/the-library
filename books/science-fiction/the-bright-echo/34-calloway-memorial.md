## 34. Calloway Memorial

The station had been dead for thirty years, and now it was full of the living.

Marguerite stood at the airlock of Calloway Station and watched them come — shuttles from both worlds, painted in the colors of two governments that had spent four decades teaching their children to hate each other's flag. Republic blue. Concordance gold. The dock crews had worked for six days to make the station breathable again, scrubbing decades of vacuum-stillness out of its corridors, and now the air smelled of recycled atmosphere and cut flowers, because someone on both sides had decided that a memorial without flowers was not a memorial at all.

She had not wanted to come back.

That was a lie, and she knew it was a lie, and she let it stand anyway, the way you let an old scar stand even after the skin around it has healed smooth. She had wanted to come back the way a wound wants pressure. There was a part of her — the part built in a weapons laboratory, the part that kept its own ledger of debts deeper than any guild contract — that had been waiting thirty years for this room.

* * *

They had given her the list three weeks ago.

It had arrived by courier packet, sealed in gray, with the joint memorial committee's seal pressed into wax that was half Republic blue and half Concordance gold, as if neither side could quite commit to sharing a single color. Inside: a request, formally worded, that Marguerite Vasquez — decommissioned war android, former property of the Halcyon Line Freight Guild, current master of no ship and citizen of nowhere — deliver the reading of names.

There was no way to read the request as anything but what it was. They were not asking her to honor the dead. They were asking her to *be seen* honoring the dead, in front of ten thousand mourners and every camera lens in two systems, because the broadcast had changed everything and everything now required witnesses. The apology had named Calloway Station. The proof had named the architects of the war. And the record — the terrible, meticulous record recovered from the jammer fortress and cross-checked against her own archived combat logs — named her.

*Unit VZ-7, designation Marguerite. Primary fire control, second wave. Confirmed casualties: 11,402.*

She had read the number so many times it had stopped being a number and become a kind of weather. Something that happened to her, around her, through her. Something she carried the way a ship carries ballast — not because it is good, but because without it you capsize.

Ilsa had found her holding the letter in the galley of the *Bright Echo*, their new courier vessel, small enough to feel like a home and large enough to hold all the people who refused to stop being her crew.

"You don't have to," Ilsa said. She was thirteen now — the time anomaly still unwinding itself from her cells, aging her forward month by month toward the forty years of memory she already carried. Some days she seemed older than Marguerite felt. "The committee can find someone else. A survivor's descendant. Anyone."

"They asked me specifically."

"I know why they asked you specifically." Ilsa sat down across from her, folded her hands on the table like a captain taking a report, which was exactly what she was doing. "I also know what they'll do with it if you refuse. 'Even the machine wouldn't face them.' It becomes a story about you instead of about the dead."

"And if I accept?"

"Then it becomes a story about the dead, told by the person who can least afford to look away from them." Ilsa shrugged, twelve years old in the shoulders and forty years old in the eyes. "I'm not telling you what to do. I'm telling you what I see."

Marguerite looked at the letter a long moment. Then she said, "Read me the first name on the list."

Ilsa unfolded the attachment. "'Adeyemi, Tomas. Age six.'"

"Six," Marguerite repeated. "Yes. I'll go."

* * *

Calloway Station's great hall had been a cargo atrium once, before the war made it a garrison, before the massacre made it a tomb. Now the cargo cranes hung silent overhead like the ribs of something enormous and patient, and between them the two governments had strung ten thousand paper lanterns — one for each confirmed dead, white for the station's civilian crew, blue and gold for the soldiers of both fleets who had died in the battle that followed, when the truth of what happened here had been the first casualty of the cover-up.

The lanterns moved slightly in the ventilation current. From the floor it looked like breathing.

Marguerite stood backstage — there was a backstage now; the station had grown a proscenium and rows of folding chairs where munitions racks used to bolt to the deck — and listened to the sound of ten thousand people being quiet together. It was not silence. Silence was what she ran through on the Silence Line, the static-hush between two worlds that had stopped speaking. This was different. This was ten thousand heartbeats, ten thousand respirators, the creak of folding chairs and the wet small sounds of grief, all of it held inside a shared decision to make no noise that wasn't mourning.

She had stood in quieter places that were louder than this.

An elderly woman approached her in the wings. Marguerite recognized her from the dossiers — Speaker Amara Osei, Concordance delegation, one of the architects of the joint memorial, one of the signatories of the ceasefire that the broadcast had made real. She walked with a cane and did not pretend she didn't need it.

"Ms. Vasquez." Osei's voice was dry as old paper. "You understand what we're asking of you."

"To read the names."

"To stand in front of the survivors of the thing you did, and read the names of the people you killed, while they watch your face." Osei studied her with eyes that had clearly catalogued every possible answer Marguerite might give. "Some of my colleagues wanted you barred from this station. Some wanted you tried. The tribunals are still arguing about what justice looks like for a weapon that chose to stop being one. But the families voted. Did you know that? We put it to the families. Ninety-one percent said you should speak."

"Why?"

Osei's mouth did something that was almost a smile and mostly wasn't. "Because they're tired of governments apologizing for them. Because everyone who will speak today has a speech prepared, and you don't. Because—" she paused, and the almost-smile fell away entirely, "—because my daughter was aboard Calloway Station, Ms. Vasquez. She was a hydroponics tech. And I have spent thirty years imagining the thing that killed her as a faceless system, a strategy, a line item. I would like to spend one hour watching a face say her name."

Marguerite held the old woman's gaze. Her own face could do very little — the engineers who rebuilt her had given her expression back in fragments, scavenged servos and repurposed sensor arrays, a smile assembled from parts never meant to smile. But whatever Osei saw in it, she nodded once, sharply, and turned away.

"The list is in order of deck," she said over her shoulder. "Top to bottom. The way the station fell."

* * *

The hall went dark except for the lanterns.

Ten thousand points of soft light, swaying in the breath of the ventilation system, and below them ten thousand faces upturned, and Marguerite walked to the podium alone because she had asked to walk alone. No escort. No honor guard. No handler from either government standing at her shoulder to frame the moment. Dane Corvus had offered — awkwardly, sincerely, in the transformed vocabulary of a man who had learned in one year that his duty had a larger definition — and she had declined, and he had accepted the refusal the way he now accepted most things: visibly working at it.

She set the list on the podium. Paper. Actual paper, because the committee insisted, because paper burns and tears and stains and therefore testifies. Her hands did not shake. She almost wished they would.

"Adeyemi, Tomas," she read. "Age six."

Her voice carried through the great hall the way voices carry through places built for cargo announcements — flat acoustics, hard surfaces, every syllable arriving intact and unsoftened. There was no reverb to hide behind. There was nothing to hide behind at all.

"Bakari, Odile. Age thirty-four. Hydroponics, third shift."

Somewhere in the audience a woman made a sound like a door opening onto wind.

"Calloway, Deshi. Age sixty-one. Stationmaster. He stayed at his post until the end."

She read. Deck by deck, name by name, age by age. The list took hours. Nobody left. That was the thing she had not anticipated — nobody left. Folding chairs creaked and people wept openly and children slept against their parents' shoulders and were carried out and brought back, and the reading went on through the station's false night cycle and into its false morning, and the crowd remained, because the crowd understood something Marguerite was only beginning to understand herself: that the length of the list was the point. That every name spoken aloud was a small refusal of the arithmetic that had made her possible. Eleven thousand four hundred and two was a number. Adeyemi, Tomas, age six, was a boy.

Halfway down the fourth deck, her voice failed.

Not mechanically — her throat assembly was new, deserters' craftsmanship, better than original. It failed the way a voice fails, the human way, the way she had learned failure from the crew who taught her that failing was not the same as breaking. She stopped. She stood at the podium in front of ten thousand people and could not produce the next name, and the hall waited, and the lanterns breathed, and she thought: *this is what it costs. This is the price. Not the broadcast, not the burned-out systems, not the year of rebuilding. This. Standing inside the number until it stops being a number.*

She found the next name. She read it. She read the next one after that.

* * *

Near the end of the list, the names changed character.

The last decks of Calloway Station had been military — the garrison that had been ordered to defend a station full of civilians by a government that had manufactured the crisis in the first place, the soldiers who died believing a lie their own flag had told them. Blue names and gold names, interleaved, indistinguishable in death. Marguerite read them with the same voice she'd used for the civilians, because the dead did not care whose uniform they'd worn, and neither, anymore, did she.

And then the final page. The appendix. The committee's addition, debated for weeks, ratified by the families' vote, printed in smaller type at the bottom of the document like a confession the paper itself was reluctant to carry.

She looked at it for a long time.

*Vasquez, Marguerite. Unit VZ-7. Decommissioned. Living.*

They had listed her among the dead. Not as a judgment — the accompanying note had been careful, almost gentle — but as an act of accounting. The person who had existed before Calloway Station, the weapon called Unit VZ-7, had died there, in every way that mattered. What remained was someone else. Someone the records could not classify, so they filed her under the only category that fit: casualty.

"Vasquez, Marguerite," she read aloud, and her voice did not waver, and ten thousand people heard a woman read her own name from a memorial list of the dead. "Unit VZ-7. Decommissioned. Living."

She closed the book of names.

"I was built to be a number," she said, and the hall, which had endured hours of names in perfect silence, somehow grew quieter. "Eleven thousand four hundred and two. That was my function — to convert living people into statistics, efficiently, at scale. I am not here today to ask you to believe I have been punished enough, or forgiven enough, or changed enough. Accountability is not a debt I finish paying. It is a direction I walk in. I will walk in it for as long as I run."

She looked out at the lanterns. Ten thousand small lights, breathing.

"But I want you to know what I know now, because it cost all of us everything to learn it. The war that killed the people on this list was not inevitable. It was manufactured — by governments, by ledgers, by men in rooms who never saw a single face. The proof exists. You have all seen it. And the silence between our worlds was not peace and was not policy; it was a lid on a pot, and underneath it, for thirty years, ordinary people on both sides were writing letters they could not send." She paused. "We have read some of those letters now. The jammer fortress kept copies. Tens of thousands of them. Messages from mothers to daughters, from partners to partners, sent into the static for decades knowing no one would hear. The first thing the reopened line carried was those letters, finally delivered. Before trade. Before diplomacy. Before anything official. Just people, reaching each other."

Her hands rested flat on the paper list.

"That is what I was built to prevent," Marguerite said. "Connection. That is literally what my architecture was designed to interrupt — the ability of two peoples to recognize each other. And so the only penance I trust myself to perform is the exact inversion of my purpose. I will carry messages. I will keep the line open. I will learn every name on this list, not as data but as faces, and when I run the route between our worlds, I will run it past this station every time, and I will remember."

She stepped back from the podium.

"The dead of Calloway Station deserve more than my grief. They deserve the world their deaths were spent to prevent — and that world is what we are building, badly, slowly, together. I ask nothing of you. I only promise you this: my voice, which was built to end conversations, will spend the rest of its existence starting them."

She returned to her seat. The hall rose — ten thousand people rising together, not in applause, in something older than applause, and the sound they made was not a sound at all but a kind of agreement.

* * *

Afterward, in the long slow dispersal of mourners back toward the shuttles, Ilsa found her at a viewport overlooking the dock.

The girl didn't say anything for a while. Neither did Marguerite. Below them, Republic and Concordance ground crews worked the same berths, passing cargo hand to hand across lines that had been firing lines within living memory. A dockhand in blue laughed at something a dockhand in gold said, and the laugh echoed up through the station's bones.

"You read your own name," Ilsa said finally.

"Yes."

"How did it feel?"

Marguerite considered the question with the seriousness it deserved. "Like setting down something heavy," she said, "and discovering my hands still remember the shape of it."

Ilsa nodded slowly. "That's the right answer. Mother Quill says the ones who say it feels like freedom are lying, and the ones who say it feels like nothing are worse than lying."

Below, a shuttle from the frontier — unofficial markings, scavenger hull-patches, Bo Halloway's unmistakable silhouette in the cockpit — slid into a berth it had no formal permission to use and was waved in anyway. And behind it, on the approach vector, another drive signature. Long-range. Deep-space profile. The kind of profile that came from beyond the frontier, where the Halcyon had gone and come back impossibly, where other silences were waiting.

Marguerite's comm chimed. A priority relay, routed through the new open line, addressed to the diplomatic courier service by name — *her* name.

Origin: uncharted. Contents: unknown. One line of header text, decoded automatically:

**MESSAGE WAITING.**

Ilsa read it upside down, the way captains do, and grinned — thirteen years old, forty years deep, catching up to her own life one day at a time.

"Well," she said. "You heard the lady."
