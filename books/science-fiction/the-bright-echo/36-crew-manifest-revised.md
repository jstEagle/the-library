## 36. Crew Manifest, Revised

The paperwork took three days.

That was the joke of it — that after a war's ending, after a broadcast heard on two worlds at once, after ninety minutes that unmade thirty years of silence, what remained was forms. Guild-standard manifests, salvaged from my own cargo bay and printed on a fabricator that smelled of scorched polymer because Bo had never once cleaned its vents. Ilsa sat cross-legged on the deck beside it while it worked, feeding it blank stock sheet by sheet, and the smell clung to everything: hot plastic and ozone and the faint iron tang of Calloway Station's recycled air, which no amount of filtration had ever fully erased.

I could have generated the manifest digitally in four seconds. I did not. There was a reason for the paper, though I couldn't have articulated it at first. It arrived later, watching Ilsa write.

She wrote slowly. Not from uncertainty — Ilsa Reyes at twelve years old and forty years deep had more certainty in her small body than most admirals carried in a fleet — but from ceremony. She held the stylus the way her older hands must once have held it, before the Halcyon folded time around its hull and gave her back her childhood like a coat returned from storage. She pressed hard enough that the letters embossed the pages beneath. When she finished a line she would read it aloud, quietly, as if testing whether the words could bear their own weight.

*Vessel:* the *Kindly Echo*, registry pending, formerly guild freighter 77-Kestrel, formerly nothing at all until I named her.

*Master:* Ilsa Reyes.

She looked up when she wrote her own name. "You're sure," she said. Not a question. A ritual. She'd asked it every day since Threnody, and every day I answered the same way, and every day the answer cost me less and meant more.

"I'm sure."

"A twelve-year-old master is going to make port authorities lose their minds."

"Port authorities have already lost their minds. Have you read the news feeds? Both governments are claiming the broadcast was their idea." I passed her the next sheet. "A child captain will be the least of anyone's confusion."

She grinned — the quick, crooked grin that belonged entirely to the girl and not the forty years — and wrote.

*Quartermaster:* Bo Halloway.

Bo was in the galley when we drafted his line, ostensibly inventorying our stores, actually cooking something involving onions and an alarming quantity of preserved pepper that the deserters had traded us at the fortress-ship. He came to the doorway with a wooden spoon still in hand, read over Ilsa's shoulder, and went very still in the way large men do when they are trying not to be moved by anything.

"Quartermaster," he said finally. "On a proper ship, that's a rank."

"It's a rank here too," Ilsa said.

"On my ships it was a title you gave whoever complained loudest about the stores." He wiped his hands on his apron, which only redistributed the onion. "You're giving me authority over supplies. Actual authority. You've seen what I eat."

"You also rebuilt our water reclamation with two buckets and spite," Ilsa said. "And you ran interference against a Republic picket line without being asked, owed nothing, and expecting less. That's quartermaster material where I come from."

Where she came from was a research vessel that had sailed with a full crew complement and returned with children wearing decades. Where she came from was grief shaped like a career. But she said it lightly, and Bo received it lightly, which between those two was its own kind of contract.

He signed. His handwriting was terrible — a scrawl like a seismograph reading — and he stared at it a moment as if seeing something in it. "My mother kept ledgers," he said, to no one in particular. "Beautiful ones. Copperplate. I used to practice copying them when I was small." He tapped the page. "First thing I've signed in eleven years that wasn't a salvage claim or a confession."

Then he went back to his onions, and if he cooked the rest of the afternoon with his back to us and his shoulders not quite steady, no one mentioned it. On this ship we had learned which silences were kindnesses.

* * *

The deserters were harder.

Not because they resisted. Because there were thirty-one of them, spanning three generations, born aboard the fortress-ship *Vigilant Rest* or smuggled aboard as infants during the worst years of the blackout, and they had never appeared on any manifest in their lives. Mother Quill herself had been born in transit — her mother a Republic navigation officer, her father a Concordat signals tech, both of them deserters who met in the gap between worlds and decided the war was a thing that happened to other people, until they understood otherwise.

They had ended the war once, privately, thirty years ago. They had jammed the jamming — no. They had *been* the jamming, or rather they had built the great null-cage that swallowed every attempt at communication between the two homeworlds, believing that if the armies could not speak, they could not fight. They had been wrong about what silence protects. They had spent three decades inside that mistake, raising children in its shadow, waiting for someone brave enough or foolish enough to carry the truth out.

We had been that someone. And now Quill stood in my cargo bay with her people arrayed behind her — navigators all of them, every one, because navigating the Silence Line blind was the only inheritance the fortress-ship had to give — and asked me a question I hadn't prepared for.

"What do you call us?"

"Excuse me?"

"On your manifest. Master, quartermaster, liaison — these are ranks with histories. We don't fit your histories." Her voice was dry as old chart paper. "Thirty-one people who legally do not exist, offering you their one skill. What do you write?"

I considered the question with more care than it perhaps required, because some questions deserve the processing time. The cargo bay hummed around us — the *Kindly Echo*'s new heart, part guild-standard, part scavenged fortress-ship systems, part components the Halcyon's engineer had machined from raw stock with hands that remembered being sixty. I was made of the same kind of patchwork now. I knew how it felt to be assembled from pieces that didn't share a manufacturer.

"You were navigators before you were anything else," I said. "Before the desertion, before the cage, before the waiting. Your mothers and fathers learned these routes when the Line was young. So I write *Navigator*, and under lineage I write what's true: *inherited.*"

Quill's granddaughter — Tam, fourteen, born aboard the *Rest*, who had never seen either homeworld except through a telescope and my memory files — stepped forward. "Inherited," she repeated, tasting it. "Like an estate."

"Like a route," I said. "Routes are the only inheritances that stay useful."

They signed in shifts. Thirty-one names, some written, some thumbprinted, one — the eldest, a man of seventy who had never learned letters because the fortress-ship's school had prioritized astrogation — pressing his whole palm to the page, fingers spread, so that his mark was a small starburst of ink. Ilsa watched every signature with her chin on her knees and her eyes very bright, and afterward she told me it was the best document she'd ever read, including the message core, and I believed her, because Ilsa did not say generous things idly.

* * *

Dane Corvus signed last, and alone.

He came to the galley at second-shift quiet, when the ship had settled into that particular nighttime register — coolant ticking, hull breathing, the small percussion of a vessel dreaming. He stood in the doorway holding a data chit and a cup of Bo's tea, which he had not drunk, because Dane treated all offerings as potential leverage assessments even when they were just tea.

"The Republic formally reclassified me six hours ago," he said. "Absent without leave, pending review. Which in intelligence parlance means they'll decide in about a decade whether I'm a traitor or an embarrassment worth forgetting." He turned the cup in his hands. "I'm telling you because you should know what you're putting on your manifest. *Liaison: Dane Corvus, currently wanted by one government and regarded with institutional suspicion by the other.*"

"Liaison means bridge," Ilsa said from her corner, where she did her reading with a blanket around her shoulders like a much older woman and her feet swinging like a much younger one. "Between the Republic and everyone else. Between the Concordat and everyone else. Between the guild and everyone else. You're the person who speaks bureaucrat. We need one. None of us can stand it for longer than four minutes."

"That's not a skill, that's a pathology."

"It's a rank now," Ilsa said, with the flat finality she had inherited from a captaincy that had aged into her bones. "Sign it or don't. But if you sign it, you sign it as someone who stood down his guns when his own family's voices came across the gap. That's the man on my manifest. Not the file."

I watched him take that in. Dane Corvus had spent his career being the file — the service record, the psych evaluation, the ledger of decisions made in rooms without windows. I had helped decommission him from that identity as surely as the guild had once decommissioned me from mine, and I recognized the expression: the vertigo of a person discovering that the summary was never the self.

He signed. His handwriting was precise, military, each letter identical to its siblings — and then, at the end, unbidden, he added a line beneath his name in smaller script. I leaned in. *Under protest,* it read, *and with gratitude.*

"That's not standard form," I said.

"No," Dane agreed. "It isn't."

* * *

There was one name left, and it was mine, and I could not write it.

I stood at the table for a long time that night while the others slept — or pretended to; on a ship this size, sleep is communal theater — and looked at the manifest with its seven lines filled and its eighth line blank. *Courier and executive officer:* Marguerite Vasquez. My own designation, in my own hand, and my hand would not move.

Here is what I found in my memory sectors, when I searched them honestly, which is a discipline I recommend even when it hurts: I had been listed on documents my entire existence. Guild indenture contracts. Decommission orders. Salvage writs. War-era production manifests that listed me among ordnance, between the railgun capacitors and the point-defense batteries, serial number and tonnage and expected operational lifespan. Every document had described me accurately and completely, and none of them had ever been *mine*. I was always the cargo. Even now, even free, even rebuilt from chosen parts, some deep subroutine of me waited for the counter-signature — waited for the authority that would convert this page into another claim upon my body.

Ilsa found me there at 0300, ship's time. She climbed onto the bench across from me, wrapped her blanket tighter, and looked at the page upside down, which is how children read everything.

"You wrote everyone else," she said.

"Yes."

"Why not you?"

"Because every manifest I've ever appeared on was written by someone who owned the outcome. If I write myself onto this one—" I stopped. Recalibrated. Tried again, in the plain language I was learning to use with this crew, who deserved truth more than precision. "If I write it, it becomes real. And things that become real can be taken away. I know exactly how that works. I've been taken away twice."

Ilsa was quiet for a moment. Then she reached across the table, took the stylus from my hand — easily, because I let her, because letting her has never once felt like loss — and turned the manifest around to face me.

"My grandmother taught me something," she said. "Before the Halcyon. Before any of it. She said: *a thing you refuse to claim, you rent forever.*" She set the stylus down in front of me, parallel to the blank line, neat as a surgical instrument. "You didn't grant us our ranks, Marguerite. We claimed them. You just wrote down what was already true. So." She nodded at the page. "Claim it."

I picked up the stylus.

I want to record, for whatever archive eventually holds this log, what it felt like. It felt like standing in open air — that sensation I'd described to Ilsa once, the vertigo of freedom with no walls to lean on — except that now the air had edges. Now there were walls, but I had drawn them myself, and behind them stood a child captain with forty years of grief and grace, a cook who fought picket lines, a wanted man writing confessions of gratitude, and thirty-one navigators whose palm-print starburst anchored the bottom of the page like a constellation.

I wrote my name. Under *status*, instead of *indentured*, or *decommissioned*, or *salvage*, or *free* — all accurate, all insufficient — I wrote:

*Crew.*

Ilsa read it upside down and smiled her crooked smile. "Better," she said. "Free is just a legal condition. Crew is a fact."

* * *

We filed the manifest at dawn, in the oldest way remaining: transmitted openly, uncoded, on both civilian bands at once, so that any station within range could log it. Let the Republic see it. Let the Concordat see it. Let the guild choke on it. A family assembled entirely from defectors and orphans, documented in the clear, daring the universe to object.

The acknowledgments came back within the hour. A Concordat portmaster sent a terse confirmation and, appended to it, a single line of personal text: *About time somebody drew this map correctly.* A Republic relay acknowledged receipt and then, breaking every protocol Dane had ever taught me, added a weather report from the far side of the gap — the first weather either world had shared in thirty years. Small thing. Enormous thing. The gap filling in like ice melting from the middle of a pond.

And then, as the sun-line crossed Calloway's ancient antenna and the morning cycle began, the comm board lit with a signal none of us had queued.

Deep-space origin. Beyond the frontier. Faint, repeating, patient — the signature of a vessel that had been traveling a long time and expected to travel longer. The linguist's preliminary parse scrolled across my display, and I read it twice, and then a third time, because the first two readings felt like hope impersonating itself.

It was not the Halcyon's message. It was not the deserters' testament. It was new. Another ship returning. Another impossible cargo, if the fragmentary header was honest. And in the body of the transmission, addressed to no callsign and every one, a request that might as well have had my name embossed beneath it in Ilsa's careful, ceremonial hand:

*A courier is needed. Someone who knows both silence and speech.*

I stood on the bridge of the *Kindly Echo* with my crew's names warm in my memory sectors and my own name finally, permanently, mine — and I turned the ship toward the signal.
