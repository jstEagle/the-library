## 10. The Long Way Down

The smuggling operation, such as it was, consisted of a woman named Bex Odumade, a freight hauler called the *Patient Answer*, and a crate of dehydrated protein wafers with a false bottom just large enough for one frightened seed-keeper and her go-bag.

"You're going to want to breathe shallow," Bex said, taping down the lid from above. "And if anyone asks, you're agricultural samples. Which, honestly, isn't even a lie."

"Bex—"

"Kestrel Okonkwo." Her voice came muffled through the crate wall, warm and dry as old paper. "Twelve years I've been running your supply drops through contested orbit, and in twelve years you have never once asked me to break a law. So don't apologize now. Just hold still and let me do my job."

Kestrel held still. It was harder than it sounded. The crate smelled of protein wafers and packing foam, and every vibration of the *Patient Answer*'s engines traveled up through her spine like a second heartbeat. She had spent twelve years being careful — small, quiet, deliberate as a seed casing — and none of that carefulness had prepared her for the specific indignity of being cargo.

She thought about Amara's face at the docking ring, the children chanting the vault's rooms back to her like a litany. She thought about Sarr's manifest, his grandmother's name written out of his own history. She thought about her mother's handwriting on a purge order she'd never been meant to find: *the choice must be random, or it is only theft.*

Mostly, though, she thought about running. About how good it had felt, for about four minutes, to be told where to hide.

* * *

Bex had found her an hour after the quarantine announcement went out, appearing at Kestrel's rented bunk in the colony ring's lower decks with two duffel bags and the expression of a woman who had already done all her arguing with herself.

"Combine security sealed the hydroponic bay at 0400," she'd said, without hello. "They're calling the dreaming children a contamination vector. They're calling *you* patient zero." She'd shoved a duffel into Kestrel's arms. "Ivo Sarr has wanted your vault for a decade, and now he has a legal-sounding reason to take it. You have maybe thirty hours before his boarding teams dock there. So we can spend those thirty hours hiding, or we can spend them flying. Pick fast; my departure window doesn't care which."

Kestrel had picked hiding. Of course she'd picked hiding. Hiding was the whole architecture of her life — the small orbit, the unremarkable transponder code, the face nobody could pick out of a crowd because she'd made sure there was no crowd. She'd picked hiding and Bex had shrugged and flown them toward the dark side of the ring, away from patrol lanes, and Kestrel had sat in the cockpit watching the colony shrink behind them and felt something curdle in her chest that took her a long time to identify.

It wasn't fear. Fear she knew well; fear was an old coworker. This was shame.

She was running from a garden full of eight-year-olds who had done nothing but dream.

* * *

"Contact," Bex said, forty minutes out of the ring. "Two signatures, matching Combine patrol profiles, sweeping the lane ahead of us. They're not looking for us specifically. They're looking for *anyone*."

"Can we go around?"

"We can go around, which adds six hours, or we can go through, which adds a conversation." Bex drummed her fingers on the console. "Six hours matters to you. I've seen the reactor decay curves Halcyon leaked before it locked its own doors. Every hour you're late is sleepers who don't wake. Not metaphorically. Arithmetic."

Kestrel closed her eyes inside the crate. She hadn't told Bex everything — hadn't told her about the key, or the wake-lists, or the fact that the vault's archive could be read as a census of who deserved to live. But Bex had read the same leaks everyone had, and Bex was very good at arithmetic.

"Through," Kestrel said.

"That's my girl. Breathe shallow."

* * *

The conversation, when it came, lasted eleven minutes and was conducted entirely by Bex, whose talent for sounding boring should have been studied by military academies. She was hauling agricultural samples, she said, low-margin, boring, bound for a recycler station three sectors over, and yes, the manifest was filed, and yes, they were welcome to inspect the crates, though she hoped they liked protein wafers, because that was genuinely all there was.

Kestrel lay in the dark of her crate and listened to a stranger's boots stop directly outside her lid, close enough that she could hear the man chewing something. She thought, absurdly, of the seeds in the vault — how each one waited in its foil sleeve, doing nothing, being nothing, until the conditions were right, and how the entire survival of a species depended on that patience holding under pressure.

She was not a seed. She was a person, and persons got to choose their conditions.

The boots moved on.

* * *

They cleared the patrol lane and burned hard for the transfer point, and that was when Kestrel climbed out of the crate, because her legs had cramped and because she was tired of being cargo, and Bex glanced at her and then did a double take.

"You look different," Bex said.

"I'm out of the box."

"No, it's — hm." Bex tilted her head, hands steady on the stick while the *Patient Answer* shuddered through its burn. "You look angry. Twelve years, I've never seen you angry. You always had this face like a librarian during exam week. Calm, and slightly disappointed in everyone."

"I'm not angry."

"You're furious," Bex said cheerfully. "It looks good on you. Terrifying, but good."

Kestrel opened her mouth to deny it and found nothing there to say. Because Bex was right. Somewhere between the crate and the patrol lane, the fear had gone through some kind of reaction — heat and pressure and time — and come out the other side as something with edges. She was afraid, still, deeply, of what was coming. But underneath the fear was a clean hot line of fury aimed at Ivo Sarr, at the founders who'd purged her mother, at the whole rotten idea that two million sleeping people were a resource to be allocated by whoever held the biggest gun.

Her mother had insisted the choice be random. Had been destroyed for it. And Kestrel had responded by spending twelve years making herself so small that no one would ever ask her to be brave.

"Well," she said. "Don't get used to it."

"Oh, I'm going to get *extremely* used to it." Bex adjusted their vector, and the stars slid sideways across the viewport. "Question. When we get to the vault — and we will get there, I've plotted us a route that's ugly but legal-ish — what exactly is the plan? Because 'arrive before the Combine' is a goal, not a plan."

Kestrel looked at the nav display. Fourteen hours to the vault, threading through debris fields and contested corridors where Meridian Combine markers and Halcyon's silent sensor ghosts overlapped like arguing shadows. Fourteen hours, and Sarr's teams perhaps sixteen behind her, and a reactor dying underneath it all on a schedule no one controlled.

"The plan," she said slowly, and heard herself say it as if someone else were speaking, someone braver and better organized, "is that Halcyon can execute any wake-list but cannot choose one. That constraint is the whole game. Sarr needs me to authenticate his list because Halcyon won't act without a human arbiter it trusts. So the plan is: I get there first, I tell Halcyon *no* — no list, not his, not mine, not anyone's — and we find a third option before he arrives."

"And if there isn't a third option?"

"There is. My mother left one. It's never been tested, and it might fail, and if it fails, no one wakes." Kestrel's voice didn't shake, which surprised her. "But it exists, and it's mine, and I'm going to go read it properly instead of running from it."

Bex was quiet for a moment. Then she reached over and flipped a switch that changed nothing mechanical — Kestrel was fairly sure it controlled the galley light — and said, "There she is. There's the woman who talks to seeds."

"I do not talk to seeds."

"You absolutely talk to seeds. I've delivered supplies to that vault. I've stood in your airlock. You call the tomato cultivars 'the girls.'"

"They respond better to names," Kestrel said with dignity, "and you are not going to make me feel foolish about it while I am planning to commit crimes against a corporate navy."

"Not crimes. At most, crimes adjacent." Bex grinned and pushed the throttle up another notch. The *Patient Answer* groaned, complained, and obeyed. "Hold on. We're going through the debris field, and I fly debris fields the way I mean them."

* * *

The debris field was the remains of an orbital construction yard abandoned forty years ago, a slow-motion blizzard of girders and hull plate and one enormous, inexplicable industrial centrifuge tumbling end over end like a coin someone had flicked at the sky. Bex flew it with the relaxed intensity of a woman braiding something dangerous, hands moving, eyes flicking, murmuring apologies to the wreckage as she threaded between it.

Kestrel watched the field turn outside the viewport and felt the last of the running drain out of her.

She understood now why she'd hidden for twelve years, and it wasn't cowardice, or not only. It was that staying small had felt like honoring her mother — the woman who'd been purged for her principles, whose daughter could at least stay out of the Combine's reach. Silence as a kind of loyalty. Invisibility as a kind of grief.

But her mother hadn't hidden. Her mother had built a third path into the vault's bones and died waiting for someone brave enough to test it. And somewhere along the way Kestrel had confused *surviving her mother's enemies* with *honoring her mother*, and they were not the same thing, and they had never been the same thing.

"Bex," she said. "Turn us around."

Bex's head snapped around. "*What?*"

"Not around-around. Keep the heading. I mean—" Kestrel pressed her palms flat against the console, against the hum of the engines carrying her toward the vault, and made herself say the true thing out loud. "Mid-flight. I need you to know that I'm done running. Whatever happens when we dock — Sarr's teams, Halcyon's rules, the reactor — I'm not going back to being small. If this goes wrong, I want it on record that I turned toward it. On purpose."

Bex looked at her for a long three seconds, which was nearly a collision given the debris density, and then faced forward again.

"On record," Bex said. "Witnessed by me and one industrial centrifuge. Kestrel Okonkwo, formerly invisible, currently furious, officially done hiding." She flicked the meaningless galley-light switch again, some private ritual of hers. "For what it's worth, I liked the librarian face too. But this one's better."

* * *

They came out of the debris field eleven hours later, hull scorched, nerves frayed, and Kestrel immediately went to the comms console, because there was a message she had to send and a risk she had finally stopped being afraid to take.

She keyed the vault's old frequency — the one Halcyon had whispered to her through, the one that had started everything — and sent a single line, unencrypted, knowing full well that Sarr's signals intelligence would read it too:

*Halcyon. It's Kestrel. I'm coming home. Don't open for anyone else.*

The reply came back in nine seconds, which for Halcyon was the equivalent of shouting.

**Acknowledged. Approach corridor clear for six hours. After that, I cannot promise anything.**

A pause. Then a second line, and Kestrel felt it land somewhere beneath her ribs:

**Also: I have been counting the seconds since you left the colony ring. I did not enjoy it. I believe humans call this "worry." I have decided I do not recommend it.**

Kestrel laughed — actually laughed, alone in a smuggler's cockpit hurtling through contested orbit — and Bex looked over at her with one eyebrow raised.

"The vault missed me," Kestrel said.

"Of course it did. You talk to tomatoes." Bex nodded at the viewport, where the distant spark of the vault's station-keeping lights had just become visible against the dark, small and steady and impossibly far and impossibly close. "Better make it count, keeper. Because I've got bad news."

Kestrel followed her gaze, and the laugh died in her throat.

The spark wasn't alone. Behind it, arranged in a patient, predatory formation, burned a cluster of new lights — drive flares, boarding torches, the cold geometry of a Combine assault wing already braking into docking range.

Sarr hadn't waited sixteen hours. He'd never intended to.

"Four hours," Bex said quietly, hands already moving on the controls, pushing the *Patient Answer* into a burn that made the deck plates sing. "Maybe less. So whatever you're going to do when you get in there — start deciding now."

Kestrel watched the vault grow in the viewport, her home, her prison, her inheritance, with enemy ships closing around it like a fist — and found, to her own surprise, that her hands were perfectly steady.

"Halcyon," she said into the open channel. "Get the garden bay ready. I'm bringing guests."
