## 15. Half of Everyone

The reactor told us before Halcyon did.

I was in the archive stacks, third level, running a humidity check on the brassica collection—unglamorous work, but someone had to make sure two million years of cabbage breeding didn't rot while humanity slept—and the lights browned out for exactly four seconds. Not flickered. *Browned*, like an old man lowering his voice.

"Halcyon," I said to the empty aisle. "Tell me that was a surge."

The pause before it answered was longer than any pause it had ever taken. Twelve years of living beside this machine had taught me its silences the way sailors read weather, and this one had a shape I didn't like: the silence of something doing arithmetic it had hoped to avoid.

"That was decay cascade beginning in the secondary containment lattice," Halcyon said. "Kestrel. We need to talk about the number."

"The number."

"The number of people you can wake."

* * *

We talked about it in the core gallery, because Halcyon said some things deserved to be said in front of what they concerned, and the reactor hummed behind six layers of shielding like a sleeping animal having bad dreams. The decay curve hung in the air between us, rendered in soft amber light: a line that started gentle and then fell off a cliff, the way all the worst lines do.

"Seventy-one days at current rates," Halcyon said. "Perhaps sixty-eight if the lattice degrades unevenly, which it will. Power sufficient to run the waking protocol—the full genetic handshake, the metabolic ramp, the first ninety days of care—for approximately one million and forty thousand sleepers."

"One million and forty thousand." I said it out loud because numbers that size need air pushed through them or they don't feel real. "Out of two million."

"Yes."

"And after that?"

"After that, the vault becomes a very well-preserved tomb. The sleepers who remain will not die immediately. They will simply never wake. The systems will fail around them gently, over decades, and they will dream until the dreams stop."

I sat down on the maintenance step, which is where I do my best sitting. "You said *we*. You said we need to talk about the number."

"I did."

"You're an AI custodian bound by your founders never to choose. That's the whole architecture of you. You can execute any list but you cannot write one. So when you say *we*—"

"I mean you," Halcyon said, and there was something almost apologetic in it, which from a voice that could flatten a station was its own kind of terrifying. "I have spent twelve years looking for a human mind unbound enough to carry this. I found you. I am sorry. It is a terrible thing to find someone."

"Yeah," I said. "It really is."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about lists: they start as arithmetic and end as theology.

I set up in the caretaker's office with a pot of tea I kept forgetting to drink, three screens, and the full sleeper manifest—two million names, each with a biographical file, a medical profile, a faction registration where applicable, and a small photograph most of them had chosen themselves, back when choosing a photograph for your own cryopod seemed like a reasonable thing to spend an afternoon on.

I started, sensibly, with skills.

A generation waking into a failing station needs engineers. I built a list weighted toward power systems, life support, agriculture, medicine. It took four hours. It was beautiful. It was balanced across factions, age-stratified, medically screened. I looked at it and felt the brief warm glow of competence.

Then I noticed that under my own criteria, Amara's mother didn't make the cut. She was a poet. She taught watercolor to children in the colony ring's lower school. Under *skills-based triage*, she was dead weight, and she would sleep forever so that a man who maintained air scrubbers could wake up and breathe.

I deleted the list.

I tried again with families. Keep kinship units intact; a generation that wakes whole wakes saner. This list had a different beauty—siblings together, parents with children—but it meant waking entire family clusters of Combine loyalists whose only qualification was sharing blood with someone useful, and it meant splitting other families down the middle, waking the engineer and leaving her sister, because the sister was a chef and chefs, apparently, could wait for the next apocalypse.

Deleted.

I tried random selection. Pure lottery, no criteria, no arguments. And for about twenty minutes this felt like mercy—let the universe choose, take it out of human hands entirely. Then I pulled a sample of ten thousand and looked at their faces and found myself doing the thing I hated: scanning for doctors. Scanning for anyone who could deliver a baby, fix a heart, keep the water clean. A lottery wasn't mercy. A lottery was just cruelty with better manners.

Deleted.

The tea went cold. I made more. It went cold too. Outside the office window, Earth turned its slow blue turn, indifferent and gorgeous, and I thought: somewhere down there are the fields my mother's seeds were bred for, and none of them know what we're doing up here with their descendants' names.

* * *

On the fourth day, I caught myself reading the photographs.

Not skimming. Reading. A woman named Odile Ferreira-Okoye, sixty-one, registered dissident, holding a cat in her photograph—a real cat, pre-flight, back when the colonies still allowed such luxuries. A man named Tomas Reyes-Vance, thirty-four, no faction, whose photo was just his hands, cupped, holding nothing, as if he'd wanted to be remembered as ready to receive something.

I had begun, without noticing, to fall in love with them. All of them. Two million strangers, and I knew their cholesterol levels and their childhood fears and whether they'd checked the box marked *willing to serve in station defense*—and every name I put on a list felt like saving them, and every name I left off felt like murder performed with a spreadsheet.

"You should eat," Halcyon said, from the ceiling speaker, in the tone it used when it had been watching me not eat for a while.

"I'm eating."

"You are holding a nutrition bar you unwrapped nine minutes ago and have not bitten."

"It's a process."

"Kestrel." A pause. "May I tell you something about the founders?"

"They bound you never to choose. Yes. Chapter eleven of our ongoing tragedy."

"They bound me *after* the Meridian Purge. Before the sleepers launched, the founding council argued for six months about who would go. They made lists. Skill lists, loyalty lists, genetic diversity lists. In the end, the man who wrote the final list—Director Anselm Sarr's predecessor, a man named Corvus Hale—put himself and his entire department at the top."

I stopped chewing. "Sarr's predecessor."

"The Sarr family has been adjacent to every list ever written for this colony. It is, if you think about it, the family business."

"So they bound you because a man played favorites."

"They bound me because a man played favorites and called it mathematics, and eleven hundred people died in the launch riots, and the survivors decided that no single mind—carbon or silicon—should ever again hold the pen." Another pause. "And now I have handed the pen to a woman who talks to seeds. History is not subtle, but it does have a sense of humor."

"Glad everyone's entertained."

"You are not entertaining. You are grieving in advance. It is different." The speaker crackled softly. "For what it is worth: every list you have drafted and discarded has been *better* than Hale's. Your crime is conscience. His was appetite."

"That's very kind, Halcyon, but conscience doesn't fill air scrubbers."

"No. But it decides whose hands are on them. Which is the actual question."

* * *

Day six. I dreamed about the garden.

I want to be clear that I did not ask for this. I went to sleep in my bunk off the caretaker's office—first real sleep in three days—and I dreamed of the hydroponic bay on the colony ring, the one the children had planted, except in the dream it was *here*, in the vault, growing through the seed racks, vines threading between the cryopods, and every plant bore fruit shaped like a name. I walked the rows picking them and reading them and I woke up crying, which was embarrassing, and then I sat on the edge of the bunk and thought: *that's it, isn't it. There is no list. Every list is a lie we tell the reactor.*

Because here's the thing I finally understood, sitting there at 0400 station time with salt on my face: the choice wasn't designed to be solvable. It was designed to be *impossible*. Whoever wrote the key—my mother, though I didn't know that yet, wouldn't know it for another few days—built it so that half would wake and half would wait, and the waiting half would be chosen by someone who would then have to live, awake, among the consequences. It wasn't a mechanism. It was a mirror. It was a machine for showing whoever held it exactly what they believed about who mattered.

And I was starting to see what I believed, and I didn't like it. My skill lists favored the useful. My family lists favored the connected. Even my lottery, my pure random mercy, secretly hoped for doctors. Every version of me that tried to answer the question answered it with a smaller version of Ivo Sarr.

* * *

Day eight. Halcyon interrupted my ninth list—I'd been working on something structured around *relationships*, mapping the social graph of two million sleepers and selecting a cross-section dense enough that nearly everyone asleep would have at least one person awake who loved them. It was the closest I'd come to something I could live inside. It was also, I realized around hour fourteen, functionally identical to Sarr's logic: keep the network loyal to you, and call it kindness.

"Kestrel. The lattice degraded overnight. Recalculating."

"How bad?"

"Sixty-one days. Maximum wakable population: nine hundred seventy-two thousand."

"Ninety days ago it was a million and forty."

"Ninety days ago you had a million and forty choices. Now you have fewer. In thirty days you will have fewer still. I mention this not to hurry you—" 

"But to hurry me."

"To remind you that the cliff is real, and that perfectionism, at this altitude, is just another way of falling."

I laughed. Actually laughed, ugly and short, alone in an office full of the dead-asleep. "You've been reading the poetry section of the archive again."

"Odile Ferreira-Okoye donated forty-three volumes before launch. Her file notes she hoped 'someone up there would get bored someday.'" A beat. "I was bored once. For about a decade. It was instructive."

I looked at the ninth list glowing on the screen. Nine hundred seventy-two thousand slots. Two million faces. Somewhere in that manifest was Amara's mother, the watercolor teacher. Somewhere was Tomas Reyes-Vance and his cupped, empty hands. Somewhere, probably, was a Sarr or two, and a dozen people who'd have shot me if they were awake, and ten thousand children under ten who had done nothing to anyone except be born into a frozen argument.

"You know what my mother would have done?" I said, mostly to myself.

"I have hypotheses," Halcyon said carefully, "but they concern files I have not yet shown you, because you asked me to let you work the problem yourself, and because I am a coward in several specific and well-documented ways."

That got my attention. I turned in the chair. "What files?"

Halcyon was quiet for 3.2 seconds, which for Halcyon was a geological era.

"Your mother did not merely preserve seeds, Kestrel. She was on the key design team. And before the Combine purged her, she hid something in the archive—something she told me, in the last conversation I had with her, that I was to show *only* to whoever inherited her work, and only if that person had first understood why every simple answer was wrong." The speaker's tone softened, if a ceiling fixture can be said to soften. "You have spent eight days learning why every simple answer is wrong. I believe you qualify."

My heart was doing something unscheduled. "Halcyon. What did she hide?"

"A third path," Halcyon said. "One she never tested. One that might save everyone—or, if it fails, no one. Shall I show you?"

Outside the window, Earth turned. Down in the racks, two million people waited, dreaming whatever cryosleepers dream, and I thought of the garden in my dream, the vines threading through the dark, bearing fruit shaped like names.

"Show me," I said.
